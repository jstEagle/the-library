## 8. The Ninth Name

They took him at the ford.

Sebastian would remember it afterward in fragments: the cold water at his knees, the willows going silver in the last light, and then the sound — not hooves, but the absence of them, the way the forest went quiet all at once, like a held breath. He had time to think *Yara was right, they never travel loud* before the net came out of the dark and the world became rope and iron and the taste of his own blood where his lip split against a fist.

He did not call out. That was the first thing he noticed about himself, the thing that frightened him most. Somewhere behind him, east along the river, Tomas and Yara and the others were making camp under a hollowed-out hillside, and every instinct he had screamed to shout their names into the night and bring the whole hunt down on them rather than be taken alone. He kept his mouth shut. He had been an oath-keeper for eleven years. Silence was the first discipline he had ever learned, standing vigil at the boundary stones with Odile's hand on his shoulder: *What is kept must never be spoken.*

The silence had never cost anything before tonight.

* * *

They did not beat him. That surprised him more than anything else. The witch-hunters bound his wrists with cord rubbed in something bitter — he felt his gift go dim the moment it touched his skin, a candle pinched between wet fingers, and he understood that even this small stolen spark of magic Yara had woken in him was known to them, catalogued, countered — and then they simply walked him through the dark until the trees opened onto a road, and the road climbed, and at the top of it burned a camp of white tents.

Captain Vess waited by the largest fire.

She looked exactly as she had on the ridge above the valley, when he'd seen her through Yara's scrying-glass: a narrow woman in grey, unarmored, her hair cropped short as a soldier's, her hands folded as though she were waiting for bread to rise. There were scars on those hands. Old ones. He found himself staring at them and could not have said why.

"Sit," she said.

There was a stool by the fire. A real stool, brought from somewhere, set down for him as if he were an expected guest. Sebastian's legs nearly buckled with relief at the offer of sitting, and he hated himself for it, and sat.

"You're Pemberton," Vess said. It wasn't a question.

"I'm no one."

"You're the youngest oath-keeper the Valeward Guild has sworn in a hundred years." She said it flatly, without admiration, the way one might recite a supply inventory. "You're the last keeper of the old charter. And you are carrying, or you know the location of, a woman named Yara Solenne, who stole documents from the royal archive and fled across three provinces with them." She crouched, finally, so that their eyes were level. Her eyes were the color of rain over slate. "I am going to tell you what those documents say, Sebastian Pemberton, because I suspect you've only heard half of it."

"I don't—"

"Your guildmaster showed you the charter." Not a question either. Vess never asked questions; she made statements and let you correct them, and every correction told her more than the question would have. "The vessel. The prison. The null-field that isn't a ward but a cage. Yes?"

Sebastian said nothing. His heart was a fist against his ribs.

"The charter has nine names on it," Vess went on. "Nine keepers who swore the founding oath, binding the vessel to the valley, binding themselves to the vessel. Eight of those names died within a generation. The ninth—" she paused, and for the first time something moved behind her flat grey eyes, something almost like pity, "—the ninth name was never crossed out. Because the oath was written to pass down a bloodline, not to end with its signatories. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"No," Sebastian said. His voice came out very small.

Vess reached into her coat and drew out a folded parchment. Even scorched at one corner, even creased to softness from years of handling, he knew it instantly — had seen it only once, by lamplight in Odile's study, but some things imprint themselves on you the way a brand imprints on hide. The Valeward charter. Or a copy of it. Aldric's hunters had taken the original from the guildhall; of course they had a copy.

She unfolded it and turned it toward the firelight, and held it out.

"Read the ninth name."

His hands were bound, so she held it for him, which was somehow worse — being read to like a child, like a prisoner being shown the terms of his ransom. His eyes went down the column of ink-faded signatures. Maerlin of the Ford. Ossian Vale. Cathla, called the Quiet. And then the last line, the freshest ink on the page despite its age, written in a hand that slanted leftward, hurried, pressed hard enough into the vellum to leave grooves:

**Elira Pemberton, last true keeper, mother of the heir.**

Below it, smaller, in the same slanting hand: *Sebastian.*

"That's not possible," he said. "My mother died when I was born. Odile told me—"

"Odile told you what the charter required her to tell you." Vess rolled the parchment closed with brisk, gentle efficiency, as if putting away something fragile. "Your mother didn't die in childbirth, boy. Your mother walked out of the valley twenty-two years ago carrying you, and she walked back in alone, and she stood at the boundary stones and spoke the ninth name into the oath — your name — and then she asked the guild to take your memories of her. All of them. Yours and everyone else's. Because a keeper who is loved can be leveraged, and a keeper who is grieved for can be hunted, and she wanted you to grow up free of both."

The fire cracked. Somewhere in the camp a horse stamped. Sebastian sat very still, the way he used to sit at the boundary stones at dusk, feeling protected and imprisoned, and understood at last that those two feelings had always been the same feeling wearing different clothes.

"She erased herself," he said.

"She erased herself *from you*. From all of you." Vess studied him with that terrible level gaze. "But oaths remember what minds forget. The moment the null-field began to fail — the moment your friend Yara bled against the stones and the old magic woke — the ninth name woke with it. You've felt it. Don't lie to me; I've hunted the gifted for nine years and I know the look. Something in you has been *opening* since you crossed the valley border."

He had felt it. For weeks. A warmth behind his sternum whenever Yara worked her small cantrips on the road, a sense of words just beneath hearing, dreams of a woman's hands braiding his hair — dreams he'd assumed were inventions, comforts his mind had spun from nothing. They had not been nothing. They had been memory, leaking back through a broken seal.

"Why are you telling me this?" he whispered.

"Because my king wants the same thing your mother wanted, in the end." Vess rose, and the firelight threw her shadow long across the trampled grass. "Aldric holds a stolen crown. Every lord in the realm knows it; half of them are only waiting for a reason to move against him. But a king who controls the vessel controls the oldest magic in the world — the magic that decided borders, crowned the first kings, broke the second empire. With the vessel, Aldric isn't a usurper. He's an inheritor. Legitimacy written in fire instead of ink." She turned back to face him. "And the vessel answers to the ninth name. To you. No one else alive can open it. Which makes you, whether you wish it or not, the most valuable person in this kingdom."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we take you anyway, and we find other ways to make the vessel answer, and those ways involve things I have seen done to people and do not recommend." She said it without cruelty, which was the horrifying part. Vess spoke of torture the way a carpenter speaks of joinery. "Or — and hear me out, Pemberton, because I am offering this once — you swear service to the crown. Freely. In writing. You help us open the vessel, and in exchange you live. Not merely survive — *live*. Lands. A title. Protection for the five misfits you've dragged across half the realm. Odile is old; the guild is finished; the charter you're protecting is a dead woman's suicide note. Give me the ninth name given freely, and everyone you love walks away."

*Everyone you love walks away.*

The words went into him like a blade finding the gap in armor. He saw it all instantly, treacherously, vividly: Tomas laughing over a shared loaf, flour on his knuckles. Yara's tired smile as she coaxed sparks from his fingertips. Odile's hand on his shoulder at dusk. Merrin and Doss arguing over maps. All of them safe. All of them fed and sheltered and far from the pyres Aldric had built in every provincial square. All he had to do was betray a dead woman's last wish, break an oath older than the kingdom, and hand the world's most dangerous power to the man who had murdered his way onto a throne.

It would be so easy. That was the awful truth of temptation — it never asked you to become a monster. It only asked you to be reasonable.

"My mother gave up everything," Sebastian said slowly, "so that I wouldn't be leverage."

"Yes."

"And now you're leveraging me."

"Yes." Vess's mouth twitched — not quite a smile. "You see the problem. So did she. It's why she erased herself. Love is the flaw in every ward ever built, Pemberton. We don't need to break your oath. We only need to make breaking it the kindest thing you can do."

He looked into the fire for a long time. When he finally raised his head, something had settled in him — not peace, nothing like peace, but the stillness of a man who has stopped trying to hold two doors shut and chosen which one to walk through.

"You're right about one thing," he said. "The vessel answers to the ninth name."

Vess went very still.

"But my mother wrote that name into the oath," Sebastian continued, and felt the warmth behind his sternum flare like a struck match, "and the oath says what *she* meant it to say, not what Aldric wants it to mean. You should have asked me what the ninth name actually does."

For the first time, Captain Vess's folded hands came apart.

Outside the tent, a sentry shouted — a single word, cut short — and the camp erupted into torchlight and running feet, and from the darkness beyond the pickets came a sound Sebastian knew better than his own heartbeat: Tomas's laugh, wild and reckless, and the crackle of Yara's fire answering it like a promise.

* * *

They came for him fast, his family, out of the burning edge of the camp — and Sebastian rose from the stool with the bitter cord still biting his wrists, and smiled at Vess through the smoke, and said:

"You should have asked about the eighth name, too. It was never crossed out either."
