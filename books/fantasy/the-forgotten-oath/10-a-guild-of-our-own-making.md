## 10. A Guild of Our Own Making

The valley smelled different now.

Sebastian noticed it every morning when he stepped out of the guildhouse — the old stone building that had stood at the valley's heart for three hundred years and had never once, in all that time, smelled like bread. Now it did. Someone was always baking. The refugees who had come east over the pass in the first weeks after the Breaking had brought their ovens with them, their recipes, their stubborn insistence on feeding strangers, and the wind carried it down through the terraces where new houses were rising out of the ruins of the old wards.

He walked the boundary path anyway. Habit, or something older than habit. The stones still stood along the rim of the vale, but they were just stones now — gray, lichen-crusted, ordinary. He touched one as he passed, the way he'd touched them a thousand times before, and felt nothing but cold rock and morning damp. No hum. No pressure behind his ribs. No sense of being watched by something vast and sleeping beneath the earth.

Yara said he would stop missing it eventually. He wasn't sure he wanted to.

* * *

Below him, the market square was already loud.

It had been a sheep pasture once, back when the Valeward Guild numbered seven and the valley's population had been exactly seven. Now canvas stalls crowded the grass, and the air was full of a dozen languages. Mages from the free cities had come west in ones and twos at first, then in families, drawn by rumors of a valley where magic pooled like spring water and no one asked what you could do with it before they asked your name. Farmers came too, and smiths, and a whole troupe of players whose wagon had broken an axle on the mountain road and who had simply never left. Children ran between the stalls shrieking, and some of them left faint trails of light behind them when they laughed, and nobody minded.

Tomas held court near the well, arguing with a one-armed stonemason about whether the new meeting hall should face east or south. Tomas argued about everything with tremendous conviction and no authority whatsoever, which was precisely why people loved him. He caught Sebastian's eye across the square and raised two fingers to his temple in the lazy salute they'd invented somewhere on the road between here and the free cities — *still alive, are you?* — and Sebastian returned it.

*Still alive.* It meant more than it used to.

Mira was teaching a circle of children at the far end of the square, holding up cards with sigils painted on them, letting the little ones guess what each one did. Half her guesses were wrong; half of hers were wrong too, since Mira's own gift had come in crooked and strange after the vessel woke, and she could light candles but not lamps, heal scrapes but not fevers. She taught anyway. She said the mistakes were the best part. Sebastian watched her laugh at something a gap-toothed girl said, and felt the familiar ache-and-warmth in his chest that had become, over these months, simply the way his chest felt.

His family. Not found anymore. Just *made*, daily, the way you make anything worth having.

* * *

Odile was waiting for him in the guildhouse, in the room that had once held the sealed charter.

She looked smaller than she used to. That was the thing no one said aloud: the Breaking had cost her something more than it cost the rest of them. For forty years she had carried the weight of the lie — the charter, the null-field, the erased memories, the knowledge that the guild she loved was a wall around a prison — and when the truth flooded back into all their minds that terrible night, it had drained out of her like water from a cracked jar. She was not dying. But she was finished, in the way a long road finishes a traveler, and everyone could see it except, apparently, Odile herself.

On the table before her lay two documents.

The old charter, its wax seal broken forever, its nine names visible in the afternoon light. And beside it, a single sheet of fresh paper, blank except for the heading Sebastian had written three nights ago and then crossed out and written again eleven times.

"You've been avoiding this," Odile said.

"I've been rebuilding a valley."

"You've been hiding in a valley." Her voice was dry as ever, but there was warmth under it now, unhidden. There was very little hidden in Odile anymore. It was one of the strange gifts of her aging — the woman who had lied to protect them all had lost the knack for it entirely, and seemed relieved. "Sit down, Sebastian. Read me what you have."

He sat. He picked up the paper. His hands were steady, which surprised him.

"'We belong to each other,'" he read. "That's the first line. I couldn't get past it for days because everything else felt like it had to live up to it." He glanced up. "I finally decided nothing does. It just stands there at the top like a doorframe, and everything else walks through it."

"Read the rest."

He read the rest. It was short. The old charter had run to forty pages of obligations and blood-clauses and the careful architecture of secrecy; the new one filled a page and a half. It said the guild existed to shelter the gifted and the hunted, that its members chose each other freely and could leave freely, that leadership passed by vote and not by blood, that magic belonged to no vessel and no lineage but to whoever took up the work of tending it. It said the boundary stones would remain standing, not as a wall but as a threshold — a marker, so that anyone crossing into the vale would know they had crossed into a promise.

At the bottom, where the old charter had borne nine names in a mother's hand, there was space for names. As many as wanted to sign. Already fourteen were inked there, and more came every week.

When he finished, the room was quiet. Outside, someone was hammering; the sound came through the window in slow, patient strokes, like a heartbeat.

"The ninth name," Odile said at last. "Your mother wrote it at the bottom of the old charter, and it bound you to a prison you didn't know you were guarding. Do you understand the difference in what we're doing now?"

Sebastian looked down at the signatures. His own was third from the top — Yara's above his, because she'd refused to sign second and Mira had refused to sign first, and they had nearly come to blows over it in front of the entire market square until Odile had confiscated the pen like a schoolmistress.

"My mother gave me a name I never chose," he said, "and called it duty. This is—" He stopped. Started again. "This is choosing, out loud, in front of witnesses. Every day if we want. That's the difference. The oath isn't a chain anymore. It's a handshake."

Odile smiled. It transformed her face, made her look briefly like the young woman who must once have believed the guild could be something better than a tomb. "Then let us do the last thing," she said. "The vote."

* * *

They gathered at dusk, all of them, in the market square, because Odile refused to hold it in the guildhouse. "The guildhouse is a building," she said. "You are the guild. Buildings don't inherit anything."

There must have been two hundred people there. Refugees and mages, farmers and players, the one-armed mason and the gap-toothed girl and old Hesk who had walked out of Aldric's territory with nothing but a fiddle and the clothes he stood in. Torches burned in iron brackets around the square. Above the ridge, the first stars were coming out over mountains that no longer hid anything.

Odile stood on the well's low wall, and Sebastian stood beside her, and she told them plainly what she had never told anyone in forty years: everything. The charter, the vessel, the lie, the erasure, the years of rituals performed over a grave while calling it a garden. She told it without flinching, and the crowd listened without jeering, because most of them had fled places where the truth was a capital crime and they knew what it cost to speak one.

"Leadership of the Valeward Guild has passed from keeper to keeper for three hundred years," she said. "By blood, by oath, by a name written before any of us were born. I am the last keeper of that guild, and today it ends — not because it failed, but because it finished its work. What comes next is not inherited. It is elected. So I am asking you, all of you, to choose the person who will hold this charter and speak for this place. Raise your hand for Sebastian Pemberton."

Every hand in the square went up. Even the mason's, even though Tomas had loudly campaigned for himself that morning and gotten four votes and a pie out of it.

Sebastian hadn't expected the weight of it. Two hundred hands, held up in torchlight, choosing him — not because of his blood, not because of a name in an old book, but because he had walked the boundary stones with them and argued maps with them and buried the dead with them and baked, badly, with them. His throat tightened. Beside him, Yara's shoulder pressed warm against his arm, and Mira was crying openly and pretending she wasn't, and Tomas was shouting something about demanding a recount.

"He accepts," Odile said, before Sebastian could find his voice. "I can see he accepts. Somebody get him a pen."

* * *

Later — much later, when the fires had burned low and the square had emptied to stragglers and lovers and one extremely drunk fiddler — Sebastian climbed alone to the ridge.

The war was coming. Everyone knew it. Word had come down the pass only that morning: Aldric had taken the river cities, and his armies were turning west, and the free cities were sending envoys to ask the valley — the *new* valley, the impossible open valley full of ungoverned magic — which side it would stand on. Vess had sent word too, from somewhere in the east. Three lines, unsigned, in her narrow cold hand: *He knows what you built. He is coming to take it. I will meet you at the border, and this time I will be standing on your side of it.*

Sebastian sat against the old boundary stone and watched the lights of the market square flicker below, two hundred small flames that no ward protected and no oath concealed.

They would come for it. Of course they would. A king who stole a crown would steal a miracle to keep it. There would be armies on this ridge, maybe within the year, and the valley that had spent three centuries forgetting how to fight would have to learn, and the charter in Sebastian's pocket weighed almost nothing and would have to bear the weight of all of it.

But below him, a child's laughter rang up the hillside, trailing sparks. Somewhere in the dark, Yara was teaching Mira to bend candlelight into shapes, and both of them were laughing at their failures. Somewhere, bread was rising for tomorrow.

Sebastian took out the charter, and by starlight added a fifteenth name to the bottom of the list — a boy of perhaps ten who had arrived that evening, hollow-eyed and silent, carrying nothing, his gift still smoking faintly off his fingertips like a doused fire. He hadn't spoken yet. But he had watched Sebastian write, and when Sebastian handed him the pen, he had taken it.

*We belong to each other,* the first line read.

Far to the east, past the mountains, past the borders, torches were massing like a storm gathering on the horizon. Sebastian folded the charter, put it away, and went down the hill toward the light.
