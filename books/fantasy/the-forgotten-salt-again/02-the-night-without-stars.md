## 2. The Night Without Stars

The last charm of the day was a luck-knot for a fisherman's daughter, and Zephyrine tied it badly on purpose.

Three crossings in the cord instead of five, a knot that would hold through ordinary misfortune but unravel at the first touch of anything truly hungry. The girl—Maren, eleven, salt-crusted braids, her father's jaw already set in her face—watched the fingers work with the sharp patience children have when they suspect they are being cheated.

"That's not how Gran does it," she said.

"Your gran is a better knotter than I am." Zephyrine slid the cord across the counter. "This one's honest, though. It won't promise what it can't keep."

"What's it promise?"

"That you'll come home."

It wasn't nothing. It wasn't much. It was the size of life Zephyrine had built for themselves over three years in Greyharbor: small magics, honestly sold, each one deliberately weaker than it could have been, like bread cut thin to make the loaf last. The hum under their skin—the old power, the swordspeaker's inheritance that lived in the blood like a second pulse—stayed folded down where they kept it, beneath layers of habit and tedium and the particular gray exhaustion of pretending to be less than you were.

Maren counted out coppers one by one, licking her thumb between coins. Outside the shop window, Greyharbor did its evening arithmetic: gulls arguing over the day's offal, the tide coming up the channel smelling of iron and weed, lamplighters working the crooked streets with their long poles, flames blooming behind fogged glass one by one until the whole city glowed like something drowned and lit from within.

"Gran says there used to be a swordspeaker in Greyharbor," Maren said, tying the cord around her wrist. "Before. She says he could talk steel into singing."

Zephyrine kept their hands very still on the counter.

"People say a lot of things," they said.

"She says he died. Or left. Or got taken." The girl shrugged, the way children shrug off whole catastrophes. "Anyway. Thanks for the knot."

The bell above the door chimed her out into the fog. Zephyrine stood a moment in the settling dust of her departure, listening to the shop settle around them: the tick of the cheap clock, the drip of the roof leak into the bucket they'd never fixed because fixing it would mean admitting they were staying, the soft tick-tick-tick of cooling lamp oil.

Three years. Three years of this. A life shrunk deliberately small, folded up like a map so no one could read it. Some nights the smallness fit like a coat. Most nights it fit like a coffin that hadn't been nailed shut yet, and Zephyrine lay awake inside it counting the ways they'd learned to be invisible: the flattened accent, the charm-work pared down to things Warden inspectors could dismiss as folk superstition, the true name buried so deep even they almost never said it aloud to themselves.

They banked the lamp, ate standing up—barley and salt fish, the city's two food groups—and went out to walk the seawall, because sleep had been unreliable lately and walking was the only prayer they still remembered how to say.

* * *

Greyharbor's sky was usually an apology. Fog came off the water in the evening and smothered the stars before they could get properly started, so that the city lived most of the year under a low gray ceiling with only the moon showing through sometimes, pale and watermarked, like a coin seen through dirty ice.

Tonight the fog had lifted. That was the first wrong thing. Zephyrine noticed it halfway along the seawall—a strange brightness overhead, a clarity—and stopped, and looked up, and saw the stars all out at once, hundreds of them, thousands, the whole cold inventory of heaven spilled across the dark like salt from a torn sack.

They hadn't seen the full sky since they'd come here. For one breath it was beautiful. They stood with the wind pulling at their coat and let themselves feel it, the old vertigo of smallness under vastness, the feeling every swordspeaker was trained past in childhood and which Zephyrine had secretly never gotten over.

Then the stars began to go out.

Not fading. Not clouding over. Going out—one, then ten, then a hundred, in no pattern Zephyrine's eye could find, here and here and here, as if someone high above were pinching them out between finger and thumb. A constellation lost its heart first; the rest of its shape hung a moment, meaningless, and then followed. The sky went patchy, then ragged, then black in great spreading continents of nothing.

Zephyrine's body understood before their mind did. Their skin prickled. The hum under their skin—the folded-down thing, the dead magic, the lie they'd told themselves for three years—turned over in its sleep and *opened one eye.*

"No," Zephyrine said aloud, to the sky, to themselves, to nobody. "No, no—"

The last star went out somewhere near the horizon, a small white point snuffed like a candle dipped in water, and the night became total. Not dark the way nights were dark—dark with a floor to it, dark you could stand in—but absolute, a darkness with weight, pressing down on the city like a lid on a pot. The sea vanished. The seawall vanished. Zephyrine's own hands vanished when they raised them.

Somewhere behind them, in the city, dogs began to scream.

Not barking. Screaming—the high, continuous, awful sound of animals that know something. Then other sounds joining it, and voices, doors banging open all across Greyharbor at once, a hundred thousand people coming out into the streets to look up at a sky with nothing in it.

Zephyrine stood on the seawall in the blind dark with their heart slamming and the old power wide awake under their skin for the first time in three years, humming against its folds like a bird testing a cage, and felt the truth of the night sink into them colder than the wind off the water:

Stars didn't go out. Stars burned whether anyone watched or not. Something had to be *eating* them—or something had to be standing between them and the world, something vast enough to blot the whole sky at once, and either way—

Either way, the hiding place had never been safe. It had only been unnoticed. And tonight, whatever moved in the dark had noticed everything at once.

They fumbled their way back along the seawall by touch, by memory, by the rope rail worn smooth by a hundred years of fishermen's palms, and did not once succeed in making themselves breathe slowly.

* * *

The wards fell four hours after midnight.

Zephyrine knew the exact interval because they did not sleep—they sat in the dark shop with every lamp lit and the shutters barred, watching the candle flames, and the candles behaved strangely all night, leaning together toward some draft that wasn't there, whispering in a language of guttering that Zephyrine hadn't needed to read since before the exile. Fear, the flames said. Fear, and distance, and something coming closer.

At the fourth hour, the sound came.

Later, everyone in Greyharbor would describe it differently—some said thunder, some said a giant door slamming, some said the crack of the world's spine—but Zephyrine heard it as what it was: breaking glass heard through stone. A deep, vast, crystalline shatter, muffled by leagues of earth and mountain, rolling up out of the south and east where the border wards ran, the old wards, the First Warding, laid down centuries ago by ward-keepers whose names were carved into the stones themselves. Every child in the free cities grew up knowing those wards the way they knew the ground under their feet: not noticing them, the way you don't notice your own heartbeat, until it stops.

The sound lasted eleven seconds. Zephyrine counted. At the fifth second the hum under their skin rose to a whine. At the ninth, every candle in the shop went flat and blue. At the eleventh, silence—and a sensation like a great held breath finally released, a pressure that had pressed against the world for three hundred years suddenly gone, leaving everything feeling strangely thin, strangely *open*, like a house with all its doors flung wide in winter.

The border wards were down. All of them. At once.

Which was impossible. The Warding was redundant seven times over—it had been built to survive the fall of any three strongholds, any siege, any betrayal short of the end of the world. To bring it down in a single night you would need to unmake it from inside. From the heart of it. From the mountain court itself, if the stories were true about where the keystone slept now.

Zephyrine sat in the blue-flat candlelight with their hands flat on the table, feeling the tremor in their own fingers, and made themselves think it through like a ward-keeper, like their mentor had taught them, back when they'd had a mentor and a name and a self:

Wards don't fall. Wards are spent, or broken, or *unbound*. Someone had paid the price. Someone had paid a price large enough to buy the death of the First Warding, and there was only one currency Zephyrine knew of that big.

Souls.

The thought arrived whole and terrible, the way the worst thoughts do—not reasoned toward but simply *there*, fully grown, as if it had been waiting in them for years. King Aldous Vane. The usurper. The man who'd taken the throne in blood and then, instead of ruling, had begun to *dig*. Everyone said so, in the careful voice people used for treason: that Vane had emptied the prisons, then the poorhouses, then started paying for the living, and that the mountain court had begun to glow at night, visible from thirty miles, a light that wasn't firelight and wasn't torchlight and made livestock refuse to graze on the southern slopes.

Zephyrine had filed it away with all the other far-off horrors, the ones that happened to other people in other places. The war was north. The purges were west. Greyharbor was fog and fish and honest knots, and no one looked twice at a charm-seller with tired eyes.

But the stars had gone out over *Greyharbor*. The wards had fallen within earshot of *Greyharbor*. And the hum under Zephyrine's skin was awake now, wide awake, singing softly against its bindings like it knew something they didn't.

"You're imagining it," they told the empty shop. Their voice sounded wrong in the dark, too loud, too alone. "You've been alone too long. You're a charm-seller. You sell luck-knots to children."

The shop did not answer. The candles stayed blue.

* * *

The refugees came with the dawn—or with what passed for dawn now, a slow graying of the east that brought no sun anyone could see, just a bruised half-light like the sky after a fire. Zephyrine heard them before seeing them: wheels, hooves, the shuffle of a crowd too tired to panic anymore, moving up the coast road into the city.

By midmorning the fish market square was full of them. Zephyrine went, because not going would have been stranger than going, and because three years of practiced invisibility had a limit, and the limit was this: a sky with no stars in it, and roads filling with the displaced.

They were mostly farmers and fisherfolk from the southern coast, from the villages that huddled in the shadow of the border mountains. They came in carts pulled by oxen too thin to pull them, on foot, carrying children and chickens and, in one case, an entire loom dismantled and strapped to a man's back like a wooden tortoise shell. They had the look of people who had left in the night without deciding to—no plan in their faces, just motion, just the animal certainty that staying meant dying.

And they carried stories.

Zephyrine moved through the square the way they moved through everything now, quiet, hooded, buying nothing, offering nothing, listening. It wasn't hard. Nobody who'd walked forty miles on no sleep wanted to do anything but talk.

"The lights went out over the mountain," said a woman with flour still on her forearms, as if she'd fled mid-baking. "Not the stars. Lights. Under the mountain. Like the whole hill was a lantern with someone inside turning it up and up, and then—" she snapped her fingers, a dry little click, "—out. Everything out. Sky, lights, all of it."

"The king's men came through Vellmarsh a week ago," said an old man, sitting on his cart's tongue like it was a bench in his own kitchen. "Buying. Not grain. People. Paying silver for souls, is what my granddaughter called it, and we laughed at her, and then the wagons came back through empty and the families that sold wouldn't speak, wouldn't eat, just sat looking at the wall. My neighbor sold himself. Walked off smiling. His wife says he smiled the whole way down the road."

"They took the ward-stones," said a boy of maybe fifteen, eyes too old, talking to anyone, to no one. "I saw it. Soldiers at the Waymeet shrine, prying the stones out of the ground with bars, loading them on wagons. Old Marrowstone herself, the big one with the Ravensworth marks—"

He stopped. He'd said the name and something crossed his face, some fear of having said too much, though gods knew why, in a square full of strangers, in a city at the edge of the world.

Zephyrine did not move. Zephyrine had gotten very good, these three years, at not moving.

"—loading them on wagons," the boy finished, quieter. "Heading east. Toward the mountain."

Ravensworth marks. On the keystone of the First Warding. Zephyrine's own sigil, the coiled sword-and-tide, cut into the oldest stone of the oldest protection in the free cities—because it had been a Ravensworth, three generations back, who'd helped raise the Warding, back when the family served the crown and the crown deserved serving. Back when being a Ravensworth meant something besides a name you buried.

The pieces were arranging themselves in Zephyrine's mind with a horrible click-click-click, like a lock being picked from the inside: the stars eaten, the wards bought down, the soul-wagons rolling east, the mountain glowing like a lantern. Vane wasn't building an army. Vane was building a *fuel supply*. And the First Warding had been the wall between his hunger and the free cities, and it was down, and the thing that had blotted the stars—

A bell began to ring. Then another. Then a dozen, all across the city, the Warden-bells, the alarm pattern, three-and-one, three-and-one, and the square turned as one exhausted animal toward the harbor heights where the Warden tower stood.

On the tower's gallery, tiny with distance, figures in Warden gray were lighting the beacon pyre—the signal fire lit only when the city itself was threatened. Its flame caught and climbed, red and desperate against the starless sky, and Zephyrine watched it and felt the last comfortable lie of their exile burn away with it.

Because here was the thing they couldn't say to the flour-dusted woman or the boy or anyone: the beacon, the bells, the panicked gray coats scurrying on the tower—all of it assumed the danger was coming *up the road*. An army. Raiders. The usual shapes of ruin.

But Zephyrine had felt the wards fall through their own skin, had heard the glass break through the stone of the world, and what they'd felt in that moment, underneath the fear, underneath even the waking of the old power—was *recognition*. Somewhere south and east, something enormous had turned its attention across the ruined wards and swept it over the free cities like a lighthouse beam, and for one instant, one horrible instant, that attention had touched Greyharbor, had touched the seawall, had touched—

Had paused. Just slightly. Just long enough.

Something in the dark knew there was a swordspeaker in Greyharbor. Knew it the way a hand knows a coin in a pocket. And the oath in Zephyrine's blood, the binding they'd run three years to escape, had sung back to it like a struck wire before Zephyrine could clamp a hand over it.

They slipped out of the square before the boy could finish his story, before the Wardens began their inspections, before anyone could look too long at the charm-seller with the tired eyes and the shaking hands. Behind them the beacon burned and the refugees murmured and the city rang like a dropped bell, and ahead of them lay the shop, the shutters, the small gray life—which was over, had been over since the fourth hour after midnight, had perhaps always been a loan rather than a possession.

Zephyrine barred the door and stood in the middle of the dim shop, breathing, and it was then that they noticed the smell.

Fog and brine and lamp oil, the shop's ordinary smells. And under them, faint but unmistakable, growing stronger as the half-light failed outside: woodsmoke. Cold woodsmoke, the kind that clings to a coat long after the fire's out. The smell of a hearth in a house where someone has just stopped burning things.

There was no fire in the shop. There had been no fire.

From the corner by the cold stove, where the shadows pooled deepest, a girl's voice—soft, amused, terribly patient—said:

"Took you long enough. I've been trying to get your attention for three years, Zeph."
