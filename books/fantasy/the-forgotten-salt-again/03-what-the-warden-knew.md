## 3. What the Warden Knew

The refugees came in through the Salt Gate at dawn, and Ilex Vane—no relation, and he'd have killed anyone who suggested otherwise—was there to count them.

Not that anyone had asked him to count them. That was the thing about disgrace: it didn't release you from the work, it only stripped you of the authority to do it properly. He still woke before the bells the way he had for twenty years. Still walked the wall circuit with his hands in his pockets and his eyes moving in the old patterns—gate, gutter, ward-anchor, gate again. The Wardens' Guild had taken his badge, his keyring, and his name from their rolls, but they had never figured out how to take the habit out of his spine.

So he stood at the Salt Gate in the grey hour with a tally-stick he wasn't authorized to carry, counting the people who had walked out of a night without stars.

Forty-one by the time the fog thinned enough to see faces. Forty-two. A family from the marsh villages, salt-crusted to the knee, the youngest child asleep against its mother's shoulder with the boneless trust of the truly exhausted. Two drovers whose oxen had bolted three days ago and hadn't stopped running until Greyharbor's walls rose up. An old woman carrying nothing but a birdcage, empty, which she would not set down or explain.

And one man who would not stop talking.

"—and I'm telling you, warden or no warden, you can write this down or spit on it, but the sky went black like a hand over a candle." The talker was a carter named Ossory, or so he'd announced four times. Big man, road-worn, with the particular wildness of someone who had spent too many hours walking beside his own thoughts. "Not clouded. Black. I've seen storms. This wasn't weather. My wife's grandmother used to say the stars were nails, right? Nails holding the roof of the world up. Well, somebody pulled them."

"Somebody," Ilex said. He kept his voice flat, unhurried, the voice he'd used on a thousand frightened travelers at a thousand checkpoints. "Start at the beginning. Where were you when they went out?"

"Twelve miles east of Harrow Ford, camped at the crossroads."

"Did you hear anything? Before, after?"

Ossory's mouth worked. "After," he said, quieter now. "Maybe an hour after. A sound like—" He stopped, looked down at his own hands as if they might demonstrate for him. "You know when ice goes out on the river in spring? That cracking, that groaning? It was like that, but wrong. Like glass breaking, if the glass was the size of the horizon. And it didn't come from the sky. Warden, it came from *under*. From the ground. From the direction of the mountain."

Ilex wrote nothing on his tally-stick. His hand had gone very still around it, and he was grateful, suddenly, for the fog, because he did not want his face read just then.

The mountain. Of course the mountain. Everything in the last three years circled back to the mountain eventually, the way water circles a drain.

"Keep talking," he said. "What else did you see on the road?"

* * *

He worked the gate until midmorning, and what he learned, assembled piece by piece from forty-two mouths, was this:

The stars had gone out everywhere. Not faded—every witness was precise about this, almost offended by the suggestion of fading—*extinguished*, all at once, in the same instant, from the marsh villages to the coast roads. No clouds. No smoke. One moment the sky was doing what the sky has always done, and the next it was a lid.

The wards had fallen an hour later. Every traveler from the interior described the same sound, and every description made the hair on Ilex's arms stand: not thunder, not collapse, but a vast crystalline shattering heard through stone, through earth, through the soles of the boots, as though the ground itself had been the bell and something enormous had struck it once.

And the border wards—the great lattice that had stood since before the free cities were free, that had turned back the raiders and the revenants and the things that came down off the high passes in hungry winters—that lattice had not been broken *from outside*.

Ilex knew wards the way other men knew their mothers' faces. He had helped raise the eastern anchor-lines himself, twenty years ago, young and proud and certain, kneeling in the cold with his palms flat against raw ward-stone while the sigils drank his blood and his certainty in equal measure. He knew how they failed. They frayed. They eroded, century by century, and they could be cracked by force—a siege-engine of sorcery hurled against them from beyond—but they could not be *shattered*, cleanly, all at once, along their whole length, unless the failure began at the heart.

At the anchor-point. At the mountain court, where the oldest stones slept beneath the throne.

Someone had undone them from inside.

He was turning this over, walking the wall circuit without quite seeing it, when he saw the boy.

The boy was maybe ten, one of the marsh family, sitting alone on the harbor steps while his mother argued with the relief-wardens about ration chits. He was turning something over in his hands. Something pale. And even at thirty paces, even in fog, Ilex's eye went to it the way a compass needle goes north, because he had spent twenty years learning the shape of that particular pale grey, that faint internal shimmer, that weight-in-the-hand that was less than stone and more than bone.

Ward-stone. A fragment of it, small as a knucklebone.

His heart began to behave very badly.

"Hello," he said, crouching to the boy's level, keeping his voice gentle. "That's an interesting rock you've got."

"It's not a rock." The boy held it up, unafraid. Children always showed him things; it was one of the small ironies of his disgrace that children trusted him more than the badged Wardens ever had. "Da found it on the road. Near where the loud noise came from. He says it fell out of the sky, but Ma says rocks don't fall out of clear skies, and Da says the sky wasn't clear anymore, was it."

"No," Ilex said. "It wasn't." He held out his hand. "May I?"

The boy gave it over.

It was warm. That was the first thing, the thing that made his stomach drop through the harbor steps and into the black water below. Ward-stone should be cool. Ward-stone that had been part of a living lattice for twenty years carried the lattice's temperature, the slow cold heartbeat of anchored magic, and this fragment was warm as a living thing, warm as a hand, warm as—

He turned it over.

Sigils covered its face. Fine-cut, deep-carved, the lines still sharp despite everything, and Ilex read them the way he breathed, without deciding to: anchor-sigil, binding-sigil, and threaded through both, unmistakable, a name-form.

*Ravensworth.*

The world went narrow and quiet. The fog, the gulls, the boy's chatter, the relief-wardens shouting about chits—all of it receded, and there was only the fragment in his palm and the name carved into it and the memory rising in him like floodwater.

Because he knew those sigils. He had cut some of them himself, or watched them cut, twenty years ago, kneeling in the cold at the eastern anchors. The Ravensworth line had been ward-masters to the crown for six generations; their name-forms were woven through the border lattice like thread through cloth. And there had been a Ravensworth at the raising—Ilex remembered him, or her, the memory was blurred at the edges the way his worst memories were blurred, a tall figure in a travel cloak with a voice like a drawn blade, who had argued with the crown's engineers about load-bearing sigils and been overruled, who had placed the final anchor-stone with their own two hands and pressed their palm to it and spoken words Ilex had been too far away to hear.

Three years ago, the court records said, the last swordspeaker of House Ravensworth had vanished. Deserted, the warrant said. Traitor, the heralds said, when they bothered to say anything at all.

And here was a shard of the border lattice, torn loose from its heart, inscribed with the Ravensworth name, *warm as a living hand.*

"Hey," the boy said. "You've gone funny-colored."

"I'm fine." Ilex closed his fingers over the fragment. "I need to borrow this. I'll bring it back. Tell your father a warden borrowed it—"

"You're not a warden. You've got no badge."

"I'm a warden," Ilex said, and heard, to his own surprise, how much it still cost him to say the word and how much more it cost him not to say it. "That's rather the problem."

* * *

He took it to his rooms above the chandler's shop on Rope Walk, barred the door, shuttered the windows, and lit one lamp, and then sat looking at the fragment under the light for a long time without touching it again.

Here was what he knew. Here was what twenty years of ward-craft told him, in the flat, merciless voice of craft itself:

A lattice does not shatter from outside. Force cracks it locally; force makes holes; force is what raids and sieges do, and the lattice heals around holes the way flesh heals around wounds, slowly, scarred, but whole. For the entire border lattice to fail at once—instantly, catastrophically, with a sound like the world's largest bell breaking—the failure had to begin at the master anchor. At the heart. Under the mountain.

And a master anchor does not fail on its own. It fails when its own name-forms are unwoven. When someone with access—someone the lattice trusts, someone whose blood and name are already written into it—stands at its heart and *unmakes* it.

Someone with Ravensworth sigils. Someone whose name was carved into the deepest stones.

Or someone who had *taken* those sigils. Who had harvested them. Who had peeled the name off a traitor and used it like a stolen key.

Ilex put his head in his hands.

Because here was the other thing he knew, the thing he had not said aloud in three years, the thing that lived in him like a swallowed coal: he had helped build those wards. Not the deepest anchors, not the heart—he had never been permitted near the heart, none but the Ravensworths and the crown's own engineers were—but the eastern lines, yes. The secondary lattices. The relay-stones. He had knelt in the snow with his blood on his palms and given the lattice his certainty, and the lattice had taken it and held the border safe for twenty years, and last night it had died, and every soul-eating horror that the lattice had held back was now standing at the edge of the free cities with nothing between it and them but distance and luck.

And he had been *proud*. That was the worst of it. He could live with the graft charge, the falsified inspection logs, the disgrace, the badge on the Guildmaster's desk—he had done what he'd done and he'd paid for it, mostly, though some nights he suspected the payment was still arriving. But he could not live with the pride. Twenty years old, kneeling in the snow, thinking: *I am building the wall that will hold for a hundred years.* And the wall had lasted exactly as long as it took for someone inside it to want it gone.

The lamp guttered. Outside, the fog pressed against the shutters, and somewhere down Rope Walk a woman was telling her neighbor, in the carrying voice of people who want to be overheard, that the stars going out meant the gods had blinked, and that what the gods saw when they opened their eyes again would not be forgiven.

Ilex lifted his head. He took out his knife—not the ceremonial ward-knife, long confiscated, but the plain working knife—and scraped a curling shaving from the fragment's edge into a saucer of lamp oil, the old field-test, crude and half-superstitious and reliable.

The oil caught light without flame. Just for a moment: a soft blue glow, rising from the saucer like breath, tracing shapes in the air above it. Anchor-sigil. Binding-sigil. Name-form.

And beneath the name-form, visible only in the glow, only for a heartbeat, a second mark. A countermark. Fresh-cut, shallow, ugly—the signature of an unmaking, the tool-mark of whoever had unwoven the lattice from its heart, scored right across the Ravensworth name like a blade dragged through a signature on a contract.

Someone had used the Ravensworth name to open the border. And then they had defaced it.

Which meant the Ravensworth whose name it was was either complicit—or dead, or emptied, or *spent*, their name peeled off them like bark off a branch and used by hands that had no right to it.

Ilex sat back. His hands were shaking, and he let them, because there was no one to see.

There was a Ravensworth in Greyharbor. He'd known it for three years, the way you know a splinter is in your thumb—aware of it, ignoring it, aware that ignoring it was itself a choice. The charm-shop on Brine Street. Luck-knots and fever-charms and small honest magics sold by a person who kept their head down and their door open and their power folded up small as a moth's wing. Everyone in the trade knew; nobody said. Greyharbor's mercy was the mercy of people who minded their own business because their own business needed minding.

A disgraced ward-keeper and a deserted swordspeaker. The Guild would call it a conspiracy if they spoke twice.

But the Guild's badges were still on their rolls, and the border was down, and the stars were out, and somewhere under the mountain someone was cutting countermarks into stolen names.

Ilex wrapped the fragment in flannel, put it in his coat pocket, checked that his knife was sharp, and pulled the shutters open onto the fog.

Brine Street was fifteen minutes' walk, if the Wardens weren't watching the charm-shops today.

He locked his door behind him and went to find out what a Ravensworth knew about the dark.

* * *

The fog thickened as he crossed toward Brine Street, and by the time he reached the lane the lamps were haloes and the shopfronts were suggestions. The charm-shop's sign hung motionless—no wind tonight, no wind anywhere, air gone dead as held breath—and Ilex stood across the street a moment, feeling foolish, feeling twenty years old and proud again, feeling the coal in his chest shift.

Through the shop window, lamplight. A figure moving behind the counter. Ordinary. Deliberately ordinary, he'd have bet his last coin on it.

Then the light inside flickered—not guttered, *flickered*, in a rhythm that had nothing to do with oil or wick, a stutter like a heartbeat skipping—and even at this distance, even through fogged glass, Ilex felt it against his skin: a pulse of raw ward-pressure rolling off the shop like heat off a forge door flung open.

Something was happening in there. Something with teeth.

He crossed the street, took hold of the handle, and pushed the door of the charm-shop open just as a girl's voice—from the corner where no girl stood—said clearly:

"He's coming for your name next, Zeph. And I think he already knows it."
