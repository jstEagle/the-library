## 4. The Girl Who Remembers

The morning after the stars went out, Greyharbor did the thing Greyharbor always did: it pretended nothing had happened.

Zephyrine heard it through the shutters before dawn—the fish-carts rattling over cobbles still slick with fog, the gulls arguing over the day's first offal, a neighbor's voice calling that the herring run was early this year, as if an empty sky were weather and weather were ordinary. The city had survived plagues, blockades, three changes of crown without changing its habits. It would survive this too, or it would not, but either way there would be herring.

Zephyrine opened the shop anyway.

It felt like a small betrayal to do it—unrolling the awning, setting out the tray of luck-knots, arranging the little glass bottles of salt-and-iron dust in their careful rows—as if commerce were a kind of lie told loudly enough to drown out the truth. But hiding required routine the way drowning required water. You could not simply stop breathing; you had to keep doing it so smoothly no one noticed you were doing it at all.

So: charms for sale. Luck-knots, three coppers. Wart-charms, five. A jar of *safe-passage* salts that were mostly table salt with a whisper of something real stirred through, because even a fraud needed a spine of honesty or the Wardens smelled it on you.

By midmorning the fog had come in off the harbor the way it always did, thick as wool, and the shop's single window turned the color of old milk. Zephyrine sat behind the counter with a knot half-plaited and let the silence of the sky press down. Three years of practice had taught them how to sit with dread without letting it show in the hands. The hands kept plaiting. The hum under their skin—the one they'd spent three years smothering, starving, pretending was only blood—had been restless since last night, though. It rose along their forearms like a tide coming in, and they pressed their palms flat against the counter until the wood drank it.

No one came in all morning. That was unusual. Fear kept people home, or kept them clustered in the fish market where the news arrived loudest, and Zephyrine found they could not decide which they preferred: customers, who might look too long at their face, or emptiness, which left room for thinking.

They were re-counting the luck-knots for the fourth time when the temperature in the shop dropped.

Not the way fog made things cool—a wet, external chill—but a cold that started *inside*, a cold like remembering winter from the middle of it. The lamp guttered though there was no draft. The luck-knots on the tray shivered, every thread standing away from its neighbors, and the bottle of safe-passage salts went cloudy as if something had breathed into it.

Zephyrine knew what cold like that meant. They had known it once, in another life, in the life they could not remember. Their body knew it the way a scar knows rain.

"Show yourself," they said quietly, "or leave."

"I've been here since yesterday," said a girl's voice, from the corner by the drying rack. "You just weren't looking."

She stepped out of the shadow the way a swimmer steps out of deep water—one moment submerged, the next standing in the lamplight, dripping light instead of water. She looked sixteen, perhaps seventeen. Dark hair cut short and crooked, the way hair gets cut when someone does it themselves with a knife. A dress of undyed wool, patched at both elbows. Her feet didn't quite touch the floorboards, and where she stood, the grain of the wood showed through her hem.

A ghost. An actual ghost, in Zephyrine's shop, on the morning after the night without stars, and the first coherent thought Zephyrine managed was: *the Wardens will fine me for harboring an unregistered spirit.*

The second thought, arriving slower and colder: *she is looking at me like she knows me.*

"You're the charm-seller," the girl said. Not a question. She tilted her head, studying them with an expression far too old for her face—patient, almost fond, the way you look at someone you've watched for a long time without their knowledge. "Three years selling knots and salts and telling everyone your grandmother taught you. Your grandmother never existed. I know. I checked."

Zephyrine set down the plaiting. Kept their voice level, because a level voice was armor, and armor was the only inheritance they trusted. "Who are you?"

"Sable." She said it plainly, without ceremony, the way you say a name you've had to say many times to people who didn't want to hear it. "I died two years ago. Up past the ridge line, in the pass road, in the snow. And I remember—" she stopped herself, and something flickered across her face, some grief she folded away with visible effort, "—I remember a great many things. That's rather the point of me."

"The point of you," Zephyrine repeated.

"Ghosts don't linger because we're sentimental, charm-seller. We linger because we're *unfinished*. Most of us unfinished about our own lives—who wronged us, what we left undone. But I'm unfinished about someone else's." She drifted closer, and the lamp flame bent toward her like a plant toward sun. "Yours."

"My what?"

"Your past." Sable stopped an arm's length from the counter, close enough that Zephyrine could see she had freckles, that one of her ears was pierced with a bent fishhook in place of an earring, that her eyes were the gray-brown of harbor water. Details. Ghosts, in the stories, were smoke and suggestion. This one was *specific*, and the specificity frightened Zephyrine more than the apparition itself. "You're missing three years. You know that much, don't you? You woke up in the Saltmarsh ferry-house with a headache and a purse full of coin and no idea how you got there, and everything before that is a wall with a door painted on it."

Zephyrine's hands had gone still on the counter. They hadn't told anyone that. Not the landlady, not the neighbors, not the confessor they'd sat with twice out of desperation and never returned to. The gap in their memory was the one secret they'd kept even from themselves, approached only sideways, at dusk, never directly.

"How do you—"

"I was there," Sable said. "At the end of it. At the beginning of it. I saw what was taken from you, and I saw who took it, and I have spent two years being dead and furious about it." Her voice cracked on the last word, and the temperature dropped again; frost feathered up the inside of the window, delicate as lace. She mastered herself. "I would have come sooner. The dead don't travel well through wards, and this city wears its wards like a coat. But last night the stars went out, and every ward in the world hiccupped, and I walked straight through yours like a door left open."

Outside, somewhere down the lane, a dog began to howl, then another, the whole street of them catching it like fire catching a roofline. Zephyrine barely heard.

"Taken," they said. The word tasted strange. "My memory wasn't lost. It was *taken*?"

"That's what I said."

"By whom?"

Sable smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of someone holding back a wave with her hands. "That's the wrong question. You're asking *who*, because *who* is a person and persons can be hated, and hatred is comfortable. Ask *what* first. Ask what has the power to take three years out of a person's head and leave the rest tidy." She leaned forward. "Ask what you were willing to pay."

The hum under Zephyrine's skin surged, sudden and hot, and for one vertiginous heartbeat the shop doubled in their vision—this cramped room with its trays and bottles, and beneath it, overlaid like a watermark, another version of the same room, brighter, fuller, hung with swords—

Gone. Zephyrine gripped the counter's edge. The wood creaked.

"What did you see?" they asked, and their voice came out rougher than they intended. "When you looked at my past. What's behind the wall?"

"Would you believe me if I told you?"

"No."

"Good." Sable nodded approvingly. "Then listen anyway. You were someone, charm-seller. Before Greyharbor, before the knots and the salts and the small gray life—you were someone with a title and an oath and a name that people used carefully, the way you handle a blade. You had a teacher. You had enemies. You had—" she hesitated, and for the first time the ghost looked uncertain, almost shy, "—you had people who loved you. Plural. More than one. I remember their faces better than you ever will now, and that makes me angry, that *I* should have what's yours."

"Stop." Zephyrine hadn't meant to say it aloud. The shop seemed very small and very bright and very far away, all at once. "Stop talking."

"Why? Because it's true?"

"Because I *chose* this." Zephyrine stood, and the stool scraped back, and their own voice sounded foreign to them, too loud in the fog-muffled quiet. "Whatever I was, I walked away from it. I built this life brick by brick. Small. Quiet. Invisible. That wasn't exile, it was—it was *carpentry*. And you come drifting in through my walls with your frost and your fishhook earring and you tell me the person I decided to be is a wound that needs opening?"

"Yes," Sable said simply.

"It isn't!"

"Isn't it?" The ghost drifted around the counter—not walking, exactly, but progressing the way smoke progresses—and stopped before Zephyrine, near enough that the cold coming off her raised the fine hairs on their arms. "Then why do you flinch at mirrors? Why do you sleep with your back to the wall and your boots within reach, if you're only a charm-seller? Why, when the stars went out last night, did your first thought—your very first, before fear, before the Wardens—was *they've found me*?" She tilted her head. "Invisible lives don't brace for discovery, charm-seller. Only hidden ones do."

Zephyrine opened their mouth. Closed it. Because the terrible thing—the truly terrible thing—was that she was right, and they had known she was right for three years, and knowing it had changed nothing, because a life shrunk deliberately small is still a life, and a small life held is worth more than a large one lost.

"I can't get it back," they said finally. Quieter now. "If it was taken—if someone *took* it—then it's gone wherever taken things go, and I built my house on the empty lot, and I'm not tearing it down for a ghost's spite."

"Not spite." Something moved across Sable's face, quick as a fish under ice. "Debt. I owe you. You won't understand that yet either, and I can't tell you why, and believe me I have tried, in two years of trying, to find a loophole in the rules of what the dead may say." She spread her hands. "But the stars going out loosened more than the wards. Things are moving, charm-seller. Things are coming down out of the mountain, and names are being bought and sold in places you don't want to imagine, and one of those names is yours. It's already in a ledger. I've seen the entry."

The words landed like stones dropped into still water. *One of those names is yours.* Zephyrine thought of the border wards falling with that sound like breaking glass heard through stone. Thought of the refugees' stories—souls fed to the court beneath the mountain, armies that did not tire, a king who paid his soldiers in other people's light.

"If my name is in a ledger," Zephyrine said slowly, "then someone means to collect on it."

"Someone already has," Sable said. "Once. And means to again." She glanced toward the window, toward the fog and the milk-white absence of sky, and when she looked back at Zephyrine her young-old face had gone grave. "Which is why I'm not going to tell you everything today. Today I'm going to tell you one thing, and you're going to hate me for it, and then you're going to spend tonight not sleeping, and tomorrow you'll start asking the right questions."

"Which is?"

Sable reached out—not touching, ghosts couldn't touch, but her hand hovered over Zephyrine's wrist where the pulse beat, and the cold of her sank through the skin like a needle of winter finding the vein.

"The man who took your three years," she said, "is the same man who put out the stars. And he knows exactly where you are. He's known for a long time. He hasn't sent for you yet because he's waiting for something—waiting for the wards to finish dying, waiting for the city to burn its witches and drive you into the open." Her hand withdrew. "He doesn't need to hunt you, charm-seller. He only needs to make Greyharbor too hot to hold you. And he's very good at heat."

The lamp went out.

Not guttered—went out, all at once, though the wick was fresh and the oil half-full, and in the sudden gray dark Sable remained, faintly luminous, a girl-shaped piece of the vanished starlight standing in Zephyrine's shop.

"Two days," she said. "Maybe three. When the mob comes for the magic-sellers—and it will come, it's already gathering in the fish market—you need to be gone. There's a man who can help you. A ward-keeper. Disgraced, bitter, drinks more than he should, hates your family name with a passion I find frankly impressive. His name is Ilex."

"Ilex." The name snagged on something deep in Zephyrine, some hook below the waterline of memory. They frowned. "I know that name. I don't know *how* I know it, but—"

"You knew him," Sable said softly. "Before the wall. He was there when you lost everything. Whether he helped or hindered—" the ghost's mouth twisted, "—even I'm not entirely sure. But he's the only door out of this city that Vane's people aren't watching, and doors matter more than friends right now."

"Vane," Zephyrine repeated. The usurper's name, spoken aloud in their own shop, felt like letting cold air into a warm room. "You're saying King Aldous Vane took my memory."

"I'm saying," Sable said, drifting back toward the corner, already thinning into the shadow she'd come from, "that the ledger doesn't record kings. It records buyers. Go find Ilex. Find him before the burning starts. And charm-seller—" she paused at the edge of visibility, and for a moment her voice caught, young and raw and grieving, "—when you meet him, watch his hands. Whatever else he's become, he still carries something of yours. He doesn't know what it is. But he carries it."

Then she was gone, and the cold went with her, and the lamp relit itself with a soft *pop* as if embarrassed by the interruption.

Zephyrine stood alone in the shop for a long time. Outside, the fog pressed against the glass, and beyond the fog, where the sky should have been, there was nothing at all.

Slowly, mechanically, they began closing the shop. Luck-knots into the box. Bottles into the cabinet. The motions of a life built brick by brick—and for the first time in three years, Zephyrine looked at each small familiar object and saw it clearly for what it was: furniture arranged in an empty room. A house built on a lot someone had stripped bare.

*I was someone,* they thought, turning the lock. *I had a teacher. I had people who loved me.*

The thoughts should have comforted. Instead they fell through Zephyrine like stones through deep water, down and down, into the dark where the memories should have been, striking nothing.

Tomorrow, then. Ilex. The disgraced ward-keeper whose name fit a hole in them perfectly, like a key fitted to a lock they couldn't see.

And underneath the thought, quieter, colder, keeping time with their pulse:

*Watch his hands.*
