## 5. Oath-Bound

The ghost did not sleep. Zephyrine learned this the way one learns a neighbor's habits—by small accumulations of evidence. Sable sat on the shop's counter through the small hours, swinging legs that cast no shadow in the lamplight, watching Zephyrine pretend to inventory charm-cords that needed no counting.

"You're going to wear a groove in that floor," Sable said, "walking the same six feet all night."

"Ghosts don't need sleep. The living do."

"The living also don't lie to themselves for three years and then act surprised when it catches up." Sable hopped down, bare feet making no sound on the boards. She was perhaps sixteen, or had been once; her dress was river-gray at the hem, and when she moved too quickly her edges went soft as breath on glass. "I know what you're doing. You're waiting for me to get bored, or fade, or turn out to be a trick of the salt fog. You're very patient. It's almost admirable."

"And you're very persistent."

"I'm dead," Sable said simply. "Persistence is the only thing I have left." She drifted closer, and the lamp flame leaned toward her like a flower toward sun, which was wrong, which was deeply wrong, because ghosts should take warmth from a room, not give it. "You want to know the worst part? You *do* remember me. Somewhere under everything they took. I can see it in your face every time you decide not to look at me. It's like watching someone hold a door shut against a gale."

Zephyrine set down the charm-cord. Their hands were steady—they had trained them to be steady even before they could remember why—but their pulse ticked in their throat like a second, smaller heart.

"They," Zephyrine said. "You keep saying *they took*. Taken by whom? How?"

Sable smiled, and there was no joy in it, only a kind of terrible gentleness. "Not how I wanted you to find out. But the stars are still gone, Zeph, and the wards are still broken, and every day you spend pretending is a day the man on the mountain gets to keep his head start." She raised one translucent hand. "So. Hold still. This part stings."

"What part stings—"

Sable touched two fingers to Zephyrine's forehead, and the memory came up like a hook through water.

* * *

Rain. The rain came first, always, in the memory: silver sheets of it hammering the flagstones of the Oathcourt, running off the eaves of the old palace in ropes, pooling around the boots of nine hundred swordspeakers kneeling in ranks. Zephyrine was twenty-two and could feel every stone through the wet wool of their knees. Beside them knelt Maren Solenne, their mentor, gray at the temples even then, her mouth moving silently through the words she'd made Zephyrine rehearse for a month.

*"Do you understand what you're giving?"* Maren had asked her, the night before, over cheap wine in a barracks that smelled of oil and wet leather. *"Not your sword. Not your life. Any fool can give those. Your voice, girl. Your name. Once you swear, whoever holds the crown holds the word that moves you. Kings die. Thrones change hands. The oath doesn't care. It listens for the name, not the man."*

*"Then I'll make sure good people hold the throne,"* Zephyrine had said, young and certain and insufferable, and Maren had laughed—a short, unhappy sound—and drunk her wine.

Now the herald's voice rolled across the court, magnified by ward-work so that it seemed to come from everywhere at once, from the sky itself:

*"By voice and by steel, by salt and by star—"*

Nine hundred voices answered. Zephyrine's own voice rose among them, and this was the part they had forgotten, the part that lived now behind Sable's fingers like a coal: the sensation of the oath going in. It did not feel like chains. That was the horror of it, remembered from the inside—it felt like *alignment*, like a bone setting true, like a compass needle finally allowed to point. Something in the deep architecture of Zephyrine's self turned and settled and faced the throne, and would face it, forever, wherever the throne was, whoever sat upon it.

The old king spoke each initiate's true name in turn—not the name their mother used, but the deeper one, the one given at the whispering, the one that was less a sound than a key. When he spoke Zephyrine's, the rain seemed to stop falling *around* them, a column of dry air in the drowning world, and Zephyrine felt the name leave their own keeping and enter the crown's.

Maren gripped their hand afterward, both hers around one of theirs, hard enough to hurt.

"Now you belong to an office," she said, "not a person. Remember that. Offices are slower to rot than men." Her eyes were wet, and Zephyrine had thought, foolishly, that it was pride.

It was grief. It had always been grief.

* * *

Zephyrine came back to themselves on the floor of their own shop, cheek against cold boards, Sable kneeling beside them with her hand still hovering an inch from their brow, afraid to touch.

"There you are," she whispered. "There you are. Breathe."

Zephyrine breathed. The shop swam back into focus: the luck-knots, the jars of salt, the crooked sign that read CHARMS & SMALL MERCIES. Ordinary things. Safe things. None of it felt safe anymore. None of it had ever been safe; it had been *unnoticed*, which was a different animal entirely.

"The oath," Zephyrine rasped. "I swore the oath. To the crown. To—the office of the crown."

"To whoever holds it," Sable said. "Yes."

"And my true name—"

"Went with it. Into the keeping of the throne." Sable sat back on her heels. In the lamplight her translucence looked less like absence and more like something worn thin. "That's what a swordspeaker is, Zeph. Not a knight. Knights are hired. Swordspeakers are *owned*. Your magic doesn't answer to you. It answers to the name, and the name answers to the crown, and any king who speaks it can walk your body like a puppet walks on strings."

Zephyrine pushed upright, and the room tilted, and they let it, because the alternative was thinking clearly about what they had just remembered. Three years of missing time. A life shrunk deliberately small. And underneath it all, wired into the marrow, a leash they hadn't known they were wearing.

"Vane," they said. "If Vane has the archives—if he has the rolls of names—"

"He has the mountain," Sable said. "He has everything the mountain kept. And the mountain kept everything."

Zephyrine stood. Walked to the window. Greyharbor lay below in its usual shroud, salt fog curling through the streets like something alive and indifferent, lamps blooming yellow in the murk, fishing boats knocking gently at their moorings. A city that did not know it was sleeping next to a loaded weapon. *I am the weapon,* Zephyrine thought, and the thought had teeth.

"How do I break it?" Their voice came out flat. Practical. The voice of someone pricing a job. "An oath that strong—there's always a flaw. Maren taught me that. Nothing woven stays woven forever."

Something flickered across Sable's face—too fast to name, gone before it landed. Guilt, maybe. Or fear. Or the particular grief of someone who knows an answer and knows its price.

"Not tonight," she said softly.

"Sable—"

"*Not tonight.*" For a moment her form sharpened, solidified, and Zephyrine saw her as she must have been alive: stubborn, bright-eyed, jaw set like a girl who had argued her way out of worse things than death. Then she softened again. "Tonight you need to hear the other thing. The thing I actually came here to say, before I got distracted trying to be gentle."

Zephyrine turned from the window. "Which is?"

"The oath needs a name to work. Vane has the archive—but names in a ledger are just ink unless someone speaks yours *correctly*, with the cadence, the binding-words. There are maybe four people alive who ever heard your true name spoken whole." Sable held up fingers, folding them down one by one. "Your mentor—dead, and her soul sold to the court beneath the mountain, which means Vane may have her memories along with her. The officiant who crowned the old king—dead two winters, natural causes, I checked. The Master of Ledgers—who serves Vane now, and who has never in his long, gray life refused a price." She paused on the last finger but did not fold it. "And you."

The silence stretched. Outside, somewhere down the lane, a dog began to bark—short, panicked bursts—and then stopped all at once, as if a hand had closed over its muzzle.

"So either Vane finds someone who can speak it," Zephyrine said slowly, working it through, "or he finds a way to pull it out of a bottled soul. Out of Maren."

"Or he doesn't need to speak it at all yet," Sable said. "Because a leash works both ways, Zeph. He can't command you until he speaks the name. But the oath still *pulls*. Even dormant. Even after three years of you burying it under charms and salt. It pulls you toward the throne like a tide toward the moon. Why do you think you've never managed to leave Greyharbor? Why do you think, of all the cities on the coast, you hid in the one directly beneath the mountain's shadow?"

Zephyrine opened their mouth to argue and found nothing there. Three years of smallness. Three years of telling themselves it was caution, strategy, patience. And all along it might have been the oath, reeling them in by inches, patient as water.

"I have to get ahead of it," they said. "Before he learns the name. Before—"

The door burst open.

Not kicked—burst, as though flung by wind, though the night outside hung windless, and the fog poured in across the floorboards like something invited. Zephyrine's hand went to the wall where a sword should have hung and found only a drying rack of herb-bundles, and the absence of the blade rang louder than any alarm.

In the doorway stood a woman Zephyrine knew by sight and reputation both: the Warden Ilex, disgraced keeper of the harbor wards, gray-cloaked, mud-spattered, wild-eyed, with a fragment of pale stone clutched in one fist like a talisman. Behind him, far off in the fog, a bell had begun to ring—not the harbor bell, not the temple bell, but the rough, urgent clangor of the fish market's alarm.

"Ravensworth." His voice cracked on the name. He looked at Zephyrine, then at the ghost by the counter—he could see her, Zephyrine realized distantly, he could *see* her—and his face did something complicated and grim. "They pulled a charm-seller out of his stall in the fish market. Rope and torches. The city's decided what magic is worth tonight, and it isn't coin." He swallowed. "And your name is on a list. It's on a list that came down from the mountain, and the Wardens' new commander is reading it aloud at the market cross right now."

Sable rose without weight, without sound, and her eyes met Zephyrine's over the Warden's shoulder.

"Told you," she said quietly. "No more gentle nights."
