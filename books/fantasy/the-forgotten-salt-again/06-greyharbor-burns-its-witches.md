## 6. Greyharbor Burns Its Witches

The fish market smelled of brine and blood on any ordinary morning, but this morning it smelled of pitch.

Ilex had been a ward-keeper for nineteen years before they took the badge off him, and in nineteen years he had learned to read a crowd the way he once read ward-lines: by its tensions, its knots, the places where pressure gathered and waited for an excuse. The crowd packing Saltwife Square that morning was knotted tight as a snare-line. They weren't there for herring.

They were there because the stars hadn't come back.

Four nights now. Four nights of black sky, of lamps burned past midnight because no one could sleep under a heaven with nothing in it, of priests on every corner declaring it either punishment or omen depending on which god paid their stipend. And beneath all that, quieter and more dangerous, the question nobody said aloud: *who did this?* Because things like this didn't simply happen. Things like this were done. And when people needed someone to have done a thing, they did not look to kings or mountains. They looked next door.

He saw the charm-seller before he understood what he was seeing.

Old Maren — Ilex knew her vaguely, the way everyone in Greyharbor's lower quarters knew everyone vaguely; she'd sold luck-knots and fever-charms from a blanket near the fish stalls for as long as he'd lived in the city, a small round woman with ink-stained fingers and a voice like a gull's cry. She wasn't at her blanket now. She was at the base of the old mooring post in the square's center, and three men had her arms, and a fourth was looping rope over the iron hook where fishing crews once hoisted whole sharks.

"No," Ilex said, though he was forty feet away and no one heard him. "No, no—"

Someone had painted a white circle on the cobbles around her. He recognized the geometry even from here, even half-scuffed: a containment ring, ward-work, the kind he himself had drawn a thousand times. Except this one was wrong — inverted, its lines crossed where they should run clean, a ward turned inside out into something meant not to hold power in but to mark what stood inside it as unclean. Someone had taken the grammar of protection and taught it to mean *burn.*

A woman on a crate was speaking. She wore the grey sash of the Harborwardens' auxiliary, the ones who swept streets and broke up brawls, and her voice carried the flat certainty of someone reciting words she had decided in advance never to examine.

"—the wards fell because they were *fed upon,*" she called. "Fed upon by those among us who drink what isn't theirs! You've seen them. The charm-peddlers. The knot-sellers. The ones who take your coin and your troubles and give back *nothing you can see.* The stars went out because our sky has been leeched, neighbors, and every leech in Greyharbor is known to us by name—"

Maren was saying something. Her voice was thin against the crowd's noise, and Ilex pushed forward through bodies that smelled of smoke and herring and fear, elbowing, muttering apologies he didn't mean. He got close enough to hear her.

"I cured your daughter's croup," she was saying to the man holding her left arm, and her voice wasn't pleading, which somehow made it worse. It was bewildered. "Tam. Tam, I sat up three nights with your daughter. You brought me a hen as payment. You *wept* on my floor."

Tam looked at the cobbles. His jaw worked. "That was before," he said. "Before the sky went wrong."

"That was four days ago!"

"Sky's been going wrong longer than four days," someone shouted from the crowd, and there was laughter — ugly, high, the laughter of people who have found a permission they intend to use. "Just ask the witches!"

*I helped build those wards,* Ilex thought, and the thought arrived with its familiar companion, the one that had lived behind his sternum since the night the border lights died: *and I never asked what they were built from.*

He reached the white circle's edge just as two men tipped a barrel of lamp-oil across Maren's skirts.

Later — much later, in the years after, when he tried to reconstruct that hour — Ilex could never say why he stepped over the line. Cowardice would be the honest answer, if cowardice ran in that direction. It would have been easier to stand outside the ring and shout with the rest of them, easier still to go home and drink until the screaming stopped. What he did instead was step into the circle, put his body between Maren and the torch, and hear the crowd's noise change key.

"Stand aside, keeper," said the woman with the sash, and the word *keeper* came out of her mouth like something scraped off a boot.

"The wards fell from the inside," Ilex said. Loudly. Clearly. Using the voice he'd once used in the Wardens' Hall, the one trained to carry through stone corridors. "From the mountain court. I've held fragments of the border-stone in my hands and the break-marks face inward. Whatever drained your sky, it's a hundred leagues east and wearing a crown. Burning a charm-woman won't bring back one star."

"You'd say that." Spat, from somewhere. "You're one of them."

"I'm a *disgraced* one," Ilex said, "which means I'm the only one telling you the truth, because I've got nothing left to protect."

It almost worked. That was the terrible thing he would remember — it *almost* worked. He saw doubt move through the front rows like wind through barley, saw Tam's grip loosen on Maren's arm, saw the woman with the sash open her mouth to answer him with something that might have been sense.

Then someone threw the torch anyway.

Not at Maren. At the oil-soaked cobbles beside her, where the spill had crept beyond the white circle, and flame went up in a blue-gold sheet, and the crowd made a sound Ilex had only ever heard once before — at a shipwreck, years ago, watching from the cliffs as a hull broke and the sea took everything — a sound that was not cheering and not horror but some fusion of the two, the sound of people witnessing something enormous and refusing, collectively, to stop it.

Maren screamed. The men holding her let go — whether from conscience or simple startlement, Ilex never learned — and she came up burning, and Ilex caught her, and the heat of her went through his coat like a hand.

He got her down. He rolled her. He beat the flames out with his own sleeves, with his bare hands, feeling skin blister, and when the fire was out she was looking up at him with eyes already going somewhere else, and she said — clearly, almost conversationally, the way people say the truest thing they'll ever say —

"They'll come for the quiet ones next."

Then she said nothing again, ever.

* * *

They let him carry her out. That was the strangest part. Once the fire was spent and the woman lay charred and still in his arms, the crowd parted the way water parts around a stone, and no one raised a hand against him, and the woman with the sash looked at him with an expression he would spend many sleepless nights trying to name and finally settled on *embarrassment* — the embarrassment of a mob confronted with the physical evidence of what it had done, wanting it removed from view.

He carried her to the harbor steps and washed her face with seawater, uselessly, gently, and then he sat with her while gulls argued overhead, and he did the arithmetic he had been avoiding for four days.

*They'll come for the quiet ones next.*

Greyharbor had perhaps two hundred practicing charm-workers. Most were frauds selling knotted string and confidence. Some were hedge-talents who could warm a room or find a lost ring. And one — he had seen it once, years ago, briefly, in a moment he'd convinced himself afterward he'd imagined — one was something else entirely. One had a hum under their skin like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing, and had spent three years folding that sound smaller and smaller, hiding it the way a candle hides its flame inside a storm-lantern.

Zephyrine Ravensworth.

He hadn't spoken to them in eleven years. Not since the inquiry. Not since he'd stood in the Wardens' Hall and given testimony — careful, hedged, technically honest testimony — while they stripped Ravensworth's rank and name and wrote *oath-breaker* in the margin of a record that would follow them forever. He had told himself a hundred versions of that day. In the kindest version, he'd had no choice. In the most honest version, he'd been afraid, and he'd let a colleague hang so that his own ledger stayed clean, and he had built a career on top of that foundation and called it a life.

And now the city was lighting torches, and the last swordspeaker in the world was sitting in a charm-shop above a chandler's on Rope Walk, pretending to be dead.

Ilex stood up. His hands were bandaged with strips torn from his own shirt, clumsy, already weeping. Maren lay quiet on the steps. He pulled his coat off and covered her with it, because no one else was going to.

Then he walked toward Rope Walk as fast as his legs would go.

* * *

The shop looked exactly as it had the one time he'd passed it, months ago, and told himself he was merely curious about property values: narrow-fronted, salt-stained shutters, a hand-lettered sign reading CHARMS & KNOTS, LUCK SOLD FAIR. Through the window, shelves of small bright nonsense. A person moving behind the counter with the deliberate unhurried motion of someone who had rehearsed being ordinary until the rehearsal became real.

Ilex knocked. Then, when nothing happened, he knocked harder, and said through the glass, in the old formal cadence of the Wardens' Hall, words he was certain no one had used in years:

"By salt and star and standing stone. Open, swordspeaker. We need to talk before the mob does."

The figure behind the counter went very still.

Long seconds. Long enough that he counted his own heartbeats and lost track twice. Then bolts slid — one, two, three — and the door opened a hand's width, and Zephyrine Ravensworth looked out at him through the gap.

They had not aged well, or rather they had aged in the specific way of people performing invisibility: shoulders drawn in, hair colorless, eyes that flicked past his shoulder to scan the street before settling on his face with an expression composed of equal parts recognition and loathing.

"Warden," they said. The word had edges.

"Former."

"Former things burn slower." But they opened the door, because they had seen his hands, or his face, or whatever it was that people see when they decide to let trouble in rather than leave it freezing on the step. "You have five minutes."

Inside smelled of dried herbs and tallow and, underneath, faintly, of ozone — the smell of held-back lightning, though Ilex doubted anyone without ward-training would ever notice it. Zephyrine barred the door behind him and turned, arms folded, waiting.

"Maren's dead," Ilex said. No preamble. There was no gentle way to build to it, and gentleness would have been a lie about what kind of errand this was. "Fish market, this morning. They burned her in a containment ring drawn backwards. Two hundred people watched and the only crime anyone was punished for was mine — I stepped in the way and got these." He held up his bandaged hands. "She was alive when I carried her out. Barely. She said—" His throat closed unexpectedly. He made himself finish. "She said they'll come for the quiet ones next."

Zephyrine had gone very pale. Not theatrically; Ilex had seen enough death delivered to know the genuine article. Their hands, folded on the counter, had gone white at the knuckles.

"Maren," they repeated. "The luck-knot woman. She bought net-thread from me. Every spring." A pause. "Was it fast?"

"No."

Zephyrine closed their eyes. When they opened them, something had rearranged itself behind them — grief packed away with the swift brutal efficiency of someone who has practiced having no room for it. "Say the rest. You didn't cross eleven years of silence to deliver an obituary."

"The border wards were broken from inside the mountain court. I have the stone to prove it. Vane's court is feeding on something — souls, if the refugee stories hold any truth — and the same hunger that dropped the stars will not stop at borders. And this city—" Ilex laughed, and hated the sound. "This city has decided the fault lies with anyone who can light a candle with a word. There are Warden auxiliaries drawing lists. Names. Charm-sellers first, obviously, they're visible. But lists grow, Ravensworth. Lists always grow downward. And do you know what's on the bottom of every list in this city, if anyone ever gets that far?"

"Don't say my name."

"It's already on three lists I know of." He said it flatly, because there was no other way to say it. "Your purchase records. Your landlord talks. The child on Rope Walk who watches your shutters and counts the nights your lamp burns past midnight. You have hidden beautifully for three years, and hiding works right up until the moment someone decides to look, and tonight — tonight they are *looking.*"

Silence. From the street came a distant sound — shouting, a bell, the ordinary noises of a city sliding toward something extraordinary — and Zephyrine listened to it with their head tilted, like a sailor reading weather.

"You want something," they said finally. "You don't warn people out of decency, Warden. I remember how you operate. So name it."

The old shame rose in him like floodwater, and he let it, because drowning in it was the only honest option left. "I want to help you get out of Greyharbor. Tonight. And then I want to come with you."

Zephyrine stared. For the first time, the performance cracked — surprise plain on their face, naked and human. "Come with me *where?*"

"East. To the mountain." He pulled the fragment of ward-stone from his coat — he'd carried it for days like a coal in his pocket — and set it on the counter between them. Its broken faces caught the lamplight. "Because I helped raise those wards, Ravensworth. Twenty-two years ago. I drew half the anchor-lines myself, and I never asked what fed them, because asking questions was bad for advancement. Everything that's falling apart out there — the stars, the borders, Maren on those steps — it grew from seeds I planted. I am not offering to help you out of decency. I'm offering because I owe a debt the size of a mountain, and the mountain is where I intend to pay it."

Zephyrine looked at the stone a long time. Something moved across their face — not softening, exactly. Recalculation. The look of a chess player realizing an enemy piece has offered itself for sacrifice.

"If Vane learns my true name," they said quietly, "he owns my sword. You know that. Everyone who knew me before knows that. Helping me is helping whoever holds the throne, the moment they speak three syllables."

"Then we make sure he doesn't learn it."

"And if he already has?"

Ilex had no answer. He let the silence hold it, because a false comfort would have been worse than none.

Zephyrine exhaled slowly. Outside, the bell was ringing faster now, and there were voices in the street below — several, moving, purposeful. Ilex felt the hair rise on his neck. He had spent nineteen years learning the difference between a crowd passing and a crowd hunting, and this was the second kind.

"They're checking shops on Rope Walk," Zephyrine said. Flat. Already certain. "Auxiliaries with lists. Aren't they."

"Almost certainly."

Another silence, shorter. Then Zephyrine moved — and it was the first time in three years, Ilex realized, watching them, that he had seen them move like themselves. Fast. Certain. Hands pulling a floorboard, retrieving a wrapped bundle that clinked with the particular music of steel. They slung it across their back without ceremony, and when they turned, the invisible person was gone and something older stood in their place, tired and furious and awake.

"One condition," Zephyrine said. "We don't travel as allies. We travel as two people whose debts happen to point the same direction. The day your debt is paid, Warden, we part ways, and you will not look back."

"Agreed," Ilex said, meaning none of the relief he felt, or all of it, he couldn't tell.

Zephyrine blew out the lamp. In the sudden dark, their voice came low and close:

"Back door. Stay behind me. And Ilex—" The use of his first name landed like a blow; he couldn't remember the last time anyone had used it. "If we die on the road, I want you to know I never forgave you. Understand? Whatever else happens. Never."

"Yes," Ilex said into the darkness. "I know. Come on."

Below the window, torchlight bloomed against the shutters, and fists began hammering on the shop's front door.
