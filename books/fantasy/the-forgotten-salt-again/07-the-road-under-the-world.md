## 7. The Road Under the World

The entrance to the mines was a wound in the hillside north of Greyharbor, half-swallowed by gorse and rusted iron. A sign still hung there, crooked on one nail: *GREYHARBOR SALT WORKS — CLOSED BY ORDER OF THE WARDENS' GUILD*. Beneath it, someone had scratched a newer message in charcoal: *DON'T GO DOWN. THE DARK IS FULL.*

"The dark is always full," Ilex said, shouldering his pack of ward-tools and tallow candles. "That's what dark is."

Zephyrine didn't answer. They were watching the girl-shaped shimmer standing at the mouth of the tunnel, her bare feet on ground that hadn't held living weight in years.

"You're sure about this," they said—not to Ilex.

Sable turned. In the grey daylight she looked like breath on glass, like something the world had almost finished forgetting. "I died down there," she said. "Which means I know every passage better than anyone who ever walked out of them." Her smile was thin and bright as a knife's edge. "Besides. You want to reach the mountain without crossing a single road where Vane's riders can see you. There is exactly one road like that left in the world, and it runs under everything."

"And you remember it because—"

"Because I spent my last day being marched through it." She said it lightly, the way people say things they have decided will not hurt them anymore. "Come on. The dark's not so bad once you introduce yourself."

Zephyrine took one last look at Greyharbor below—the smoke rising straight from a hundred chimneys, the harbor glinting dull silver, the fish market already crowded with morning buyers who had no idea what had been done to the sky. Three years of smallness. Three years of luck-knots and lowered eyes and magic kept folded up small enough to hide in a lie.

They had burned a witch in that market two nights ago. An old man who sold fever-charms. Zephyrine had watched from the crowd with their hood up and their hands shaking, and had understood, finally and completely, that hiding was over. The city had chosen its fear. The only question left was which direction to run.

Down, then. Down and east, under the world, toward the mountain where their mentor's soul sat bottled in the dark.

"Keep your hand on the wall," Ilex said behind them. "Old mine law. If the dark takes your eyes, let it keep your fingers instead."

Zephyrine stepped into the earth.

* * *

For the first hour there was still light—thin, greenish, leaking down through ventilation shafts cut by men three generations dead. Then even that failed, and the world contracted to the circle of candleflame: Ilex's stub of tallow guttering in his fist, the walls sweating brine, the ceiling low enough that Zephyrine stooped and Ilex, taller, bent nearly double.

The salt did strange things to sound. Their footsteps came back to them wrong—too slow, too many, as if other feet followed at a courteous distance. The walls glittered when the flame caught them, whole seams of crystal flashing like frost on a window, and more than once Zephyrine caught themselves staring into that glitter and losing count of the seconds, minutes, whole swallowed stretches of time.

"Don't look too long at the pretty parts," Sable said, drifting ahead. "Salt remembers light. It'll show you yours back if you let it."

"What does that mean?"

"It means my grandmother got lost for two days staring at a wall she swore was showing her her wedding." Sable glanced back, and her translucence caught the candlelight and threw it, briefly, into color—a flash of brown hair, a chipped front tooth, a freckle. Then it was gone, and she was breath on glass again. "This way. Left at the flooded shaft."

"How do you know it's flooded?" Ilex asked. His voice was carefully neutral. He had been careful with her since the shop, since the night she'd appeared out of nothing and started rearranging their lives. Careful the way a man is careful around a fire he both needs and fears.

"Because I drowned in it."

He stopped walking. "You—"

"Not all the way." She was already moving on, her voice floating back through the dark, bright and terrible. "Mostly. It's waist-deep if the rains haven't been bad. It wasn't bad, the week they brought us through. Waist-deep and cold enough to stop your heart if you stayed in too long, but the guards made us cross anyway because the dry passage had collapsed and they didn't want to lose the toll-time." A pause. "Fourteen of us went in. Eleven came out. I was one of the ones who came out, if you're wondering. I got the other way later."

Zephyrine stood in the black with the candle trembling in their hand and felt grief move through the tunnel like weather.

"I'm sorry," they said. It was inadequate. It was the only thing there was.

"Don't be sorry yet," Sable said. "Save it. You'll need it further in." And she pointed, with a finger that cast no shadow, toward a gap in the rock barely wide enough for a body. "There. The crawl. Forty feet, then it opens up. Keep your chin down and don't think about how much stone is above you."

"That instruction," Ilex muttered, wedging himself into the crack, "is deeply unhelpful."

"It's honest, though. That's more than most instructions manage."

* * *

They crossed the flooded gallery at dawn-that-was-not-dawn, some unknowable hour measured only by the ache in Zephyrine's knees. The water was exactly as cold as Sable had promised—cold that climbed up through the boots and closed around the spine like a hand. Ilex held the candle above his head in one fist, teeth gritted, ward-chalk from his pack dissolving in his pocket. Halfway across, his foot found a submerged rail and he pitched forward; Zephyrine caught his collar and hauled him upright, and for a moment they stood chest-deep in the dark holding each other's weight, breathing hard, saying nothing.

"My thanks," Ilex managed.

"Don't mention it. Ever."

"I intend to mention it constantly. It's the only embarrassing thing I've seen you do, and I plan to hoard it."

Despite everything—despite the drowned girl waiting patiently on the far shore, despite the mountain and the king and the bottled souls—Zephyrine laughed. It came out cracked and rusty, a hinge unused for three years, but it was a laugh.

Sable watched them from the bank, and something moved across her face that was too complicated to be a smile.

* * *

Past the water, the mine changed. The workings grew older—the timber supports rougher, hand-hewn rather than sawn, and then no timber at all, just pillars of uncut salt left standing in rooms vast enough that the candlelight couldn't find their ceilings. The air tasted different here. Older. Under the brine there was something else, something mineral and deep, like the smell of a struck flint, like blood in the nose.

"This isn't the salt works anymore," Ilex said quietly. He'd stopped consulting his maps an hour ago; the Guild charts ended at the works boundary, drawn in confident ink over territory no Guild surveyor had ever entered. "These galleries predate the charter. Look at the tool marks." He ran his thumb along a wall. "Chisel-and-fire. This is First Settlement work. Maybe older."

"The road under the world," Zephyrine murmured. Every child in the free cities knew the phrase as a story—a fable about the old roads that ran beneath the mountains, made before the kings, before the wards, when the world was supposedly stitched together by people who trusted each other. Nobody believed the roads were real.

"Somebody used it recently, though." Ilex crouched. His candle found bootprints pressed into a crust of salt on the floor—many boots, unshod feet, and drag-marks between them. He didn't say what the drag-marks meant. He didn't have to. Sable had already told them what the guards had marched through here, and what they'd marched.

"They were taking people to the court," Zephyrine said. "Before the wards fell. Before any of this. It was happening all along, right underneath—" They stopped. Pressed their palm flat against the cold salt wall. "Under everything. Under Greyharbor. Under my shop."

"Yes," Sable said simply.

"Why didn't anyone know?"

"Everyone knew." For the first time since the shop, her voice lost its brightness. "That's the worst part, Zeph. Everyone knew, the way you know the sea is full of fish. It never became real until it was your fish. Until it was your sister walking into the mountain and not walking out." She drifted ahead, into the next gallery. "Come on. We're close to the place I need to show you."

"Close to what?"

She didn't answer, and the dark answered for her, in the voice it always had: *keep walking.*

* * *

The place Sable needed to show them was a chamber where the road forked—or where it had forked, once. Now the left-hand passage was sealed by a collapse, tons of fallen salt-rock packed tight as mortar, and the right-hand passage ran narrow and steep downward, breathing cold air against their faces like something inhaling.

Sable stood before the collapse. Even translucent, even in candlelight, she somehow looked lit from within—and the light she stood in was not kind.

"This is where I need to tell you something," she said, "and I need you to hear all of it before you ask me anything."

Ilex set the candle on a ledge. Neither of them spoke.

"There were twenty-two of us in the last group they marched through here. From the villages east of the pass. I told you I sold my soul to buy medicine—that part's true, that happened first, in a tent with a man whose scales weighed things that weren't gold. But selling it and delivering it are different transactions." Her hands opened and closed at her sides, grasping at air that couldn't hold her. "They walked us down this road for two days. And at this fork, the ones who'd sold willingly went right, down toward the court. And there was another passage, left, going up—toward daylight, toward the pass, toward anywhere. It wasn't guarded. It didn't need to be. Because the guards told us: anyone who runs gets caught in the collapse they've rigged, or gets found, and when they get found, the price of their soul doubles, and the difference comes out of the village."

She looked at the sealed passage the way people look at graves.

"I knew that. I knew all of it. And I stood right here, in this exact spot, and I told the others to run anyway. All of them. I told them a life was worth more than a debt, that Vane's men wouldn't dare punish whole villages, that I'd stay behind and take the blame." Her voice didn't break. That was the most terrible thing about it—it didn't break anywhere. "Eleven ran. The collapse killed six. The guards caught five, and they did exactly what they said they'd do, and my village paid, and the medicine I bought bought nothing, because everyone who could have taken it was dead or chained inside a month."

The candleflame leaned, though there was no draft.

"So when I guide you," Sable said, turning to face them at last, "understand what I am. I am not your innocent ghost. I am not your tragic little sister spirit. I am a girl who tried to be noble and buried eleven people under that rock, and I have spent whatever I am now learning every inch of these tunnels so that the next person who stands at this fork goes the right way." She pointed down the narrow passage, the cold one, the breathing one. "Down. Always down. Toward the court. Because the only way to fix what's under the mountain is to go under the mountain, and anyone who tells you otherwise—including me, including you, including the sweetest voice you've ever heard—is lying."

Silence filled the chamber like water filling a hull.

"You were going to tell us to go up," Zephyrine said slowly. "Weren't you. When we first met. You had a speech ready."

"I had several speeches ready." Sable's grin came back, crooked and wet-eyed and human. "You were supposed to be harder to love than this. It would have made the speeches work better."

Ilex picked up the candle. "Then we go down," he said, and his voice was rougher than usual. "And when this is over—if anything of you is left over—we'll carve your name somewhere it isn't a grave."

"No names," Sable said quickly, and for a flicker she looked frightened, genuinely frightened, the first fear Zephyrine had ever seen on her. "Not mine. Not written anywhere. Promise me. Names are—" She stopped. Swallowed a breath she didn't need. "Names are currency down here. Promise me."

"We promise," Zephyrine said, though they didn't understand, and the not-understanding sat in their chest like a seed.

* * *

They walked down through the dark for what the candles said was a day and what their bodies insisted was longer. They slept in shifts against warm-ish salt, ate hard bread and salt-pork that needed no curing in that air, and drank from condensation Sable showed them how to coax from the walls with cloth and patience. Twice the tunnel echoed with distant sounds—once a rhythmic knocking that might have been water and might not, and once, horribly, singing: a thread of melody threading up through the stone from somewhere far below, wordless, patient, in a minor key that made the fillings of Zephyrine's teeth ache.

"That's the court," Sable said, when it faded. "Or the edge of it. The souls sing when they're being counted."

"Counted?"

"Ledgers, Zeph. Everything down there is ledgers." She said it lightly again, that knife-edge brightness, but her eyes were on the dark below. "You'll see."

On the second day—Ilex's counting, marked in chalk ticks on his wrist—they emerged onto a ledge, and the candlelight fell away beneath them into a glow that needed no candle at all.

Far below, down a shaft so wide it might have been a swallowed cathedral, the darkness itself was faintly luminous. Thousands of points of soft white-gold light drifted in the deep like plankton in a night sea, rising and falling in slow tides. And threaded among them, faint as veins in marble, were structures—walls, terraces, bridges—architecture built in the light of the things it imprisoned.

Zephyrine gripped the ledge. The hum that lived under their skin, the magic they had spent three years smothering, rose up unbidden and sang toward that light like a dog hearing its name.

"There it is," Sable whispered beside them. Not triumphant. Not afraid. Just true. "The court beneath. Home of every soul King Aldous Vane ever bought." She looked at Zephyrine, and in the glow from below her face almost seemed solid, almost seemed alive. "One of them is your teacher. One of them, if the ledger's honest, has your name written next to it in a column called *paid*."

Below them, far down in the shining dark, the singing changed key.

And up the shaft, drifting down past them like ash, came a scrap of paper—a herald's proclamation, printed in a thousand copies, scattered along every road in the kingdom. Ilex caught it against the rock and held the candle to it, and read aloud, and his voice went flat as he read:

*"By order of Aldous Vane, Fifth of That Name, rightful King: the stars shall return when the last traitor kneels. Amnesty and reward to any who deliver Zephyrine Ravensworth, oath-breaker, alive."*

The name rang off the salt walls. Once. Twice.

And somewhere in Zephyrine's blood, in the oath older than memory, something heard it—and began, very softly, to pull.
