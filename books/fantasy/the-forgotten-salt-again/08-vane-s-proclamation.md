## 8. Vane's Proclamation

The herald's name was Corvess Dune, and he had learned long ago that a proclamation was not read—it was performed.

He stood now on the scaffold built overnight in the market square of Marrowgate, the third city in nine days, and waited for the trumpets to finish their long silver argument. Below him, the crowd had been arranged rather than gathered: wardens at the corners, soldiers in loose ranks behind the common folk so that no one could drift away without being seen drifting. The square smelled of wet wool and woodsmoke and, faintly, of fear, which Corvess had come to think of as the true cologne of the new reign.

Above him, where the sky should have been, there was nothing.

Nine days since the stars went out, and the dark had not softened. It was not cloud-cover; clouds moved, thinned, showed their grey bellies to the moon that no longer existed. This was a clean black, a swept black, like the inside of a closed box. People had stopped looking up after the third day. That was the thing Corvess noticed most—the way an entire city learned, in less than a week, to keep its eyes down.

*Good*, he thought, and hated himself for thinking it, and thought it again anyway.

The trumpets died. He lifted the scroll.

"By grace of the crown and by right of conquest," he began, and his voice carried the way it always did, trained across forty years of squares and scaffolds, pitched to reach the back rows without ever seeming to shout, "King Aldous Vane, First of His Name in the New Counting, Sovereign of the Mountain Throne, Protector of the Nine Provinces, declares before all people these truths."

He let the titles sit. Titles were load-bearing. Say them slowly enough and they seemed like architecture instead of assertion.

"First: the night without stars was not a punishment sent from beyond. It was a theft committed from within." A murmur ran through the crowd, the sound he wanted, the sound of a hundred private fears being given one public shape. "Second: the wards that guarded our borders for three hundred years did not fail. They were unmade—deliberately, treacherously, by hands that ate their own country's safety and called it freedom."

In the front row, a woman held a child too tightly. Corvess saw her knuckles whiten around the small shoulders and looked elsewhere. There was a discipline to this work: you learned which faces to hold and which to release, the way a swordspeaker—no. He corrected himself. The way a fisherman learns which lines to pull.

"Third," he said, "the King has taken steps. The King has *always* taken steps. While the free cities slept behind wards they did not pay for, Aldous Vane kept watch. While the old order counted its privileges, the King counted its debts. And the debt is called by its name tonight."

He unfurled the second scroll, the long one, the one with the seal heavy enough to swing like a censer when he walked. Wax the color of dried blood, stamped with the mountain and the five-pointed star that was no longer in any sky.

"The traitor who broke the wards has a name. The King knows it. The King has known it for some time." Here he paused—one beat, two—and let the silence do what silence does in a frightened square, which is everything. "But the King is merciful. The King extends his hand even to those who have earned only the edge of it. Hear the terms of amnesty:

"To any soul who delivers the last oath-traitor—Zephyrine Ravensworth, once sworn to the crown, now fled and hiding among honest folk—the King offers full pardon for all crimes past, present, and future. Land in the reclaimed provinces. A house in the capital. A place at court, if the service warrants it.

"To any soul who shelters this traitor—who feeds them, houses them, sells them so much as a luck-knot—the law will assume the traitor's guilt entire. The wards fell because of Ravensworth. Let every door that opens to Ravensworth understand what it opens onto."

He rolled the scroll shut with a snap that echoed off the shuttered shopfronts.

"And when the last traitor kneels," he said, and here his voice changed—not louder but lower, dropping into the register he saved for the endings, the part every crowd remembered whether they wished to or not—"when the last traitor kneels upon the mountain and confesses before the court, the sky will be returned to you. Star by star. As it was written. As it was promised. The King does not ask your faith. He asks only your patience, and your vigilance, and your eyes."

He raised one arm and pointed, deliberately, upward, into the blank.

Every head in the square followed his finger. For three heartbeats, four thousand people stared together into the nothing above Marrowgate, and Corvess felt the moment land the way it had landed in six other squares: a collective flinch, a shudder passed shoulder to shoulder like a current through a wire fence. They looked up because he told them to. They saw nothing because there was nothing to see. And they would go home tonight and tell their families *the herald pointed at the empty sky and said his king would give it back*, and the telling would make it truer than the hearing had.

Power, Corvess had learned, did not need to be real. It needed only to be *performed convincingly in front of witnesses who were too afraid to applaud wrong.*

"Long live King Aldous Vane," he said. "Long live the Mountain Throne. Long may the light return."

The soldiers' cheer was drilled and adequate. The crowd's echo came late and thin, the way a congregation answers a priest it no longer believes but still fears. Somewhere near the back, someone coughed, and the cough sounded almost like a laugh, and three wardens turned toward the sound at once, and the coughing stopped.

* * *

Afterward, in the warden-house that served as his lodging, Corvess sat with the courier's satchel open on his knees and did not touch the wine they'd brought him.

There were other documents. There were always other documents. The public scroll was theater; the satchel was administration. He turned the papers by lamplight with the care of a man handling things that bit.

The first was a list. Names, hundreds of them, in the tight clerical hand of the Office of Reclamation: citizens of Marrowgate flagged for assessment. Not arrest—assessment. The distinction mattered to the men who wrote such lists. Beside each name, a column of figures. Corvess had stopped pretending, around the fourth city, that he didn't understand what the figures meant. Souls had weight. Souls had value. The court beneath the mountain ran on them the way a mill ran on water, and the Office of Reclamation was the sluice-gate. *Assessment* meant: how much is this person worth, and can they be spared?

The second document was thinner, and stranger. A standing order, sealed directly by the King's own chancery, bypassing the Office entirely. It authorized purchase. *True names*, freely surrendered, to be bought at fixed rates from any bearer willing to sell—paid in coin, in grain, in pardons, in whatever currency the seller was starving for. The order listed collection points along the river roads. One of them was barely half a day's ride south of where he sat.

Corvess had asked about it once, in the second city, of a chancery clerk with ink-stained cuffs and careful eyes. *Why buy what we can take?*

The clerk had looked at him for a long moment, as though deciding how much truth a herald could safely carry.

"Because a taken name resists," the clerk had said. "A sold name doesn't. You know how a lock works, herald? A broken lock is just a broken lock. But a lock that's been *opened*—a lock opened from the inside, with its own key—that lock remembers being open. It wants to be open again." The clerk had smiled then, briefly, without warmth. "His Majesty prefers doors that want to open."

Corvess hadn't asked anything else. Some knowledge was a stone in the boot: better to walk on it than stop and take the boot off, because taking the boot off meant looking at it.

Now he weighed the standing order in his hand and thought about the river roads, and the collection points, and the fixed rates. Someone was doing that work already. Someone downstream—or upstream—was moving along the waterways with a strongbox and a ledger, trading people their own names for bread. And every name purchased was a thread pulled out of the weave of some town or village, a small hole that wouldn't show until the whole cloth shifted.

He thought, too—he couldn't help it—about the name on the public scroll. *Ravensworth.* He had spoken it aloud nine times now, in nine squares, and each time he had felt something faint and unpleasant pass through the word, like a draft under a door. A herald's trade was words; he knew when a word was merely heavy and when it was *loaded*. Ravensworth was loaded. Somewhere in the crowd today, he'd have sworn, someone had flinched at the name not with fear but with recognition—and had hidden it well, hidden it the way you hide a coal in your fist.

The King wanted the last swordspeaker kneeling on the mountain. The King wanted it badly enough to promise the sky back for it, which told Corvess something the proclamations never said outright: the sky was not coming back on its own. Whatever fed the dark above them, the King did not control it. The King was *negotiating* with it. And negotiations, in Corvess's long experience of watching powerful men perform certainty, were conducted from weakness dressed as strength.

He closed the satchel. He drank the wine after all, because a man had to sleep, and the wine helped, and tomorrow they rode south along the river road toward the coast, toward the salt-fogged last of the free cities that still pretended the old world existed.

Greyharbor, the maps called it. The clerks called it something else in the margins of the assessment lists: *high density of unregistered practitioners; priority for collection.*

Corvess blew out the lamp and lay in the dark of the warden-house, listening to the garrison change watch, and found himself thinking—not for the first time, and he suspected not for the last—of the woman in the front row with her arms around her child, holding on as though the sky itself might take the boy if she loosened her grip.

*Deliver the traitor,* the scroll said. *Shelter them, and share their guilt.*

He wondered, as sleep took him, how many doors in Greyharbor had already opened to a fugitive with kind eyes and a dead magic humming under their skin—and how many neighbors were already counting the price of a pardon against the price of a lie.

South along the river, meanwhile, in the dark between the lamplight and the dawn, a ferry waited at its crossing, and something old in the water below it was keeping accounts of its own.
