## 9. The Toll of Names

The river found them before they saw it.

They had been walking since the grey hour before dawn, following Sable's drifting shape through a country that had begun to feel less like land and more like an argument between land and water—marsh grass, drowned orchards, the black ribs of a boat someone had abandoned years ago and never come back for. Then the ground simply gave up pretending to be solid, and there it was: the Vell, wide as a lake deciding to be a river, moving slow and brown-green and patient under a sky still scabbed with cloud.

No stars had come back. Three nights now. Zephyrine had stopped looking up, mostly, because every time they did, the absence hit them fresh, like opening a door expecting a room and finding a wall.

"There," Ilex said. He pointed with his chin, hands jammed in his coat pockets the way he always kept them, as if his fingers might otherwise do something honest. "Crossing."

It was not a bridge. It was not even properly a dock. A single flat barge sat moored to a post of black wood at the end of a plank walkway, and standing on the barge was a figure that resolved slowly out of the river-mist the way things do when your eyes would rather not see them.

Tall. Too tall. Wrapped in something that might have been robes or might have been weed, dripping without dripping, its face a smooth grey oval with two hollows where eyes should have been—and in those hollows, faintly, a light like the last coin at the bottom of a well.

"A wight," Zephyrine said quietly.

"River-toll kind." Ilex's voice had gone flat, careful, the voice of a man reading fine print. "Old law. Older than the crown, older than the wards. They don't take coin."

"I know what they take." Zephyrine's hand drifted to the strap of their pack, adjusting nothing. "I've crossed the Vell before."

"When?"

The question landed in the space between them and sat there. Zephyrine looked at the water. *I don't remember* was becoming the most-used sentence in their mouth, and each time it came out it cost a little more.

"I don't remember," they said anyway, because lying to Ilex was a door they weren't going to open. Not after the fish market. Not after he'd stood in the crowd of smoke and shouting and pulled them bodily out of it, growling *not this one, this one's mine to arrest* with such conviction that two Warden irregulars had actually believed him.

Sable drifted ahead along the plank walkway, her feet not quite touching the boards. She'd grown more solid in the days since the mines—or perhaps Zephyrine had grown better at seeing her. She paused at the edge of the water and looked back, and her expression was one Zephyrine was learning to dread: the look of someone who knows how the story goes and cannot say.

"It'll ask," she said. "It always asks. But listen—it doesn't just want the name. Listen to what else it says."

"What else would it say?"

Sable didn't answer. She stepped aside, into the reeds, and became a shimmer among shimmers, and the mist closed over her like a page turning.

Ilex exhaled through his nose. "Your ghost gives riddles now."

"She's not my ghost."

"You talk to her more than you talk to me."

"That's because you're annoying."

Something almost like a smile touched the corner of his mouth and died there. He looked out at the river, and the smile's ghost left his face entirely. "Three years ago you could have crossed this water laughing. Now you flinch at a toll-keeper." He said it without cruelty, which was worse. "Come on. Daylight's burning, and daylight is the only thing we have plenty of."

* * *

The planks groaned under their boots. Up close, the wight was worse than tall; it was *long*, as if someone had taken a man and drawn him downward like taffy, and its stillness had a quality Zephyrine recognized from deep caves and deep wells—the stillness of a thing that has never once needed to hurry.

It turned its head. The well-light in its eye-hollows brightened.

"Travelers," it said. Its voice came from everywhere the water went. "The toll is a true name. Spoken freely. Held no longer."

"We know the law," Ilex said.

"The law knows you." The wight's head tilted, slow as a crane swinging. "Ward-keeper. Third circle. Struck from the rolls in the spring of—" It stopped. The light in its hollows flickered, and for a moment Zephyrine could have sworn the thing looked *confused*. "Struck from the rolls," it repeated. "The rolls are gone. The rolls were on the border wards. The wards are broken glass." It said this without inflection, the way a clerk reads a casualty list. "How strange. How very strange. Everything I knew how to check is gone."

Zephyrine's skin prickled. Under it, deeper, the old hum stirred—the magic they kept pressed down like a hand over a singing bowl. The wight's hollows swung toward them, and the hum *answered*, a faint sympathetic note, and Zephyrine clamped down on it so hard their teeth ached.

"And you," the wight said softly. "Oath-bound. Swordspeaker. Last of—" Again the flicker, the clerk losing his place. "Last of the line. Your name is in me somewhere. Old crossings. You paid once before, I think. Or you refused once before. The memory is—" It raised one long hand and pressed it against the side of its own face, a gesture so human it was obscene. "There are holes in me. Someone has been fishing in this river with a very large net."

"Fishing for what?" Zephyrine asked.

"Names." The word dropped into the water between them and sank. "Names are the small coin of souls. Did you know that? A soul is a purse. A name is what buys the purse open." The wight's hollows dimmed, then brightened, then dimmed again, like a lamp guttering. "Someone upstream has been buying in bulk."

Ilex shifted beside Zephyrine. "Bulk," he repeated. "Meaning what? How many?"

The wight regarded him. "The Vell runs four hundred miles to the mountain's roots," it said. "Every crossing on it is mine or mine's kin. We keep accounts older than kingdoms. In three years, the accounts upstream have changed by—" It paused, and Zephyrine understood suddenly that the pause wasn't hesitation. It was arithmetic. Enormous, terrible arithmetic being done behind that smooth grey face. "By eleven thousand names. Purchased. Not taken—purchased. Freely sold, most of them. Some traded. A few won at games." The well-light steadied. "One king holds eleven thousand names, ward-keeper. Do you understand what a man can do with eleven thousand names?"

The river lapped the hull. Somewhere in the reeds, a bird started up and thought better of it.

"He can find anyone," Ilex said. His voice had gone quiet, the quiet of a man doing his own arithmetic and hating the total. "Any soldier who ever swore under his birth-name. Any ward-keeper. Any—" He stopped.

"Any swordspeaker whose true name was ever entered in a roll," the wight finished gently. "Yes."

Zephyrine felt it like cold water rising inside their own chest. *He may already know it.* Sable had said that weeks ago, in the shop, in the dark, holding the torn memory of the oath up to the light like a dead bird. And here was the mechanism laid bare—not sorcery, not prophecy, just bookkeeping. Names bought in bulk. Ledgers. A soul-economy with clerks and accounts and a king at the top of it, feeding the court beneath the mountain one purse at a time.

"How much for two crossings?" Zephyrine asked, because the alternative was standing there drowning in the scale of it.

"One name each. Spoken freely. Held no longer—I do not keep what I am given. I spend it. That is the law." The wight spread its too-long arms slightly, and the barge beneath them creaked as if settling under a weight. "The river takes, the river spends. What I buy with your name, I cannot say. The current decides."

"And if we refuse?" Ilex asked.

"Then you swim. The Vell is deep and patient and does not care about your errands. People swim sometimes." The hollows flickered. "People surface downstream sometimes."

* * *

They walked to the far end of the barge to argue, which was pointless, because Zephyrine was fairly sure the wight heard everything on its water the way a spider hears everything on its web. They argued anyway. It was easier than thinking.

"You have to pay," Ilex said, low. "Whatever your name is. Whatever it costs you to say it—you have to. If Vane doesn't already have it, giving it to a river-wight is a risk. If he *does* have it—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to. They had both seen chapter fourteen of that story: the oath seizing Zephyrine's body mid-stride, the self watching from inside, the steel coming down on command.

"If I give my name to the river, and the river spends it, and the current carries it upstream—"

"Wights don't lie about their law. It's the one thing they can't do. If it says held no longer, it means spent and scattered. Untraceable." Ilex rubbed his jaw. "It's the safest place in the world to lose a name. Which is a sentence I never thought I'd say."

"And yours?"

He was quiet for a moment. Out on the water, the mist moved in slow banks, and the far shore was a rumor.

"My name's been struck from the rolls," he said finally. "Officially. Disgraced ward-keepers get their names taken out of the registry—that's part of the disgrace, did you know that? You don't just lose the rank. You lose the *entry*. As far as the crown's books are concerned, Ilex of the Third Circle never existed." He laughed, short and airless. "Turns out that makes me cheap. Nobody's buying a name that isn't for sale anywhere. I'm the only currency I've got left, and it's worthless everywhere but here."

"Ilex—"

"Don't." He held up a hand. "Don't make it a whole thing. It's a toll. It's a bridge. People cross bridges." He turned and walked back toward the wight before Zephyrine could answer, and there was something in his stride—shoulders set, chin down—that Zephyrine recognized from mirrors, from years ago, from a person they had apparently once been: the walk of someone doing the necessary thing quickly so the fear doesn't catch up.

"Ilex of the Third Circle," he said to the wight. "True name spoken freely. Take it and spend it."

The wight bowed—a slow, jointless folding, like kelp in current. The well-lights in its hollows brightened until they hurt to look at, and something left Ilex. There was no other way to describe it. Not blood, not breath. Something subtler, a thread of warmth pulled out through the crown of his head, visible only because the morning was cold enough to show such things. He swayed. Zephyrine caught his elbow.

"I'm fine," Ilex said. His voice sounded the same. His face sounded the same, if faces could sound. But when he blinked, it took a half-second too long, like a man surfacing. "Fine. Just—give it a moment. Feels like forgetting why you walked into a room."

"Does it feel like anything else?"

"Yeah." He stared out at the brown-green water. "It feels like being a little less. Like a coat with one pocket sewn shut." He managed half a smile. "Ask me later what I lost. Maybe I'll know."

*My turn,* Zephyrine thought. And could not.

They stood at the center of the barge, and the wight waited, patient as water, and Zephyrine reached for the name the way you reach for a tooth that isn't there anymore. There was a socket where it should have been. There was even a shape to the socket—they could feel the outline of it, the way a missing finger still itches. *Zeph—* something. A first syllable like wind through a doorway. Their mother's voice saying it, maybe, or a mentor's voice, sharp across a training yard—but every path toward it ended in the same smooth wall, three years wide, and behind the wall, nothing they owned.

"I can't," they said aloud, before deciding to.

The wight's hollows fixed on them. "You will not, or you cannot?"

"I don't know." Honest. It was all they had left, honesty. "There's a hole where it should be. Someone took it, or I buried it, or—" The hum under their skin rose, unbidden, and this time Zephyrine didn't clamp it down, because the wight was old law, older than the crown, and old law deserved old truth. "I'm a swordspeaker with no sword-word. An oath with no oath-taker's name. Whatever I was, I've been robbed of the receipt."

For a long moment the wight did nothing. The river moved. The mist moved. Then it leaned down—down and down, the smooth grey face descending until it hung a hand's width from Zephyrine's own, and the well-light poured into their eyes, and Zephyrine saw, reflected in those hollow depths, not their face but a ledger. Rows and rows of names in a script that flowed like water, and one line glowing near the top, recent, wet ink:

*Ravensworth — purchased — paid in full.*

Not read. Not copied. *Purchased.*

"No," the wight said, straightening, and its voice had changed—quicker now, almost urgent, a clerk who has found an error in a column that should balance. "No, that is not refusal. That is a prior transaction. Someone bought your name before you ever reached my water." The hollows flickered hard. "And the purchase mark is not mine, nor my kin's, nor any river's. It is a court-mark. Mountain work. Ledger-script." It drew itself up to its full wrong height, and for the first time, the ancient thing sounded afraid. "Swordspeaker. Do you know what it means when a name appears in ledger-script as *paid in full*?"

"Tell me."

"It means you were not robbed." The well-lights guttered low. "It means you were *sold*. By someone authorized to sell. And there is only one authorization in the world that covers a swordspeaker's true name." It extended one long arm toward the far shore, toward the south, toward the mountain waiting beyond the horizon like a debt. "The oath itself. Whoever held your oath held the right of sale. Cross, if you can—I cannot charge what has already been collected. But hear me, last of the line: you are not hunting a thief."

The barge began to move, unpoled, unpushed, sliding out into the current on its own consent.

"You are hunting your buyer. And upstream, he is buying by the thousand."

* * *

The far shore came up gray and reedy. Ilex stood at the rail, hugging himself against a chill that had nothing to do with the morning, occasionally blinking too long, occasionally mouthing a word he couldn't find. When the hull kissed the mud, Zephyrine took his arm and steered him onto the bank, and only then let themselves stop walking, turn around, and look back.

The wight stood on its barge, already receding into the mist, a tall smudge with two dying coins for eyes.

"Eleven thousand names," Zephyrine said to the emptying river. "And mine was paid in full three years ago."

Behind them, in the reeds, Sable's voice came thin and strange, like something remembered rather than heard:

"Now you know why I couldn't tell you who sold you. Look at the ledger-line again, Zephyrine. Look at whose hand signed it."

But the mist had already closed over the water, and the river carried the answer away faster than any ghost could speak it.
