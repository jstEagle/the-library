## 10. Sable's Last Day

The morning of the last day of her life, Sable Merren woke before dawn because the fever in her brother's chest had gone quiet, and quiet was worse than coughing.

She lay listening to him breathe — a thin, papery sound, like wind through a keyhole — and counted the intervals between each breath the way she had counted them every night for two weeks. When the gaps stretched too long her heart stumbled after them, trying to fill the silence with its own beating, as if it could lend him some.

Outside, Greyharbor's salt fog had crept up the river valley overnight and settled over Fenlow like a shroud that hadn't yet decided whose shoulders to lie on. The fishing boats would not go out in it. The drying racks stood empty. The whole village smelled of brine and woodsmoke and the sweet-rot stink of the sickness that had come down from the hills with the autumn rains and taken root in one house out of three.

Sable rose without waking Tam. She banked the fire, set the kettle on, and took down the ledger book their mother had kept, though there was nothing left in it to count. She looked at the last page anyway. Old habit. Their mother had believed that debts written down were debts half-paid, and that hope was just arithmetic you hadn't finished yet.

The arithmetic was simple enough. The herb-woman in Marrow's Cross wanted forty crowns for a course of lungwort tincture and feverfew, and wanted them by week's end or she'd sell to the next village up the road, where they paid faster. Sable had eleven crowns hidden in a jar beneath the floorboard, and a boat worth less than the wood it was built from, and a soul.

Everyone in Fenlow had a soul, of course. That wasn't the strange part. The strange part was that lately, men came down from the mountain offering fair coin for them.

* * *

They had come first at midsummer, riding fine horses through a village that had never seen a fine horse, wearing grey cloaks cut so cleanly they made every coat in Fenlow look like sackcloth. There were three of them, and they did not drink, and they did not sweat, and when the sun went behind a cloud they all turned their heads at exactly the same moment, like birds.

The tall one had done the talking. He had a voice like a well — deep, and cool, and with something at the bottom of it you couldn't see.

"The crown requires certain... contributions," he said, standing in the square while the whole village gathered at a wary distance. "Voluntary ones. In exchange for which, His Majesty King Aldous Vane offers generous compensation."

"Contributions of what?" old Wenna had asked, arms crossed, because Wenna had never met a question she was afraid of.

The tall man smiled. It was a good smile. It reached his eyes, which somehow made it worse. "Of yourselves. A tithe of the spirit. Painless. Permanent." He let the word sit in the air like a stone dropped in a well. "You will feel nothing. You will lose nothing you can name. And your families will eat through the winter."

Nobody had sold that day. But the coin he'd shown as demonstration — a single gold piece, heavy as an anchor — had stayed in the village's mind the way a splinter stays in a thumb.

By autumn, times had done what times do. The herring moved north. The blight got into the barley. And one night in September, Cobb Tanner's eldest son had walked up to the grey-cloaked men in the inn yard, taken their purse, and signed his name in a book that seemed to drink the ink. He'd walked home whistling. He'd bought his father a new boat. And he'd woken the next morning exactly himself, he swore it, laughing, spending, alive — only now and then he'd stop in the middle of a sentence and stare at nothing for a heartbeat, like a man who'd walked into a room and forgotten why, except about everything.

He was still laughing. That was the thing Sable kept coming back to, turning over like a stone in her pocket. He was still laughing, and his family was eating, and whatever he'd lost, he couldn't name it, just as the man had promised.

And Tam could not breathe.

* * *

She found them at the crossroads above the village, where the road to Marrow's Cross forked away toward the mountain pass. They kept a kind of office there now — a table under an awning, a ledger, a lantern that burned without flickering even in the wind off the sea. Two of them today. The tall one, and a woman with a face like a closed door.

Sable stood at the edge of the awning's shadow for a long moment, working up the words, and the tall man watched her with patient courtesy, as if he had all the time ever minted.

"I want to sell," she said. Her voice came out smaller than she meant it to. She planted her feet and tried again. "My brother is sick. Lung-fever. I need forty crowns."

"How much of a tithe does forty crowns purchase, Corvel?" the woman asked, and her voice was dry as ledger paper.

The tall man — Corvel — opened the great book on the table. Its pages were vellum so fine it was nearly translucent, and the ink on them seemed to move when Sable wasn't looking directly at it, settling, rearranging, like sediment in dark water.

"Sable Merren," he read aloud, and she felt her own name go through her like a cold current, "of Fenlow. Nineteen years. Unsworn, unbound, unbought." He looked up, and his smile was gentle this time, almost kind. "A clean soul. Rare, these days. Forty crowns buys perhaps a tenth part. A small tithe. You would keep everything that makes you *you* — your loves, your griefs, your name, your face. We take only..." he considered, "...the far edges. The parts of you that reach past yourself. Most people never notice they're gone."

"And if I sell more?" Sable heard herself ask. "How much for the whole of it?"

Corvel's eyebrows rose, genuinely surprised, and for a moment something moved behind his face that might have been respect, or appetite, or both wearing the same coat.

"The whole of a soul is not sold for coin," he said. "It's sold for a price named by the seller. Anything within reason. A life's comfort. A miracle, small ones only. Or—" and here he leaned forward, and the lantern light bent toward him slightly, which lantern light should not do, "—a wish granted after death. Some of our clients find that arrangement attractive. Payment deferred. You get your forty crowns now, your brother breathes by week's end, and the balance comes due when you do. Naturally."

Naturally. As if death were a merchant's stall and eternity a bag of figs.

Sable thought of Tam's breathing. Keyhole wind. Longer gaps.

"What happens to it?" she asked. "After. Where does it go?"

"That," said Corvel, closing the book with a soft sound like a kiss, "is crown business."

* * *

She walked back down into the village to think, because Wenna always said a bargain made standing up was a bargain made badly, and because some traitor part of her hoped the grey-cloaked men would be gone when she returned, or that she'd wake up and it would have been a dream, the whole rotten autumn of it.

That was when she met the stranger.

He — no, *they*, she decided, though she couldn't have said why; something about the way they carried themselves, like a secret worn loosely — was sitting on the sea-wall at the bottom of the lane, feeding gulls bits of dried fish with the concentration of someone performing a task they'd read about but never seen done. Grey traveling cloak, travel-stained boots, a sword wrapped in oilcloth across their back like something being smuggled rather than carried. Dark hair going silver early at the temples. And eyes — when they glanced up at her — eyes that were kind in a way that seemed to cost them something, as if kindness were a language they'd learned late and spoke with an accent.

"You're staring," the stranger said. Not unkindly.

"You're new," Sable said. "Nobody comes to Fenlow in fever season unless they've got to."

"Fever season." The stranger's hands went still. Something crossed their face — quick, swallowed, like a fish under ice. "How bad?"

"One house in three. Two dead so far. My brother's the worst of the living." She heard herself say it plainly, without self-pity, and was briefly proud of that, and then briefly furious that pride was the coin she had to spend instead of money.

The stranger stood. Up close they were shorter than they'd seemed, and tired around the mouth, and there was a faint hum about them that Sable felt more than heard — like standing near a struck bell that had almost finished ringing. The gulls screamed at the loss of their benefactor.

"Lung-fever," the stranger said. "Wet cough? Ribs ache to the touch? Worse at night?"

"Yes. All of it. How did—"

"There's a tincture. Lungwort and feverfew, but it has to be cut with bog-myrtle or it sits wrong in the blood. Most herb-women skip the myrtle because it's dear." The stranger was already digging in a pouch. "I don't carry any myself, but—" they paused, hand halfway into the pouch, and gave her a long, careful look, the kind of look a person gives a locked door they very much want to open and have promised someone they won't. "There's a woman in Marrow's Cross. Greta. Tell her the charm-seller from Greyharbor sent you, and tell her the myrtle comes out of her own stores or I'll hear of it."

"Why would she listen to that?"

"Because she owes me, and she knows I collect." A ghost of a smile. "Go on. Before the fog eats the road."

Sable didn't move. Later — much later, from a distance she could not then imagine — she would understand that this was the hinge of her whole last day, the moment balanced on its point like a spinning coin deciding which face to land on. She could have run home. She could have run to Marrow's Cross. Instead she stood on the shingle with the fog beading in her eyelashes and asked the question that had been sitting in her chest since the crossroads, because the stranger had kind eyes, and because she was nineteen, and because she wanted, just once, to say it out loud to someone who might understand.

"If you sold a piece of yourself," she said, "to save someone you loved — would you?"

The stranger went very still. The fog drifted between them. Somewhere behind the murk, a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"I did once," the stranger said quietly. "Something like it. It didn't work the way I hoped." Their jaw tightened. "The people who buy souls — they aren't merchants, girl. They're *minters*. Whatever they pay you, they make sure the coin is worth less than what you gave. And they never, ever tell you what they're making with the metal."

"They said I wouldn't miss it. The part they take."

"They said that about mine too." The stranger looked out at the fog where the sea should have been, and their face did something complicated and old. "I can't even tell you what I lost. That's how well it worked."

* * *

She went back up to the crossroads anyway.

She told herself it was the arithmetic. Forty crowns now, plus a free cure from Greta besides, minus a tenth of a soul she couldn't name and therefore, by Corvel's own logic, could hardly mourn. Her mother's ledger logic. Debts written down are debts half-paid. Hope is arithmetic you haven't finished.

But underneath the arithmetic, honest as a bruise, was the other thing. The stranger's face when they said *it didn't work the way I hoped*. The way they'd looked at the fog like it owed them an answer. Sable had spent her whole life watching people leave Fenlow — for the cities, for the ships, for the war that had eaten her father — and every one of them had looked forward, hungry, certain the world beyond the headland owed them something better. This stranger was the first person she'd ever met who looked like they were running *from* something, and she found she wanted to know what it was with a hunger that frightened her.

Also — and she admitted this to no one, least of all herself — if the mountain's men were buying souls by the purseful, then somebody high up wanted them badly. And things that powerful men want badly are things that matter. And if it mattered, then maybe it could be *fought*. Maybe the stranger with the sword wrapped in oilcloth was already fighting it. Maybe there was a side to be on.

Or maybe she was just tired, and scared, and nineteen, and ready to believe any story that ended with Tam breathing easy.

Corvel saw her coming and opened the book before she reached the table, as if he'd known — as if he'd *always* known — which, she would later learn, he had.

"Changed my mind," Sable said. "I'll sell. Forty crowns. The small tithe."

"Wise," said Corvel, and produced a pen.

"But I want terms." She put her hands flat on the table, and was gratified to see the woman with the closed-door face shift her weight slightly, as if something unexpected had entered the room. "Written down. My mother kept books. I want it in the ledger: the tithe is a tenth, no more; my name stays mine; my memories stay mine; and when I die, whatever's left of me goes free. No deferred payment. No wishes. No balance owing. Free and clear, and you write it so."

For a moment the only sound was the fog dripping off the awning.

"A negotiator," Corvel murmured, and there was that thing behind his face again, the thing that was not quite respect. "Very well. Terms in writing. The house honors its contracts, Mistress Merren; we are not the monsters of your grandmother's stories." He began to write, the pen whispering, and the ink drank into the vellum with a sound like a slow inhale. "Sign here. And here. Press firmly. The book prefers a firm hand."

The pen was cold. The signature came out of her looking exactly like her handwriting, which seemed obscene — that her own hand would cooperate so readily in its own diminishment.

Forty crowns, counted out in gold, heavy as anchors. A receipt, folded twice. And a sensation as she stepped back from the table that she could only describe as *thinner* — as if a coat she'd been wearing all her life had been quietly taken in at the seams, everywhere at once, and she could not have said which seams, only that the wind reached her more easily now.

She was three steps down the road when Corvel's voice caught up with her, mild as milk:

"Mistress Merren. A curiosity, nothing more. The stranger on the sea-wall — did they speak to you?"

Sable turned. The lantern burned steady. The closed-door woman watched her with the fixed attention of a cat at a mousehole.

"They gave me a cure for my brother," Sable said. "Why?"

"No reason." Corvel smiled his good smile. "Only that we've been looking for them for rather a long time. If they should pass this way again — and they will, they're bound for the mountain same as everyone, eventually — you might earn back a portion of what you sold tonight. Information is also currency, in the court below." He tapped the great book once, gently, the way one taps a sleeping child. "Think of it as credit."

* * *

She ran home through the fog with the gold hammering against her ribs and the thinness singing in her like a plucked string, and she burst through the door, and Tam was awake, propped on his elbows, gray-faced and furious with weakness and *alive*, glaring at her with their mother's exact scowl.

"Where in the nine hells have you been?" he croaked.

"Buying you a future," she said, breathless, laughing, already counting coins onto the blanket. "Get dressed. We're going to Marrow's Cross."

She never told him about the crossroads. Not that evening, not in the weeks after, when the tincture worked and the fever broke and he grew strong enough to complain about porridge again. Not ever. What was the use? He'd have looked at her the way she now sometimes caught strangers looking at Cobb Tanner's son — searching for the missing piece, the far edge, the part that used to reach past itself — and she could not have borne being seen like that by him.

Besides, she had decided something, in the fog on the road home, walking with a hole in her she couldn't point to. She had decided that if the mountain was minting coins out of souls, then someone ought to walk up that road and see the mint with her own eyes. Someone small, and sharp, and already partly spent, with nothing left to threaten her with except the rest.

She told herself she'd wait until spring. She told herself she'd think it over properly, like her mother would have, ledger open, sums neat.

But the stranger with the kind eyes was still in the village that night — she saw them at the inn window, talking low and urgent with Wenna, and the way they held their wrapped sword was not the way a person holds a thing they mean to keep hidden forever — and Sable understood, with the terrible clarity of the young, that spring was a luxury other people planned in.

Three days later, the stars went out.

And Sable Merren, one-tenth lighter, wholly determined, packed her brother off to Greta's care, kissed his forehead, lied to him beautifully about where she was going, and started up the road toward the mountain — following, though she did not know it yet, the same stranger with the kind eyes, who was following the same darkness, all three of them bound for the court beneath the world where her soul sat in a bottle on a shelf, waiting, patient as debt, for her to come and find it.
