## 11. The Court Beneath

The mountain did not open for them. It had to be *entered*, the way a body is entered—through passages that narrowed and darkened and grew warm, until Zephyrine could no longer pretend they were walking through stone at all. They were descending into something that breathed.

Three days in the deep roads, and the salt mines were behind them, the last of Greyharbor's world sealed away like a letter never sent. The air changed first. It lost its mineral bite and took on a sweetness, faint and wrong, like fruit rotting somewhere out of sight. Then the walls began to shine.

Not torchlight. Not any light Zephyrine knew. The veins in the rock glowed a pale gold, soft as candlelight through skin, and it pulsed—slowly, patiently, in a rhythm that took Zephyrine far too long to recognize because they did not want to recognize it.

A heartbeat.

"The deeper we go," Ilex said quietly, "the brighter it gets."

He walked ahead with his ward-keeper's staff held crosswise, the way he'd carried it since the ferry, as if the weight of it reminded him who he had been before disgrace. His face was gray in the golden light. He had not slept well since the river. None of them had. Sable drifted at the rear of their little procession, her feet not quite touching the ground, her outline flickering whenever the pulse of light swelled—as though the light itself kept trying to touch her, and she kept slipping away from it.

"It's souls," Sable said. She said it simply, the way she said everything now, but her voice had a crack in it that hadn't been there before the mines. "The light. Didn't you wonder why the mountain glows on every map? Cartographers think it's fire in the roots. It's not fire." She looked up at the pulsing vein above them. "It's us. Or people like me. All of us, burning slow."

Zephyrine's hand found the hilt of the sword wrapped in oilcloth across their back. They hadn't unwrapped it since Greyharbor. They weren't sure they wanted to know what it would do down here, so close to whatever power fed the oath in their blood.

"How much farther?" they asked, because asking was easier than thinking.

"Past the toll-gate," Ilex said. "Then the galleries open up, and—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I helped design the toll-gate. Thirty years ago, when I still believed the wards were built to keep things *out*."

"And they're not," Zephyrine said.

"They're built to keep things *in*. We called it containment. We drew diagrams." His laugh came out broken. "We were very proud of our diagrams."

* * *

The toll-gate was exactly what Ilex had described in his flat, guilty voice: a throat of black basalt where the passage pinched to the width of two men, spanned by an arch carved with wards so old their script had been worn smooth by the passage of countless shoulders. But the arch was dead. Whatever had once flowed through those grooves had been diverted, siphoned, drunk. The stone was cold. And piled against its base, half-buried in the dust of centuries, lay the things that had been carried in and never carried out: a child's shoe. A wedding ring fused to a fingerbone. A wooden bird with one wing.

Sable knelt and picked up the bird. Her fingers passed through it twice before she managed to lift it, and when she did, it glowed faintly in her hands—the same gold as the walls.

"This is mine," she said.

Zephyrine went still. "You've been here?"

"I died in the mines. Chapter seven of my life, remember?" She smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "But before that—I came this way. Everyone from my village came this way. Vane's agents don't take your soul where you die. They bring you here first, to be *assessed*. There's a hall past this gate where they weigh you." She turned the little bird over in her glowing hands. "They weighed me. I remember the scale. It wasn't a scale, really. It was a man with kind eyes and a ledger, and he looked at me and said, 'Village stock, unremarkable, four years' burn at standard draw.' Four years." She set the bird down gently, the way you'd set down a sleeping child. "That's what I was worth. Four years of light for someone else's hallway."

The pulse in the walls quickened, just slightly, as if the mountain had heard her and was pleased.

Zephyrine felt something in their chest turn over—not grief, not yet, something colder and more precise. Rage learning its own address.

"Then we walk quietly," they said, "and we do not stop to grieve until we're back in daylight."

"No," Sable said. She rose, and for a moment her outline burned brighter than the walls. "We walk quietly. But we *remember* every step. That's the difference between us and them, Zephyrine Ravensworth. They keep ledgers. We keep memories. And memory is the only currency down here they can't mint."

* * *

Past the gate, the passage opened the way a fist opens—and Zephyrine stopped breathing.

The court beneath the mountain was not a cave. It was a cathedral inverted, a hollow vast enough to hold weather, and it was *full of light*. Galleries tiered the walls in sweeping arcs, carved from the living rock and faced with gold-veined stone, and every gallery blazed with chandeliers—but there was no wax, no flame. Each chandelier was a lattice of glass vessels, hundreds upon thousands of them, and each vessel held a swirl of pale gold radiance that turned slowly, endlessly, like a thought that would not resolve. Souls. Bottled souls, hung by the ten thousand, burning at a rate some clerk had calculated, lighting the halls of a king who had never seen them burn.

And the court moved beneath them. Courtiers in silk the color of dusk crossed marble floors that gleamed like frozen water; servants carried trays of wine that glowed softly in the decanters; musicians played on instruments whose strings caught the ambient light and threw it back in shivering threads. Everything shimmered. Everything was beautiful. And everything, Zephyrine realized with a lurch, was *lit*—every lamp, every jewel, every thread of gold embroidery, every glass of wine—fed from the same source. The whole court glowed because the whole court *consumed*.

"There," Ilex whispered. His staff trembled in his grip. "The vaults are below the central dais. The archive beyond them. If we can reach the archive—"

"Look at them." Zephyrine's voice came out strange, flattened. Below, a courtier raised a glass of luminous wine to her lips and drank, and laughed, and the light in the glass dimmed as she swallowed. "They're drinking them. They're drinking *people*."

"That's the vintage from the southern villages," Sable said. Her voice had gone very quiet. "Good year. Bad harvest, though—the famine made thin souls. The nobles complain about it." She drifted closer to Zephyrine, and her chill settled over their shoulder like a hand. "I told you I remember things. I remember the vintage lists. I read them while I was waiting to be weighed. They have vintages, Zephyrine. Years. Regions. The 891 Greywater press was considered delicate. Delicate." The word cracked in her mouth. "There were children in that press."

Below them, the music swelled. A dance had begun on the marble floor, dozens of couples turning in slow, glittering spirals, their jewels drinking the light of the chandeliers overhead, which drank in turn from the veins in the rock, which pulsed with the stolen hearts of everyone who had ever trusted the border wards to hold.

Zephyrine had imagined many horrors on the road. Armies. Torture chambers. Vane himself, crowned in shadow. They had not imagined *beauty*. They had not imagined that evil down here would look like a festival, that the machinery of theft would wear silk and play music and complain about the quality of its wine. Power made visible was supposed to be ugly. No one had warned them it would be gorgeous—that awe would arrive first, unbidden, treacherous, and only curdle into revulsion slowly enough to make you doubt yourself.

For one shameful moment, standing at the mouth of that shining hollow, Zephyrine understood the courtiers. Understood the temptation of a light you could hold, pour, spend. Then Sable's hand—or the memory of a hand—brushed their arm, and the moment broke, and what flooded in behind it was not doubt but clarity, cold and total, like plunging into winter sea.

Every chandelier was a village. Every vintage was a name. Somewhere in that glittering sprawl, in a vault or an archive or a clerk's careful hand, was a bottle with a girl-shaped warmth inside it, and another bottle, older, holding a woman who had taught Zephyrine everything except how to run away.

"Maren," Zephyrine whispered. Their mentor's name, spoken for the first time since Greyharbor, tasted like iron. "She's down there. In one of those lights."

"Then we get her out," Ilex said. "And then we burn the racks."

"You can't burn souls, ward-keeper." Sable's flicker steadied. "You can only free them or spend them. That's the whole economy. That's the whole *tragedy*." She pointed, and her finger left a brief trail of gold in the air. "The archive. Third gallery, behind the dais, past the Master of Ledgers' desk. Everything is written there—who was taken, what they fetched, who paid. Every soul in this mountain has a line in that book."

"Then that's where we're going," Zephyrine said.

They descended along the service stair Ilex remembered from his diagram-drawing days—a stair built for slaves and clerks, invisible from the marble floors, stinking of tallow and old fear. The music faded above them. The pulse of the walls grew louder here, intimate, everywhere, the heartbeat of a thing that had long ago stopped being a mountain.

And as they came down the last flight, hugging the shadows behind a rack of empty bottles—empty, Zephyrine noted, and tried not to think about what *emptied* meant—a voice drifted down from the dais above, smooth as poured wine, carrying the particular authority of a man who has never once been interrupted:

"—and when the last traitor kneels, my friends, the stars return. Until then, we make our own light."

Courtiers laughed. Glasses chimed. Somewhere below, in the dark behind the racks, Zephyrine closed their eyes and felt the oath stir in their blood like a hook settling into flesh—because the voice was familiar, because the voice had spoken words over them once before, words they could not remember, in a life they could not recall—

—and Sable's chill hand found theirs in the dark, and squeezed.

"He's here," she breathed. "Zephyrine. *He's actually here.*"

Above them, unhurried footsteps crossed the dais. And with them, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of a ledger opening.
