## 13. The Ledger of Souls

The archive breathed.

That was the only word for it. Zephyrine had expected a vault—stone and iron and the dead patience of locked things—but the archive beneath the mountain court *breathed*, slow and tidal, its walls ribbed with pale channels through which light moved like blood. The light came from everywhere and nowhere: from the shelves themselves, from the jars nested in their cradles of velvet and bone, from the floor, which was not stone at all but compressed centuries of something that had once been alive.

Souls. The whole room was lit by souls.

Zephyrine pressed themself flat against a shelf-shadow and did not let themself look up. Looking up was how you were caught; looking up meant you wanted something, and wanting rang in this place like a struck bell. They could feel it already—the faint harmonic pull of all that bottled brightness tugging at the small ember they kept banked under their skin, the one they'd spent three years teaching to pretend it wasn't there.

*Not yet,* they told it. *Not here.*

Sable drifted beside them, or rather, Sable occupied the space beside them in the way fog occupies a doorway. She had been quieter since the mines—quieter even than a ghost should be—and when she spoke now her voice came thin, as if she were speaking through the walls of one of these very jars.

"They keep the ledgers on the far side," she whispered. "Past the reading gallery. There's a door with no handle. It opens for ink."

"Ink."

"Blood-ink. A drop from anyone who's ever been entered in the books." Her ghost-light guttered. "You've been entered, Zephyrine. I'm sorry. I should have said sooner. I didn't know how."

Zephyrine's mouth went dry. Three years of forgetting sat behind that sentence like a dog behind a gate, and they made themselves not look at it. Not yet. First the mentor. First Maren's soul, if it could be found, if it could be freed. Everything else—the vertigo, the hole where their past should be—could wait until there was air enough to fall apart in.

"Show me," they said.

* * *

The reading gallery was worse than the shelves.

Here the court's wealth was displayed as openly as jewels in a merchant's window: souls decanted into wine-glasses of smoked glass, poured for courtiers who moved through the gallery in gowns stitched with ward-sigils, laughing softly, sipping light. Zephyrine watched a woman in silver tilt back a glass and shiver with pleasure, watched the glow travel down her throat and bloom briefly in her cheeks—a borrowed vitality, someone else's morning, someone else's last summer, drunk for vanity.

Zephyrine had seen cruelty before. They had cut it out of the world, once, with an oath-bound blade. But this—this was cruelty made *domestic*. Made fashionable. That was what curdled awe into revulsion: not the scale of it, but the ease. The souls here were not prisoners. They were *inventory*.

A page boy hurried past carrying a tray of small bottles, each no bigger than a thumb, each glowing a different color. Zephyrine counted without meaning to. Twelve bottles. Twelve people who had once been afraid of the dark, who had once loved someone badly, who had once sung off-key in kitchens. Now condensation on glass.

*Ilex is holding the pass above,* they reminded themself. *Sable is here. You are not alone.* The thought helped less than it should have.

"The door," Sable breathed. "There. Do you see it?"

Zephyrine saw it. Set into the far wall between two display cases, a slab of black stone so smooth it reflected nothing—not the soul-light, not Zephyrine's face. No handle, no hinge, no seam except the suggestion of one. As they approached, the surface warmed, and letters surfaced in it like fish rising in a pond:

**THE LEDGER ADMITS THE ENTERED ONLY.**

Zephyrine drew the little knife from their boot—the one they'd used for three years to prick thumbs for luck-knots, charging two coppers a charm and hating every honest coin. They opened the pad of their finger and pressed it to the stone.

For a heartbeat, nothing. Then the black drank the drop, and the door sighed open like a held breath released, and cold air rolled out smelling of iron and old paper and, underneath both, faintly of salt.

Greyharbor salt. Home salt.

Zephyrine stepped through, and the door closed behind them with the soft finality of a grave being patted down.

* * *

Inside, there were no shelves. There was no ceiling. There was only the ledger.

It filled the room—or the room existed only to hold it—a book the size of a chapel, chained open upon a lectern carved from a single tooth of some creature Zephyrine refused to imagine. Its pages turned themselves, slowly, patiently, in a wind that came from nowhere. Each page was dense with entries in a hand so precise it looked printed, and each entry glowed faintly, the way coals hold light after the fire dies.

Zephyrine climbed the lectern's steps and read.

The entries were simple. Brutally, beautifully simple. A name. A date of purchase. A price. A buyer. And in the margin, in smaller script, a note of *condition*—for souls, apparently, came graded like wool or grain.

*Harrow, Tamsin. Bought at the border, year one of the reign. Price: two measures of ward-stone. Buyer: Office of Provisions. Condition: frightened but intact.*

*Odo of the Marsh Road. Sold by his brother. Price: forgiveness of debt. Condition: bitter. Best used for lamp-oil.*

Zephyrine read on, and the room grew colder, and they understood that they were reading the true history of Aldous Vane's reign—not the proclamations, not the heralds' trumpets, but this: names, prices, buyers. An economy of everything people were. Somewhere upstream, a ferry-wight had told them, someone was buying names in bulk. Here was the warehouse. Here was the counting-house. Here was the whole terrible arithmetic laid out in glowing rows.

They found Maren on the third page they turned.

*Maren Solenne. Teacher of the sword, speaker of oaths. Surrendered voluntarily, year two of the reign. Price: remission of sentence against her student. Condition: serene. Reserved for the King's table.*

*Surrendered voluntarily.*

Zephyrine read the line four times. The words did not soften. *Voluntarily.* Maren, who had taught them that the oath was a chain dressed as an honor, who had warned them—hadn't she?—to run, to hide, to bury the swordspeaker deep and live small. Maren had walked into this mountain and sold herself, and the price—

*Remission of sentence against her student.*

Against her student. Against—

The page turned under Zephyrine's hand, patient as tide, and stopped.

And there it was.

*Ravensworth, Zephyrine. Last oath-bound swordspeaker. Entered year two of the reign. Paid in full, three years ago.*

Paid in full.

Three years ago.

The numbers did not agree with each other. Year two of the reign was three years ago—that part held—but *paid in full*? Zephyrine stood in their own body, breathing, bleeding, alive. No one had purchased them. No one had drunk them from a smoked-glass glass. They were here, in the flesh, holding a knife with their own blood still wet on it.

Unless.

Unless what had been bought was not the body. Unless what had been sold was the thing inside it—the three missing years, the swallowed country of their own past, the memories that ended mid-stride one ordinary afternoon and resumed in a rented room in Greyharbor with salt on the windows and no idea how they'd arrived.

Someone had sold them. Not their life. Their *self*—the years that made the self. And the ledger said *paid in full*, which meant the transaction was complete, which meant somewhere in this mountain, perhaps in a vault, perhaps in a king's throat, those years were sitting in a bottle. Or worse: already drunk. Already worn. Already smiling out of someone else's face with Zephyrine's laughter.

"Sable," Zephyrine said. Their voice came out strange, flattened, as if heard through the wall of a jar. "Who sold me?"

Silence. The pages turned. The wind that turned them smelled of salt.

"Sable. You said you remember what I forgot. You said—" Zephyrine's hand closed on the edge of the lectern, and the old wood bit their palm. "You knew. When you came to my shop. You knew my name was in this book."

The ghost gathered herself slowly out of the air, dimmer than Zephyrine had ever seen her, her edges fraying like cloth rubbed too long. When she finally spoke, it was barely a voice at all.

"I know what I remember," she said. "I remember a room with a table. I remember a man whose face I can't—I remember his hands. He counted coins onto the table. And I remember that he wasn't buying from you, Zephyrine. He was buying from someone about *you*. Someone who knew your true name. Someone close enough to give it away with a signature."

"Who?"

"I don't—" Sable's light flickered hard, like a candle in a draft. "I died before I could learn it. That's the truth. But I remember the signature on the receipt. I remember it because it was beautiful. Loops and flourishes, like something written by someone proud of their penmanship. Someone educated. Someone *courtly*."

"Courtly," Zephyrine repeated. The word dropped through them like a stone through water. Ilex had been courtly once—a ward-keeper of the mountain, before his disgrace. Ilex had built the wards. Ilex had appeared in Greyharbor within days of the wards falling, knowing too much, apologizing too fluently, crossing lines of old enmity to warn them with suspicious perfect timing. *Reluctant alliance born of shared shame*, they had called it, and shame was exactly what a seller would carry.

No. No, Ilex had fought beside them in the mines. Ilex had stood in the dark while the wight took his name and paid it like a man paying with his own fingers. You didn't do that if you'd already sold your friend.

But then—who? Who else had been close? Who else had known the true name that bound the oath? Maren knew it. Maren was here, *voluntarily*, her surrender priced against a sentence on her student—

The thoughts began to turn in a circle, each one sharpening the others, and Zephyrine made them stop. Not here. Not in this room that breathed. Betrayal without a face was a knife in the dark; better to walk toward it slowly than to spin and slash at shadows.

They bent over the ledger again and forced themself to read their own entry to the end. Below *paid in full*, in that same proud, looping hand, was a final notation:

*Buyer: His Majesty, by right of conquest. Note: goods withheld from consumption pending the kneelings. See also: cross-reference, Oath-Bound Assets, restricted shelf.*

"Withheld," Zephyrine whispered. "He hasn't drunk them. He's keeping them."

"For leverage," Sable said. "Or for bait. Or—" She stopped. Her whole form went still in the way only the dead can be still, absolutely, completely, and when she spoke again her voice had changed, gone urgent and thin as wire. "Zephyrine. The pages have stopped turning."

Zephyrine looked up. It was true. The great book lay motionless for the first time, its self-turning wind stilled, and in the silence that followed Zephyrine became aware of another sound, distant but approaching, echoing down the corridors of the living rock: footsteps. Many. Measured. Preceded by the high clear note of a herald's trumpet, muffled by stone, growing louder.

And beneath the trumpet, threading through it like a dark ribbon through bright cloth, came a voice. A man's voice, warm and resonant and horribly familiar in the way a word is familiar when you've heard it in dreams you can't keep.

It was saying something. It was coming closer. And though the words were still too far away to resolve, Zephyrine felt them anyway—in their teeth, in the banked ember under their skin, in the oath scar wrapped around their spine like a collar they'd forgotten they wore.

The ledger's final page lifted once in a breathless wind and settled flat, and across its bottom margin, fresh ink bloomed as if written by an invisible hand:

*His Majesty approaches. All assets will be presented for review.*

"Zephyrine," Sable whispered. "He's coming *here*."
