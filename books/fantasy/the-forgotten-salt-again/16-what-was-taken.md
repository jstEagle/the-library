## 16. What Was Taken

The stairs went up forever.

Zephyrine counted the turns to keep from counting their own heartbeats. Forty-one steps per turn, salt-veined stone slick underfoot, and above them—above everything—the thread. It had loosened when Sable burned herself against the oath's knot, but *loosened* was not *gone*. Zephyrine could feel it still, a filament of cold running up through the soles of their feet, through the marrow, into the place behind the breastbone where a swordspeaker's voice lived. Like a fishhook that had been backed out halfway and left sitting in the flesh.

Ilex climbed behind them, breathing hard, one hand on the wall. In the vaults below, the freed souls were still rising—a sound like distant rain on a tin roof, thousands of small bright things going up through stone—and the light they left behind was changing, the court's glittering corridors dimming by degrees as their currency of bottled glow drained away.

"They'll notice," Ilex said. "Down there. The ledgers, the wardens of the vaults. They'll notice the accounts emptying."

"Let them notice."

"That's not a plan, Ravensworth."

"It's not supposed to be." Zephyrine did not slow. "Plans are for people who have time."

Sable drifted at their shoulder, or near enough to it—she had grown strange since she burned herself, thinner in some places and brighter in others, like a candle guttering in two directions at once. When she spoke, her voice came from slightly too far away, as if she were speaking through the wall of another room.

"You're bleeding again," she said.

"I know."

"From where?"

"Everywhere the hook sits." Zephyrine flexed their right hand. The fingers answered, but slowly, reluctantly, like servants who had served a crueler master recently and expected him back. "It's letting go. That's what it feels like. Letting go is worse than holding."

They came out of the stairwell into a gallery Zephyrine remembered from the descent—or thought they remembered; memory down here was an untrustworthy servant. A long corridor arched with ribs of white stone, veins of soul-light running along the arches like luminous mortar. Half the veins had gone dark already. The other half flickered, uncertain, as if the mountain itself were trying to decide whether it was dying or waking.

And there, set into an alcove as casually as a man might hang his coat, stood a mirror.

Not glass. Zephyrine knew glass, sold hand-mirrors in Greyharbor with charms worked into the silvering. This was something else: a pane of still water standing upright, framed in black iron, and in it—

In it, Zephyrine saw themselves whole.

* * *

They stopped walking without deciding to. Ilex nearly walked into their back.

"What is it?"

"I don't know." But they did know, the way you know a door in a dream. The water in the frame lay perfectly flat, perfectly dark, and beneath its surface moved a figure: themselves, but not as they were now. This Zephyrine stood straighter. Wore the oath-sash of the crown's swordspeakers, storm-grey shot with gold. Had a scar over the left eyebrow that Zephyrine did not carry, because whatever wound had made it had been taken from them along with everything else.

Three years ago. Three years of face looking back at them out of three years ago.

"Don't," Sable said quietly.

"Sable—"

"Don't touch it. Please." She had come around in front of them, and though she had no body to bar the way, she arranged herself there anyway, a girl-shaped insistence. "That's a tithe-mirror. They keep them near the vaults. It shows you what was paid. Not what was lost—what was *paid*. There's a difference, and the difference is the trap."

"What difference?"

"The mirror shows the payment," Sable said. "It doesn't show the transaction. You look at yourself in there and you think: that's mine, give it back. And then you reach in. And reaching in—" her brightness shivered— "reaching in is how they collect the rest."

Ilex had gone very still. "You've seen someone reach in."

"I've seen three someones reach in." She did not look at him. "They're still in there. Look at the edges of the water. Look closely."

Zephyrine looked. At first the surface seemed only dark. Then, at the margins, where the water met the iron frame, they saw them: shapes pressed thin as flowers in a book. A man with a merchant's soft hands. A woman whose mouth was open in a shape that might have been singing or screaming. And a third, smaller, curled at the bottom corner like a child hiding under blankets.

All of them reaching. All of them caught mid-reach, forever.

Zephyrine stepped back. Their heart was slamming, but underneath the fear something else was assembling itself, piece by piece, with the terrible patience of a lockpick working in the dark.

"Sable," they said. "When I tried to break the oath. Three years ago. Tell me what actually happened."

She turned. Even bodiless, even burning herself away, she managed to look at them the way no one else ever had—as if Zephyrine were a person and not a weapon or a debt.

"You don't remember any of it?"

"I remember fragments. Rain. A table. My own voice saying words I didn't understand." Zephyrine's jaw tightened. "Vane told me—I heard him say it, before, when he wore my body like a glove—that he *bought* my memory. Paid fair price, he said. To whom? Who sells a person their own past?"

Sable was quiet for a long moment. The dark veins in the ceiling pulsed once, twice, like a slowing heart.

"He lied," she said finally. "Or rather—he told the truth in the shape most useful to him. Sit down, Zephyrine."

"I'm not sitting down."

"You should sit down for this." Her voice gentled. "Please. For this one thing, do as I ask."

Zephyrine sat. On the floor of a usurper's mountain, in a corridor lit by the last flickers of stolen light, the last oath-bound swordspeaker folded their legs beneath them like a child at a story, and waited to be told about themselves.

* * *

"Three years ago," Sable said, "you were in the capital. You'd been Vane's sword for four years by then—you swore to Aldous the Fourth, and when he died, the oath passed to whoever held the throne, which is the whole obscenity of it, and you knew that better than anyone. You'd felt it. Every order Vane gave, your voice carried it out. Every soul his agents collected, your blade escorted the wagons. You were the crown's proof that magic serves the throne, and you hated it, and the hating wasn't enough to stop it, because the oath doesn't care what you feel."

"I know how the oath works," Zephyrine said.

"You know how it works from inside the cage. You don't know what you did about it." Sable drifted closer. "There's a flaw in the binding. Old. Older than the mountain. The oath-takers built it in deliberately, ages back—not mercy, exactly. Insurance. Because a binding that cannot be broken is a binding that can be *stolen*, used by anyone who takes the throne, forever, and the first oath-takers feared that more than they feared disobedience. So they left a seam. One word. A breaking word. Spoken by a swordspeaker, freely, it unravels the oath root and branch—for every speaker alive, everywhere. Not just you. All of you, all at once."

Ilex made a low sound. "Then why does anyone still swear it?"

"Because of the price." Sable's light dimmed. "The word doesn't cost blood or years or gold. Those would be cheap. It costs the self. To break the bond between voice and king, you have to renounce the thing the bond was sworn *through*—your remembered self. Everything you are, given up, all at once. The oath holds your identity as collateral. Break it, and the collateral burns."

The corridor was very quiet. Somewhere far below, water dripped in a rhythm like a patient counting.

"So I tried," Zephyrine said. Their voice came out flat. "Three years ago. I found the word."

"You found the word. You climbed to the top of the Oathspire in the capital at night, in the rain—you always liked doing important things in the rain, you thought it made them honest—and you spoke it." Sable paused. "And you failed."

"Failed *how*? If the word works—"

"The word works. But it has to be spoken *freely*. Whole. Undivided. With nothing held back, nothing withheld, no part of you standing apart from the renunciation, keeping watch, keeping score." Her voice softened further. "And you couldn't do it, Zeph. You got to the edge of it—all of it, every memory lined up for the burning—and at the last moment, some part of you flinched. Some part of you wanted to live. Wanted to keep being *you*. And the oath felt that hesitation, and instead of breaking—"

"—it foreclosed," Zephyrine whispered.

The word arrived in their mouth from somewhere they did not recognize, and it was true, and knowing it was true was like swallowing ice.

"Yes," Sable said. "The oath took the payment anyway. Or rather—it took the *collateral*. Your memories weren't bought. They weren't sold. They were seized. Defaulted. You tried to burn the contract and the contract burned you first, took everything you were as penalty, and left you a shell with a sword-hand and a hum of power and no idea why the world felt like a house where all the furniture had been removed." She drifted lower, until her brightness was level with their eyes. "Vane found you on the Oathspire floor at dawn. Blank. Harmless. And he understood immediately what had happened, because Vane understands ledgers better than any living man. He couldn't use a broken oath—but an oath in *default*, an oath holding its debtor's entire self in escrow—"

"—that he could use," Zephyrine finished.

"He's kept your memories for three years. In the vaults. Bottled, like everything else he keeps. He tells himself—and tells the court—that he *purchased* them, because purchase implies legitimacy, implies contract, implies that somewhere there exists a document making him the rightful owner of you. But there is no document. There never was." Sable's voice hardened, and for a moment she sounded less like a ghost and more like the girl who had died selling her soul for medicine and had regretted nothing except the necessity. "He is not your buyer, Zephyrine. He is your *creditor*. And creditors can be robbed."

* * *

Zephyrine did not move for a long time.

Rage came first. It came the way floodwater comes—silently, then all at once, filling every channel of them, rising past the ribs, past the throat. Three years. Three years of selling luck-knots in a fog-bound city, of flinching from Wardens, of shrinking and shrinking and shrinking, believing themselves a coward, a deserter, a woman-shaped hole in the world who had run from her own history. And none of it had been choice. None of it had been weakness. They had fought. They had climbed a tower in the rain and put everything they were on the altar of freedom, and they had flinched—one human instant of wanting to live—and the machine had taken them for it, and the man who profited had spent three years telling the world, and telling *them*, that it had all been a fair transaction.

*I paid for it,* Vane had said, wearing their body, marching their legs. *Fair price.*

"No," Zephyrine said aloud.

Ilex glanced over. "No what?"

"He didn't buy me." Zephyrine rose. The rage was still there—it would always be there—but as they stood, as the blood moved, something happened to it. Rage is a fire, and fire needs fuel, and the fuel was running out even as the fire burned, because rage wants an enemy and what Zephyrine felt now was larger than an enemy. It was arithmetic. Cold, clean, merciless arithmetic. "He never owned it. He's been *holding stolen goods* for three years and calling it property. He let me believe I abandoned myself. He watched me hide in Greyharbor, watched me sell charms to fishermen, watched me flinch from my own reflection—and he called it mercy. Amnesty. He offered amnesty for my capture, Ilex. Amnesty. As if I were the criminal. As if the debt ran *from* me."

"And now?" Ilex asked quietly.

"Now I know the shape of the account." Zephyrine turned to the tithe-mirror. The three-year-old face still gazed out of the dark water, sashed and scarred and whole, waiting. "Here's what I understand that I didn't understand an hour ago. The memories aren't gone. They're *held*. Held in the vaults, in bottles, in his ledger. Which means they can be taken back. Not reached for—" they glanced at the thin pressed shapes at the mirror's edges, and their stomach turned— "not grabbed. Taken. By force. By right. By someone willing to walk into the deepest room in this mountain and take back what was stolen."

"The vaults are guarded," Ilex said.

"The vaults were full," Zephyrine corrected. "An hour ago they were full. Now they're draining. Every soul we freed downstairs was a guard of a kind, Ilex—every bottle of light was a brick in his wall. We've already started demolishing it. We just didn't know we were demolishing it." They looked at Sable. "Where. Where in the vaults. Which shelf holds a swordspeaker's defaulted self?"

Her brightness steadied. "You won't like the answer."

"I haven't liked any answer in three years. Say it anyway."

"He wears them." Sable's voice dropped. "The most valuable holdings, Vane keeps on his own person. Closest held. Your memories aren't in a vault, Zeph. They're in the signet ring he never takes off. He drinks from them, the way the court drinks bottled souls—little sips, whenever he wants to feel what it's like to be someone real."

The rage came back, and this time Zephyrine welcomed it, and this time it did not cloud anything. It clarified. It focused to a point, the way light focuses through salt-glass, and at the center of the point was a single image: a man's hand, a ring of black iron, and everything Zephyrine had ever been locked inside it like a fly in amber.

"Then it's simple," they said.

"Nothing about this is simple," Ilex said.

"It's simple the way war is simple. Someone has the ring. Someone has to take it off his finger." Zephyrine drew the sword—not the oath seizing their arm now, but their own hand, their own grip, the grip they had chosen on the stairs below—and looked down the long corridor toward the upper court, where the boots had descended, where the thread of the oath still trembled faint as a plucked string. "He's coming to finish this himself. Good. I'm done being finished by other people."

Above them, faint through miles of stone, the boots had stopped descending.

They had stopped—which meant he had arrived.
