## 18. Storming the Mountain

The horns began an hour before dawn, and Ilex knew them the way a man knows the voice of a debt collector at his door.

He stood on the shoulder of the pass called the Shepherd's Teeth, where the old salt road narrowed between walls of black stone, and he counted the fires below in the valley of the mountain's foot. Too many. He had known there would be too many, and still his hands shook. Below, arranged in ranks that caught the light of a thousand soul-lanterns, King Aldous Vane's army waited for the sun that would not rise—the stars were still gone, three nights now, and the sky hung overhead like a lid nailed shut.

"They're moving," said Brannoc, captain of the Greyharbor fisher-militia, a woman with rope-scarred hands and no business commanding anyone. She had four hundred people behind her: dockworkers, salt-cutters, two hedge-witches, a company of retired wardens who had come when they heard Ilex's name spoken without shame for the first time in years. "They'll be up the road by midmorning."

"Then we have until midmorning to become something worth remembering," Ilex said, and hated how steady his voice sounded, because inside him was nothing steady at all.

* * *

Zephyrine heard the horns from inside the mountain's roots, and understood them as a sentence being read aloud.

They stood with Sable in the last gallery before the court proper—a corridor of veined white stone where the walls sweated light, harvested souls pressed into the rock like fossils of fire. Sable drifted ahead, her edges fraying where she had burned herself loose from the oath's grip two nights past. She was thinner than a memory now. She pretended not to be.

"That sound," Zephyrine said. "That's Vane marching out."

"He's going up to meet them." Sable turned, and her face did the thing it always did—half a girl, half a wound. "Your fisherwoman. Your wardens. Everyone who came because of you."

"Not because of me." But it was a lie and they both knew it. The free cities had risen because word had run down the roads like water finding its level: *the last swordspeaker walks again; the usurper fears her.* They had come for a legend. The legend was currently standing in a dead girl's shadow, holding a sword she could not fully trust her own hands to wield.

"The pass will hold," Zephyrine said. "It has to hold long enough."

"And you go down alone."

"I go down alone." They touched the hilt at their hip—old bone and river-iron, their mentor's blade, the only inheritance that had never been sold. "Sable. If this goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"If it does, I want you to know—" The words snagged, the way they always snagged. Three years of missing memories stood between Zephyrine and every sentence that mattered. "You remember me better than I do. Whatever I was, whatever I forgot—I'm glad some part of me knew you."

Sable looked at them for a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes like weather over deep water.

"You knew my name," she said finally. "Before. You said it once, and I've been trying all this time to get you to say it again. Not 'ghost.' Not 'girl.' My name." Her voice cracked along its whole length. "Say it when you come back up. Promise me that much."

"I promise."

She believed them. That was the terrible thing—she believed them, and belief was the one currency the mountain had never learned to counterfeit.

Zephyrine went down.

* * *

On the pass, the first wave broke against the defenders like surf against a seawall built overnight by frightened hands.

Ilex had spent the night doing the only magic left to him: geometry. He had no wards strong enough to stop a soul-fed soldier—he had helped build those soldiers' power source, and he knew what they could shrug off—but stone did not care about souls. He collapsed the first switchback with charges of salt-blast, funneled the vanguard into a killing corridor, and put the fisher-militia behind barricades of sharpened pilings dragged up from Greyharbor's own harbor. Everything they fought with had been something else first. A city's junk, reforged into a wall.

The soul-fed came anyway.

They came wrong—not like men but like men *worn*, faces slack, eyes lit from within with a pale gold glow that leaked through the skin like candlelight through parchment. They did not flinch. They did not tire. When they fell, they fell empty, and the light guttered out of them, and Ilex had to look away from the first one because he recognized the boy's face from a refugee ledger he had signed three winters ago. *Family of four, seeking passage north.* Someone had found the other three too.

"Hold!" Brannoc's voice, raw as gull-cry. "Hold the teeth! Every minute is hers!"

Every minute is hers. That was the arithmetic Ilex had given them, and they had taken it up like a prayer: hold the pass, buy the swordspeaker time, and everything you lose buys something stolen back. It was a good lie. It was even mostly true.

By midmorning the second switchback was gone. By noon the barricades at the Teeth themselves were burning, and the defenders had fallen back to the last choke-point, and Ilex stood in the smoke doing sums he could not make come out kind. Four hundred had become perhaps two hundred and sixty. The hedge-witches were both dead, burned out feeding strength into the militia's blades. Brannoc had taken a spear through the meat of her shoulder and kept shouting.

And still the soul-fed climbed, patient as tide.

"We can't hold another hour," Brannoc said, pressing her arm against her ribs. "Tell me she's close. Lie if you have to."

"She's close," Ilex said, and did not lie, because he could feel it—a pressure in the stone under his boots, a wrongness rising from the mountain's heart like heat from a forge. Something down there was about to break. He had built enough wards in his life to know the feeling of one about to fail.

Or about to be broken. From inside.

* * *

The Court Beneath received Zephyrine the way a throat receives a swallowed thing.

They walked the great gallery alone, sword drawn, and the vaults rose around them on every side—shelves of bottled light climbing into darkness beyond sight, each flask a soul, each soul a small sun imprisoned. The court's courtiers had fled or hidden; the Master of Ledgers lay senseless where Zephyrine had left him, his precious archive breached, his ledgers scattered across the floor like the entrails of a dissected god.

Zephyrine had done that. An hour ago, with the flat of a blade and three years of rage. Now the rage was quieter, colder, more useful.

At the center of the vaults stood the throne of bottled light itself—Vane's seat, a chair built of ten thousand flasks mortared in gold, glowing with the combined stolen brightness of a nation's dead and sold. And upon it, waiting, sat King Aldous Vane.

He had not marched with his army after all. Or he had marched, and returned, or he had never truly left—down here, distance was a rumor. He wore plain black, no crown, and he smiled the smile of a man watching a debt walk through his door on its own legs.

"Swordspeaker," he said. "You've cost me a fortune."

"You'll want a receipt," Zephyrine said, and struck the nearest shelf.

The first flask shattered, and the soul inside went up like a released breath—light unspooling, expanding, *leaving*, pouring upward through the mountain's veins toward the sky it had been stolen from. Then the next. Zephyrine waded down the aisle of shelves breaking them two-handed, methodical as a reaper, and around them the vault began to sing—a sound like a choir inhaling all at once, ten thousand voices remembering they had throats.

Vane did not move from his throne. He simply spoke.

"*Zephyrine Ravensworth.*"

Not the name. Not yet. Just the shape of it, testing—and Zephyrine felt the oath stir in their blood like a hook testing a line. Their stride stuttered. Half a step lost. They gritted their teeth and broke another flask, and another, and the light was rising through the mountain now in rivers, and somewhere far above, they hoped, it was reaching the sky.

"You can't break what owns you," Vane said conversationally. "I've read the old texts. I bought the archivists who wrote them. The oath runs deeper than your bones—it runs in the *wording*. And wording, swordspeaker, is mine. I hold the crown. I hold the contract. I hold—"

"You hold paper," Zephyrine said, and drove their sword into the throne itself.

The blade sank into the mortar between two flasks, and the whole structure shuddered, and Vane rose from his seat for the first time, and his face finally stopped smiling.

* * *

Above, the sky tore open.

Brannoc saw it first—one seam of light splitting the starless dark directly above the mountain, thin as a knife cut in cloth. Then a second seam. Then the whole black lid of heaven began to crack along lines no map had ever drawn, and through the cracks came not stars but *souls*—ascending, streaming, thousands of points of light punching up through the broken ceiling of the world toward whatever waited beyond it.

The effect on the army below was immediate and obscene.

The soul-fed soldiers stopped mid-stride, all together, like puppets whose strings had been gathered into one hand. The golden light behind their eyes flickered—then *drained*, pulled upward out of them through their open mouths, their wide staring eyes, drawn home by the gravity of freedom. They dropped like cut wheat. Rank after rank, the army of Aldous Vane emptied itself onto the salt road, and what remained standing when the light finished leaving were just men—gaunt, hollowed, blinking in the sudden returning dark, most of them too far gone to even understand they had been freed.

"Hold," Brannoc whispered, though there was nothing left to hold against. Then louder, cracking: "Hold—they're falling—*gods*, look at the sky—"

The seams widened. Through them, faintly, impossibly, the first true stars began to show—old stars, patient stars, coming back to a sky that had been swept clean of them. The defenders of the pass stood among their dead and their enemies' dead and watched the heavens being handed back piece by piece, and Brannoc, who had never prayed in her life, found she was praying anyway, downward, into the mountain: *hurry, hurry, whoever you are down there, hurry.*

* * *

In the vaults, the throne was coming apart in Zephyrine's hands.

Flask by flask they tore it open, and Vane fought them now in earnest—with words first, the true name half-spoken twice, the oath wrenching Zephyrine's body against its own will until Sable's burn-loosening held, barely, a torn ligament of the soul stretched to its limit. Then with the court's remaining guards, hollow-eyed ledger-men with soul-blades, whom Zephyrine cut down almost without seeing them, because the whole world had narrowed to the throne and the king and the light pouring out of both.

"You don't understand what you're spending," Vane snarled, backed against the last intact section of his seat, and for the first time he sounded not like a king but like an accountant watching his books burn. "Do you know what I bought with these? Order. Borders. An *army*. You're giving it all away—to *them*—" he gestured at the ascending light with something like hatred, "—to ghosts and farmers and fishwives who couldn't govern a rowboat—"

"Yes," Zephyrine said, and broke the flask beside his head.

The light took him full in the face, and he screamed—not burned, nothing so merciful as burned, but *seen*, seen by a soul passing through on its way home, seen and known and dismissed, and he staggered back against the ruins of his throne clutching his face as though the light had shown him something in himself he could not survive knowing.

Zephyrine raised the sword over the last of the vaults—the deepest shelf, the oldest bottles, where the mentor's soul had waited three years in its narrow glass.

And the mountain shook.

Not from the battle. From below—from beneath the court, from depths even the ledgers had not charted, a sound rolled up through the roots of the world like a door being opened that had been sealed since before names. The remaining flasks rattled in their shelves. Dust of gold sifted from the dark overhead. Somewhere far down in the dark, something vast turned over in its sleep—or woke—or answered.

Vane lifted his bleeding face from his hands, and Zephyrine saw, with a lurch, that he was not surprised.

"You shouldn't have fed it so well," the king whispered, and began, incredibly, to laugh. "Three years of souls. Three years, straight down. You think I was building an army?" His laugh climbed toward the register of hysteria. "*I was paying rent.*"

The floor of the court split along a seam of ancient light, and the dark below opened like a mouth, and Zephyrine—sword still raised over the mentor's bottle, the breaking word still locked behind their teeth, Sable's name still owed—understood that the mountain had a bottom, and that they had just reached it.
