## 22. The Charm-Seller's New Sign

The sign above the shop was new, and that was the only thing about it Zephyrine was sure of.

They had painted it themselves, three coats of blue over the scorch marks the fire had left on the old boards, and lettered it in careful white: *Ravensworth Charms — Luck Knots, Warding Threads, Small Kindnesses.* They did not know why they had chosen the name Ravensworth. It had simply been there when they reached for one, the way a hand knows the shape of its own knife, and Ilex—who came by every few days with bread or salt or some excuse he never bothered to make convincing—had gone very still when he saw it, and then very carefully smiled.

"It suits you," he'd said, in the voice of a man agreeing with a ghost.

The shop stood on Cordwainer Lane, four doors down from where the fish market used to be. The market was a market again now; the gallows-frame the mob had built there in the bad autumn had been taken apart plank by plank, and a woman named Odile sold mussels from the spot where the charm-seller had died. Greyharbor had a genius for rebuilding without apologizing. The city wore its scars the way the harbor wore its fog: completely, and without comment.

Zephyrine opened the shutters each morning to the smell of brine and tar and the long gray light coming off the water, and each morning they performed the small liturgy of not knowing.

It was not so terrible, most days. That was the strange part—the part they could never explain to Ilex when his face did that thing it did, that folding-in around the eyes, whenever Zephyrine asked an innocent question like *what was my mother's name* or *did I always hum when I worked.* Not knowing was quiet. Not knowing was a room with clean walls. Whatever had lived in them before—and something had, clearly, because their hands knew knots their mind had never learned, because they woke some nights with a word half-formed on their tongue that made the candleflame lean toward them like a listener—that whatever had been heavy. You could tell by the way other people looked at you. Grief has a weight, and strangers feel it before you do.

So Zephyrine sold charms. Luck-knots mostly, braided from gray harbor-grass and red thread, and warding-threads for door lintels, and little stitched stars for children who were afraid of the dark—which was most of them, still, though the sky had been full of stars again for two seasons now. The first night the stars returned, the whole city had come out onto the quays and stood in silence looking up, and an old man had wept openly into his beer, and someone had started singing, badly, and no one had stopped him. Zephyrine remembered none of it. Ilex told them about it sometimes, when he thought Zephyrine wasn't watching his face.

"You cried," he said once. "You stood at the end of the pier with your head back and your arms out, and you cried like the sky owed you money."

"Doesn't sound like me."

"No," Ilex said, and looked into his cup. "I expect it doesn't."

* * *

The journal had been there when Zephyrine came back to themselves—or came *to*, rather, since *back* implied a direction, and Zephyrine had woken in a rented room above a chandler's shop with no past and a purse of coin and, on the desk, a leather-bound book with their name pressed into the cover in gold.

The first pages were in a hand that was not theirs. Zephyrine knew this the way they knew the name Ravensworth: absolutely, without evidence. The hand was slanted and quick, a little showy, the letters of someone who wrote fast because they had things to say and limited time to say them. And what the hand had written, page after page, was kindnesses.

*Ilex brought honeycakes today and pretended he hadn't. Eat them cold, they're better.*

*The neighbor boy fixed the shutter hinge and wouldn't take payment. Pay him in luck-knots when he isn't looking.*

*You laugh at your own jokes before the punchline. Don't stop. It's the best thing about you.*

*When you can't remember why you're sad, go down to the water. The water remembers for both of us.*

And, on the page before the handwriting changed—changed to Zephyrine's own current hand, shakier, newer, as if the pen itself had to relearn its trade:

*If you're reading this, then it worked, and I'm sorry, and don't you dare be sad about me. Be glad. Being glad is harder and it counts double.*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be. Zephyrine had read that last line perhaps four hundred times, and every time, somewhere behind their sternum, a warmth gathered—small, bright, girl-shaped—and lingered a moment near the door of their heart the way a guest lingers near a door when neither of you wants to say goodbye.

They did not know her name. That was the cruelest arithmetic of it: the journal was full of her, her voice in every line, her humor in every aside, and nowhere—not once—had she written her own name, as if she had known exactly what she was leaving and how much of it would survive, and had chosen, deliberately, to leave only the parts that would make Zephyrine kinder instead of sadder.

Except that Zephyrine was sad anyway. Glad and sad, both at once, which Ilex said was called being alive.

* * *

On market days Zephyrine took a tray of charms down to the fish stalls, because the journal said *sell where the people are, not where the rich are,* and because Odile saved them the good mussels and refused, always, to discuss why.

Today the fog was thin and high, a ceiling rather than a wall, and the sun got through in patches, laying gold coins across the wet cobbles. A child tugged Zephyrine's sleeve—a girl of six or seven with a gap in her smile and a stitched star already pinned inside her coat, frayed from love.

"Mistress Ravensworth. Mistress Ravensworth, my brother's boat goes out past the point tomorrow and my mam says the currents are wrong there."

"The currents are wrong everywhere," Zephyrine said. "That's what makes it the sea." But they knelt, and chose a luck-knot from the tray—one with an extra turn in the braid, though the child couldn't know why that one, and neither, honestly, could Zephyrine; their hands simply went to it—and pressed it into the small palm. "Tell him to tie it to the tiller. And tell him the knot doesn't keep him safe. He keeps himself safe. The knot just reminds him he's the kind of person who comes home."

The girl considered this with the gravity of a harbormaster. "Did somebody teach you that?"

Zephyrine opened their mouth. Closed it. Somewhere in the fog above, gulls argued over something dead.

"Yes," they said finally. "Somebody did. I just can't—" The warmth stirred behind their sternum, patient, familiar. "I can't thank her properly anymore. So I pass it along. That's the whole trick, if you want to know. Everything I know how to do is secondhand."

The girl nodded as though this were a perfectly ordinary commercial arrangement, paid with a bent copper, and ran off toward the herring barrels shouting for her brother.

Ilex was leaning against the mussel stall, watching, with the expression he wore when he thought he wasn't being observed—which was to say, the expression of a man carrying something breakable across ice. He was thinner than he had been, Zephyrine gathered from the journal's earlier entries; the months since the mountain had put some meat back on him, and some gray in his beard that hadn't been earned by age alone. He had gotten his ward-keeper's rank back, or something like it—the free cities had need of men who understood wards, especially ones who understood how they failed. He had refused a title. The journal said: *He turned down being Lord Warden of Greyharbor. Said walls are a young man's ambition. I think he means to stay where the charm-seller can see him.*

"I brought bread," Ilex said, holding up a loaf as evidence.

"You always bring bread."

"Bread is honest work. Unlike warding. Unlike—" He stopped himself, the old habit, the old flinch around whatever history sat between them that Zephyrine couldn't see. Then, deliberately, he set the flinch down. "There's news from upcountry. Thought you'd want it."

"Vane?"

"What's left of him. They found the court under the mountain empty—all those vaults, all that bottled light, gone out like lamps at dawn. His army walked off the field mid-battle, every soldier of them, just set down their weapons and started walking home. Some of them are still walking, they say. Some of them forgot how to stop." Ilex's jaw worked. "Vane himself they found sitting on his throne in the dark, old. Old overnight, older than any man's entitled to be. He keeps asking the guards who he is. Nobody tells him. Seems fitting, somehow, that he should have what you have."

Zephyrine was quiet a moment. The fog drifted. Down the lane, Odile was arguing cheerfully with a customer about the moral character of eels.

"Do you hate him?" Zephyrine asked. "Still?"

Ilex looked at them for a long moment. "I did. For years I carried it like a stone in my boot. And then I watched a woman give up everything she was to unmake him, and she didn't even get to know she'd done it, and I thought—hate's expensive, is all. I'd rather spend it on bread." He held out the loaf. "Here. Honest work."

Zephyrine took it. Their fingers brushed his, and he didn't pull away, and neither did they, and the moment stretched just long enough to be noticed by both of them and acknowledged by neither, which was, apparently, the arrangement.

"Ilex," Zephyrine said. "The person who wrote my journal. Did I love her?"

The question landed on him visibly, like weather. He looked out at the harbor, at the masts and the fog and the far gray line of the sea, and when he answered his voice was careful as a man crossing ice indeed.

"You loved her the way you loved everything," he said. "Completely, and without asking whether it was sensible. She'd have hated a smaller answer."

"She sounds like she hated a lot of things."

"She hated being pitied. She hated dishonest comfort. She hated—" and here Ilex smiled, unexpectedly, wholly, a smile Zephyrine had never seen on him and would remember forever without knowing why, "—she hated that you apologized for existing. She used to say, *tell her she takes up exactly enough room.*"

Behind Zephyrine's sternum, the warmth rose like a struck match.

They stood together in the market noise, the charm-seller and the ward-keeper, two people stitched together by a story one of them couldn't remember, and the loaf was warm between their hands, and somewhere overhead the thin fog tore open and let down a single bar of sunlight that found them both.

* * *

That evening, Zephyrine closed the shop early and climbed the stairs to the little room above it where they slept, and took down the journal, and read the last line again.

*Be glad. Being glad is harder and it counts double.*

"All right," Zephyrine said aloud, to the empty room, to the girl-shaped warmth that had followed them home from the market and now hovered near the window where the last light came through. "I'm glad. Are you counting?"

The warmth did not answer. It never did. But it pressed closer, the way a cat presses against a shin—not demanding, just confirming—and Zephyrine felt it settle against them with the particular weight of someone leaning their head on your shoulder because they trust you to hold still.

They dipped a pen. On a fresh page, beneath all the accumulated kindnesses of two hands, they wrote:

*A girl bought a luck-knot today for her brother's boat. I told her the knot doesn't keep anyone safe; people keep themselves safe. I know I learned that from someone. Whoever she was, she's still teaching me. I hope wherever she is, she knows she takes up exactly enough room.*

Then, because the pen felt insufficient, Zephyrine set it down and went to the window and opened it wide to the evening air—to the salt and the tar-smoke and the cries of the gulls and the lamplighters beginning their rounds below, and beyond the rooftops, the harbor, and beyond the harbor, the open sea, and above it all, coming out one by one like lamps lit in windows of an enormous house, the stars.

They stood there a long while, arms on the sill, head tipped back, letting the light land on a face that no longer knew what it had paid for all this, only that it had been paid, and gladly, by someone who loved them enough to leave instructions.

Behind them, in the warm dark of the little room, the journal lay open on the desk. And if the candleflame leaned toward it, gently, like a listener—if the pages stirred once, softly, as though a hand too light to move anything heavier than grief had turned toward the light and settled—well.

Greyharbor was a city that rebuilt without apologizing.

Its ghosts kept shops.
