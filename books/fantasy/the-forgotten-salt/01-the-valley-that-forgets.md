## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The salt came up white in the morning, the way it always did, and Odile Ravensworth came down to meet it with her sleeves rolled and her thoughts folded small.

She had learned, over ten years, to think in small shapes. The width of a rake. The depth of a pan. The number of steps between her cot and the drying sheds—two hundred and eleven, if she took the path past the brine pools rather than the one that skirted Maren's inn. Small shapes kept a mind tidy. A mind that wandered was a mind that remembered, and remembering was a luxury the valley did not sell.

She dragged her rake through the crystallizing pan with long, even strokes, and the salt sang under it—a thin mineral whisper, like sand shifting in an hourglass the size of the world. Around her the Salt Valley lay flat as a held breath. White pans to the east, white pans to the west, and beyond them the mountains rising gray and indifferent, their peaks lost in haze that never quite burned off. The air tasted of brine and iron. It got into everything: the seams of her boots, the grain of the wood, the creases of her palms. After a decade she no longer noticed it, the way a fish does not notice water.

That was the point of the place. She had chosen it for exactly this—the way the salt deadened everything, including the thing inside her she refused to name. Here, magic went to sleep. Here, she was only a woman with a rake and a bad knee and a cottage that leaked at the north corner when the rains came.

Here, she was nobody. And nobody, she had learned, was the safest person in the world to be.

* * *

By midmorning the sun had burned the haze off the eastern ridge, and Odile had filled three baskets of coarse salt and one of the finer flake that fetched a better price at market. Her shoulders ached in the familiar way, a good ache, an honest ache. She paused at the edge of the pan to drink from her waterskin and let herself do the one dangerous thing she permitted each day: she looked south.

The valley narrowed there into a throat of gray stone, and through it you could just see, on clear days, the far country she had come from—the green hills, the storm-light, the towers. Not today. Today the haze held, and the south was only a smudge, a rumor, a place that might not exist at all.

Good, she thought, and capped the skin, and turned her back on it. That was the ritual. Look once, refuse it, turn away. Ten years of looking once and turning away. She could have done it blindfolded. Sometimes she thought she did it blindfolded already, out of some deep self-protection her body understood better than her mind.

Her hands were steady. They were always steady now. In the first years they hadn't been—in those early months she'd woken screaming words in a language she didn't remember learning, and once she had shattered every glass in Maren's kitchen without touching them, and once—

No. She set the memory down the way she set down a heavy basket: carefully, in its place, and walked away from it.

The valley helped. That was the bargain she'd struck with it, though no one had offered terms aloud. The salt ate magic the way rust ate iron, and it ate other things too—sharp edges of recollection, the bright hot wire of fear. Her memories of before were still hers, technically. But they came to her wrapped in wool, muffled, drained of color and urgency, like letters read through frosted glass. She knew she had fled something. She knew she had been someone. The knowing sat in her like a stone at the bottom of a well—present, weighty, and too deep down to matter much.

Some days she hated the wool. Most days she was grateful for it. On the worst days, which were rarer now, she suspected the two feelings were the same feeling wearing different coats.

She shouldered her baskets and started toward the village.

* * *

Saltrest was less a village than an argument the valley was having with itself: eleven buildings clustered around a well that produced water so brackish even the goats complained, a chapel with no priest, and Maren's inn, which was the largest building and the warmest and the closest thing the settlement had to a heart.

The inn was called The Drowned Bell, for a bell that had supposedly sunk in the marshes a century ago and still rang, some said, on windless nights. Odile had never heard it. She had never heard anything in this valley that wasn't made by living hands or weather, and she liked it that way.

Maren was in the kitchen when Odile came through the back door, flour to her elbows, arguing with a loaf of bread as though it could hear reason.

"—and I'm telling you, if you rise any slower I'll sell you as a doorstop," Maren was saying. She was a broad woman built like a door herself, with forearms that could crack walnuts and a laugh that could crack them at forty paces. Her hair was going gray at the temples in streaks she refused to dye because, she claimed, it frightened off dishonest customers. "There you are. You look like a salt mound with legs."

"I feel like one."

"Sit. There's tea." Maren nodded at the table, where a chipped blue pot waited beside two cups—one plain, one painted with larkspurs, which was Odile's, because Maren had decided the matter eight years ago and would hear no appeals. "And don't tell me you can't stay. I can see your feet are lying to you about how busy you are."

Odile sat. This was another ritual, older than the morning one: the tea, the larkspur cup, the chair by the window where the light came in slantwise through salt-fogged glass. Maren poured without asking how she took it, because Maren had also decided that matter years ago—honey, always honey, "because you're sweet enough underneath all that crust, girl, whatever you say."

They talked about nothing. The price of flake salt, down again. Old Tam's cough, better. The peddler expected next week, the one who came twice a year with cloth and tinware and books gone soft with damp. Odile felt her chest loosen by degrees, the way it always did here, in this kitchen, in this hour. If peace had a location, it was this chair. If it had a sound, it was Maren complaining about bread.

"You're quiet today," Maren said, not looking up from her dough. "Quieter."

"I'm always quiet."

"You're quiet like a closed door today. Usually you're quiet like a shut window." Maren glanced up, flour on her cheekbone, eyes sharp and kind. "Something in the air this morning?"

"Just wind off the flats."

"Mm." Maren kneaded. The silence stretched, comfortable and watchful, the way their silences always were. Then, lightly, the way you might toss a scrap to a bird you didn't want to startle: "Ten years this autumn. You know that?"

Odile's cup stopped halfway to her mouth.

"I keep accounts," Maren said mildly. "It's what innkeepers do. You came in off the south road with dust in your hair and a look like the sky owed you money, and you paid for a week's lodging with a coin I've never seen before or since. And you stayed." She punched the dough down, gently, almost tenderly. "I'm not asking. I never ask. But I mark the day, is all. Some things ought to be marked."

"Some things are better buried," Odile said, before she could stop herself.

The words came out with an edge she hadn't intended, and she saw it land—saw Maren's hands still for half a beat in the flour before resuming. When Maren spoke again her voice was easy, but her eyes weren't. "Well. Buried things grow salt flowers in this valley, girl. Ask any harvester. You dig down far enough, everything comes up white."

"That's morbid."

"That's chemistry." Maren wiped her hands and sat across from her, uninvited, unhurried. "Drink your tea. Then go home and sleep, because you've got shadows under your eyes like bruises, and don't think I haven't noticed you've been sleeping badly for a fortnight. Bad dreams?"

"No."

"Liar."

"Yes," Odile admitted, and looked into her cup. The honey swirled there, golden and slow. "Dreams about... before. More than usual. Which makes no sense. Nothing's changed."

"Nothing ever changes here," Maren agreed. "That's either the valley's blessing or its curse, and I've never decided which." She reached over and squeezed Odile's wrist, brief and hard, her grip warm and strong as a hearthstone. "Whatever it is you're dreaming about—it's not here. Whatever it was, it can't follow you into a place like this. Salt sees to that."

Salt sees to that. Odile nodded and smiled and believed it the way you believe a rope bridge will hold: fully enough to cross, never enough to stop checking.

* * *

She left in the late afternoon, when the light went amber and long across the pans. At the door Maren pressed a heel of fresh bread into her hands and refused payment, and Odile performed the annual pantomime of protest that they both enjoyed and neither meant.

"Peddler comes Thursday," Maren called after her. "Bring me that list. And Odile—" Odile paused. Maren stood in her doorway, backlit, broad and solid and immovable as the mountains themselves. "If the dreams get worse, you'll tell me. Not what they're about. Just that they're worse. Deal?"

"Deal," Odile said.

She walked home along the pans with the bread warm against her ribs and the sun going down behind the western ridge, painting the salt fields pink and gold, beautiful as a lie. Two hundred and eleven steps past the brine pools. Her cottage sat where it always sat, small and crooked and hers, smoke-stained and salt-eaten and safe.

Safe. She turned the word over as she banked the fire and set the bread on the sill and checked the latch, then checked it again, then checked the latch on the back door, then stood a moment in the middle of her one room listening to the silence.

Nothing changed here. Nothing ever changed here. That was the whole architecture of her life: routines laid down like courses of brick, mortar made of habit, walls made of forgetting. She had built it stone by careful stone, and she maintained it daily, and it had held for ten years against everything the world could throw at it.

So it unsettled her, deeply and without explanation, when she found the book.

It was sitting on her table. She had not put it there.

It was a small volume, swollen with damp, its cover furred with white crystals of salt bloom—as if it had been carried through the valley for a very long time, or had been waiting here longer than she had. No note. No footprints in the fine salt dust on her floor. The fire crackled; the latch held; the evening light failed outside her window exactly as it should.

Odile stood very still, her heart beginning a slow, heavy drumbeat she recognized from ten years ago, from the life before the wool, from the person she had worked so hard to bury.

On the cover, in faded gilt letters worn nearly smooth, she could just make out a title.

*The Forgotten Salt.*

And beneath it, smaller, in ink that looked fresher than it had any right to be:

*For O.R. — who has exactly seven days.*
