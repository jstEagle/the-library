## 2. Ink Older Than Grief

The peddler came to the valley on the last day of the dry season, when the salt pans lay white and blinding as a field of fallen stars and the air itself tasted of old tears.

Odile saw him first from the ridgeline above her claim, a dark speck crawling along the trade road that wound down from the pass. She stood very still, harvest rake in hand, and watched him come with the particular attention she gave to anything that moved against the valley's grain. Salt-harvesters did not get visitors. The road existed mostly for the memory of traffic; three wagons a year passed through at most, bound for the lowland markets, and their drivers never lingered. Nobody lingered in the Valley That Forgets. The brine-choked air saw to that. Magic curdled here, thinned to nothing, and with it went whatever strange gravity drew wonder-seekers and wanderers toward trouble.

She told herself he was only a tinker. She told herself it all the way down the ridge, all the way through the midday meal of bread and brined fish at Maren's inn, all the way through the afternoon while the peddler's cart sat outside the inn like a patient animal and its owner drank Maren's weak cider and spun stories for the two other harvesters who had come in from the eastern pans.

Odile did not go inside. She told herself that too.

* * *

It was nearly dusk when she finally pushed open the inn door, and by then the common room had emptied except for Maren, who was stacking bowls with the unhurried economy of a woman who had run an inn for thirty years and would run it thirty more, and the peddler himself, who sat hunched over the last of his cider like a crow over carrion.

He was older than he'd looked from the ridge — or younger, it was hard to say. His face had the quality of something weathered past its own age, skin gone fine and creased as paper left out in rain. His coat was layered in pockets, each one bulging, and his eyes were quick and gray and missed nothing, which Odile disliked immediately and thoroughly.

"The salt-witch herself," he said, without being introduced.

"I'm not a witch." The words came out flat and automatic, worn smooth by years of use. "I'm a harvester."

"Of course you are." He smiled. It was not an unkind smile, which somehow made it worse. "I have books."

"I don't read."

Maren snorted from behind her bowls. "She reads everything. She reads the labels on my preserves. She read the tax notice I keep nailed over the privy."

"That's practical reading," Odile said. "It doesn't count."

The peddler was already digging in one of the coat's many pockets, and there was something in the way he moved — deliberate, almost ceremonial — that raised every hair on Odile's arms. He set a book on the table between them and pushed it across the wood with one finger.

It was a sorry thing. Water-damaged past saving, its cover swollen and warped, its spine cracked into soft segments like a dried riverbed. Mold bloomed along the edges in constellations of gray. Whatever it had once been — ledger, psalter, someone's treasured history — the water had taken it a long time ago.

"It's ruined," Odile said.

"It's cheap," the peddler said. "And it's yours. Consider it a gift, if you like. A gift travels better than a sale, where I'm going."

"Where are you going?"

"Away," he said simply, and something in the way he said it made her stop asking questions.

Maren leaned over Odile's shoulder, smelling of lye soap and woodsmoke. "Take it," she murmured. "Free is free. You can strip the pages for kindling."

But Odile's hand was already on the cover, and the moment her fingers touched the warped leather she felt it — faint as a heartbeat under a floorboard, but unmistakable. Ink. Old ink, soaked deep, holding a shape the water could not quite erase. There was writing in this book that wanted reading. She had spent ten years learning to feel the absence of magic the way sailors feel weather in their joints, and this book hummed against her palm like a struck bell fading.

She took it home.

* * *

Her cottage stood alone at the edge of the southern pans, a low stone thing she had built herself in the first year, when her hands were still shaking and every shadow in the valley seemed to have teeth. She lit the lamp, bolted the door out of habit rather than fear — nothing came to the valley, nothing ever came — and set the book on her table beneath the light.

Up close it was worse and better. Worse because the damage was total: pages fused into solid blocks, covers shedding flakes of black leather like frost. Better because now, in the lamplight, she could see what she had felt. Here and there across the ruined surface, lines of text had survived, ghost-pale, legible where the water had pooled and dried and pooled again. Not the whole book. Just fragments. As though the water had drowned everything except the words it had been sent to deliver.

She worked at it for an hour with a knife and warm water and a patience she didn't know she possessed, teasing apart the fused leaves near the back, where the binding had failed completely and a single signature hung loose. The paper there was different. Older. Thicker, ragged-edged, the color of old bone. And the ink on it was not faded at all. It sat on the page black and wet-looking, as fresh as if it had been written that morning.

The script was beautiful. That was the first thing she noticed, before she read a single word — a flowing, careful hand, each letter formed with the loving precision of someone who wrote slowly because they meant every stroke. Then the letters resolved into words, and the words resolved into a sentence, and the sentence stopped her heart.

*On the third night of the drying season, when the moon sets over the salt, the Ravensworth child comes down.*

Odile read it again. And again. Her lips moved without sound. The lamp flame leaned in a draft that wasn't there.

Ravensworth. No one in the valley knew that name. No one alive knew that name; she had buried it herself, a decade ago, in another country entirely, under another sky. She was Odile of the southern pans. She was the quiet woman who sold good salt and kept to herself. She was nobody's child.

But the page was not finished with her.

*She will burn these words,* the prophecy continued, in that same tender, terrible hand, *and they will find her skin instead. For ink written in truth cannot be drowned, cannot be burned, cannot be forgotten — only carried.*

Below that, the page changed character. The beautiful script gave way to something cramped, hurried, pressed hard enough into the paper to score the leaf beneath — as though the writer had been weeping, or running, or both.

*I am sorry. I did not know whose name they would make me write. If I had known — if I had known it would be you —*

The rest dissolved into a water-blurred smear, and then, near the bottom of the page, one final line surfaced, clear and cold as ice in a spring pond:

*The debt comes due at moonset. Tonight.*

Odile looked up at the window. Through the glass, over the white silence of the pans, the moon hung fat and yellow and already leaning toward the mountains in the west.

"No," she said aloud, to the room, to the book, to whoever had written those words a hundred years before she was born. "No, I don't accept this. I don't—"

Her hands were moving before her mind agreed to it. She swept the loose page off the table, crossed to the hearth in three strides, and fed it into the coals. The paper caught at once, curling, blackening, the beautiful script glowing orange at its edges before collapsing into ash. She stood over the fire and watched until the last fragment crumbled, breathing hard, hands fisted at her sides.

"There," she whispered. "There. It's done."

The words appeared on her left forearm a moment later.

They rose through the skin like bruises surfacing after a blow — no pain, only a strange warmth, a tingling pressure, as though her arm remembered something it had always known. She stared. The same flowing script, letter for letter, line for line, wrapped around her wrist and up toward her elbow in elegant black curves:

*On the third night of the drying season, when the moon sets over the salt, the Ravensworth child comes down.*

*She will burn these words, and they will find her skin instead.*

*I am sorry.*

*The debt comes due at moonset. Tonight.*

She scrubbed at it with pumice until her arm bled. The words stayed. She held her arm over the candle flame until she smelled her own scorching hair, and the words stayed, patient and lovely and utterly indifferent to her pain, while around them her skin reddened and blistered and healed itself faster than it should have, knitting closed within minutes as though even her body were no longer entirely hers to command.

Somewhere beyond the western mountains, the moon touched the peaks.

Odile sank onto the floor beside the hearth, back against the cold stone, and began, very quietly, to laugh — the high, unsteady laughter of a woman whose life has just been revealed to her as a scheduled event. Ten years. Ten years of routines, of careful distances, of salt in her lungs and magic dead in her blood, of believing she had escaped. And all of it — the valley, the forgetting, the safety — had been a stage set, dressed and waiting for tonight's performance. She had not been hiding. She had been *waiting*, like a parcel in a warehouse, like a coin in a vault, marked and dated and promised to someone else.

Her invisibility had been an illusion. Her whole life had been an appointment.

The moon slid lower. On her arm, the final line seemed to darken, the ink drinking the firelight.

And that was when she heard the footsteps on the path outside her door — slow, uneven, deliberate — and a voice, dry as old paper, speaking through the wood.

"You burned it," the voice said. "You always burn it. May I come in, Odile Ravensworth? We have very little time, and I have been waiting a hundred years to apologize."
