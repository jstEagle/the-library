## 3. The Scribe Who Would Not Die

Casimir had counted a hundred years by the comings and goings of the salt caravans.

He kept his vigil in the ruined scriptorium above the valley's northern rim, where the wind came through broken windows like a pen scratching endlessly across parchment. Once the room had belonged to the Order of the Ledgered Word—monks who believed every soul was an entry, every life a line of ink, every death a closing of accounts. He had been their finest hand. He had written prophecies so precise that kings had wept reading them, and one prophecy so terrible that the storm-court had decided its scribe should never be permitted to die and escape what he had made.

So they had killed him badly. Or rather, they had killed him just enough.

He sat now at the same desk he had occupied for a century, his hands resting on the wood as though he might at any moment take up a quill again. His skin was the color of old vellum, cool to the touch, faintly translucent at the wrists where the veins had gone dark and still. He did not breathe unless he remembered to, and mostly he forgot. Dust settled on him and he did not brush it away; it seemed honest, somehow, to let the years accumulate on the outside when they refused to pass through him.

Below, past the rim, the Salt Valley lay white and blind under the afternoon sun. Magic died there—that was its purpose, though only three people alive knew it had a purpose. The brine-choked air smothered spells the way wet ash smothers embers. To the storm-court it was a prison without walls, a place to bury inconvenient bloodlines where their power would starve quietly, generation after generation, until no one remembered there had ever been anything to starve.

And Odile Ravensworth lived down there, in the white blindness, harvesting salt with her bare hands.

Casimir watched her every day. He told himself it was duty—the bound witness must witness—but duty had never made his chest ache before he died, and it should not have been able to make anything ache now. Yet there it was, that dull persistent grief, like a splinter worked too deep to draw.

*She hangs her washing facing east,* he thought, for perhaps the ten-thousandth time. *She has never once looked north, toward me. She thinks the valley forgets her. She does not know I have spent a hundred years memorizing her.*

* * *

The binding was simple and absolute, as the cruelest things usually are.

When the storm-court had condemned him, they had not merely refused him death. They had written the refusal into him, in the old grammar, the grammar that predates mercy: *You will see. You will know. You will speak only when spoken to, and you will never intervene.* They had wanted a witness to the working of the prophecy—a living (or near enough) record of its unfolding, so that when the debt came due, someone would exist who could confirm the court's arithmetic. A ledger must have a keeper. They had simply neglected to consider that a hundred years is long enough for even a ledger-keeper to develop opinions.

He had tried, in the early decades, to warn her mother. He had stood at the edge of the valley with words burning in his throat, and the binding had sealed them behind his teeth like a dam holding back a flood. He had tried to write warnings and leave them where she might find them; the ink had faded from the page before her eyes could touch it. He had tried to move a single stone from her path, once, when she was nine and wandering too close to a sinkhole, and his hand had passed through the stone like smoke.

So he had learned the only rebellion left to a bound thing: patience, and precision, and the long slow cultivation of circumstance. He could not act. But he could arrange for a peddler to take a certain road. He could ensure a certain water-damaged book survived a certain flood. He could not put the prophecy into her hands—but he could make certain that when her hands reached for it, it would be there.

It had taken eleven years of small arrangements since she fled into the valley. Eleven years of nudging chance the way one nudges a boat toward shore with a single oar. And yesterday, at last, she had read her own name in ink older than her grief, and burned the page, and found the words rising on her skin instead.

Tonight she would come looking for answers. He knew it the way he knew the tides of the valley—not because the binding told him, but because he had studied her for a century, and Odile Ravensworth did not burn things and walk away. She burned things and then demanded to know why they wouldn't stay burned.

* * *

She came up the cliff path at dusk, moving carefully over the scree, her lantern held low so its light wouldn't spill against the sky. Even now, even coming to confront whatever had marked her, she moved like a woman who had practiced being unseen until it became her native language.

Casimir rose from his desk. His joints did not creak—they had gone past creaking into something more like the settling of a house—and he crossed the ruined nave to stand where she would see him when she reached the doorway. He had debated, all afternoon, how to greet her. Every greeting he composed felt like a theft: *I have known you since you were born. I have loved you since before you could speak. I wrote your tragedy in my own hand.*

In the end he chose the truth, or the smallest piece of it.

"You burned the page," he said, as her boots scraped the threshold. "It always comes back. Ink remembers what fire forgets."

She froze in the doorway, lantern raised, and the light caught her face—the sharp wary eyes, the mouth set in the particular line he had watched harden over eleven years of solitary labor. She looked older than twenty-eight. She looked like a woman who had been holding a door shut for a decade and had just heard a knock.

"You're dead," she said.

"Mostly." He inclined his head. "The storm-court found it useful to keep the scribe with the scripture. Death, for me, is a door they locked from the outside."

"The prophecy." Her voice didn't shake, which cost her something; he could see the cost in her knuckles, white around the lantern's handle. "Your handwriting. I checked the book twice. The ink that named me is the same ink that wrote the rest—a hundred years ago. Which means either you wrote my name before I existed, or—"

"Or your name was always going to be yours," Casimir said. "Written before your parents met. Before their parents met. Prophecy is not prediction, Odile Ravensworth. It is accounting. Somewhere, a debt was entered, and everything since has merely been the interest accruing."

"What debt?" She stepped inside. The lantern light swung across the ruined shelves, the rotted codices, the dust of a century. "Whose debt? Speak plainly, dead man, or I'll walk back down this mountain and salt my fields and pretend tonight never happened."

"You couldn't," he said gently. "That's rather the point."

Her jaw tightened. And then, because she was who she was, she set the lantern down on a shelf, deliberately, as if establishing terms, and folded her arms. "Then talk."

* * *

He told her most of it. Not all—he kept back the parts that were his alone to carry, the guilt that had no practical use and would only wound her—but most.

He told her about the Undercourt beneath the mountain, the court of salt and shadow that predated the storm-court, that predated the kingdoms, that dealt in the only currency that never spoiled: souls. He told her that the mountain's deep vaults were full of them, thousands upon thousands, entered in a ledger older than any nation, each one owned, each one owed. He told her that the storm-court itself paid tribute to the Undercourt, and that the price of that tribute was collected in the dark places where no one counted the missing.

"And mine?" she said. "Is mine in that ledger?"

"Yes."

"When?"

He hesitated. The binding stirred in him like a current under ice—it permitted truth; it had never required kindness. "Before your birth. Your father came to the mountain in the winter your mother carried you. There was a sickness in your bloodline—a storm-gift that surfaces once in four generations, wild and ruinous. The court had already drowned two children of your line for it. Your father bought your exile instead. The valley, the deadening air, a life below their notice. In exchange—" he met her eyes, and did not look away, because she deserved to be looked at while she heard this— "he signed over the soul you would be born with. Collateral, held against the day the gift matured. That day is three nights hence. Moonset."

The silence that followed was very long. Outside, the wind worried at the broken windows.

"My whole life," she said finally, and her voice was terribly quiet, "was a payment plan."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't." The word cracked like a whip. "Don't you dare be sorry. You *wrote* it. You said so—the scribe with the scripture. You sat here with your fine monk's hands and you wrote the date of my damnation a hundred years before I was born, and then you *watched*. For eleven years you watched me haul salt and mend nets and call Maren my friend, and you never—" She stopped. Breathed. He watched her rebuild herself out of fury, plank by plank, the way she rebuilt everything. "Why am I even talking to you? Why shouldn't I put a knife in whatever passes for your heart?"

"Because you can't," Casimir said. "And because I cannot help you. Either fact would spare us both this conversation, if either of them were true. But they aren't."

"What do you mean, you can't help me? You're bound—I understand that much. Bound to witness, never intervene. So why summon me here? Why mark me? What good is a prophet who can't lift a finger?"

"Because the binding forbids me to *act*," Casimir said. "It says nothing of what I may teach. And it says nothing—" he paused, choosing the words with a scribe's care, knowing they would change everything— "of what another may do with what I teach. The court bound my hands, Odile. They never thought to bind the reader."

She stared at him. The lantern flame guttered in a draft, throwing his shadow huge and wrong against the far wall—a shadow with too many angles, the shadow of something that had been a man for a very long time ago.

"You want me to steal myself back," she said slowly.

"I want you to know that it can be done. Whether you do it is yours to decide. That, at least, they left unwritten." He almost smiled; it was an expression his face had to remember how to make, like a word in a language he'd stopped speaking. "There is a road beneath the mountain. There are tolls along it, and courts, and a queen who keeps your soul in a vault beside ten thousand others. The descent takes three days. Moonset on the third night is when the debt matures—and moonset is also, if the old texts are true, the one hour when the vaults can be opened from the inside."

"And the tolls," Odile said. Her eyes had gone narrow and bright, the brightness of a blade being drawn. "What do they cost?"

"More than it seems," Casimir said. "Always more than it seems. That is the first law of the mountain, and the last."

She was silent a moment, looking at him—at the vellum skin, the still chest, the dust of a century—and he endured her looking, because he had earned the enduring.

"If I go down there," she said, "you're coming with me. Not because I trust you. Because you wrote the map, and I don't read dead languages."

"It will cost you," he warned. "Traveling with me. There are doors below that open only for the quick and the dead together. The mountain will notice us both, and the mountain takes notice personally."

"Everything costs," Odile Ravensworth said, picking up her lantern. "I've been paying since before I was born. I'd like to see the receipt."

Casimir bowed his head—the deepest bow his ruined body could manage, the bow of a scribe surrendering his finest and worst work to be corrected. And as he straightened, he felt it: the binding shifting within him like a great wheel beginning, after a hundred years, to turn. The court had bound him to witness the prophecy's unfolding.

They had never imagined he would be walking inside it.

"Then come," he said, and took up the lantern she had brought, because his own had gone out decades ago. "We descend tonight. And Odile—when we reach the first gate, whatever they ask of you, ask me first. Promise me that much."

"Why?"

"Because," said the scribe who would not die, "some debts I intend to pay myself."
