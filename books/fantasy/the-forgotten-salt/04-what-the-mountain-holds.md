## 4. What the Mountain Holds

The fire in Maren's back room had burned down to a red heart by the time Casimir finished speaking, and Odile wished he would stop. She wished it the way a drowning woman wishes for the surface—instinctively, desperately, without any real belief that wishing would save her.

He had told it plainly, which was the cruelest part. No softening, no ceremony. He sat across from her with his ink-stained fingers laced together on the table between them, and his voice had the flat patience of a man who had rehearsed this confession for a hundred years and never once been allowed to deliver it.

"Your parents came to the Undercourt the winter before you were born," he said. "Your mother carried the bargain herself. That was unusual. Most send brokers. Most can't bear to look at what they're buying."

Odile's hands were wrapped around a cup of tea that had long gone cold. She watched the surface of it, the way the last heat made it tremble faintly, and she thought: *I will not cry in front of him. I will not give him that.*

"What were they buying," she said. It was not quite a question.

"Exile." He said the word gently, as if setting down something breakable. "The Salt Valley is not an accident of geography, Odile. It was made. Carved out of the world's magic like a wound carved out of flesh, and sealed with brine so nothing inside it could be found. Your parents asked the Salt Queen to hide you where no storm could reach you, no scrying could mark you, no court—not hers, not any other—could claim you."

"And she agreed."

"For a price."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, Maren's floorboards creaked as she moved through the inn, closing shutters against the night wind. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of the life Odile had built, plank by plank, harvest by harvest, out of salt and silence and the careful daily work of not remembering.

"Say it," Odile said.

Casimir's eyes—gray as slate, gray as the ledger pages she'd seen in her dreams since the peddler's book—did not flinch from hers. "They sold your soul. Not its use. Not a tithe of it. The whole of it, root and branch, into the Queen's keeping. You have lived these ten years as a body walking around an absence. The valley's dead air only half explains why your magic sleeps. The other half is that there was nothing left in you to wake."

* * *

She had imagined, in the first wild minutes after he said it, that she would feel grief. She had braced for grief the way one braces for cold water.

What came instead was fury, and it arrived not all at once but in layers, like frost climbing a windowpane.

Her mother's hands. That was the first layer. Her mother's hands, cracked from kneading bread in their old kitchen in the storm-court's outer provinces, pressing a charm into Odile's palm the night they sent her away with the caravan. *Wear this always,* she had said. *It will keep you.* And Odile had worn it until the cord rotted, and then kept the charm in a tin box under her bed for six more years, and it had never once occurred to her that the charm was not protection. It was a receipt.

"They didn't do it to save me," she heard herself say. Her voice sounded strange, scraped thin. "Don't tell me they did it to save me. If they wanted to save me they would have run with me. They would have crossed the sea. They sold me because running was expensive and I was—"

"Do not finish that sentence." For the first time, something moved behind Casimir's stillness—a crack in the marble, quickly papered over. "I knew your mother. I wrote the contract while she stood at my desk. Whatever else she was, whatever you are entitled to feel about her, she wept through every clause. She made them write in a mercy clause. Did you know that? A rider, unenforceable, worthless in law—and she insisted on it anyway. *When the debt comes due,* she said, *let her be given a chance. Let her be allowed to fight for it.* The Queen laughed at her. But she signed it, because the Queen finds hope entertaining. She keeps it like a spice."

"A chance." Odile laughed too, and hated the sound. "Three days. Moonset three days from tonight. That's my chance. That's the mercy clause. A countdown."

"The debt matures at moonset on the third night. After that, the Queen collects, and no rider, no bargain, no god in the deep places can unmake what's collected. Before it—" he spread his ink-dark hands—"before it, everything is negotiable. Everything has a price. That is the nature of the mountain. It runs on debt the way a heart runs on blood."

"And mine has been running on borrowed time since before I drew breath."

"Yes."

She stood up so fast the chair went over backward. The clatter of it was enormous in the small room, and she was distantly aware that Maren would hear, would come to the door, would see her guest standing white-knuckled over a fallen chair and her strange pale visitor sitting unmoved as a gravestone. She did not care. She walked to the window instead, because if she did not put her body somewhere, she was going to put her fists through the wall, and the wall belonged to Maren, and Maren had never lied to her about anything.

Outside, the valley lay silvered under a fat rising moon—the moon that would wane, wane, wane, and set on the third night full of her name. The salt flats stretched to the horizon, white and patient, and beyond them the mountains stood black against the stars, and somewhere beneath those mountains, in courts she had spent ten years telling herself were fairy stories, sat a ledger with her soul written in it like a line item. *One Ravensworth, Odile. Maturing.* She thought of the harvest ledgers in Maren's back office, the neat columns of sacks weighed and owed, and felt sick.

Ten years. Ten years of rationing her own heart, of sleeping with her back to the wall, of learning the taste of brine and calling it peace. She had thought she was hiding. All that time she had been *stored*. Kept like grain in a dark barn, like coin in a vault, growing toward maturity. The valley hadn't forgotten her. The valley had been her packaging.

"I burned the page," she said to the glass. Her breath fogged it. "In the book. I burned the prophecy and it came back on my skin, and I thought—that was the worst thing. I thought the worst thing was being written. Being fated." She turned. "But it isn't, is it. The worst thing is that I was *sold*, and everyone who ever told me they loved me was holding the bill of sale."

Casimir rose too, slowly, the way old things move even when they wear young faces. "Your parents are dead, Odile. They paid more than you know. The court took its interest from them for years before the fever took them—small payments, memories mostly, years off their lives. Your father forgot how to whistle. Your mother forgot the smell of rain. I watched them pay, and I wrote it all down, because witnessing is the only thing I am permitted to do." His voice roughened on the last word, ground against something buried. "I am not asking you to forgive them. I am asking you to aim your anger upward. At the mountain. Where it belongs."

* * *

They talked until the candle drowned in its own wax.

He told her about the descent: the Tollgate market where travelers paid in memories, the courts stacked beneath the mountain like stones in a well, each one colder and older than the last. He told her the Queen's true name, or the shape of it—he said names like hers could not be spoken twice in the same century without cost. He told her that souls in the vaults were not destroyed but *used*, lent out, invested, that the Queen's whole dominion was built on the interest of other people's selves.

"And yours," he said, "is the largest deposit the mountain has held in a hundred years. A storm-caller's soul, untouched, undiluted, maturing in the dark. When the Queen collects you, she will not consume you. She will *become* you, in part, forever. Every storm you might ever have called will answer her instead."

Odile looked at her own hands in the guttering light. Hands that had hauled salt rakes for a decade, calloused, competent, ordinary. Somewhere underneath the skin, according to a scribe who could not die and a queen who could not be satisfied, there was a thing that called storms.

"If I go down," she said slowly, working it through like a knot, "and moonset hasn't come yet—then the debt is still mine. Still *in* me. Which means I'm carrying the most valuable thing in the mountain straight through its front gate."

Casimir went very still. She was beginning to learn his stillnesses; this one was alarm.

"That is either very brave," he said, "or exactly what the Queen is hoping for. She wrote the mercy clause, Odile. She let your mother's hope stand because hope walks debts willingly through her door. No one has ever stolen anything from the Undercourt. But people *lose* things there constantly. Willingly. Piece by piece, toll by toll, trial by trial, until they arrive at the bottom with nothing left to bargain and thank her for the privilege."

"Then I'll arrive with nothing," Odile said, "and bargain with empty hands, and it will still be more than she expects, because she'll be expecting someone who wants to survive." She picked up the fallen chair and set it right, deliberately, the way she did everything in this valley—careful, grounded, both feet on the salt. "I don't want to survive, Casimir. I want myself back. There's a difference, and I think you understand it better than anyone alive or otherwise."

Something flickered across his face—grief, or gratitude, or the ghost of both—and he bowed his head the smallest degree.

"Then we go down," he said. "Tomorrow, before the market at the Tollgate opens, while the border is thin. There is a road under the salt flats that only opens for those the mountain already owns." He met her eyes. "You should know the last thing before we sleep. The tolls are not paid in coin. The mountain takes payment in what you carry here." He touched his own chest, briefly. "And it always takes more than it says it will."

Odile looked out the window one last time—at the moon already leaning toward its waning, at the black shoulder of the mountain holding up the stars, at the valley that had kept her like a jewel in a locked box.

"Let it take," she said. "I've been paying my whole life. It's time I found out what I bought."

Above the mountains, the moon began, imperceptibly, to set.
