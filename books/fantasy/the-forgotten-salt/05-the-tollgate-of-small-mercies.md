## 5. The Tollgate of Small Mercies

The road down from the valley did not exist until you needed it.

Odile had lived ten years within a day's walk of the mountain and had never once seen the path that now unspooled before her, pale as a scar through the salt flats. It appeared only when the light failed—when the sun dropped behind the western ridges and the brine-choked dusk made the world honest. Casimir walked beside her, his footsteps making no sound at all, which she was learning not to resent, and the mountain rose ahead of them like a held breath.

"You'll want to decide what you're willing to lose," he said, "before we reach the gate."

"I've already lost everything worth losing."

He glanced at her then, and there was something in his face she couldn't read—pity, perhaps, or the particular grief of a man who has watched a hundred years of travelers say exactly that and be proven wrong. "No one has ever lost everything worth losing," he said quietly. "That's what makes the toll cruel."

The gate announced itself first by sound: a low murmur, like wind through reeds, that resolved as they drew closer into voices. Dozens of voices. Hundreds. Then the market opened out of the dark like a wound in the hillside—a crescent of stalls and lanterns carved into the mountain's root, lit by lamps that burned without flame, their light the color of drowned things. Travelers moved between them in slow procession: pilgrims in road-dusted gray, merchants with guarded eyes, a woman carrying a child who could not have been more than four and whose small hand clutched something invisible to her chest.

And everywhere, the stalls themselves sold nothing. That was the horror of it, Odile realized as they passed the first row. The merchants sat behind empty tables, behind scales of black iron and brass, behind little velvet cushions where memories were laid out like jewels for inspection. She saw a young man hold his cupped hands over a cushion, and in them shimmered something faint and golden—the shape of a summer afternoon, though she couldn't have said how she knew—and the merchant across from him weighed it with a small stone weight and shook his head.

"Too thin," the merchant said. "Childhood summers are common as gravel. Everyone thinks theirs is precious."

"What do they want?" Odile asked. Her voice came out smaller than she intended.

Casimir didn't answer immediately. He was watching the woman with the child, who had stopped at a stall near the gate's far arch and was speaking to its keeper in low, urgent tones. When he finally spoke, his voice had gone flat, careful, the voice of a man reciting something he'd memorized to keep it from hurting.

"The Undercourt doesn't take coin. Coin is only a promise, and promises can be broken. It takes what cannot be taken back." He turned to face her fully. "Memories. Not all of them—only what the tollkeeper names for you. Each traveler is assessed. Each is asked for the thing that will hurt most."

"That's monstrous."

"It's efficient." His mouth twisted. "The Queen believes pain is the truest currency. A memory given freely hurts; a memory surrendered under compulsion is merely theft, and theft has no weight on her scales." He paused. "You will be asked for something, Odile. And when they name it, you may refuse—but refusal means turning back, and turning back means the debt comes due at moonset anyway."

Ahead of them, the woman with the child finished her bargaining. Odile watched her press both hands flat against the stall's counter, watched her shoulders bow, watched the tollkeeper reach across and touch her brow with two fingers—and then the woman straightened, blinking, and looked around the market with the mild, pleasant confusion of someone who has forgotten why she came. The child tugged her sleeve. The woman smiled down at the child with perfect, vacant love, and Odile felt something in her chest turn over and go cold.

"She gave up—" Odile started.

"The child's father," Casimir said. "I'd guess. She kept the child. The Court always leaves you the living."

They joined the queue at the great arch, and Odile used the waiting to study the tollkeeper. It was not a person, or not only one. The figure behind the central stall shifted as she watched—now an old man with ink-stained fingers, now a woman with river-gray hair, now something younger and sexless with eyes like polished slate. Its face was every face that had ever paid here, worn briefly and set aside.

When their turn came, the tollkeeper looked at Odile for a long moment, and its borrowed features settled into a shape she almost recognized—an old woman, kind-eyed, with flour on her apron—and Odile's stomach dropped, because she knew that face, had known it in another life, in the years before the valley.

"No," the tollkeeper said, in a voice like pages turning. "Not that one. Interesting." The slate eyes flicked to Casimir, lingered, and something like amusement moved through them. "The scribe returns. Still witnessing. Still unable to lift a finger." Then back to Odile, and the kindness drained out of the borrowed face entirely. "For you, traveler: your home. The house you grew up in. Every room of it, every season, every voice that ever called your name from its doorway. The Court finds this memory... heavy. Heavy things make good ballast."

Odile's hands went cold. "That's all I have left of them."

"Yes," said the tollkeeper, gently, horribly. "That is why it weighs so much."

She thought of the kitchen with its crooked shutters. Of her mother singing off-key. Of her father's boots by the door, salt-crusted, always salt-crusted even then. Ten years she had carried those rooms inside her, and they were the last proof that she had ever been loved by anyone, anywhere, before the storm-court branded her and the whole world turned its face away.

If she gave them up, she would be a woman with no origin. A creature assembled from exile and fear.

"If I refuse?"

"Then you keep your grief," the tollkeeper said, "and lose everything else."

* * *

Casimir had stood in this line a hundred times.

He had watched farmers surrender their wedding days and soldiers surrender their moments of mercy and lovers surrender each other, and in a hundred years he had never once been able to intervene—not when the prophecy bound him to witness, not when the Queen's law stitched his tongue, not when the child with the invisible treasure reached the front of the queue and he had wanted, with a violence that surprised even him after all these centuries, to burn the whole market to ash.

Witness. Never intervene. Those had been the terms of his undeath, the price of the pen that would not run dry in his hand. For a century he had told himself it was bearable, because bearing witness was a kind of service, because someday the ledger would be balanced, because—

Because he had written her name into the book before she was born, and every hour since had been interest accruing on that original sin.

Now Odile stood before the tollkeeper with her chin lifted and her hands trembling at her sides, and Casimir felt the old binding settle over him like a wet cloak. He could not pay her toll openly. He could not speak for her. He could not so much as suggest.

But the binding said nothing about what a man might do while no one was watching.

While the tollkeeper's attention fixed on Odile—while it named her home, her mother's voice, her father's boots—Casimir let his hand drift to the counter's edge, where a second scale waited, smaller, older, half-hidden beneath a cloth. The tollkeeper's private scale. The one it used to weigh payments offered on behalf of others, a service so rare most travelers never learned it existed.

He laid his palm upon it.

*I offer,* he thought, shaping the words the way one shapes a signature. *In place of hers.*

The scale drank from him greedily. He had wondered, idly, over the years, what an undead man's memories weighed—whether a hundred years of watching counted for more than a single mortal lifetime of living. Now he learned. The tollkeeper's eyes widened, just slightly, and the market seemed to lean toward him, and Casimir fed the scale everything it asked for: his mother's garden, gone three centuries. The first manuscript he ever copied, and the pride of his twenty-year-old hands. The face of the girl he'd loved before the Queen found him, before the pen, before all of it—he gave that up last and longest, and it went out of him like blood from a wound, warm and unstoppable, and when it was done he could not remember her name, only that there had been a name, and that losing it had cost him something he would spend eternity measuring.

The tollkeeper withdrew its fingers from Odile's brow without touching her.

"Your toll," it said, "has been paid."

Odile stared. "I haven't given you anything."

"Someone else has." The slate eyes slid past her, to Casimir, and there was something almost like respect in them, which was worse than contempt. "A rare generosity. Rarer than you know, traveler. Payments made in secret carry double weight on the Queen's scales—she counts hidden sacrifice above all virtues, being incapable of it herself." The tollkeeper gestured toward the arch. "Pass. Both of you. And scribe—" its borrowed face flickered, became for one instant the girl whose name he no longer remembered, "—you are lighter now. Isn't it strange, how little remains?"

They passed under the arch together, into the throat of the mountain, and Odile walked in silence for a long time. Casimir kept his eyes forward. He had gotten good, over the centuries, at keeping his eyes forward.

Finally she stopped. Turned. Looked at him with an expression he had no defense against—not gratitude, not suspicion, but the terrible arithmetic of a woman recalculating everything she believed about a man.

"My toll was paid," she said. "By you."

"The binding forbids me to intervene. It doesn't forbid—" 

"Don't." Her voice cracked on the word. "Don't explain it away. What did it cost you? What did you give them?"

"Nothing that matters."

"Liar." She stepped closer, and in the drowned-colored light of the passage her eyes were bright and furious and wet. "Nothing that matters doesn't leave a man looking like that. Nothing that matters doesn't—" She stopped. Breathed. When she spoke again it was barely above a whisper. "Why? You wrote my name in that book. You doomed me before I drew breath. Why would you pay for me now?"

And Casimir, who had rehearsed a hundred answers across a hundred years, found he had none that weren't lies, and so he gave her the truth, raw and unpolished:

"Because I have watched ten thousand people pay that toll, and I have never once been able to stop it, and I could not watch it happen to you." His voice failed and steadied. "Because the debt is mine, Odile. The ink is mine. Whatever the mountain takes from you, it takes from my hand first."

Above them, deep in the rock, something vast exhaled—a sound like a ledger closing, like a page turning toward the entry that bore her name. The passage ahead sloped down into darkness, and from that darkness came music: faint, sweet, wrong, the sound of a court tuning itself for moonset.

Odile looked at the dark. Then at him. Then she took the first step downward, and said, without turning:

"When this is over, you'll tell me what you gave them. All of it. That's not a request, Casimir. That's a toll of your own."

And somewhere below, in a vault of salt and bone, something that had been sleeping for a hundred years opened its eyes, and began, softly, to count.
