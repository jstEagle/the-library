## 6. Court of Brine and Bone

Casimir had walked into courts before. He had knelt in the storm-court's hall of living lightning, had bowed his head before kings whose crowns were woven from weather, and he had done it all with a scribe's ink-stained hands folded and a scribe's heart beating steady behind his ribs. That was a hundred years ago, when his heart still beat.

Nothing had prepared him for the way the Undercourt announced itself.

They felt it first as a change in the air—a thickening, as though the dark itself were turning to look at them. The tunnel they had followed for two days narrowed, then opened without warning, and Casimir heard Odile catch her breath beside him, a small sharp sound in all that vastness.

The cavern was a cathedral built by something that had never seen a cathedral and only been told about them secondhand. Stalactites hung like inverted spires, each one crusted with salt that caught the light of veins of luminous brine running through the walls, so that the whole chamber glowed a bruised silver-green, the color of deep water at dusk. And rising from the center of it, tier upon tier, stood the court itself: terraces of white salt carved into galleries and thrones and colonnades, all of it glittering, all of it silent.

Silent except for the humming.

It came from everywhere and nowhere, a low chord sustained by ten thousand throats. Casimir knew what made it. He had written about them often enough—the ledger-souls, the harvested dead, the spirits bound into service until their essence wore thin as old vellum. They lined the walls in niches like reliquaries, each one a faint shape of light pressed behind a pane of salt, and each one humming its single note in the great chord that kept the mountain breathing.

"Odile." He said her name quietly, because there were things you did not say loudly here. "Whatever you see—whatever is offered to you—remember that nothing in this place is given. Everything is lent."

She didn't answer. She was staring up at the niches, and her face in the brine-light was the face of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't want to finish. Counting. He could see her counting the lights.

"How many?" she asked.

"More than the ledger can hold," he said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

They descended the terraces under escort of the Queen's ushers—tall figures of compacted salt and shadow, faceless but for a seam of light where a mouth might be, walking with the terrible patience of things that had never once needed to hurry. Casimir walked half a step behind Odile, as he had for a hundred years, watching. It was the only duty left to him, witnessing, and he performed it now with the desperation of a man who knew he might be forced to watch her be unmade.

The Salt Queen received them on the lowest terrace, where a pool of undisturbed brine lay like a mirror set into the floor.

She rose out of it.

That was the only way Casimir could describe it, though he had watched it happen twice before in the long centuries of his death. The brine gathered itself upward, drop by silver drop, building a woman out of water and salt and the memory of both—a tall figure in robes that moved like tide, her skin the pale gray of pearl, her hair coiled and pinned with needles of crystal. Her eyes were the color of the pool at its deepest point, which is to say they were the color of not being able to see the bottom.

"Odile Ravensworth." Her voice was the hum given shape, every soul in the wall resonating behind her words. "A century I have waited for this appointment. Your parents were punctual people. You are merely late."

"You have my soul," Odile said. Her voice held. Casimir marveled at how it held. "I've come to collect."

The Queen smiled, and it was not an unkind smile, which was somehow worse. Cruelty Casimir understood; he had catalogued its every grammar in a century of writing other people's bargains. But kindness from power—that was the dialect he feared most, because it was the one debtors never saw coming.

"Collect," the Queen repeated, tasting the word. She drifted closer, robes whispering against the salt floor. "Do you know what I am, child? Everyone assumes I am a tyrant. A hoarder. Some dragon of the deep places sitting on a pile of glowing coins." She gestured, one elegant hand, at the walls of humming light. "But look at them. Look closely. When did any of them last feel fear? Pain? The betrayal of a body failing, or a lover leaving, or time taking what time always takes?" Her head tilted. "I keep them safe. I keep them *paid*. Every soul in my ledger earns interest. Can your valley say the same?"

"They're in cages," Odile said.

"They're in *vaults*." The Queen said it gently, correcting a child's pronunciation. "And you—you above all should understand. You spent ten years in a valley that deadens magic, hiding from a court that wanted you dead. Was that freedom? Or was it simply a smaller vault, with better light?" She extended her hand, palm up, and in it lay a single grain of salt, glowing softly. "Your soul, Odile Ravensworth. Sold before your first breath. Matured tonight at moonset. I could hand it back to you, and you could walk up into your grey little valley and harvest your grey little salt until your grey little heart stops. Or—"

* * *

"—or," the Queen said, and the whole court leaned in, ten thousand souls shifting their note by a fraction, "you could stay."

Casimir's dead heart clenched around nothing. Here it was. Here was the thing he had dreaded since the peddler's cart, since the prophecy, since the first night he had watched her burn a page and wear its words instead.

"I am old," the Queen said, drifting along the terrace, her voice wrapping the chamber like fog. "Older than the ledger. Older than the mountain. And I am *tired*, Odile Ravensworth, in the way that only immortal things get tired—not sleepy, but worn smooth, like a stone the sea has handled too long. I need an heir who understands what it is to hide. What it is to be sold. What it is to survive by making yourself small." She turned, and her bottomless eyes found Odile's face. "You would learn the ledger. You would learn to read souls the way you read weather. And in a century—two, three, however long it takes—you would take my seat, and my vaults, and my peace. No more running. No more fear. Power that no storm-court would dare touch."

Beside him, Odile had gone very still. Not the stillness of refusal. Casimir knew refusal; he had written refusals into a hundred contracts, watched heroes spit in the faces of queens. This was different. This was the stillness of a starving woman looking at bread.

And why shouldn't she hunger? He had watched her for ten years through the valley's grey veil—watched her flinch at thunder, wake gasping from dreams of wings and lightning, work her small plot of salt with hands that remembered calling storms and hated their emptiness. He had loved her, in his quiet, unwitnessed, unforgivable way, precisely because she was hungry and honest about nothing else.

"Odile," he said, and his voice cracked like old paper.

Her eyes flicked to him. For one moment—one terrible, suspended moment—he saw her weigh him against eternity and find him wanting. Of course she did. He was a dead man's handwriting. He was a witness who had watched her whole life unfold and never once lifted a finger, bound by his own cursed terms. He was the reason the prophecy existed at all.

Then she looked back at the Queen, and Casimir saw something else move behind her eyes—something colder and finer than hunger. She was looking past the Queen now, at the walls. At the niches. At the little lights, humming their one note each, safe and paid and caged forever.

"That one," Odile said suddenly, pointing at a niche near the ceiling where a soul burned a defiant gold against the green. "Who is that?"

The Queen's smile thinned by a degree. "A musician. Stubborn. She refuses to harmonize."

"She's screaming," Odile said quietly. "Isn't she. All of them. The hum isn't peace—it's a thousand people trying to scream in the same key." She turned to face the Queen fully, and Casimir saw her spine straighten, saw the girl who had fled a storm-court become, in the brine-light, something with a queen's own architecture. "You didn't offer me a throne. You offered me a bigger cage with a better view—and someone else's screams to keep the walls warm."

The silence that followed had weight. Ten thousand souls held their note. Even the brine-pool went glass-still.

"Careful," the Queen said softly, and for the first time the gentleness fell away like a dropped veil, and beneath it was the thing that had hollowed a mountain. "You are standing in my court, on my salt, over my pool, with a soul in my ledger that matures at moonset. You forget yourself, heir."

"I'm not your heir." Odile's voice didn't rise at all, and that was the frightening thing. "But I'll make you a wager, since that's the language of this place. Three trials. Whatever stakes you name. If I win, I walk out with my soul—and you open the ledger."

The Queen regarded her for a long moment, and Casimir watched the bottomless eyes calculate, and knew with sick certainty that whatever terms were coming, they had been designed a thousand years ago and were only waiting for a debtor foolish enough to ask.

"A wager, then," the Queen said, and the pool began to rise around her like a crown. "Three trials. Each will demand a sacrifice of something you love. Refuse any trial, or fail any trial, and your soul returns to my ledger—permanently, and your friend's last breath with it." Her gaze slid to Casimir, and there was something almost tender in it, which was the most horrifying thing yet. "I know what you brought me, scribe. Did you think I wouldn't recognize my own ink?"

The pool swallowed her. The hum resumed. And Odile turned to him in the silver-green dark, chin high, hands trembling only slightly, and said:

"Well then. Let's find out what I love enough to lose."
