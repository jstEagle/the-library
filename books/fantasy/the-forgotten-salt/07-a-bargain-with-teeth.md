## 7. A Bargain With Teeth

The Salt Queen's throne room smelled of old rain and older grief.

Odile stood on a floor of pressed bone-white salt, before a dais of black coral that had grown, not been built—she could see the slow spirals of it, the way it had curled around the Queen like something living deciding to hold her forever. The Queen herself did not sit so much as pool. Her gown ran from her shoulders in rivulets of grey silk, and her face was beautiful the way a drowned thing is beautiful: preserved, precise, and wrong in some small essential way that Odile could not name and could not stop looking for.

"You want your soul back," the Queen said. Her voice was the sound water makes in a deep cave—patient, echoing, indifferent to who hears it. "Everyone who comes down here wants something back. A memory. A year. A name. The mountain keeps excellent records." She gestured, and the air beside her unfolded into a ledger taller than a man, its pages turning themselves, each leaf crowded with script that moved like insects. "Yours is here, Odile Ravensworth. Sold before your first breath. Paid in full by two frightened people who loved you enough to make you someone else's problem."

Odile's hands curled at her sides. She had thought she was past the fury—Casimir had told her the truth on the stair, and she had walked three days down through the dark with it burning in her chest like swallowed coal—but hearing it from the Queen's mouth, in that cave-water voice, with no apology in it at all, lit the coal again.

"Name your price," she said.

The Queen smiled. It was a careful smile, arranged rather than felt. "A wager. Three trials. Pass them, and the vault opens; take what is yours and walk out under your own sky. Fail—" she let the word hang, letting the ledger's pages whisper through the silence, "—and the debt collects early. Your soul comes to me tonight, and your body walks back up the mountain empty, smiling, and grateful."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then moonset takes you anyway, three days hence. The wager only changes *when*, dear child. Never *whether*." The Queen leaned forward, and the coral throne creaked like a ship's hull. "But you should know what the trials cost. Each one asks you to give up something you love. Not something you own. Something you *love*. The mountain can tell the difference."

Behind Odile, Casimir shifted. She heard the dry rustle of him—the sound his body made now, paper and salt where there should have been breath—and she did not turn around. If she looked at him, the Queen would see exactly how much she had to lose.

"I accept," Odile said.

"Odile." Casimir's voice, low, urgent, at her shoulder. "You don't know what she'll ask."

"Yes, I do." She kept her eyes on the Queen. "She just told me. That's the cruelty of it. That's the whole design." She lifted her chin. "I accept."

The Queen's smile widened by one measured degree. "Then let us begin."

* * *

The first trial took her storm away.

They led her to a chamber beneath the throne room—a vault of black glass where the walls wept brine in slow, glistening threads—and there, suspended in the center of the room like a heart in a chest, hung a small grey cloud. It turned slowly, shedding a fine cold drizzle that never reached the floor. Lightning moved inside it, soft and rhythmic, like breathing.

"That is yours," the Queen said. "It has followed you since you were six years old, when you called lightning out of a clear summer sky and terrified your mother half to death. We caught it when your parents sold you. Souls have weather, Odile. This is yours."

Odile stared at it. She had forgotten—no, she had *buried* it, buried it so deep that even she had lost the grave's location. But seeing it now, she remembered everything: standing in her father's field with her arms flung wide, the sky answering her like an old friend, the wild joy of it, the sense that she was *known* by something vast. Every year in the valley she had felt the absence without understanding it, the way a tongue finds a missing tooth. The dead air of the Salt Valley hadn't just hidden her. It had starved her.

She loved it. God help her, she loved it more than she had ever loved anything, because it was the only part of her life that had ever answered back.

"The trial is simple," the Queen murmured. "Give it up willingly, or fail."

"It's already gone," Odile said. "It's been gone ten years. What difference does—"

"The difference," the Queen said gently, "is hope."

And that was the teeth of it. As long as the cloud hung here in the dark, some future existed in which Odile walked out of this mountain with her soul *and* her storm, whole, restored, everything returned. Sacrifice it, and she was choosing—in advance, irrevocably—to leave as less than she came. To pay for her own self with pieces of herself.

Her eyes stung. She thought of the valley's flat grey skies, and how she had told herself she preferred them. She thought of Maren saying, once, *you always look like you're listening for something, love.*

"All right," Odile whispered.

She reached out and put her hand into the little cloud.

The lightning did not hurt her—it knew her, it had always known her—but it *left* her, pouring up her arm and out through her chest in a long silver rush, and she felt it go the way you feel blood leave a wound: warm, then cold, then nothing. The cloud shrank, guttered, and went out like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

The silence afterward was total. Somewhere in it, very far away, Odile heard her own heartbeat and understood, with a strange calm, that it was the loudest weather left in her body.

"One," said the Queen.

* * *

The second trial nearly broke her, because she saw it coming and could do nothing about it.

The chamber this time was different—a round room lined with shelves, and every shelf crowded with jars, and every jar holding a small drifting light. Memories, harvested and shelved. The Queen walked the aisles slowly, trailing her fingers along the glass, and Odile stood in the center with her fists clenched, knowing, *knowing*, what was coming, hating herself for knowing.

"There are so many things you love," the Queen mused. "Your friend the innkeeper. The taste of bread. The valley itself, though you'd never admit it. But those are shallow loves, dear child. Recent. Replaceable." She stopped before a jar near the top shelf, one that glowed a low amber, warm as lamplight. "This one is deeper."

"Don't," Odile said. Her voice cracked on it.

"This one grew in the dark," the Queen continued, lifting the jar down with terrible tenderness. "In a stairwell. In shared rations and bad jokes and a hundred years of waiting finally ending. This one grew *against* your judgment, which is how you know it's real." She held it up to the light, and inside, the amber glow shifted, and Odile saw—briefly, horribly—a fragment of it: Casimir's hand steadying her elbow on a broken stair. His voice saying *I wrote your name a century before I met you, and I have regretted the ink ever since.* His face, doing that thing it did when he almost smiled.

"No," Odile said again, and now she was shaking. "Take the valley. Take my memories of home, of Maren, of all of it—I don't need them, I'll build a new life, I've done it before—take anything else—"

"The trial does not negotiate." For the first time, something moved behind the Queen's drowned-beautiful face—something that might have been pity, or might have been appetite wearing pity's clothes. "That is rather the point."

"Odile." Casimir stepped forward from the shadowed doorway, and his voice was steady in the way that cost him everything. "It's all right."

"It is *not* all right—"

"Listen to me." He crossed the room, unhurried, and took her hands, and his fingers were cool and dry as old vellum. Up close she could see the places where the undeath showed through: the faint translucence of his skin, the salt-crusted seams at his wrists where a hundred years had begun to unstitch him. "Whatever she takes, she cannot take what matters. Do you understand? Memory is the jar. What's between us isn't in the jar." His mouth quirked, that almost-smile. "I'll simply have to introduce myself again. I'm told I make a poor first impression. You'll probably hate me. It'll be very funny."

She laughed, or sobbed, or both. "You're insufferable."

"I'm *centuries* insufferable. There's a difference." He squeezed her hands once, hard, and then let go, stepping back, folding his hands, arranging himself into stillness the way he did when he intended to endure something without giving it the satisfaction of watching him break. "Do it," he told the Queen. "And be quick about it. She hates long goodbyes."

"*Casimir*—"

The Queen uncorked the jar.

The light poured out and struck Odile in the forehead like a key turning in a lock, and the memories went—not faded, not dimmed, but *lifted*, peeled away clean as skin from fruit. She felt them go one by one: the stairwell, the rations, the night he told her about the prophecy and she threw a tin cup at his head. The night he confessed, in a voice like a man walking a ledge, that he had written her doom and then waited a hundred years for the privilege of watching it happen. The moment on the glacier road when she had realized, with helpless fury, that she trusted him more than she had ever trusted anyone alive.

Gone. All of it. Lifted out and shelved.

When it was over, Odile stood alone in the round room, blinking, her cheeks wet without her permission. Before her stood a tall figure in a scribe's coat, salt-grey and patient, watching her with an expression of such careful, disciplined tenderness that it made her chest ache—and she did not know why. She did not know *him*.

"I'm sorry," the stranger said quietly. "We haven't met. My name is Casimir."

"Two," said the Queen, and her voice was warm as a closing trap.

* * *

They gave her an hour in a cell of white salt to recover—or to despair; the distinction seemed not to interest her jailers. Odile sat on the floor with her knees drawn up, pressing her palms against her eyes, taking inventory of herself the way you'd inventory a house after a flood.

Storm: gone. She reached for the feeling of lightning and found smooth blankness, like a wall where a door used to be.

Casimir: gone. There was a stranger somewhere in this mountain who watched her with familiar sorrow, and she felt toward him only what any sensible woman would feel toward a stranger in a haunted place: wariness, curiosity, and an inexplicable grief she had decided not to examine, because examining it felt like handing the Queen another jar.

What remained? Maren. Bread. The valley's grey skies. Her own stubbornness, which had survived everything else and showed no signs of failing now.

The door opened. The Queen stood in it, alone, unaccompanied, and for the first time she looked tired—which was somehow worse than everything that had come before.

"One trial remains," the Queen said. "And I confess, child, I did not design this one. It designed itself. The mountain knows what you love better than I do." She stepped aside, and beyond her, the corridor filled with a light that had no source, pale and rising, like dawn coming up from underground. "Come. It's time to meet the third trial."

Odile rose on legs that shook and would not stop shaking, and followed the Queen into the light—

—and found Casimir waiting at the end of the corridor, standing in the center of a circle of burning salt, and the look on his face told her, before anyone spoke a single word, that the third trial was him.
