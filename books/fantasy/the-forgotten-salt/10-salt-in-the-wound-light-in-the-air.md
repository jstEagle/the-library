## 10. Salt in the Wound, Light in the Air

The valley smelled the same as it always had—brine and woodsmoke and the faint iron tang of the flats at low tide—but Odile woke to it now as if for the first time.

That was not quite true. She had been back eleven days. Eleven mornings of waking in her own narrow bed above Maren's inn, of hearing the gulls argue over the drying racks, of standing at the window with her hands wrapped around a clay mug while the light came up gray and gentle over the Salt Valley. Eleven days, and still every ordinary thing arrived with the strange weight of a gift she could not remember ordering.

She had traded away so much to come home. Her storm-calling, gone—the sky was only sky now, vast and indifferent and beautiful in a way that made her throat ache. Her memories of Casimir, gone, spent like coins in the dark courts beneath the mountain. And yet here she was, alive, mortal, free, walking through a life with holes in it the way old nets have holes: you learned where they were, and you worked around them.

She went down the stairs before dawn, as she did every morning now, because the harvest waited and because work was the only prayer she trusted. The inn's common room was cold and dark except for the banked fire, and Maren was already there, as she was every morning, kneading bread dough with flour to her elbows and a look on her face Odile had never learned to read.

"You're up early again," Maren said.

"The salt doesn't wait."

"The salt has waited ten years for you. It can wait an hour." But there was no argument in it, only the soft scold of someone who had decided, sometime during those terrible three days when Odile had vanished down the mountain road, that she would never waste another morning being angry at anyone she loved.

Maren set a bowl of porridge in front of her without asking. Odile ate. Outside, the first pale light touched the ridgeline, and somewhere below the world, deep under stone and salt, the mountain slept at last with its dead unchained.

* * *

The ledger's fall had changed everything and nothing, or so the survivors said. Odile remembered little of the escape itself—only running, only Casimir's hand around hers, only the mountain screaming through its roots as a century of stolen souls poured upward like light through water. She remembered the surface. She remembered moonset, and the last of the Queen's court dissolving into brine, and the silence afterward, so complete that she could hear her own heart working, stubborn and human, keeping time for no one but herself.

The souls had gone wherever freed souls go. Some said north, into the aurora. Some said simply out, the way smoke leaves a room. Odile liked to think some of them passed over the valley on their way, and that the strange sweetness in the air some mornings was their passing—a blessing from people whose names no one living knew.

And Casimir—

Casimir had woken the next morning, in the loft of Maren's stable where they'd collapsed, and asked for a mirror.

Odile had found one, a small hand-glass with a cracked silver frame, and held it up with hands that shook. And the man in the glass had looked older than he had the night before. Not ancient. Not dying. Just—a man. Lines at his eyes that hadn't been there. A thread of gray in his dark hair. Skin that took the morning light and kept it, warm and mortal, instead of letting it pass through like light through sea-glass.

He had stared at himself for a long time. Then he had laughed—not the careful, sanded-down laugh she'd known in the courts, but something rougher, younger, startled out of him—and he'd said, "I'm going to get wrinkles," as if it were the finest news the world had ever delivered.

She didn't remember that morning. That was the cruelty of it. Maren told her these things, patiently, in pieces, whenever Odile asked, and Odile collected them the way a woman collects shells from a beach she can't recall walking: this one is yours, this happened to you, you were happy here.

But she could see the truth of it every day without anyone telling her. Casimir aged. Slowly, blessedly slowly, but truly. He had taken a room at the inn and then, within a week, a job—mending the inn's ledgers, then writing letters for the harbor-folk who couldn't write, then teaching the cooper's daughter her letters in exchange for firewood. His hands, which had once transcribed prophecy in ink older than grief, now copied out fish prices and love notes and a will for old Sela the net-mender, and he seemed to treasure each page as if it were the only book in the world.

He did not try to make Odile remember him. That was the thing she noticed most, the thing that sat in her chest like a coal that wouldn't cool. He never once said *you loved me*. He never described what they had been to each other down in the dark. He simply stayed near, patient as tide, and let her find out whatever she could find out on her own.

Though sometimes—she caught it, she always caught it—he looked at her the way a man looks at a door he knows opens, and waits, and does not knock.

* * *

On the twelfth morning, Maren called her back before she reached the door.

"Wait." The innkeeper wiped her hands on her apron and disappeared into the back room, and came out holding something small wrapped in linen. "I've been waiting to give you this until you stopped flinching at loud noises."

"I don't flinch at loud noises."

"You flinched at the kettle yesterday."

"That was strategy."

Maren almost smiled. Then she set the bundle in Odile's hands, and her face went serious in a way that made Odile's stomach drop. "You left this with me. Ten years ago, your first week in the valley. You said—" Maren paused, choosing the words carefully, the way she chose everything. "You said, *if I ever forget why I came here, give me this.*"

Odile unwrapped the linen.

Inside was a ring. Simple silver, worn thin on one side, the kind of plain band a working woman might wear—or might keep in a pocket and touch, sometimes, when no one was looking. There was an inscription inside, small enough that she had to hold it to the window light to read it.

*What the mountain takes, the heart returns.*

She turned it over twice. Three times. Nothing stirred. No memory rose, no flood, no sudden knowledge of who had given it to her or when or what vow had come with it. The magic-dead valley kept its promises; even a keepsake was only metal here.

And yet.

Her thumb moved over the worn inner curve, over the place where a finger had polished it smooth across years of turning, and something in her body recognized it before her mind could object. A warmth. An ache. The specific ache of a name just on the far side of remembering, like a word caught in the throat.

"Maren." Her voice came out strange. "Who gave me this?"

"You never said." Maren's eyes flicked toward the common room door, toward the sound of Casimir's voice outside, reading numbers aloud to the cooper's daughter in his patient, unhurried way. "But you wore it on a cord around your neck for a year. And you stopped wearing it the day he walked into my inn with a book under his arm and your name already written in it."

Odile closed her hand around the ring.

It was such a small thing. A circle of silver, worth perhaps two days' harvest. It weighed less than a sparrow. But she stood there in the gray morning light of the inn she had built her peace inside of, holding the proof that the peace had always had a hole in it shaped exactly like a man, and she felt the whole careful architecture of her forgetting lean, gently, like a fence in wind.

"Thank you," she said.

Maren nodded once, briskly, and went back to her bread, and gave her the privacy of noise.

* * *

She found him at the harbor at midmorning, sitting on an overturned dory with his coat off and his sleeves rolled, writing out a letter for the widow Brand while she dictated and her grandson chased gulls. The sun was fully up now, honest and warm, and it lay across Casimir's face the way sunlight lies across any living face—without mercy, without mystery, gilding the new lines at his eyes, catching the gray at his temple.

He looked up when her shadow fell across his page.

"Odile." He said her name the way he always did now—carefully, gladly, without expectation. Like a man setting a cup down on a table rather than reaching for one. "Good harvest?"

"Good enough. The east beds are thick this year." She stood there, awkward, the ring burning in her pocket like a coal. The widow Brand watched them both with the frank interest of a woman who mended nets and marriages alike and saw the loose threads in everything.

"Then the valley eats well," Casimir said, and smiled, and began to turn back to his letter.

"Casimir."

He looked up again.

She meant to be careful. She had planned to be careful all the way down the harbor path—planned to ask him something small and safe, about the letter, about the weather, about anything. But her hand had already gone to her pocket, and the ring was already in her palm, and the words came out the way the words in the prophecy had once come out on her skin: inevitable, older than her fear, hers.

"This was mine," she said. "Ten years ago. Before I forgot."

His breath went out of him slowly. He looked at the ring in her open hand, and then at her, and she watched a hundred years of patience war with something younger and hungrier in his face, and lose.

"Yes," he said quietly.

"Was it—" Her voice cracked; she steadied it against the sound of the gulls, the water, the ordinary blessed noise of a world that owed her nothing and gave her mornings anyway. "Was it from you?"

The widow Brand gathered up her letter and her grandson and withdrew with the grace of long practice, and then it was just the two of them and the tide coming in, filling the harbor channel inch by inch, the way it had filled it before either of them was born and would fill it after.

Casimir stood. He crossed the space between them slowly, giving her every chance to step back, and she did not step back. He took her hand—her empty one, the one not holding the ring—and turned it over, and pressed the ring into her palm herself, closing her fingers around it with both of his, warm now, blood-warm, a living man's hands.

"It was from me," he said. "And it will be from me again, if you'll have it. There's no hurry. I've learned how to wait, and I'm learning how not to need to." He smiled, and the lines at his eyes deepened, and she thought: *I am watching him grow old. I am going to get to watch him grow old.* "Ask me anything you want. Or nothing. Or start with my name, if you like. I've always thought it was a good one."

The tide came in. The sun climbed. Somewhere behind them the valley went on forgetting and remembering, harvesting its salt, keeping its small faithful lights burning.

Odile looked down at the ring in her hand, and then up at the man who had given it to her twice, and she found—to her surprise, to her enormous, inconvenient, hard-won relief—that she was smiling.

"Start with your name," she said. "We have time."

And for the first time since the mountain fell silent, the words felt not like a wound, but like a promise.
