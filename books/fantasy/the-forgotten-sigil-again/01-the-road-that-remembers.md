## 1. The Road That Remembers

The trick to being nobody, Edmund Eastwick had learned, was to be useful in small ways and memorable in none.

He practiced it the way other men practiced swordwork or prayer. When the toll-keeper at the mouth of the Saltway asked his name, he gave a shrug and a handful of coppers and said, "Does it matter? I'll be gone by morning." When the innwife at the Waystone asked where he'd learned to pack a mule so a load wouldn't shift on switchbacks, he said, "Badly, once," and let her fill in the rest with whatever story she liked. People were generous that way. They'd rather invent you than remember you.

Nobody owed him anything. He owed nobody. He had spent six years making certain of it, and if you'd told him that this — the mule, the salt, the cold, the careful blankness of his own face reflected in a mountain stream — was the sum total of what he'd built with those years, he'd have called it a fair trade.

The mule, whose name was Breakfast because naming things was one indulgence Edmund permitted himself as long as the name was ridiculous enough to deflect curiosity, picked her way up the switchback above the treeline. Thornfell's passes in late autumn were a study in gray: gray stone, gray sky, gray lichen, and somewhere above it all the white teeth of the peaks, which were not gray at all but seemed to suck the color out of everything beneath them.

"Almost there, girl," Edmund murmured. "Then it's oats and a warm stable and neither of us thinks about any of this again."

Breakfast flicked an ear. She was the only creature alive whose opinion of him he cared about, and even that he'd have denied under torture.

The salt came from the pans below Dunmarrow, and it went over the pass to the shepherding villages on the far side, who paid in wool and cheese and never asked questions, because asking questions about your salt-man was a good way to end up salting your own soup with tears. It wasn't glamorous work. It wasn't safe work either — Veyra's war had been creeping up the valleys for two years now, and her tithe-men had started appearing at crossroads like mushrooms after rain — but it was *his* work, and it required exactly one skill Edmund possessed in abundance: the ability to pass through a place without leaving a mark.

He'd been thinking about that, smugly, when the weather turned.

* * *

Six years ago, give or take a season, Edmund had run from a house that smelled of rosemary and burning hair.

Maren's cottage sat at the hem of the Thornfell foothills, half swallowed by a hedge gone feral with age and spite. Inside, the walls were lined with jars — some holding herbs, some holding teeth, some holding things that shifted their weight when you looked away. Edmund had lived there seven years, from the age of eleven, apprenticed to a hedge-witch who taught him sigils by day and made him pay for them by night.

Not coin. Maren didn't deal in coin. She dealt in memory.

"Everything costs," she'd told him the first week, tapping his forehead with one cracked fingernail. "The world runs on it. You want to learn the sigil for mending? Fine. Give me the taste of your mother's bread. You won't miss it. You don't even remember her face anymore."

And the terrible thing — the thing Edmund only admitted to himself on bad nights, alone, staring at ceilings — was that she'd been right. He *didn't* miss it. How could he miss something he couldn't remember having?

But the tithes got bigger as the lessons got deeper. The taste of bread became the sound of his mother's voice. The sound of her voice became the whole of one summer afternoon. And then, one raw evening in early spring, Maren had stood over her cauldron with that look she got — the appraising look, like a woman pricing a horse — and said:

"The next working needs something substantial, boy. I'll be taking the year you were twelve."

The year he was twelve. The year Sable had found him.

Edmund hadn't known he was going to run until his feet were already doing it. He remembered — would always remember, because it was the last debt-free thing he owned — standing in the doorway with his bindle in his hand while Maren stirred and hummed, and saying nothing, and walking out past the hedge, and not stopping until the cottage was a smudge behind him and the road ahead was dark and going downhill, toward the lowlands, toward anywhere.

She'd find him eventually. Witches like Maren always did; debts had a scent, and he'd owed her seven years of room and board and instruction. But he'd made himself a promise on that dark road, huffing like a bellows, thirteen years old and furious and terrified: *I will never owe anyone anything ever again.*

It was, he would later reflect, the kind of promise that invited the universe to test it.

* * *

The storm caught him two hours below the summit, which was the worst possible place for a storm to catch anyone, being too high to shelter and too committed to turn back.

Snow came sideways off the peaks in great white slaps, erasing the trail, erasing the sky, erasing everything except the three feet of world immediately around Breakfast's stubborn little ears. Edmund pulled his hood down, cursed the weather, the mountains, the salt trade, and the general architecture of his life, and kept moving, because stopping meant freezing and freezing meant dying and dying meant — well. Dying meant owing Maren a debt she could collect from whatever was left of him. Hedge-witches were funny about that sort of thing.

"Left here," he told Breakfast, squinting at a cairn that was mostly snow. "I think. Possibly right. We'll call it an adventure."

The pass narrowed into a ravine where the wind funneled through with a sound like a giant whistling through his teeth. Edmund knew this stretch. Every smuggler did. Widow's Teeth, they called the whole upper pass, but this throat of it had its own name among the salt-men: the Quiet Cut, because sound died in there, and men who stopped to talk froze before they finished their sentences.

So when Edmund heard a voice, thin and cracked, saying words he couldn't make out, his first thought was that he'd finally gone mad, and his second thought was that madness was warmer than the ravine and he was almost tempted.

His third thought arrived when he saw the hand.

It lay across the trail like something dropped from a great height — pale, gloveless, fingers curled around the shaft of a broken spear as though the spear had personally betrayed it. Edmund followed the arm up the bank to its owner: a man crammed into the lee of a boulder, dressed in courier's grays gone dark with frozen blood, a leather satchel still strapped across his chest with the grim determination of a man who had been told, in no uncertain terms, what would happen if he lost it.

"Hey." Edmund knelt, already reaching for the man's collar, already calculating — warmth, shelter, distance, odds. "Hey. Can you hear me?"

The courier's eyes opened. They were brown and very clear, which was wrong; dying eyes should have been fogging over, and these looked instead like eyes that had seen too much too recently and were still deciding whether to believe it.

"Thornfell," the man rasped. "You're going over Thornfell."

"I'm going nowhere if I stand here chatting. Can you walk?"

A laugh, or the ghost of one. "No." He said it simply, without drama, the way a man reports the weather. "No, I can't. Listen to me. There isn't time, and I need—" His hand left the spear and clawed at the satchel buckle. "There's a name. I was carrying it to a name. Do you understand? Not a place. A *name.*"

Edmund understood couriers. Couriers were the smuggler's cousins, and cousins helped each other, mostly, when the helping didn't cost too much. But something in the man's voice put a cold finger on the back of Edmund's neck, colder than the snow.

"You're bleeding out in a ravine in the middle of Veyra's war," Edmund said carefully. "Whatever name you're carrying, it's past carrying. Let me get you down to—"

"There's no time." The courier's hand shot out and gripped Edmund's wrist with a strength that had no business surviving in that body. His breath fogged between them, rapid and shallow. "They marked me. You'll see it. When they mark you, the memories start going — hers, theirs, everyone's they've bought. It leaks. I've been leaking for two days. I remember snow that fell before I was born. I remember a woman singing in a language that's dead. I remember—" His grip tightened. "I remember a boy on a road. Twelve years old. Slavers' cart. Someone cut him loose."

The cold finger on Edmund's neck became a fist.

"That's not yours," Edmund heard himself say. His voice sounded strange, distant, like someone else's. "That memory. It's not yours."

"No," the courier agreed, and smiled, and it was the worst smile Edmund had ever seen, because it was full of pity. "It isn't, is it. You know it."

The wind screamed through the Cut. Somewhere above them, the storm was burying the trail, and Edmund knew — with the flat, practical clarity of a man whose survival had always depended on knowing such things — that every minute he spent kneeling here was a minute subtracted from his own life. The sensible move was to take the satchel if it was valuable, or leave both if it wasn't, and walk away, and be nobody again by nightfall.

The courier pressed the satchel into his hands anyway.

"Wax seal. Don't break it until you must. It remembers who touches it." His voice was fraying now, words coming apart at the edges like wet rope. "The name. Find the name. Tell him—" A cough, red-flecked. "Tell him it wasn't wasted. Tell him the time was *bought*, and tell him—"

"What name?" Edmund leaned close. "Whose name?"

The courier's eyes fixed on him, and inside them Edmund saw — or thought he saw, or *remembered*, though the remembering felt like a door opening in a wall he'd built himself, stone by stone, over six careful years — a mountain road at dusk, and laughter, and a hand ruffling a boy's hair, and a voice like rough honey saying *you're going to be all right, sprout, do you hear me? You're going to be all right.*

"Sable," the courier whispered.

The word went through Edmund like a spear of his own.

The man sagged. The grip on Edmund's wrist loosened, finger by finger, and the clear brown eyes drifted upward toward the buried sky, and Edmund caught him, and held him, and knelt there in the Quiet Cut where sound went to die, holding a stranger who had just spoken the one name Edmund had spent six years teaching himself never to say aloud.

The snow kept falling. It fell on the courier's open eyes and did not melt.

After a long time — how long, Edmund couldn't have said — he laid the man out decently, closed his eyelids with two numb fingers, and took the satchel. It was lighter than it looked. Under his palm, through the oiled leather, he felt a faint warmth, like a loaf carried home from the baker's, like a living thing asleep.

And threaded through the wind's screaming, so faint he might have invented it, Edmund heard — humming. Low and patient, rising out of the mountain itself, out of the rock under his knees and the peaks above his head, a note that seemed almost familiar, almost like a song someone had once sung to him on a road at dusk.

Breakfast stamped and blew, impatient, sensible, unhaunted.

"Yeah," Edmund said hoarsely, hauling himself up by the mule's harness, the satchel clutched against his chest like a second heartbeat. "Yeah. I know. Keep walking. Forgettable, that's us. Nobody at all."

But the map against his ribs was warm, and the name in his skull was loud, and the mountain hummed on, and for the first time in six years, Edmund Eastwick was afraid that somebody, somewhere, had never forgotten him at all.
