## 2. A Stranger's Last Mile

The trouble with ravines, Edmund had always maintained, was that they collected things. Lost hats. Lost goats. Lost mules, on one memorable occasion, along with the tax collector who'd been riding it—a loss Edmund still counted among the year's genuine improvements.

So when he heard a sound from below the switchback—wet, rhythmic, like a bellows with a hole in it—he should have kept walking. He told himself so quite firmly, in fact, even as his boots were already picking their way down through the scree. That was the whole problem with being forgettable, he reflected. Nobody remembered to teach you any sense.

"Hello?" he called, and immediately regretted it. Hello was a word for people who existed. He'd spent three years perfecting the art of not existing: no name given at inns, no debts owed, no favors banked that anyone might come to collect. A man who answered to *hello* was a man who could be found again.

The sound came again, and this time it shaped itself into words.

"Here. Here, damn you."

Edmund edged around a shoulder of rock and found him: a courier, or what had been one. The man lay wedged between two boulders like something the mountain had tried to swallow and given up on. His dispatch coat was fine once—deep green, silver frogging—but now it was dark with blood from his throat down, and his face had gone the color of old candle tallow.

And there, on his throat, just above the collar: a mark. A sigil burned into the skin, small and precise as a wax seal. Edmund knew it without knowing how he knew it—the coiled serpent biting its own tail, the mark of Veyra, the woman who called herself Queen of the Thornlands and whose tithe-men had been emptying villages of their memories for two years now. You didn't need to have seen it before to know it. It was the sort of knowledge that traveled, like lice.

"Oh, you're not my problem," Edmund said aloud, mostly to convince himself. "You are categorically not my problem."

"Good," the courier rasped. "Then go away."

There was something almost companionable about it. Edmund crouched at the edge of the boulders, keeping a careful arm's length and a careful conscience. "I haven't got anything worth stealing," he offered. "If that's your concern."

"It isn't." The man laughed, which turned into coughing, which produced a quantity of blood that made Edmund's stomach perform an undignified maneuver. "I'm dying, salt-man. I can tell by the smell of your pack. Salt and cheap tallow soap. Smuggler."

"I prefer *independent logistics specialist.*"

"I prefer *alive*," the courier said, "but here we both are, disappointed."

Edmund looked up at the switchback, at the pass beyond it, at the clean gray sky where nothing was chasing him. Three years of practice said: walk away. Dying men attracted questions, and questions attracted tithe-men, and tithe-men attracted exactly the kind of attention Edmund Eastwick had crossed two ranges and sold his good cloak to avoid.

But the man's hand had come out from under the coat, and it was shaking, and there was something clutched in it.

"What's that," Edmund said, in the flat voice of someone who already knows the answer and hates it.

"A map." The courier's eyes were fever-bright and terribly clear. "Wax-sealed. Black wax. You'll want to sell it, because you're the kind of man who sells things. Don't."

"That's terrible advice. Everything sells. That's rather the point of everything."

"Don't." The grip on Edmund's wrist, when the man seized it, was astonishingly strong for someone so far gone. His fingers were cold as river stones. "They'll ask who gave it to you. They'll ask where you got it. And then they'll open your head like a pantry and take out whatever answers them." He pulled a breath that whistled going in. "I'm Corvin. Was Corvin. Courier third rank, Thornfell circuit."

"You just violated your own rule," Edmund pointed out. "Names. Debts. Very sloppy tradecraft."

"I'm dying. Sloppiness is permitted." Corvin's mouth twitched toward a smile and didn't make it all the way. "Besides. Someone has to owe someone. Otherwise what's the point of any of it?"

Edmund had an answer to that, a good one, three years polished smooth: *the point is staying unowed and unremembered, alive and alone and free.* But looking at Corvin's tallow face, the answer felt suddenly like a coat that no longer fit—one he'd outgrown somewhere on the road without noticing.

"Who did this to you?" Edmund asked quietly.

"Tithe-men. Two of them. I ran the wrong pass." Corvin's gaze drifted upward, past Edmund, to something at a considerable distance. "They took the dispatches. Didn't find the map. It doesn't look like anything, you see. Just wax and vellum. It only matters if you know what it is."

"And do I?"

"No." Another ghost of a smile. "But you're going to learn."

"I really don't think—"

"*Take it.*" The strength went out of Corvin's grip all at once, like a tide withdrawing, and the packet pressed into Edmund's palm was lighter than a dead bird. Black wax, unmarked, warm somehow—or perhaps that was Corvin's blood. "One thing first. One name. Say it back to me."

"I don't even know it yet."

"You will." Corvin's eyes fixed on him with the terrible focus of the nearly gone. "Sable."

The name went into Edmund like a splinter going into a fingertip—small, trivial, and instantly impossible to ignore.

Because he *did* know it. Of course he knew it. Sable. Sable, who had lifted a twelve-year-old boy off a slaver's cart with hands quick as pickpocket weather and eyes the color of wet slate. Sable, who had fed him stolen plums on a mountain road and taught him which stars were liars. Sable, who had vanished one morning outside Dunmarrow leaving nothing but a folded blanket and the specific, engineered silence of a man who meant never to be found.

Edmund had spent eleven years not thinking about him. He'd gotten quite good at it. Not-thinking-about-Sable was, after Maren's door closed behind him, the second-finest craft he possessed.

"Sable," Edmund repeated, and hated how easily it came.

"Yes," Corvin whispered. "Tell him—" And then his face changed. Not relaxed—*changed*, the way a landscape changes when cloud-shadow crosses it. His pupils blew wide and black, and his grip returned, sudden and crushing, and Edmund felt something slide into his mind like cold water into a boot.

Snow.

Not the snow of Thornfell's passes, which Edmund knew intimately, professionally, the way a sailor knows waves. This was other snow. Snow falling through the branches of trees that grew in rows—*planted* rows, orchard rows—and landing on a stone wall that someone's bare hand was touching, and the hand was chapped and the knuckles scarred and none of it, none of it belonged to Edmund Eastwick, and yet he could feel the cold of that wall right down to his own wrist—

He wrenched backward and fell against the scree, and the vision snapped off like a snuffed lamp.

"What," Edmund said, when he could say anything, "was *that.*"

Corvin had slumped. The clarity was draining out of him by the second, but he managed, faintly, "Map does that. Leaks. Sorry. Should've warned—" A breath. "Should've warned you about a lot of things. Tell Sable the ledger's real. Tell him the debts are *real*, they were never metaphorical, he'll understand—"

"Corvin. Corvin, look at me. What debts? Whose ledger? What is this map *of?*"

But Corvin's eyes had gone past him again, to the far country, and this time they stayed there. His breathing slowed, spaced itself out, and stopped altogether with the mild surprise of a man remembering an appointment elsewhere.

Edmund sat in the scree for a long moment, holding a dead stranger's packet and a name he'd buried twice.

"Well," he said finally, to nobody, because nobody was his specialty. "This is precisely why one avoids ravines."

* * *

He buried Corvin properly, because apparently he was the sort of person who did that now. It took the better part of two hours with a smuggler's short-handled spade, digging into the thin mountain soil while the wind read over his shoulder. He laid the man straight, folded the ruined green coat across him, and marked the grave with a cairn, because crosses required explanations and cairns required nothing.

"There," he said, dusting off his knees. "Owed and paid. We're square, you and I. No debts outstanding."

The cairn, naturally, said nothing. But the packet in his inner pocket sat against his ribs like a second heartbeat, and every few steps he caught himself checking that it was still there, the way you check a tooth that aches.

He climbed back to the switchback and stood a long time looking north, toward Dunmarrow, where a broker named Hallic owed him eleven crowns and a favor, and where a man could sell anything if he didn't ask where it came from. Eleven crowns would buy salt for the next run. Eleven crowns would buy a season of comfortable anonymity.

The sensible plan was written out plain as a toll notice: go to Dunmarrow, sell the map to Hallic, take the crowns, forget the name, forget the snow-that-wasn't-snow, forget the feeling of a stranger's last breath spending itself on a word that belonged to Edmund's own worst year.

He started walking south.

He'd made it perhaps half a mile when the memory hit him again—not asked for, not summoned, just *there*, arriving the way weather arrives. Snow through planted trees. A stone wall under a scarred hand. And beneath it, fainter, like a song heard through a floor: laughter. A laugh he knew. A laugh he had personally, carefully, lovingly packed away eleven years ago in the deepest cellar of himself, behind the plums and the stars and the morning the blanket was folded.

It wasn't his memory. He was certain of that the way you're certain of your own hands. And yet it fit him like his own hands too, and that was infinitely worse.

"Right," Edmund said to the empty pass, in the bright, brittle voice of a man losing an argument with himself. "Absolutely not. No. Whatever this is, it is a *problem for brokers*, and brokers are paid to have problems, and I am paid to deliver salt and remain unmemorable."

His feet kept walking south. Toward Dunmarrow. Toward Hallic. Toward eleven crowns and the end of it.

He was aware, distantly, in the part of himself that had watched him turn back for a dying man in a ravine, that he was lying. Brokers talked. Buyers asked. And somewhere in the space between the asking and the talking, a name would surface—a name Edmund had spent eleven years keeping below the waterline—and the world, which had been content to leave Edmund Eastwick forgotten, would remember him all at once.

The black wax warmed against his ribs.

Behind him, the cairn stood small and patient on the mountainside, the only monument in the world to a man who had died owing one delivery—and to the fool who now owed it in his place.
