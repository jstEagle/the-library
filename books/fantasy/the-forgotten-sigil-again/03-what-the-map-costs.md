## 3. What the Map Costs

Dunmarrow smelled the way all free cities smelled to Edmund: of salt, smoke, and money changing hands in a hurry. He came through the south gate at midmorning, hood up, pack light, wearing the face he'd practiced in a hundred puddles—the one that said *nothing here worth robbing, nothing here worth remembering*. It was his best work. Twenty-six years of practice.

The map rode against his ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, warm from his own body heat. He'd told himself all night on the road that it was just paper. Just wax. Just a dead man's last errand that had landed in the wrong pockets, as errands did. Somebody would pay good coin for a sealed courier's map out of Veyra's territory—somebody always paid for anything with a usurper's fingerprints on it—and then it would be somebody else's problem, and Edmund Eastwick could go back to being nobody, which was the only thing he'd ever been truly excellent at.

He repeated this to himself like a prayer. Prayers, in his experience, worked about as often as they deserved to.

* * *

The broker's name was Halvard Tine, and he kept his office above a chandlery on Candlewick Row, which meant the whole building smelled of tallow and ambition. Edmund had sold him contraband twice before—once salt, once a crate of Maren's sigil-inked candles that she'd never missed because she'd never remembered making them, which should have been Edmund's first warning about how easy forgetting could get.

Halvard was a soft-bellied man with rings on six fingers and a seventh finger bare, which Edmund had once heard him explain was where he kept his honesty, for emergencies.

"Eastwick." Halvard didn't look up from his ledger. "Thought you'd drowned in the passes."

"The passes thought so too. We disagreed."

That got a grunt that might have been laughter. Halvard finished his entry, dusted it with sand, and looked up with the expression of a man preparing to be disappointed by commerce. "What have you got?"

Edmund set the oilcloth bundle on the desk between them. "Courier's map. Sealed. Out of the usurper's country."

Something flickered behind Halvard's eyes—that quick animal interest every broker tried to breed out of themselves and never quite managed. "Sealed?"

"Found it on a dead man."

"Found," Halvard said, in the tone brokers used for *stolen*, which was the tone everyone in Dunmarrow used for *found*, including Edmund, who had genuinely found it and resented the implication anyway.

"Dying man," Edmund corrected. "His throat was marked. The sigil." He drew it in the air without meaning to—a hooked line crossing three bars—and felt something turn over in his chest when Halvard went very still.

"You saw the mark yourself."

"I saw a dead man with a mark on him. That's all I'm selling. The map, not the story."

Halvard rubbed his bare seventh finger with his thumb, a habit Edmund had never seen him do before. "Price?"

"Forty crowns."

"Ten."

"It's sealed courier-work out of Veyra's own roads. Forty."

"Fifteen, and I don't ask what else you took off the corpse."

"Thirty-five, and you keep asking, because I'll tell you: I took nothing. Not even his boots." Edmund had taken the boots. They fit badly and he hated them, but a dead man's boots were still boots, and sentiment was a luxury item he'd stopped stocking years ago.

They settled at twenty-eight crowns and a bottle of plum brandy, which was more than the map deserved and less than Edmund needed, and both of them knew it, and neither cared, because that was what haggling was: two people agreeing to be slightly cheated and calling it friendship.

Halvard reached for the seal.

Later—much later, in the bad hours—Edmund would try to remember whether he'd wanted to stop him. Whether some part of him, the part that had slept badly since the ravine, the part that kept dreaming of snow falling upward, had opened its mouth to say *wait*. He honestly couldn't say. What he remembered was Halvard's thumb breaking the wax, the smell of hot metal and juniper that rose out of the broken seal like breath from a grave, and the broker's face going through four expressions in the space of a heartbeat: greed, confusion, terror, and then a kind of naked, unguarded grief that no one should ever have to see on a moneylender's face.

"Oh," Halvard said softly. "Oh, *no*."

"What?" Edmund leaned forward. "What is it? What does it say?"

Halvard wasn't looking at the map. He was looking through it, through the desk, through the wall, at something a long way away and a long time ago that had never happened to him. His lips moved. Edmund caught fragments: *"—the orchard—Mama, the bees are in the orchard again—"*

"Halvard."

"They're white," the broker whispered. "The hives are all white. Why would anyone paint the hives white?" A tear slid down into the folds of his jowl, and he wiped it away with the heel of his hand, furious at it. "I've never kept bees. I've never—I don't know why I said—"

He shoved the map across the desk as if it had bitten him. Then he stood, walked to his window, and was quietly sick into the gutter of Candlewick Row while a chandler's apprentice watched from below with professional disinterest.

When he came back, wiping his mouth, his face had reassembled itself into broker-shape, but the seams showed. "Get out."

"The payment—"

"*Get out.*" His voice cracked on the second word. He sat down heavily, pressed both hands flat against the ledger as though anchoring himself to columns of honest numbers, and said, in a smaller voice: "Keep your twenty-eight crowns. Keep the brandy. Take that thing and take it far from here, Eastwick, and don't bring it back, and don't tell anyone you brought it here, because if Veyra's people learn it passed through my office, I'm a dead man, and worse, I'm a *ruined* man, and only one of those is permanent."

Edmund gathered the map slowly. "You saw something. When you touched it."

"I saw an orchard I never owned," Halvard said, "with bees I never kept, and a woman calling me home for supper who was never my mother." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and old and frightened. "And it was the happiest moment of my life, Eastwick. Do you understand? Thirty years behind this desk, and the truest thing I ever felt belonged to a dead stranger." He laughed, a horrible small sound. "And now I can't stop missing her. I miss a woman who never existed, and I will miss her until the day I die, and there is no refund for that. No refund. Get out."

* * *

Edmund did not get out of Dunmarrow. This was, he reflected later, the single stupidest decision of a life well-stocked with them, and he made it for the most human reason there was: twenty-eight crowns was not enough, and curiosity is cheaper than wisdom.

He tried three other buyers that afternoon, telling himself each time that Halvard had been soft, that Halvard had been having a day, that maps did not do *things*.

The first was a fence named Odji who ran a pawnshop full of other people's heirlooms. She held the map for perhaps five seconds before her eyes rolled back and she began, in a clear young voice not her own, to recite multiplication tables in a language Edmund didn't know—and then, weeping without changing expression, to count someone over and over: *"Four hundred and six, four hundred and seven,"* like a woman counting sheep, or soldiers, or graves. She came back to herself mid-number, stared at Edmund, and threw a boot at him. He left without the boot or the sale.

The second was a priest of the Quiet Accord, on the theory that holy men bought secrets. The priest touched the map, smiled gently, and said, "Ah. There you are, little fish," in a voice full of such love that Edmund's throat closed up entirely. Then the priest blinked, looked at the map, looked at Edmund, and said, "Burn that. Whatever you're paid, it won't cover it. Some things are carried, boy, not sold." Which was exactly the sort of thing hedge-witches said too, and Edmund had run a very long way to stop hearing it.

The third buyer never got the chance. Edmund was crossing Tanner's Square toward the third contact—a debt-collector's clerk who owed Halvard a favor and might therefore be desperate enough—when he noticed the wagon.

It was painted grey, unmarked, and parked at the edge of the market with the patient confidence of something that had never once been told no. Two figures stood beside it, and everything about them was wrong in the specific way that Veyra's people were wrong: too clean for a market day, too still for living bodies, their grey coats buttoned to the throat in weather that had everyone else sweating. They weren't selling anything. They were *watching*, and they were watching the way fishermen watch water—with total patience and total certainty that something in there was already hooked.

One of them held a slate. On the slate, Edmund could see even at distance, someone had chalked a sketch: a hooked line crossing three bars. Underneath, in careful letters: *SEEKING. REWARD.*

A courier's sigil. A dead man's mark. And a reward for information, which in Dunmarrow meant informants, which meant that by tonight, half the city would know a courier had died in the passes, and the other half would be trying to remember who'd come through the south gate at midmorning with a hood up and a pack too light.

Edmund turned around with the smooth unhurried grace of a man who had absolutely nowhere else to be, and walked, at a stroll, at a *saunter*, back the way he'd come, past Odji's pawnshop, past the chandlery, up the stairs to Halvard's office, because panic makes idiots of us all and Edmund had never needed help.

Halvard opened the door before he knocked. The broker looked ten years older than he had at lunch. "I told you—"

"There are tithe-agents in Tanner's Square," Edmund said. "Asking after the courier."

Halvard closed his eyes. Opened them. Stepped aside to let Edmund in, which was the most damning thing he could have done, and they both knew it.

Inside, the office had changed. The ledger was gone. In its place, spread across the desk, were three sheets of cheap paper covered in Halvard's cramped hand, and Halvard watched Edmund read them with the expression of a man watching his own funeral procession go by and critiquing the flowers.

"You wrote it down," Edmund said. "What you saw. The orchard."

"I write everything down. It's how I sleep." Halvard poured two fingers of the plum brandy and drank it straight, no glass, which Edmund had also never seen him do. "Here's what matters, and then you leave and I never met you. Three buyers. You took it to three buyers after me. Every soul who touches that thing remembers something they never lived—the courier's memories, leaking out of it like blood through a bandage. And every soul who remembers something they never lived is going to talk about it, because people can't help themselves, and Veyra's agents are paying for exactly that kind of talk." He jabbed a finger at the window, toward Tanner's Square. "You didn't sell a map today, Eastwick. You rang a bell in a town full of listening ears. By tomorrow morning, every agent she has will know the map is in Dunmarrow. By tomorrow noon they'll know whose hands it passed through. And by tomorrow evening—" he drained the brandy, "—they'll be asking me questions with brass needles, and I'll tell them everything, because I am many things, but I am not brave, and I want that understood between us as a matter of commercial record."

"Then I'm sorry," Edmund said, and found, to his surprise, that he meant it.

Halvard waved this off. "Sorry is free. Everything else costs." He picked up one of the written sheets—the orchard, the white hives, the woman calling him home—and looked at it for a long moment. Then he folded it with terrible care and tucked it inside his coat, over his heart, like a letter from someone he loved. "Take my advice, since you won't take my coin. That map isn't cargo. It's a *debt*. Someone wrote every mile of it in memory instead of ink, and debts like that don't rest until they're paid. You're carrying a dead man's last word, Eastwick, and his last word was a name, and you know it, because you went white as whey when I mentioned the sigil, and I've been in trade thirty years and I know a man hiding from something when he's standing in my office pretending to haggle."

Edmund's hand had gone, without his permission, to his ribs, where the map lay warm as a second heartbeat.

"He whispered a name," Edmund said. It came out rougher than he intended. "Before he died. One word."

"And you've been trying not to think it since the ravine," Halvard said, "because thinking it makes it yours." He shrugged, huge and sad and soft. "Bad news, merchant. It's already yours. Debts don't wait to be accepted. They just wait to be collected."

Down in Tanner's Square, faintly, a bell began to ring—not the market bell, but the sharp triple stroke of the watch-call, and beneath it, rising, a murmur of voices doing what voices in Dunmarrow always did when reward-slates appeared: talking, trading, selling each other for coin.

Halvard looked at the window. Looked at Edmund. And with the weary kindness of a man giving away the last of his stock, he said:

"Back door's through the candle store. Move fast, move quiet, and whatever name you've been refusing to say—" he tapped Edmund's chest, right over the map, "—say it soon. Before somebody else says it for you."

Edmund took the stairs two at a time, the map hot against his ribs, and behind him Dunmarrow began, softly, to talk.
