## 4. The Name He Buried

Edmund did not sleep that night. Or rather, he slept the way a man sleeps in a house where someone else is walking around downstairs — lightly, and with one ear open.

The ear in question belonged to a dead man.

He'd made camp in the hollow of an old watch-shrine above Dunmarrow, one of those crumbling waystations the salt-caravans used before the war made every road a question. He'd built a small fire, because he was cold, and because fire meant he was still a person making choices. Then he'd lain down, closed his eyes, and found himself standing ankle-deep in snow that had never fallen on him.

*Not his snow.* That was the first thing he'd learned about the courier's memories: they came labeled, in a way, by their wrongness. His own memories were worn smooth, like river stones; these were sharp-edged, too vivid, smelling of someone else's sweat and someone else's fear.

In this one, the courier — Aldric, the name surfaced like a fish rising, *Aldric of the Vellum Road* — was running through a drift up to his knees, laughing, while a woman threw snowballs at him from behind a pine. She had flour on her cheek. She was saying something about how he'd promised to be home for the baking, and he was saying something back, something foolish and tender, and Edmund lay by his own small fire with tears freezing on his lashes because the love in it was so plain and so entirely not his.

"That's enough," he said aloud to the empty hillside. "Thank you. No."

The memory let go reluctantly, the way a dog lets go of a sleeve.

He sat up and looked at the map, which he had propped against his pack at a respectful distance, the way you'd keep a suspicious animal in view. The wax seal was broken now — Aldric had broken it with his dying hands, pressing it into Edmund's grip — and the vellum beneath showed roads Edmund knew and roads he didn't, all of them converging, sooner or later, on Thornfell mountain. On the mountain itself, drawn in ink so fine it might have been done with a single hair, there was a door. And beside the door, in a cramped hand:

*For Sable. Tell him it wasn't wasted.*

Edmund read it eleven times. The twelfth time, he stopped reading and started throwing things into his pack, because if he kept reading it he was going to have to think about what it meant, and thinking about what it meant required saying a certain name inside his own head, and he had spent four years not saying that name with the discipline of a monk.

"Sable," he said finally, to the fire. It came out flat as a dropped plate.

The fire, being a fire, said nothing helpful.

* * *

Here is what Edmund Eastwick knew about being forgettable: it was a craft, like any other, and he was good at it. Not invisible — invisible people got noticed precisely because they were invisible. Forgettable. A face the innkeeper would struggle to describe to a tithe-man. A name given only when law demanded it, and then a boring one. No debts, no favors owed, no letters waiting anywhere. He paid cash. He tipped exactly the local average. He laughed at jokes a half-beat late, so no one remembered him as either slow or quick.

It had taken years to build, this nothing of a life, and he had been proud of it in the quiet, shameful way a man is proud of a scar he earned doing something stupid.

And here is what the map had done to it in three days: everyone who touched it remembered things they'd never lived, which meant everyone who touched it talked, which meant Edmund could no longer walk through Dunmarrow without feeling the whole city leaning toward him like grass toward wind. Worse — far worse — the courier's memories were leaking into him faster now, as though distance from the place Aldric died loosened some seal inside Edmund's own skull. He'd caught himself humming a lullaby in a northern dialect he didn't speak. He'd reached for a sword hilt he didn't carry. This morning he had called the innkeeper's daughter *Mira* and been stricken with grief so sudden and specific that he'd had to sit down on a barrel until it passed.

Mira. Aldric's daughter. Seven years old, gap-toothed, afraid of geese.

"Stop it," Edmund told his own head, gently, the way you'd talk to a leaking roof. "She's not mine to miss."

But grief, he was learning, did not check ownership papers.

* * *

The slaver caravan came through the Thornfell passes in the autumn Edmund turned twelve, and it took him on a Tuesday.

He remembered that detail — the Tuesday of it — because his mother had always said Tuesdays were unlucky for the Eastwicks, and she'd been right in the end, hadn't she, since the fever took her the following spring and left him alone with an uncle who sold him within the month to pay a debt he probably invented. Edmund had been in the caravan nine days. Long enough to learn the weight of the wrist-irons, long enough to know the names of the two boys chained nearest him (Pell, who cried silently; Odo, who never cried at all, which frightened Edmund more), long enough to understand that nobody was coming.

Then somebody came.

He never saw how it started. There was a night, a bad road along a ravine, a mule going lame — and then fire in the bracken, screaming, and a figure moving through the smoke like the smoke had decided to grow opinions. Small, quick, dark-haired, armed with a knife and what appeared to be total contempt for the concept of odds. Later Edmund learned the man had been hired to recover a single girl from the caravan — a merchant's daughter, ransom job, clean work — and had instead, in the chaos, cut loose every chain he could reach.

Eleven slaves ran into the hills that night. Edmund was the youngest and the slowest, and he fell, and the others left him, and he understood that too — you left people behind or you died with them, that was arithmetic — and he lay in the bracken listening to the slavers regroup and waited to be recaptured or worse.

And then a hand closed on his collar and hauled him upright, and a voice said, close to his ear, dry as good wine:

"Well. You're the ugliest ransom I've ever been paid."

Sable. Of course it was Sable. Even at twelve, even half-dead with terror, Edmund had known instantly that this was a person the world would refuse to categorize. Smuggler, the girl they'd rescued said later, with awe. Smuggler and liar and thief, but the kind who stole *from*, never *for*. He walked Edmund out of the hills over three days because turning back was death and leaving the boy was — well. Sable had tried to explain it once, waving a hand vaguely at his own chest as if the reason lived there and annoyed him.

They ate hard cheese and harder bread. Sable taught him which stars lied about direction. He taught him a sigil — scratched in dirt with a stick, a little hooked mark like a fisherman's hook — "for finding your way back to a place you mean to return to." He said it carelessly, the way he said everything, but Edmund drew it every night for years afterward, in dust, in frost, in the condensation on Maren's windows, until the hedge-witch caught him at it and asked, in her terrible gentle way, *who taught you to hope in writing, child?*

On the third morning they reached a crossroads, and Sable crouched to Edmund's eye level, and said, "This is where I stop being interesting and you start being alive. There's a hedge-witch down that road. Maren. She's terrifying. Tell her I sent you and she'll feed you anyway."

"Are you coming?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because everything I'm near gets interesting," Sable said, "and you've had enough interesting for one childhood." He stood, ruffled Edmund's hair with a hand that smelled of woodsmoke and cheap tobacco, and added, as if it were a joke, as if it were nothing: "Draw the hook, eastwick-boy. If you ever need me, draw the hook somewhere I'll see it."

Then he walked north, and Edmund watched him go with the specific devastation available only to twelve-year-olds, and drew the hook in the dirt of the crossroads, and waited.

Sable never came back for him. Not that winter, not the next year, not ever. Edmund grew up on Maren's hearth and told himself the story with increasing sophistication: smugglers die young; the roads are war now; a promise made to a child is a coin of the smallest denomination. He believed it the way you believe a roof holds — not by inspection, but by never looking up.

Until the tithe-men came for Maren's ledgers, and Edmund fled rather than pay another memory, and only later, much later, lying awake in smugglers' lofts, did he admit the second reason, the true one, the one shaped like a man walking north: he had run because staying meant grieving, and he had already grieved Sable once, thoroughly, at age thirteen, and once in a life was plenty.

That was the buried name. That was the stone he'd rolled over the tomb.

And now a dead courier had gone and written on the lid.

* * *

By the time he reached Dunmarrow's lower market, Edmund had catalogued forty-one of Aldric's memories and hated every one of them individually. Some were trivial — the taste of a particular plum, the exact squeak of a particular boot. Some were enormous. One was a battlefield at dusk, seen from behind a rock, which Edmund suspected explained why Aldric had become a courier instead of a soldier. One was of a jail cell, and a voice through the wall, low and amused, telling Aldric a story about a boy at a crossroads.

Edmund had sat bolt upright at that one.

Because the voice through the wall was Sable's voice. And the story was *his* story — embellished, obviously, in the telling, in the way Sable embellished everything, but unmistakably his: the caravan, the fire, the ugly little ransom. And Aldric, in the memory, had laughed and asked why a man like that was sitting in a Veyran holding cell, and the voice had said, lightly, so lightly:

"Because I signed something. Best contract I ever made. Bought a lot of people a lot of time." A pause. "Don't tell the boy, if you ever meet him. He'd only feel guilty, and guilt looks terrible on him."

Edmund stood in the middle of the fish-market with a basket of nothing in his hands and felt the anger arrive like weather.

It was a clean anger, at first. It had edges. *How dare he.* How dare Sable get himself taken, get himself *sold* — because that was what the whisper in the ravine meant, wasn't it, that was what the map was for, that was why Aldric had crawled on his elbows through a frozen gully with Veyra's sigil burned into his throat: to bring the ledger-road to the court beneath the mountain, to the place where purchased souls were kept, to buy back — or steal back, or beg back — one particular soul out of thousands.

How dare he do something so enormous and so stupid and so *himself*, and how dare he do it without asking Edmund whether he wanted to be dragged out of four careful years of nothing.

The basket trembled. A fishwife eyed him with professional sympathy and offered him a herring at a discount.

The anger lasted until sundown. Anger always did, with Edmund; it was a coat that fit badly and wore out fast, and underneath it was the thing he actually felt, which was simpler and much heavier: *he remembered me.* All those years, all that silence, and Sable had carried the story of a boy at a crossroads into a prison cell and told it to strangers like it was worth telling. Like Edmund was worth remembering.

And Edmund had repaid the favor by burying the name so deep he'd nearly convinced himself it had never existed.

"You absolute coward," he told himself, quietly, on the market steps, while Dunmarrow's lamps came on one by one below him. "You miserable, careful, forgettable coward."

Because that was the shape of it, wasn't it. He had built his whole life out of not owing anyone anything, and the truth was that he owed a man his life and had never once drawn the hook. Never scratched it in the frost of some northern window and waited. He'd had the sigil. He'd had the invitation. He'd chosen the silence, year after year, and called it self-sufficiency.

The map rustled inside his coat, patient as a creditor.

*Tell him it wasn't wasted.*

"Wasted?" Edmund said aloud, to the vellum, to the dead man whose snow was still melting somewhere behind his eyes. "What wasn't—"

But he knew. He knew with the horrible certainty of a man reading his own handwriting on a note he doesn't remember writing. Whatever Sable had traded himself for — whatever time, whatever lives — part of the price had been bought with the belief that the boy from the crossroads had grown up safe. That the rescue had held. That it *wasn't wasted.*

And Edmund had spent four years making sure that belief stayed true by never once letting himself be findable.

Below him, in the market square, torches flared out of pattern — too many, too fast, moving in formation. Somewhere a bell began its ugly triple stroke, and a woman screamed something short and was silenced, and the word came up the steps on the backs of running feet:

*Tithe-men. Tithe-men in the square!*

Edmund's hand went to the map without his deciding anything. And the map — he would swear this later, swear it on anything — seemed to pull, faintly, toward the old smuggler tunnels under Dunmarrow's east quarter, like a compass needle finding a north nobody had charted.

He went. Grief and anger and all, he went — because the courier's last mile deserved finishing, and because a man who owed as much as Edmund Eastwick owed could not afford, anymore, to be forgettable.

Behind him, the bells kept ringing. Ahead, the tunnels breathed cold air that smelled of salt and old stone.

And somewhere beneath all of it, under the mountain, under the world, a jar on a shelf was whispering a laugh Edmund had tried for four years to forget.
