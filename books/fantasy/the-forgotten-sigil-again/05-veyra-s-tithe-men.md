## 5. Veyra's Tithe-Men

The market at Dunmarrow woke the way markets do—badly, and with a great deal of shouting.

Edmund had been awake since before dawn, which was not unusual. What was unusual was that he'd spent those hours sitting on the floor of his rented room with the courier's map unrolled across his knees, watching candlelight crawl across wax seals he could not bring himself to break. The map had not done anything. Maps, in Edmund's experience, were famously inert objects. And yet twice in the night he had caught himself leaning toward it, the way you lean toward a window when someone outside is saying your name.

He'd rolled it up, stuffed it inside his coat against his ribs, and gone down into the market to be ordinary.

Dunmarrow's morning market smelled of frying onions, wet wool, and the particular optimism of fish that had been dead for three days but refused to admit it. Edmund bought a heel of bread and an apple of genuinely criminal hardness, and let the crowd carry him. This was the skill he prided himself on above all others—not swordwork, not sigils, not even smuggling, but *being forgettable*. A man who moved with the current, haggled without passion, tipped his cap to no one important, and left behind exactly the impression of nothing at all.

"You there. Salt-man."

So much for that.

The speaker was a pie-seller with flour to her elbows and the squint of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. "You've been past my stall four mornings running and never bought a pie."

"I don't like pies," Edmund said.

"Nobody doesn't like pies. That's like not liking weather." She jabbed her thumb at the tray. "Onion and mutton. Half price, because you look like a man who's been eating alone, and pity makes me generous."

It was, Edmund reflected later, the first kindness anyone had shown him in Dunmarrow, and it lasted approximately as long as it took him to bite into the pie.

Because that was when the bells started.

Not the market bells—the *other* bells. The ones up in the watchtower that nobody rang except for fires, floods, and the thing that was now happening, which the watch-captain had apparently decided deserved its own category.

"Tithe-men," someone said, and the word went through the market like cold water through cloth.

* * *

Edmund had heard of tithe-men the way you hear of wolves—stories from people who'd never seen one, delivered in the confident tones of people hoping never to check. Veyra's collectors. The usurper didn't tax coin; coin was beneath her. She taxed *owing*. Every debt in the territories she claimed was registered, weighed, and called in—and what she collected when a debtor couldn't pay was not property.

It was memory. It was self. It was, if the stories were true, the soul entire, decanted neat.

He saw them come through the south gate: six riders in grey, unhurried, their horses walking as though the streets belonged to them because they did. They wore no livery. They needed none. Each carried a long case of oiled leather on their back, and each wore brass rings on every finger, polished to a shine that caught the morning sun and threw little coins of light across the shopfronts.

The market did not scatter. That was the worst part. It *compacted*—stalls closing with the soft apologetic sounds of merchants who had learned that running marked you. A tithe-man's attention was a debt-collector's nose. Move fast, and you owed something worth chasing.

Edmund stood very still by the pie-seller's stall and ate his pie and was nobody.

The lead rider dismounted in the square's center. He was younger than Edmund expected, with a pleasant open face, the kind of face meant for selling things. He unslung his case, laid it across a fountain's rim, and opened it with the tenderness of a man unveiling instruments rather than weapons.

Inside, nested in velvet, lay needles. Brass, slender as writing quills, each one etched with fine spiraling script.

"Good people of Dunmarrow!" the tithe-man called, cheerful as a fair-crier. "Routine audit! His Radiant Majesty's ledger shows outstanding balances against forty-one souls in this quarter. We are here to offer *amnesty*." He smiled. "Pay your arrears today, or arrange terms, and go home whole. Those who cannot—" he gestured graciously at the needles, "—we have installment plans."

A woman near Edmund made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter could bleed.

They worked down a list. That was the horror of it, Edmund thought later—it was *orderly*. No screaming charge, no broken doors. A clerk with a ledger read names. Debtors stepped forward or were gently fetched. The needle went to the temple, the tithe-man murmured something in a language that sounded like water running backward, and the brass drank.

Edmund watched a cooper—a big man, forearms like hams—forget the name of his late wife. He watched it happen. One moment the man's mouth was moving around the word like it lived there; the next his face went smooth and puzzled, and he said, "I had a wife? I had—I know I had—" and then stopped, because grief needs a name to hold onto, and his had been poured out of him into a brass needle and a velvet slot.

The pie turned to ash in Edmund's mouth.

*Run*, said the sensible part of him, the part that had kept him alive through Maren's tutelage and seven years after. *You owe debts. Everyone owes debts. You're standing in a room full of wolves taking attendance.*

But his feet wouldn't move, because they had brought a child.

She couldn't have been more than eight, dragged forward by a harried aunt whose own name was on the list. The girl's mother had died owing money—apothecary bills, the aunt was saying, fever medicine, how was anyone to know—and the debt passed down, that was the law now, that was Veyra's law, debts flowed downhill like everything else.

"She's a child," the aunt said. "Take from me. Take double from me."

"The ledger," said the tithe-man kindly, "does not accept substitutions."

The needle touched the girl's temple.

Edmund had seen cruelty before. He'd grown up around hedge-witches and slavers and smugglers; cruelty was a country he held citizenship in. But this was different. This was cruelty wearing an apron and smiling, cruelty with paperwork, cruelty so *reasonable* that the crowd stood by and let it happen because objecting would mean admitting you might object next.

The girl's mother had died in spring. Edmund learned this afterward, from the aunt, screaming it. What the girl lost was smaller and therefore worse: she forgot her mother's *face*. Not the death, not the illness, not the funeral—just the face, lifted out clean, the way you'd lift an egg from a nest. When they led her back past the crowd she looked up at strangers with wide wet eyes, searching, searching, a child hunting through a house where every portrait had been taken down.

"I knew her," she told the aunt, bewildered, furious, tiny. "I *knew* her. Why can't I—why does everyone else get to—"

Edmund's apple hit the cobbles. He hadn't known he'd dropped it.

Something in his chest that had been carefully folded away for seven years—the part of him that was twelve years old and chained in a slaver's cart, the part Sable had cut free with a stolen knife and a grin—came unfolded all at once, and it was not afraid. It was *furious*.

Maren had demanded tithes. He'd hated her for it, fled her for it. But Maren took memories one at a time, from willing bargainers, for workings that healed and warded and fed villages through hard winters. She'd wept over the scale, sometimes. This—this was harvest. This was a *granary*. Veyra wasn't buying souls; she was strip-mining people, and calling the craters an economy.

And somewhere in those vaults, according to a dying man's last breath, was Sable.

Edmund turned toward the south gate, because the south gate was nearest, and found two tithe-men already there, checking papers with the mild patience of men fishing a stocked pond.

Turned toward the north. More grey coats.

*Move fast and you owe something worth chasing.* But staying still had just stopped being survivable too, because the clerk with the ledger was working alphabetically through the market's registered traders, and Edmund had been seen buying pies four mornings running, and the pie-seller—bless her, curse her—was pointing.

"That one," she was saying. "Salt-man. Never buys a pie till today. Nobody doesn't buy a pie for four days. That's a man hiding something."

Edmund decided, with some bitterness, that he would never forgive her, and also that if he lived he would buy every pie she ever baked.

* * *

He ran.

There was no dignity in it. He knocked over a basket of eels, apologized to two separate grandmothers, and took the first gap between buildings like a fox taking a hedge. Behind him he heard the pleasant-voiced tithe-man say, mildly, "Now that's a man who knows he owes," and heard boots start after him at a trot, because tithe-men never ran either—they simply *continued*, in your direction, forever.

Left. Right. A courtyard with a dead well. Left again—

—and stopped, because he didn't know these streets. Three days in Dunmarrow and he knew the market and his rented room and the road out, and none of that helped, because the roads out were covered.

Against his ribs, under his coat, the map grew warm.

That was the only way to describe it. Not hot—warm, the way a sleeping animal is warm, the way a hand is warm when it finds yours in the dark. Edmund had told himself for three days that the map was paper and wax and a dead man's obsession. Now it pressed against his side like a finger tapping his shoulder, and when he pulled it out with shaking hands, the wax seals—both of them, the ones he'd checked a dozen times—were broken.

He hadn't broken them. He was almost sure he hadn't broken them.

The map had changed. Or rather, it had *revealed*: ink that hadn't been there at dawn now crawled across the vellum in a fine brown hand, and it was drawing Dunmarrow. Not the city above—the city *under*. Smuggler tunnels, old cellar runs, the forgotten veins of a town built on mining before it was built on trade. And one line burned brighter than the rest, a thread of ink moving even as he watched, sliding through the labyrinth like a lit fuse:

*This way. Hurry.*

"You're a map," Edmund told it. "Maps don't hurry."

The line pulsed, impatient.

Boots in the alley behind. Edmund went.

What followed was the strangest flight of his life, and Edmund had once fled a wedding party consisting entirely of a witch's animated scarecrows. He ran through Dunmarrow's guts following a moving line of ink, and the map was *right*, every time. The cellar door it showed was unlocked. The tunnel it chose was clear of rubble. Where the line bent sharply left, he bent sharply left, and thirty seconds later heard tithe-men clattering straight on into a dead end, cursing with great politeness.

The tunnels smelled of earth and old lamp oil and, faintly, of salt—his own old trade, written into the walls. Somewhere ahead, water dripped in a rhythm almost like speech. Edmund slowed to a walk, then a stop, and leaned against the stone, and laughed until it wasn't laughing anymore.

Because here was the thing he'd figured out somewhere between the well and the second junction, the thing that sat in his stomach like swallowed river-stone: the map hadn't saved him out of kindness. Maps didn't have kindness. The map was going somewhere. It had been going somewhere since a dying man pressed it into his hands and said a name, and it had just demonstrated—with brass needles and a child's emptied eyes—that it was entirely willing to spend Edmund's life getting there.

Fine, he thought. Fine.

He slid down the tunnel wall until he was sitting in the dark, map across his knees, and let himself remember properly for the first time in years: snow on a mountain road, a fire built small and bright, a smuggler with a crooked smile teaching a rescued boy to pick locks because "you'll want to leave places, kid, it's a whole skill." Sable. Who had vanished one morning leaving warm ashes and no note. Who had, if the courier's dying whisper was truth, sold himself—soul and all—to buy *time* for cities full of strangers.

Time. Edmund looked up into the dark, toward the mountain looming somewhere overhead beyond miles of rock.

Veyra was buying time with other people's souls. Sable had paid for some of it. And a child in the market above would grow up knowing her mother only as a shape-shaped absence, because the world had decided that owing was a crime and forgetting was the currency.

Edmund had spent seven years perfecting the art of being forgettable. No name given, no debts owed, no one hurt by losing him because no one had him to lose.

Well. He'd been robbed of that delusion thoroughly enough.

"All right," he said aloud, to the map, to the dark, to a dead courier and a living ghost. "I'll carry you where you're going. But we do this my way once we arrive, and my way starts with learning what this thing actually is—which means there's exactly one person alive stubborn enough and wicked enough to teach me."

The ink-line on the map pulsed once, slow, like a heartbeat deciding to agree.

Edmund got up, brushed off his coat, and started walking north through the dark, toward Thornfell, toward the passes, toward a door he'd sworn never to knock on again.

Toward Maren.

The map hummed against his ribs the whole way, and if he didn't know better, he'd have said it sounded pleased.
