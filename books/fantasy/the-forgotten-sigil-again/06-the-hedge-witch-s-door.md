## 6. The Hedge-Witch's Door

The cottage at the edge of the Mirefen hadn't changed, which was the cruelest thing about it.

Edmund stood in the drizzle for a long while before knocking, the map a hard little weight against his ribs where he'd sewn it into his coat lining. Three days out of Dunmarrow, two of them spent doubling back through fenland because he kept feeling eyes between his shoulder blades, and every road he might have taken had led here anyway. That was the trouble with running from someone who taught you everything you knew about running. She knew all your tricks because she'd invented half of them.

Smoke curled from the chimney. Someone was home. Someone was always home; Maren Oyelaran had not left this crooked house in eleven years except to buy salt and bury a neighbor, and she considered both errands tedious.

He raised his hand to knock. Lowered it. Raised it again.

"You'll wear the door out with all that hovering," said a voice behind him, dry as autumn leaves. "It's oak. It can take it. But I can't stand watching."

Edmund turned. Maren stood in her garden gate with a basket of yarrow on her hip, gray-streaked hair pinned up with what appeared to be a bone knitting needle, looking at him the way one looks at weather that has arrived inconveniently early.

"Maren," he said. His voice came out smaller than he wanted.

"Edmund." She set down the basket. "You look terrible."

"I've been traveling."

"You looked terrible when you traveled well." Her eyes went over him the way they always had — cataloguing, weighing, pricing. They stopped on his chest, on the place where the coat hung slightly wrong, sewn shut in a hurry by someone whose needlework was a disgrace. Something moved behind her face, quick as a fish under ice. "Come inside before the tithe-men come asking why a hedge-witch is entertaining vagrants."

"They're not in the fens yet."

"They are everywhere eventually. That's rather their whole business model." She pushed past him and opened her own door, then paused with her hand on the latch. "Well? Are you coming in, or have you grown too proud to be fed by an old woman?"

Edmund had rehearsed a dozen speeches on the road. *I only need shelter for a night. I'm not staying. Don't ask me anything.* All of them dissolved now, and he followed her inside like he was twelve years old again and late for supper.

* * *

The cottage smelled the same: rosemary, tallow, the faint mineral tang of sigil-ink. A kettle was already on, because Maren's house operated on the assumption that anyone arriving would need tea whether they deserved it or not. She poured two cups without asking how he took his, because she remembered, and handed his over black with too much honey, and Edmund hated how much comfort there was in being remembered correctly.

"So," she said, settling into her chair by the fire. She did not ask him to sit. He sat anyway, on the stool across from her, the way he had sat for six years of lessons. "Let's hear it. You ran from me in spring. It's nearly harvest. Either you've come crawling back to finish your apprenticeship, or you're in trouble, and I notice you haven't apologized yet, so my money's on trouble."

"I'm not apologizing."

"No. You never were good at it. You once broke my best scrying bowl and blamed the cat."

"The cat *did* knock it over."

"The cat was blind, Edmund, and dead, and had been for some years." But there was no real edge in it. She sipped her tea and watched him over the rim. "Why did you run?"

And there it was. The question he'd been outrunning since spring, catching up with him in a warm kitchen that smelled like his childhood.

"You know why," he said.

"Say it anyway."

"Because you asked for another tithe." His hands tightened around the cup. "Because every year it was something else. The day my mother sang in the kitchen. The smell of the harbor at Carrowgate. The name of the boy who taught me dice. You took them and put them in jars and I could feel them going, like teeth coming loose, and you never told me what you *did* with them—"

"I told you. Memory keeps. Memory is the only coin that doesn't rust."

"That's not an answer, that's a sales pitch."

Maren set down her cup. For a moment she looked old — genuinely old, not the theatrical age she wore like a shawl but the kind that lived underneath. "No," she said quietly. "It isn't. And you deserve better than that, though you won't get it tonight, because tonight isn't about us." Her gaze dropped again to his coat. "Tonight is about whatever you've got stitched against your ribs that made three separate charms on my gatepost sing out warnings as you walked up the path. Take it out."

Edmund's mouth went dry. "How did you know—"

"Because you're sitting like a man protecting a wound, and because you never learned to lie to me with your body even when you managed it with your mouth." She held out her hand, palm up. An old teacher's gesture. He had seen it a thousand times. *Give it here, boy, and we'll see what we've got.*

Every instinct screamed at him to run again. He was good at running. He'd been doing it his whole life — from slavers, from Maren, from a name he couldn't say without his chest hurting.

But the courier's blood was still under his fingernails in his mind, no matter how often he scrubbed. And the memory of snow that wasn't his had woken him twice last night, a woman's voice saying *hold on, hold on, we're nearly there,* in a language he didn't speak and somehow understood.

He unpicked the terrible stitches and laid the map on the table between them.

* * *

Maren did not touch it at first.

She leaned over it the way a physician leans over a patient whose illness she recognizes and hoped never to see again. The wax seal — unbroken, because Edmund was a coward as well as a smuggler, and had never quite dared open it — caught the firelight and threw it back red.

"Where did you get this?" she asked.

"A dying man in Corbie's Ravine. A courier. There was a mark on his throat—" he touched his own throat, below the ear, "—like a brand, but wrong. Like a sigil drawn by someone who hated sigils."

"Veyra's mark." Maren said it flatly, without surprise, and that frightened him more than anything else so far. "Open it."

"It burned everyone who touched it in Dunmarrow. Not burned — the broker who looked at it saw things, memories that weren't hers, and she went white as milk and wouldn't take payment. Everyone who touches it remembers something they never lived. I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "I thought maybe you'd know why. Or maybe you'd throw me out. Those were roughly my two plans."

"And the third plan?"

"There wasn't one. I told you, I'm not good at planning. I'm good at leaving."

Something flickered across her face at that — hurt, quickly banked, the coals of it still visible. Then she nodded once, briskly, and broke the seal herself.

The map unfurled across the table, and Edmund braced for the flood, the intrusive memories, the snow and the woman's voice and the taste of a fruit he'd never eaten.

Instead, Maren went still.

Not the ordinary stillness of reading. This was the stillness of a deer that has heard something in the brush — total, animal, every part of her locked in place while some ancient part of her decided whether to bolt. The fire crackled. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Edmund counted ten of his own heartbeats before she moved at all, and when she did, it was only to lay one finger, very lightly, on a point near the map's center, where the inked passages beneath Thornfell mountain opened into a chamber marked with a symbol like an eye seen through water.

"She drew it true," Maren whispered. "After all these years. Someone finally drew it true."

"Maren?"

"My sister's handwriting." She said it the way other people report a death. "This is Liss's drafting hand. She was the finest cartographer in the free cities before—" Her finger stayed on the eye-through-water. "Do you know what this is, Edmund?"

"The court beneath the mountain. The pale court. Every smuggler's grandmother swears it exists and every smuggler swears it doesn't."

"It exists." Maren straightened. She looked, suddenly, twenty years younger and infinitely more dangerous, the way a blade looks when it's finally been taken off the wall. "I traded there once. Thirty-one years ago. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I walked through that gate, and I bargained with the pale steward, and I paid—" Her voice caught, just barely, a thread pulled tight. "I paid the only price they asked, and I lost my sister's soul to them, and I have spent thirty-one years telling myself the stories were exaggerated, that nothing truly valuable could be bought and sold in jars, that I imagined the whole thing because grief makes liars of us all."

Edmund stared at her. In six years of apprenticeship she had never spoken of a sister. She had never spoken of anything before the day he arrived on her doorstep, a half-starved child with a stolen loaf and a talent for noticing things. He had assumed she had no past. He had assumed, in the arrogant way of children, that she had simply been *made*, fully formed, out of rosemary and bad temper.

"What did you trade for?" he asked quietly. "That was worth it?"

"Nothing." She laughed, and it was a terrible sound, bright and broken. "That's the joke of it, boy. I traded for nothing. I was young and proud and I wanted to see if the court was real, and the steward let me look at the vaults, and there — on a shelf, between a farmer's last harvest festival and a soldier's wedding day — was a jar with Liss's laugh inside it. I knew the laugh. I'd have known it anywhere. And I understood that she had sold it, willingly, years before, for reasons I never learned because she died before I found the courage to ask." Her hand closed slowly into a fist on the table. "So I made my own bargain. I offered them anything for it back. Anything. And the steward smiled — they smile like cracks in porcelain — and said the ledger does not run backward. Debts may be added, never subtracted. And then it charged me a tithe for the *asking*. Took the memory of her funeral. I don't remember burying my sister. I remember knowing that I did, the way you know a fact from a book."

The fire popped. Edmund realized his tea had gone cold in his hands.

"The courier," he said. "The dying man. He pressed this into my hands and said a name." He hesitated. He had not said it aloud since Dunmarrow. It came out rough, like a stone pulled from a boot. "Sable."

Maren's head came up sharply.

"He sold his soul," Edmund went on, faster now, the words spilling because if he stopped he'd never start again. "To Veyra, or through Veyra, I don't know — the memories that leak off this map, they're his, or the courier's, or both, and there's snow in them, and a road, and he was buying time for the free cities, I think, trading himself for a delay, and I keep seeing—" His voice cracked and he hated it. "I keep seeing a night on a mountain pass. A fire. Someone laughing. And it isn't mine, but it fits me like a glove, and I don't know anymore which memories are mine and which ones are leaking, and I am so *tired*, Maren."

Silence. Maren studied him for a long moment, and he watched her arrive at the truth of him the way she arrived at the truth of any ingredient — by turning it over, sniffing it, refusing to be fooled by the packaging.

"Sable," she said. "The smuggler. The one who pulled you out of the slaver caravan when you were twelve and vanished before you could thank him."

"You *knew*?"

"You talked in your sleep for a month after you came to me. 'Sable, wait, Sable, come back.'" She said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "You ran from me in spring because of the tithes. But you'd have run from me eventually regardless, because you were already running from a grief you refused to carry, and this house was too quiet for a boy trying not to think." She rose, went to the shelf above the hearth, and began pulling down jars with sudden, ferocious purpose — salt, ash, a black powder that smelled like thunderstorms. "Well. Now the grief has found you, wearing a dead courier's boots and carrying a map to hell. That's usually how it happens. Grief is patient. It waits until you're cornered."

"What are you doing?"

"Packing. What does it look like?" She swept vials into a leather roll with the efficiency of a woman who had done this before, long ago, and never expected to do it again. "You came here expecting punishment. I can see it in how you're sitting. Well, I'm sorry to disappoint you — punishment would require me to care enough to bother, and instead I find I care far too much, which is infinitely less comfortable."

"Maren." He stood. "What are you saying?"

She tied the roll shut and turned to face him, and the firelight made her shadow enormous on the rafters.

"I'm saying I know the way into that court, because I've walked it. I'm saying I owe that place a debt thirty-one years old, and debts to the pale court accrue interest, and I would very much like to settle mine while I still have memories left worth stealing. I'm saying that whoever drew this map meant it to reach Sable, and Sable cannot walk, and you are standing in my kitchen holding his soul's forwarding address." She jabbed a finger at him. "I will help you. I will teach you what the map is, what the court is, how to bargain with things that smile like cracks in porcelain. And in exchange—"

"Here it comes," Edmund muttered. "Another tithe."

"In exchange," Maren said, "you will owe me a price. To be named later."

Edmund laughed despite himself — a short, disbelieving bark. "'Named later'? That's worse. That's so much worse than a tithe. Even I know that. Open-ended debts to a hedge-witch are how apprentices end up as toads."

"Yes," Maren agreed serenely, pulling her traveling cloak from its peg. "Isn't it wonderful? Now you'll actually stay somewhere, out of sheer self-preservation. It's the first thing that's ever worked." She paused at the door, and for a moment her voice softened into something almost gentle. "You should eat before we go. And Edmund — the memory leaking off that map, the one with the fire and the laughing. On the mountain road."

His breath stopped. "What about it?"

"Keep it close. Whatever else you pay, whatever they take — keep that one close." She opened the door onto the rain-washed dark. "Trust me. I know exactly which memories are worth the carrying."

Outside, far off across the fens, something hummed — low and vast, felt in the teeth more than heard, like a bell buried in the bones of the world. Wren would have known what it was. Edmund only knew that Maren went rigid beside him, listening, and that when she spoke again her voice had gone quiet and certain as a drawn line on a map.

"Well," she said. "The mountain knows we're coming. That's either very good or very bad."

"Which?"

"I haven't the faintest idea, dear. Isn't it exciting?"
