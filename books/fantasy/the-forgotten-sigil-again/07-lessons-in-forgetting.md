## 7. Lessons in Forgetting

Maren's cottage smelled the way it always had: rosemary, woodsmoke, and something faintly burnt that she insisted was medicine and Edmund insisted was dinner she'd given up on.

"You'll sleep in the loft," she said, not looking up from the kettle. "Same as when you were fourteen. Same as when you were fifteen. Same as the night you climbed out the window with my good scissors and left a note that said *gone to be free, don't follow*."

"I said don't follow."

"And yet here you are, following me around my own kitchen like a guilty dog." She set two cups down—chamomile for him, and for herself something dark enough that he could smell the bitterness from across the room. "Sit. We have work."

Edmund sat. The table between them was scarred with old sigils, burned and scratched into the wood over decades, some of them his own childish attempts. He recognized one near his elbow: a wobbly little mark for *safekeeping* he'd carved at eleven, convinced he was a genius.

"The map," Maren said, laying it out flat. The wax seal had been broken days ago, but the parchment still did its quiet, horrible work. When Edmund looked at it too long, he saw ravines he'd never walked and heard a woman's voice saying *you're late, Corvin* in a tone of fond exasperation. "It's not just ink. It's soaked. Whoever made this bled memory into every line—the routes, the seals, the names. That's why people remember things touching it. It's less a map than a diary pretending to be one."

"Can you un-soak it?"

"No. But I can teach you to swim." She fixed him with the look she'd used on him when he was twelve and had claimed, with total sincerity, that a weasel had eaten his homework. "You've been leaking since Dunmarrow. I can smell another man's grief on you like cheap perfume. So. Lessons."

"Lessons," Edmund repeated flatly.

"You wanted to be a hedge-witch's apprentice once. Badly. You cried about it."

"I was *nine*—"

"And now you're going to get what you asked for, which is the great joke of adulthood, so drink your tea."

* * *

The first lesson was carrying.

"Every memory has weight," Maren said. She held out her palm, and above it hovered a small grey light, no bigger than a moth. It pulsed gently, like something breathing. "This is a recollection. Mine—a market day, forty years ago, honey cakes. Light things are easy to hold. Heavy things—" the light darkened, thickened, sank toward her hand like a stone through water, "—heavy things drag."

"What's that one?"

"My sister's wedding." Her voice didn't change at all, which was how Edmund knew it cost her. "I keep it close. Some people bury their heavy ones. I prefer mine where I can see them. Burying is how you lose track of what you actually paid."

She closed her fist and the light went out. "Now you. Close your eyes. Find the courier's memories—the ones leaking into you. Don't fight them. Fighting makes them stick. Just... find the edge of one."

Edmund closed his eyes. It wasn't hard. The courier's memories had been seeping into him for weeks, like damp through a wall. He found one near the surface: snow, falling sideways, and someone laughing—

He flinched.

"There," Maren said quietly. "What is it?"

"Snow. Someone laughing. It's not—it doesn't feel like mine, but it feels *good*, and that's worse somehow. Like wearing a dead man's coat because it's warmer than yours."

"Mm. Now, instead of pushing it away, cup your hands around it. Mentally. Like holding water."

Edmund cupped. The memory flickered—and settled, suddenly distinct, suddenly *shaped*. Snow on a mountain pass. A young man with a crooked grin and frost in his dark hair, throwing a snowball at the courier—Corvin—with terrible aim.

"That's it," Maren said. "You've stopped leaking and started *holding*. A memory you hold is a memory you can examine. A memory you can examine is a memory you can hide, move, or forge. Those are your three tools. Hide, move, forge. Say it."

"Hide, move, forge."

"Again. You were a slow student at nine too."

"I was a *delightful* student at nine, you told my mother so."

"I lied to your mother constantly. Again."

* * *

Hiding, it turned out, was the easiest. A hidden memory was tucked behind one of your own—a false front, like a smuggler's compartment built into a wagon wall. Edmund practiced by hiding the snow-memory behind his recollection of buying boots in Dunmarrow, and for three hours afterward, whenever he thought about boots, he briefly smelled cold air.

Moving was harder. Moving meant taking a memory out of yourself entirely and putting it somewhere else—an object, usually, or another person willing to carry it. Maren demonstrated with a pebble, pouring into it a small annoyance (a neighbor's rooster) that she'd apparently been hoarding for six years.

"Why would anyone want this?" Edmund asked, turning the pebble over.

"Because someday I'll want to give that rooster's owner a very specific piece of my mind, and I want it fresh." She took the pebble back, absorbed the annoyance, and scowled with renewed vigor. "See? Perfectly preserved spite. Memory-keeping is mostly spite and love, boy. Everything else is bookkeeping."

Forging was the hardest, and the one she taught last, after dark, with candles.

"A forged memory isn't a lie exactly," she said. "It's a true thing wearing a borrowed shape. You take real feeling—real *substance*—and attach it to invented details. Do it well, and even the person it's planted in can't tell. Do it badly—" she tapped his forehead with one finger, "—and it sits in the mind like a fish bone. Every thought catches on it."

"Why would I ever need to do that?"

"Because you're going into a court that eats memories, wearing memories that aren't yours, hunting a man whose soul is in a jar. You will need to lie with your own mind, Edmund. To wardens who can taste truth like salt." She paused. "And because whoever comes looking for that map will look at *you* and expect to find Corvin's ghost in your head. Better they find a forgery than the real thing."

That night, Edmund practiced forging until his temples throbbed. He built a false memory of meeting Corvin in a tavern—real tavern, real ale-stink, invented conversation—and hid it behind his memory of the dying man in the ravine. When he checked it later, it almost convinced *him*.

Almost. In the forged version, Corvin ordered cider. In the true memory, buried deeper, Corvin had coughed blood onto the snow and gripped Edmund's wrist hard enough to bruise and said a name.

Sable.

* * *

On the fourth day, Maren spread the map again and did something Edmund hadn't seen her do before: she listened to it. Literally leaned down, ear nearly to the parchment, eyes closed, one hand hovering.

"You're being strange," Edmund said.

"Hush. I'm reading the marginalia." Her lips moved slightly. Then she went still in a way that made the hair on his arms rise—not the stillness of concentration, but the stillness of someone who has stepped on a stair that isn't there.

"Maren?"

"Corvin," she said slowly, "was not a courier."

"He—"

"Couriers carry messages. Corvin was carrying a *person's name* to a *specific place*, with a route charted past every toll, every checkpoint, every tithe-post between here and Thornfell's deep gate. This isn't a delivery route. It's a rescue plan." She straightened and looked at Edmund, and there was something in her face he'd only seen twice before: once when his father died, and once when she caught him stealing her sigil-book and, instead of punishing him, taught him the sigil he'd been trying to steal. "Someone hired him to reach the mountain and bring word to a man named Sable. Word of how to get in. Word of how to get *out*."

The kitchen was very quiet. The burnt-medicine smell hung between them.

"That can't be right," Edmund said. "Sable's already *in* there. His soul's in a jar. Veyra bought him years ago—I heard it in Dunmarrow, everyone's heard it—"

"Bought. Yes." Maren sat down heavily. "But bought *how*, Edmund? Think. Veyra buys souls wholesale—from debtors, from prisoners, from the desperate. But Sable?" She shook her head. "Sable was never desperate a day in his life. Smugglers with gold in their boots and friends in every city don't sell their souls by accident."

"Then why—"

"Because the free cities needed time." She said it plainly, the way you'd report weather. "Veyra's power runs through the court beneath Thornfell—the soul-trade, the tithes, all of it flows through that mountain. And a court like that can't be fought from outside. Someone had to go *in*. Voluntarily. With his soul intact enough to matter, and his will strong enough to survive the jar." She met his eyes. "Sable didn't get captured, boy. Sable *enlisted*."

Edmund stood up so fast his chair went over.

"No. No, that's—he wouldn't. He *abandoned* me first, he vanished, he didn't even—" His voice cracked and he hated it. "He left. People who leave don't get to be heroes."

"People who leave," Maren said gently, "are sometimes leaving to do the hardest thing available. Sit down."

"I don't want to sit down."

"Sit down anyway, you're making the kitchen anxious."

He sat. The chair stayed upright this time, out of what he assumed was professional courtesy.

"Think about Corvin," Maren continued. "A man who knew Sable—look at the snow-memory, the fondness in it. A man who learned there was a way into the court, a ledger, a door that opens for debts. Who spent months mapping a route through Veyra's whole apparatus to bring Sable word: *there's a way out, there's a way to break this from inside.* And he died in a ravine with the map still sealed, and the name still unsaid—until he said it to *you*."

"To me," Edmund whispered. "Of all the people in the world. To the one man who abandoned him."

"Yes." Maren let that sit. She was always good at letting things sit; it was her most terrifying skill. "You've been treating this map like a curse. Something that dragged you back into a war you fled. But curses don't route themselves past every tithe-post. Coincidences don't die holding your wrist." She pushed the tea toward him. "Corvin couldn't finish his errand. So the road finished it for him, the way roads do—by putting the map in the hands of the one person alive who would burn the world down to reach Sable, if he could only stop feeling sorry for himself long enough to notice that's what he'd do."

Edmund stared at the map. The lines seemed different now. Not a trap. An itinerary.

"He sold his soul," Edmund said slowly, "to buy the cities time. And I ran from a witch because remembering him hurt."

"Yes."

"And now I'm sitting in your kitchen drinking chamomile."

"You're drinking *my* chamomile, which you complained about for four days, yes."

* * *

That night, Edmund climbed to the loft and lay under the eaves where he'd slept as a boy, and held the snow-memory in both hands, mentally speaking, and examined it properly for the first time.

Not just snow. Not just laughter. Corvin and Sable on a pass above Dunmarrow, a supply run, years ago—Sable throwing snow like a child, Corvin calling him an idiot with enormous affection, Sable saying, breathless and grinning: *"When this is over, Corv, I'm going to find him. The kid from the caravan. I owe him a debt I never paid, and you know how I feel about debts."*

Edmund lay in the dark with his heart hammering.

*You know how I feel about debts.*

The mountain's gate opened for those owing them. Everyone said so. Wren the pickpocket had said it in Dunmarrow, giggling, before anyone knew what she was talking about. The broker had gone white and refused payment. Debts opened doors.

Sable owed *him*.

And Edmund—Edmund owed Sable a life, an apology, eleven years of silence, and the simple human decency of having stayed. He owed more than anyone alive, maybe. He'd certainly never met anyone who'd collected fewer.

Downstairs, Maren's floorboards creaked. She was awake too, then. Probably doing sums of her own—her sister's soul was in that court somewhere, shelved among thousands, and Maren had lost her once to these ledgers and had clearly been doing arithmetic ever since.

In the morning, Edmund came down to find her packing. Not bags—she was hedge-witch, she traveled with what she could burn—but lists. Long ones. Names, crossed out. Names circled.

"We'll need help," she said without preamble. "People the tithe-men don't watch, because the tithe-men think they're nothing. Debtors. Deserters. Thieves. The invisible ones." She glanced up. "You were a smuggler, Edmund. You know where the invisible ones live."

"I know a few," he admitted. "There's a deserter in Lowbridge who remembers nothing of her war service—not a day of it. Bought clean, or stolen. There's a tithe-man who went rogue last spring; half the guild wants his head. And there's a pickpocket who swears the mountain hums to her."

"A pickpocket who hears the mountain." Maren wrote the name down. "Either mad or useful. Historically the categories overlap." She blew on the ink. "Eat something. Tomorrow we start walking, and you're going to need your strength, because you're about to do the bravest stupid thing of your life, and I intend to be there to say I told you so."

Edmund ate. Outside, the wind came down off Thornfell's shoulder, and if you listened—really listened, the way Wren supposedly did—you could almost believe the mountain was humming.

Almost believing was, he was learning, how everything important started.
