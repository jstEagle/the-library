## 8. The Company of Debts

Maren's cottage smelled the same as it always had—dried yarrow, woodsmoke, and something faintly burnt that she insisted was intentional. Edmund spent three days there learning to hold the courier's memories without letting them spill out of him like water from a cracked jug, and on the fourth morning she looked at him over her tea and said, "You can't do this alone."

"I've done everything alone," Edmund said.

"Yes, and look how well it's going. You're sitting in a witch's kitchen with a stolen ledger in your coat and a dead man's grief behind your eyes." She set down her cup. "The court beneath Thornfell doesn't open for lone wolves, boy. It opens for debtors. And you—" she pointed at him with a spoon, "—are going to need people who owe as much as you do."

"I don't *owe* anyone."

"That's the saddest lie you've ever told me, and I once watched you swear you hadn't eaten my honey cakes when the evidence was still on your chin."

He wanted to argue. He'd gotten good at arguing with Maren over the years, the way you get good at any exercise you practice daily. But the map sat between them on the table, and when he touched its edge he felt the courier's last mile again—the cold, the wet stone, the name *Sable* shaped by dying lips—and he thought about how many miles lay between here and the mountain's gate.

"Fine," he said. "Who?"

* * *

Ivetta found them before they found her, which Maren said was how deserters usually worked.

She came into the Drowned Bell tavern in Dunmarrow's lower quarter while Edmund was nursing a beer he had no intention of finishing, and she sat down across from him uninvited, which he respected immediately. She was broad-shouldered and gray-eyed, with the kind of face that made strangers assume she was angry even when she wasn't. She wasn't angry now. She was studying him the way a butcher studies a carcass.

"You're the map-man," she said.

"I'm nobody."

"Everyone in this city is nobody. It's why I like it here." She leaned forward. "But you're a nobody who's asking questions about Thornfell, and questions get people tithed. So either you're very stupid or you're planning something, and stupid men don't sit like they're counting exits."

Edmund counted his exits. There were three, and she was sitting in front of the best one.

"What do you want?" he asked.

"To come along. Whatever it is." She said it flatly, without ceremony, as if she were ordering bread. "I was a sergeant in the Veyran third column before I walked away from it. I remember the marching. I remember the pay. I remember every road between here and the coast." Her jaw tightened, just slightly. "I don't remember the year I spent in the Ashvale campaign. They took it. Tithe-men, on the eve of battle—they said it was for morale. Took the worst of it out of us so we'd keep fighting." She turned her cup around and around on the table. "Now there's a hole where a year used to be, and the hole has teeth. Some nights I wake up sure I did something unforgivable. I'd rather find out than keep wondering."

"And if you find out you did?"

"Then I'll know what I'm carrying." She met his eyes. "You've got that look too. Like a man carrying someone else's luggage. I know the weight when I see it."

Edmund thought about saying no. He thought about how Maren would laugh at him for saying no. He slid his beer across the table toward her instead.

"We leave in four days," he said. "There's two more we need."

"I know one of them already," Ivetta said, and drank his beer.

* * *

Brannoc was harder.

They found him—or rather, Wren found him, and dragged the rest of them to a tanner's yard on the edge of the tanners' quarter where the smell alone could strip paint. Brannoc was mending a hide with the patient hands of a man who had learned to love quiet work, and when he saw Ivetta his whole body went rigid.

"Sergeant," he said.

"Tithe-man," she said back, and the word landed between them like a dropped anvil.

"I'm not—not anymore." Brannoc set down his awl slowly, the way you move when you think someone might be about to draw steel. "Eleven years I collected. Memories, mostly. Debts owed to the crown. I told myself it was honest work. Someone has to keep the ledgers, right? Someone has to make people pay what they promised." His voice had the worn smoothness of a speech rehearsed too many times. "Then last spring I was sent to collect from a widow in Harrowgate. She owed nine years of memories for her husband's debts. Nine years. She asked me if she could keep one day. Just one—the day her daughter was born. She offered me everything else and begged for that single day, and I—" He stopped. His hands had started shaking; he looked at them with something close to betrayal. "I took all nine years anyway. Because the quota was the quota. And then I went home and couldn't remember why I'd become a tithe-man in the first place, because I'd sold *that* memory years ago to cover my own debts. I'd been feeding myself into the same machine."

Nobody spoke. In the distance, a dog barked at nothing in particular.

"So now I mend hides," Brannoc finished. "And I wait."

"For what?" Edmund asked.

"For someone to give me a job I can't refuse." Brannoc looked up, and his eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "What's the job?"

Edmund laid the map on the workbench between them. Brannoc looked at it for a long moment, and then he laughed—a short, broken sound with no humor in it at all.

"The pale court," he said. "Do you know what I collected *for*? Where the tithes go? I never knew. We carried the jars to waystations and never asked past that. But I heard things. Rumors, from the old collectors, before Veyra streamlined the routes." He tapped the map with one scarred finger. "This is real. This is where they all go." He looked up at Edmund. "Every jar I ever carried. Every widow. Every debtor. They're all under that mountain."

"Then you know the way," Edmund said.

"I know the way," Brannoc agreed quietly. "And I owe passage to everyone I ever put in a jar. All of them. At once." He picked up his awl and set it down again, decisively this time. "When do we leave?"

* * *

Wren they didn't find at all. Wren found Edmund's purse.

It happened in the market square on their last morning in Dunmarrow, in the middle of buying travel rations. One moment Edmund's coin-pouch was at his belt; the next moment it was gone, and a small figure in a patched brown coat was sprinting through the fish stalls with the effortless grace of someone who had been dodging authority since before they could read.

Edmund chased. He wasn't fast, but he was stubborn, and stubbornness counts for something in crowded markets. He cornered the thief in an alley behind the chandler's shop, breathless, and found himself looking at a kid—fourteen at most, sharp-faced, freckled, with hair cropped short and eyes that kept flicking to the alley's other exit.

"Give it back," Edmund panted.

"Make me." The kid grinned. Then the grin faltered. The kid was looking past Edmund's shoulder—at the map case slung across his back, half-visible under his coat. "Wait. Wait, you're the one who smells like the mountain."

"I beg your pardon?"

"You smell like Thornfell. Like deep-down Thornfell, under the stone." The kid stepped closer, suddenly unafraid, which Edmund found far more alarming than the running had been. "I hear it humming. Have since I was little. Everyone says I'm mad, but I hear it, especially at night, especially lately—it's gotten louder, like something turning over in its sleep." The kid's eyes were wide and fierce. "And now here's a man walking through my city smelling like the inside of it. So either you tell me what's going on, or I scream 'thief' and let the watch have you, and believe me, friend, nobody looks innocent in this alley."

Edmund considered his options. They were poor ones. Behind him, he heard Ivetta's boots stop at the mouth of the alley, and Brannoc's heavier tread beside hers, and he realized with a strange lurch that for the first time in years he wasn't facing trouble alone.

"Wren, is it?" he said. "I've heard about you. The pickpocket who hears the mountain."

The kid's chin came up. "Wren. And I'm not a pickpocket, I'm a redistribution specialist."

"You stole my purse."

"Redistributed it. Temporarily." Wren tossed it back; Edmund caught it against his chest. "So. The mountain. Talk."

Edmund talked. He gave them the shape of it—the map, the courier, Sable's name, the court beneath the stone where souls were bought and shelved like preserves. He watched Wren's expression shift from calculation to hunger to something quieter and rawer as he described the humming.

"It's not madness," Wren whispered, when he finished. "It's really there. Under the stone. Something's *there*."

"Something is," Edmund said. "And I'm going down to it. I can't promise you anything except danger and probably a bad end."

"See, most adults try to bribe me with safety." Wren shouldered a small bag that had apparently been packed for some time—a detail Edmund chose not to examine. "Danger and a bad end. That's the first honest offer I've had all year." The kid stuck out a hand, small and calloused. "I'm in. But I want it noted that I'm the only one of us who isn't running *from* something. I'm running *toward*. That makes me the optimist."

"It makes you the lunatic," Ivetta said from the alley mouth.

"Optimist," Wren repeated, and shook Edmund's hand with a grip far stronger than it had any right to be.

* * *

They left Dunmarrow at dawn, five of them on the north road: a deserter with a hole full of teeth in her memory, a rogue tithe-man carrying eleven years of guilt, a pickpocket who hummed along with a mountain two hundred miles away, a hedge-witch's former apprentice with a dead man's memories leaking through his skull, and—though none of them knew it yet—a smuggler's soul waiting in the dark beneath the world.

They made strange company. By the second evening, camped in the shelter of a ruined waystation, Edmund caught himself laughing at something Brannoc said—actually laughing, the sound surprising him on the way out like a sneeze—and he looked around the fire at these damaged, indebted, invisible people and felt something he hadn't felt since he was twelve years old on a mountain road with a smuggler's arm around his shoulders.

Belonging. Or the rough draft of it, at least.

"Don't get comfortable," Ivetta said, catching his expression. She was sharpening her knife with slow, even strokes. "We haven't even reached the pass yet."

"Which pass?" Wren asked, mouth full of hardtack.

"Widow's Teeth," Brannoc said, and the fire seemed to dim a little at the name. "Only way through to Thornfell's southern gate since Veyra closed the low roads. It's called that because the rock spires take travelers the way widows in the old stories took husbands. Slowly. And keeping whatever they take."

"Lovely," said Wren. "Any other cheerful facts?"

Brannoc hesitated. He looked into the fire, and when he spoke again his voice had gone careful and flat, the voice of a man reading from a ledger he wished he could burn.

"One more thing. The burning villages we've seen from the ridgelines—that pattern, the way the fires are set in a line, driving survivors toward the passes." He swallowed. "That's a third-column technique. I helped design the supply routes for it."

Ivetta's knife stopped moving.

The silence stretched, long and thin as wire. Edmund watched Ivetta's hand tighten on the haft, watched Brannoc not flinch, watched Wren glance between them with wide eyes—and understood that the next chapter of their unlikely company would be written tonight, one way or another, around this fire.

"Ivetta," Edmund said quietly. "Put the knife down. There's something you both need to hear first—something I've never told anyone."

The knife did not move. The fire crackled. Somewhere far to the north, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, Edmund thought he could almost hear the mountain humming—and Wren, head cocked, heard it too.
