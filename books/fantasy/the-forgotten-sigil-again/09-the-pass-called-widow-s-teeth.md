## 9. The Pass Called Widow's Teeth

The pass had a name like a warning and a shape like a broken promise, and Brannoc announced both with the cheerfulness of a man who had never once been responsible for anything.

"Widow's Teeth," he said, gesturing at the ridge ahead with his one good hand. "See how the stones come up sharp along the top? Like a jaw. Somebody's grandmother named it, back when grandmothers still got to name things."

"It's called that because widows made it," Ivetta said flatly. She was walking at the front, the way she always did, as if she expected the mountain to attack from behind and wanted to be there to meet it. "Men fell off. Women buried them. Then they kept using it anyway, because the other way around was three days longer."

"That's the spirit," said Brannoc.

Edmund adjusted the strap of his pack and said nothing. He had learned, in the weeks since Dunmarrow, that silence was a kind of currency among this company. Wren spent hers in humming. Ivetta spent hers in watching. Brannoc, who had once been a tithe-man with brass needles and a ledger of other people's lives, spent his in talking — an endless, cheerful torrent of talk that Edmund suspected was the only thing standing between the man and whatever waited underneath it.

And Wren — Wren had stopped humming.

That was the first wrong thing. She'd been humming since the tunnels under Dunmarrow, a low thread of sound she claimed came from the mountain itself, *it likes when I do it back*, and Edmund had gotten so used to it that its absence rang louder than any alarm. Now she walked close beside him, her small hand hooked into the back of his belt like a child's, and her eyes were on the valley below.

"Smoke," she said.

He looked. Far down and eastward, where the foothills softened into farmland, a gray feather of smoke rose from a place that should have been too small to matter. A village. One chimney's worth of burning, except chimneys didn't burn all at once, and didn't send up smoke in that flat, greedy column that meant a whole roofline going at speed.

"Veyra's vanguard," Brannoc said. His voice had changed. The cheer was still in it, but stretched thin now, like cloth over a frame. "Scouts burn what they can't garrison. Standard practice."

"You would know," Ivetta said, without turning around.

"I would know," Brannoc agreed, and for once he didn't dress it up.

They climbed.

* * *

Widow's Teeth earned its name by midmorning. The path narrowed to a shelf of stone barely wide enough for two boots abreast, with a drop on the left that went down long enough to develop opinions about you on the way past. Wind came up the face of the mountain in slow, muscular gusts, and each one pressed against them like a large polite hand saying *not yet, not yet*.

Edmund hated heights. He had never told anyone this, because being forgettable meant having no weaknesses anyone could use, but the mountain wasn't asking his permission. Every gust made his stomach perform small acrobatics, and he found himself fixing his eyes on the pack straps in front of him — Ivetta's, then Brannoc's — and counting steps. Forty-one. Forty-two. Below, through the gaps between his boots, the world went on burning quietly.

"There's another one," Wren said.

She was right. A second column of smoke had joined the first, farther north, and then — as they watched from the height, helpless as birds on a wire — a third. Not random. Edmund's smuggler's eye did the arithmetic without being asked: the fires were spaced evenly, a day's march apart, each one sitting where a road met water. Where people gathered. Where a fleeing population would naturally go.

"They're herding them," he said.

"Yes," Brannoc said.

His voice was very quiet now, and Edmund turned to look at him properly. The big man had stopped walking. He stood with his hand braced against the rock wall, staring down at the valley, and his face had gone the color of old tallow.

"Brannoc," Edmund said.

"The spacing." Brannoc swallowed. "A day's march apart. Roads and water. Burn the gathering points, leave the paths open, drive them toward the garrison towns where they can be counted and tithed. It's called the Shepherd's Pattern." He laughed, one dry bark with nothing in it. "I named it that. God help me, I sat in a room in Veyra's war college with a map and some counters, and I moved little wooden pieces around, and I named it the Shepherd's Pattern because I thought it was clever."

Nobody said anything. The wind pushed at them. Down in the valley, somebody's whole life was making that flat gray column.

"How many villages?" Ivetta asked. Her voice was level in the way ice is level.

"In the original design? Fourteen." Brannoc's hand slid against the stone, finding no grip. "It scales."

Ivetta turned around.

Edmund had seen her angry before — at toll-keepers, at Brannoc generally, at the universe's habit of putting stairs in places. This was different. This was very quiet, and it moved the way water moves before it decides to be a flood. She crossed the shelf of rock in four strides, took Brannoc by the collar with her good arm, and walked him backward until his heels found the edge of the drop.

"Ivetta," Edmund said.

"He *designed* it." She didn't raise her voice. That was the frightening part. "You want to know why I don't remember my war crimes, Edmund? Because someone paid for them. Someone stood over me with a needle and took them out and sold them, and I woke up clean, and I have spent every day since trying to decide whether I'm the woman who did those things or the woman who doesn't know. And this man—" she shook him once, gently, which was worse than hard, "—this man kept his. Kept every one. And used them to draw maps for her."

"Ivetta." Edmund stepped closer. The wind gusted; the drop yawned. "Look at his hands."

She didn't look. But Brannoc did, and Edmund saw her see it — the way the big man's fingers were spread flat against the stone, not fighting, not grabbing at her arm. Surrendering. Had been surrendering since the word *Pattern* left his mouth.

"He could have lied," Edmund said. "He's been lying cheerfully since Dunmarrow. It's practically his native language. He could have said *raiders, bandits, hard to say*. Instead he gave us the name and the number and the man who drew it, and the man who drew it is standing right here with your fist in his collar."

"Confession isn't absolution."

"No," Edmund agreed. "It isn't. Believe me, I know."

Something in his own voice surprised him. Ivetta heard it too. Her grip loosened by a degree, her eyes flicking to him, waiting.

Below them, the third column of smoke bent in the wind, and Edmund found that the words were already coming, the way a sneeze comes, inevitable and undignified.

"When I was twelve," he said, "a slaver caravan picked me up outside Corveil. My mother had died in the spring and I'd run through the grief-money by summer, and I was small, and I was alone, which is the whole job description as far as slavers are concerned. There was a boy on that caravan. Older. Called himself Sable because he said his real name belonged to somebody better than him." Edmund's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "He got me out. Third night, cut the wagon rope, put me on a horse, slapped its rump, and told me to ride north and never look back. And I rode north. And I never looked back."

The wind filled the pause. Somewhere below, faintly, Wren had started to hum again — just a thread of it, barely there, as if even she understood this was a moment that needed holding together.

"Sixteen years later," Edmund said, "a dying man pressed a map into my hands and whispered Sable's name, and I learned that the boy who saved my life sold his soul to buy the free cities time. And do you know what my first feeling was? Not grief. *Anger*. At the courier. At the map. At the mountain. Because he'd dragged me back into caring about something, and caring is a debt, and I had built my entire life on owing nothing to anyone." He looked at Ivetta. "I abandoned him twice. Once when I rode away, and once when I decided that riding away was wisdom instead of cowardice. So when I tell you confession isn't absolution, I'm not philosophizing. I'm telling you what I'm carrying."

Ivetta's fist was still in Brannoc's collar. But her weight had shifted off her front foot.

"What do you want to do with him?" Edmund asked. "Throw him off the pass? Fine. Then we lose the only man among us who knows Veyra's patterns from the inside — the burns, the garrisons, the roads she'll leave open because she wants us to take them. You want to keep him alive and hate him? Also fine. That's harder. That's the one I'd pick. But it's yours to pick. You're the one holding him."

For a long moment the only sounds were wind and Wren's hum and, impossibly far below, the small indifferent noises of a burning world.

Then Ivetta let go. She did it all at once, stepping back as if the collar had burned her, and she turned to face the cliff wall, and her shoulders were shaking in a way that Edmund pretended not to see, because pretending not to see was sometimes the only kindness available.

"If we find a village with people still in it," she said to the rock, "you go in first."

"Yes," Brannoc said hoarsely.

"And if there's a needle anywhere in your kit—"

"There isn't. I melted it. First night after I ran." He managed something like his old grin, and for the first time Edmund could see the fear behind it clearly, and respected it more than the grin. "What do you think I've been talking about ever since? A man with nothing left to hide has to fill the silence somehow."

Despite everything — despite the smoke and the drop and the ache behind his sternum — Edmund huffed a laugh. "That explains so much."

* * *

They made the high shoulder of the pass by late afternoon, where the Teeth themselves reared up — a crown of shattered granite spikes against a sky going copper. From there Edmund could see the whole descent into the next valley, and what he saw stopped him with his boot half-raised.

The path down was gone.

Not blocked — *gone*. A section of the shelf perhaps fifty paces long had simply dropped away, leaving a raw scar of fresh rock and a scatter of boulders down the mountainside, and at the far end of the gap, small in the failing light, figures were moving. Men in Veyra's colors, methodical, patient, levering at the last supporting stones with iron bars.

"They're collapsing it behind us," Ivetta breathed. "Or ahead of us. Sealing the pass."

"Why?" Wren asked. She had gone very still, her head tilted, listening to something none of them could hear. "Why seal a pass nobody's using?"

Edmund looked at the map-case against his chest, where the courier's wax-sealed chart slept against his heartbeat, and felt the familiar cold walk up his spine. The map guided him. It had guided him out of Dunmarrow's tunnels, through Maren's door, across half of Thornfell — always subtly, always deniably, a nudge here, a path there. And the path it had chosen ran through Widow's Teeth, down into the high valley beyond, toward the court beneath the mountain.

Toward the one door Veyra seemed suddenly desperate to close.

"She knows where we're going," he said quietly. "Or she knows where the map goes. Either way—" he nodded at the work party on the far shelf, at their patient iron bars, "—she's not trying to catch us anymore. She's trying to make sure nobody reaches the mountain at all."

Brannoc squinted across the gap, and Edmund watched the ex-tithe-man's mind do its terrible arithmetic. "Those supports," he said slowly. "If they finish, the whole southern shelf goes. But if someone crossed *now* — while they're prying at the far anchors — the vibration might bring the rest down on top of them. Seal it permanently. Behind us and in front of us."

"Crossing now means crossing during a collapse," Ivetta said.

"It does," Brannoc agreed.

Silence. The copper sky. Wren's hum, rising now, urgent, the mountain answering her through the soles of their boots in a low tremor that Edmund realized, with dawning horror, was not the wind.

The stones of Widow's Teeth had begun, very softly, to sing.

"Well," said Edmund, and tightened the strap of the map-case over his heart. "I've owed worse debts to kinder creditors. Let's go give the widows something new to talk about."
