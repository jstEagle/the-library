## 10. The Court Beneath

The gate was not what Edmund had expected, which by now he should have known to expect.

Three days past Widow's Teeth, the mountain stopped pretending to be a mountain. The trail narrowed into a shelf of black stone where nothing grew—not lichen, not moss, not even the stubborn grey grass that clung to everything else in Thornfell like a beggar to a coat hem. And there, set into the cliff face as if the rock had grown around it rather than been carved, stood a door.

It was tall. That was the first absurdity. Tall enough for giants, or for grief, and made of a pale wood that might have been bone that had decided to try being timber. No handle. No hinges. No sigils, which after everything Maren had taught him felt less like an absence and more like an insult.

"Well," said Ivetta, who had said little since the pass, "it's very rude."

"It's sealed," Brannoc corrected. He'd gone quiet in the way of men doing arithmetic they don't like. "Sealed things have locks. Locks have keys."

"The map has no key," Wren said. She was crouched at the base of the door with her ear nearly touching the stone, her fingers splayed against it like a spider listening through a wall. "But it doesn't need one. Listen."

They listened. Edmund heard wind. He heard the distant, sickening crack-and-boom of Veyra's vanguard working its way through the valley villages below, a sound that had become so constant over the last two days it had begun to feel like weather.

And beneath all of it—so low he felt it in his back teeth before he heard it—a hum.

"There it is," Wren said softly, and smiled, and Edmund thought: *she smiles at it*. She'd been smiling at it since Widow's Teeth, humming snatches of melody under her breath that she swore she wasn't inventing, only remembering forward. It should have frightened him more than it did. Instead it felt like having a lantern in the party that burned on something other than oil.

"So we knock?" Ivetta asked.

"We can't knock," Brannoc said. "There's no—"

Ivetta knocked. Three raps of her knuckles against pale wood, and the sound went *into* the door rather than off it, swallowed whole, and then the hum changed pitch, and every hair on Edmund's arms stood up like soldiers called to attention.

The map, tucked inside his coat, grew warm.

That was the other thing about the map. Since Dunmarrow it had developed opinions. It warmed when they walked true and cooled when they strayed, and once—in a moment none of them discussed afterward—it had grown briefly, distinctly damp, when Wren had fallen asleep leaning against Edmund's shoulder. Maren would have had three theories and a lecture. Edmund had settled for keeping it close and not sleeping well.

The warmth spread up through his ribs, and with it came the courier again—that flicker of borrowed memory, the snow that wasn't his, the taste of someone else's fear. But this time the flicker resolved into something clearer, almost deliberate, like a voice deciding to be understood:

*The door opens for debt.*

Edmund stepped back. "Everyone stop touching it."

"Too late," said the door.

Not in words. In pressure. The hum swelled, and Edmund's mind filled—not with images this time but with *accounting*, with a sensation like fingers running down a ledger column, itemizing him. He felt it take stock of his hunger, his fear, the salt-smuggling years, the name he never gave anyone, the debts he'd spent a decade insisting he didn't owe.

He felt it find them anyway. All of them. Every one.

"Ow," said Wren, with interest.

"What is it doing?" Ivetta had her knife out, which Edmund suspected would help exactly as much as knocking hadn't.

"Reading us," Edmund said. His mouth tasted of ink. "Maren told me about this. The old courts—the ones under mountains—they don't deal in coin. Coin is just memory you can drop." He swallowed. "They deal in what you carry."

Brannoc laughed, one short ugly note. "Then we're rich. Every soul in this company is carrying more debt than a merchant fleet."

"That's the problem," Edmund said. "Or the answer. I don't know yet."

* * *

They argued about it the way exhausted people argue, which is to say badly and while sitting down.

The door wanted payment. That much was plain. The map's borrowed knowledge supplied the rest in reluctant fragments: the court beneath Thornfell did not open for heroes, or kings, or thieves of great skill. It opened for the indebted. A free man walking up to that gate was a stranger; a debtor was *kin*. The old bargain, older than Veyra, older probably than the free cities themselves: you may enter owing, and what you owe will be weighed.

"And if what we owe weighs too much?" Ivetta asked.

"Then I assume the door keeps it," Edmund said. "Us included."

Silence. Below them, faintly, another village burned. Wren put her hands over her ears, then took them away, then hummed four notes of the mountain's tune as if to drown out the human noise with the older kind.

"I'll go first," Edmund said.

"No," said Ivetta.

"You don't get to—" Brannoc started.

"I'm the one carrying the map. I'm the one it's been talking to since the ravine." Edmund stood, brushing stone dust from his knees, and found that his hands were steadier than he deserved. "Also, I've been paying debts my whole life. I know how to haggle."

"With a *door*," Ivetta said.

"With whatever's behind the door." He looked at each of them in turn—Brannoc with his tithe-man's brass needles still strapped to his forearm like a confession he couldn't remove, Ivetta with her beautiful hollowed-out eyes, Wren already half-turned toward the gate like a compass needle finding north. "If it takes me, walk away. Go back down. Tell no one, especially not Maren, because she'll only say she told me so, and she'll be insufferable about it."

"She'll also be right," Brannoc said.

"She usually is. It's her worst quality."

He approached the door alone. Up close, the pale wood had grain like fingerprints, millions of them, overlapping. He raised his hand and pressed his palm flat against it, and the hum rose to meet his skin, and the ledger-fingers ran down his columns again, patient, thorough, *hungry*.

*What do you owe?*

The question arrived without sound, directly behind his eyes, and Edmund—who had built his entire life on giving no name, no history, no handle for anyone to grip—discovered that lying to a door was impossible. It already knew. It simply wanted him to say it aloud, because a debt unspoken is a debt deniable, and the court dealt only in what was admitted.

"I owe a man named Sable my life," Edmund said. His voice cracked on the second word, which surprised him, though it shouldn't have. "Twelve years old, slaver caravan, and he cut me loose and vanished, and I never paid it back, and then I stopped even trying to find him, because looking hurt too much. I owed my mistress Maren ten years of service and I ran from the last of it. I owe the courier whose map this is—I don't even know his name—I owe him a burial and a message delivered, and I've done neither. I owe Ivetta a war she can't remember fighting. I owe—" His voice failed entirely. He stood there with his hand on the door, breathing, while somewhere below a village burned and somewhere above a mountain hummed. "I owe everyone. I've spent my whole life being forgettable so I wouldn't have to pay any of it. Is that enough? Does the ledger want that?"

The door was silent for a long moment.

Then, gently, horribly, like a grandmother asking a child to show her what's in his pockets:

*Everything is enough. What will you pay?*

"I don't—" Edmund began, and stopped, because he understood suddenly that *everything* had not been a compliment. It had been a price range.

The map burned hot against his chest. And into the space where his courage should have been, the courier's memories stirred one final time—not snow this time, not fear, but something small and warm: a kitchen. A woman with grey in her braid and flour on her knuckles, laughing at a boy of maybe nine who had drawn a crooked circle in the air and gotten it, actually gotten it, light clinging to his fingertips like honey—

*Maren,* Edmund realized. *That's Maren. That's her kitchen. That's me.*

His first sigil. The first thing he'd ever made that stayed made. She'd laughed and said *look at you, you terrible little prodigy*, and ruffled his hair with floury hands, and for one moment—one whole, unguarded moment—he had belonged somewhere, to someone, and he had carried that moment through ten years of running like a coal wrapped in cloth, taking it out only in the worst nights, warming himself on it, never letting it gutter.

The door wanted it.

"No," Edmund whispered. "Anything else. Take the smuggling. Take—take the ravine, take the courier's face, take—"

*The court does not choose,* the door said, with something that in a lesser voice might have been regret. *The debtor chooses. That is the only law older than us.*

He could walk away. That was the trap of it, the teeth in it—he could turn around right now, and Ivetta and Brannoc and Wren would follow him down the mountain, and Sable's soul would stay on whatever shelf it sat on, whispering its best day to nobody, forever. Or he could open his hand and let go of the single warmest thing he owned.

*You terrible little prodigy.*

"Take it," Edmund said. "But hear me—I'm not paying it *to* you. I'm spending it. There's a difference, and your ledger can mark the difference, because I'm going to come back for it someday, and I want the entry to say *loaned*."

The hum paused.

Then, unmistakably, the door laughed.

The pale wood split along a seam that hadn't existed, and light poured out—but not firelight, not daylight, nothing with any warmth a body could trust. It was the light of bottled things. It was the color of preserved lightning bugs, of jam-jar stars, of souls kept fresh.

Edmund's knees buckled. He caught himself on the doorframe, and the last thing he saw before stepping through—because he stepped through, he chose to step through, that distinction mattered to him desperately—was Ivetta catching Wren's arm, Brannoc drawing a needle he'd sworn never to use again, all of them following him in, because of course they were, because that was the whole point of people.

* * *

Inside, the court was vast the way the sea is vast, and lit the way a dream of drowning is lit.

They stood on a gallery of white stone that curved away in both directions until it became a suggestion of itself. Below, tier upon tier descending into blue-grey depths, stretched shelves. Not hundreds of shelves. Not thousands. Ranks upon ranks upon ranks of them, carved from the same pale material as the door, and on every shelf, jars.

Each jar held light. Each light was a different color, a different pulse, a different rhythm—and as Edmund's eyes adjusted, he began to hear them. Not words. Not quite. More like the way a house sounds when you press your ear to its wall: lives going on. One jar murmured in a rhythm like waves, over and over, some fisherman's perfect morning replayed eternally. Another glowed gold and steady and hummed what could only be a wedding song. A third flickered erratically, quick and bright, laughter-shaped.

Every jar whispering its owner's best day. Thousands of best days, shelved in the dark, tended by no one.

"Oh," said Wren, very quietly. Her humming had stopped altogether, which frightened Edmund more than anything else he'd seen. "Oh, it's *sad* in here. I thought—it's not hungry, it's *lonely*—"

"Don't," Brannoc said hoarsely. "Don't listen to it. That's how it gets you. I extracted from forty people before I went rogue and every one of them said the needles were cold, but the *court*—" He shut his mouth hard.

Ivetta had gone still in the particular way Edmund had learned meant she was seeing something she'd lost. "My jar would be empty," she said. "Wouldn't it. They took everything worth keeping. There'd be nothing left to bottle."

"That's not how it works," Edmund said, and didn't know how he knew, except that the map's knowledge was seeping into him faster now, here, in the heart of it. "Empty jars are the expensive ones. They're the ones the court fills with somebody else's light."

Ivetta looked at him. For a long moment he thought she might hit him, or thank him, or both.

Above them—around them—the architecture assembled itself out of the gloom as their eyes surrendered to it: pillars like frozen waterfalls, arches carved with figures whose faces had been deliberately smoothed blank, and everywhere, drifting at the height of chandeliers, motes of loose light that were not dust. They moved with intention. They gathered near the living visitors and hovered, curious, like moths that had learned manners.

And at the far end of the gallery, where the shelves ended and a dais of black stone rose, something waited. Edmund couldn't see it clearly. He could see that it was pale, and tall, and that it had been watching them since the door opened, and that it was pleased.

"Company rules," Edmund said, barely above a whisper. "Nobody touches a jar. Nobody answers if something asks your name. Nobody wanders."

"And you?" Ivetta asked.

Edmund looked out over the tiers of whispering light, all those best days glowing patiently in the dark, and somewhere among them—somewhere on these endless shelves—was a laugh he'd tried for twelve years to forget.

"I'm going to find one specific jar," he said, "and I am going to steal it back."

Far below, in the dark between the shelves, something with too many joints unfolded itself from a shadow and began, unhurried, to climb.
