## 11. Bottled Light

The door behind them closed with the sound of a book shutting.

Edmund had expected darkness. What he got instead was light—thousands upon thousands of small lights, arranged in rows that climbed out of sight into a dark so high it might have been sky. Each light sat inside glass. Each glass sat on a shelf of pale stone. And each one, as he stood there catching his breath, was whispering.

Not all at once. Not loudly. It came to him the way rain comes to a roof—one voice, then another, then a hundred overlapping until he realized the whole vast room was murmuring, and had been murmuring long before he arrived, and would go on murmuring long after.

He took a step closer to the nearest shelf and listened.

*"...the day my daughter was born, and the midwife said 'a strong one,' and I laughed because I'd never been strong a day in my life..."*

A jar no bigger than a wine bottle, glowing the soft gold of candlelight through honey. Inside it something turned over, slow as a sleeper.

The next jar along: *"...rain on the ferry roof, and her hand in mine, and I thought, this is it, this is the best I will ever be, and I was right..."*

And the next: *"...bread. Just bread. The first loaf after the famine year. We ate half of it standing up and cried about the other half..."*

Edmund's legs decided things without consulting him. He sat down hard on the cold floor.

"They're not screaming," Wren said behind him. She sounded almost disappointed, which was very Wren. "Everyone says souls in jars scream. These are just... talking."

"Telling their best days," Edmund said. His voice came out strange, scraped thin. "That's what they bought. Or what Veyra bought from them. Look—" He pointed down the row. "Every single one. It's the same shape of story. A wedding. A birth. A meal. One good day each, bottled and shelved like preserves."

Ivetta crouched beside him. She had been silent since the gate—the gate that had opened for her without hesitation, that had tasted her debts and found them sufficient—and now she reached out and laid two fingers against the nearest jar, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you didn't want to spook.

The whisper sharpened. *"...the morning we crossed the river and nobody shot at us, and I put my rifle down in the mud and just watched the water..."*

Ivetta snatched her hand back as if burned.

"That one's a soldier," she said flatly.

"You know that how?"

"Because I know what it sounds like when someone's best day is the day they stopped." She stood, arms folded, jaw set in the way that meant the conversation was over whether or not anyone else agreed. "Keep moving. Brannoc said the vaults run deep, and he said it in the tone of a man who wished he didn't know."

Brannoc himself had stayed at the threshold with the map open across his knees, watching the corridor behind them with a tithe-man's patience. "Ledger-rooms are always organized," he'd said before they split. "Souls are inventory. Inventory has an index. Find the index, find your smuggler." It was the most hopeful thing Edmund had ever heard him say, and it had been delivered in the exact voice of a man reading a grain manifest.

So they moved. Down aisles of whispering light, past shelves that climbed beyond the reach of any lantern, through chambers where the jars grew larger—some the size of barrels, holding days so big they needed the room—and smaller, small as acorns, holding days that must have been tiny and perfect and enough. Edmund made himself look at all of them. It felt like a debt. Somewhere in these thousands were people whose names he'd never known, whose salt he might have carried, whose villages he'd slipped past in the dark while they slept. The least he could do was witness the merchandise.

It was Wren who noticed the humming changed.

She'd been doing it since Widow's Teeth—that odd head-tilt, the half-smile of a girl listening to music no one else could hear—but here in the vaults she stopped walking entirely and held up both hands like a conductor.

"It's different down here," she said. "Up top the mountain hums low. Like a held note. Down here it's—" she frowned, hunting for the word, "—layered. There's the held note underneath, but there's all these little threads woven through it, and every thread is one of them." She gestured at the shelves. "They're not just stored here. They're being *played*. All together. Constantly."

"To keep something asleep," Edmund said quietly. He hadn't meant to say it aloud. It had simply risen, the way the courier's memories rose—except this wasn't the courier's knowledge. This was his own, arriving from somewhere deeper, some intuition fed by Maren's lessons and the map's pull and the simple arithmetic of a machine this size. *You don't bottle ten thousand best days unless something needs feeding.*

Wren looked at him with an expression far too old for her face. "Yeah," she said. "That's what I hear too."

* * *

They found the index by accident, which is to say Wren found it by pickpocketing it.

It was a cabinet—not grand, almost clerical—standing at the junction of three vault-chambers, its drawers labeled in a script that hurt to read directly, the way bright snow hurts. Brannoc, who had caught up once the corridors proved quiet, ran his thumb along the labels and went the color of tallow.

"I've seen this script," he said. "On the tithe-masters' orders. We were told it was administrative shorthand." He pulled a drawer. Inside, hundreds of slim lead tablets, each stamped with a sigil and a string of characters. He lifted one, squinted, and his lips moved as he translated. "'Jar nine thousand four hundred and six. Seller: unnamed. Purchased under covenant of protection. Contents: one day, summer, age eleven.'" He stopped. Swallowed. "Age eleven," he repeated. "Someone sold the best day of their life and it happened when they were *eleven years old*."

"Put it back," Ivetta said.

He put it back, more carefully than he'd done anything in the weeks Edmund had known him.

Edmund leaned over the drawer and searched for an organizing principle. Alphabetical wouldn't work—he didn't know what alphabet. Chronological? He pulled tablet after tablet, dates scattering across centuries, sellers listed as *unnamed, unnamed, unnamed*—of course, anonymity was the whole product, that was the pitch, *sell us your worst memory's neighbor and no one need ever know*—until his fingers found a cluster of tablets near the front of the drawer, newer, their lead still bright, and one of them stamped not with *unnamed* but with a mark he knew better than his own heartbeat.

A sable-colored feather crossed with a knife.

His hands stopped working. That was the only way he could describe it. The signal went from his eyes to his fingers and died somewhere in between, and he stood there holding a lead tablet stamped with Sable's mark and felt the numbness he'd been wearing since Dunmarrow crack straight down the middle.

"Edmund?" Ivetta's voice, distant.

"He signed it," Edmund said. "The idiot *signed* it. Everyone else is anonymous and he signed it, because he's arrogant, because he's proud, because he wanted whoever came looking to know exactly whose soul this was—" His voice cracked and he hated it. "Because he *wanted* someone to come looking."

Wren took the tablet from his slack fingers and read it aloud, gently, like breaking bad news twice so it could be survived twice. "Jar twelve thousand and thirty-one. Seller: Sable, of no fixed address, master of the Thornfell roads. Contents: one night, autumn, a mountain road, laughter, shared." She paused. "Shared with whom?"

"With me," Edmund said.

* * *

He'd tried to forget that laugh. He wanted that on the record, filed, witnessed. For seven years he had performed the forgetting with the discipline of a hedge-witch's apprentice: he had taken the road east when Sable's caravan went west; he had refused letters carried by three separate couriers; he had taught himself, in Maren's kitchen, the little binding-sigils that press a memory down flat the way you press flowers, keeping the shape while killing the color.

It hadn't worked, of course. Nothing Maren taught ever worked the way it claimed to. The sigils hadn't killed the memory—they'd embalmed it. And now, walking down aisle after aisle of whispering strangers toward jar twelve thousand and thirty-one, Edmund discovered that the embalming had failed too, because the laugh was already in his ears.

He heard it before he saw the jar.

It drifted down the aisle the way woodsmoke drifts—unhurried, finding its own level—and it was *his* laugh answering it, younger, seventeen, ridiculous, and the two laughs tangled together over some joke Edmund couldn't retrieve but whose warmth he could feel through seven years of pressed flowers and salt roads and running.

"There," Wren whispered, and pointed.

The jar sat on a shelf at eye height, third from the aisle's end. It was larger than most—a full flagon rather than a bottle—because the night inside it had been large. The light it gave off wasn't gold like the others. It was the amber-brown of lamplight through cider, of campfire seen from the far side of a cold field, and it pulsed, very slightly, in rhythm.

In rhythm with laughter.

Edmund approached it the way you approach a deer. Slowly. Breathing shallow. As if the moment might startle and bolt back into the dark of him forever.

Inside the glass, the memory turned. He couldn't see it, not properly—it wasn't an image, it was a *weather*, a pressure of warmth against the glass—but he could feel its edges: wet grass, a fire's smoke, the specific ache of ribs from laughing too hard at altitude. Two voices. One of them Sable's, pitched low and rough with delight. One of them his own, cracking on the high notes because he was seventeen and hadn't grown into his voice yet.

*"...and then the mule—"*

The whisper was faint, but it was there, the jar telling its best day to the dark the way all the others did, and Edmund knelt on the cold stone floor of a vault beneath a mountain and put his forehead against the glass and listened to the mule joke he couldn't remember and wept without dignity, without silence, without any of the control he'd spent seven years building like a wall around himself.

Ivetta stood watch at the aisle's mouth and pretended not to see. Brannoc examined the ceiling with great interest. Wren sat down next to Edmund, cross-legged, shoulder touching his shoulder, and hummed the mountain's held note very softly, as if to say *you're allowed, the walls here hold worse.*

"She told me once," Edmund managed, eventually, wiping his face on his sleeve like a child, "that everyone should know their best day. So they can aim at it. She said most people drift through life shooting at nothing in particular, but if you know your best day you can spend the rest of your life trying to give it away." He laughed, wet and broken. "I thought it was smuggler nonsense. I thought—I don't know what I thought. I thought if I loved him less loudly he'd stop being a target. I thought forgetting him was *protecting* him."

"And instead you sold your best day to a witch and he sold his to a mountain," Ivetta said, not unkindly. "You're both idiots. It's very romantic."

Edmund huffed something that was almost a laugh, which was, he suspected, exactly why she'd said it.

He lifted the jar from the shelf.

It was lighter than it should have been. Warm. It fit in both hands like something born to be held, and the light inside it flared at the contact—brightened, steadied, turned toward him the way a sunflower turns—and the whisper resolved, suddenly, perfectly clear, two voices on a mountain road:

*"Tell me again why we're carrying salt uphill."*
*"Because downhill's already been done, love, and we're innovators."*

And his own seventeen-year-old voice, helpless with laughter: *"You're going to get us killed."*
*"Probably! But memorably."*

Edmund held his best day in his hands and understood, finally, with terrible clarity, that he had come down into the dark not to steal a soul but to steal it *back*—that some part of him had known since the courier died in the ravine that this jar existed, that this laugh existed, that the map had been leading him home the entire time.

"We're getting it out," he said. He didn't recognize his own voice. It sounded like someone who had finished running. "Whatever it costs. Whatever the court asks. I'm not leaving it on a shelf."

"Edmund." Brannoc's voice, low and urgent, from the junction. "Company."

Down the central aisle, between the whispering shelves, lights were going out. Not failing—*dimming*, deliberately, one after another, like candles pinched by an unseen hand, and in the spreading dark Edmund heard footsteps. Unhurried. Polite. The footsteps of something that owned the shelves and everything on them and had just been informed, by the flare of one particular jar, precisely where the thieves were standing.

The light around them dimmed to a single amber glow—the jar in Edmund's hands, burning steady, refusing to be afraid.

"Well," said a voice from the dark, dry as ledger paper, "you've certainly made yourself easy to find."
