## 12. The Pale Bargainer

The steward came to them the way fog comes off a river—without footsteps, without warning, and smelling faintly of cold stone and something sweeter underneath, like honey left too long in a cellar.

Edmund had been counting the soul-jars again. It was a bad habit he'd developed in the last hour, the way a man with a toothache keeps touching it. Shelf after shelf of bottled light, each one murmuring its owner's best day to anyone patient enough to listen. A woman's voice repeating *the wedding, the wedding, the rain on the tent and everyone dancing anyway*. A fisherman humming about a morning so foggy he'd caught nothing and called it perfect. A child—gods, a child—whispering about a dog.

"You'll hurt your eyes," said the steward, "staring at what isn't yours."

Edmund spun. Ivetta's knife was already out; Brannoc had his back to the wall, hands raised in the old tithe-man's posture of professional surrender. Wren just looked delighted.

The steward was tall in the way that made you unsure whether height was really the right word. Pale as the court itself, dressed in robes the color of unwritten paper, with a face that seemed to be assembled fresh each time Edmund blinked—not changing, exactly, just *reconsidered*. Where eyes should have been there were two soft glows, like jars seen through cloth.

"I am the Steward of Accounts," it said. "You are holding stolen property."

Edmund's hand went, entirely without his permission, to the map inside his coat. "I found it."

"Yes," said the steward pleasantly. "That is also theft. The court has very generous definitions. We've had centuries to refine them." It folded its hands—or suggested folding its hands; the gesture happened somewhere near where hands would be—and inclined its reconsidering face toward Edmund with an air of tremendous patience. "But I did not come to accuse. I came to make you rich."

Behind him, Wren whispered, "Oh, this is my favorite part," and Edmund elbowed him without looking.

* * *

They were given a room for the negotiation, which struck Edmund as absurdly civilized: a round chamber walled in pale stone, lit by three bottles of soul-light that had been set into niches with actual brass brackets, like someone had once cared about this place. In the center stood a table. On the table sat a decanter of wine that, when poured, was the exact color of whatever drink you'd loved best at age nine. Edmund refused a cup on principle and then spent several minutes regretting it, because the smell coming off Brannoc's cup was unmistakably the blackberry cordial from the fair at Millbrook, and Edmund had thought about that cordial more times over the years than he'd ever admit sober.

"Comfortable?" asked the steward.

"No," said Edmund honestly.

"Good. Honesty is the only currency we accept here, though ironically no one ever pays with it." The steward seated itself across the table—a chair appeared beneath it at the last moment, which Edmund chose not to think about. "Let us speak plainly, Edmund Eastwick. Yes, we know your name. We know all the names in our ledger, and your name has been in it since you were twelve years old."

The room went very quiet. Ivetta's knife-hand stopped moving.

"A debt," the steward continued, "incurred the night a smuggler named Sable Vane paid a slaver's caravan forty crowns and a broken nose to walk away from a boy hiding under a tarp. Forty crowns, plus interest, compounded across nineteen years." Its glow-eyes fixed on Edmund. "Sable never collected. He told the caravan men the debt was his alone. He signed it that way. Which means—" and here the steward's voice took on something almost like tenderness, which was somehow worse than menace, "—which means every day since, Edmund Eastwick has been walking around inside a debt he doesn't know he owes, to a man who sold his own soul partly to keep that boy's ledger clean."

"That's not true," Edmund said. His voice came out strange. "He didn't do it for me. He did it to buy the cities time. Maren read it in the courier's memories—the whole thing was strategic—"

"Everything is strategic," agreed the steward, "when you're explaining it afterward. Sit down, Edmund. Let us talk about what you want."

And here is the thing about temptation that nobody tells you because nobody survives to report it accurately: it doesn't look like a devil with a contract. It looks like a reasonable person in a comfortable room offering to solve your problems while you're tired.

"I want Sable back," Edmund said. "Whole. Alive. Out of that jar."

"Done," said the steward instantly.

Edmund blinked. "Just—done?"

"The jar sits four shelves from here. You could carry it out yourself if the wardens weren't waking. I can simply... unwrite the theft. Open the vault. Hand him to you breathing, remembering, grateful." The steward leaned forward. "In exchange for the map."

Silence. Somewhere far above them, muffled by a hundred feet of mountain, Wren's humming drifted down through the stone—that low binding-note he'd been carrying for days, the sound the mountain made back at. Edmund realized he'd been leaning on it without knowing, the way you lean on a wall.

"The map," Edmund said slowly, "is a ledger."

"It is *the* ledger," said the steward. "Every debt the court holds, every soul purchased, every contract signed since before Veyra's grandmother learned to write. It was stolen from us sixty years ago by a mortal archivist who grew a conscience at a deeply inconvenient time. We have wanted it home ever since." It spread its suggestion-of-hands. "Return it, and the books balance. And when the books balance, Edmund, everything becomes possible. Wealth. Power. Your friend restored. Even—" the glow dimmed slyly, "—your own debts forgiven. All of them. You could walk out of this mountain owing nothing to anyone. Can you imagine? A man like you, *free*?"

And Edmund could imagine it. That was the horrible part. He imagined it so vividly his mouth went dry: no Maren, no tithes, no forty crowns compounding in the dark, no ledger with his name in it. No one he owed. No one who owned a piece of him.

No one he belonged to, either, whispered a small cold voice that sounded annoyingly like Wren.

"Here's my problem," Edmund said, and was surprised to find his voice steady. "If I give you the ledger back—if the books 'balance'—then every sale becomes legitimate. Every soul Veyra bought, every memory those tithe-men pulled out with their brass needles, every jar on every shelf—it all becomes *paid for*. Legal. Final." He looked up at the steward's reconsidering face. "You don't want the ledger home because you miss it. You want it because right now, technically, none of these sales are complete. The deed is missing. Without it, every soul in this mountain is stolen goods. With it..."

"With it," the steward said softly, "they are ours. Forever. Yes." It sounded, horribly, proud of him. "You see why we wanted the archivist's conscience problem solved. Mortals always figure it out eventually. Usually around now."

* * *

"So we're not taking the deal," Brannoc said, when they'd been shown—politely, firmly, with a door that hadn't existed before—to a side chamber to "deliberate." The word deliberate was doing a lot of work, given that the door had no handle on the inside.

"We're not taking the deal," Edmund confirmed.

"Because it's evil," said Ivetta.

"Because it's *accounting*," said Edmund, "which is worse. Evil at least admits it wants something from you. This just wants everything to be tidy." He paced the small room, three steps and turn, three steps and turn. "Here's what's eating me. He offered me Sable. Instantly. No haggling. Do you know what that means?"

"That he's desperate," said Ivetta.

"That Sable's jar isn't worth much to the court *unless the ledger comes home*." Edmund stopped pacing. "Think about it. Right now, without the deed, Sable's sale is voidable. Contestable. Some future court, some future law, could crack that jar open and call it theft. But the moment the ledger returns—signed, sealed, balanced—his sale is iron. Forever. The steward wasn't offering me a trade." His stomach turned as he said it. "He was offering to let me be the one who locks the door. Me. The man Sable went into debt for. He wants my signature on it, because a signature freely given is worth ten thousand coerced ones. That's how ledgers work. That's how *Maren* taught me they work, gods help me."

Brannoc whistled low. "Tithe-men used to say that. Freely-given memories bind tighter than taken ones. We hated collecting the voluntary ones. Felt like grave-robbing with extra steps."

"Then we burn the ledger," said Ivetta flatly. "Problem solved. Books can't balance if the book is ash."

"It's not a book," said Wren.

Everyone looked at him. He was sitting cross-legged on the floor with his ear against the wall, fingers spread on the stone, and he looked, for once, completely serious.

"It hums," Wren said. "Same note as the mountain. Same note I've been hearing since Dunmarrow. I thought it was the mountain at first, but it's not—it's the *ledger*. It's not paper, you idiots, it's a jar. The biggest jar in the place. Every debt ever signed, all that grief and wanting and owing, pressed down into one vessel and sealed." He lifted his head, and his grin came back but thinner, scared around the edges. "You can't burn it. Burning a jar in here wakes everything it touches. And everything it touches is *everyone*."

The silence that followed had weight. Edmund thought of shelves upon shelves of whispering light. Thought of the child whispering about her dog.

"So we steal it," he said finally. "Not return it. Not burn it. Take it out of the mountain, past the wardens, past Veyra's army if it comes to that, and put it somewhere no court can ever file it again." He laughed, short and unhappy. "A map that's actually a ledger that's actually a soul. Of course it is. Nothing in my life has ever just been what it looked like."

"And Sable?" Ivetta asked quietly. "The steward offered him to you. Walk away from the deal and the offer dies."

Edmund looked at his hands. Nineteen years ago they'd been small and dirty and gripping the edge of a tarp while a stranger with a crooked smile bled from the nose on his behalf. He'd repaid that with nineteen years of not thinking about it.

"He offered me Sable *as a reward for burying him deeper*," Edmund said. "That's not Sable. That's a puppet wearing my guilt." He straightened. "We take the jar ourselves. Both of them, if we can manage it—the ledger and Sable. And then we get out of this mountain before the steward remembers it doesn't actually need to negotiate."

There was a soft sound behind them.

The door had opened. None of them had touched it.

The steward stood in the frame, and its face had stopped being reconsidered and settled into something older and plainer and much more frightening: patient disappointment, the expression of every creditor since the dawn of money.

"I hoped you'd be sensible," it said. "They rarely are. It's why the wardens sleep with their boots on." Its glow-eyes moved from face to face, and lingered, briefly, on Wren. "You should know one more thing before you choose your crime. The ledger is not merely a record of debts. It is the *seal*. The binding that keeps what sleeps beneath this court asleep. Remove it from the mountain, and you will learn within the hour why Veyra buys souls by the thousand." It stepped aside, gesturing with terrible courtesy toward the dark corridor beyond. "But please. Do go on. The court so admires initiative."

Down the corridor, deep below the shelves and the whispering jars, something shifted in its sleep—and the mountain changed its hum by half a note.

Wren went white. "It heard us," he whispered. "It's *counting*."
