## 13. Wren's Humming

The vaults had a sound to them.

Edmund noticed it first as a thing he could ignore—a low note under the whisper of the jars, like a finger run along the rim of a wet cup. But the deeper they went into the pale court, following the ledger-light toward the steward's hall, the more insistent it became. By the time they stopped to rest in an alcove between two shelves of bottled souls, the hum was in his teeth. In his fillings. In the soft parts behind his eyes.

"Does anyone else hear that?" Ivetta asked. She had her knife out, which was Ivetta's way of saying she was thinking.

"The mountain," Brannoc said flatly. "It's a mountain. Mountains groan."

"It's not groaning." Wren sat cross-legged on the cold floor with her back against a shelf, and the nearest jar—something small and amber, humming its owner's best day in a voice like a child counting coins—seemed to lean toward her. "It's singing. It's been singing since Widow's Teeth. You all just hum too loud inside your own heads to notice."

Edmund crouched beside her. The girl was fifteen at most, all elbows and quick fingers, with a smudge of soot on her chin that had survived three days of mountain travel and would probably outlive them all. "Sing what?"

Wren looked up at him, and for once there was nothing flippant in her face. That frightened him more than the wardens had.

"I don't know the words," she said. "But I know the shape of it. My gran used to sing over cradles. Lullabies aren't about the words, right? They're about—" She made a vague gesture, fingers fluttering. "About keeping something quiet. About saying *hush, hush, I'm here, don't wake.*" She pressed her palm flat against the shelf. "This is that. Only bigger. And it isn't a lullaby anyone's *singing* to the mountain."

She let that sit.

"It's a lullaby the mountain is being *made* to sing. To itself."

* * *

Brannoc laughed. It was not a kind laugh; it was the laugh of a man who had spent thirty years extracting memories with brass needles and had learned to laugh at things that scared him, because the alternative was putting the needle down.

"That's a story," he said. "That's a pickpocket's ghost story for keeping other pickpockets from going down old wells. The court is a court. Ugly, greedy, but a court. Courts have stewards. They have ledgers. They have rules you can exploit, which is the only reason we're still breathing."

"Do you hear it?" Wren asked him.

"No."

"You would." She said it without malice, which somehow made it worse. "You spent your whole life making people forget. Your head's full of holes. Sound goes through holes."

Ivetta made a noise that might have been a snort or might have been agreement. Brannoc's jaw worked. Edmund watched the old tithe-man decide, visibly, not to reach for his needle-case, and felt a small, tired pride in him for it.

"Say it plain, then," Edmund said to Wren. "What are you telling us?"

Wren hugged her knees. Around them, the jars whispered their little treasures—a wedding, a harvest, a dog that had come home after nine years—and beneath it all ran the great low note, patient as water wearing stone.

"The court isn't the top of this place," she said. "It's the middle. There's something underneath. Something the court sits on like a lid. And the court feeds it souls—not because the court wants to, but because if it stops feeding, the thing underneath wakes up. The humming is the cage. The whole court—the steward, the wardens, the vaults, all those poor bottled lights—it's not a kingdom. It's a *lock*. And Veyra isn't robbing the lock." Wren swallowed. "She's the lock-keeper. She buys souls wholesale because the thing underneath eats them, and as long as it's eating, it stays asleep."

The silence afterward had weight. One of the jars—a woman's voice, murmuring about rain on a roof—went quiet, as if listening.

"That's mad," Brannoc said, but softly now, testing the words rather than throwing them.

"Is it?" Ivetta leaned against the opposite shelf, arms crossed. "Think. Why does a usurper buy souls? Not power—you can get power cheaper with soldiers. Not immortality—she'd have drunk one by now if that were the game. No. She buys them by the thousand, ships them here, never spends them. What kind of miser hoards like that?" She tapped her temple. "A jailer stocking a larder."

Edmund thought of the steward's face when it had offered him anything—wealth, power, Sable restored—for the return of its stolen ledger. He had read the offer as greed. Now, turning it over, he saw the desperation underneath it, the way a drowning man offers everything for a rope. The court hadn't wanted the ledger back because it loved its accounts.

It had wanted it back because the ledger was the last piece of the lock anyone had ever pried loose.

"How do you know all this?" he asked Wren. "Truly."

Wren shrugged, embarrassed. "I told you. I hear it. Always have—wells, caves, the cellar under the Ropewalk in Dunmarrow. Gran said I had a hole in my head where sense should be and sound got in." Her mouth twitched. "Down here it's not a hole anymore. It's a door. And through the door I can feel—" She pressed her lips together, hunting for the word. "*Grief.* Old grief. Like a sea behind a dam. If the dam breaks—"

"If the dam breaks, every soul in this mountain drowns in it," Edmund finished quietly. "And maybe everyone standing near the mountain. Maybe everyone, period."

"Or," Wren said, "the usurper loses her power source, her soul-economy collapses, her armies desert, and the free cities go free." She spread her hands. "Could be either. Could be both. That's the fun part."

* * *

They argued for two hours.

It began reasonably. Brannoc laid it out like a supply manifest: they had one job, which was Sable's jar, currently wrapped in Edmund's cloak and radiating a faint warmth against Edmund's chest like a sleeping bird. Get in, get out, negotiate later. Anything else was vanity.

Then Ivetta said, "And leave the lock intact? Leave Veyra feeding it forever?" and it stopped being reasonable.

"You don't know what's under there," Brannoc snapped. "Wren doesn't know. A feeling and a humming. You'd crack open a cage on a *guess*, and whatever's inside gets to vote with its teeth."

"And you'd keep the cage shut forever," Ivetta shot back, "on the word of the people who built it. You wore their brass around your neck for thirty years, Brannoc. How many times did you tell some debtor *it's for the greater order* while you took their mother's face?"

"That's different."

"It's exactly the same. It's always the same argument. Keep the awful thing running, because changing it might be worse. That's how every awful thing survives." Her voice cracked on the last word, and Edmund understood—Ivetta remembered nothing of her war crimes, nothing at all, and every closed system, every locked box, every *we can't look, it might be worse* was, to her, the shape of her own missing years.

Brannoc stood up. So did Ivetta. Between them, Wren sat very still, watching them like someone watching weather turn.

"Enough," Edmund said.

Neither moved.

"*Enough.*" He put Sable's jar down between them, gently, on the cold floor. "Look at it. This is why we came. One jar. One man who saved my life when I was twelve and stupid, and whose soul I am going to carry out of this mountain if it costs me everything I have left to pay with. That hasn't changed." He looked at each of them in turn. "But I'm not fool enough to think we'll all walk out agreeing. So here's what I propose: nobody decides tonight. We rest. We take Sable's jar to the gate. And if any of you wants to come back for the thing underneath—with a plan, with more than a humming and a guess—then that's a war you choose another day, with your eyes open. Not tonight, in the dark, angry."

For a long moment nobody spoke. Then Brannoc sat down heavily, wincing like an old hinge, and said, "Your mistress taught you that? Bargaining?"

"She taught me that everyone has a price," Edmund said. "The bargaining I learned from smugglers. Smugglers know you never split the cargo mid-run."

Ivetta laughed—a short, surprised bark—and the terrible tightness in the alcove loosened by a degree. She sat. Brannoc sat. Wren reached over and patted Sable's jar, twice, the way you'd pat a horse.

"He snores," she reported. "Did you know that? The jar snores."

"He always did," Edmund said, and found, to his astonishment, that he could smile.

* * *

He took first watch, because he didn't trust himself to sleep.

The others settled into the alcove's corners—Ivetta with her knife unsheathed across her knees even now, Brannoc curled like a man accustomed to sleeping in ditches, Wren already breathing slow. The jars whispered. The mountain hummed. And Edmund sat with his back to the shelf and let himself, for the length of a watch, simply grieve.

Because Wren was right. He knew she was right, in the way you know a thing in your body before your mind consents. He had felt the grief under the mountain since the gate opened—a vast, patient sorrow, older than Veyra, older than the free cities, soaked into the stone like wine into bread. Whatever lay beneath the court had been bound a long time, and binding was just cruelty with good posture. Every jar on these shelves was a brick in the wall. Including, until two days ago, Sable's.

And yet.

He thought of Maren, somewhere above or below—he no longer knew which direction she'd come from, only that she was in the mountain, following her sister's debt like a bloodhound. He thought of what freeing the thing underneath might cost, and whether grief drowned in quantities that large ever really drained away, or just found new ground to soak. He thought of the child in Dunmarrow's market who had forgotten her mother's face, and whether she was better off now, and hated himself for wondering.

"You're doing the face," Wren said.

He startled. She was supposed to be asleep. She was sitting up, watching him, chin on her knees.

"What face?"

"The one where you're deciding something big and pretending it's small." She scooted closer. "I see it a lot. On rich people, mostly. They're the worst for it."

"Wren—"

"You're going to break the lock." She said it simply, without accusation. "Not tonight. But you're going to. I can tell, because you keep touching the jar like it's an apology, and men only apologize like that when they've already decided what they're willing to lose."

Edmund looked at her. Fifteen years old, ears sharp enough to hear a caged god grieving, and wise enough to be terrifying about it.

"And what would you do?" he asked. "If it were yours to decide?"

Wren was quiet a long time. The hum filled the pause, and for a moment—Edmund would swear this later, and swear it sincerely—she hummed back, one low note, matching it, and the nearest jars went still as held breath.

"My gran sang to me every night till she died," Wren said at last. "And when she died, I forgot her voice within a year, and it nearly killed me, and I'd give anything to have it back." She looked at the shelves, hundreds of jars, thousands of best days, all of them bought from people who presumably needed the money more than the memory—or been robbed of the choice entirely. "So I understand wanting to keep things safe even when the keeping hurts. But gran also said—" Wren's voice wobbled, steadied. "She said a song you force someone to sing isn't a song. It's just a scream you've trained to keep time."

She lay back down, curling into her corner.

"Whatever you decide," she mumbled, already half-asleep, "decide it before the steward finds us again. He knows we're here. The whole mountain knows. It's been humming *nervous* for an hour."

Edmund sat with that.

Nervous. The mountain, nervous. As though the lock itself could feel hands upon it—as though the prisoner beneath, dreaming its long grief-dream, had stirred at the approach of one thief with a stolen jar and a heart full of debts.

Far off, down the black throat of the vaults, a bell rang once. Then again. Wardens' bells—but wrong, somehow, faster than the patrol-rhythm Brannoc had mapped for them, urgent as a pulled thread.

And beneath the bells, beneath the whispering jars, beneath everything: the hum changed key.

Wren's eyes flew open.

"That's not nervous," she breathed. "That's *awake*."

From the darkness beyond the alcove, cold and courteous and far too close, a voice like poured milk said:

"There you are. We should talk about the ledger."
