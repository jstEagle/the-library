## 14. The Price Named Later

The trouble with a court that ran on debts, Edmund was learning, was that it never stopped collecting. He'd been in the vaults for what felt like two days and might have been two hours, and every time he turned around, something new had been added to his account. The wardens' attention. The steward's patience, thinning like cheap broth. Wren's humming, which had gone from eerie to comforting to the only thing keeping him from screaming, and which he now owed her for, apparently, in some currency he didn't want to examine.

He was crouched beside Sable's jar when Ivetta's hand closed on his shoulder—hard enough to mean *trouble*.

"She's here," Ivetta said.

"Who's here?"

"Your hedge-witch." Ivetta's face did something complicated. "She walked through the gate."

Edmund stood up so fast his knees cracked. "That's impossible. The gate opens for debtors. She has no business with this place."

Ivetta just looked at him, the way soldiers look at people who haven't seen a war yet.

* * *

Maren stood at the edge of the great vault hall, and the bottled light of ten thousand souls bent toward her like wheat toward sun. She looked exactly as she always had—small, sharp-eyed, wrapped in a traveling cloak that had outlived three fashion trends and one minor plague—and she looked entirely wrong there, the way a kitchen knife looks wrong on a battlefield: familiar, domestic, and suddenly very dangerous.

"You," Edmund said.

"Me," Maren agreed. "You look terrible. Have you been eating? Don't answer. You haven't."

"How did you get in?"

"I followed my debts." She said it simply, the way other people said *I followed the road*. "Thirty years ago I came to this court to buy something. A cure, if you must know, for a fever that was eating my village alive. The price was steep, but I was young and arrogant and I thought I understood what I was paying." Her eyes went somewhere far away. "They took my memory of my sister's face. Not my love for her. Not my grief. Just her face. And then they sold it back to me, years later, at interest, as a *gift*, and I spent a decade wondering why looking at her made me cry."

Edmund's throat tightened. "Ysolde. Your sister. The one whose soul—"

"—is on a shelf down here somewhere, yes." Maren's voice stayed level, but her hands, he noticed, were white-knuckled around her walking stick. "I've had thirty years to find her jar. Thirty years of tithes and trades and teaching ungrateful apprentices, all of it building toward one thing." She turned and looked at him fully, and Edmund felt the floor tilt, because he knew that look. He'd seen it on the courier's dying face. He'd seen it in mirrors.

"Maren," he said carefully. "What did you do?"

"I paid ahead," she said. "That's how I got through the gate. That's how I found you. I've been paying since before you were born, boy, and now—" she drew a breath, and for the first time since he'd known her, the hedge-witch looked old, "—now the bill comes due, and I need an anchor."

"A what?"

Wren, who had drifted over with the silent, sock-footed grace of someone who'd grown up stealing from people, made a small sound. Brannoc, standing guard by the vault door, went very still.

"The court needs a mortal anchor," Wren said quietly. "To stay bound. One willing person, holding the cage shut from inside. That's why Veyra buys souls wholesale—one anchor isn't enough anymore, the binding's failing, so she feeds it thousands instead. It's wasteful. It's *loud*. That's the humming." She looked at Maren with something like horror. "You're offering to be the anchor?"

"No," Maren said. "I'm offering to stop being anything at all. I'm going into the binding with Ysolde's soul, and I'm going to try to pull her free from inside. And while I do that—" she turned to Edmund, and her voice gentled in a way that frightened him more than any threat could have, "—someone has to hold the door. Someone has to be the anchor. Someone who owes this place more than anyone alive."

"No," Edmund said.

"You haven't heard the price yet."

"I don't need to hear it. The answer is no."

"The price," Maren continued, with the serene momentum of a woman who had once talked him into eating a boiled sheep's eye on the grounds that it built character, "is your life, Edmund. Not your death—your *life*. Years of it. Decades. You'd stand at the center of this court and hold it shut, and the court would drink from you slowly, gently, the way a hearth drinks wood, and outside, the world would go on without you. Sable would wake to freedom and no one waiting. The free cities would keep their defense. And I would have my sister back, or die trying, which after thirty years is more than I deserve."

The silence stretched. Somewhere in the vaults, a jar whispered its owner's best day—a wedding, maybe, or a first snow.

"You planned this," Edmund said. His voice came out strange. Flat. "From the beginning. The map. The lessons in forgetting. Sending me down here. You've been grooming me for this since chapter six."

"I don't know what a chapter six is, but yes," Maren said. "I planned it. I'm not proud of it. I'm also not sorry, which I suspect is worse, and which you're welcome to hate me for."

"Hate you?" Edmund laughed, and it came out cracked. "Maren, I fled across half a mountain range to get away from you. I smuggled salt. I prided myself on being forgettable. And the first thing that happens is I find a map, and the map leads here, and here leads to *you asking for my entire future*." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "Do you know what the worst part is? The worst part is I can't even tell if you love me or if I'm just the most convenient debtor you ever met."

Maren was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was rough.

"When you were fourteen, you ran away because I asked for one memory too many. Do you remember which one?"

"The day we met," Edmund said, before he could stop himself. "You wanted the memory of finding me. Of choosing me. I wouldn't give it up."

"And I let you keep it," Maren said. "At cost. Do you know what that cost me? Because I do. I remember it exactly. I remember deciding that my greedy, stubborn, terrified boy could keep one thing, just one, even though the tithe was due and the court's ledger doesn't forgive and I had to pay the difference myself." She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, brave and bitter and true. "I've been paying the difference my whole life, Edmund. This is just the last installment."

"That's not fair," Edmund whispered. "You can't make love look like accounting. It's cruel."

"Love usually is," Maren said. "Accounting, I mean. All those ledgers everyone keeps—the debts we owe our parents, our dead, the people who saved us from slaver caravans when we were twelve. The only question is whether you pay them honestly or spend your life pretending the books balance themselves." She reached out and touched his cheek, briefly, the way she used to when he was small and feverish. "I'm not asking you to decide now. I'm telling you the price exists, and it's named, and it's yours if you choose it. Take tonight. Talk to your friends. Shout at me. I'll be here. I'm rather good at waiting—it's most of what hedge-witching is."

She turned and walked toward the deep vaults, toward the shelf where a jar held thirty years of a sister's face, and Edmund watched her go and hated her and loved her and could not, for the life of him—which was, precisely, the point—tell the difference.

* * *

They argued in a side chamber lit by three stolen jars, their glow pooling on the floor like spilled honey.

"It's suicide," Brannoc said flatly. "Slow suicide. I've seen what the tithe-needles take in minutes. Imagine decades."

"It's not the same," Wren said. "A willing anchor isn't drained. They're... woven in. Like a keel. The ship doesn't eat the keel; it couldn't sail without it." She hugged her knees. "But he'd never leave. Never see the sky again. Never—" she stopped, because she'd been about to say *never laugh again*, and everyone in the room knew whose laugh was sitting in a jar three halls away.

"He hasn't even woken Sable yet," Ivetta said. "One disaster at a time. Steal the jar tomorrow. Decide about anchors after."

"There won't be an after," Brannoc said. "The steward knows we're here. The wardens are waking. Whatever window we had is closing, and Maren just handed us a deadline dressed as a choice."

Edmund sat apart from them, turning Sable's jar over in his hands. Through the glass, faintly, that laugh again—the one from a mountain road, a fire, a night when a twelve-year-old orphan had laughed for the first time in a year and meant it.

"He saved my life," Edmund said quietly. Everyone stopped talking. "When I was twelve. Slaver caravan. He cut me loose and gave me bread and told me the road was wide enough for both of us if I walked fast. And then he vanished, and I spent twenty years pretending I hadn't spent those twenty years looking for him." He looked up. "And now a woman who taught me everything wants me to trade every tomorrow I have left for a stranger's sister and a mountain full of stolen souls, and the truly obscene thing—the genuinely disgusting thing—is that I'm *considering it*."

"Because it's a good trade," Wren said softly.

"Because I finally understand the ledger," Edmund said. "That's worse."

He stood, and the jars dimmed slightly, as if the court itself were leaning in to listen.

"Here's what happens tomorrow," he said. "We steal Sable's jar. We burn the ledger pages as distraction. Nobody agrees to anything Maren says until we're standing in daylight, and *then*—" he pointed at no one in particular, at all of them, at himself, "—then I decide what I'm willing to lose. Not before. I've spent my whole life paying debts I never agreed to. If I pay this one, I pay it *choosing* it. That's the only coin I have left that's worth anything."

Ivetta grinned, sharp as a drawn blade. "There he is. The ledger-breaker."

"Don't call me that. It'll catch on."

"It's already caught on," Wren said. "Brannoc's been calling you that for two days."

"I will turn this company around," Edmund threatened, and got nowhere, because companies don't turn around inside mountains, and everyone knew it, and for one moment—in a stolen chamber under a stolen court—they laughed together, all four of them, quiet and ragged and real.

It was the last easy moment any of them would get.

Because when they opened the chamber door, the corridor beyond was full of pale light, and the steward was waiting, and behind the steward, rank upon rank, the wardens had finished waking.
