## 15. Ledger-Breaker

Edmund had spent his whole life learning the arithmetic of escape: which exits weighed least, which debts could be dodged, which lies were light enough to carry uphill. He did that arithmetic now, standing in the pale court with the steward's offer still hanging in the air like smoke that refused to rise.

*Anything you want,* the steward had said. Wealth. Power. Sable restored, whole and smiling, in exchange for a map that was never a map at all.

It was a ledger. Of course it was a ledger. Edmund had held it for weeks, felt it tugging at strangers' memories, watched a broker go white as milk just from touching its wax seal — and he'd told himself it was a treasure map, a smuggler's chart, anything but what it plainly was: an accounting of every soul Veyra had bought, written in the court's own hand, carried back toward the mountain by a dying courier who hadn't known he was carrying the receipts of a crime.

If Edmund handed it over, the court would file it. Filed meant legitimate. Legitimate meant *paid in full*, and every soul on those shelves — whispering their best days to the dark — would belong here forever, tidy as tax records.

"No," Edmund said.

The steward tilted its head. It had no face worth describing; it had a suggestion of one, the way fog suggests a coastline. "No is not a word we hear often from debtors."

"I've had practice." His mouth was dry. His heart was doing something embarrassing against his ribs. "You offered me everything I want. That's how I know it's a trap. Nobody who's met me would offer that."

Behind him, Wren made a small sound that might have been a laugh or might have been a prayer. Brannoc shifted his weight, brass needles hidden in his sleeve — a tithe-man's habit, kept even after going rogue, the way a man keeps a scar. Ivetta stood by the gate with her hand flat against it, feeling for the hum.

"You will choose," the steward said gently, terribly, "one of the bargains. Everyone does. It is only a question of when, and what you are when you do."

"See," Edmund said, "that's where you're wrong. I'm a hedge-witch's runaway. I was raised on the principle that you never sign anything on the first reading."

And then he moved.

* * *

He had planned it, insofar as Edmund planned anything, during the long walk through the vaults. Not well. But the shape of it had been sitting in him since he found Sable's jar on the ninth shelf of the third vault, since he'd pressed his palm to the glass and heard that laugh — *that* laugh, the one from a mountain road, the one he'd tried to bury under salt routes and false names — come bubbling up through the stopper like air through marsh water.

The jars sat in racks. The racks sat beside the archive, where the court kept its working copies of the debt record: pages and pages of pale vellum, each line a name and a price and a date of purchase. The pages were old. The pages were dry. The pages, Edmund had noticed with the particular attention of a man raised by Maren Oyelaran, were stored directly beneath a shelf of bottled lightning-souls, because the court had never once needed to worry about fire.

The court had never met Maren's worst student.

He snatched Sable's jar off the rack — it was heavier than it should have been, the way grief is heavy — tucked it inside his coat against his chest, where it thumped faintly like a second heartbeat, and swept his arm across the nearest brazier-stand as he turned.

The coals caught the first page before anyone finished being surprised.

For one heartbeat, nothing. The pale court did not deal in color, and flame was the rudest color there was. Then the vellum curled, and a name on it — some debtor dead two hundred years, some small soul sold for a winter's grain — went up in a whump of orange, and the whole court *screamed*.

Not the wardens. The jars. Every soul on every shelf cried out at once, a chord of stolen voices, because somewhere in that burning page was a memory they all recognized: the feeling of being owned, being entered into a book, being *filed*. Fire ate the record and the souls felt themselves, for one impossible moment, unrecorded. Unowned.

Edmund ran.

* * *

"The gate!" he shouted, and the word came out cracked. "Ivetta, the gate!"

She was already there. Of course she was. Ivetta, who remembered nothing of her war crimes, who had crossed Widow's Teeth with empty hands and an emptier face, had planted herself in the archway like she'd been grown there. The gate itself was waking — the great sealed stone grinding, groaning, trying to swing shut on its own ancient hinges, because the court had decided negotiations were over.

Ivetta put her shoulder against it.

Edmund would think about this later, in the quiet, and be unable to explain it even to himself: a woman who couldn't remember what she'd done holding shut a door so everyone else could keep what they'd chosen. She wasn't strong enough. That was the plain truth of it. The gate outweighed her by more than a siege engine. But she braced her boots and set her back and said, in the flat voice she used for things that mattered, "Go. I've got nowhere to be."

Wren darted past him, small and quick, and did the last thing Edmund expected: stopped, turned around, and began to hum.

The binding-hum. The mountain's hum, the one Wren claimed to hear through boot-soles and bedrock, the one that kept the wardens drowsy. Wren threw it out loud now, pitched high and steady, a child's skipping-song rhythm wrapped around something older, and the wardens — rising from their alcoves like frost given ambition — faltered.

They were tall. They were pale. They had faces after all, and Edmund wished they didn't: faces like unfinished portraits, features smudged where expression should be. They carried no weapons. They didn't need any. One of them reached toward the burning archive and the flames simply *slowed*, thickening like honey, and Edmund understood that time itself was on the court's side here.

But there were three wardens and four of them, and one of them was Brannoc.

Brannoc, who had spent twenty years extracting memories with brass needles and had joined this company because he couldn't stand the silence where his own conscience used to be. Brannoc stepped between the wardens and Edmund with the unhurried calm of a man who has finally, after decades, been offered exactly the right job.

"I know your procedures," Brannoc said to the wardens, conversationally, drawing his needles. "I helped write half of them. Veyra's people learned extraction from your archives, did you know? We were students." He smiled, and it was the saddest thing Edmund had ever seen on a living face. "Let me show you what I learned."

"Brannoc—" Edmund started.

"Ledger-breaker," Brannoc said, without turning around. "That's what they'll call you, if there's anyone left to call you anything. Make it worth the name." He rolled his shoulders. "Get the jar out. Get *her* out." A nod toward the gate, toward Ivetta, still holding. "I owe debts too. More than anyone alive, maybe. Let me pay one."

The first warden reached for him, and Brannoc drove a needle into its smudged wrist and *pulled*, and something came out — a memory, a century of them, the warden's own hoarded recollections of every debtor it had ever processed — and the creature staggered, suddenly full of everything it had taken, drowning in other people's best days.

It was, Edmund thought wildly, the most fitting death a tithe-man could ever arrange. Dying in a flood of returned memories.

"GO," Brannoc roared, and his voice broke on it, and Edmund went.

* * *

The vault corridors blurred past. Edmund ran with Sable's jar clutched to his chest and the heat of burning vellum chasing him down the pale halls, and behind him the screaming chord of the souls rose and rose — not in terror anymore, he realized. In something else. In *hope*, terrible and rusty, the way a joint cries when it moves for the first time in years.

Wren's hum threaded through it all, thin and brave. Then another voice joined it — Ivetta, humming against the gate, off-key and stubborn. Then, impossibly, a third: deep and rough and laughing at itself, Brannoc humming while he fought, choosing his side out loud in the only language the mountain understood.

The gate loomed ahead. Ivetta was white to the lips, arms shaking, the stone pressing her down inch by inch.

"Through," she gasped. "Both of you. Now."

"Wren—"

"Already going." Wren squeezed sideways through the gap like water, then turned, and looked up at Ivetta with an expression far too old for that young face. "Come *on*."

"In a moment." Ivetta's voice was steady. Her arms were not. "Someone holds the door. That's how doors work."

"That's how *traps* work," Edmund said, shoving Sable's jar into Wren's arms and grabbing Ivetta's collar. "We hold each other's doors. That's the whole point. That's the entire lesson, and I paid a fortune to learn it, so *move*."

Something in her face cracked — not weakness, relief, the relief of being given permission — and she let go.

The gate slammed.

The sound was enormous, final, a tomb sealing. On the far side of it, muffled through stone, they heard Brannoc laugh one last time — genuinely laugh, the laugh of a man setting down a weight — and then the hum changed key, and went quiet, and did not come back.

Edmund stood in the dark passage with his forehead against the cold rock. Wren held the jar. Ivetta leaned on the wall and shook, silently, the shaking of someone who has just discovered she can still choose things.

"He chose," Ivetta said at last. It wasn't a question. It was a woman checking her arithmetic.

"He chose," Edmund said.

"Good." She wiped her face with the back of her hand, briskly, like brushing off snow. "Then we don't waste it. Where's the way out?"

Edmund opened his mouth to say he didn't know —

— and the map answered for him.

Except the map was ash now, or nearly; he'd fed pages of it to the brazier too, the crucial ones, the ones naming the court's debts. But the courier's memories lived in his head still, bleeding in mile by mile, and one of them surfaced now like a fish rising: a smuggler's tunnel, a stair cut into living rock, daylight at the top of it. The courier had known this mountain. The courier had died getting this ledger *out*.

Edmund laughed — a broken, disbelieving sound — and pointed left, down a passage he had no earthly reason to trust.

"That way. Don't ask me how I know."

"Is it the dead man in your head?" Wren asked, hugging the jar.

"It's the dead man in my head."

"Then it's better than most guides," Wren said, and started walking, humming softly, and the mountain hummed back.

* * *

They climbed for what felt like hours, through passages that narrowed and warmed, and the jar against Wren's chest grew lighter with every step, as if what was inside it could smell daylight. Twice, far below, they felt the mountain shudder — the wardens, awake and angry, finding the burned archive, finding the gap on the ninth shelf of the third vault where a jar used to sit.

Twice, Edmund put his hand on the stone and sent back, along whatever thread connected him to this place, one clear thought:

*He's mine. I paid for him. Check your books.*

The shuddering stopped, both times. Whether the court believed him or simply paused to check, he never learned.

At the top of the final stair, grey daylight leaked through a crack barely wide enough for Wren. Beyond it: wind, pine, the smell of snowmelt. The world.

Ivetta went first, widening the crack with her deserter's shoulders. Wren passed the jar up through the gap, and Edmund took it in both hands, and for a moment he just stood there in the cold clean air, holding the whole weight of what he'd done and what it had cost and what it might yet cost, because the negotiation was doomed now, the truce was dead, and somewhere behind them Veyra's armies would soon learn that the free cities had declared war on the oldest creditor in the world.

"We're going to need help," Ivetta said.

"We're going to need a miracle," Edmund said.

"No," said Wren, nodding at the jar, at the faint golden glow seeping between Edmund's fingers, at the laugh that was almost audible again, almost ready. "We're going to need a smuggler."

Below them, far down the valley, horns began to blow — many horns, in a pattern Brannoc had once described over a campfire with a haunted look: the signal that Veyra herself was moving.

Edmund tucked the jar inside his coat, against his heart, and started running.
