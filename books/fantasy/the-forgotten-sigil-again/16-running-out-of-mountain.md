## 16. Running Out of Mountain

The first scream came from a jar on the third shelf.

Edmund felt it before he heard it—a vibration in his teeth, in the bones behind his eyes, as if someone had struck a tuning fork against his skull. Then the sound arrived: high and thin and terribly human, a woman's voice singing a note that bent upward into something no throat should make.

The burning pages were still drifting down from the steward's desk like black moths. Edmund had maybe counted to ten since he'd tipped the ledger-jar's contents into the pale fire. Ten seconds in which the entire court beneath Thornfell had gone from silence to—this.

"They know," Ivetta said. She didn't sound afraid. She sounded like a woman doing arithmetic. "Wardens wake in about that long. We run now or we don't."

Edmund clutched Sable's jar to his chest. It was warm. That was the worst part—it was *warm*, like a sleeping body, and through the glass he could see a faint gold swirl turning slowly, and inside the swirl, fainter than breath on a window, a shape that might have been a man with his arms crossed, waiting.

"Hold it like you mean it," Wren said, appearing at his elbow. The pickpocket had been quiet since the schism—since she'd told them what the humming meant—and now her voice had a strange steadiness to it, the steadiness of someone who has already made a decision and is simply waiting for everyone else to catch up. "Not like a stolen thing. Like a promise."

"I don't have time for riddles, Wren."

"Good. Because I'm not giving you one." She looked back down the long aisle of shelves, toward the heart of the court, where the hum lived. Where the wardens were even now unfolding themselves from the walls—tall shapes like candle-smoke given opinions, their faces smooth as unblown glass. "Someone has to keep them calm."

"No," Edmund said immediately, the way you say no to a thing you saw coming three miles off.

"The mountain hums because the court is caged," Wren said. "I told you that. And the wardens are part of the cage—they're its hands, its teeth, its *shepherds*. But they answer to the hum. They always have. If somebody keeps singing it—keeps it steady, keeps it *soothing*—they stay slow. Confused. Gentle, even." She shrugged, and it was the most terrible shrug Edmund had ever seen, light as a girl deciding which apple to eat. "I've been listening to this mountain my whole life. I know all its verses."

"You're a child," Brannoc said hoarsely. The rogue tithe-man was leaning against a shelf, one hand pressed to his side where a warden's edge had caught him during the theft. His blood, Edmund noticed distantly, was very red against all that paleness. "You're fifteen and a thief and you pick pockets because you're too small to do anything else."

"I'm small enough to fit places other people can't," Wren said. "And I hear things other people can't. This is the only job in the world I'm qualified for." She grinned at them, quick and bright, and Edmund thought: *she learned that grin somewhere, from someone, and whoever it was would be proud and furious in equal measure.* "Besides. You're all forgetting the important part. I'm not dying. I'm staying. There's a difference, and the difference is I get to choose."

*Choosing what you're willing to lose.* Maren's voice, in his memory, though she wasn't here yet—though she was still somewhere below, following her own old debts into the dark. Edmund pushed the thought away. He'd deal with his mistress when the mountain stopped trying to kill them.

"Wren—" he started.

"Don't waste my song on speeches." She turned, walked three steps down the aisle, and began to hum.

It was the same note the mountain made—the one Edmund had felt in his ribs since they'd entered—but shaped, carried, given a melody's patience. And the nearest warden, which had been gliding toward them with the smooth inevitability of an avalanche, stopped. Tilted its blank face. And stood there, swaying slightly, like a man hearing a lullaby he half-remembered from a country he couldn't name.

"Go," Wren said over her shoulder, not stopping the hum. Her voice came out round and strange around the melody. "And Edmund? When you get him out—" she nodded at the jar, "—tell him about the night on the pass road. The one with the wine and the terrible singing. He'll want to know somebody kept it."

Edmund ran.

* * *

The vaults of the pale court were built like a honeycomb, if honeycombs were designed by something that had never seen a bee but had only had them described by a frightened prisoner. Corridors branched at angles that hurt to look at. Shelves lined every wall, floor to impossible ceiling, and every jar on every shelf was screaming now—or singing, or weeping, or laughing; it was hard to tell sympathy from terror when ten thousand souls felt their ledger burning.

A jar shrieked as Edmund passed and he flinched so hard he nearly dropped Sable's. The sound went through him like a fishhook. *They can feel it,* he thought. *Every page I burned was a debt forgiven, and they felt the forgiveness like a wound.* He didn't understand that. He filed it away in the place where he kept everything he didn't understand, which was getting crowded lately.

"This way," Ivetta said. The deserter moved through the chaos like water finding a crack—she remembered nothing of her war crimes, but apparently her feet remembered how to retreat, and Edmund had never been so grateful for muscle memory in his life. "The gate's up two galleries and across the bridge of—"

She stopped.

Ahead of them, filling the corridor from wall to wall, stood a warden. Not swaying. Not lulled. Its glass face reflected them back at themselves, warped: Edmund small and clutching gold, Ivetta tall and blade-drawn, Brannoc slumped and bleeding.

Behind it, more were coming. He could hear them—not footsteps, exactly, but a sound like frost spreading.

"Ivetta," Edmund said carefully. "The gate opens for debts."

"I know what it opens for."

"And you owe the biggest debt of any of us. You said so yourself. A whole war's worth, and no memory of paying it." He swallowed. "It'll open for you fastest."

Ivetta looked at the warden. The warden looked at Ivetta, insofar as a faceless thing looks at anything. And then she did something Edmund would think about for the rest of his life, however much of it remained: she smiled.

"Brannoc," she said. "You remember designing burn-patterns for Veyra's vanguard. You remember every village. So tell me—which way does fire move slowest?"

Brannoc blinked blood out of his eye. "Updraft... updraft stalls it. Why?"

"Because I'm going to give this corridor an updraft." She pointed at the shelf beside her—at a jar, one jar among thousands, whispering its owner's best day. "These are bottled souls. Bottled *light*, the steward called them. Light burns. And if I break enough of them in the right order—" she glanced back at Edmund, and her smile went crooked, human, almost young, "—well. I never got to choose anything in that war. Let me choose this."

"You'd be freeing them," Edmund said. "All of them. Every soul on this gallery."

"I know what I'd be doing." For just a moment her composure cracked, and underneath it was something raw and enormous. "Do you know what it's like, owing a debt you can't even remember taking on? This is me paying some of it forward. Go. Take the smuggler home. I'll hold the gate as long as the fire holds the wardens."

Edmund wanted to argue. He was, he realized, becoming a person who argued with people who had already chosen—and losing, every time, to the sheer stubbornness of the freely given. He'd lost to Wren. He was going to lose to Ivetta.

He put his hand briefly on her shoulder. She let him.

"For what it's worth," he said, "whoever you were in that war—I don't think she'd want you to carry it alone anymore."

"Then it's a good thing," Ivetta said, drawing her blade and turning to face the warden, "that I'm not alone now. *Run*, Eastwick."

* * *

They ran.

Brannoc lasted longer than Edmund expected him to, which was its own kind of miracle, because the wound in his side was bad and getting worse, and they both knew it, and neither of them said it, which was its own kind of mercy. The old tithe-man kept pace through two galleries and a stair that spiraled the wrong direction, wheezing jokes the whole way—terrible jokes, jokes about brass needles and bureaucratic paperwork in hell—and it was only at the foot of the gate-bridge that he grabbed Edmund's arm and stopped walking.

"Bridge is narrow," he gasped. "One wide enough to fight on. Wardens coming up fast behind, and there'll be more at the far end unless somebody makes them busy." He patted the satchel at his hip, and Edmund saw the brass needles inside, dozens of them, gleaming. "I spent eleven years learning how these work. Extraction, storage, transfer. Never once used them for anything good." He smiled, and it hurt his face, Edmund could see it hurting. "Give me a minute to practice."

"Brannoc—"

"You talked me down on Widow's Teeth. Talked Ivetta down. Made us a company out of people who owed each other nothing but damage." He pushed Edmund gently toward the bridge. "Let an old bookkeeper balance his column. And Eastwick—when Veyra comes for you, and she will, ask her who taught her the extraction rite. Ask her whose initials are on the original needle-molds. I never dared look. You should."

Then he walked back down the corridor, needles in both hands, humming Wren's tune badly, and Edmund ran across the bridge with Sable's jar held like a promise, and behind him he heard the first warden meet the first needle, and heard Brannoc laugh—a real laugh, delighted, the laugh of a man finally using the right tool for the right job—and Edmund did not look back, because looking back was a luxury the living couldn't afford, and he intended, against all odds and all sense, to go on living.

* * *

The gate was open.

Of course it was. It had opened for their debts on the way in, and debts don't close. But standing in it, silhouetted against the grey daylight of the mountain pass, was a figure Edmund knew by her posture before he knew her by her face: straight-backed, arms folded, silver-streaked hair pinned with a quill.

"Maren," he said. Of all the reunions he'd rehearsed on the road—angry ones, pleading ones, one humiliatingly tearful one—he hadn't rehearsed this: relief so violent it felt like grief.

"You took longer than I calculated," his former mistress said. Her eyes went to the jar in his arms and something moved behind them, deep, like a fish under ice. "Is that—"

"Sable. Yes." He hugged the jar a little closer, absurdly, the way a child hides sweets. "And before you say anything: yes, I burned the ledger, yes, I doomed the negotiation, yes, the whole court is probably waking up angry, and no, I'm not sorry."

"I wasn't going to scold you." Maren stepped aside to let him through the gate, and her hand hovered near his shoulder without landing—an old habit, touching without touching, affection administered in homeopathic doses. "I was going to say that you finally learned to read a bargain properly. The steward offered you everything. You took the one thing that mattered and paid for it with everything else. That's not theft, boy. That's *accounting*."

From far below, muffled by miles of stone, came a sound like a bell the size of a cathedral being struck by grief. The mountain shuddered. Dust sifted from the gate's arch.

"The wardens are free of the hum," Maren said quietly. "Or the hum has changed. One of the two." She looked back into the dark, and her composure slipped—just for a heartbeat, just enough for Edmund to see the girl she'd been, the one who'd once traded here and lost her sister. "My sister is still down there. In a jar. On a shelf."

"I know."

"And I am going back in."

"Maren—"

"She waited forty years, Edmund. I will not make her wait forty-one." But she caught his arm as he moved to follow, and her grip was iron, and her eyes were wet and furious. "No. Your road is *up*. Yours and his. Someone has to carry what we win out into the daylight, or none of this was worth the price—and the price, boy, has been *appalling*." Behind her, in the dark, another figure was climbing: slight, humming, unhurried. Wren, come down from her post, or perhaps never having left it, or perhaps the mountain moving her like a piece on a board. Maren squared her shoulders. "Tell Ivetta she owes me a drink. Tell Brannoc—" her voice caught, just barely, "—tell Brannoc whatever survivors tell each other. Now *go*."

Edmund went.

* * *

The daylight hit him like a blow.

He came out of the mountain's mouth into late afternoon, into wind and the smell of pine and snowmelt, into a world so loud with ordinary things—birds, water, his own ragged breathing—that he staggered. The pass road stretched below, gold-lit. Somewhere beyond the ridges lay Dunmarrow, and the free cities, and the war.

He set the jar down on a flat stone, gently, the way you set down something sleeping.

The gold inside had gone still. No swirl. No shape. Just a soft, steady glow, like a coal banked for morning.

"Come on," Edmund whispered. His voice cracked on the second word. "Come on, you great infuriating smuggler. Twelve-year-old me waited nine years for you. Don't make the grown version wait too."

Nothing. Wind. The distant, fading thunder of the mountain settling its accounts.

And then—faint, so faint he might have imagined it, might have dreamed it, might have paid a memory for it without knowing—a tap. From inside the glass. Twice. Impatient.

The way Sable knocked on doors.

Edmund laughed, and it came out as half a sob, and he gathered the jar up and turned toward the road—

—and found the road full of soldiers.

Grey cloaks. Brass needles glinting at belts. The sigil of the usurper Veyra stitched at every shoulder, and crossbows leveled, and at their center a woman on a white horse looking at him with an expression he couldn't read at all.

Veyra herself. Come up the mountain. Come for him.

"Well," Edmund said to the jar, under his breath. "Bad news. We're not out of the mountain yet."
