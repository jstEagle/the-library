## 17. What Veyra Wants

They made it eleven miles before the army found them.

Edmund counted the miles because counting was the only thing keeping him sane. Eleven miles of downhill scree and pine shadow, Sable's jar wrapped in his coat against his chest like a feverish child, Ivetta limping on a bound ankle, Brannoc's blood still under everyone's fingernails because there had been no water to wash with and no time to want any. Wren's humming still echoed in his skull, fainter with every step, the way a bell keeps ringing after you've left the tower.

At mile nine, Ivetta had said, "We're being followed," without turning around.

At mile ten, Edmund had said, "I know."

At mile eleven, the trees ended all at once, the way a sentence ends when someone else decides it should, and there were soldiers. Hundreds of them, in grey-and-crimson, spread across the valley floor in neat ranks that looked less like an ambush than a reception. Banners hung limp in the windless morning. Somewhere ahead, a kettle was boiling over a cookfire, because apparently even usurpers' armies ate breakfast.

"Run?" said Ivetta.

"Where?" said Edmund.

"Fair point."

They could have scattered. Edmund thought about it honestly for perhaps four seconds—the old Edmund, the forgettable one, would have been gone into the treeline before the first shout, no name given, no debts owed. But the jar was heavy against his chest, and inside it something laughed at nothing, softly, the way Sable had always laughed at things nobody else found funny yet. You didn't run carrying that. You walked.

So they walked down into the valley, hands raised, and the soldiers parted around them with the strange courtesy of people who had been told *don't touch them, she wants them whole*, which was somehow worse than being handled roughly. Roughness you understood. Courtesy from an army whose brass needles you'd seen in a child's temple—that was its own kind of violence.

They were given tents. Actual tents, with actual food—stew, bread, tea that tasted faintly of iron—and left alone, which told Edmund everything about how confident Veyra was. You didn't guard mice. You guarded the cheese.

"She's going to take the jar," Ivetta said through the tent flap, watching the camp. Her ankle was propped on a crate; her hand never strayed far from the knife she'd been allowed to keep, which meant they wanted her to feel armed. "You know that. Whatever she offers, whatever she says—it ends with her taking it."

"I know."

"And you're going to listen anyway."

"I'm going to listen anyway." Edmund turned the jar in his hands. The light inside it moved like a candle flame deciding whether to be bothered. "She burned those villages, Ivetta. Widow's Teeth is still smoking. Whatever she wants to tell me, I'll listen, but I'm not going to *believe* her."

"That's what worries me," Ivetta said. "You used to be better at not listening."

* * *

Veyra came at dusk, alone.

That was the first wrong thing. Usurpers did not come alone. They came with banners and honor guards and trumpets, because half of ruling was noise. But the woman who ducked into Edmund's tent wore a plain soldier's coat with the rank insignia unpicked, and her hair was tied back like she'd done it herself, badly, and she looked—tired. Not theatrically tired, not weary-sovereign tired. Tired the way Brannoc had looked in his last hour, the tiredness of someone doing arithmetic that kept coming out wrong.

"You're smaller than the stories," she said.

"You're politer than the stories."

"The stories are written by my enemies and my flatterers, and both of them are terrible at their jobs." She sat down uninvited on the supply crate across from him, studied the jar in his lap, and did not reach for it. That was the second wrong thing. "May I see him?"

"Him?"

"Sable. The smuggler. He spent three days in my custody two years ago. We played cards." Something crossed her face that was too quick to name. "He cheated. Badly. It was the most honest anyone's been with me in a decade."

Edmund's arms tightened around the jar without his permission. "You bought his soul."

"I did."

"You buy *everyone's*. I watched your tithe-men work. A little girl in Dunmarrow forgot her mother's face while her mother stood right there holding her hand. Don't you dare sit in my tent and call that anything but theft."

For a long moment Veyra said nothing. Then she leaned forward, elbows on knees, and when she spoke her voice had dropped all its authority like a coat taken off at the end of a long day.

"How much do you actually know about the mountain, Eastwick? And before you answer—everything you learned, you learned from a hedge-witch and a pickpocket and a ledger written by the court itself. So let me tell you the version the court doesn't put in its own books."

"I'm listening," Edmund said, hating that he was.

"The pale court is not a kingdom. It's a cage. Has been for—" she waved a hand, "—longer than names. There's something underneath it, older than Veyra the usurper, older than the free cities, older probably than the concept of *underneath*. And it is hungry. Not evil—hungry, the way winter is hungry, the way fire is hungry. Left alone, it wakes, and it eats, and it doesn't distinguish between Veyra's armies and Dunmarrow's orphans. Everything gets eaten. The grief, the joy, the mother's face, the child. All of it, gone down a throat the size of a valley."

"So you feed it souls instead."

"I feed it *willingly sold* souls," Veyra said, and there it was—the thing under her voice, the thing she'd built an empire to avoid saying. "That's the trick of it. The binding requires consent. Real consent. The court can't be starved by force and it can't be chained by force, so every soul in those vaults signed itself away. Debtors, gamblers, desperate people, yes—I won't pretend the ledgers are clean, I'm not a fool—but willing. Every one. Because a thousand sleeping souls, dreaming their best days on repeat, keep it drowsy. Keep it dreaming instead of waking." She looked at the jar. "Your smuggler volunteered. Did Maren tell you that part? He heard what was coming. He heard what happens if the mountain wakes hungry. And he did the arithmetic none of us wanted to do, and he sold himself to buy the free cities time. Three years, maybe five. That was the deal he struck."

The tent felt very small. Edmund became aware that his hands were shaking, and made them stop, mostly.

"You're lying."

"Probably," Veyra agreed pleasantly. "Check it against what you saw down there. Check it against the humming. Check it against the fact that the wardens only woke when you started *burning pages*—when you started removing consent from the equation. If I were lying, boy, wouldn't the truth have been more convenient?"

And that was the worst part. It fit. It fit the hum, the vaults, the whispering jars each dreaming their best day on loop—it fit like a key Edmund had been hoping wouldn't fit anything.

"If what you're saying is true," he said slowly, "then taking Sable's jar out—"

"—starts a clock. The binding frays at the edges first. Little dreams slip loose. The mountain stirs. And in some number of weeks, depending on how stubborn your hedge-witch's rituals were, it wakes up *hungry*, and there aren't enough souls in all the vaults combined to put it back down, because you also burned the ledger, and the ledger was half the binding." She spread her hands. "Congratulations. You may have doomed everyone. I'd offer you a drink but I finished mine on the walk over."

Outside, somewhere in the camp, soldiers were singing a marching song badly. It was obscene and about a mule. Edmund held onto the sound of it like a rope.

"What do you want from me?"

"The jar back. The ledger restored—or enough of it reconstructed. And you, ideally, to stop improvising." She stood, brushed off her coat, and for one moment looked every inch the usurper again, tall and cold and certain. "In exchange: truce. Full amnesty for your ragged company. The free cities keep their walls and their markets and their little grubby freedoms. I keep feeding the mountain, quietly, the way jailers have always fed the thing in the cellar. Nobody sings songs about it. Everybody lives."

"And the tithe-men? The needles? The girl who forgot her mother?"

"Grief is cheaper than extinction," Veyra said simply. "I have made peace with being the villain in exchange for everyone getting a tomorrow. Can you say the same? Because I don't think you can. I think you're a smuggler who stole a soul because he couldn't stand owing a debt, and now the whole world gets to pay his bill."

She paused at the tent flap, silhouetted against the fires.

"You have until dawn. And Eastwick—if you run, I won't chase you. I don't need to. The mountain will collect us both." The flap fell shut behind her.

Edmund sat in the lamplight with the jar warm against his chest, and inside it, faint and oblivious and beautiful, Sable laughed at something nobody else had heard yet.

Dawn was six hours away. Edmund spent the first hour not thinking, the second hour thinking too much, and somewhere in the third hour he noticed that the jar's light had begun to flicker—not steadily, not like a candle, but like a heartbeat skipping.

Like something waking up.

Or something reaching out.

He pressed his palm flat against the glass, and for half a second—half a second, no more—he smelled woodsmoke and rain on a mountain road, and heard a voice that wasn't the courier's and wasn't his own say, clear as a bell:

*"Eastwick. You absolute idiot. What did you do?"*
