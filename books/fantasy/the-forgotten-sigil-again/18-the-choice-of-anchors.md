## 18. The Choice of Anchors

The mountain had stopped screaming.

That was the first thing Edmund noticed, standing in the wreckage of the vaults with Sable's jar clutched to his chest like a stolen loaf of bread. The bottled souls had gone quiet—not silent, never silent, but quiet the way a room goes quiet when someone finally says the true thing out loud. A held breath. A listening.

Veyra stood ten paces away with her soldiers at her back and her hands, absurdly, folded in front of her like a woman waiting for a market stall to open.

"Return the jar," she said, "and I will call off my tithe-men from every free city within a season's ride. You have my word."

"Your word," Edmund said. His voice came out rougher than he intended. He was aware, distantly, that Ivetta was bleeding at the gate behind him, that Brannoc was dead somewhere in the dark of the vaults, that Wren's song still drifted up from the deep places like smoke through floorboards. He was aware of all of it the way you're aware of rain when you're drowning. "The word of the woman who taught children to forget their mothers' faces."

"I kept them *asleep*," Veyra said, and there it was again—that terrible patience, that weariness worn smooth as river stone. "You think I wanted this? You think I woke one morning and decided to become an accountant of souls? The court beneath Thornfell is hungry, Eastwick. It has been hungry since before your grandmother's grandmother learned to write. Left alone, it wakes, and when it wakes it does not buy. It takes. Every soul in every city from here to the sea, taken whole, taken screaming. I found a way to feed it slowly. In driblets. In debts." She spread her hands. "I built a machine of misery because the alternative was annihilation, and you—*you*—have just smashed the gauge on the boiler."

Edmund looked down at the jar. Through the pale glass, faint as breath on a window, something moved. Not struggling. Waiting.

"You could have asked," he said. "For help. For anchors. For anything."

"Asked whom?" And for one unguarded moment, the usurper of half a continent looked simply tired—a middle-aged woman who had been carrying a weight so long she'd forgotten her shoulders were ever straight. "Who believes a jailer?"

* * *

It was Maren who broke the silence.

She came limping out of the side gallery where the old debts had led her, her gray braid coming loose, her hedge-witch's coat scorched along one hem from wardens that were no longer chasing anyone. She looked at Veyra for a long moment. Then at Edmund. Then at the jar.

"Well," she said. "You've made a proper hash of it, haven't you."

"Maren—"

"Hush. I'm thinking." She walked past both of them—past the usurper, past the soldiers who parted for her with the uneasy deference people show to storms—and pressed her palm flat against the nearest wall of the vault. The stone hummed under her hand. Edmund felt it in his teeth.

"The ledger's burned," she said. "The pages you tore out are ash, and the court can't collect what's written nowhere. But the hunger doesn't read books, boy. The hunger just *is*. It'll wake by winter. Maybe sooner, now that its feeding's been interrupted mid-meal." She turned. Her eyes were wet, which frightened him more than any spell she'd ever cast. "There's a way. There's always been a way. My sister knew it, before they took her. The court doesn't need thousands of souls. It needs one."

"One what?" Ivetta asked from the gate, leaning on her spear.

"One anchor." Maren's voice went very steady, the way it did when she was about to do something unforgivable. "One living mortal, willing, bound to the court's heart. Not consumed—*rooted*. The court feeds on belonging, on debt, on the ties between people. Give it one person who chooses to stay, freely, forever, and it has a hearth instead of a hearth-fire gone wild. One anchor, and the jars could be emptied. All of them. Every soul returned."

The silence stretched.

"And the anchor?" Edmund asked, though he already knew. He had known since the words *willing* and *forever* left her mouth. Some part of him had known since chapter one of his own miserable life—the boy who ran from every door before it could close behind him.

"Gives up the world," Maren said. "Not their memories. Not their soul. Just... the leaving. They belong to the court, and the court belongs to them, and neither can walk away. It's not death, Edmund. It's worse. It's *staying*."

He watched her square her shoulders. He watched her begin, with terrible calm, to chalk the first sigil of the binding rite onto the vault floor—an old sigil, a sigil he knew, a sigil she had taught him over a kitchen fire when he was fifteen and furious and grieving, back when the worst thing in his life was the memory-tithes and not the whole rotten architecture of the world.

"No," Edmund said.

"It has to be me. I owe the court a soul already—my sister's. I've owed it thirty years. A debt this old, freely paid, will hold better than—"

"*No.*" Louder this time. The jars around them shivered in sympathy, whispering their best days to one another. Edmund stepped forward and put himself between Maren and her own chalk circle, which was, he reflected, the most backward thing he had ever done. "You don't get to do this. You don't get to spend thirty years being cold and charging me tithes and teaching me that love is a ledger, and then—then *this*. Buying forgiveness with yourself at the last minute like some—some tragic ballad."

Maren's chin came up. "This isn't about forgiveness, boy. This is arithmetic."

"Then let's do arithmetic." His heart was hammering. His hands were shaking. He set the jar down on the floor between them, gently, the way you set down something you might still lose, and he heard Sable's laugh through the glass—one bright note of it, muffled, patient—and he made himself look at his mistress, his teacher, the closest thing to a mother the world had ever grudgingly given him.

"You said a debt freely paid holds best. Whose debt is oldest here? Yours is thirty years. Mine—" He laughed, and it came out cracked. "Mine is everything. I owe Sable my life and twelve years of abandonment. I owe Brannoc a grave with his name spelled right. I owe Wren a song finished. I owe Ivetta the truth she traded away. I owe the courier whose skull I've been renting a burial. I owe you every sigil you ever taught me and every night you sat up worrying whether I'd frozen in some pass. I am, by any honest accounting, the most indebted man alive. If the court runs on debt—if it *is* debt, caged and starving—then I'm not a sacrifice, Maren. I'm a feast. I'm the richest meal this mountain has ever been offered."

"Don't," Maren whispered.

"You taught me that survival means choosing what you're willing to lose." His voice steadied. Strange, how the words only became true once he stopped running from them. "I've spent my whole life choosing nothing. Choosing to be forgettable. Choosing no names, no debts, no doors. And it bought me nothing but empty roads and a head full of other people's dying. So here's my choice. I choose this door. I choose to stay."

"Edmund." Maren's composure finally broke, all at once, like ice off a roof in spring. She grabbed the front of his coat with both hands. "You are twenty-three years old. You are a smuggler with salt in his boots and a man in a jar who is going to wake up and need you. You cannot—"

"He'll wake up remembering one night of laughter on a mountain road," Edmund said softly. "Because I chose to leave that in him. That's enough. That's more than enough. And he'll be able to visit. You said it yourself—the gate opens for the honest indebted. He owes me a rescue; I owe him a dozen. He'll come down. They all will." He covered her hands with his own. "And you'll come down too, old woman. Every week. Bring tea. Complain about my housekeeping. The court is going to learn what nagging is, and honestly, it deserves it."

A sound escaped Maren then that was half laugh and half sob, and it echoed strangely in the vault, and several hundred bottled souls sighed in answer, as if they'd been waiting decades for someone to say something kind in this place.

Behind them, Veyra spoke quietly. "The rite requires a witch of lineage to perform it. She must consent to bind you, or it fails. If she refuses—"

"She won't refuse," Edmund said. "Will you, Maren? Refuse me the one thing I've ever asked for free?"

Maren looked at him for a long, long time. He watched her do the arithmetic—she was right, it was always arithmetic with her—and he watched the sum come out wrong no matter how she arranged the numbers, and he watched her understand that he had learned that trick from her: how to make the losing column look like winning.

"You horrible boy," she said, weeping openly now, not bothering to hide it. "You learned everything I taught you."

"Everything worth keeping."

* * *

They cleared a space at the heart of the vaults, where the walls glowed softest and the humming was deepest—where Wren's distant song met the mountain's own note and harmonized. Ivetta carried Sable's jar and set it close, close enough to touch. Veyra ordered her soldiers back up the long stair, and to Edmund's lasting surprise, she stayed herself, standing at the edge of the light with her hands clasped, watching, a jailer witnessing the end of her prison by other means.

Maren chalked the circle. Her hands shook, so Edmund steadied them, and after a while they stopped shaking.

"It will hurt," she said. "The binding. Not the pain you're thinking of. The pain of roots going down. Like growing a whole new limb in a single breath."

"I've had worse hangovers."

"Liar."

"Apprentice," he corrected. "You never officially freed me. Which means, I believe, I owe you one final tithe." He smiled at her, and it hurt, and he did it anyway. "Consider this the payment."

Maren made a sound that might have been a curse in three dead languages, and then she began the rite.

She started, of course, with the first sigil she'd ever taught him—the little looping mark for *door*, drawn in chalk and memory and thirty years of regret. Then the next, and the next, each one a lesson he remembered, each one a debt named aloud as she spoke the old words: *for the salt you stole, for the truth you dodged, for the night you let me worry.* Edmund answered each one. *Paid. Paid. Paid.* The circle filled with light the color of early morning, and the mountain's hum rose to meet it, and the jars all around them began to glow like a city seen from a hilltop at dusk.

At the center of it, Edmund knelt. He thought of snow that wasn't his. He thought of a courier's last mile. He thought of a kitchen fire and a boy of twelve pulled from a slaver's cart by a laughing man with a knife-scarred grin. He thought: *I was never good at staying. Teach me now, mountain. I'm ready to learn.*

The roots went down.

It hurt exactly as much as she'd promised. He screamed, briefly, and then—strangely—it became something else, something like the moment when aching feet finally rest, when a held breath releases, when a door you've been pushing your whole life turns out to open inward. The court poured into him and he poured into the court, and there was no edge anymore between Edmund and the vast pale dark, and it was not drowning. It was *arriving*.

The last thing he saw, from the inside, was Maren on her knees at the circle's edge, tears cutting clean lines through the chalk dust on her face, and beyond her the jar—cracking, glowing, opening—

And a laugh. Unmistakable. Free.

"Sable," Edmund said—or the mountain said, or the mountain-and-Edmund said, warm as a hearth, welcome as morning—"you're late."

* * *

Outside the circle, the vaults were changing. The jars dimmed, one by one, not going dark but going *restful*, their whispers softening into sleep instead of longing. Somewhere far above, in the passes of Thornfell, tithe-men across half a continent dropped their brass needles and stared at their hands, feeling memories return to them like birds coming home to roost. Somewhere in the deep galleries, Wren stopped singing, listened, and laughed out loud, because the mountain was singing back, and it sounded—for the first time in an age older than kingdoms—happy.

Maren gathered the broken jar shards into her apron with shaking hands, because even now, especially now, someone had to tidy up.

And at the center of the fading circle, wrapped in the slow golden light of a court learning gentleness, Edmund Eastwick opened his eyes—which were also the mountain's eyes, which were also ten thousand windows—and watched a tall figure with a knife-scarred grin take his first uncertain step into the new-made dawn, looking around at the pale beauty of the vaults with the expression of a man checking an inn's exits, and finding, for once, that he didn't want one.

"Well," Sable said, to the room, to the mountain, to whatever remained of the boy he'd once pulled from a cart. "Somebody want to tell me why I feel like I've been asleep for a year and owe about six people my life?"

From everywhere and nowhere, warm as banked coals, Edmund laughed.

"Sit down," he said. "We've got debts to settle. And I remember every single one."
