## 19. Sable, Waking

The jar stopped whispering the moment Edmund set it down.

That was the first wrong thing. All through the vaults it had murmured—Sable's best day, over and over, a laugh folded into light—and now, in the grey morning outside Thornfell's gate, with ash still sifting from the sky and Ivetta's bandage soaked through and Brannoc dead somewhere behind them in the dark, the jar went silent as a held breath.

Edmund knelt in the trampled grass with it between his knees. His hands wouldn't stop shaking. He told himself it was the cold. It was not the cold.

"You don't have to do this today," Ivetta said. She stood a few paces off, one arm cradled against her ribs, watching the treeline for tithe-men who had, for the moment, stopped coming. "The mountain's quiet. Veyra's people are running around like ants with their hill kicked over. Nobody's going to take him back."

"I know how to wake a soul," Edmund said. Maren had taught him that much before she'd gone down into the dark to do something braver and stupider than anything he'd ever done. "Carry it out of the glass. Give it somewhere to land." He looked down at his own hands, at the faint sigil-marks still glowing along his knuckles where he'd gripped the ledger-jar too hard. "I just—"

He just didn't know what would be left when it landed.

A soul kept in a bottle for years didn't come out whole. He'd seen the returned ones in the vaults since the binding changed—pale, blinking things, remembering their names and nothing else, wandering the court's halls like guests who'd forgotten which room was theirs. Sable might wake and look at Edmund and see a stranger. A stranger holding the jar he'd been shut inside. A stranger wearing the face of whatever debt had bought him free.

Or worse. Worse was that Sable would wake and remember everything except Edmund—the slaver caravan, the snow, the twelve-year-old boy he'd pulled out of the chains—and understand exactly what kind of person Edmund had been to leave him behind.

"Edmund," Ivetta said again, gentler. "Whatever comes out of there, it isn't your fault. None of it."

"That's the problem," Edmund said. "It is."

* * *

Maren had explained it once, in her kitchen that smelled of tallow and burnt rosemary, while she stirred something in a pot that was definitely not soup: *A memory isn't a stone, boy. It's a fire. It needs feeding, and it gives off heat whether you want it to or not. And when you carry someone else's memory long enough—* she'd pointed the ladle at him— *it starts carrying you back.*

He hadn't understood then. He understood now, kneeling in the grass with his palms flat against cool glass.

Because here was the thing he had never told anyone, the thing he'd buried so deep even Maren's needles hadn't found it: when he'd held Sable's jar in the vaults, when the laugh inside it had washed over him like warm water, Edmund had done something no careful, forgettable smuggler should ever have done.

He'd reached in.

Not to steal. Not to tamper. Just—one moment. One small theft of tenderness, pressed into the soul like a flower into a book: a night on a mountain road, years ago, when Sable had been alive and laughing and whole, and Edmund had been seventeen and stupid and happy without knowing it. They'd shared a fire. Sable had stolen Edmund's boots as a joke and worn them all night, and in the morning he'd given them back filled with pinecones, and Edmund had laughed until his ribs hurt, and Sable had said, *You've got an ugly laugh, Eastwick. Like a goose being robbed.*

One night. Out of all the nights. It was all Edmund had dared to leave, because leaving more felt like forgery and leaving nothing felt like murder.

Now he broke the seal.

* * *

The soul came out the way smoke comes out of a doused candle—not rising but *unfolding*, spilling up through Edmund's fingers and into the air above the jar, coalescing by degrees into a shape, then a man, then—

Then Sable.

He was thinner than Edmund remembered. There were new lines around his eyes, earned somewhere in the years of willing captivity, and his hair had gone iron-grey at the temples in a way that suited him appallingly well. For a long moment he simply stood in the grass, swaying, breathing like a man surfacing from deep water, and Edmund forgot how to breathe himself.

Then Sable opened his eyes.

They were brown. Warm brown, flecked with gold, the exact brown that had looked at twelve-year-old Edmund across a slaver's campfire and said *not this one* —and they went straight past Ivetta, past the smoking horizon, past everything, and landed on Edmund with an expression of profound, offended recognition.

"You," Sable said. His voice was rough as a whetstone. "You're the one with the ugly laugh."

Edmund made a sound that was possibly a word.

"The mountain road." Sable frowned, pressing two fingers to his temple like a man trying to read writing through paper. "There was a fire. Pinecones. In my—" he gestured vaguely at his own feet— "someone's boots. My boots? Whose boots were those?"

"Mine," Edmund managed. "They were mine. You stole them."

"I did." Sable's frown deepened, but it wasn't confusion anymore—it was frustration, the frustration of a man reaching for a shelf that keeps receding. "And you laughed like a goose being robbed, and I thought—" He stopped. His hand dropped. He looked at Edmund fully, and what was in his face was worse than anger or blankness. It was grief. "I thought I knew you. I *know* I knew you. And it's gone. All of it's gone, except one stupid night, and I can feel the shape of everything else, like—like footprints in snow after the snow's melted. Hollows where things used to be."

"Sable—"

"How many years?" Sable asked quietly.

"Seven."

"Seven years." He said it flatly, testing the weight of it. "And the last thing I remember is a boy laughing at pinecones." He looked down at his own hands, turning them over, cataloguing the calluses, the scars. "These aren't a boy's hands. These are hands that did things. Seven years of things, and I'm standing here with one night and a pair of stolen boots."

Ivetta shifted her weight. "For what it's worth," she said, "most of us would kill for one good night to start from."

Sable glanced at her—at the deserter's posture, the soldier's eyes, the bandaged arm—and something in him recalibrated. Smugglers learned fast; seven years in a jar apparently hadn't dulled that. "Who are you people? Where am I? Why does the air taste like burnt sugar and bad decisions?"

"Thornfell," Edmund said. "Outside the mountain. The war's—changing. Veyra's economy runs on souls, and we just—" He faltered. How did you explain a ledger burned, a warden woken, a friend left singing in the dark, a witch anchoring a god? "We changed it."

"We," Sable repeated. His eyes narrowed. "You keep saying *we* like you weren't alone when you found me. You were alone, weren't you. I can tell. You've got the face of a man who did something enormous and has nobody to be modest about it to."

"He had us," Ivetta said. "He's just bad at counting."

* * *

They made camp in the ruins of a shepherd's hut a mile downslope, because Sable, on standing unassisted for longer than a minute, had gone grey and sat down hard in the grass, and because none of them trusted the gate's vicinity yet. Wren's humming still drifted faintly from the mountain's throat if the wind came from the right direction—a thin, sweet thread of sound keeping the wardens drowsy, keeping the old prisoner dreaming. Every time Edmund heard it his chest hurt in a way he refused to examine.

Sable slept three hours and woke ravenous, ate an entire bag of Ivetta's hardtack while complaining about it with genuine artistry, and then sat sharpening a knife he'd found somewhere within ten minutes of regaining corporeal existence, because some men were simply built that way.

"So," he said, without looking up from the blade. "Tell me about me."

Edmund, who had been pretending to inventory their supplies, stopped pretending. "What?"

"Seven years is a hole." Sable tested the edge on his thumb, nodded approval. "I intend to fill it. Start anywhere. Start with—" the knife paused— "start with why a stranger's laugh lives in my head."

"It's not a stranger's laugh."

"No," Sable agreed. "That's the strange part. It's *mine*. Or it's mine-from-outside, if that makes sense. The way your own voice sounds in someone else's ears." He finally looked up. "Someone carried that night carefully. Someone kept it clean. Souls in jars get handled rough—I can feel the bruises, all the places the tithe-men's fingers went. But that one night is untouched. Pristine. Somebody loved it enough to leave it alone." His voice did something complicated on the last words. "So. Who was he? The boy on the road?"

Edmund sat down across the fire from him. The firelight did its honest work, showing every line of exhaustion on both their faces.

"A boy I abandoned," Edmund said. "After you saved his life. After you vanished. He spent years angry about it, and then he spent years making sure he couldn't be angry about anything, because he sold his memories rather than feel them. And then he grew up and became a coward with a map, and the map led him to you, and he—" His voice cracked. He pushed through it, because Sable deserved the whole ugly account, not the edited version. "He took your soul out of a vault and ran, and people died doing it, and he'd do it again, and he doesn't know what to do with any of that except tell you the truth, which is: I'm sorry. For all of it. For leaving, and for coming back, and for only being able to give you one night."

The knife had gone still in Sable's hands.

"One night," Sable said slowly, "that you gave *me*, or one night you kept for yourself?"

"What?"

"When you handled the soul. When you put the memory in—or found it, or however it works." Sable set the knife down. "A man who wanted to erase himself wouldn't have bothered preserving anything. A man who wanted to punish himself wouldn't have chosen the *good* night. So which is it, Edmund Eastwick?" — and Edmund started, because he had never told Sable his name — "did you leave that night for me, or for you?"

The wind moved through the ruined hut. Somewhere far below, the mountain hummed.

"For me," Edmund admitted. "At first. It was the only thing I had left of you. Then—" he scrubbed a hand over his face— "then I realized it was the only thing *you'd* have left of yourself that wasn't bought or bruised. So I kept it clean. That's all. It's not noble. It's just—housekeeping."

Sable was quiet for a long time.

Then he laughed—that laugh, the real one, the one from the jar, and Edmund's heart did something structurally unsound—and said, "Housekeeping. Seven years in a bottle and my rescuer describes love as housekeeping." He shook his head. "You're exactly the sort of man I would have liked. I can tell. It's infuriating."

"You don't know that you would have liked me."

"I know the boy on the road laughed at pinecones at midnight in the freezing cold with a thief wearing his boots." Sable leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight catching the gold in his eyes. "I know he stayed awake talking to me instead of sleeping, even though he was tired, because I remember wanting him to stay awake. Whatever else got sold or lost or buried—" he tapped his own temple— "the shape of that is still here. And the shape fits you."

"It shouldn't," Edmund said hoarsely. "You should wake up hating me. Everyone else does."

"Mm. The woman with the arm thinks you're worth following. The dead one clearly thought you were worth dying for—I can smell the grief on all of you, it's practically a seasoning." Sable shrugged. "And I woke up in a burning world with one memory and one name in my mouth, and the name was yours. That's not nothing, Edmund. That's a foundation. Small. Ugly. But foundations usually are."

* * *

Near midnight, Ivetta took first watch and Sable lay staring at the roof beams, and Edmund sat by the low fire doing the arithmetic of survival—he was always doing the arithmetic now; it was what remained when a man had sold his softer sums—and Sable spoke into the dark.

"The night. The one I've got. Tell me the parts I'm missing."

"You have all of it."

"I have the *feeling* of it. Fire, cold, laughter, pinecones, a boy whose face won't resolve no matter how I squint." Sable rolled onto his side, propping his head on one hand. "But there are gaps. Little ones. Things that happened around the edges. What did we talk about?"

Edmund stared into the flames. "You told me about the sea. You'd grown up on the coast, before. You said salt water was the only honest thing in the world, because it never pretended to be anything but what it was."

"Sounds like me." A pause. "What else?"

"You asked why I was traveling with the caravan. I lied. You knew I lied. You let me lie, because you could see I wasn't ready to say it out loud." Edmund's throat tightened. "And then in the morning, when the slavers came back around, you put me on a horse and told me to ride east and not look back, and I didn't ask your name until the last second, and you shouted it after me. Sable. Like the color. Like the bird."

"And then you rode east," Sable said softly, "and never looked back."

"And then I never looked back," Edmund agreed. "For seven years."

Sable was quiet. When he spoke again, his voice had the careful evenness of a man handling something fragile. "Here's what I know, Edmund Eastwick, and you can argue with it in the morning if you like. I woke up owning one night and one debt. The night is yours—we'll share it, that seems fair. But the debt is mine to pay, and I pay my debts. So tomorrow, you're going to tell me everything. The war, the mountain, the witch, the ledger, the people we lost. All of it, the ugly included. Especially the ugly." He settled back down, closing his eyes. "And then we're going to figure out what a man with seven missing years and one very good night can do to help. Because I felt that mountain humming, Edmund. And I don't think it's done with any of us."

Edmund looked at him—at the iron-grey temples, the scarred hands, the face that was and wasn't the face from the caravan—and felt the strangest thing loosen in his chest. Not forgiveness. Not yet. Maybe not ever, fully. But something adjacent. Something that might, with work, become a foundation.

"Get some sleep," Edmund said. "Tomorrow's going to be terrible."

"They usually are," Sable murmured, already half under. "Wake me if the mountain stops humming."

Edmund promised he would. And an hour later, when the hum beneath their feet stuttered—just once, just slightly, like a singer drawing breath—Edmund was on his feet with his heart hammering before the echo faded, watching the treeline, watching the gate's distant glow, listening.

The hum resumed. Thinner now. Strained.

As if Wren, somewhere in the deep, had begun to run out of song.
