## 20. The Usurper's Fall

The first thing to go was the ledgers.

Ivetta saw it happen in the camp below Widow's Teeth, where Veyra's army had penned them for three days while their queen went off to parley with a smuggler carrying a jar. The tithe-clerks kept their records in brass-bound books, one entry per purchased soul: name, price, memory taken, date of sale. On the morning after Edmund walked into the mountain, every clerk in the camp opened her book and found the pages blank.

Not torn out. Not burned. *Blank*, as though the ink had been called home.

"They're screaming," Wren had said—not that Wren was there anymore, but Ivetta would swear afterward she heard the pickpocket's voice on the wind, faint and humming. "Well. They would be."

What actually happened was quieter and far stranger. A soldier of the Ninth Column, a broad-shouldered woman named Ferren who had sold four years of her life to buy her brother out of a debtors' pit, woke at dawn remembering a wedding. Not hers—a stranger's wedding, some free-city couple dancing badly under a paper lantern—and then she remembered it was *hers*, that she had been the one dancing badly, that someone had laughed at her and she had kissed them anyway. Four years came back in the space between one heartbeat and the next, and with them came the knowledge of what she'd done while those years were missing.

She put down her spear. She picked up her bedroll. She started walking east, toward a city she hadn't seen since before the war, humming a tune she didn't know she knew.

By noon, a tenth of the army had done the same. By evening, half.

* * *

Sable watched it from the ridge above the pass, the jar's emptiness still strange against his chest—he kept reaching up to touch the place where it used to hang, like a man probing a socket where a tooth had been. His memories were mostly gone. He knew that the way you know weather: there were shapes where a life should have been, coastlines of feeling with nothing inland. He knew he had been brave once, because his body still moved like a brave man's. He knew he had loved someone, because when Edmund's name crossed what remained of his mind, something in him leaned toward it like a plant toward light.

And he knew one night, whole and perfect: a fire on a mountain road, bad wine passed back and forth, Edmund laughing so hard at Sable's impression of a tax collector that he snorted wine through his nose and then laughed harder at himself, and the stars over Thornfell so thick they looked like spilled salt, and Sable thinking—with a certainty that had survived everything else, everything that had been bought and sold and drunk away—*this one. Whatever happens, this one.*

That was all. It was enough to stand on.

"Look," said Ivetta beside him.

Below, the army was coming apart like a knot untied. Not fleeing—deserting was too orderly a word. Men and women simply stopped being soldiers. Officers shouted themselves hoarse and were ignored, or worse, were recognized: a sergeant who suddenly remembered his daughter's face and dropped his sword mid-order to stare at his own hands, as if wondering what they'd been doing all this time. Some wept. Some laughed. One entire company sat down in the road and began comparing memories, tentatively at first, then with the wild joy of people discovering they'd been robbed by the same thief.

"They're not going to fight," Ivetta said. She sounded almost disappointed, which Sable suspected was her way of sounding relieved. "All those years Veyra spent buying loyalty, and it turns out loyalty doesn't keep. It goes stale. Like bread."

"Everything goes stale," Sable said. "That's how you know it was real."

Ivetta looked at him sideways. "You're very calm for a man with no past."

"I have one memory," Sable said. "It's a good one. I'm rationing it."

* * *

Veyra's parley tent stood empty for two days before anyone worked up the courage to look inside.

When Ivetta finally did—because someone had to, and Brannoc was dead, and the pickpocket was singing to a mountain—the tent held only a table, a chair, and a single sheet of paper weighted down with the queen's signet ring. The ring was warm, as if recently worn. The paper read, in a hand so neat it looked printed:

*I bought souls because the alternative was feeding it freely. I was wrong about which prison needed keeping. Tell the cities the debt is discharged. —V.*

Beneath it, smaller:

*If it wakes hungry, I will be the first thing it eats. That is not a threat. It is an accounting.*

"She went into the mountain," Ivetta reported, back at the ridge. "Alone. No guard. No crown." She paused. "No shoes, actually. She'd left those by the gate. Like she was going somewhere she wanted to feel the floor."

Nobody said anything for a while. Grief for a tyrant is a complicated meal; nobody was sure which parts were safe to swallow.

"She built the cages," Ivetta added finally, "and then she spent twenty years learning why the cages were wrong, and then she walked into her own prison to apologize in person. That's either the most cowardly thing or the bravest thing I've ever heard, and I genuinely cannot tell which."

"It's both," Sable said. "Most true things are both. That's what makes them true instead of convenient."

Ivetta gave him a long look. "You know," she said, "for a man missing ninety-nine percent of himself, you've got an awful lot of opinions."

"The one percent is concentrated," Sable said, and Ivetta surprised herself by laughing—the first laugh in the free cities that week not born of hysteria.

* * *

Inside the mountain, Edmund felt the collapse the way a lighthouse feels ships coming safely home.

He was learning the shape of his new work. The court beneath Thornfell had been a mouth for centuries, swallowing souls to stay bound; now it was something gentler, fed by a single willing anchor, and the change ran through it like warmth through cold hands. The vaults still stood—shelves upon shelves of bottled light—but the jars had begun, one by one, to empty themselves upward. Souls rising. Going wherever souls go when nobody owns them.

He felt each departure as a small loosening, a knot untying somewhere in his chest. And threaded through all of it, faint but constant, he felt the world above: armies dissolving, ledgers blanking, a woman walking barefoot down toward him through stone that parted for her like water.

*Here comes the jailer,* the court murmured around him, in its voice like wind through bottle-necks. *What shall we do with her?*

"That depends," Edmund said aloud, to the dark, to himself, to whatever he was becoming, "on whether she's still holding the key."

* * *

Veyra descended for six hours.

She did not hurry. She stopped at each vault shelf and read the labels—every jar in the court bore its owner's best day written in the court's pale script—and at several she reached out and touched the glass, briefly, the way you'd touch a gravestone. At one jar she stopped for a long time. The label read: *A sister teaching a boy his first sigil, and getting the ink on her nose.*

"That one's mine," she said quietly, to no one. "I paid for it myself, years ago. I thought if I owned enough other people's best days, I'd stop needing my own." Her thumb traced the glass. "It didn't work. In case anyone was wondering. It never works."

Then she walked on, down and down, to the place where the court pooled like moonlight in a cellar, and where a young man made of ordinary flesh and extraordinary debts sat waiting on a step, looking tired and kind and slightly annoyed, the way he apparently always would.

"You're shorter than the stories," Edmund said.

"You're younger," Veyra said. "That's worse. Do you have any idea what I sacrificed to keep this thing asleep?"

"Yes," Edmund said. "I've read your ledger. All of it. You were right about the hunger and wrong about everything else, and both of those things are going to take a very long time to explain to the cities, and I don't envy you the explaining."

"There won't be any explaining." Veyra looked around at the vaults, at the emptying shelves, at the soft gold light of souls in passage. "I came to settle accounts. That's all a ruler is, in the end—an accountant with better clothes. So." She folded her hands. "Item one: the souls I purchased. Discharged. Item two: the armies I misled. Discharged—they'll find their own way home, they always do. Item three—" and here her composure flickered, just once, like a lamp in wind, "—item three is my sister's soul, if it's still here, and I would like to pay whatever remains unpaid on it, and I would like to do it myself, and I would like you to stop looking at me like that."

"Like what?"

"Like I'm a person. It's unbearable."

Edmund almost smiled. "The court says you can't pay for her. The debts are broken. Everything's returning whether anyone likes it or not."

"Then I'll wait for her," Veyra said, "and I'll say the things I should have said forty years ago, and then I'll stay, because someone has to help you tend this place, and frankly you look like you could use the staff." She paused. "Also I'm extremely organized."

Edmund stared at her. Somewhere in the vaults, a jar whispered its owner's best day, and another went dark as its soul rose free, and the mountain hummed a note that might, in a generous mood, have been called approval.

"You want to be *clerical staff*," Edmund said, "in a cave, forever."

"I want to be useful," Veyra said, "somewhere my name doesn't make children cry. This seemed like the only option left." She lifted her chin. "Well? Do I get the position or not?"

Edmund thought about Maren, who had traded herself for love and called it a bargain. About Wren, singing in the dark by choice. About Sable, standing on a ridge with one memory and calling it enough. Everyone he'd ever met, it seemed, was trying to give away exactly the thing everyone else was trying to buy.

"Probationary," Edmund said. "Six months. If you alphabetize anything without asking, you're out."

* * *

Above, the free cities held.

They held the way old walls hold—not triumphantly, but stubbornly, with mortar missing in places. Dunmarrow reopened its market and found, scattered among the stalls, dozens of people standing perfectly still, overwhelmed by returned memories, and the merchants did the decent thing, which was to feed them until they could speak again. The tithe-men's brass needles were melted down and recast as bells, which rang slightly flat, which everyone agreed was fitting. Children who had forgotten their mothers' faces were carried home in carts, and the reunions were not always easy—memory returns faster than forgiveness—but they happened, which was more than anyone had dared expect.

Ivetta took command of what remained of the defense forces, mostly because she was the only one still giving orders and mostly her orders were "go home," which proved wildly popular. She cried exactly once, privately, over a brass-bound ledger book with blank pages, and then she had it buried with honors, because dead things deserve graves even when the death is a mercy.

And on the ridge above Widow's Teeth, Sable stood in the evening light and touched the empty place on his chest one last time, and did not reach for the one memory, and did not need to. It wasn't going anywhere. Best days, he was beginning to understand, were the one thing that couldn't be bought, because nobody who had one would ever sell.

Below him, the mountain hummed.

It sounded, if you listened closely—and Sable listened closely, with everything he had left, which was one perfect night and a heart that leaned toward a name—it sounded less like a cage now. Less like hunger. More like a door, opening.

More like someone inside, waiting to be visited.
