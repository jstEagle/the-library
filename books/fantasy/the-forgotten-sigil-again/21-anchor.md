## 21. Anchor

The first thing you learn as an anchor is that the court dreams when you sleep.

Nobody warned me about that. Maren taught me the rite in eleven words and a handful of tears she pretended were dust in her eye, and then she stepped back through the gate with Sable's empty jar under her arm like a woman carrying home bread, and I stood alone in the pale dark expecting—what? Torment. Hunger. The slow grinding of a soul against a mountain's teeth.

Instead I got Tuesday.

That's what I call it. The court has no days; it has moods, tides, weather made of other people's afternoons. And every so often the whole vast hollow of it settles into something warm and drowsy and smelling faintly of woodsmoke, and I have decided, arbitrarily and with great authority, to call it Tuesday.

I was tending shelves.

That sounds small. It is not small. There are — I stopped counting at some point, because counting felt like accounting and I had done enough accounting for one lifetime — there are a very great many jars here, and since the ledger burned and Veyra's economy collapsed like a bad roof, most of them are emptying. The souls go home. That's the whole miracle, really: they simply *go home*, drifting up through the stone like bubbles through honey, back into whatever bodies still want them or out into whatever comes next for the ones whose bodies gave up waiting. My job — my chosen, unpaid, deeply unglamorous job — is to mind the ones who linger.

Some linger because they're frightened. Some because they're stubborn. One, an old ferryman named Hesk who sold his memory of his wife's face to pay a gambling debt and then spent forty years trying to buy it back, lingers because he says he likes the company. "You talk too much," he told me our first week, from inside his jar, which I thought was rich coming from a man who'd been a jar for two years.

"I'm all you've got," I said.

"Exactly," said Hesk. "Tragic."

* * *

Maren's sister's name was Odile.

I learned it on my fourth day, when I finally worked up the nerve to go looking. Her jar sat on a high shelf in the eastern vaults, in a row of jars from the same year — the year Maren came down here trading and lost more than she'd bargained for. I carried it down with both hands, the way you carry something sleeping, and set it on the low stone table near the gate where the light pools, if you can call bottled-soul-glow light, which by then I had decided you could.

"Odile," I said. "Your sister sent me. Well. Your sister sent herself, eventually, but she sent me first, and she's terrible at goodbyes, so I'll do this part."

The jar hummed. They all hum, faintly, each with its own note — a best day makes a sound, did you know that? Sable's had sounded like laughter. Odile's sounded like rain on a kitchen roof and someone singing badly over the noise of it.

"She taught me my first sigil," I told her. "Your sister. She drew it in spilled tea on a table and told me if I ever drew it wrong the tea would come back up. It didn't work, obviously. She just wanted me to be careful about things that matter." I turned the jar slowly, the way Wren had shown me calms them. "She's freeing you. She's up there right now doing the rite, and she's going to get it right, because she's Maren, and Maren gets things right even when she pretends she's doing them badly on purpose."

The humming changed key. Warmer. I chose to believe that meant thank-you and not *you talk too much*, though honestly it might have been both. Everyone in this mountain thinks I talk too much. I have decided to take it as affection.

It took three weeks. Three weeks of me sitting at that table reading aloud — I read her the courier's memories, the ones that bled into me on the road, all his small human cargo: a dog named Turnip, a letter he never delivered to a woman in Dunmarrow, the taste of the last apple he ever ate. On the twenty-second day the jar went quiet, and then it went *empty*, and I felt her pass through me on her way up like a draft of cold clean air, and somewhere above us a hedge-witch in a leaky cottage suddenly found her kitchen full of rain-song and a sister she'd mourned for thirty years.

I cried a little. Then I put the empty jar on the shelf marked for empties, which Wren insists on calling the cupboard of the retired, and I made myself tea, because anchors are allowed rituals, and ritual is just grief with better posture.

* * *

Sable comes every Thursday.

He can't stay long — the gate opens for the honest indebted, and Sable keeps himself scrupulously, almost offensively debt-free these days, so each visit costs him something small. A coin owed to a carter. A favor promised to Ivetta. He arrives owing, stays an hour, leaves square. It's the strangest pilgrimage schedule I've ever heard of and I love him completely for it.

"You look terrible," he said last week, dropping onto the bench across from me. He always says that. He always means *hello*.

"I'm a man living inside a mountain sustained by consent," I said. "I'm thriving."

"You've got shelf-dust in your hair."

"That's prestige dust."

He grinned, and for a moment — just a moment — I saw the twelve-year-old boy in him, the one who'd cut a slave-collar off a snotty kid in a caravan twenty years ago and vanished before the kid could even say thank you properly. Then the grin settled into the older, wearier thing, the smuggler's face, the man who sold his own soul to buy a few cities a season of time and never once mentioned it to anyone, including me, especially me.

"How's the road?" I asked.

"Wet. Profitable. Boring." He leaned back. "Wren wants to know if you've noticed the singing yet."

"The whole mountain sings, Wren knows that."

"No — lately. Since you took the anchor. She says the binding-hum changed key. Says it sounds less like a cage now and more like—" he waved a hand, searching, "—a house. Somebody humming while they cook."

I hadn't noticed. You don't notice your own heartbeat either. But I filed it away, the way I file everything now, carefully, in the place where I keep the things I refuse to lose.

"There's something else," Sable said, and hesitated, which Sable never does. "There's a night. On a mountain road. Stars like spilled salt, and you were — we were laughing about something stupid. I don't remember what. I remember *that* I laughed until my ribs hurt, and I remember your face when you laughed, and I don't remember anything else around it. No before. No after. Just that."

My hands went very still on the table.

"It's the only thing I kept," he said quietly. "When they pulled me out of the jar. Everything else came back except — there's a hole shaped like you, Edmund. And in the middle of the hole, one night, burning like a coal." He looked at me. "Did you do that?"

I could have lied. I'm good at lying; I was raised on it, practically. But the court hears everything said near its anchor, and besides, I had promised myself, down here among all these returned souls, that I would stop lying to the people who mattered.

"You paid everything," I said. "Your whole self, to buy the cities time. When I stole the jar back, there wasn't much left to give you. So I left you the best thing I had that was ours."

Sable was quiet for a long moment. Down the vault-row, Hesk's jar hummed something that sounded suspiciously like a snicker.

"One night," Sable said at last. "Out of everything."

"It was a very good night."

"It was," he agreed, and his voice cracked on it, and he reached across the table and gripped my wrist, hard, the way you grip a rope in weather. "I'll build the rest around it. Give me time. I'll come back next Thursday owing a fishmonger, and the Thursday after owing a priest, and one of these days I'm going to walk through that gate owing you nothing but the future, and we'll see what the door does then."

"It opens for debts," I said. "Not for their absence."

"Then I'll owe you forever," said Sable, "and consider it a bargain," and he laughed — that laugh, the one I'd tried so hard to forget and failed, thank all the patient stars — and the whole eastern vault hummed along with him like a struck bell.

* * *

Ivetta comes less often, but when she comes, she brings reports, because Ivetta cannot sit still and cannot stop soldiering and has decided, in her own words, that I am "the worst-placed officer in the free cities."

"The northern passes are clear," she told me two weeks ago, pacing the length of the gate-chamber like a caged cat. "Veyra's deserters have mostly gone home. Half of them woke up with their purchased memories flooding back and just — dropped their weapons in the road. Brannoc's burning-pattern manuals are being burned, which I find poetic, and the council in Dunmarrow has voted to formally recognize the mountain as—" she checked a scrap of paper with visible disgust, "—'a sovereign sanctuary of returned persons.' Your idea?"

"Wren's. I just wrote it down in fancy language."

"You write fancy?"

"I contain multitudes, Ivetta."

She snorted. Then she stopped pacing, and stood there in the soul-light with her arms crossed, and I watched her wrestle with whatever it was, because Ivetta always wrestles visibly, like a woman fighting her own shadow in broad daylight.

"They offered me my war crimes back," she said finally. "The council. A restoration rite, some hedge-mage out of Corvale. They said a deserter who remembers nothing can't properly atone." Her jaw worked. "I told them no."

"Why?"

"Because I've spent a year deciding who I am without those memories. And I'm not going to let some committee hand me a stranger's sins and call it honesty." She looked at me, fierce and uncertain all at once. "Was that wrong?"

I thought about it honestly, because she deserved honest. "I think you're the only person I've ever met who's building herself from scratch," I said, "and I think the fact that you keep choosing to guard gates and carry reports and visit a man in a mountain suggests the scratch you're building from is better than most people's foundations." I paused. "Also, if the memories come back someday on their own, you'll handle it. You handle everything. Annoyingly."

Something in her shoulders loosened. "Brannoc would have liked hearing that."

"Brannoc would have argued with it."

"He argued with everything. It's how he loved." She said it lightly, but not lightly enough, and we let the silence sit for a moment, respectful, the way you let silence sit around a grave. Brannoc had died buying us minutes in the vaults, warden-fire eating through his shield-arm, and his last words had been a complaint about the quality of Edmund Eastwick's escape plans. I keep a jar for him on the lowest shelf. It's empty. It will always be empty. Some men give everything and leave nothing behind to bottle, and that's not a tragedy; that's just arithmetic done generously.

"Next report in a month," Ivetta said, squaring up. "Try to look worse by then. It comforts me."

"Anything for morale."

* * *

And Wren — Wren doesn't visit. Wren *performs*.

Every evening, if the wind is right, her voice comes down through the mountain's throat: thin and bright and slightly off-key, threading through the stone like water through chalk. She sits at the summit, she says, cross-legged on the flat rock where the old door used to be sealed, and she sings the binding-hum — except it isn't a binding anymore, hasn't been since I took the anchor, and she refuses to update the lyrics.

"It's traditional," she called down to me once, when I shouted up the shaft asking why she was still singing a prison-lullaby to a free mountain.

"It's *outdated*!"

"So are you! Sing anyway!" And she sang anyway, and the mountain hummed back, and I lay on the floor of the gate-chamber with my hands behind my head and listened to the two of them harmonize without either of them meaning to, and I thought: *this*. This is the thing I paid for without knowing its price. Not safety. Not victory. This — being the fixed point around which other people's voices arrange themselves, and finding that it doesn't feel like a cage at all.

It feels like being the hearth.

* * *

Here is the strange part, the part I still turn over at night like a coin I can't quite read: I remember everything.

The tithe-men and their brass needles. The courier dying in the ravine with snow in his memory that wasn't his. Maren's tea-table sigil. The wardens waking. Brannoc falling. Every mile, every bargain, every betrayal I committed to survive and every one I refused. It's all there, whole and sharp, and I've stopped flinching from most of it.

Except one thing.

I know *that* I paid. I know the shape of the transaction the way you know the shape of a room you slept in as a child. But when I reach for what I gave — my dearest memory of belonging, the price the court demanded for Sable's soul — I find only smooth stone. A wall where a door used to be. I know it was dearest. I can feel the absence ache, the way a tooth remembers the dentist. But the tooth is gone, and what's funny — what's genuinely, absurdly funny, in the way that makes you laugh alone in a vault at midnight — is that I don't miss it.

Whatever it was, I chose it. Chose it freely, eyes open, over wealth and power and every easy version of my life. And whatever it was, it bought this: a mountain full of going-home souls, a smuggler who visits on Thursdays owing a fishmonger, a deserter building herself from scratch, a girl singing outdated songs to a liberated god, and me, Edmund Eastwick, forgettable no longer, unforgettable in exactly one direction, tending shelves in the dark and calling the warm days Tuesday.

Belonging, I have learned, isn't something you inherit. It isn't the family that raised you or the mistress who trained you or the road that made you. It's something you choose, and keep choosing, and pay for, gladly, in the only currency that was ever real.

Tonight the gate-chamber is quiet. Hesk is humming. Odile's shelf stands empty and honored. Somewhere far above, Wren's voice threads down through the stone, and the mountain hums back, and I put my palm flat against the warm rock — my rock, my post, my chosen anchor-ground — and I feel it answer.

And then, from the top of the shaft, faint but unmistakable, comes a second voice joining Wren's. Lower. Rougher. Laughing between the notes.

Thursday came early this week.

Sable is singing, badly, on my mountain — and he's brought someone with him, because there are footsteps on the summit rock, plural, and Wren is shouting something down the shaft that I can't quite make out, except for one word, repeated, getting louder:

"Edmund! EDMUND! You'll never guess who's standing at the gate — and she says she owes *you*—"
