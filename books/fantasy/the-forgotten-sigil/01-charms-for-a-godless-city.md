## 1. Charms for a Godless City

There are three rules to selling outlawed charms in Vell, and I learned all of them the expensive way.

One: never sell to a drunk after midnight, because drunks talk, and what they talk about is you. Two: never sell anything that actually works too well, because a charm that cures a fever in an hour gets you noticed by people whose job is noticing. Three — and this is the one that keeps you breathing — help no one too much.

I was thinking about rule three the night everything went wrong, which is either irony or punishment, and in my experience the gods don't distinguish between the two. Not that they were doing much distinguishing of anything those days. Mine had been quiet for seven years, and the rest of them had gone silent right along with him, like a tavern going hush when the wrong man walks through the door.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. The market first. Markets are honest, at least about being dishonest.

* * *

The night market of Vell set up in the gutted shell of the old Temple Quarter, because there's a particular kind of joke the free cities love: build your black market on the bones of the thing you're not allowed to believe in anymore. Stalls of oiled canvas and scavenged pewter crowded between the broken colonnades where the pantheon's statues had been pulled down and dragged off to lime kilns. All except one. They'd never managed to move the great head of Ossuar, my god, the Keeper of Names — it weighed as much as a fishing boat and had rolled into a drainage channel during the pulling-down, and there it sat half-buried, face upturned at the sky, wearing the expression of a man who has just heard something disappointing and decided not to comment.

I'd been selling out of a blanket near his stone chin for six years. It felt less like blasphemy than company.

"Charms," I called, low, the way you're supposed to — loud enough for the timid, quiet enough for the patrol. "Charms against fever, against bad milk, against mothers-in-law. Charms for luck at cards, though I'll want a cut if you win big. Charms to make your sweetheart look twice at you, though no charm alive can make her stay."

A laugh from a fishwife. A coin from a dockhand who wanted the mother-in-law one, and I sold him the fever charm with a knot tied wrong on purpose, because rule two. The coins went into a pouch that never got heavy. That was the arrangement: enough to eat, enough for the room above the chandler's, never enough to be worth robbing or worth reporting. Poverty as camouflage. I'd gotten very good at it, the way a soldier gets good at sleeping standing up.

The charms themselves were mostly theater. Dried rowan berries in wax. Knotted red thread. Bits of river-glass I'd polished on my sleeve until they looked like they'd been wept by something holy. Ninety parts fraud to ten parts craft, and the craft was mostly knowing which lie each customer needed told.

Except the small ones. Except the ones I made at dawn, when I still thought nobody was watching, with my hands moving through patterns I hadn't chosen and couldn't fully explain. Those worked. Not miracles — nothing so vulgar. But a fever broke a day early. A rope held one hour past breaking. And every time one of those left my blanket, I felt it go out of me like warmth out of an open door, and I hated myself a little for selling it, and I did it anyway, because a man has to eat, and because some small stubborn part of me needed proof that the silence wasn't total. That somewhere under all that quiet, something of the old covenant still leaked through the cracks, and had chosen, of all its possible vessels, a washed-up mercenary selling snake oil in a graveyard market.

Pride, my old captain would have said. She'd have said it while sharpening something.

* * *

The witch-finders came through around the second bell, the way they always did, walking in pairs with their gray lanterns and their gray faces and their little brass scales for weighing sin.

Officially they were the Office of Purity and Public Health, which tells you everything about how Kaelen Voss likes his atrocities packaged. Unofficially everyone called them ash-men, because of what they left behind. They moved through the market without hurrying, and the market performed its nightly transformation: fortune-tellers became fruit-sellers, hedge-witches became grandmothers buying onions, and I became exactly what I appeared to be, a tired man with a blanket full of worthless trinkets, which is the best disguise in the world because nobody looks twice at failure.

They stopped at Maretta's stall. Maretta read palms, badly, and had survived seven years of purge by being so conspicuously terrible at it that even the ash-men concluded no real power would bother inhabiting her. Tonight, though, she'd made the mistake of telling a young clerk he'd come into money soon, and the clerk — clerks are the worst kind of customer, they write things down — had mentioned it to someone.

I watched it happen the way you watch weather. The taller finder turned her lantern on Maretta's hands, and the light did whatever their lights do — I'd never been close enough to see properly, and I intended to keep it that way — and then there was a conversation conducted entirely in the language of shoulders and pauses, and then two other finders came over, and Maretta's stall was being taken apart panel by panel with a thoroughness that had nothing to do with law and everything to do with theater. The audience needs to see the machinery sometimes. Fear doesn't maintain itself.

Maretta herself they marched out between them, gently, almost courteously, which was somehow worse than chains. She didn't look back at her stall. Smart woman. Looking back is how they learn your face well enough to dream about it.

The market exhaled and resumed. A man bought a luck charm from me with shaking hands and didn't meet my eyes, and I didn't meet his, and that mutual blindness is the closest thing Vell has to civic virtue these days.

I packed up early. Some nights the whole performance curdles, and you know if you stay one more hour you'll do something stupid, like sell a working charm to someone who needs it, or worse, say something true out loud. Rule three exists precisely for nights like this. Help no one too much. Because the ones you help get remembered, and the remembering goes both directions — the ash-men remember them, and interrogate them, and the interrogated remember you. Every kindness is a thread, and Voss's people spend their careers pulling threads.

That's what I told myself, anyway, walking home through streets that smelled of salt and tar and the lime kilns' sweet dead smell. That my coldness was discipline. That a man who helps no one too much helps everyone a little, forever, instead of helping one person completely and then hanging beside them.

It's amazing what a man can make himself believe before bed.

* * *

My rooms were above Halvard's chandlery on Rope Street, two flights of stairs that complained louder than Halvard did, and Halvard complained professionally. The room had a bed, a chair, a table scarred by forty years of candle burns, and a window that looked out — because the city has a sense of humor too — directly onto the buried face of Ossuar, or rather onto the slope where his forehead disappeared into the earth. Every night I lit my candle where he could see it. Every night I said the words.

Not prayers. Prayers ask for things. What I did was closer to bookkeeping.

I took the little tin from under the floorboard — inside it, a stub of white chalk, worn to the size of a thumbnail — and I knelt, and I drew the sigil on the floorboards. The old sign of the covenant, the one they'd scraped off doors and bridges and gravestones across nine kingdoms: a circle, open at the top, with a line descending into it like light falling into a cup. Then I spoke the First Tongue, or the four words of it I still carried, the way a drowning man carries water.

*"Ossu ven. Ossu keth. Ossu mara."*

Hear me. Hold me. Remember me.

Seven years, and no answer. Seven years of saying it anyway, because the alternative was admitting it was over, and a man can keep a ritual going long after the faith dies out from under it, the way a widow keeps setting two plates. I said the words. I waited the length of ten breaths, which is the traditional length, though tradition doesn't explain why ten, and nothing came. Nothing ever came. The candle flame stood perfectly straight, indifferent as a tax collector.

"Good talk," I told the ceiling, and got up, and blew out the chalk-dust, and put the tin away.

Here is the thing I didn't know yet, lying there in the dark listening to Halvard's dog dream audibly through the floor: the silence had a shape. Silence isn't nothing. Silence is a door held shut from the other side, and somewhere out in the world that night — I understand this now, though I understood nothing then — something small and mud-caked and terrified was running toward my door through the dark, carrying the sound of that door opening in his mouth like a stolen coin.

But that was later. That night there was only the routine, and the routine is a kind of armor, and I slept the way I always slept: lightly, with one hand near the knife, alone in the specific way that only men who used to be part of something are alone.

* * *

Morning came gray off the harbor, and I went down to argue with Halvard about the rent, which is our version of affection. He wanted coin; I gave him most of it and a charm against rats that was ninety percent theater but which he swore by, because the rats did seem to prefer his neighbor's warehouse, and who am I to correct a miracle? On the way out I bought yesterday's bread and today's gossip from the baker, and the gossip was the usual: another sanctuary burned up-country, another edict posted on the customs house, prices up, ships down, and the ash-men recruiting, which meant Voss was preparing to move on the free cities proper and needed more hands for the burning.

"They say he's coming here next," the baker said, in the tone people use for weather and doom, the tone that means *nothing will be done about it.*

"He says that about everywhere," I said. "Man's ambitious the way a fire is ambitious."

"You're not worried?"

"I'm a seller of dried berries and knotted string," I said. "I'm beneath concern. It's the one advantage of failure."

He laughed and gave me the heel of the loaf for free, which violated rule three in both directions, and I took it anyway, because bread is bread and hypocrisy keeps better than most virtues.

I spent the afternoon making stock for the night market — wax, thread, glass, lies — and it was while my hands were tying a knot they knew better than I did that I heard the bells.

Not the market bells. The deep ones, from the citadel, the ones they rang for edicts. Three strokes, pause, three strokes. Everyone in the street stopped at once, the whole city flinching in unison, and a crier climbed the customs house steps with a scroll and read out the new decree in the flat voice criers use so they can pretend it's not theirs:

By order of Lord Protector Kaelen Voss: any person harboring, concealing, or aiding practitioners of forbidden arts, or persons marked by such arts, shall be judged complicit. Complicity carries the same sentence as practice. Report your neighbors. Report your family. The Protector desires only your safety.

*Complicit.* There's a word built like a trap. It means they don't need to prove you did magic anymore. They only need to prove you failed to hate it loudly enough.

The street resumed, quieter. Somewhere a child was crying and was shushed fast, hard, the shush itself frightened. I stood in the doorway of the chandlery with a half-tied charm in my hands and thought, with the clarity that comes right before disaster, *this changes the arithmetic.* Because rule three had always assumed a rough balance: help a little, risk a little. But complicity meant the scale was broken now. Helping at all was the crime. Which meant every kindness I'd ever sold or given was already a ledger entry waiting for an auditor.

I should have run that night. I want that on the record, wherever records go when no one keeps them. I had coin enough for passage, contacts enough for a berth, and a face forgettable enough to start over in some port where they'd never heard of the covenant. Seven years of careful, cowardly survival had earned me exactly one skill: leaving.

Instead I went to the night market, because the routine is armor, and armor is the last thing a man clings to when he's already bleeding.

* * *

The market that night was half its usual size and twice its usual whisper. Sellers kept their goods under blankets until buyers proved themselves. Nobody laughed at anyone's jokes. Near the broken colonnade, somebody had chalked the old circle-and-line on a pillar — small, knee-high, easy to scrub off — and someone else had scrubbed it nearly away, and the ghost of it remained, which felt like the whole city in one image.

I sold three charms in two hours. To a sailor, a widow, and a boy of maybe twelve who counted out coppers for a fever charm with the solemnity of a man paying ransom, and I caught myself giving him the good knot, the working one, and stopped myself just in time, and gave him the ordinary one, and hated the relief I felt at my own stinginess.

Rule three. Say it until it sounds like wisdom.

The ash-men came at the fourth bell, and this time there were eight of them, and they weren't browsing. They moved down the rows in a line, lanterns swinging low over blankets and hands, and where the light lingered, sellers began packing. It was a sweep, casual as dredging a net, and I was already folding my cloth when the tall finder from the night before — the one who'd taken Maretta — stopped three stalls down and turned her lantern on a girl I knew slightly: Liss, the tallow-chandler's daughter, who sold love charms that were pure theater and knew it, and giggled when she said so.

The lantern light found her hands. I saw her face do something I'd seen once before, years ago, on a battlefield where a god should have answered and didn't: the look of a person realizing the story has stopped being the one they were told.

Liss ran. Of course she ran. Running is human. The finders followed, unhurried, because nothing runs faster than a lantern's reach in Voll's narrow lanes, and the market folded up around the absence like water closing over a stone.

I stood there with my bundle under my arm, halfway to the alley, perfectly positioned to leave. Perfectly positioned, in fact, to pass within ten feet of the lane where they'd cornered her, and to hear her crying, and to keep walking, because rule three, because complicity, because seven years of surviving had taught me the exact weight of a stranger's life versus my own.

I keep trying to tell this part honestly, and honestly is this: I calculated. I stood in the dark and did sums. Her life on one side, mine on the other, and the new edict tipping the scale, and underneath the sums a colder question I couldn't stop asking: *where was the god for Maretta? Where was he for the sanctuaries, for the burned, for my company in the pass? Why should I spend what he wouldn't?*

And then, from the lane, the girl made a sound — not a scream, something smaller, the sound of someone deciding to stop resisting — and my feet moved before the accounting closed. I'd like to tell you it was principle. It was older than principle. It was the four words I say to a silent ceiling every night, *hear me, hold me, remember me,* turning in my chest like a key in a lock I'd sworn was rusted shut.

What happened in that lane happened fast and ugly and I'll not dress it up: a thrown charm that flared brighter than any theater I've ever sold, a finder on his knees clawing at eyes full of light, the tall one whirling with her lantern rising — and me hauling Liss over a wall into the lime-yard dark while the light died behind us and boots scattered wrong ways on purpose, because I've fled professionals before and lived, and the trick is everyone flees differently.

We ran the long way, the way I'd mapped years ago for exactly this and hoped never to use. I shoved her through Halvard's cellar hatch, and Halvard — bless his greedy, complaining heart — asked no questions, because I paid him not to have any.

Then I climbed to my room, and my hands were shaking, and I knelt down and drew the circle and the falling line on the floorboards with a chalk stub worn to nothing, and I said the four words, and for the first time in seven years I didn't wait ten breaths. I waited one, and laughed at myself, and started to pack.

Because here's what the sums had finally shown me, in the only currency I trust: I'd broken rule three, and the sky hadn't fallen. The ash-men hadn't materialized at my window. For one whole hour I had helped someone too much, and I was still alive, and that fact frightened me more than the lanterns did — because if the rules were wrong, then the seven years of silence I'd built my life around might be wrong too, and a man can survive anything except discovering his wound was self-inflicted.

I was stuffing a spare shirt into a bag when I heard it: footsteps on the stairs. Small ones. Uneven, dragging, the gait of someone running on legs that had stopped agreeing to it hours ago.

Then knocking. Faint. Once, twice — and then a voice through the door, a child's voice, cracked with exhaustion, speaking four words in a language I had only ever said alone, to a ceiling, to a god who never answered.

*"Ossu ven. Ossu keth."*

Hear me. Hold me.

I stood very still, bag in hand, and listened to my own heart do something it hadn't done since the pass, since the silence, since the world ended politely and everyone pretended otherwise.

The knocking came again. And this time — faint, unmistakable, impossible — the chalk stub in my bag glowed through the canvas like a coal refusing to die.
