## 2. The Child at the Door

The knock came at the hour when even the honest people of Vell had stopped pretending to be honest, which meant it was either trouble or a customer, and in my experience those were usually the same thing.

I was counting the night's take on my workbench — eleven coppers and a button that someone had sworn was silver — when the knock came again. Not a knock, really. More of a collapse against wood, the sound a body makes when it has run out of arguments with gravity.

My rule is simple. I've kept it for seven years, polished it like one of my charms: help no one too much. A little help is commerce. A lot of help is a debt, and debts in Vell are collected by people who don't knock.

I opened the door anyway. Old habits. Old stupidities.

He was maybe nine years old, maybe younger — hard to tell under the mud, because he was wearing most of the riverbank. His feet were bare and split open at the heels, his shirt was somebody else's shirt once, and his eyes had that particular shine I'd seen before, on soldiers the night before a battle they weren't going to win. He looked up at me, opened his mouth, and said:

*"Veth-kara il. Veth-kara il, shen dor."*

I want to tell you I slammed the door. I want to tell you I was smart.

What I actually did was stand there like a man who'd heard his own name spoken by a dead friend, while a mud-caked child repeated words that hadn't been spoken aloud in this city — or any city — in seven years.

The First Tongue. The covenant-speech. The language my god used to use, back when my god used to do anything at all.

*Veth-kara il.* Help me carry it. That's the literal translation, if you can call anything about the First Tongue literal. It means more than *help me*. It means *help me carry what cannot be put down*, and it is the first sentence every priest ever taught a child of the old faith, because the whole religion, when you boiled away the incense and the architecture, came down to exactly that: nobody carries it alone.

Nobody had said it to me since the pass at Serel's Gap. Since the mountain where forty-one men prayed into a silence so complete you could hear your own blood giving up on you.

"Where did you hear that?" I asked, and my voice came out wrong, scraped thin.

The boy swayed. Then he fell forward into my doorway, into my arms, all elbows and river-mud and the smell of woodsmoke — not hearth smoke, burning smoke, the kind that clings to hair after a building goes down — and by the time I got him onto the floor inside, he was unconscious, breathing shallow but steady, one hand still curled around something in his fist.

I pried the fingers open. A wooden bead, plain, uncarved, the kind strung on prayer-cords in the old sanctuaries. Except this one had been split and rejoined, a hairline seam of gold running through it, mended the way you mend something you refuse to throw away.

I sat back on my heels and looked at him for a long time.

Here's what a sensible man does with a found child: nothing. You prop him up, point him toward the charity houses, and you go back to your charms and your coppers and your careful, small, survivable life. There are witch-finders in Vell now. They wear gray and they smile too easily and they pay informants in coin that doesn't jingle. A man with my inventory — outlawed charms hidden in false bottoms, blessing-sigils disguised as carpenter's marks — cannot afford a miracle sleeping on his floor.

I am, on balance, a sensible man. Ask anyone. Ask the empty chair across from me where no one sits, ask the second cup I never pour, ask seven years of nights spent talking to a god who stopped answering, alone, quietly, like a fool writing letters to a dead man.

I fetched water and a rag and started cleaning the mud off the boy's face, muttering the whole time about how this was practical, purely practical, couldn't have him choking on silt, and if anyone asked I'd say he forced his way in and collapsed and I was merely preventing a corpse on my property, which is bad for business.

His face under the grime was ordinary. Freckles. A healing cut above one eyebrow. Ears slightly too big for his head, the kind a boy grows into around twelve and hates until twenty. Nothing marked him as special. Miracles are disappointing that way; they almost never look the part.

Then, without waking, he spoke again.

*"Shen dor veth. Shen dor, il-ka."*

And here is the thing I have never told anyone, the thing I will deny if you repeat it: when he said it, the words didn't just mean what they meant. They *did* what they did. The candle flame leaned toward him. The water in my basin went still as glass. And somewhere behind my sternum, in the place where my faith used to live before it was repossessed, something turned over in its sleep.

Seven years of silence, and a child says two sentences in his sleep, and the room remembers how to listen.

"No," I told the boy, or the room, or myself. "No, absolutely not."

That's the trouble with prayers. Even unanswered ones leave a residue. You can scrape out the temple, board up the shrine, rename all the streets — but some part of you stays kneeling, waiting, and when something holy finally walks through your door covered in mud, that part stands up and says *finally* before the rest of you can vote.

* * *

I worked through what I knew while I waited for him to wake, because working is what I do instead of feeling.

Sanctuaries were still legal in Vell — barely, the way a lungful of air is legal underwater — but Kaelen Voss's edicts had been tightening for a year. First the registration of practitioners. Then the licensing. Then the burnings, which the criers called "purifications" right up until even the criers got tired of lying. The free cities were holding out, but Voss's witch-finders moved through them like dye through water, patient, diffuse, everywhere eventually.

A boy fleeing a burned sanctuary would come downstream. The river runs through everything in Vell; it's the city's oldest smuggler. And a boy who speaks the First Tongue — fluently, natively, the way you speak the language you dreamed in as an infant — isn't a practitioner. Practitioners learn scraps. This boy *was* the language, or it was him. Either way, he wasn't running from the witch-finders.

He was running *toward* something. Or being pulled. Covenant-logic, if the old stories held: the sigil broken, the gods silent, and somewhere a key looking for its lock.

I caught myself. Looked at my hands. Realized I'd been sketching the old sigil-shapes in spilled flour on the bench without noticing, the way a man whistles a song he hates.

"Help no one too much," I reminded the flour.

The flour, sensibly, said nothing.

* * *

He woke near dawn, all at once, the way hunted things wake — no groggy surfacing, just eyes open and body coiled, a wild animal checking exits. He saw me and went very still.

"It's all right," I said, which was a lie, and then, because lies sit poorly with me at that hour: "It's not all right. But you're not dead, and neither am I, and there's bread."

He watched me tear the loaf. He took his piece only after I bit mine first, which told me things about his last few weeks that I didn't want to know. He ate like a soldier — fast, thorough, guarding the rest with his forearm — and then he drank the water I gave him and looked at me with those too-old eyes and said, in trade-tongue, accented but clear:

"You understood me. Last night. Before."

"Some of it." I kept my voice level. "Where did you learn the old speech?"

He hesitated. I know that hesitation. It's the pause of a person deciding whether the truth is safe, and I've spent enough years among liars and mercenaries to recognize it as a kind of compliment — he was considering telling me.

"The sanctuary," he said. "Brother Aldous said it was dying. Said only old men remembered it. But I—" His hand went to his chest, to the split-and-mended bead, which he'd apparently retrieved from his fist while I wasn't watching. "I didn't learn it. I always knew it. Like knowing how to breathe."

"And the sanctuary?"

The fire came into his eyes then, but not anger. Worse. Memory. "They came at night. Gray cloaks. They read names from a list. Brother Aldous put me in the root cellar and told me to walk downstream and not stop walking, and—" His voice did the thing children's voices do when they're holding something enormous with a child's hands. "The singing stopped. That's how I knew. You could hear them singing from the road, always, even far away. And then I couldn't anymore."

I looked at my hands. Charmer's hands. Small magic, safe magic, the kind that gets a man ignored rather than burned. For seven years I had built a life precisely sized to fit through the gaps between the witch-finders' fingers, and every instinct I owned was screaming at me to fold this boy into a bundle of rags, point him at the river gate, and wash my hands of him before the gray cloaks added my name to their list.

"Veth-kara il," the boy said softly. Watching me. Testing, maybe. Or asking.

*Help me carry what cannot be put down.*

"Don't," I said. "Don't say that to me. Not that phrase. Not to anyone. Do you understand? If anyone hears you speaking that tongue—"

The knock on my door was polite. That's what made my blood go cold. Witch-finders don't kick doors in Vell; they don't need to. They knock politely, smile easily, and let the fear do the breaking for them.

Two knocks. A pause. Two more.

I put my finger to my lips. The boy — Sef, I'd learn later, though he didn't offer it then — went silent and still with a speed that confirmed everything about his last few weeks. I tipped the washbasin over beside the bench, dumped my dirty laundry behind it, and pointed at the gap beneath the floorboards where I keep the charms worth hanging for. He folded himself into it without a word. I scattered laundry over the boards, picked up my ledger, and opened the door with the expression of a man mildly annoyed at the hour.

There were three of them. Of course there were three; they travel in threes, one to question, one to watch, one to write. The front one was young, pleasant-faced, with the kind of smile you see on tax collectors and surgeons. Behind him, the watcher's eyes went past me into the room with the slow sweep of a man cataloguing.

"Master Nystrom." My name in his mouth sounded like evidence. "Forgive the hour. We're making inquiries. A child, missing from the riverside parishes. Nine, ten years. Mud on him, likely. Been seen?"

"A hundred children fit that bill in this city," I said. "Half of them are mine, morally speaking. Which one?"

The smile widened a fraction. "One that talks strange, perhaps. Old words."

"Old words." I scratched my jaw. "You mean like the fishwives swearing? Because yes, I've heard language that would curdle a saint, mostly from customers I've overcharged."

The writer's pen scratched. The watcher drifted half a step sideways, angling for a view past my shoulder, and I shifted with him, lazy as anything, a tired shopkeeper blocking his own doorway because the draft was cold. Under the floorboards, I could feel the silence below me like a held breath. The boy wasn't moving. Good. Whatever else he was, he'd learned to be quiet the hard way.

"We'll search the quarter regardless," the pleasant one said. "Standard practice. You understand."

"I understand you'll want tea, and I haven't any, and you'll be disappointed." I leaned on the doorframe. "Anything else? Only, some of us work for a living, even at hours civilized folk sleep."

They left. Eventually. Politely. The watcher looked back once from the end of the lane, and I met his look with the flat boredom of a man with nothing to hide, which is the hardest performance I give, and gave it well, and closed the door, and stood with my forehead against the wood for a long moment while my heart informed me, at length, of its opinion regarding my life choices.

* * *

I lifted the floorboard. The boy looked up at me from the dark, and I braced for gratitude, or tears, or whatever it is rescued children are supposed to produce.

Instead he said, quietly, in the First Tongue: *"Il-ka veth. Shen dor."*

*I carried it. What could not be put down.*

And then, in trade-tongue, watching my face with those too-old eyes: "Brother Aldous said someone would understand. He said the river would bring me to a door, and the door would be angry, but it would open."

I sat down on the floor across from him, because my legs had opinions about standing.

"I'm not angry," I lied.

"You're frightened," he said, with the terrible accuracy of children and drunks. "So am I. Brother Aldous said that's how you know it matters."

Outside, somewhere down the lane, a gray cloak knocked politely on another door. The candle flame leaned toward the boy again, just slightly, like a plant toward light. And in the seven-year silence behind my sternum, something that I had buried alive and told myself was dead turned over, stretched, and began — patiently, horribly, like all old duties — to wake.

I broke my rule that night. I've been breaking it ever since.

But that's getting ahead of the story.
