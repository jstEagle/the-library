## 3. What the Witch-Finders Burn

The first thing I remember is the smell.

Everyone thinks fire is the worst part of a burning building. It isn't. Fire is loud and bright and honest about what it wants. The smell is worse. The smell is what comes after — wet ash and burnt feathers and something underneath that you know is people even though your brain keeps trying to call it *bread* or *candles* or anything else, because your brain is a coward and would rather lie to you than let you know what you're breathing.

I was in the loft of the sanctuary when they came. Brother Anselm had put me up there to sort candle stubs, which was the job they gave me when they wanted me out from underfoot, which was most days. I didn't mind. Candle stubs don't ask questions. You melt them down, you pour them into molds, you make new candles out of old ones. Everything gets used again. That seemed like how the world was supposed to work.

Then the bells started wrong.

Sanctuary bells have a language. *Supper* is two slow rings. *Visitors* is three quick ones. *Flood* is everything at once, all night, until your ears bleed. What came through the windows that morning wasn't any of those. It was one ring, then nothing, then one ring, like the bell itself couldn't decide whether to bother screaming.

Brother Anselm's face when he pulled me off the loft ladder — I'd never seen a grown man look like that. Like a door opening onto a room he'd spent his whole life pretending wasn't there.

"Under the floor," he said. "The loose board by the altar. Don't come out no matter what you hear."

"What will I hear?"

He didn't answer, which was an answer.

* * *

Here is what I heard:

Boots. Many boots, but not marching — witch-finders don't march, they *arrive*, the way weather arrives. Then Father Colm's voice, very calm, saying words like *guests* and *welcome* and *of course*, the way you talk to a wolf that has gotten into the sheep pen and hasn't decided yet whether to be hungry.

Then a different voice. Dry. Patient. The kind of patient that makes your skin crawl because you understand, somehow, even at eight years old, that this person has all the time in the world and intends to use every second of it.

"We have a list, Father. Your name appears on several documents as having sheltered marked persons."

"Sheltered the sick," Father Colm said. "The poor. The usual sinners."

"The usual sinners." A pause, like the dry voice was tasting the words. "Yes. We'll begin with those."

I was under the floor with my knees against my chin and my hands over my mouth, and through the gap between the boards I could see the altar cloth hanging down like a curtain, and beyond it, feet. So many feet. And then feet that weren't walking — feet that were *dragging*, heels bumping on stone, and I knew whose those were because Sister Maret had a limp from the year the river flooded, and Sister Maret's limp had a sound, and now that sound was being made without her consent.

They burned the sanctuary at midday, which everyone agrees is obscene. Fires belong to the evening. Burning a holy house in daylight is like laughing during a funeral — technically possible, deeply wrong.

I got out through the root cellar because Brother Anselm had shown me that too, months ago, back when showing me escape routes still felt like a game. "You're the important one, Sef," he'd said then, ruffling my hair like I was a ledger he needed to keep safe. "Not because of anything you've done. Because of what you are. There's a difference, and someday someone will explain it badly."

He didn't come out of the cellar behind me. I waited by the well for as long as waiting was survivable. He didn't come.

I'm not going to describe the smoke. I've decided that. Some things you get to keep private even inside your own head.

* * *

After that there were roads. Roads are just the world's way of telling you *keep going* without offering a reason.

I learned things on the roads. I learned that if you walk behind carts, the drivers pretend not to see you, because charity is easier when it's deniable. I learned that dogs can smell fear but cats can smell *lies*, and a cat will look at you like it knows exactly which parts of your story are true, and honestly the cat is usually right. I learned that the proclamations nailed to every post had my description on them — not my name, they didn't have my name, but a drawing of a boy with dark hair and a birthmark on his left wrist shaped like a hook, and the word beneath it was *VESSEL*, and above it, smaller, the seal of Kaelen Voss, which looks like a sword standing upright in a book, which is supposed to mean order over knowledge but mostly means *we will cut whatever we need to cut*.

I learned the word *purge*. I'd known it before, the way you know the word *drown* without knowing water. Now I knew it the other way.

In Harrow's Ford they took the herb-woman who'd given me bread. I watched from the top of the mill hill. They stood her in the square and read from a paper, and she didn't cry, she just kept looking at her own hands like they belonged to somebody else, and then they did the thing with the oil and the post, and I ran, and I didn't stop running until dark, and I am not going to describe that either. My privacy is getting very crowded. Good. Let it be crowded.

The thing nobody tells you about watching people burn for magic is that afterward you start checking yourself. Every time my skin prickled. Every time a word rose up in my throat that wasn't Common — that old, rolling, heavy word-stuff that lived in me like a second heartbeat. Every time, I thought: *is this the thing? Is this what killed the herb-woman? Is this what made them drag Sister Maret by her heels?*

And here is the truly stupid part, the part I can laugh about now only because laughing is the other thing you do instead of screaming: I couldn't stop the words. They came anyway. In dreams, mostly. Big slow syllables that felt older than stones. I'd wake up with my mouth full of them, and I'd spit them out into the dark where nobody could hear, and I'd whisper to myself in plain Common, *my name is Sef, I am nobody, I am a candle-stub sorter, please forget me,* and the words would settle back down inside me like a dog told to lie down, obedient and miserable, waiting.

* * *

Vell was supposed to be safe. Everyone said so. Free city, they called it, like freedom was a thing you could put walls around.

By the time I reached Vell I had been walking for — I don't know. Weeks. Time went strange. Days were just the space between hiding places. I was so hungry I'd eaten candle wax, which I want noted somewhere official: the sanctuary trained me for nothing except eating its own inventory.

I asked after sanctuaries, refuges, anyone. People gave me directions the way you shoo a cat — quick gestures, no eye contact. One woman pressed a heel of bread into my hand and then looked around fast, like she'd committed a crime, which in Voss's territories she basically had. Bread as contraband. That's the world he built. That's the world he'd tell you he *saved*.

The charm-seller's door was the last one I tried because it was the last one there was, at the crooked end of a crooked lane, and by then I wasn't choosing anymore, I was just falling forward.

I remember his face when he opened it. Tired. Guarded. The face of a man who has answered the door at night enough times to know it's never good news. And I remember thinking, with the weird clear stupidity of the nearly dead: *he sells charms. Charms are little bits of magic. Magic is what gets people burned. This is either the safest door in the city or the most dangerous one, and I cannot tell which, and I cannot stand up anymore, so I suppose it doesn't matter.*

I tried to say *please help me.* What came out was the First Tongue.

It came out the way it always does — not chosen, more coughed up, a whole sentence of it, rolling and heavy and old, and I watched the man's face change like ice cracking on a pond. Not fear. That's the thing I want written down correctly, whoever ends up keeping records of this, if anyone does: he wasn't afraid. He looked like a man hearing a voice he'd stopped believing in.

Then the floor came up and hit me, and the world went out like a pinched candle.

* * *

Waking up is its own small horror these days. Every time I open my eyes I have to run the check: where am I, whose roof, am I tied, is this a cell, is this a pyre with extra steps. So when I woke on a narrow bed in a room that smelled of wax and dust and old smoke, I lay perfectly still and did the inventory before I let myself breathe loud.

One window, shuttered. One door, closed but not locked — I could tell because the latch hadn't been dropped; there's a sound a dropped latch makes even from inside, a kind of finality. One man, sitting across the room in a chair, not touching me, not looming, just *there*, the way you sit with a frightened animal you don't want to spook.

"You've been out a day," he said. "Drink this. Slowly."

Watered wine. I drank it like it might be a trick, because everything might be a trick, and he watched me do it and didn't say anything clever, which I appreciated. Clever people scare me. Cleverness is what witch-finders are made of.

"I'm going to ask you some questions," he said. "You don't have to answer. But I'd take it kindly if you'd stop answering in a language that got four hundred people burned in the last province over."

My stomach dropped through the bed, through the floor, through the world. "You understood it?"

"Some." He rubbed his jaw. "Enough. Where did you learn it?"

"Didn't." The truth came out before caution could catch it. "It's just *in* me. It's been in me since — forever. Since always. The brothers at the sanctuary said—"

"Sanctuary." His eyes changed. "Which one?"

"Greyfall. Saint Verene's." I watched his face while I said it, and I saw him hear the past tense in my mouth, and I saw him understand. Word travels fast when the news is ash. "It burned. Eleven days ago. Maybe twelve. I lost count."

"Anyone else out?"

I shook my head, and then, because lying to him felt suddenly impossible and also because my chest was full of broken glass that needed somewhere to go: "Brother Anselm hid me. Under the floor. He said don't come out no matter what I heard." My voice did something embarrassing. "I heard plenty."

The man — Noor, I knew that much, the whole lane knew that much, *the charm-man at the crooked end* — looked at me for a long moment. And then he did the thing that undid me more thoroughly than any kindness had managed in eleven days of running.

He didn't say *I'm sorry.* Sorry is what adults hand children like a coin, and it spends for nothing. He just nodded, once, slow, like a man acknowledging a debt, and said: "Then he bought you time, and you spent it getting here. That's not nothing. That's the whole game, actually. Staying alive long enough to matter."

"That's a horrible way to comfort someone."

"Yeah." Almost a smile. "It's the only kind I stock."

* * *

He asked questions for a long time, gentle ones, circling, the way you'd circle a fire you weren't sure was banked. Who else spoke the tongue around me. Did the brothers mark me. Did anyone ever write the words down. Did I dream them.

That last one I answered honestly, because I was tired and because some animal part of me had already decided he was safe, which history suggests is a decision I make far too easily.

"All the time. Big ones. Old ones. Words that feel like—" I hunted for it. "Like the ground talking. Like before people, when the ground still bothered to speak up."

"And when you say them," he said, very carefully, "what happens?"

"Nothing. That's the joke. All that burning, all that hunting, and when I say them, nothing happens. They just sit in the air like fat rain. Useless." I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "Maybe the witch-finders have the right idea and I'm just a defective—"

"Give me your hand."

I flinched. Of course I flinched. Hands reaching for me lately had meant ropes and posts and oil. But his hand stayed open in the air between us, palm up, patient, asking instead of taking, and after a while — a long while, long enough that he must have counted a hundred reasons to withdraw it and refused every one — I put mine in it.

His grip was warm and rough, a working man's hand, a scarred one. And he said a word.

Not in Common. In the First Tongue. Low, roughened, like a blade that had been left out in weather — but real, real the way bread is real when you've been eating wax, and the effect was instant and enormous. Every old word sleeping in my chest woke at once. They rose up my throat like water finding its level, and where his skin touched mine they *rang* — I don't know how else to say it. The air between our hands went taut as a plucked string. The candle flame leaned toward us. The shutter rattled though there was no wind.

And Noor — the weary, guarded, help-no-one-too-much charm-man of the crooked lane — went white as salt and whispered, in Common, barely voiced:

"It answers. Seven years of nothing, and it *answers*."

"What does?" My voice came out small. "Answers what?"

He didn't let go of my hand. Outside, somewhere down the lane, boots passed — the arrhythmic, arriving tread of a patrol — and we both went silent, two statues holding one live wire between us, until the boots faded toward the market square.

Then he looked at me, and I saw that whatever calm he'd been wearing since I woke was gone, burned away, and underneath it was something rawer and older and much more frightened, and he said:

"Kid, I need to tell you a story about a god who stopped speaking. And then you need to tell me why, out of every door in Vell, you fell through mine."

* * *

I should have slept. Instead I lay on the narrow bed listening to him move around downstairs — the small honest noises of a man banking his fire and counting his stock and deciding, you could hear it in every clink and creak, deciding something enormous.

Because I'd felt it too, when our hands touched. Underneath the ringing, underneath the waking of the old words, there had been a pull. Faint. Directional. Like a fisherman feeling the line go tight.

It pointed down. Through the floor, through the earth, south and west, toward water.

Toward something drowned.

And deep under my breastbone, in the place where the First Tongue lives, a word I had never spoken and never been taught rose up and sat on my tongue like a bird on a wire, patient, certain, waiting for morning:

*hazzerath.*

Later I would learn what it means. Later I would wish, briefly, that I hadn't.

But that night I only knew that it meant *coming*, and that it was not me doing the coming, and that outside the shuttered window the patrol boots had stopped — right there, at the corner of the crooked lane, where they had no reason to stop at all.
