## 4. The Inquisitor Who Quit

*Maren*

The dead have excellent posture.

That was the first thing I learned when I faked my drowning in the Gray Strait three years ago: if you want to pass as a corpse, you can't slouch. Corpses don't apologize. Corpses don't flinch when a witch-finder's bell rings too close. Corpses certainly don't cry, which is why I did all my crying underwater, where the salt could take the blame.

I'd been Maren Solde then, Senior Inquisitor of the Ninth Bell, and I had a reputation for thoroughness that my superiors mistook for devotion. It wasn't devotion. It was the slow, grinding horror of a woman who kept expecting to find a limit — some line Voss's office would not ask her to cross — and kept finding instead that the line moved whenever she got near it. Burn the hedge-witch. Burn the midwife who knew a charm for fever. Burn, in the end, the children, because children born to covenant-bloodlines carried "residual contamination," and residual contamination, said the doctrine, was a seed that grew.

I stood in a courtyard in Karst with a torch in my hand and a boy of six chained to a post, and he asked me — this is the part I don't tell anyone — he asked me if it would be quick.

And I looked at the torch, and at the boy, and at Captain Rask's face beside mine, smooth and certain as an egg, and I understood that there was no line. There had never been a line. The line was a story we told ourselves so we could sleep.

So I put out the torch in the rain barrel, reported a sudden illness, walked to the harbor, and drowned Maren Solde with great ceremony off the end of a piloting skiff. My boots came ashore two days later. Very convincing boots. I'd weighted them myself.

The woman who climbed out of the water on the far side of the strait had no name, no rank, and a face altered by a smuggler's knife and a month of healing salves that scar more than they smooth. She had one skill set: she knew exactly how witch-finders think, because she had been one, and she knew exactly how they hunted, because she had taught half of them.

Which is why, on a gray morning in Vell, I stood across the street from a charm-seller's tenement and watched a man come out his door with a child hidden under his coat, and thought: *well. There you are.*

* * *

*Noor*

The boy slept like the dead, which after last night felt less like a figure of speech than a professional courtesy between them.

I'd managed three hours myself, sitting upright in the chair by the door with a boning knife in my lap and every ward-charm I owned — the real ones, not the market trash — hung around the room like laundry. Sef had talked in his sleep. Not words I knew. Words I knew *of*. The First Tongue has a sound like water deciding to be a blade, and hearing it come out of a mud-caked child in his dreams made the hair on my arms stand up and file complaints.

Here was my problem, laid out plainly: I had broken my rule. Help no one too much. Seven years of scraping by on that rule, and in one night I'd taken in a fugitive speaking the language of my silent god, lied to a witch-finder's face about it, and now I couldn't decide whether to feed him breakfast or throw him out the window before whatever was coming for him arrived.

The window option was losing. He'd eaten the heel of bread I left out like it was a religious experience, and something about the way he'd said thank you — in trade-tongue, clumsy, but he'd tried — had lodged under my ribs like a fishhook.

"Stay here," I told him, shrugging into my coat. "Don't open the door. Don't look out the window. If anyone knocks, you're a very boring piece of furniture."

He nodded solemnly, the way children do when they understand maybe a third of what you've said and have decided to trust you with the rest. That trust sat badly on me. Trust usually does.

I went out to work the morning stalls, because a man who suddenly stops selling charms is a man witch-finders remember, and I needed coin for passage besides. I told myself that was the reason. Coin for passage. Two tickets' worth, eventually, once I figured out where exactly one flees to with a vessel-key of the old covenant and a conscience that had started making noise again after seven quiet years.

The market was wrong that morning. I felt it before I saw it: the vendors too cheerful, the beggars gone from their corners, a smell of wet ash that had nothing to do with any chimney. In Vell, absence is a warning. When the people who usually haunt a place stop haunting it, they know something you don't.

I sold six charms in an hour, all fake, all blessed with the same mumbled nonsense, and I was counting coppers into my tin when the bells started.

Not the harbor bells. The *finding* bells — three notes, repeated, the rhythm witch-finders used to flush a district. I'd heard them in other cities, from the outside, always from the outside, always thinking *there but for the silence of god go I.*

They were ringing four streets over. Then three.

Then, distinctly, they were ringing outside my own door, and I was still six streets away from it.

* * *

*Maren*

Let me tell you how a raid works, since I designed half the protocols myself and it seems a shame for the knowledge to die with my alias.

You ring the bells in a pattern that tells your teams the target block while telling everyone else to clear out. You seal the ends of the street first — that's the mistake amateurs make, going in the front door while the quarry strolls out the back. You bring a sniffer, a handler with a leashed scent-hound bred on burnt sigil-ash, because magic leaves a residue like smoke in wool. And you bring a reader, someone trained to feel the pull of living covenant-work, because hounds can be fooled but the pull cannot.

Vell's garrison had lent Voss's men a reader named Cott. I'd trained Cott. He was competent, ambitious, and color-blind in the way that mattered: he saw the world in shades of guilty. He would find the child. He would find the charm-seller harboring the child. And both of them would burn, because that was the protocol, and protocols are just cruelty with good handwriting.

I'd spent two days establishing the pattern of the charm-seller's life. Noor Nystrom, former mercenary, minor priors, no guild ties, sells outlawed trinkets of the harmless sort — luck-knots, fever-marks, things that worked through suggestion and copper. A survivor. Survivors keep their heads down. Which meant the child in his house was either leverage or charity, and men like him didn't do charity.

Except he'd done it. I'd watched him carry the boy in under his coat like contraband, watched him stand in the doorway afterward for a long moment with an expression I recognized because I'd worn it in a courtyard in Karst: the face of a man discovering he has a line after all, and hates himself for being surprised.

So. Professional assessment: Noor Nystrom was harboring a covenant-vessel, the highest-priority category of heretic in the Nine Edicts, and the correct move — the move that kept a dead woman dead — was to walk away and let Cott and the hounds do their work.

I gave that assessment the consideration it deserved, which is to say I was already moving before I finished having it.

* * *

*Noor*

I ran the last two streets like a man younger and stupider, which is to say like myself at twenty. The bells were everywhere now, folding the neighborhood in layers of sound, and ahead I could see them: gray cloaks, the white hand-and-flame stitched at the shoulder, a hound straining at its leash with its nose full of my stairwell.

My stairwell. My door. Behind which a child was pretending to be furniture.

There's a moment in every mercenary's life — usually around the time the paymaster skips town — when he realizes that everything he built his survival on was borrowed. My rule had been borrowed. Help no one too much. It had kept me alive the way a shallow grave keeps a body safe: technically, and only until someone comes looking.

I put my hand on the door and the bells stopped, which was worse. Silence meant they were inside the radius. Silence meant the hound had the scent and the reader had the pull and the only thing left between Sef and a pyre was a locked door and a liar's tongue.

Then a woman stepped out of the alley beside my building and rang a bell of her own.

It was a finder's bell — I knew the shape, the dull bronze, the way it cut through noise without seeming loud. Three notes, but wrong. Off-pattern. Every gray cloak on my street turned toward it like dogs hearing a whistle, and the woman — tall, scarred along the jaw, moving with the particular economy of someone who has never once wasted a motion — shouted in the clipped cant of the Ninth Bell: *"Counter-pull! Reader reports a second signature, east lane, moving!"*

Cott — I'd learn his name later, and enjoy learning it considerably less — snapped orders, and half the raid peeled away east toward nothing. The hound handler hesitated. The woman strode up to him, flashed a seal I couldn't read at that distance but clearly could, and said something low and vicious that made him go pale and follow his colleagues.

Then she turned, looked directly at me standing frozen at my own door like a burglar caught in the act of living there, and said, conversationally:

"Well? Are you going to invite me in, or shall we all stand here admiring the architecture?"

* * *

*Maren*

Inside, the charm-seller did the sensible thing, which was to put a knife between us and ask questions in the tone of a man who has already decided the answers won't help.

"Who are you."

"A friend," I said.

"I've had friends. They cost less and leave sooner."

"Good. Then you'll appreciate that I'm neither." I let my hood back and gave him the face the knife had made, scars and all. "Three years ago there was an inquisitor named Maren Solde who drowned in the Gray Strait. Her body was never recovered. Her boots were. I'm told they washed up very convincingly."

His eyes did the arithmetic. Mercenaries are fast at that particular sum — it's threat assessment dressed as curiosity. "You're her."

"I'm nobody. That's rather the point." I looked past him. The boy stood in the shadow of the stair, one hand on the rail, watching me with the flat, ancient caution of a child who has learned that adults arrive in categories and most categories end in fire. Something in my chest, which I had believed thoroughly decommissioned, shifted its weight. "Hello, Sef."

He flinched at the name. Of course he did. Names were death warrants now; Voss's edicts had made a sacrament of a child's name spoken aloud.

"How do you know that name?" Nystrom said, and the knife came up a fraction.

"Because I read the manifest of the sanctuary at Hollowfen before it burned. Forty-one souls listed for evacuation. Thirty-nine accounted for." I kept my voice level, because the alternative was the other thing, the thing under the level voice, the thing that had made me drown myself rather than carry it further. "Two unaccounted. One was the vessel-key. I've spent three years assuming the second was ash in a brazier somewhere, and feeling appropriately damned about it. Imagine my surprise."

"You quit," Nystrom said slowly. "You didn't just quit. You—"

"Faked my death, yes. It's remarkably easy. The hard part is the eulogy. Mine was given by a man I despise, and he cried, which ruined it entirely." I turned back to the boy and crouched, putting my hands where he could see them, palms out, the old habit of a woman who had spent years making children afraid of her and had run out of excuses for it. "Sef. I'm not going to hurt you. I burned my uniform rather than burn you. That's the whole of my credentials, and I know they're thin, but they're true."

He studied me the way children study weather — for signs, for the shift before the storm. Then he said something in the First Tongue, soft, questioning.

And I'll admit what happened next, because a report should be honest even when its author isn't: the words went through me like cold water through cloth. I hadn't heard the First Tongue spoken since seminary, since the days when the pantheon still answered and the covenant still held and nobody had yet decided that the answer to grief was arson. It sounded the way my mother's lullabies had sounded, if my mother had been a cathedral.

"He asked," Nystrom translated quietly, "if I trust you."

"And what did you tell him?"

"That I was deciding." He lowered the knife, which from a mercenary is roughly equivalent to a signed affidavit. "Why us? Why now? You've been dead three years. You could stay dead. Walking into a raid to save strangers is a poor way to keep it that way."

It was a fair question and deserved better than the answer I gave, which was the true one anyway.

"Because I stood in a courtyard once with a torch," I said, "and a boy asked me if it would be quick, and I have spent every day since paying interest on that debt. You're not strangers. You're the payment plan."

* * *

*Noor*

I've fought beside priests, liars, and one man who claimed to be both, and I know the sound a person makes when they've decided something irreversible. Maren Solde had that sound. Underneath the dry delivery and the corpse's discipline, she was a woman standing on a bridge she'd already burned, waving at the far shore.

I respected it. I also noted, professionally, that burned bridges tend to draw pursuers.

She confirmed it within the hour, spreading a stolen duty roster on my table like a general planning a war that consisted entirely of retreat. "Cott will realize the east lane was empty within the day. He'll retrace the bell signal — it was off-pattern, I made it off-pattern deliberately, but he's not stupid, only ambitious, and ambition notices anomalies. By tomorrow night he'll have your description, this address, and a warrant with your name spelled correctly, which is more courtesy than Voss's clerks usually extend."

"So we run tonight."

"We run tonight." She tapped the map. "But running needs a door, and every gate in Vell has finder-posts since the Edict. You don't have papers. I'm dead. The boy is—" she glanced at Sef, who was solemnly eating the rest of my bread and listening to every word with the terrifying completeness of children, "—the most wanted object in nine provinces. We need a smuggler."

"I know a name," I said, and hated how easily it came. "Odile Ferran. Runs salt and secrets out of the Tidewrack chapel. If half the stories are true, she still keeps rites the rest of the city forgot."

Maren raised an eyebrow. "A priestess-smuggler. How convenient."

"In my experience the convenient things are the ones that cost most. She'll want payment."

"What do you have?"

I looked at my tin of coppers, my coat of fake charms, my seven years of careful nothing. Then at the boy, who had finished the bread and was watching us with those eyes that held, faintly, at the edges, a light like water deciding to be a blade.

"Everything I've got," I said. "Apparently that's the rate now."

* * *

*Maren*

We went out through the coal-yard behind the tenement, because front doors are for people with futures. Nystrom carried the boy. I carried the rear and the arithmetic of pursuit: Cott would find the anomaly by dusk, the warrant by midnight, and the coal-yard by dawn at the latest. We had hours. Hours are a currency that spends faster than it earns.

Halfway down the yard, Sef tugged Nystrom's collar and spoke — one word of the First Tongue, soft as breath — and every rat in the coal-yard went still, and then, in perfect silence, left. All of them. At once. Like a congregation dismissed.

Nystrom stared after them. "Did you teach him that?"

"No," I said, and my mouth had gone dry. "Nobody teaches that. That's the land answering him." I had spent my career reading reports of covenant-work, cataloguing miracles as crimes, reducing wonder to evidence. Watching a child dismiss the rats of Vell with a word, I finally understood what I'd actually been burning all those years, and understood it too late, the way one does.

We reached the canal path as the bells began again — three notes, corrected, on-pattern, Cott's voice implicit in their rhythm like a signature on a confession. Behind us, in the direction of the tenement, a glow was starting that had nothing to do with lamps.

"They're burning it," Nystrom said flatly. "My building. There's nothing left to find and they're burning it anyway."

"It's a message," I said. "For anyone who helped you. Voss believes in messages." I had drafted some of those messages myself, in another life, in another woman's handwriting. The ash smell followed us down the canal, patient as a debt.

Ahead, past the tanneries, the Tidewrack chapel leaned over black water on stilts, its bell-less tower hung with fishing floats instead of a bell, and light moved behind its salt-crusted windows — not lamp-light. Candle-light, dozens of candles, arranged in patterns I recognized from a seminary education I'd spent three years pretending to forget.

Nystrom saw it too. His step hitched, just slightly, the hitch of a man passing a church he thought had closed forever and finding it lit.

"Odile Ferran," I said. "Let's hope your convenient things are worth their price."

He knocked — a complicated rhythm, trader's code — and the door opened on candlelight and the smell of old incense, and a voice from inside, wry and unhurried, said:

"Well. A dead inquisitor, a godless mercenary, and the covenant's own key on my doorstep before supper. Come in, then. The tide's turning, and so is everything else."
