## 6. Seven Years of Silence

There is a particular kind of quiet that comes after a battle ends and you are the only one left to hear it.

I had heard it before, in small doses — the hush after a skirmish, the pause between volleys. But I had never heard it the way I heard it at Karst Pass, on the morning after the last night. It was not the absence of noise. It was the absence of answer.

We had gone into those mountains because a god asked us to.

I want to be precise about that, because seven years of telling this story to no one has taught me how easily it curdles into something else — a soldier's grudge, a drunk's lament, a madman's testimony. So: a god asked us to. Not a priest interpreting a god, not a king claiming divine sanction, not a prophet with a book and a collection plate. The Ashen Shepherd himself, in his own voice, in my own head, the way he had spoken to every sworn blade of our company since the covenant was whole.

*Hold the pass,* he said. *The sigil frays at its northern anchor. Hold it while I mend what is broken.*

And Dainna Vael, captain of the Company of the Gray Standard, knelt in the snow outside the shrine at Highmoor and said, "Then we hold it."

Forty-one of us went up the mountain. I was twenty-six years old and I believed — gods, I *believed* — the way only a man who has heard an actual god speak can believe. Belief like that isn't faith. Faith is a wager made in the dark. What I had was knowledge. I had heard the Shepherd's voice the way you hear your own heartbeat: constant, interior, undeniable. When he spoke, the world rearranged itself around his words. Stone felt steadier. Cold felt purposeful. Even grief, when it came, felt like it had somewhere to go.

That is what he took from me, in the end. Not my comrades. Not my hand or my youth or my luck. He took the architecture of certainty, and he left the rooms standing empty, and I have been living in them ever since.

* * *

You should know what we were, before the mountain. The Company of the Gray Standard was old — older than most kingdoms, though we never said so out loud, because companies that brag about their age tend to get hired for their funerals. We were sworn blades in the old manner: each of us bound by oath and sigil-work to one of the nine who remained of the pantheon. Ours was the Ashen Shepherd, keeper of thresholds, walker of borders, the god you prayed to when you stood between something and the something it must not reach.

He was not a grand god. That was the joke among us, and we meant it fondly. The Shepherd kept flocks, not thrones. His shrines were waystones and doorposts, bridges and gates. Farmers left him wool and bread. Soldiers left him their fear, which he took whether they offered it or not. And in return, when you held a line — any line, a riverbank, a doorway, a pass — he held it with you. Every mercenary company in the world wanted a Shepherd-sworn contract, and there were only ever three companies worth the name that had one.

We were the last.

Dainna used to say the Shepherd chose soldiers the way a shepherd chooses dogs: not for beauty, but for the refusal to leave. She'd been his voice-bearer for nineteen years. When he spoke through her, her voice came out layered, hers and something vast underneath it, like a bell rung inside a drum. I can still hear it. I will always still hear it. That is the cruelty of memory — it keeps what you would give anything to lose and drops what you would give anything to keep.

The night before we marched, she found me sharpening my sword by the cookfire, which is what I did instead of sleeping, then as now.

"You're frightened," she said.

"I'm practical," I said. "It looks similar from the outside."

She sat down across from me. Firelight did things to her face that made her look both older and younger than she was, the way fire does. "Noor. Do you know why the Shepherd picked you? Out of all of them."

"Because I'm cheap."

"Because you listen." She tapped her own temple. "Most people, when the god speaks, they hear the words and stop there. You hear what's under the words. That's rare. That's going to matter."

"What's under the words?"

She looked into the fire for a long moment. "I don't know yet," she said. "But something's wrong with him lately. He speaks slower. Like a man talking through a wall." She shook it off, smiled, threw a pebble at me. "Get some sleep, Marchetti. Tomorrow we're heroes."

Tomorrow we were forty-one people walking into a story someone else had written.

* * *

Karst Pass is a throat of stone between the northern peaks, the only road through the range for sixty leagues either side. In the old days it had been pilgrim country — wayshrines every mile, the Shepherd's crook carved above every lintel. By the time we got there, the shrines were already dark. We told ourselves it was weather. Sigil-fraying, the god had said. Storms in the covenant. Things break; things get mended; hold the line while the mending happens.

For six days, nothing came.

That was the first wrongness, and I want you to understand that we noticed it. Soldiers notice. An empty pass where an army should be is louder than an army. On the fourth day, Dainna climbed to the high shelf and prayed, properly prayed, the full rite, and came back down with her face carefully arranged into calm.

"He says the enemy is delayed," she reported. "He says hold."

Bren Aldous, our sergeant, spat into the snow. "Delayed by what?"

"That's not for us to ask."

"It's exactly for us to ask. We're the ones bleeding if the answer's bad."

"Bren—"

"No, Captain. With respect. Something's off. Six days, no scouts, no supply trains, no smoke from the far valleys. Nobody fights a war through a pass without feeding an army through it first. Either the war's a lie, or—" He stopped. He didn't finish it. None of us finished it, because the thing he wasn't saying was unsayable: *or the god is lying.* You might as well accuse the sun of forgery.

On the sixth night, they came.

Not an army. That was the second wrongness, and it took until dawn to understand it. What came through the pass in the dark was a mob — thousands of them, but unarmed, half of them barefoot, driven from behind by riders with whips and torches. Refugees, we thought at first. Then the front ranks saw us on the barricade and began to scream, and the ones behind pushed forward, and the ones behind *them* were not refugees at all but soldiers dressed as refugees, and the torches started fires in the dry pine on both slopes, and the whole throat of the pass became a furnace with forty-one of us standing at the hinge of it.

They weren't trying to take the pass. They were trying to fill it.

I have fought men who wanted to kill me, and I have fought men who wanted to win, and I have fought men who simply enjoyed it. I had never fought men whose orders were to die loudly enough to draw a god's attention. Because that's what it was — I understood it by the third hour, cutting through bodies that didn't fight back, screaming at civilians to run while our own lines dissolved — the whole assault was a signal fire. Someone had built a pyre of human beings and lit it in the Shepherd's own shrine-country, and whatever the god was doing behind that wall of silence, mending or not mending, he could not fail to hear it.

Unless he was already gone.

* * *

Here is the part I have never said aloud, to anyone, and I am saying it now only because a child spoke the First Tongue on my doorstep and broke something loose in me that I had bricked over with work and wit and cheap charms.

At midnight, at the worst of it, with Bren dead beside me and the barricade burning and maybe nine of us left standing, I heard the Shepherd's voice.

Not through Dainna. Through *me*. Directly, for the first and last time, and it was not the voice I knew. It was thin. Strained. A great man whispering through clenched teeth. And what he said was not *hold*, and it was not *be strong*, and it was not any comfort at all.

He said: *Run.*

One word. And then — silence. Not the silence of a god choosing not to speak. The silence of a door being shut from the other side. Of a cord cut.

I stood in the burning pass with a word in my skull that I have spent seven years deciding how to hate, and I did not run. I want that recorded. Whatever else is true, that is true: I did not run. I went back for Dainna, who was pinned under the barricade timber with her legs broken, and she grabbed my wrist with a grip that had no business surviving in that body, and she said:

"He's still speaking to you. Noor — if he's still speaking, then he's still *there*. Find out why he stopped. Promise me."

And I promised her, because you promise dying captains things, and then the roof of the world came down — the riders had cut the snowpack above the pass, whether by chance or design I never learned — and I woke three days later in a shepherd's hut thirty miles south, alone, with frostbitten fingers and a silence in my head where a god had lived.

Forty-one went up the mountain. One came down.

* * *

The years after that were a long exercise in not thinking. I drank in Corvane for a season. I took contracts that should have killed me and resented them for failing. I sold my sword, then my armor, then pieces of my oath-sigils to collectors who paid well for authentic divine work, which is how I learned that everything I was could be priced, and none of it was worth much.

Somewhere in there I stopped praying. Not gradually — that's the lie comfortable people tell. All at once, like a man stepping off a ledge. I remember the exact moment. Third winter after the pass, some shrine festival in Vell, bells ringing for gods who answered, and I stood in the crowd with my fists closed and thought: *you asked us to hold. We held. Forty-one of us held until holding was ash, and where were you? Where were you when the snow came down?*

And the silence answered, the way it always answers, with nothing, and I walked out of that crowd and never knelt again.

But here is the wound under the wound, the one I only admit at the bottom of bad nights: I don't actually believe he abandoned us. That's the terrible part. Dainna's last words live in me like a splinter — *if he's still speaking, he's still there* — and everything I've seen since, every outlawed charm that works, every rite that half-functions, every rumor of the covenant fraying, fits one story better than the easy one. The easy story is betrayal. The true story, I think, is worse. I think something happened to him. I think the Shepherd — wounded, throttled, *cut off* — spent his last strength buying one man an escape he never asked for, and I have spent seven years cursing a god for dying, and calling it abandonment so I wouldn't have to call it grief.

Because if he didn't abandon us — if he was taken, or broken, or silenced by hands rather than choice — then the pass wasn't a betrayal. It was a murder scene. And I wasn't the survivor of a god's failure.

I was the witness.

And witnesses, unlike survivors, owe a debt. They owe the truth. They owe it to the dead, who cannot collect it themselves, and to the living, who do not want to hear it, and most of all to themselves, who have built seven years of charm-stalls and cynicism on top of the grave of it.

I owed a debt I had refused to pay since the snow came down at Karst Pass.

Until a mud-caked boy fell through my door speaking words I had heard once before, at midnight, in a burning pass — the Shepherd's own cadence, the covenant's own grammar, alive in a child's mouth like a coal that refused to go out.

I had spent seven years telling myself the silence was an ending.

Sef's voice, cracked and terrified and unmistakably *his god's*, sounded a great deal like a beginning.
