## 7. The Usurper's Edict

I have been called many things in the last seven years — usurper, tyrant, the Gray Hand, the God-Butcher, and once, memorably, by a dying hedge-witch whose cottage I had ordered burned with her inside it, "a man who mistakes arithmetic for a soul."

That last one has stayed with me. Not because it stung, but because it was nearly accurate, and accuracy is rarer than flattery.

My name is Kaelen Voss. I was a physician before I was anything else, and I want that understood, because everything else follows from it like symptoms from a disease. I did not come to power hungry for thrones. I came to power the way one comes to a diagnosis: reluctantly, methodically, and with the full knowledge that the treatment would be worse than pleasant for the patient.

The patient being, of course, the world.

* * *

Let me tell you about the Gray Fever, since everyone else tells it wrong.

Twenty-two years ago, it took my city of Corvane in eleven months. It began in the temple districts — always the temple districts, though the chroniclers prefer to begin elsewhere — and it spread along the ley-roads where charms were sold and blessings were traded like fish at market. The afflicted did not simply die. They *unmade*. Skin went gray as ash. Voices failed first, then memory, then the sense of one's own edges. I watched a woman spend her final week staring at her hands as if they belonged to a stranger, mouthing a name she could no longer remember. Her own, presumably. Or perhaps a god's. By the end, the distinction seemed not to matter to her.

I was thirty years old and I kept the ledgers. That is what people forget about plague work: it is mostly bookkeeping. I recorded every death, every district, every variable I could think of — water sources, grain shipments, burial practices, rat populations, the phases of the moon because the priests insisted and I had grown tired of arguing.

And when I finished, the numbers said one thing, plainly, in the language numbers speak, which is the only language that has never lied to me:

The fever followed the magic. Not the temples — the *magic*. Districts with active charm-sellers suffered four times the mortality of districts without. The great working done at the Cathedral of Nine Springs, meant to cleanse the city, preceded the worst month of all. The more the mages prayed, the faster the gray spread through their congregations like rot through wet cloth.

I brought this to the Archmagister himself. A kind old man, genuinely kind, with dust on his sleeves and starlight in his eyes and the particular serenity of someone who has never once checked his own work. He listened to my figures with the patience one grants a clever child, and then he said the sentence that made me what I am.

"The covenant is not subject to your ledgers, physician. Some things must be taken on faith."

Some things must be taken on faith.

Eleven months later, the fever had taken one in three of Corvane's souls, including my wife, who had never cast so much as a cantrip, and my daughter, who was six and believed — this is true, I have verified the memory a hundred times — that the little charm I wore for luck was what kept wolves out of our garden.

There were no wolves in Corvane. There had never been wolves in Corvane. She knew this, and loved the charm anyway, and died gray and voiceless while the Archmagister's acolytes sang over the city and made everything worse.

So yes. Call me a tyrant. But understand that my tyranny began as grief wearing the mask of arithmetic, and that I have never once removed the mask, because underneath it there is nothing left that could bear the light.

* * *

The war council met at dawn in the command tent outside Harrowgate, which is to say that nine men sat around a table arguing about things I had decided three weeks ago, while I drank tea and let them feel important. Command is largely theater. The generals need to believe their counsel shapes events; the soldiers need to believe their generals are wise; and I need all of them believing something other than the truth, which is that I have already weighed every life at this table against every life beyond it, and found the sums satisfactory.

"Vell will not surrender," General Ostrava said. He is a bull of a man with a butcher's forearms and a surprising gift for understatement. "The free cities never do. They'll dig in behind the harbor chain and make us pay for every street."

"They will," I agreed. "And we will pay it. Have the engineers double the siege-tower timber. We take Vell by autumn or we bury our dead trying."

"And the witch-finders?" This from Prelate Sarn, who manages the Order with the fussy devotion of a man alphabetizing a library he intends to burn. "Their reports from the city are troubling. Charms moving openly again in the night markets. And there is a matter they've flagged — a child."

"A child," I repeated.

Sarn consulted a paper with the reverence lesser men reserve for scripture. "Found speaking the First Tongue. Or so the local finder claims. Most likely a street performer's trick, some parroted syllables—"

"The First Tongue is not parroted." I set down my cup. Around the table, the generals went quiet in the way soldiers do when the physician's voice changes. "It cannot be learned from hearing it, Sarn. The scholars tried for centuries. The words reshape the mouth that speaks them; you cannot mimic a key by whittling a stick. If a child in Vell spoke it, the child didn't learn it. The child *was spoken through*."

"You believe the old stories, then." Ostrava, carefully neutral. "The vessel-keys. The living sigil."

"I believe in evidence, General, and the evidence is that the old covenant was real, functioned for two thousand years, and was severed seven years ago — precisely seven, if the tremors in the ley-roads are to be trusted, and they are the only instruments I have found that don't lie. Something cut it. Something with access to the deep shrines." I let that sit. "And now a child surfaces in a free city, speaking the language of the thing that was cut. I do not require faith to find that pattern interesting."

Sarn shifted. "The finders recommend burning, as with any vessel. Containment through destruction. It is the standing edict—"

"The standing edict applies to practitioners, Prelate, who choose their craft. A child does not choose to be a door." I rose, and the tent rose with me. "We are not butchers. I want that understood down to the last corporal. Whatever the boy is, he is a symptom, not the disease, and I do not treat patients by setting them on fire."

A pause. Ostrava's mouth twitched — he has known me long enough to hear the joke I am not making.

"The child is to be taken alive," I continued. "Unharmed. Gently, if gentleness is possible; efficiently if it is not. He is to be brought to me personally, fed, warmed, and frightened as little as his circumstances allow. Am I understood?"

"Understood," Sarn said, in the tone of a man writing *alive* into an order he suspects will be misread anyway.

Good. Let him suspect. Half of command is knowing which orders will be disobeyed and arranging matters so that the disobedience lands where you want it.

* * *

That evening I walked the camp alone, as I do most evenings. The men pretend not to watch me and fail utterly. They call me the Gray Hand behind my back — not for the fever, though the fever haunts the name, but for the gauntlet I wear on my right hand, the one recovered from the drowned shrine at Cael Morath before the waters closed over it forever.

It is a piece of the sigil. Cut steel and older metal, cold at any temperature, and it hums sometimes at night, very faintly, like a struck bell that cannot finish ringing. My physicians — the ones who treat *me*, the ones who know better than to record their findings — say the hand beneath it has gone numb these seven years. I tell them that is the price of holding a tool. Tools cost the hand that holds them. Everything costs the hand that holds it. That is the whole of ethics, reduced to a surgeon's maxim.

Seven years ago, the covenant broke. Seven years ago, the mercenary companies holding the passes for their silent gods died to the last man, waiting for miracles that had been amputated at the source. Seven years ago, I stood in the flooded dark beneath Cael Morath with a blade forged from ledger-iron — iron smelted from the chains of plague-dead, because symbolism matters even to rationalists — and I severed the cord that bound gods to land to mortal tongue.

I have told no one this. The histories, when they are written, will say the sigil rotted on its own, or that the gods abandoned us, or whatever version keeps the peace. But I will set it down here, in this private record, because I promised myself one honesty: the account books of Kaelen Voss balance.

I cut the cord because the covenant was killing us. Slowly, invisibly, in the manner of all things taken on faith — but killing us nonetheless. Every prayer siphoned something from the pray-er; every blessing drew interest; every miracle was a loan against human life, called in during plagues and famines when the collateral ran shortest. The Gray Fever was not an accident. It was a *collection*. The gods — patient, loving, magnificent — had built a system in which our suffering paid their splendor, and no one had ever audited them.

No one but a physician with nothing left to lose.

Was I certain? No. Certainty is for priests. I was *probable*, overwhelmingly probable, with confidence intervals wide enough to drive a funeral procession through. And I have lived seven years with the possibility — small, unkillable, shaped exactly like my daughter's face — that I am wrong. That I did not amputate a gangrenous limb but a healthy one. That the silence in the world is not convalescence but exsanguination.

This is why the child matters. If the First Tongue lives in him, then the covenant was not destroyed but *severed* — and severed things can be rejoined, by the hand that cut them or another. I must know which world I live in before I march on Vell. A general can fight a city. Only a physician can fight a diagnosis.

* * *

Near midnight, the report came from the witch-finders' forward post, carried by a rider whose horse had foam at its bit and terror in its saddle-sweat.

The finder in Vell — a thorough creature named Rask, whose cruelty I tolerate because it is efficient and whose reports I read twice because they are honest — wrote that the child had vanished. Taken in by someone. A charm-seller, possibly; Rask suspected a former mercenary named Nystrom, one of the Cael Morath company, sole survivor, a seller of outlawed trinkets with a reputation for helping no one too much.

Helping no one too much. I almost smiled. I know the type. I invented the type. It is what a man becomes when he has learned that every kindness is a debt and every debt gets collected.

But here is the passage I read three times, and which I copy here verbatim, because my private record demands precision:

*"The boy speaks words that answer touch. Nystrom laid a hand on him and the warding-charms on my own kit went dark — every one, all at once, like snuffed candles. I have burned forty vessels and never seen the craft die in the presence of its own kind. Recommend immediate escalation. Recommend the Order send no one less than an Inquisitor. Recommend, with respect, that the Lord Protector consider whether the child is a weapon aimed at him — or a message."*

A message.

Yes, Rask. That is precisely what it is. The question — the only question that has kept me awake these seven years, the question I severed heaven itself to escape — is *from whom, and saying what.*

If the covenant reaches back toward the world through a child's mouth, then perhaps I was wrong. Perhaps the gods were not parasites but partners, and the fever was a wound in the system rather than the system's purpose, and my blade fell on the healer instead of the sickness. In that case the child is a pardon, and I should kneel to him, and I will, gladly, publicly, with the same precision that I have done everything else.

Or the covenant reaches back the way infection reaches back — along the cleanest channel, through the weakest host, wearing a child's face because nothing disarms a garrison like a child. In that case he is the disease returning, and I will hold him down and cut it out again, and I will hate every second of it, and I will do it anyway.

Either way, I must have him alive. Either way, the truth sits in a mud-caked boy in a smuggler's city, waiting for me like a diagnosis waiting for the scalpel.

I sealed the order by candlelight: the host marches on Vell at first light. The child Sef — even the name has the ring of the old tongue in it, *sef*, root-word for *threshold*, according to the one scholar I trust — is to be found, protected, and delivered. Alive. Unharmed. *Gently.*

And beneath the seal, in my own hand, one line for whoever finally holds him:

*"Tell the boy the Gray Hand does not want his fear. Tell him I only want to ask him a question. Tell him to answer honestly, and no harm will come to anyone he loves. And then, Inquisitor — whoever you are — watch his face when you say it. Because if the covenant truly lives in him, he will already know whether I am lying."*

I blew out the candle. On my right hand, under the gauntlet, the numb flesh hummed — once, softly, like a bell remembering it used to ring.

Somewhere south, past the gray strait, a child slept in a stranger's house, dreaming in a language older than my grief.

March, I thought. Before it learns to say anything I cannot undo.
