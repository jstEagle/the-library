## 8. Salt and Old Words

The sea, I have decided, is a liar.

It looks like a floor from the docks of Vell — all flat and blue and harmless, with gulls walking on it like it owes them nothing. Then you get on it, and it turns out the sea is not a floor at all. It is an enormous animal that has agreed, for now, to let you stand on its back, and every hour or so it remembers this arrangement annoys it.

"Stop gripping the rail like that," Odile said, "or your hands will rust before the rest of you does."

I let go. Mostly.

Her ship was called the *Merciful Lie*, which Noor said was the most honest name for anything in Vell. She had three masts, a hold full of smuggled relics that were technically illegal in four cities, and a figurehead of a woman whose face had been worn smooth by salt until she looked like she was laughing about something she'd never tell you. The crew called her the Lady. They touched her mast when they passed it, quick and secret, the way people touch charms they don't believe in anymore but can't quite throw away.

I liked the *Merciful Lie* immediately, because she lied beautifully.

"You're staring again," Odile said. She was at the stern rail beside me, drinking something out of a tin cup that I was fairly sure wasn't tea. "Stare less. Sailors get superstitious about children who stare."

"What do they think I'm doing?"

"Marking them," she said. "For the deep. Old habit from before the Silence. Children who stared too long got blamed for bad weather." She snorted. "Which is ridiculous, obviously."

She looked at me over the rim of her cup.

"Obviously," I said.

"So." She set the cup down on the rail, careful, like it was a relic too. "Noor tells me you've been talking in your sleep."

My stomach did something complicated. "Has he been listening?"

"He's been sleeping two feet away from you and he has ears, boy, so yes, everyone's been listening. You talk in a language none of us know, and then the lamp flames lean toward you while you do it." She said it lightly, the way she seemed to say everything, but her eyes weren't light at all. "That's not nothing, Sef. That's the opposite of nothing."

"I don't mean to."

"I know." She pushed off the rail and stood in front of me, blocking my view of the lying sea. "Which is exactly why we're going to fix it. Not stop it — fix it. There's a difference, and the difference is whether you drown us all."

"That's very reassuring."

"I'm a smuggler and a priestess of a silenced pantheon. Reassurance is not among my sacraments." She jerked her chin toward the hatch. "Come. Lessons."

* * *

The lessons happened in the hold, because Odile said the First Tongue shouldn't be practiced under open sky until I could be trusted with it, and also because the hold was where she kept her illegal gods.

There were eleven of them. Small figures, no bigger than my hand, carved from driftwood and bone and one that might have been whale tooth, arranged on a shelf that ran the length of the hold behind a curtain that only opened for her. Nine of the faces had been chiseled flat by witch-finders' hammers generations ago. Two still had faces: a woman with her hands cupped like she was holding water, and a tall thin figure whose head was a door.

"The Lady of the Shallows," Odile said, tapping the cupped-hands one, "and the Doorkeeper. Everyone else got their faces taken, but their bodies stayed, because even Voss's grandfather's men couldn't bring themselves to burn them entirely. Superstition," she added, with a certain approval. "It keeps us honest."

"They're really silent?" I asked. "All of them?"

"Seven years, give or take. Before that, they whispered. After, nothing." She shrugged, and the shrug cost her something; I saw it land in her shoulders. "A priestess without a god is just a woman with opinions and good posture. But." She held up one finger. "Silence isn't death. A man underwater is silent too. Doesn't mean he's gone. Means you've got to learn to listen different."

She sat down on a crate across from me, knees apart, forearms on her thighs, the way Noor sat when he was about to ask me something hard.

"Now. Say what you say in your sleep."

"I don't know what I say in my sleep. That's the whole problem."

"Then say the word you said on the dock at Vell. The one that made the fish jump."

I knew that one. It had come out of me whole, the way sneezes come, when a witch-finder grabbed Noor's collar and I'd been so scared that the word arrived in my mouth already shaped. I hadn't known what it meant. I still didn't, exactly. I knew it the way you know a stone you've swallowed — by its weight, not its shape.

I said it.

Every lantern flame in the hold leaned toward me. Not much. An inch, maybe. Like flowers finding sun through a window. The two faced idols on the shelf seemed, for half a heartbeat, to be listening.

Odile didn't flinch. I found out later that she absolutely had flinched, internally, and had simply decided years ago that her face belonged to her and not to her feelings.

"Good," she said. "Again. Slower."

"It hurts a little," I admitted. "Like holding my breath."

"Because you're holding your breath. You're clenching around the word instead of letting it pass through you." She made a gesture with both hands, scooping and releasing. "The First Tongue isn't a weapon, whatever the witch-finders think, and it isn't a prayer, whatever my colleagues think. It's a *covenant*. A bargain spoken out loud. You're not commanding the fire, boy. You're asking it, politely, in the language it was born in, to remember what it wants. And things remember what they want very quickly, once asked properly. So you'd best be polite, and you'd best be sure, because the world takes requests from you seriously now."

"Why me?"

"Ah." She smiled, and it was the saddest smile I'd ever seen on a grown-up. "That's the question, isn't it. The one Noor won't ask out loud because he's afraid of the answer, and Maren won't ask because she thinks questions are traps, and I won't ask because I already suspect it." She tapped my chest, once, gently, right over the sternum. "Something in there is a key, Sef. Keys don't get to choose what doors they open. But they do get to choose who holds them."

We practiced for hours. Small words. The word for *still*, which made the swaying lanterns hang motionless and made my nose bleed a little. The word for *warm*, which made the cold iron stove glow cherry-red until Odile yelped a much less sacred word and told me to take it back, and I discovered I could — that words given could be words gathered back, if you moved gently. The word for *listen*, which did nothing visible at all, but afterward Odile sat very quietly for a long time and wouldn't tell me why, except that her grandmother's voice had sounded in her ear clear as bells, singing a lullaby, and her grandmother had been dead nine years.

I apologized. She said never apologize for that, ever, and then she went up on deck alone for a while.

That night I dreamed in the First Tongue, and in the dream the words weren't sounds. They were roads. And something vast was standing far down all the roads at once, in every direction, waiting for someone to walk toward it.

* * *

The storm came on the fourth day, out of a sky that had promised nothing all morning.

Maren spotted it first — she spotted everything first, being the kind of person who treated relaxation as a tactical error. She came down the companionway and said, "Squall line, west, moving fast," in the exact tone she used for everything, as though weather were a suspect she intended to interrogate.

By the time it hit us, it wasn't a squall. It was a wall.

I had never seen rain fall sideways before. I had never seen a wave taller than the rail. The *Merciful Lie* climbed one such wave and hung at the top for a moment that lasted approximately my entire life, and I saw the whole gray world from up there — the trough below like a valley, the next wave coming like a mountain range — and then we fell, and the world became noise.

The crew shouted. Odile shouted back, and her shouting had structure to it, orders wrapped in curses wrapped in prayers. Maren appeared beside me with a rope in her fists and tied me to the base of the mainmast with three knots so fast I didn't see any of them happen. Noor was suddenly there too, soaked black, his hand clamped on my shoulder, shouting into my ear:

"Stay tied! Whatever happens, stay tied!"

And then the boom swung.

I heard it before I saw it — a crack like the world's largest bone breaking — and a sailor named Piet was simply *under it*, folded wrong against the deck, and the ship groaned and lay over on her side with green water pouring across the rails, and somewhere above us the mainmast made a sound like a decision being made badly.

They couldn't hear each other anymore. The wind had eaten all the words.

I don't remember deciding. That's the part I keep turning over, later, when I can't sleep. I don't remember choosing. The rope was cutting my wrists, Noor was hauling at Piet, the sea was coming down like the sky had declared war, and a word rose in me the way the water rose in the ship — from underneath, from somewhere with no bottom.

I spoke.

Not the small words. Not *still*, not *warm*. Something older, something with more road in it, and I felt it leave me like blood leaving a wound, and —

The storm stopped.

Not ended. Storms end; they blow themselves out over hours. This *stopped*. The way a dog stops when a whistle it was born knowing cuts through the dark. One moment the wind was trying to peel the ocean off the world, and the next there was a circle of quiet around the *Merciful Lie* maybe fifty feet wide, rain hanging in it like beads on a string, waves lying down flat as a chapel floor. Beyond the circle, the storm raged on, enormous and furious, circling us like something baffled.

In the silence, everyone turned to look at me.

Piet was breathing. Someone was already binding his arm. The mast had stopped making its bad decision. Rain hung in the air around our little island of calm, each drop lit silver by the lightning that kept flashing outside the circle and never, ever entered it.

"Sef," Noor said. Very quietly. In all that quiet, I heard him perfectly. "What did you say?"

"I don't know." My voice came out small and cracked. "I said *stop*? I said — I said something older than stop. I don't know the word for what I said."

Odile came toward me through the beaded rain, and I braced myself, because I had seen what people looked like when they decided what I was. The witch-finder in the sanctuary had looked at me exactly one second before the burning started, and it was a particular look, and Odile was wearing a version of it.

But when she reached me, she put her hand flat on my chest, over the place she'd tapped in the hold, and she laughed. It was a shaky laugh, a laugh with a crack down the middle of it, but it was a laugh.

"Well," she said, to the sky, to the sea, to the nine faceless gods in her hold below. "*He hears us after all.*"

"Who?" Maren demanded. She had her knife out, which was somehow both alarming and completely typical. "Who hears us? Ferran, what did the child just do?"

"He told the storm to mind its manners," Odile said. "In the language storms dream in." She looked at me, and her eyes were wet, and I honestly could not tell if it was the rain. "Seven years of silence, and the first thing the old powers do when someone finally speaks to them properly is *obey*. Do you understand what that means, boy? Do you have any idea—"

"No," I said. "I don't. And I'm scared of it."

The honesty dropped into our circle of calm like a stone into a well. Odile's hand stilled on my chest. Noor crouched down in front of me, water running off his chin, and studied my face with the expression he wore when he was deciding whether to break his own rules.

"Scared how?" he asked.

"Like the words aren't mine." I wrapped my arms around my knees, rope and all. "Like I'm a door, and things walk through me, and someday something big is going to come through and I'll just be — a doorway. Standing there. Empty."

Nobody laughed. That was the worst part. Nobody said *don't be silly*.

Instead Odile knelt beside me, indifferent to the water soaking through her coat, and said, "Then we make sure you're a door with a good lock, and better hinges, and people on your side of it worth opening for." She glanced up at Noor. "Both of you. He needs practice, real practice, and he needs it before the Gray Strait, because if he can do *that*—" she pointed at the circling storm, "—then every toll-keeper, every informer, and every one of Voss's witch-finders between here and the drowned shrine is going to feel him coming like a bell ringing. We are no longer smuggling a child. We are smuggling a cathedral."

Outside our circle of calm, the storm howled, patient now, like it was waiting to see if the lesson would stick.

And beneath my feet, through the deck, through the hull, through forty fathoms of cold green water, I felt it again — the thing from my dream, standing far down every road at once. Closer than before. Not menacing. Worse than menacing.

*Waiting.*

Noor followed my gaze down, as if he could feel it too, and his face went gray in a way that had nothing to do with seasickness.

"How long to the Strait?" he asked Odile.

"Two days," she said. "If the weather holds."

The weather, hanging in silver beads around our ship, held its breath.
