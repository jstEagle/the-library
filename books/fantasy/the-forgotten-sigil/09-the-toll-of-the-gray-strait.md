## 9. The Toll of the Gray Strait

There are three ways to cross the Gray Strait, and two of them involve dying.

The first is the ferry out of Saltmere, which is honest work run by honest men who will sell you to Voss's witch-finders for the price of a good dinner and a better lie. The second is Odile's cousin's boat, which sank last spring and took her cousin with it, so that option is mostly theoretical now. The third is the toll-bridge at Graymarch, a mile of black stone spanning the strait's narrowest throat, where the water runs gray as old pewter and the toll-keeper decides who lives based on how much he likes your face.

I have never had a face people like.

"Cheer up," Odile said, walking beside me on the cliff road down to the bridge. "Worst case, he takes the boy and we drown ourselves in the strait out of grief."

"That's not cheering me up."

"You're welcome."

Sef walked between us with his hood up, which made him look less like a child and more like a small suspicious adult, which was somehow worse. Maren brought up the rear, and I noticed she'd been quiet since dawn — not her usual quiet, which was the quiet of a woman conserving words like coins, but a different kind. A woman rehearsing something.

The bridge appeared out of the morning fog all at once, the way bad news does. Black stone arches marching across the water, and at the near end a squat tower flying no flag at all, because the toll-keeper of Graymarch recognized no authority but his own, which in my experience is the most dangerous kind there is.

He came out to meet us before we reached the gate. Big man, gray-bearded, wearing chainmail under a fisherman's oilskin like he expected trouble from both directions — the land and the sea. Behind him, four crossbowmen on the tower wall, casual about it, the way professionals are casual.

"Four travelers," he said. His voice carried over the wind without effort. "One child. That's an interesting arithmetic you're doing."

"We're paying the toll," Odile said brightly. "Coin. The traditional kind. You know, metal discs, kings' faces, the whole romance of it."

"The toll changed." He didn't look at her. He looked at Sef, and my hand found my knife hilt without asking my permission. "New edict, three weeks back. Every party crossing the strait surrenders any unregistered magic-bearers. Children especially." He smiled, and it was the worst thing I'd seen all week, and I'd seen Odile eat a pickled squid. "There's a bounty. Paid in advance, if the goods are undamaged."

"Goods," Maren said quietly.

"Goods," he agreed.

Now, here's what I knew about the toll-keeper of Graymarch, because I make it my business to know things: his name was Harl Vossen — no relation to the usurper, though he'd have traded on it if he could — and fifteen years ago he'd been a soldier in the border wars, and soldiers who set themselves up as toll-keepers are men who learned one lesson above all others: never fight when you can be paid instead.

Which meant the play wasn't force. The play was price.

"Name your number," I said. "For passage. All four of us, no questions, no edicts. Whatever the bounty pays, I'll double it."

Harl looked at me the way a butcher looks at a lamb that thinks it can negotiate. "You'll double it. With what? I've seen your purse, friend. It jingles like a beggar's cup." He jerked his chin at Sef. "The bounty on an unregistered vessel-key is more coin than you've held in your life. And it's paid whether the child walks or is carried. So here's my generous offer: walk away, keep your limbs, and let the professionals do their work."

*Vessel-key.* So they knew what Sef was. Not just a runaway from a burned sanctuary — they knew the word, the category, the *value*. Which meant the order had come down from someone who understood what Sef was too, and that thought sat in my stomach like a swallowed stone.

"Noor," Odile murmured. "Tell me you have a plan."

"I have a knife."

"That's not a plan, that's a mood."

And then Maren stepped forward, and everything changed.

She moved differently. I'd watched this woman for weeks — watched her slouch, watched her drink, watched her perform the shabby disgraced sellsword so well I'd almost believed it myself. But now she straightened, and squared her shoulders, and put her hands loose at her sides in a stance I recognized from every witch-finder I'd ever feared, and when she spoke, her voice wasn't Maren Solde's at all. It was colder. Older. Carved from some stone I didn't want to touch.

"Inquisitor Second Rank Solde," she said. "Order of the Ashen Veil. Warrant number nine-forty-one, countersigned under Magister Corven's seal. You'll find my name in the registry at Vell, at Saltmere, and yes—" she smiled, thin as a razor, "—at Graymarch, because your predecessor took Order coin for twenty years and liked it."

The crossbowmen shifted. Harl's smile died by degrees, like a fire going out.

"The edict," Maren continued, "exempts accredited officers of the Order and their *escorted assets*. Read your own orders, toll-keeper. Or shall I read them to you? Slowly? In front of your men?"

"I heard the Order was finished," Harl said. "Purged. Voss burned the registry at Vell himself."

"He burned a *copy*," Maren said, without blinking, without breathing, without a single tremor, and I realized with something like awe that she had done this before — stood in front of armed men and lied to them with her whole spine. "The master registry travels with the Magister's field office. Which is currently mobile. Which is currently *me*." She produced something from inside her coat — a badge, black iron, stamped with the ash-veil sigil. I'd seen it before, tucked away, never displayed. Now she held it up like a sun. "So. Are you going to obstruct an officer of the Order in the execution of her warrant? Because that's a hanging offense, and I'd hate to see a man of your... entrepreneurial talents waste his potential on a rope."

Silence. Wind off the strait. Somewhere below us, the gray water chewed at the bridge pilings.

Harl looked at the badge. Looked at her face. Looked at Sef, and I watched him do the arithmetic — bounty versus rope, coin versus corpse — and I saw the moment the numbers came out wrong for him.

"Passage is paid," he said finally, grudging as gravel. "All four. Move along."

"Wise," Maren said. Then, as we passed him, she leaned close and said something too low for me to catch, and Harl went pale under his beard, and I decided I was happier not knowing.

* * *

We were a hundred paces onto the bridge before anyone spoke.

"What did you say to him?" Odile asked.

"Nothing kind."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you're getting." But Maren's voice had lost its edge already; it was fraying at the seams, and I could hear what was underneath it. We walked on. The strait churned beneath the gaps in the stonework, gray and endless, and halfway across Sef tugged my sleeve and pointed down.

Fish, maybe. Or the shapes the water makes when it wants to look like something else. Hard to say. On the Gray Strait, I've learned, it's best not to ask.

At the far end of the bridge, Maren stopped. Stopped dead, in the middle of the road, and stood there looking back the way we'd come — back at the tower, the crossbowmen, the whole gray span of it — and I watched her shoulders start to shake.

"Maren?"

"Burn it," she said. Her voice cracked clean through. "Burn the warrant. Burn the rank. Nine-forty-one." She laughed, and it was the worst laugh I've ever heard, wet and hollow, a laugh with a corpse inside it. "Do you know how long I was Second Rank Solde? Eleven years. Eleven years I wore that badge, and today is the last day it works, because the moment Harl reports an inquisitor crossed his bridge, every witch-finder between here and the coast starts hunting a ghost they buried two years ago." She turned to face us, and her eyes were red-rimmed and furious and alive. "I just spent my old self. Spent it like coin. And it bought us a hundred paces of bridge and a head start, and that's *it*. There's no going back and being her again. Ever."

Nobody said anything. What was there to say?

So I did the only thing I could think of. I took off my coat — the good one, the one with the hidden pockets, the one I'd been sleeping in for three years — and I put it around her shoulders, because the wind off the strait was cold and she was shaking.

"You'll freeze," she said.

"I've got another one."

"You don't."

"No," I agreed. "But I've got you, and apparently you're worth more than coats."

Odile snorted. Sef, solemn as a little judge, nodded once, as if I'd passed some test I hadn't known I was taking. And Maren — Maren pulled the coat tighter around herself and looked out at the gray water for a long time, and when she spoke again her voice was steady, or close enough.

"When I faked my death," she said, "I told myself it was temporary. That someday the Order would be clean again, and I'd come back, and Second Rank Solde would rise from the grave with her honor intact. Stupid. I know it's stupid. But a person needs a story about who they're going to be." She looked at me then, and there was something in her face I couldn't read — or could, and didn't want to. "What's yours, Noor? When this is over. Who are you going to be?"

And I opened my mouth to give her the easy answer, the mercenary answer, *rich and retired and drunk somewhere warm*, and found that I couldn't. Because the truth was I hadn't let myself have a story since the pass. Since the silence. Men who've been abandoned by their gods don't plan endings; they just keep walking until the walking stops.

"I don't know yet," I said. It was the most honest thing I'd said in seven years.

Maren studied me for a long moment. "Well," she said at last. "When you figure it out, let me know. I'm collecting."

We walked on. Behind us, faint in the fog, I could have sworn I heard the toll-tower bell ring once — Harl sending word down the coast, as Maren had predicted, hunting the ghost she'd just spent. Ahead of us, the road climbed into hills I didn't know, toward a shrine I'd sworn never to see again, carrying a child who spoke in the voice of gods.

And that night, making cold camp in a shepherd's ruin with Sef asleep against Odile's side and Maren keeping first watch, I caught myself doing something I hadn't done since the pass: I caught myself praying. Not the empty ritual, not the silence-prayer I'd performed every night like a man talking to a grave. This was different. This was a question, aimed at whatever might still be listening:

*If he gives everything, what's left of him?*

Because I'd seen what Maren had paid at the bridge — a name, a rank, eleven years of self, spent in a hundred paces — and I'd understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the shrine would ask the same of me. More. That somewhere ahead of us was a price tag with my name on it, written in a language I used to speak.

The god, of course, said nothing. Gods are good at that.

But the wind through the ruined roof made a sound almost like an answer, and Sef stirred in his sleep and murmured a word in the First Tongue — soft, half-dreamed, and though I couldn't translate it, I swear by every saint I don't believe in that it sounded like *soon.*

In the morning, we'd see smoke rising from the valley road behind us. Riders. Many riders, moving fast, and cutting off the only path to the sea.

Rask had found us after all.
