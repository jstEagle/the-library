## 10. The Traitor's Hand

I have killed men for less than what Rask said to me on the deck of the *Salt Vow*, but I have never regretted killing them, and I knew — standing there with the wind pulling at my collar and his smile sitting on his face like a borrowed hat — that if I killed him I would carry it. Not because he didn't deserve it. Because he'd have made me do it in front of Sef.

That was the kind of man Rask had always been. He never brought a knife to a conversation until he was sure you'd remember the conversation longer than the knife.

"Solde." He said my name the way you'd set down a heavy trunk. "You look terrible."

"You look promoted," I said.

He laughed. That was the worst part. Seven years we'd worked together, back when I still believed the witch-finders were a scalpel and not a pyre, and he still laughed like we were two guardsmen sharing a flask behind the granary. He hadn't changed. That was the horror of him. I had to become someone else to survive what I'd seen; Rask had simply kept going, the way water keeps going through a crack, widening it, patient, unbothered.

"I heard you died," he said. "Officially. Very moving ceremony. Closed casket, which everyone agreed was tasteful, given the fire."

"The fire was the tasteful part," I said. "I did the paperwork myself."

"Mm." He walked the deck like he owned it, boots ringing on wet planks, and I let him, because the alternative was stopping him and I needed another minute to count his escort. Four on the dock. Two at the gangway. One aboard already, pretending to admire the rigging with the studied boredom of a man who'd been told to look bored. Eight. Maybe nine. The *Salt Vow* carried Odile, Noor, Sef, me, and six crew who signed on for smuggling salted fish, not fighting the usurper's hand-picked hounds.

Rask stopped at the rail and looked out at the harbor of Graywater Reach, all that gray water and gray sky, and said, conversationally: "You know what I can't figure out? Why here. You could've gone east past the mountains. Nobody looks for a dead woman east. Instead you're running errands for a charm-seller and a smuggler priestess, hauling the single most valuable piece of cargo in the Nine Reaches on a fishing boat." He turned. "It's sentimental, Maren. It's not like you."

Everything in me went very quiet and very cold, the way it used to before an arrest. Old habits. I'd thought they were burned off with the rest of Inquisitor Solde. Apparently grief keeps its own archives.

"I don't know what you mean," I said.

"There's a boy aboard this ship." He said it gently, which from Rask was worse than shouting. "There's a boy aboard this ship who speaks a language that makes grown witch-finders wet themselves, and the Lord Protector wants him alive, and somewhere between the burning of Saint Verren's sanctuary and this exact deck, somebody decided to play nursemaid." His eyes came up to mine. "Somebody who's supposed to be dead."

* * *

Let me tell you about Saint Verren's, since Rask so kindly brought it up, because he knew exactly what he was doing when he did.

Two years ago I stood in the courtyard of a sanctuary in the hill country while my own order burned it. There were forty-one children inside. We were told thirty-one. The arithmetic was done by a man named Deputy Commander Hale, who counted heads the way you count sacks of grain, from a distance, quickly, wanting to be done. When the screaming started and the number turned out wrong, Hale did not stop the fire. Hale wrote *thirty-one* in his report anyway, because the report was already filed and amending it would have meant admitting the order had made an error, and the order did not make errors. The order made examples.

I pulled four children out through the cistern tunnel. Two lived. I put one of them on a carter's wagon heading south and never learned her name, and the other one — a girl called Pell, nine years old, who held my hand the whole way and asked me if the smoke meant the gods were angry — I buried under a rowan tree myself, because there wasn't anyone else to do it.

Then I went back to my barracks, cleaned my kit, and started planning my death. It took four months. Faking your own demise is mostly paperwork, which is the dirtiest thing I know about institutions: they'll believe any lie you file in triplicate. A body pulled from the canal, badly burned, identified by a colleague who owed me money and hated owing it. Rask signed the identification. He looked at the corpse — or what was left; I'd chosen my double carefully, gods forgive me, some poor drowned woman nobody claimed — and he looked at me across the morgue table three days later when I came in wearing my old face and my new name, and he signed.

I have spent two years telling myself he believed it. Standing on the *Salt Vow*'s deck with his smile sitting there like a debt come due, I finally admitted the truth: Rask signs whatever needs signing. He probably suspected. He certainly didn't care. That's the difference between us, and it took me seven years and one dead child to see it clearly: I broke because I couldn't stop counting the children. Rask never counted anything. He just carried the torch where he was told and called the warmth conviction.

"So," he said now, and produced a folded paper from inside his coat, sealed with the black wax of the Lord Protector's own chancery. "Here's how this goes."

* * *

Here is what he offered, and I want it recorded accurately, because part of surviving Rask is refusing to let him rewrite things after the fact:

Full amnesty. My death officially retracted — not pardoned, *retracted*, as though it had never happened, as though Inquisitor Maren Solde had been on deep assignment all along. Restoration of rank. A house in Vell with a garden, because Rask remembered I liked gardens, which is the sort of detail he keeps the way a spider keeps a web. And in exchange: the boy. Delivered alive, delivered calm, delivered with a story about how the traitorous witch-finder had infiltrated the smugglers' ring to recover him all along. They'd even give me a medal. Rask actually said the word *medal* without irony. I checked his face twice.

"And if I decline?" I asked.

He sighed, and looked genuinely sorry, and I believed the sorrow, because Rask is never cruel for pleasure. Cruelty is just his tool, same as his smile. "Then the boy comes with us anyway, and everyone who helped him gets a trial, and you get to watch the trials." He paused. "You know how our trials go, Maren. You've sat through enough of them. You've *run* enough of them."

He'd rehearsed this. Of course he had. Rask rehearsed everything; it was why he was good. But he'd rehearsed it for the woman he thought I was — the one who'd sign the identification and keep her head down and take the garden. He hadn't accounted for the fact that I'd spent two years becoming someone whose whole self was built out of the refusal of exactly this moment.

"Noor," I said.

"What?"

"You said I'm running errands for a charm-seller. You think that's what this is. You think I'm hiding." I stepped closer, close enough to smell the chancery wax and the road dust on him. "I'm not hiding, Rask. I'm *choosing*. There's a difference, and the difference is that hiding people run when you show up, and I'm still standing here listening to you offer me a garden."

His smile flickered. Just at the corner. Just for a heartbeat. It was the most honest thing he'd shown me all day, and I treasured it.

"The boy stays," I said. "The crew sails. And you're going to walk down that gangway and report that the dead woman wasn't here, because if you report otherwise, I will spend every remaining day of my life making sure the Lord Protector learns exactly how his deputy commander handled the accounting at Saint Verren's. Forty-one children, Rask. Your order's own records say thirty-one. I kept copies."

That was a bluff. I'd kept no copies; I'd kept nothing but the memory and the rowan tree. But Rask's face did something complicated, and I understood, with a lurch, that he thought it was true — that he'd wondered for two years whether the woman he'd identified in the morgue had left insurance somewhere, and here she was, cashing it.

"You wouldn't," he said. "They'd burn you for treason against the order."

"They'd have to catch me," I said. "I'm told I'm already dead. It's very convenient."

* * *

He didn't walk down the gangway. I want to be fair to myself here, in the privacy of my own skull, because in the years since I have replayed this moment more times than I've slept, and I need the record straight: I gave him the out. I offered him the lie. He could have taken it.

Instead he looked past my shoulder, at something over the deckhouse, and moved his hand — a small motion, two fingers flicking down, the old field signal, *take them* — and everything happened at once.

The man admiring the rigging dropped his boredom and drew steel. Two more came up the gangway fast, and the four on the dock started forward, and I heard Odile's voice from below decks shouting something in the old liturgical cadence that I'd learn later was a warning prayer and not, as I first assumed, cursing.

I got my knife into the rigging-man's thigh before his blade cleared its sheath — witch-finder training dies hard, thank all the silent gods — and then the world became the ugly short-range arithmetic it always becomes. Elbow, weight, deck slick underfoot. Someone grabbed my coat; I let the coat go. The gangway pair reached the deck and one of them — young, maybe twenty, nervous, the way I'd been once — swung at me wild, and I broke his wrist and put him down and told myself later that he'd live, and I think he did, I choose to believe he did.

And then the crew joined in.

I hadn't asked them to. That's the thing I can't get around. Odile's people were fishermen and runners, salt-crusted pragmatists who'd signed on to move contraband charms, not to fight the Lord Protector's hounds. Nobody had promised them anything. But Brannic — the big helmsman with the laugh like a rockslide, who used to cheat Odile at cards and lose cheerfully — came off the wheel with a boathook and put himself between the second gangway man and the cabin where Sef was hidden, roaring something about *not on my deck*, and the young one beside him, Tam, who couldn't have been nineteen, picked up a belaying pin and followed him.

Brannic lived. I want that clear too. Brannic took a slash across the shoulder that will ache in rain for the rest of his long, loud life, and he earned it dragging two of Rask's men off the side by main force.

Tam died.

He died badly and quickly and pointlessly, a blade through the chest in the first ten seconds of his first fight, and he fell against the rail with an expression of pure astonishment, as though the universe had broken a rule he'd only just learned existed. Nineteen years old. He'd told me two nights ago, laughing, that he was saving his share to buy his mother a house above the flood line.

Rask's men got Tam. And Rask got away — because of course he did; Rask has always known exactly when a fight stops being worth winning — but not before the last thing he did was stand at the top of the gangway, bleeding from where Brannic's boathook had opened his cheek, and look at me with something that wasn't anger.

It was disappointment. Genuine, almost paternal. *You could have taken the garden,* that look said. *You could have been sensible.*

Then he was gone, down onto the dock, and his men broke off and followed, dragging their wounded, leaving us with our dead and our blood and the harbor gulls already coming down to investigate, because gulls don't care whose side the blood is on.

* * *

We buried Tam at sea that evening, because that's the law of Odile's deck: the sea takes her own, and quickly, before the flesh decides otherwise. Odile spoke the rites of the silenced pantheon over him — the real ones, the old ones, the ones that would cost her her neck in any port Voss controls — and her voice didn't shake once, which I still don't understand, because mine shook and I barely knew the boy.

Sef stood at the rail with his fists clenched white, and when Odile finished, he said a word in the First Tongue, soft, just one syllable, and the water around the shroud went still. Perfectly, impossibly still, a flat gray circle in a choppy sea, holding the body a moment longer than buoyancy should allow, like the sea itself pausing to pay respects.

Then it took him, and the wind came back, and Sef turned to me with tears cutting tracks through the salt on his face and asked me — in trade-tongue, careful, the way he asks everything — *"Did he die because of me?"*

And here is where I should have lied. Every instinct I'd honed in fifteen years of inquisition said lie: manage the witness, control the narrative, protect the asset. And every part of me that had walked out of Saint Verren's courtyard said that lying to children is how witch-finders are made.

"Yes," I said. "Because of you, but not your fault. There's a difference, and it matters, and I need you to hold onto it. Men like Rask made a world where helping you costs blood. That's their crime, not yours. Your job — the only job I'll ever ask of you — is to make sure it wasn't for nothing."

He nodded, and wiped his face, and went below to where Noor was sitting up with cracked ribs and a grimace, and I heard the boy's voice going soft and low, telling Noor about the stillness in the water, and Noor's voice answering, rough and gentle, and I stood at the rail and let the wind have my face for a while.

* * *

Odile found me there after full dark. She handed me a cup of something that tasted like tar sweetened with regret and leaned on the rail beside me, and for a long time neither of us said anything, which is the highest form of respect one sailor pays another.

"You knew him," she said at last. "The one who ran."

"Fifteen years. Partner. He signed my corpse's papers himself."

"Mm." She drank. "And now he knows where we are, and roughly where we're going."

"He always knew where we were going," I said. "He was reading our route off the harbormaster's ledgers before we ever cleared the strait. Rask doesn't chase. He waits ahead. It's his one virtue." I stared north, where the coast bent toward the toll-keeper's station at the mouth of the Gray Strait, the only deep-water passage east, the one door between us and the free cities' council. "He'll report the route whether he lives or dies. Which means within three days, every toll post on the strait has our description, our ship, and orders to take the boy alive."

"Then we don't go through the strait."

"We go through the strait," I said. "The open sea in autumn is a slower death, and the eastern ports are all Voss's now. The strait has one toll-keeper, and toll-keepers are creatures of paper, and paper—" I touched the place where my old credentials used to sit, the ones I'd buried with my name, "—paper can be forged, bought, or bluffed. I've done all three."

Odile looked at me for a long moment. "You understand what you're saying. To pass that toll, you'll have to stand up and be Inquisitor Solde again. Out loud. In front of witnesses. After tonight, there's no taking it back — Rask's report burns your little fiction for good. Dead women don't bluff toll-keepers."

"I know."

"It'll hurt."

"It's supposed to," I said. "That's how you know it's honest."

She laughed — a short, smoky bark, nothing amused in it — and clinked her cup against mine, and below decks I could hear Noor's voice rising and falling, telling Sef something, probably a story, probably a lie shaped like a story, the specific kindness that charm-sellers and smugglers and disgraced inquisitors all learn eventually: that the truth can wait until morning, but fear cannot.

Behind us, the wake stretched back toward Graywater Reach, straight and pale and legible as a signature, pointing the way for everything Rask would bring.

Three days. One toll. One last performance from a woman who no longer existed.

I finished the tar-wine, set the cup on the rail, and went below to sharpen my knives and practice my old voice.
