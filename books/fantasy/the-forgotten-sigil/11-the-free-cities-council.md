## 11. The Free Cities Council

The Council of Free Cities met in the Salt Hall of Vell, beneath a ceiling carved with the faces of every god who had ever held a covenant with mortal hands. Half the faces had been chiseled off during Voss's rise. The other half watched us argue with expressions I can only describe as embarrassed.

I'd been in war councils before. This one smelled different — not of sweat and fear, but of ink and lamp oil and the particular stink of men who have already decided and are merely waiting for the paperwork to agree with them.

There were nine seats on the dais. Nine cities, nine voices, and roughly four hundred opinions between them. At the center sat Chancellor Aurel Vance of Vell himself, a narrow man with a narrow face and the eyes of someone who had spent thirty years learning to say no in ways that sounded like wisdom.

"Noor Nystrom," he read from a paper, as if he needed reminding. "The mercenary. The charm-seller." A ripple of something that wasn't quite laughter moved through the gallery. "You come before this council claiming that the old covenant — broken these seven years — can be *reforged*. That a child in your custody is the key to it. And that we should stake the survival of the free cities on this claim rather than on walls, garrisons, and grain."

"I'm not asking you to abandon your walls," I said. "I'm asking you to give me a boat."

That got an actual laugh. Small, but real.

"Let me be plain," Vance said, folding his hands. "Seven years ago the gods went silent. Every prayer unanswered, every rite dead, every miracle that once held back plague and famine and the sea itself — gone. Whatever caused it, it is beyond the reach of any sigil, any child, any—" he glanced at me over his spectacles— "*mercenary* to repair. What is within our reach is stone. Stone has never lied to us. Stone does not require faith."

"Stone also doesn't fight," Maren muttered beside me.

I elbowed her quiet. We'd rehearsed this. Sef stood at my left, scrubbed and combed for the occasion, wearing clothes Odile had bribed, borrowed, or possibly stolen for him. He looked like a boy pretending to be an ambassador, which was exactly what he was.

"Chancellor," I said, "three weeks ago a witch-finder patrol burned a sanctuary full of children in the hill country. Two weeks ago they burned the ferry at Graywater with forty souls aboard because one of them could light a candle without flint. Last week Voss's vanguard crossed the Marrow. Your stones are lovely. They will hold for perhaps a season. Then what?"

"Then we negotiate," said Lady Corvane of Estmark, a heavy woman with rings on every finger. "Voss is a rational actor. He wants order, not slaughter. There are terms to be had."

"He burned a city of six thousand at Ashfall," Maren said, and her voice cracked across the hall like a whip. She couldn't help herself. Former inquisitors don't do gentle. "I was there. I signed the orders. There were no terms. There were no survivors worth the word."

The gallery murmured. Someone in the back called out that the inquisitor's own record hardly recommended her judgment, and Maren went white, and I stepped forward before she could answer, because answering would have ended everything.

"Give me a ship," I said again. "Give me nothing else. If I fail, you've lost one disgraced soldier, one dead woman walking, one smuggler, and a child you clearly don't believe in anyway. If I succeed—" I spread my hands. "You get your gods back."

Silence. The kind that has weight.

It was Odile who broke it, rising from the gallery where she'd insisted on sitting among commoners, which was either humility or a good position for a quick exit. "Chancellor. Ferran. *Salt-Widow*, out of Vell harbor, registered cargo: salt fish and honest lies." She grinned, and half the hall grinned back despite themselves. Everyone knew the *Salt-Widow*. Everyone had probably ridden in her hold at least once without knowing it. "You want to know what I saw, three nights ago, off the Gray Strait?"

"Odile," I warned.

She ignored me. "I saw a boy speak two words in the First Tongue and flatten a storm that should have eaten us whole. I've sailed thirty years. I know weather. That wasn't weather bending. That was weather *obeying*." She let that sit. "You can build your walls. But when Voss comes — and he's coming, he's past the Marrow now — ask yourselves what your stones will do when the man attacking them carries stolen divinity in his pocket."

And there it was. The thing we hadn't wanted to say aloud in front of the child. Sef's hand found mine. His grip was small and cold.

Vance took off his spectacles, polished them, put them back on. All theater. I'd seen officers do the same before delivering verdicts.

"The council thanks the petitioners," he said. "Deliberations will continue in closed session."

Which meant: *no*, dressed in ceremony.

* * *

We waited two hours in an antechamber that smelled of beeswax and old arguments while the council deliberated behind doors thick enough to muffle even Maren's pacing. Sef fell asleep against my shoulder somewhere around the second hour, which told me more about how frightened he'd been than anything he'd have admitted awake.

When the herald finally summoned us back, I knew before Vance opened his mouth. You learn to read rooms when you've sold charms in enough markets. The nine seats had arranged themselves into a wall.

"The council has voted," Vance said. "Eight to one. The free cities will fortify. Grain stores doubled, militia levies authorized, the harbor chains refitted. In recognition of the... gravity of the threat, the council further votes a stipend for the child's care and protection, to be administered by—"

"You're going to *pay* me," I said, "to keep him safe inside your walls until Voss arrives to take him."

"We are offering the most prudent course available."

"Prudent." I tasted the word like bad wine. "My entire company died being prudent. We held a pass for a god who wouldn't answer, because holding was the prudent thing, the loyal thing, and every single one of them is under the water now." I heard my own voice climbing and couldn't stop it. Seven years of silence coming up all at once, like a drowned thing breaking surface. "You want prudence? Prudence is what Voss is counting on. He's betting every city in this hall will choose walls over faith, terms over truth, and he'll be right, and he'll pick you apart one by one while you congratulate each other on your grain stores."

Lady Corvane leaned forward. "And if you're wrong, mercenary? If the child is simply a child who speaks an old language? You'll have spent lives and ships chasing a fairy tale while real people die at real walls."

"Then I'll have been wrong alone," I said. "Which is more than the council can say."

Vance's jaw tightened. "The vote stands. The council will entertain no further—"

"One vote against," Maren said suddenly. "Who?"

A pause. From the far end of the dais, a thin, elderly man raised one hand. Master Ellery of the small coastal city of Brack, a place so minor I'd nearly forgotten it had a seat. He looked at Sef for a long moment, then at me.

"Thirty years ago," Ellery said, "I heard the tide-priests sing at the drowned shrine off our coast. Before the silence. My grandmother swore the sea itself answered them." He turned to Vance. "I cannot prove the covenant can be reforged, Chancellor. But I cannot prove it *cannot*, and neither can anyone in this room. We are asked to bet everything on stone. I decline to bet everything on anything." He looked back at me. "Brack keeps fishing boats, mercenary. Old ones. Slow ones. If you need a hull that isn't registered to any council fleet, the *Kestrel's Tithe* sits at pier nine. Her master owes me money and hates the usurper in equal measure."

"Master Ellery," Vance began coldly, "this council—"

"This council," Ellery said mildly, "has voted. I voted. Thank you for the opportunity."

* * *

Pier nine at dusk was all rust and rope and the smell of fish gone honest about being dead. The *Kestrel's Tithe* turned out to be a squat coastal tub with patched sails and a figurehead of some forgotten goddess whose face someone had lovingly defaced and then, later, just as lovingly tried to carve back. Her master was a leathery woman named Bree who took Ellery's letter, read it twice, looked at our little procession — me, Maren with her hood up, Odile carrying Sef asleep on her shoulder — and spat thoughtfully into the harbor.

"Ellery says you're mad," she said.

"Ellery's generous," I said.

"Where to?"

I looked at Odile. She'd made the crossing to the Gray Strait; she knew the waters better than any chart. But it was Sef, waking groggily, who answered — pointing east, toward the open sea and the drowned coast beyond it, and saying one soft syllable in the First Tongue that none of us knew and all of us understood.

Bree stared at him. Then at me. Then she sighed the sigh of a woman adding another debt to a long ledger.

"Cast off at dawn," she said. "Anyone who tells the council can swim."

Maren laughed — short, surprised, real. Odile shifted Sef's weight and pressed her lips to the top of his head. And I stood on the rotten planks of pier nine with nothing — no mandate, no coin, no army, no god — and felt something loosen in my chest that had been clenched for seven years.

They'd said no. Eight to one. And it didn't matter, because the yes we needed hadn't been in that hall. It had been sleeping on my shoulder, and arguing at my side, and grinning from the gallery, and waiting at pier nine in the shape of a slow boat and a bitter old captain.

"Voss will still come," Maren said quietly, beside me. "Walls or no walls. And we're taking their only weapon away from them."

"We're not taking him anywhere," I said. "He's taking us. Didn't you see?" I nodded toward Sef, who was watching the darkening water with eyes far older than eight years old. "He knew the way before we asked."

Far off to the east, past the harbor chain and the gray line of the sea, something flickered — faint as a dying coal, there and gone. Lightning, probably. Storms out on the open water, this time of year.

Probably.

I touched the charm at my throat — the one I'd worn for seven years, praying into silence every dawn — and for the first time since the pass, since the company, since everything, I prayed without irony.

*Hold on,* I said, to whatever was listening, or trying to. *We're coming.*

The wind came off the water and carried the smell of salt and old words, and at pier nine, in the dark, the *Kestrel's Tithe* creaked against her ropes like something waking from a long sleep.
