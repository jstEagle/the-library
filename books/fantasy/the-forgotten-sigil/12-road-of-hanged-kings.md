## 12. Road of Hanged Kings

I have smuggled contraband through nine customs houses, two blockades, and one wedding, but I had never until this week led a child past a canyon full of dead kings.

The locals call it the Throat of Vess. The cartographers call it the Vessine Gorge, which is the sort of name men give places when they want to pretend the place doesn't bite. It runs a day and a half on foot between the salt flats and the highlands, narrow enough in places that you could spit across it and hit both walls, and for most of its length there is no road — only a riverbed that floods twice a year and drowns anyone foolish enough to be standing in it the other three hundred and sixty-three days.

Kaelen Voss has decorated it.

They hang from iron gibbets bolted into the canyon walls, maybe forty of them, maybe sixty — I stopped counting after my stomach did. Deposed rulers, every one. A duke of Harrowmere with his signet ring still on his finger because Voss wanted people to know exactly whose hand had held what. A queen of the southern isles who'd ruled eleven years before her own merchants sold her out. Two brothers who'd shared a throne and, as it turned out, a gallows. Their bodies are not fresh. Voss's engineers have done something to them — salted, tallowed, treated with whatever alchemy his witch-finders permit themselves — so that they do not rot. They just *stay*. Kings and queens strung along the walls like lanterns at a festival nobody wanted, each one wearing a placard in four languages:

*THIS IS WHAT CROWNS COST NOW.*

"It's a message," Maren said, when we reached the mouth of the gorge at dawn and she went very still in the way she does, the way she went still in the Gray Strait toll-house right before she burned her old life for kindling.

"No," I said. "It's an argument. Messages you can ignore. Arguments you have to answer."

Noor said nothing at all. He stood looking up at the nearest body — a young man, crownless, hands bound with silk rather than rope, which told me he'd been hanged by his own court — and his face did the thing it does where all the weather goes inland. I've known him long enough now to read the coast instead: the set of his jaw, the way his thumb found the little outlawed charm in his pocket and worried it like a rosary.

Sef looked too. Of course he did. He's seven years old and the world keeps insisting on showing him things, and no one has yet figured out how to stand between a child and a canyon wall without becoming part of the decoration yourself.

"They were kings," Sef said, in trade-tongue, and then something longer in the First Tongue, soft, almost conversational, the way he speaks it when he isn't thinking. The words moved through the gorge and came back off the stone wrong — too many echoes for one boy's voice.

"Save it," Noor said quietly. Not unkind. Tired in the marrow. "Not here."

I put my hand on Sef's shoulder and turned him toward the riverbed. "Walk," I told him. "Eyes forward. In my trade we have a rule about cargo: you don't stare at what might be watching back."

Because that was the other thing about the Throat of Vess, the thing the gibbets were there to make you forget to check. A canyon full of hanging kings is also a canyon full of cover, shadow, and every bandit, deserter, and desperate soul between the flats and the free cities. Voss hangs his warnings where the road runs anyway, because the road runs anyway. People are like that. We look at the price tag and pay it.

We paid attention instead. All that day, and half the night, moving in the dry season shallows with our boots wrapped in wool to keep the noise down, and me doing the thing I am actually good at, which is listening to a landscape the way other people listen to a room they're about to rob.

The landscape was lying to us. I could tell it was lying because it was trying too hard. Too many birds gone quiet in the wrong stretch of afternoon. Too much goat dung on a trail where no goats had grazed recently — dung doesn't keep, it crumbles, and this was fresh enough to still hold shape. Somebody was feeding a fire somewhere up-canyon and thought they were being clever about the smoke.

"Company," I murmured to Noor as we made cold camp in a side-cut, under an overhang, with the river's ghost gurgling somewhere below. "A dozen, maybe two. Deserters by the sign of them — Voss's gray-and-iron, but worn like they've been sleeping in their grievances. They'll be hungry. Hungry soldiers don't ask questions, they take inventory."

Maren was already cleaning her blade, which is how she prays. "If they're Voss's, they'll have orders about the boy."

"If they're deserters, they'll have orders about nothing except their own bellies." Noor lay back against the stone with his arm over his eyes. "Maybe we go around."

"Around adds two days through open flats," I said. "Open flats mean witch-finder outriders. I'll take hungry men over righteous ones. Hungry men can be fed. Righteous ones can only be buried."

That got the closest thing to a smile he's produced since Odile's hold became a chapel and her cargo manifest became scripture. "You're a terrible priestess."

"I'm a magnificent priestess. That's why the pantheon went quiet — they couldn't afford the tithes."

Sef laughed. Small, startled out of him, the laugh of a boy who'd forgotten laughing was allowed. And then he asked, in that careful way of his, whether the hanging kings had been bad men.

And I — fool, smuggler, priestess of a silenced choir — I told him the truth, because he deserved it and because I'm constitutionally incapable of lying to children before breakfast. "Some were. Some were ordinary. That's the sermon, love. Voss doesn't hang monsters. Monsters are hard to find. He hangs *rulers*, because a ruler is any person who says *the buck stops here*, and he wants the whole world to learn that saying it is fatal."

He thought about that for a long time, the way he thinks about everything, with his whole body, brow furrowed, fingers working at the hem of the coat Noor had cut down for him. Then he said, "Then the word for king must be a sad word," and said it in the First Tongue, and the overhang above us hummed like a struck bowl, and none of us slept well after that.

* * *

They came at the grey hour before dawn, which is when everything comes, having learned it from wolves or taught it to them, history's unclear.

I heard them first — boots in the shallows, badly spaced, a man dragging his left heel the way men do when they've marched past the end of themselves. Twelve, as advertised, fanning out from the side-cut mouth with the confidence of people who believe surprise is a weapon they own. I counted crossbows: four. The rest had spears and the particular hunger-stooped posture of soldiers eating their own supply train.

Noor was awake before I finished counting. He's always awake before I finish counting; I've stopped taking it personally. He moved to stand between the mouth of the cut and where Sef slept, and Maren came up on his left with her hood back and her scarred face bare, which was either a surrender or a declaration, and knowing Maren it was both.

"Hold," she called into the dark, inquisitor-flat, a voice trained to stop rooms. "This party is under Church writ."

The lead deserter laughed, and it was a horrible sound, wet and cracked. "Church writ. Lady, the Church burned its own writs in Vell. We watched the pile from the wall." He stepped closer, spear low. "We're not here for your paper. We're here for food, and the woman looks like she carries more than she eats."

"We have hardtack," Noor said. "Take a share and walk on."

"And the kid?" Another voice, from the flank. "Word down-canyon says there's a kid traveling with a marked crew. Word says the Lord Protector pays a fortress for that kid alive."

There it is, I thought. There's always a word down-canyon. Rask's leak, or Voss's web, or simply the world doing what the world does — charging tolls on secrets. These men weren't hunting Sef. They were starving. But starvation and bounty make a marriage of convenience, and I have buried the children of worse unions.

"Fortresses fall," Maren said. "So do men who reach for them."

"Pretty talk." The leader raised his spear. "Last offer. Food, the kid, and you four walk—"

Sef woke up.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left of that moment. He didn't cry out. He didn't wake the way children wake, all at once and loud. He surfaced the way deep water surfaces — slow, inevitable, and then everywhere. He stood up in the grey light with sleep still in his eyes and looked at twelve armed men, and he said one word in the First Tongue.

I will not write it down. Not because it's forbidden — most of the First Tongue is forbidden, and I've been forbidden plenty of things and kept right on doing them — but because I don't think language carries it. What I heard was a single syllable. What the canyon heard was a verdict.

Every crossbow string snapped. Not fired — *snapped*, all four, clean as cut harp strings, the bolts dropping uselessly into the sand. The spears sagged in their owners' grips like the wood had remembered being a tree and lost interest in war. And the men — the men *knelt*. Not all at once. One, then three, then all twelve, folding down into the river gravel with their heads bowed and their breath coming ragged, and the sound they made wasn't fear. Fear I know. This was older. This was the sound of men who had spent years marching behind banners and burning villages and telling themselves the gods were dead and therefore off the hook — hearing, for the first time since childhood, something that sounded like being *known*.

The leader was crying. Big gray-bearded killer, tears cutting tracks through the dust of his face, whispering something over and over that might have been a prayer or might have been an apology or might, in the end, have been the same thing.

And Sef — seven years old, barefoot, blinking — looked at them kneeling in the muck and said, in trade-tongue, small and puzzled:

"I didn't tell them to do that."

* * *

We fed them. Of course we fed them. That's the cost of mercy, if you want to know — mercy is expensive, and the bill always arrives at the worst table in the house.

Twelve rations of hardtack and the last of the smoked fish, gone in twenty minutes to twelve hollow men who ate like penitents, slowly, some of them weeping into their food. They gave us names, or the shapes of names — Brannic, Tolliver, a boy called Finch who couldn't have been sixteen and had deserted after Voss's witch-finders burned a village he wouldn't describe. They told us the disposition of the outriders on the flats, the watch rotations at the far gorge mouth, the fact that a detachment under a captain named Rask was sweeping the highlands with a warrant that named four travelers and one child.

Rask. Maren went white, then went still, then went to sharpen her blade again, which is how she prays when the prayer is angry.

And then the deserters asked the question I'd been dreading, the one that comes after every miracle: *what do we do now?*

"You can't come with us," Noor said, before anyone else could speak. Flat. Final. "Thirteen is a caravan. Five is a rumor."

"They'll be picked up within a week," Maren added, and there was no cruelty in it, only arithmetic. "Deserters this close to the flats—"

"Then let them pick us up," said Brannic, the leader, still kneeling, still wet-faced. "Let them hang us in your canyon with the kings. At least we'll die having knelt to something true."

Silence. The river's ghost gurgled. Somewhere above us, a hanged queen creaked on her gibbet in the morning wind, patient as furniture.

And Sef walked over to Brannic — walked straight up to a grown armed man in the ruins of a uniform, the way he walks, like the ground has agreed in advance to hold him — and took the man's face in his two small hands, and spoke to him in the First Tongue. Low. Gentle. Three words, maybe four.

Brannic shuddered like a ship coming off a reef. When Sef stepped back, the man rose, and the others rose with him, and whatever despair had been sitting on them like ballast had been cut loose. Not hope exactly. Something quieter. Ballast-cut ships don't soar. They just stop sinking.

"What did you say to him?" Noor asked Sef afterward, and his voice had a crack in it I could have sailed a brigantine through.

Sef considered. "I told them the kings aren't the warning." He frowned, hunting for the trade-tongue shapes. "The kings are the *proof*. That crowns break. That men break. And that breaking isn't the end of the sentence." He shrugged, seven years old again, suddenly embarrassed. "It sounds better in the other tongue."

I'll say this for Noor Nystrom: he is a hard man to frighten, and I have watched him lie to witch-finders with his pulse slower than mine. But standing there in the grey light, watching a child comfort twelve killers with the grammar of creation, Noor looked afraid. Not of Sef. That's what shook me. Not of the boy — of the *distance*. You could see him doing the arithmetic every adult in that canyon was doing: this child is becoming something, and the something is vast, and vastness does not care how much you love it.

"The god speaks through him more every week," Maren said quietly, when the deserters had shouldered their packs and started down-canyon toward whatever fate they'd chosen, backs straighter than they'd arrived.

"He's not a pipe," Noor said. "He's a person."

"Both things can be true."

"That's what frightens me."

* * *

We cleared the far mouth of the Throat at dusk, and I did something I'm not proud of. I looked back.

The hanged kings caught the last light and threw it back dull as old coins, forty-odd arguments strung along the stone, and beneath them the riverbed ran empty and shining, and I thought: *he built this to make the world flinch.* And it works. Every traveler who walks this gorge flinches, and hurries, and tells the tale in taverns with the proper horror, and pays the toll of fear without ever seeing the tollbooth.

But a seven-year-old walked through it and rewrote the sermon in a single word. Crowns break, he said. Breaking isn't the end of the sentence. Somewhere up in whatever silence the silenced pantheon keeps, I'd swear something laughed.

Ahead of us the highlands climbed black against a bruised sky, and somewhere in those highlands a captain named Rask was sweeping with a warrant that had our faces on it, and Maren was walking point with her blade bare and her prayers sharp, and Noor had Sef up on his shoulders so the boy could see the first stars, and I brought up the rear doing what I do best.

Listening.

Which is how I heard it, faint on the wind off the flats behind us: horns. Not one horn. Many, spread wide, moving fast, closing on the gap we'd just come through — the sound of a net drawing shut on empty water.

They knew we'd come this way. Which meant someone had told them.

And the only souls who'd seen our faces in that canyon were twelve starving deserters we'd fed with our own hands, and one of them had known Rask's name before we ever spoke it.
