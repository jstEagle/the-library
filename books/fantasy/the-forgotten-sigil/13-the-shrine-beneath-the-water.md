## 13. The Shrine Beneath the Water

The sea at Graymarch Cove was the color of an old bruise, and it smelled like every bad decision I'd ever made.

I stood on the shingle with my boots in the foam and let the memory come, because I'd learned long ago that fighting it only made it worse. Seven years ago this beach had been crowded — forty-one mercenaries of the Company of the Broken Standard, hauling siege gear and votive banners up toward the pass, singing the marching hymn to Vaelun the Unblinking, god of oaths and watchfires. Forty-one men and women who believed their god had called them here.

Forty-one minus one. The arithmetic of my survival had never once come out clean.

"You're doing the face," Odile said behind me. She had the ship's boat hauled up above the tideline and was pretending to fuss with its mooring line while she watched me. "The one where you look like you're chewing a lemon that owes you money."

"I don't have a face like that."

"Love, you've had that face since the Strait. It's settled in. It's paying rent."

Sef came down the rocks with Maren behind him, both of them carrying what remained of our supplies. The boy stopped when he saw the water, and something moved across his face — not fear exactly. Recognition. He crouched, touched two fingers to the wet sand, and drew a line, then a second line crossing it, then a circle around both.

The sigil. Or the shape of it, anyway, the way a child's drawing of a horse is the shape of a horse.

"You know this place," I said.

He looked up. "It *hurts* here." In trade-tongue, which he mostly used now, though the First Tongue leaked through him like water through a cracked jar. "Underneath. Something underneath is hurting."

Maren set down her pack and studied the cove with the flat professional sweep I'd seen her use on crime scenes and battlefields alike. "There's no shrine marker," she said. "No road. No pilgrim cairns. If there was ever a temple here, the land has eaten every sign of it."

"The land would," I said. "That was rather the point."

* * *

Here is what the histories say, or said, before Voss's book-burners got to them: when the gods withdrew from the world after the Sundering Wars, they did not leave entirely. Each of the great powers sank a piece of itself into the earth — a shrine, a wellspring, a knot in the covenant that bound divine will to mortal soil. The shrines were drowned deliberately, hidden beneath lakes and seas and, in one famous case, a glacier, so that no king or conqueror could dig them up and hold a god hostage.

Vaelun the Unblinking had sunk his well beneath Graymarch Cove, under sixty feet of cold green water, behind wards laid by priests who had been dead three thousand years.

And seven years ago, my company had been sent here to die for it.

We hadn't known that part. We'd been told we were holding the pass against raiders out of the Ashlands. A simple contract, good coin, a god's blessing on the banner. Captain Eshe Veyra — may the mud take me gently if I speak her name without flinching, and I always flinch — had knelt on this very shingle and prayed aloud for Vaelun's guidance before we marched inland.

I remember waiting for the answer. I remember the silence. I remember thinking: *the god is testing us.* That's what we told ourselves, all forty-one of us, as the raiders came and came again, as the wounded stacked up, as the rations went, as we sent runner after runner back to the coast asking why no reinforcement, no vision, no sign had come.

The god wasn't testing us. The god wasn't anything. The voice had already been cut out of it, and none of us knew, and we held the pass until there were none of us left to hold it.

Except one. And the one ran. That's the part I've never managed to sand smooth, no matter how many times I pick it up. When Eshe fell, something in me broke loose from its moorings, and I ran — down through the pass, down to this cove, down to the waterline, screaming Vaelun's name into the surf until my throat tore. *Answer me. Answer me, you unblinking bastard, they died for you.*

The sea said nothing. The sea has been saying nothing ever since.

"So," Odile said now, breaking into my silence with the gentleness she used like a crowbar, "how does one actually get down to a drowned shrine? Because I'm guessing you're not about to suggest we all hold hands and walk in."

"There's a way," I said. "The old pilgrims' route. It starts at the cliff base, north side. My captain found it, the day we arrived. She said—" My voice did something embarrassing. I cleared my throat. "She said the god would want his house kept tidy."

* * *

The entrance was still there, because stone outlasts grief by a comfortable margin. A fissure in the cliff, half-choked with collapsed rock, breathing cold air that smelled of salt and something older — incense gone to mineral, candle-fat turned to wax-stone, the particular staleness of a room no one has entered in a very long time.

Maren insisted on checking it for traps. Of course she did. She came back after an hour looking grim and slightly damp. "Ward-lines everywhere. Most of them dead. One or two still... awake, I think, but dormant. They're keyed to something." She looked at Sef. "Not to blood. Not to faith, either, whatever the old texts claim. They're keyed to language."

"The First Tongue," I said.

"The First Tongue," she agreed.

Sef stood at the fissure's mouth, small and mud-brown and utterly unafraid in a way that frightened me more than any witch-finder ever had. He put his palm flat against the wet stone and spoke — four syllables, soft, almost conversational, the way you'd greet a sleeping animal.

Deep in the cliff, something went *click*. Not a sound, exactly. More a sensation, felt in the teeth. The air in the fissure changed temperature, and the darkness inside seemed to lean back, like a door opening inward.

"It says come," Sef reported. Then, with the terrible literalism of children: "It says only one can fit through the deep part. It says the water remembers you."

Everyone looked at me.

"Wonderful," I said. "It's got jokes. Seven years of silence and the first thing it does is make jokes."

"Noor." Maren, quietly, in the voice she used for hard truths delivered at close range. "You don't have to go first. We could send the boy—"

"You could absolutely not send the boy," I said, with more force than I'd intended. "Whatever's down there, it's mine. The silence is mine. I paid for it in forty-one lives and I'm owed the accounting." I was unbuckling my sword belt as I talked, which felt strange, like taking off a limb. "Besides, if it kills me, you'll finally be rid of the lemon face."

Odile caught my arm. Her grip was stronger than it looked, everything about Odile was stronger than it looked. "Come back up," she said. "That's not a request dressed as a joke, mercenary. That's a request dressed as a request."

"I'll do my best."

"Your best has historically been garbage."

"I know," I said. "But it's the only thing I've got that's still mine."

She let go. Sef touched my hand — just briefly, two fingers, the way he'd touched the sand — and whispered something in the First Tongue. I didn't understand the words. I understood them anyway, the way you understand a fire is warm. *Go carefully. Come back whole.*

* * *

The passage went down at a cruel angle, slick stone underfoot, the walls closing in until my shoulders brushed both sides. The light I'd brought — a witch-lamp, outlawed, purchased from myself at a markup I still resent — threw a sickly green glow that made the flooded dark below look like the surface of another world.

The water began at my knees. Then my waist. Then, where the passage leveled and widened into a vaulted chamber that must once have been the shrine's antechamber, it rose to my chest, black and utterly still, holding the cold like a grudge.

Pillars marched off into the depths on either side of me, each one carved with script I couldn't read — except that I could, suddenly, in the way you understand a language you dreamed in. *Oaths are anchors. Watchfires are memories. What is sworn beneath the water cannot be unsworn above it.*

The liturgy of Vaelun. I'd shouted pieces of it over dying friends. Seeing it carved in stone three thousand years old made my chest hurt in a place I didn't have a name for.

I waded on. The chamber opened, and opened again, and then the floor dropped away beneath me and I was swimming, or being pulled, down through a shaft of stone so smooth it might have been glass, into a vastness my lamp couldn't reach the edges of.

And there — at the bottom of the drowned world, in a hollow like the cupped hands of something enormous — was the sigil.

Broken.

I'd imagined it a hundred times without knowing it: whole, golden, turning slowly like a wheel of law. What hung in the water before me was nothing so kind. It was a ring of interlocked glyphs the size of a wagon wheel, and a full third of it was simply *gone* — sheared away, the severed ends blackened and dripping threads of light that dissolved before they could drift far. The remaining glyphs were dim, guttering, each one flickering like a watchfire in wind.

Around it, in the hollow, lay shapes. Bones, some of them. Armor, older than my grandmother's grandmother. Offerings brought by the faithful across millennia and left here at the god's front door: cups, coins, a child's carved toy boat, a soldier's oath-token stamped with the Broken Standard.

My company's mark. Someone had come here after us. Someone had carried our tokens down into the dark and left them where the god could see them.

Or — worse, and truer — the god had gathered them itself, in whatever slow crippled way it could, the way a wounded animal drags itself toward the place it was hurt.

I swam closer. The water grew warm. That was the wrong detail, the detail my mind kept snagging on, because this deep, this far north, the water should have been cold enough to stop a heart. Instead it wrapped around me like a blanket, like a hand, like—

*I know you.*

The voice didn't come through my ears. It came up through the water, through my skin, through the bones the water was touching. It was faint. It was ragged. It was the voice of someone speaking through a torn throat, forcing each word past damage.

*I know your step. I have counted it for seven years. You walked away from the pass, and you have been walking back ever since.*

Every prayer I'd ever choked out into empty air came home at once, and I did the dignified thing, which was to burst into tears underwater like an absolute fool.

"You were *silent*," I said, and bubbles tore the words apart, and I didn't care. "Forty-one people. Eshe. Dagny and her ridiculous hat. Little Corvo, who was nineteen. They died holding a pass for you and you said *nothing*, and I screamed at you on this beach until I bled, and you said *nothing*—"

*I could not.* The voice broke like a wave. *Little flame. Sworn-of-the-Broken-Standard. Last of the forty-two. I could not. Look at me. Look at what was done to me.*

I looked. Through the blur of salt water that was not entirely seawater, I looked at the broken ring, at the sheared-away third, at the blackened ends still bleeding light after all these years.

"That's not age," I said slowly. "Shrines don't just... break."

*Nothing in the covenant breaks,* Vaelun said. *It was cut. Deliberately. By hands that knew the First Tongue and used it as a knife. I felt the severing like the loss of a limb. My voice went with it — my voice lived in the whole of the sigil, and with a third gone, everything I tried to say spilled out through the wound and reached no one. Do you understand? For seven years I have been screaming, little flame. Screaming at you on that beach. Screaming at my dying faithful in that pass. And every word fell out of me into the dark, and no one heard, and I could not even tell you I was sorry.*

The water held me while I shook. I want to be clear about what that shaking was, because I've had time to think about it since: it wasn't forgiveness arriving. It was something harder and more necessary. It was seven years of a story tearing itself apart and rebuilding itself in a single breath. *My god abandoned us* was a story I had built my whole ruined life on top of, the way you build a shack on a cliff edge. Take it away and there was nothing underneath except raw truth:

They had died for a god who had been screaming for them the entire time. And I had run from a battlefield carrying a betrayal that had never existed.

"Who," I said, when I could speak. "Who cut it. Give me a name."

*The wound remembers the knife,* the god said, and the guttering glyphs flickered, and for one moment the broken sigil showed me an image burned into its own injury like a scar holds the shape of the blade: a man's silhouette against fire, a hand raised with words of power coiling around the fingers, and on the seal-ring of that hand —

I knew the crest. Everyone in the free cities knew the crest now. The tower and the open book, the mark of Kaelen Voss, the man who had built a crusade on cleansing magic from the world.

The order had been given years before his crusade had a name. The plague, the purges, the witch-finders — all of it built on top of an act of theft committed in the dark, long ago, by a man who had cut a third out of a god and kept the piece.

*He holds my severed voice,* Vaelun said, and the warmth of the water curdled. *That is the source of his power, little flame. He has been wearing a piece of me like a stolen cloak. And a cloak remembers its owner.*

Above me, far up the flooded shaft, I heard a sound that did not belong: a dull boom, transmitted through stone and water, followed by another. Rhythmic. Deliberate.

Someone was breaking into the shrine from the cliff side.

The god's voice dropped to a rasp. *They are coming for what remains of me. And for the child who can mend me. You have very little time, and I have very little strength — but listen, last of the forty-two. Listen, because I have waited seven years to say it.*

The broken sigil flared, all its guttering glyphs at once, and the words came through the water like a bell through fog:

*I never abandoned you. Not one breath. Not one death. Now go — and tell the others what was taken, because what was stolen from me can be stolen back.*
