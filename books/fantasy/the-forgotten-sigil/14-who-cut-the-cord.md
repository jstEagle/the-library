## 14. Who Cut the Cord

I had spent eleven years as a witch-finder learning how to destroy records. It turns out all those skills run in reverse.

The drowned shrine's archive sat below the waterline, a long gallery of stone shelves where the flood had come and, in its mercy or its indifference, declined to finish the job. The lower shelves were pulp. The upper ones held ledgers and scrolls swollen with damp, their ink ghosted but legible if you tilted them toward the light of Odile's salvaged storm-lanterns and squinted like a woman who'd spent too many years reading confessions by bad candlelight. Which I had.

Noor had wanted to come down with me. I'd told him no.

"You're the one who talks to gods," I said. "I'm the one who reads their paperwork. We each have our liturgy."

He hadn't laughed. He'd been in no state to laugh since he came up from the shrine's heart talking about a wounded god with its voice cut out of it. But he'd gone, and taken Sef with him, because Sef had looked at the black water at the bottom of the stairwell with an expression I recognized from interrogations — the look of someone hearing something under the floor — and none of us liked what that might mean.

So it was me, the lanterns, and eleven years of other people's sins.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being an inquisitor: you don't spend your time fighting evil. You spend your time filing. Evil is a single dramatic hour; filing is the other ten thousand. I had read more heresy manifests than any living soul should have to, and somewhere around my fourth year I'd developed a theory that never made me popular with my superiors: you can tell everything about an institution by what it chooses to write down, and more by what it chooses not to.

Kaelen Voss wrote everything down. That was the first thing I remembered, standing hip-deep in cold water among the dead archive of a dead god's house. He was a meticulous man. When he'd been raised to Lord Inquisitor — before the crusade, before the edicts, when he was just the most brilliant and most joyless man in the Vigil — he'd reformed the entire record-keeping system personally. Cross-referenced indices. Standardized confession formats. A chain of custody for every seized text, so nothing could be lost, stolen, or quietly destroyed by some soft-hearted subordinate.

Everyone praised him for it. Order out of chaos, they said. A man who believes in accountability.

What he'd actually built was a machine that could not lie. And men like Voss only build machines that cannot lie when they are very sure the truth will serve them.

I found the first thread of it on my second day in the water.

* * *

The archive was organized by the old covenant structure — I knew the taxonomy from training, though we'd been taught it as *heretical cosmology, memorize in order to refute*. Three great divisions: the Voice (prayer, invocation, the traffic between mortals and gods), the Bond (the sigil itself, covenant law, the terms of binding), and the Land (rites of place, consecration, the marriage of divinity to geography). The Vigil had burned the Voice sections across half the world. The Land sections were mostly folklore, dismissed as superstition even by believers.

The Bond section was what interested me. Because Noor had come up from the shrine's heart saying the god's voice had been *stolen*, and stolen things have thieves, and thieves have paperwork whether they know it or not.

The shelf ledgers were catalogued in the First Tongue, which I couldn't read, but numbers are numbers, and dates are dates, and the shrine's keepers had used the same calendar the free cities still used. I worked backward from the silence. Seven years ago, give or take, the god of this place went quiet. Seven years ago, Noor's company died in a pass holding ground for an answer that never came. So: seven years ago, something happened here.

Except it didn't. That was the thing. That was the thing that made me stand in freezing water until my feet went numb, holding a waterlogged inventory ledger up to the lantern light, checking and rechecking a date because I did not want it to be true.

The last entry in the Bond section was dated nine years ago. Two years *before* the silence.

An inventory doesn't record what happens. It records what's *missing*. And this one recorded, in the dry administrative language of people who never imagined anyone would care, the removal of the sigil's anchoring texts — the master copies, the binding verses, the terms of severance — under authority of a writ signed with a seal I knew better than my own name.

The seal of the Vigil. Countersigned, in a hand I would have known anywhere, because I had once been required to copy it as part of my training in document authentication:

*K. Voss.*

Two years before his crusade. Two years before the plague in the river cities that he'd built his holy war on. Two years before he stood on the steps of the Bastion and swore before ten thousand people that magic was a rot in the world's flesh that must be cut away.

He hadn't responded to the plague. He'd *scheduled* it.

* * *

I don't remember sitting down. I remember coming back to myself with my back against a stone shelf, water soaking through my coat, the ledger open in my lap, and a sound in my ears that took me a moment to identify as my own breathing.

Let me tell you about righteous fury, since I've been on both sides of it. As a witch-finder, I was taught to think of fury as fuel — something you stoked carefully and aimed precisely. What they never told me is that real fury isn't hot at all. It's cold. It's the coldest thing there is. It's standing in dark water holding proof that the man who burned four hundred children in my jurisdiction alone did it not from conviction, not from fear, not even from ambition in any honest sense — but as *maintenance*. Housekeeping. The tidy removal of an obstacle two years in advance of needing it removed.

Because that's what the ledger showed, if you followed the thread. I followed it for three more days. I barely slept. Odile sent food down the stairs twice and I ate it without tasting it, and on the third day she came down herself, hiked up her skirts, and stood in the water beside me reading over my shoulder.

"Say it plain," she said. "I've been watching you make faces at paper for three days and I want the ugly version."

"The sigil wasn't broken," I said. "It was *cut*. Deliberately. By writ. Voss authorized the removal of the binding texts nine years ago — there's a whole chain of documents, requisitions, transport orders. They didn't burn anything. You don't burn this stuff." I tapped the ledger. "You can't burn the terms of a covenant and keep the power. You *sever* it. You cut the cord between the god and the land and you hold the loose ends yourself."

Odile was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere above us, water dripped from the vaulted ceiling into the flooded nave, patient as a metronome.

"He's been carrying it," she said finally. "All this time. The stolen voice."

"A piece of it. Enough." I flipped forward through the documents to the one I'd saved for last, the one I'd read forty times, the one that had made me stop sleeping. "Look at the routing. The texts weren't destroyed. They were moved to the Bastion's deep vault — the one under the Inquisitor's residence. Restricted access. His access. And then—" I turned the page. "Then nothing. For two years, nothing. And then the plague starts in the river cities, and Voss has his crusade, and every witch-finder in the world is burning hedge-charms while the actual source of divine power sits in his basement."

"And the god?" Odile asked. "Noor's god?"

"Wounded. Not dead." I thought about Noor's face when he'd come up from the shrine's heart, seven years of grief cracking open into something worse than grief. "You don't kill a god by cutting its voice away. You make it a prisoner in its own house. It's been down here screaming into the water for nine years, and everyone who ever prayed to it got silence, and everyone who died waiting for it—" My voice did something I didn't authorize. I let it. "Everyone who died waiting got called heroes of the faith by the man who cut the cord."

Odile put her hand on my shoulder. She didn't say anything, which was the correct pastoral decision, because I was not in a state to receive comfort. I was in a state to receive a target.

* * *

Here is the confession portion of tonight's program, since apparently we're doing honesty now:

I faked my death rather than burn a child. Everyone in our ragged little fellowship knows that story; it's practically my coat of arms. What I've said less often is *why* I was assigned that child in the first place. Because I was good. Because Rask and I had the highest compliance rate in the southern district, because I could find a hidden charm-seller through six layers of false walls, because when Kaelen Voss needed a job done cleanly, he sent Solde and Rask.

I told myself for years that the machine was broken and I was a good gear in a bad mechanism. That the rot entered the Vigil gradually, that decent people were overwhelmed, that history swept us along. It's a comfortable story. It lets you sleep.

But I'd seen the indices. I'd helped build them. And some part of me — the part I drowned along with my name — had always known that a system that thorough doesn't drift into atrocity. It *plans* it. I just hadn't known how far back the planning went, or that the man at the top of it had been robbing heaven itself while the rest of us policed the poor devils selling luck-charms in night markets.

Noor sold outlawed charms for seven years. Little things — a blessing against fever, a ward against bad roads. Half of them probably didn't work. He sold them anyway, to people desperate enough to pay, because his god had gone silent and he couldn't bear the silence and couldn't say so. And the whole time, the reason for that silence was sitting in a locked vault beneath a man who preached purity with stolen divinity in his pockets.

I want you to understand: I am not a passionate woman. Passion is inefficient. I have built my entire second life on the principle that feelings are weather and decisions are architecture. But I sat in that flooded archive with Voss's signature in my hands, and I understood something with total clarity, the way you understand a blade's edge by the cut:

Justice needs a target. Not a cause. Causes are how you get witch-finders. A target is a person, with a name, whose specific crimes have specific proofs. And I was holding the proofs.

* * *

I brought the key documents up wrapped in oilcloth, tucked inside my coat, against my ribs like a second heartbeat. Copies of copies — I'd transcribed the critical pages three times and hidden the transcripts in three different places aboard Odile's ship, because eleven years as an inquisitor teaches you that no evidence survives contact with fire unless it exists in more places than the fire can reach.

They were all in the common room when I came up: Noor at the table staring at nothing, Sef asleep against his arm, Odile pouring something medicinal into tin cups. Maren Solde, former instrument of the Vigil, dripping river water and carrying the end of the world in oilcloth.

"Well?" Noor said.

I laid the documents out on the table, careful of Sef's sleeping space. I walked them through it the way I'd walk a tribunal — chronology, provenance, chain of custody. I watched Noor's face as the timeline assembled itself: the cut, the two quiet years, the plague, the crusade. I watched him arrive at the arithmetic that his company had died holding a pass for a god whose voice had been stolen two years earlier, and that the man who'd ordered the theft had then spent seven years sending men like Rask to hunt the survivors of his own sabotage.

Noor didn't rage. I'd expected rage. Instead he went very still, the way water goes still right before it freezes, and said, "The shrine. The god knew. All this time, it knew, and it couldn't tell anyone, and I—" He stopped. Started again. "I cursed it. Every day for seven years, I cursed it for abandoning us."

"It couldn't answer," Odile said softly.

"It couldn't answer," Noor agreed, "because a man with a seal and a signature decided it shouldn't." He looked up at me, and there was something in his eyes I'd only seen once before, in a mirror, the night I burned my own death certificate. "Maren. How airtight is this? If we put it in front of the free cities council—"

"It's airtight enough to hang him," I said. "If we live long enough to present it. Which brings me to the problem."

"There's always a problem," Odile said, and drank her medicine.

"Voss knows these archives existed. He wrote the requisitions; he knows what's in them. The moment he learns anyone reached the shrine alive, he won't send witch-finders. He'll send the army. And he'll send someone who knows exactly how I think, because there's exactly one person alive who trained alongside me." I heard my own voice go flat and professional, the old register, the one I'd sworn off. "Rask."

Sef stirred against Noor's arm and opened his eyes — that unnervingly direct gaze he had, like someone much older looking out through a child's face. He looked at the documents spread across the table, at Voss's signature, and then at me.

"That's the man who writes the burning lists," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"That's the man who writes the burning lists," I confirmed.

Sef nodded slowly, the way children nod when they're adding a fact to a collection of facts that will someday harden into a worldview. Then he said, in the First Tongue, a word I didn't know, and Noor translated it without seeming to decide to — the language moving through him now like breath.

"*Reckoning,*" Noor said. "The word means reckoning."

Outside, far off across the drowned valley, a bird cried once and went silent, and the water in the shrine lapped against stones that had waited nine years for someone to read their testimony. I gathered up the documents, folded them back into their oilcloth, and felt the cold fury settle into something usable at last. Not a cause. A target. A name, a crime, a proof, and a road leading from here to the Bastion's door.

We'd need to move within days. I knew it the way you know weather in an old scar.

And three nights later, when the sentry's whistle went up from the shore and Odile's lookout shouted that there were soldiers on the ridge road — too many, in formation, flying the Vigil's gray — I was already awake, already armed, and not even slightly surprised to see a broad-shouldered silhouette at their head, walking with a limp I'd helped bandage years ago, carrying a white flag and wearing the smile of a man offering a bargain he fully intended to collect on.

"Rask," I said to no one, and checked the edge of my knife.
