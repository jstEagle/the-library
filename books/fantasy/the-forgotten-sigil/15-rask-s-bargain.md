## 15. Rask's Bargain

I have been in a great many bad places in my life — sieges, swamps, one memorable wedding — but the drowned shrine of Ashkarat was the first that made me nostalgic for all of them.

We'd been there four days. Four days of dripping stone and cold water to the knee, of Maren hauling armfuls of sodden archive-scrolls into the dry chamber she'd claimed as her study, of Sef sleeping curled against the base of the broken sigil like a cat against a warm hearth. The god's presence hummed through the flooded halls now, faint but unmistakable, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. It should have been comforting. Mostly it was. But a heartbeat means something can be killed, and I'd learned seven years ago what it does to a man when he starts counting the ways.

Odile had rigged lamps from ship's stores and salt-cured tallow, and she'd taken to holding evening rites in the antechamber with the water lapping at her ankles like a congregation that couldn't sing. She said the shrine liked it. She said the old pantheon had always preferred worship with wet feet.

"Gods are like sailors," she told me that last night, wringing out her sleeves. "They don't trust anyone who won't get in the water with them."

"Is that why you're down here? Getting in the water?"

"I'm down here because you lot would starve without me." She grinned, and the lamplight caught the silver rings in her ears — one for every ship she'd lost, she'd once said, though she'd never said how many ships that was. "Also because someone has to pray for your miserable soul, and Maren's prayers still sound like arrest warrants."

"She's getting better."

"She prayed for my forgiveness last week and then asked if I had any outstanding crimes she should know about."

"That's progress," I said. "For Maren."

It was, looking back, the last easy conversation we ever had. I want to be honest about that. I want to remember that the last ordinary hour was an ordinary hour, full of jokes about wet feet and Maren's terrible grace, because afterward everything got weighed against it, and things weigh differently when you know they're the last of their kind.

* * *

Rask came at dawn, or what passed for dawn forty feet underground, which is to say the lamps guttered and the water went strange and still, and Odile stopped mid-sentence with her head cocked like a gull hearing a storm two days out.

"What?" I asked.

"Boots," she said. "A lot of them."

Maren appeared from her archive-nest with a scroll still in hand, ink on her fingers, her face already doing the arithmetic I hated her for being able to do. "The tunnels under the causeway. That's the only approach wide enough for soldiers."

"How many is 'a lot'?" Sef asked. He'd woken without anyone noticing — the boy moved quiet as spilled sand — and his voice had that careful flatness he used when he was frightened and refusing to spend the word *frightened* on it.

"Enough," Maren said. She set down the scroll. "If they've come through the Gray Strait approaches, they'll have crossed Voss's own pickets. This isn't a patrol. This is an operation."

"And operations have officers," I said. "And officers have terms."

"You don't know that."

"Maren." I looked at her, and she looked back, and for once neither of us needed the professional shorthand. "You know who leads Voss's operations in this region. You've known since the toll-keeper's station. You just didn't say it out loud, because saying it out loud makes it true."

Her jaw tightened. "Rask," she said. "Yes. Probably Rask."

* * *

He came to the edge of the torchlight alone, which was either courtesy or theater, and with Rask it was always both.

I hadn't seen him since Maren's funeral-that-wasn't, three years ago, when I'd stood at the back of a crowd of witch-finders pretending to mourn a woman who was currently alive and standing six feet behind me with a crossbow. Rask had given the eulogy. He'd cried. I'd believed him, right up until I learned the truth, and I still didn't entirely know what to do with the fact that the tears had probably been real.

"Noor Marchetti." He spread his hands, showing them empty, which meant nothing; Rask could kill you with his thumbs. "You look terrible."

"You look promoted."

"Brevet colonel." He smiled with exactly half his mouth. He was grayer than I remembered, broader, the kind of man the Empire built well and often. Behind him, out in the dark of the flooded gallery, I could hear the wet slap of boots — dozens, maybe more, fanning out along the causeway with the unhurried discipline of people who already owned the exits. "And you look like a man standing in a hole with nowhere left to run. So let's skip the part where we insult each other's mothers and talk business."

"Talk."

"The edict names the child. Only the child." His eyes flicked past me, toward the shadows where Sef stood, and I stepped sideways without deciding to, and Rask's smile widened a fraction, cataloguing the movement. "Voss wants him alive, unharmed, and delivered. In exchange: full amnesty for everyone else. Solde walks free — her death stays dead, no questions asked. The smuggler keeps her ship and her blasphemies. And you—" he tilted his head, studying me like a ledger with a discrepancy in it, "—you get to walk away. Again. Which I understand is your particular talent."

Behind me, very quietly, Sef said something in the First Tongue. Two syllables. I didn't know the word, but I knew the shape of it, the way you know the shape of a knife in the dark.

"No," I said.

"You haven't heard the rest of—"

"There isn't a rest. There's a child, and there's an army, and you're asking me to choose, and I chose seven years ago when I buried thirty-one friends in a pass your emperor's kind never bothered to look at." My voice came out level, which surprised me. Somewhere underneath it, the old silence-prayer turned over in its sleep. *Help no one too much.* Seven years of that rule, worn smooth as a river stone, and here I was spending it all at once. "No."

Rask sighed, and for a moment — just a moment — the officer fell away and I saw the man who'd wept at a fake funeral, tired and honest and trapped in the same machine we'd all been born into. "Marchetti. I burned eleven sanctuaries before I stopped counting. I have orders and three hundred men and a writ that says whatever happens down here is the child's fault. You cannot win this. But you can survive it, and so can your friends, and that's more mercy than this empire usually sells." He paused. "Maren. Tell him. You know what I'm offering is real."

Maren stepped forward into the torchlight, and I watched Rask's face do something complicated.

"You gave a fine eulogy," she said.

"You gave a fine corpse. I'm sorry it didn't take."

"Take the offer, Rask. Walk away. Tell Voss the shrine collapsed."

"You know I can't."

"I know," she said. "That's why I'm telling you to try."

He looked at her for a long moment, and then at me, and then — worst of all — at Sef, with something almost gentle in his face, the gentleness of a man who has made peace with the arithmetic of burning children and calls it sorrow. "Last chance," he said. "For all of you."

"We'll keep it under consideration," Odile said brightly, and threw the lamp.

* * *

Here is what I remember, in the order my memory insists on keeping it, which is not the order it happened:

The lamp shattering on wet stone, oil spreading fire across the water in a sheet of blue-gold flame, and the gallery filling with screaming — not just men screaming, though there was plenty of that, but the shrine itself, the god's wounded hum rising to a note I felt in my teeth.

Maren's crossbow singing twice, fast, professional, dropping the two soldiers who'd flanked Rask before they cleared their scabbards.

Sef shouting — not words, not First Tongue, just a boy's raw terror — and the water answering, rising off the floor in a wall between us and the causeway, and the look on Rask's face as the wall came down, the look of a man whose worldview had just sprung a leak.

And Odile.

Odile, who had spent the whole conversation positioning herself near the causeway arch, who had thrown the lamp not as a distraction but as a signal, who had already decided — gods know when, maybe days ago, maybe years, maybe the day she first read Sef's palm and saw what he was — that if anyone was going to hold the door, it would be the priestess, because that was the whole job description, wasn't it, the whole ancient ridiculous sacrament of it: stand between the flock and the wolf, and if the wolf gets through, make sure it remembers the price.

She fought the way she sailed, all economy and audacity, cutlass in one hand and a boarding axe in the other, laughing — actually laughing — as she gave ground inch by inch down the tunnel mouth, buying us seconds with feet of stone. I screamed at her to come on. She waved at me with the axe, cheerful, obscene.

Then a soldier got inside her reach, and then another, and I saw the moment it stopped being a fight and became a transaction, and I lunged for her, and Maren caught my collar and hauled me back with a strength I will never understand, and the tunnel mouth filled with smoke and bodies and Odile Ferran's voice, ringing off the stones, clear as a bell over open water:

*"Go! The deep stairs — left of the altar — GO, you beautiful idiots, this is a sacrament and you're RUINING it—"*

* * *

We ran. That's the truth of heroism nobody puts in ballads. We ran, all of us, while she held the door, and the last thing I saw of her was firelight on silver earrings, one for every ship she'd lost, and I understood suddenly that she'd been counting wrong all along, or counting something else entirely, and that the number had always included us.

The deep stairs were narrow and black and slick, and we went down them in the dark with Sef's hand locked in mine, his small fingers crushing mine hard enough to hurt, and behind us, muffled by stone and water, the sounds of the fight thinned and scattered and finally stopped, and none of us said anything, because there was nothing to say that wouldn't be a lie yet.

We came out into a low vault where the water ran clear over pale stone, and the god's hum was louder here, closer, almost tender, and Sef sat down abruptly in the shallows and began to shake.

Maren reloaded her crossbow with hands that weren't quite steady. I stood in the dark listening to a child cry and a god hum and my own heart doing something ugly and arrhythmic in my chest.

"She knew," Sef said finally. His voice was wrecked. "She knew they were coming. Last night, when she was praying — she wasn't praying for us, Noor. She was praying *for herself*. Getting ready." He looked up at me, and his eyes were the lamplight color of the sigil, and they were furious and grieving and seven years old in every way that mattered. "Why didn't she tell us?"

Because telling us would have made us argue. Because arguing would have cost seconds. Because some people do their arithmetic in advance and pay quietly, and the rest of us find out at the funeral. I didn't say any of that.

"Because she was the captain," I said. "And captains don't ask permission to go down with the ship."

Above us, faint through fathoms of stone, boots were moving. Lots of them. Looking for the deep stairs.

Looking for us.

Maren met my eyes across the dark water, and she didn't need to say it: Rask hadn't come all this way to lose us in a hole. There would be another bargain, or another fight, and next time there would be no lamp to throw and no Odile to throw it.

But below us — below everything — the god's hum shifted, deepened, and I felt it tug at something in my chest like a hand on a rope, pulling downward, toward the shrine's drowned heart, and I understood that the door Odile bought with her life opened onto exactly one road, and it went down.

"Come on," I said, and took Sef's hand again, and we went to meet a wounded god.
