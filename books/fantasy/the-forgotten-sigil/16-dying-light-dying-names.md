## 16. Dying Light, Dying Names

There's an old sailor's saying: the sea takes you quick or she takes you slow, but either way she takes everything you're carrying. I always thought that was about drowning. Turns out it's about gut wounds too.

I'd been stabbed before — twice by pirates, once by a customs man with strong opinions about my cargo manifests — and I'd learned the difference between a wound you walk off and a wound you negotiate with. This one wanted to talk terms. The Rask's blade had gone in under my ribs on the left side, angled up, which is where a man who knows his work puts it if he means for you to die tired instead of fast.

So we were negotiating.

"Keep pressure on it," Maren said, for the fourth time since we'd stumbled into the tunnels beneath the shrine. She had my arm over her shoulders and was doing most of the work of keeping me upright, which stung worse than the knife, frankly. A smuggler-priestess carried like luggage by a disgraced witch-finder. My mother would have laughed herself into a fit. My mother also sold me to a tide-gang when I was nine, so her opinions had limited currency.

"I am keeping pressure on it," I said. "It's not listening."

The tunnels ran back from the drowned shrine like the roots of some enormous tree, dry stone above the floodline, slick black rock below it. Noor led us with a lantern he'd found hanging on a hook near the entrance, as if someone had expected visitors. Maybe someone had. Gods are sentimental about their real estate.

Sef walked beside me without being asked. He'd been beside me since the fight, since Odile Ferran made the tactical error of stepping between a trained killer and a child, and now he kept glancing up at me with those covenant-bright eyes like I was something worth looking at.

"Stop that," I told him.

"Stop what?"

"Looking at me like I'm dying."

"You *are* dying," he said, with the brutal honesty of children and the First Tongue both.

"Well. Yes. But there's a difference between dying and being *dying*, and the difference is dignity, and you're spending mine faster than the blood."

He didn't laugh. That worried me more than the wound did. Sef laughed at everything — at storms, at ship's biscuit, at Maren's face when she woke up cranky. A boy who doesn't laugh is a boy who understands exactly what's happening.

* * *

We stopped in a chamber where the ceiling opened into a natural dome and a crack in the stone let down a thread of gray daylight, thin as a prayer. Noor set the lantern on a flat rock and looked at me the way captains look at hulls they've decided not to repair.

"How bad?" he asked. He's a direct man, Noor. It's one of the three things I like about him. The other two are that he pays his debts and he once threw a witch-finder off my gangplank without asking questions first.

"Bad enough that we skip the part where you lie to me," I said. "I've heard your lying voice. It's terrible. You sound like a customs official."

Maren turned away and started checking the tunnel mouths with a soldier's thoroughness, giving us privacy the only way she knows how — by pretending to be busy somewhere else. Noor crouched down so we were eye to eye.

"Tell me what you need," he said.

What I needed was a surgeon, a priest of the Deep Currents, and about forty years. What I had was a lantern, a heretic, a child, and maybe an hour.

"A flask," I said. "And Sef. And everyone else out of earshot, including whatever god is eavesdropping."

Noor hesitated. Men like him always hesitate before leaving a dying woman alone; they think it's courtesy. I flapped my hand at him until he went, taking the lantern's light most of the way down the passage, and then it was just me and the boy and the thread of daylight and the wet sound of my own breathing.

"All right, minnow," I said. "Sit. We have work to do, and I intend to be insufferable about it."

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about keeping a faith alive alone: it gets heavy. Not the rites themselves — the rites are light, they're salt and water and words, a fisherman could carry them in one hand. What's heavy is being the only one who remembers them. Every dawn prayer said to an empty hold. Every feast day celebrated with a crew who thought it was just a strange dinner. Seven years of being the last priestess of a silenced pantheon, and I'll tell you a secret: half the time I wasn't sure I was remembering the old ways at all. Half the time I was inventing, guessing, doing what felt right and calling it tradition. Faith is mostly improvisation with good posture.

But some of it was real. Some of it came down through my grandmother, who got it from hers, all the way back past the Silence to when the gods still answered. And that real part — the salt rite, the naming of the deep hours, the blessing of thresholds — that part couldn't die with me in a hole under a mountain. I hadn't kept it alive this long to lose it to a man with a knife and a grudge.

"Sef," I said. "Do you know what an heir is?"

"Somebody who gets things when you die."

"Somebody who gets *obligations* when you die. Things are just how the obligations travel." I fumbled at my neck, where the cord bit into skin gone clammy, and pulled free the key ring. Six keys, iron and bronze, worn smooth by thirty years of my thumb. "These open the *Kindly Tide*. Every lock on her — the hold, the strongbox, the false bottom under the captain's cabin where I keep the good brandy and the better contraband. They're yours now."

"They're heavy," Sef said, taking them with both hands, solemn as a coronation.

"That's because ships are heavy. So are promises. Same weight class entirely." I watched him close his fingers around the keys and felt something loosen in my chest that had nothing to do with bleeding. "Now listen, because I'm only saying this once and I'm saying it slow, and if you drift off I will haunt you specifically, none of these other people, just you, forever."

"I'm listening."

"The rites of the Deep Currents aren't prayers the way the witch-finders think of prayers. They're not asking. They're *keeping*. You pour salt water at a threshold and you say the words, and what you're really saying is: somebody stood here before you, and somebody will stand here after, and the standing matters. That's the whole religion. Continuity. The gods went quiet but the standing never stopped, and that's why the covenant can be mended at all — because people like me refused to stop doing the small stupid thing every morning." I grinned at him, and tasted copper. "You're going to do the small stupid thing every morning. Congratulations. Terrible career choice."

"What are the words?"

I taught him. The dawn rite first, the oldest one, the one my grandmother called *opening the door for the tide*. Salt water in the palm, poured slow, and the words in the First Tongue — which, here's the miracle, he already knew. He'd known them before I said them. My mouth would shape the phrase and his would arrive half a breath ahead of me, like he was remembering rather than learning, and each time it happened I felt the hair rise on my arms. Vessel-key, Odile had called him. I'd thought it meant he could unlock the covenant. Watching him speak my grandmother's grandmother's words back to me, I understood it meant something simpler and stranger: he was a door, and everything lost could walk back through him.

"There's a feast day," I said, when the dawn rite sat settled in him. "Midwinter. You cook fish whole, with the bones in, because you don't get to be squeamish with the dead. You eat it standing up. You leave the last bite on the plate and you put the plate outside, and in the morning the bite is gone — gulls, probably, or cats, or the god, depending on your theology, and I've never seen a reason to choose. You'll do this. Even inland. Especially inland."

"Even inland," he repeated.

"And when you're scared — and you will be scared, minnow, you're going to be scared in ways I'm glad I won't see — you put your hand flat on wood. Any wood. Deck plank, door, table. Wood remembers the sea. All wood comes from ships eventually, if you go back far enough, and ships remember the water, and the water remembers the gods. Hand on wood, three breaths, and you'll remember too."

"That's not a real rite," Sef said. "You made that up."

"Of course I made it up," I said. "That's how rites are made. Somebody scared, a long time ago, needed something to do with their hands. Do you think the first person to pour salt at a threshold wasn't just frightened and improvising? Everything holy starts as somebody's best guess in the dark. The only difference between superstition and sacrament is whether anybody keeps doing it." I tapped his chest with two fingers, gently, above his heart. "So keep doing it. That's the whole job. Keep doing it until it's true, and then keep doing it after, because that's when it counts."

* * *

Maren came back at some point — I heard her boots, careful on the stone, and pretended not to. She stood at the edge of the lamplight with her arms crossed, wearing the expression of a woman who has administered last rites professionally and hates doing it personally.

"You should rest," she said.

"I should do a great many things. Rest is low on the list." I beckoned her closer. "Come here. I need you to witness something, and you're the only one present with any legal training, however disgraced."

"Witness what?"

"Him." I nodded at Sef. "Odile Ferran, priestess of the Deep Currents, keeper of the *Kindly Tide*, does hereby name Sef-of-the-sanctuary her heir in all things: vessel, rites, keys, debts, and grudges. The grudges transfer at half value. Most of them are against customs men anyway and he can renegotiate." I took Sef's hand and pressed it against the key ring, then lifted my other hand toward Maren. "Witnessed?"

Maren's jaw worked. For a moment she looked like she might argue — she's an arguer, our inquisitor, she treats feelings like faulty testimony — and then she knelt, actually knelt, on the wet stone, and took my hand in both of hers.

"Witnessed," she said. "Before the old gods and the new silence and whatever's listening down here. Witnessed and binding."

"Overruled objections noted for the record."

"I didn't object."

"You wanted to. Your eyebrow objected. It always objects." I squeezed her hands, harder than I should have been able to. "Listen to me, Solde. You quit the wrong job and you haven't forgiven yourself, and you think guarding that boy is penance. It isn't. It's inheritance too. You're part of what he keeps. All of you are — the broken mercenary, the runaway inquisitor, the whole ragged fleet of us. That's what family is when you build it late: everybody inherits everybody."

"Odile—"

"Don't make speeches at me, I'm the one dying, I get the speeches." But my voice cracked on *dying*, damn it, and Maren heard it, and she did the kindest thing anyone's done for me in years: she didn't pretend otherwise. She just stayed kneeling and held on.

* * *

After she went to check on Noor, Sef climbed up beside me and tucked himself under my arm, careful of the wound, the way you learn to be careful of wounded animals and wounded sailors. The thread of daylight had gone from gray to gold. Evening coming, or morning — down here you take your light where it falls and don't ask its schedule.

"Will it hurt?" he asked. "At the end."

"Minnow, it already hurts. The end is just the part where I stop filing complaints." I felt him shiver and pulled him closer. "Hey. Look at me. Seven years ago the gods went silent and everyone said the world ended. It didn't. It just got lonelier. And you know what I learned, running contraband through a godless world? Loneliness breaks before people do. People keep. People find each other in the dark and call it fate and it works fine. I found you. I found a mercenary with a martyr complex and an inquisitor with a conscience. Worst crew I ever sailed with. Best one too."

"Is that a rite?" Sef whispered. "What you're doing now?"

"This?" I laughed, and it cost me, and it was worth it. "This is the oldest rite there is, minnow. Somebody dying tells somebody living what mattered. Then the living keep doing it. That's the whole chain. That's the covenant underneath the covenant." I pressed the key ring closed in his fist one more time. "Keep doing it."

His eyes were bright and wet and full of the First Tongue, and just before the light went soft at the edges, I heard him say the closing words of the dawn rite over me — *the door stands open, the tide comes home* — in a voice that sounded, for one impossible moment, like every priestess who ever said it, mine and my grandmother's and all the forgotten ones, all speaking through one small stubborn boy on a stone floor under a mountain.

Good, I thought. That'll hold.

Then the dark came in on the evening tide, and I let it carry me, the way you're supposed to, hand on wood, three breaths, and out.

* * *

They tell me — well, nobody tells me anything anymore, but I know my crew, and I know what they did — they laid me in the flooded gallery below, where the black water runs clear over the old stones, and gave me to the Deep Currents with salt and the words Sef now owns. Somewhere a gull or a cat or a god took the last bite.

And somewhere deeper, past the drowned doors, Noor Marchetti stood at the edge of the molten sigil with a god's voice rising in the dark like a tide finally turning — and the price of the crossing written plain before him, and only one coin in his pocket that the fire would take.

His name.

Sail easy, mercenary. The door stands open.
