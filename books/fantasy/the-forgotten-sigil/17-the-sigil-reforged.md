## 17. The Sigil Reforged

The water remembers what I've spent seven years trying to forget.

I stand at the edge of the shrine's heart, where the flooded nave opens into a chamber no architect built — a hollow in the world itself, lit by the pale gold of the sigil's last ember burning beneath the water. It hangs there, suspended in the deep like a dying star, cracked down the middle, its light pulsing slow as a wounded heart. Seven years of silence, and here is the wound that made it. Here is the cord someone cut.

Behind me, my family waits at the waterline. Maren with her sword drawn out of habit, though there's nothing left to fight but grief. Sef with his hands pressed to his mouth, eyes wide and wet, the First Tongue moving behind his lips without sound — a prayer, or a warning, or both.

And Odile's keys, cold against my chest where Sef hung them this morning before we descended. *Something worth keeping,* he'd said, in her cadence, with her stubbornness. The boy inherits grief the way other children inherit eye color.

"You don't have to do this," Maren says for the fourth time. Her voice echoes strangely off the water, comes back changed, as if the shrine is already editing what we say. "There has to be another way."

"There isn't." I say it gently, because she deserves gentleness, because she burned her whole life to get us here. "I asked the god. I asked it three times. It doesn't lie — that was always the one thing about it. It never lied." A breath. "It needs a name given freely. Not taken, not stolen, not bargained. Given. And it has to be mine, because I'm the one who stopped believing it would answer."

"Noor—"

"That's the point," I say, and hear how tired my voice sounds, how old. "That's the whole point of it. You can't give a name you're holding onto."

* * *

Let me tell you what a name is, since I'm about to lose mine.

A name is the first thing they take from you when they make you a soldier. In the company, we were all handles and half-names — mine shortened, worn smooth like a river stone from seven years of use. Noor. Just Noor. And underneath it, deeper, the full thing, the whole weight of it, the name my mother gave me over a birthing bed in a village whose name I also carry and will also lose tonight, and her mother's name before that, going back and back like roots into dark soil.

A name is a debt. Every person who ever spoke mine holds a piece of it. My dead comrades shouting it across a pass that ate them alive. Maren saying it like a question she hasn't finished asking. Sef saying it like an answer. When I speak it into the sigil, all of those pieces come due at once. That's the price. Not death — death would be simpler, cheaper, the kind of bargain mercenaries understand. This is worse. This is being unwritten.

And the terrible thing — the thing I'll never admit to anyone after tonight, because there won't be anyone who knows me well enough to accuse me of it — is that some part of me wants it. Seven years of carrying a dead man's guilt and a living man's silence. Seven years of flinching every time someone said my name like it might be the last time it meant anything. There's a mercy in erasure that I'm ashamed to want.

But wanting isn't why I'm doing it. I want it on the record, whoever's keeping records after tonight: I'm doing it because a child crossed a city full of witch-finders to find a stranger's door, speaking words in a language only the dead remember, and he chose *me*. Because a woman faked her own death rather than burn children and then chose to live again for ours. Because a smuggler-priestess bled out in the dark so we could reach this room. Because a god went silent rather than let its voice be used as a weapon, and waited seven years in the drowning dark for someone foolish enough to come back.

You don't abandon people like that twice. Once was the company. Never again.

* * *

I wade in.

The water is warm. That's the detail that undoes me — not the light, not the singing pressure of the place, but the warmth, like bathwater, like blood, like something that has been waiting so long to be touched that it can't help running hot. The god's presence rises around me as I go deeper, and it's not silence anymore. It's the opposite of silence. It's every word it never said, held back for seven years, pressing against the inside of the moment.

*I'm sorry,* it says, and does not use words, and I understand it anyway. *I could not speak. He took my voice and left me watching.*

"I know," I say aloud, and my voice cracks on the second word. "I know. I spent seven years hating you for it."

*Yes.* Something like sorrow moves through the water, through my bones. *I felt every hour of it. I am sorry for that too. Grief was all I could give you, and I gave it badly.*

"Yeah, well." I laugh, and it comes out broken, bubbles rising past my face toward a surface that seems very far away now. "You should meet my family. We're all terrible at it."

The ember of the sigil hangs before me, close enough to touch. Up close I can see the crack runs all the way through, and inside the crack there's darkness — not empty darkness but *hungry* darkness, the shape of everything Voss stole, everything the land has been starving for. The molten edges pulse. Waiting. It has been waiting so long.

*It must be your name,* the god says. *Freely given. I cannot take it. If you hold even one thread of it back, the forging fails, and there will not be another chance. Voss's host reaches the free cities within days.*

"I know."

*And you understand what you lose?*

I think about lying to a god. Old habits. Then I think better of it.

"I understand that tomorrow morning I won't know why these people look at me the way they do. I won't know why the boy cries when he sees me. I won't know why a woman I don't remember follows me around with a sword and an expression like she's waiting for me to break. I understand that everyone I love becomes a stranger, and I become a stranger to myself, and the only proof any of it happened will be other people's memories." I swallow, and the water tastes like iron and old incense. "I understand that I'm about to find out whether love counts for anything if the lover can't remember loving."

*It counts,* the god says, with a certainty that shakes me more than the deep water does. *It is the only thing that has ever counted. Everything else is decoration.*

"Then let's decorate."

* * *

I put my hand into the ember.

It doesn't burn. That's the last cruelty of it — I braced for pain, for fire, for something to match the size of the sacrifice, and instead there's only warmth, the same impossible warmth as the water, as if the sigil recognizes me, as if it's been waiting for exactly this hand. The molten metal flows up over my fingers like it's greeting them.

*Speak,* the god breathes. *All of it. Every part. Hold nothing.*

So I open the door in myself that I've kept locked for thirty years, the one where the whole name lives, and I speak.

I speak my mother's voice saying it over me, and the sound of rain on a roof I'll never remember. I speak the village, the street, the year. I speak the childhood nickname only two people ever used, both dead. I speak the name the company knew, and the names I called myself in the bad years, the false ones I wore crossing borders, and the god takes them all, peels them away like wet cloth, and asks for more. I speak the name Maren uses. I speak the way Sef says it — soft, careful, like something rescued. I hold that one longest. The god waits. It lets me hold it as long as I need.

Then I let that one go too.

The world goes white and quiet.

Not silent — I know silence intimately, seven years of it, and this is not that. This is quiet the way a house is quiet when everyone you love is asleep under its roof. The sigil blazes whole above me, the crack sealed with something bright and mortal, and through it pours the god's voice, returned, enormous, filling the drowned shrine, filling the water, filling me —

— filling a man I no longer recognize.

Because that's the truth of it, the thing no one warned me about: the forgetting doesn't happen before. It happens *after*, in the space of a single heartbeat, and from the inside it feels like falling upward. I feel the name leave me. I feel the covenant take root in the gap it leaves, roots running down through me into the land itself, and the land *wakes*. I feel it in my teeth, in the soles of my feet, in the water — every river remembering its course, every stone remembering its weight, every oath ever sworn to soil and season standing back up from seven years of kneeling.

And I feel the god looking at me with something new in it. Recognition without memory. Gratitude without debt.

*What is your name?* it asks — not cruelly, but because the covenant must know, because the ledger must balance.

I reach for it and find nothing. A shape where a word used to be. An empty doorway with the wind coming through.

"I don't know," I say, and my voice sounds strange to me, unowned, a stranger's voice in a stranger's mouth. "I don't know who I am."

*You are the one who gave,* the god says. *For now, that will have to be enough.*

* * *

They pull me out of the water.

Hands — many hands, small ones and scarred ones — hauling me onto stone, and I cough up shrine-water and look up into faces I don't know. A boy with mud-colored hair crying like his heart is breaking, gripping my arm hard enough to bruise. A woman with a sword and tears cutting tracks through the grime on her face, saying a name over and over — my name, apparently, though it lands on me like a coin thrown to a beggar, meaningful, foreign.

"He did it," the boy is saying, to the woman, to the water, to the blazing light pouring up through the flooded nave behind me. "Maren, he did it, the sigil's whole, can't you feel it, the whole land can feel it—"

And I do feel it, whatever I am. The stone under me hums. The water sings. Somewhere far above, I'd swear the sky is changing color.

The boy turns back to me, wipes his face with his sleeve, and looks at me with an expression I can't read — grief and joy and something fierce and determined underneath, like a decision being made.

"It's all right," he tells me, in a language I somehow understand without knowing how, a language old as the ground. "You don't remember us. That's okay. We remember you." He takes a breath. "We'll teach you. Everything. I promise."

The woman — Maren, the boy called her — puts her sword away and sits down beside me on the wet stone, close enough that our shoulders touch. She doesn't say anything. She just stays, which seems like the kind of thing a person does for someone they've decided to keep.

Above us, through the shrine's drowned throat, the sky goes gold, then green, then a color with no name in any tongue I know — though something in my chest, some tenantless room, stirs at the sight of it, as if the walls remember a tenant.

Far away, I understand without knowing how I understand, an army is reaching walls it will never breach. Far away, a usurper is feeling his stolen power turn in his hands like a snake waking. Far away, oaths are standing up and demanding their due.

And here, on wet stone in a drowned church, a boy starts talking. Telling me a story. A story about a mercenary who sold outlawed charms in a night market, and a child at his door, and a road through hanged kings, and a god who came home.

"Once," the boy says, "there was a man named Noor—"

And the name means nothing to me.

But the way he says it — careful, soft, like something rescued — makes me want to learn.
