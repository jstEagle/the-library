## 20. Aftermath of Ash

The dead are easier to count than the living.

I learned that arithmetic young, in the witch-finders' academy, where they taught us to tally pyres the way quartermasters tally grain. Two hundred and eleven burned in Vell during the Purge Years. I wrote every name myself, in a ledger I was supposed to destroy when I faked my death, and didn't. Some habits are sins you keep.

Now I stood on the seawall with a different list, and it was shorter, which felt like an obscenity.

Odile Ferran. Buried at sea with her ship's keys in her hand and salt wine poured over the rail — Sef's hands shook so badly pouring it that half went into the water and half onto his own boots, and Noor — the man who had been Noor, who was now a tall stranger with kind eyes and no past — steadied the boy's wrists without being asked, as if some part of him remembered how to be gentle even if he couldn't remember why.

Rask. Dead by my hand, or by his own severed oaths, depending on which theologian you ask. The free cities' new council will argue about that for years. Let them. I know what happened: I put a knife between the ribs of the man who taught me how to hold one, and I have not slept a full night since, and I do not regret it, and those three facts refuse to fit inside each other no matter how I arrange them.

Two hundred and eleven names in the old ledger. Forty-one new ones from the battle at the walls. One smuggler-priestess who died buying us thirty seconds in a tunnel, and would have laughed herself sick to hear me call it a sacrifice. She'd have said something like, *Solde, everything's a trade. I just got the better end of it.*

The tide came in gray and patient beneath my feet. Somewhere out there, under a fathom of cold water, Odile was keeping her final appointment with the pantheon she'd never stopped serving. I hoped they were taking notes. She ran the best illegal liturgy in the Free Coast, and gods owe their clergy better than silence.

* * *

Vell wore its scars the way old soldiers wear theirs — badly, and with pride.

I walked the city three days after the burial, because walking is what I do instead of praying, and because someone had to see it whole. The breach in the east wall where Voss's host had hammered for two days before the sky changed. The market district, half-burned, already sprouting scaffolding like stubborn weeds. The witch-finders' garrison, its doors broken open, its records stacked in the square for anyone to read — and people did read them, hour after hour, faces going grayer with each page. My old reports were in there somewhere. My handwriting. The names I'd been ordered to collect and the ones I'd invented to buy time for the real families. Both kinds now lay open to the weather.

A woman recognized me on the third day. She stood across the square with a loaf of bread clutched to her chest like a shield and stared at me with an expression I couldn't parse until she crossed the space between us and said, "You're the one who quit."

"I'm the one who ran," I said.

"Same thing, from where I stood." She tore the loaf in half and gave me the bigger piece, and walked away before I could thank her, which was correct. That's how mercy works when it's done right. It doesn't wait around to be acknowledged.

Magic was decriminalized now — the edicts struck through with the same ink that had signed them, the pyres dismantled stone by stone and carted off to rebuild the east wall, which I thought was the single most poetic act of civic engineering I'd ever witnessed. But decriminalized is not the same as welcome. I watched a charm-seller set up in the night market — a nervous young woman with Noor's old stall, actually, or near enough to it — and I watched the crowd give her a wide berth, the way you step around a dog that might bite. Not hatred. Arithmetic. Everyone in Vell had done the sums: magic had saved the city, and magic had nearly destroyed it, and both things were true, and truth like that doesn't settle. It just gets carried.

Sef understood this better than any of us. He was nine years old and he'd unbound a usurper's stolen divinity in front of ten thousand soldiers, and afterward he'd asked me, very quietly, if people were afraid of him now.

"Some," I said. Lying to children is a sin I've given up.

"Is that bad?"

"It's a weight," I said. "Weights aren't good or bad. They just tell you what you're carrying."

He considered that with the terrible seriousness of a child who has recently ended a war. "Noor carries weights too. He just can't remember whose they are."

Out of the mouths of vessels-keys. I had no answer for that, so I bought him a meat skewer from a vendor who flinched at the boy's accent — First Tongue syllables slipping out when he wasn't watching — and we ate in silence while the city hammered itself back together around us.

* * *

The council summoned me on the fifth day.

Not me specifically — "the heroes of the walls," which is what they were calling us, a phrase that made Noor's nameless successor look physically pained whenever he heard it. The summons came on good paper with a wax seal, delivered by a clerk who wouldn't meet my eye, and I knew what it was before I broke the seal. I've sat on tribunals. I know how institutions say thank you: they give you a chair.

The chamber still smelled of smoke. Half the seats were empty — dead delegates, fled delegates, delegates who'd chosen the wrong side and were now being *very* apologetic about it — and the ones who remained looked at me the way councils always look at useful soldiers: gratefully, and with one hand already reaching for the leash.

"Maren Solde," said the chairman, a shipping magnate named Corvane who'd funded the wall repairs and therefore believed he owned the peace. "Or should we say, the woman who died at Kellin Ford?"

"You should say whatever's cheapest," I said. "Councils respect economy."

There was laughter from the back benches, quickly smothered. Corvane pressed on. "The cities require leadership. Someone who understands the old order well enough to dismantle it properly. Someone the people trust." He slid a document across the table. A seat. A title. Authority over the dissolution of the witch-finders, the registry of practitioners, the whole apparatus of turning miracle into bureaucracy.

I looked at the paper for a long moment. And here is the thing I want recorded, whoever finds this account: I was tempted. Not by power — power and I parted ways years ago, badly, in a room that smelled of lamp oil and burning children. I was tempted by the ledger. By the chance to strike through all two hundred and eleven names myself, officially, with witnesses. To make the institution that made me apologize in writing.

But institutions don't apologize. Institutions *delegate*. They would have handed me the pen and the guilt and the paperwork, and in five years I'd have been Corvane with a sadder backstory, and the registry would have grown its own teeth, because registries always do. I've seen how lists begin. I used to write them.

"No," I said.

Corvane blinked. "Mistress Solde—"

"The answer's no. Put your seat back in its box." I stood. "You want to dismantle the witch-finders? Here's how: burn the ledgers publicly, pay reparations to the survivors, and then dissolve the office so thoroughly that in twenty years nobody remembers what the seal looked like. Don't build a replacement. Don't build anything. You want to know who guards the free cities?" I pointed my thumb at my own chest, and then, absurdly, toward the window, toward the streets. "People. The ones who fed each other during the siege. The ones who read the records in the rain. Start there. Institutions come later, if they must, and they should come ashamed."

I walked out before they could vote on my insubordination. Old habit. Never give a tribunal time to organize.

* * *

Sef was waiting for me outside, sitting on the steps with his knees drawn up, and beside him sat the man who had been Noor Nystrom.

I still don't know what to call him. We tried, in the first days. Sef called him Noor and watched hopefully for flickers of recognition; there were none, only politeness, which hurt worse. Odile's crew had called him captain, jokingly, once, and the joke died in the air. Now we mostly didn't call him anything, and he bore our namelessness the way he seemed to bear everything — patiently, with a slight tilt of the head, like a man listening to music from another room.

"The council offered you a seat," he said. Not a question. Word travels fast in a city rebuilding itself; gossip is load-bearing infrastructure.

"They did."

"And?"

"I told them to start with bread and public book-burnings."

He smiled. He has a good smile, the stranger does — warm, unguarded, belonging to a man with nothing to protect. Sometimes I catch myself grieving the version of him that would have answered that remark with something dry and self-lacerating, and then I hate myself a little, because the grief is selfish. He isn't suffering. That's the horror and the grace of it. He walks through the world without the seven years of silence on his back, without the pass at Graywater, without the company he lost, and he is *lighter*, and I miss a man who is not gone but redistributed.

"Sef says you refused," the stranger said.

"Sef talks too much."

"He says you told them rebuilding begins with people, not institutions."

"I also told them to pay reparations. That part they'll ignore."

Sef looked up at me. Nine years old, mud-caked the first time I saw him, and now scrubbed and solemn and wearing Odile's old compass on a cord around his neck, because she'd left it to him along with the rites and the keys and the whole impossible inheritance of a drowned faith. "Are you staying?" he asked.

And there it was. The question underneath the question, the way children ask things — no ornament, no diplomacy, just the raw wire of it. Are you staying. Are you ours. Am I about to be handed to a committee.

I sat down on the steps between them. From up here you could see most of Vell: the scaffolding, the market stalls, the east wall rising stone by salvaged stone, the harbor where a certain ship sat at anchor with no priestess and a crew who kept lighting lamps in the hold out of habit. You could see the night market setting up, the charm-seller arranging her wares, the crowd beginning — slowly, warily, one customer at a time — to approach her anyway.

"I'm a disgraced inquisitor with a fake death, a real kill count, and a talent for making governments uncomfortable," I said. "Nobody's lining up to hire me."

"That's not an answer," Sef said.

"No," I agreed. "It's a preamble." I looked at the boy who spoke the language of gods, and at the man who'd traded his name to keep that language alive, and I thought about Odile saying *everything's a trade*, and I found, to my complete lack of surprise, that I'd already made mine. "Yes. I'm staying. Somebody has to guard the covenant's living key, and apparently the god trusts me with it, which speaks poorly of the god's judgment and well of my retirement plans."

Sef grinned and leaned against my arm, briefly, the way children do when they've gotten what they wanted and don't want to seem greedy about it. The stranger laughed softly.

"What's so funny?" I asked him.

"Nothing. It's just—" he tilted his head again, that listening gesture. "Something in me agrees with you. About guarding him. Like I promised someone once." He frowned, chasing it, and let it go. "I don't remember who."

"You'll earn it twice," Sef said, with the absolute certainty of a child quoting scripture nobody else has heard yet. "That's what the god whispers. Names can be earned twice. So can promises."

* * *

Evening came down over Vell the color of woodsmoke, and I climbed to the roof of the house we'd taken — a narrow thing near the old night market, rented with prize money I didn't feel entitled to, its doorstep newly rebuilt because a certain boy insisted on doing it properly, mortar and all.

From the roof I could see the harbor, and beyond it the open water where Odile slept. I poured a measure of salt wine over the tiles — my ration of ritual, borrowed shamelessly from a dead priestess's hold — and I made my report to the only congregation I answer to anymore.

"City's standing," I told the sea. "Ledgers burned. Pyres down to their foundations. Your boy is learning his letters in two languages and teaching the rest of us the First Tongue one word at a time, whether we consent or not. Your nameless friend fixed the door today. Fixed it like he'd done it before, which I suppose he has, somewhere in the part of him that got spent." The wine ran thin and dark between the tiles. "We're keeping them. Both of them. Consider your accounts settled."

Far out on the water, a light answered — one of the ship's lamps, or a star low over the horizon, or a wounded god acknowledging receipt. With Odile's pantheon, you learned not to demand itemization.

Below me, faintly, I heard Sef's voice on the rebuilt doorstep, patient and clear, teaching the stranger a word. Just one word tonight. I couldn't make out which one from up there, but I heard the stranger repeat it — clumsy, careful, getting it almost right — and heard Sef laugh, that bright unguarded sound that had become, without any of us voting on it, the truest bell in the free cities.

Rebuilding begins with people, I'd told the council, and meant it as a rebuke.

Listening to that laughter, I understood it as a promise instead. The institutions would come, eventually, shambling and embarrassed, and they would find the work already done — doors mended, ledgers burned, a guarded child asleep behind a rebuilt threshold, a family assembled from deserters and ghosts and one very tired woman with a knife she hoped never to use again.

Let them find it. Let them take notes.

Tomorrow there'd be more ash to sort, more names to carry, more of the mistrustful arithmetic that a decade of pyres leaves in a city's bones. Tomorrow the council would send another clerk with another seal, and I'd refuse again, less politely.

But tonight the word on the doorstep sounded almost right, and the lamp in the hold was still burning, and the god — returned, restored, remembering — whispered something over the rooftops of Vell that I chose, deliberately, not to translate.

Some things you guard best by letting them be.
