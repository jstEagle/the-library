## 2. The Crown That Eats

The fall through the gate was not a fall. It was an *unbalancing* — the sensation Zephyrine knew best in all the world, the moment when a column of figures refused to sum, when the ledger's left side and right side stared at each other across a gap that no honest arithmetic could bridge.

He landed on stone that breathed.

That was his first coherent thought, lying cheek against pale rock veined with slow pulses of amber light: *the ground here has a pulse.* His second thought was of the ledger page still crumpled in his fist, the prophecy inked a century before his birth, naming tonight's date and beneath it, in a scribe's careful hand: *Zephyrine Thornbury, Keeper.*

He had meant to burn it. The gate had disagreed.

He rose slowly, and the air rose with him — thick, warm, tasting faintly of copper and old incense, like the Gilded Meridian's counting hall on the eve of an audit. But there was something under that familiarity, something wrong the way a balanced book can be wrong: every number correct, and yet the whole of it a lie.

Above him, where a sky should have been, hung a ceiling of darkness shot through with drifting embers, as if night itself had caught fire and forgotten how to burn out. And at the center of that vast dark hall — for hall it was, a cavernous space built on a scale that made the Spire's gate behind him look like a servant's door — stood a throne of black iron wrapped in chains of gold.

On the throne sat a king.

Zephyrine knew kings. He had entered their debts into the ledgers, tallied their tributes, noted with professional detachment which ones paid in coin and which in favors better left unrecorded. This one wore a crown of woven flame — or what had once been flame, now dimmed to the color of coals an hour after the fire dies. The man beneath it was hollowed, cheeks sunken, eyes fixed on nothing, hands resting open on the throne's arms in the posture of someone who had long ago stopped trying to hold anything.

He was watching. That was the horror of it. He was watching everything, and he could do nothing at all.

* * *

What the king watched was the dragon.

It lay chained across a dais of cracked obsidian perhaps fifty paces from the throne — a creature that should have been magnificent and was instead a study in elegant ruin. Its scales, once surely the green of deep summer forests, had gone gray as ash, dull as old pewter. Chains of the same gold as the throne's ran from its limbs into sockets in the stone, and from its chest — from the hollow between its ribs, where fire should have lived — thin ribbons of light were being drawn upward, thread by luminous thread, toward the crown.

Toward the thing wearing the crown.

Zephyrine understood then, with the cold clarity of a man reading a fraudulent account for the first time, that the crown was not an object on the king's head. The crown was the *inhabitant*. The king was the house it occupied, and houses could be emptied.

The threads of dragon-fire wound up through the dark air and sank into the crown's dim weave, and with each thread the flames brightened by the smallest degree — and the dragon dimmed. He could see it happening, a transaction conducted in plain sight, debit and credit, and the ledger-keeper in him recoiled even as he catalogued it: *one thread of fire, drawn; one flicker of false life, given. Repeat until the source is spent.*

"No," Zephyrine said aloud, and his voice sounded absurdly small in that vastness. "No, that's not — the gate is supposed to keep it *in*. It's supposed to be caged. That's what we were told. That's what I was told."

"It was," said a voice behind him. "And you were told correctly, if you understand what 'kept' means."

* * *

She stepped out of the shadow of a broken pillar: a woman perhaps fifty, gray-streaked hair bound back with a scribe's cord, ink stains permanent on her fingers the way they were permanent on his own. She wore the faded robes of the Meridian's scribal order — the order Zephyrine had trained under, the order whose masters had taught him that a ledger was a form of truth.

Around her neck, on a chain, hung a broken stylus.

"Odile," she said, when she saw him recognize the robes. "Formerly senior prophecy-scribe. Formerly, before I read too carefully and asked too loudly." Her eyes went to the crumpled page in his fist. "You found your entry, then. A century early, we hoped. Right on time, it seems."

"You're exiled," Zephyrine said. "There's a notation. In the personnel rolls. 'Dismissed for doctrinal error.'" He had copied that notation himself, years ago, into the master index, without ever wondering what doctrine she'd erred against.

"I erred against the ledger," Odile said. "The true one." She gestured at the chamber, at the throne, at the dying dragon, and her voice did not shake, though Zephyrine thought it must have cost her something not to. "You balance accounts for the Gilded Meridian, Keeper. So balance this one. What comes *through* the gate?"

"Tribute," Zephyrine said automatically. "Offerings. Rare things. Impossible things. Once a season—"

"Once a season, yes. And what are impossible things made of?" She didn't wait for him to answer. "Fire. Memory. Name. Years. Everything the Meridian trades in is some form of burning — things consumed elsewhere so their worth can be counted here. And the crown eats exactly that. The guild didn't build the gate to imprison it, Zephyrine. You don't build a door into a prison and then walk tribute through it four times a year. They built it to *feed* it. The gate is a mouth. We are the hand that feeds it."

The words landed in him like stones dropped into still water, each ripple worse than the last. He thought of the offering ceremonies he'd witnessed from the gallery above the counting hall — the sealed reliquaries, the vials of captured memory, the single white flame kept in a lantern of glass that the Masters carried down to the gate room with such reverence. He had assumed reverence. He had assumed imprisonment required provisioning, that even a caged god needed tending.

He had been balancing the books of a feeding trough.

"The dragon," Zephyrine said. His voice came out rough. "It's the last one?"

"The last in this world. There were seven when the crown first crossed over, a hundred years ago. Your guild's founding coincides rather precisely, if you care to check the dates." Odile's mouth twisted. "I checked. Doctrinal error, remember."

On the dais, another thread of fire drew up out of the dragon's chest, and the great head lifted — barely, an inch, the way a drowning man lifts his face toward air — and its eyes found Zephyrine across the distance. They were old eyes. Amber, banked, nearly out. And in them he saw something that undid him more than any accusation: recognition. Not of his face. Of the *ledger page*. As if the dragon had read the same prophecy a century ago and had been waiting, chained, for the name at the bottom of it to arrive.

"He knows why you've come," Odile said quietly. "He's known longer than either of us."

"Why *have* I come?" Zephyrine's hand tightened around the page. "I'm a keeper. I keep accounts. I don't—" He looked at the hollowed king, at the open hands that had forgotten how to close. "I don't free anything. That's not in my duties."

"No," Odile agreed. "Your duties are to record. To tally. To carry the ledgers that say the gate is a cage and the tribute is a kindness and the crown is contained." She stepped closer, and up close he could see the ink stains went all the way to her wrists, as if she had once tried to scrub them off entirely and failed. "But the prophecy doesn't name you as keeper, Zephyrine. Read your entry again. All of it. Every line."

He unfolded the page with hands that were not quite steady. The old ink had faded to brown, but the final line beneath his name was still legible, and he had skipped it in the counting room because it was not a date or a designation, and keepers skip what cannot be filed:

*The one who keeps the books shall learn what the books were for.*

Behind them, deep in the iron throat of the gate, came a sound Zephyrine knew intimately from the other side: the hum of the Meridian's mechanisms engaging. Someone was opening the door.

Odile went very still. "They felt the unscheduled crossing," she said. "They're coming through. Enforcers. And they will not be surprised to find you here, Keeper — only to find whether you kneel."

The dragon's amber eyes closed, opened again. Between its ribs, the last visible coil of fire pulsed once, twice — like a heart deciding.

"They'll want me to sign the season's tribute ledger," Zephyrine said numbly. "Right here. On the feeding floor."

"Yes," said Odile. "And whatever you write in it next will be the truest thing the Gilded Meridian has recorded in a hundred years. Choose your ink."
