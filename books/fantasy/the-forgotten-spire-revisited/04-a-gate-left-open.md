## 4. A Gate Left Open

The crown came off the way a splinter leaves flesh — slowly, with blood, with a sound too small for what it cost.

Zephyrine watched from the ritual circle's scorched rim as the unbound king knelt on the obsidian dais, his hands rising to the thing that had worn him for forty years. Up close, freed of the glamour the guild's ledgers had always described in flattering terms, the crown was less an object than an appetite: black metal that moved like water, filigree that writhed when looked at directly, hungry even now, even dying. The king's fingers — human fingers again, trembling, cracked nails, the knuckles of a man who had once been a swordsman and then been furniture — closed around its band.

"You could keep it," Odile said quietly beside him. Her scribe's robes were ash to the elbow, her voice raw from the incantation that had taken three hours and most of her remaining years. "Unbound, you could set it down and walk away whole. Or you could wear it until it wears you. The choice was always yours. That's what they buried."

The king laughed. It was a terrible sound, rusted from disuse, but real.

"Keeper," he said, and Zephyrine realized he meant him. "You balance accounts. Tell me — what is a kingdom owed, when the ledger says it was spent feeding this?"

Zephyrine had no answer that would fit in a column. He thought of the Gilded Meridian's great book, the one Master Corvane kept under triple lock, the one whose entries he had copied faithfully for eleven years without ever asking where the dragon-fire went, only recording that it arrived, that it was paid, that the account balanced. *The gate exists to keep the crown-thing fed.* He had burned that page rather than believe it, and the gate had pulled him through anyway, because truth does not require belief to operate.

"It's owed the truth," Zephyrine said. "And the truth is that you were never a king here. You were a meal that learned to speak."

The king nodded, slow, as if each motion had to be negotiated with muscles long atrophied by disuse. Then he lifted the crown from his own head.

* * *

It did not go quietly.

The moment the band cleared his hair, the crown-thing screamed — not with sound but with absence, a howling vacuum that pulled the heat from the air and the color from the stone. The last dragon, Vaelith, old and grey and half her former size, raised her head from where she lay coiled at the chamber's edge. Her fire was nearly gone; Zephyrine had watched her spend the last of what mattered breaking the guild's enforcers' binding-circles, watching Master Corvane's face go white as his own chains turned on him. What remained in her was not enough to burn a village. It was exactly enough for this.

"Now," Odile whispered.

Vaelith did not roar. That was the thing Zephyrine would remember longest, later, telling it to the blank pages of a new ledger: she did not roar. She exhaled — a long, deliberate breath, the way a tired woman sets down a weight carried too many miles — and her flame came out thin and blue and utterly willing. It caught the crown mid-scream.

The thing fought. Of course it fought. It wrapped itself in stolen centuries, in the heat of a hundred dragons whose names the guild had recorded only as line items, and for one horrible moment the fire bent around it like water around a stone. Zephyrine felt Odile's hand find his, grip hard enough to hurt. He did not look away. He had spent eleven years not looking away from columns of numbers that meant this exact moment, and he owed the dead dragons at least his open eyes.

Then Vaelith walked into her own fire.

She moved slowly, majestically, the way mountains move when they decide to become valleys, and where her flame touched her scales they went to light, and where the light touched the crown the crown forgot how to be hungry. The last entry in the oldest ledger of the Gilded Meridian wrote itself in Zephyrine's mind in his own neat hand: *One dragon, final flame, freely given. Account closed.*

When the light faded there was ash on the dais in two shapes — one small and curled like a sleeping animal, one a twisted circlet already crumbling — and the hollowed king stood bareheaded in the gray drift of both, weeping without sound, free.

* * *

They gave the kingdom three days.

Three days for the word to travel down from the Spire that the king was unbound and the crown was ended; three days for the hollowed ministers to remember they had names; three days for the people to come up the mountain and see for themselves the bareheaded man sitting in the ashes of the throne room, refusing all offers to put anything back upon his head.

"I wore it so you wouldn't have to," he told them, when the first bold child asked why he wouldn't take the throne back now that it was empty. "I won't insult that by pretending I know how to rule. I only know how to survive. Find someone who knows more."

He chose exile the way other men choose retirement: deliberately, with relief, with a pack on his back and no destination announced. On the morning he left, he stopped before Zephyrine at the gate's threshold and pressed something into his hands — a signet ring, plain iron, the true royal seal that the crown had never known about because it had never been worth eating.

"The ledgers lie," the king said. "Yours lied about me for a century. So write new ones. Write mine. Write hers." He glanced back at the ash-shape on the dais, which Odile had already begun covering with a cloth she'd woven from her own ruined robes. "Write everything they paid for. And when your guild comes asking what happened to their investment—"

"They'll have to read it in my handwriting," Zephyrine said.

The king smiled — the first expression Zephyrine had seen on him that wasn't endurance — and walked down the mountain into a kingdom that no longer needed him, which was, Zephyrine suspected, the only gift anyone had given him in forty years.

* * *

The gate was closing.

Not violently, not with the hungry pull that had dragged Zephyrine through a week ago, but gently, the way a held breath is finally let out. The Spire's stones had gone quiet. Whatever engine the guild had built into them — whatever bargain Master Corvane and eleven masters before him had maintained with dragon-fire and other people's kingdoms — had nothing left to burn and no reason to burn it.

Odile stood on the far side, in the world Zephyrine was leaving, holding the true ledgers bound in rope made from the enforcers' broken chains. Three books. A century of entries in Corvane's elegant hand, itemizing every delivery of fire, every payment, every lie. Beside them, wrapped separately, the pages Zephyrine had tried to burn — singed at one corner, otherwise intact, patient as all true things are.

"You could stay," Odile said. "This kingdom will need record-keepers who don't lie. I could teach you prophecy properly, not just how to copy it."

Everything in Zephyrine leaned toward yes. The guild had raised him, fed him, taught him his letters on the backs of inventory sheets. But belonging, he understood now, was not the same as being kept, and he had been kept the way the king had been kept — useful, positioned, fed upon in a currency he hadn't known he possessed.

"I have to carry these home," he said. "Someone has to put them in Corvane's hands and watch his face while he reads."

"That's not forgiveness."

"No," Zephyrine agreed. "It's accounting."

He stepped through the gate carrying the truth in his arms, and behind him the Spire sighed shut — not locked, not sealed, simply finished, a door with no further purpose, left open a crack out of something like courtesy. On the other side, dawn was coming up over the guildhall, and the great ledger lay open on Corvane's desk to a page that was still blank.

Zephyrine set the true ledgers down beside it, took out his pen, and began to write.
