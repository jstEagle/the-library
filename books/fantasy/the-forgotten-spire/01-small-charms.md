## 1. Small Charms

The trouble with selling luck, Kestrel Halloran had learned over seven years in Vharen, was that people kept wanting it to work.

"Third time this month," said the fishwife, Marta, slapping a copper coin on his counter hard enough to rattle the tray of charms. "My husband's boat came home empty again. I want a stronger one."

Kestrel picked up the coin, turned it over as if inspecting its quality, and set it back down. "Marta. The charm I sold you is made of river clay and a chicken feather. Its power extends exactly as far as making you feel better when you tie it to the mast. If I sell you a *stronger* one, you'll stop blaming your husband for fishing where the fish aren't, and start blaming me."

"So it doesn't work."

"It works beautifully," Kestrel said. "You feel better. That's the whole product." He leaned across the counter, lowering his voice into the register he used for confidences he didn't mean. "But between us — I've got one charm left from my grandmother's stock. Old work. Very old. Costs four coppers instead of one."

Marta's eyes narrowed. She was a shrewd woman with forearms like mooring rope and a face that had heard every lie Vharen had to offer twice. "Your grandmother had a stall two rows down from mine for twenty years. She sold buttons."

"She sold buttons *and* charms," Kestrel said. "The buttons were the front."

It was, technically, true. His grandmother had never existed. But the dead were useful that way — they couldn't contradict you, and everyone trusted a dead grandmother more than a living stranger.

Marta bought the charm. It was the same clay and the same feather as all the others; he'd simply tied the string with an extra knot and charged her triple. She left the shop clutching it like a relic, and Kestrel watched her go with the mild, unbothered satisfaction of a man who had just committed no crime whatsoever.

That was the trick of it. In Vharen, magic was punishable by burning — real magic, the kind that bent the world. So Kestrel sold the kind that didn't. Clay beads. Knotted thread. Dried lavender for sleep, salt packets for thresholds, little wooden birds "for safe travel" that worked precisely as well as any wooden bird. He'd built a modest reputation as a charming fraud, which was the safest reputation a man could have in a city with nine pyre-stones and a very efficient Inquisition.

Nobody suspected the charm-seller of being the last warden of a sealed gate to the old gods' realm.

Which was fortunate, because some mornings Kestrel had trouble suspecting it himself.

* * *

He closed the shop at midday — market hours were for tourists and the gullible; his real customers came after dark — and went downstairs through the trapdoor behind the drying racks.

The cellar was cold in a way cellars shouldn't be. Not damp-cold, not stone-cold. Cold like the memory of winter, held perfectly still. Seven years ago, when he'd first descended the stairs the Order's dying courier had shown him, he'd stood at the bottom for the better part of an hour before working up the nerve to touch anything.

Now he barely noticed. Routine will do that to wonder. He crossed the cellar floor, ducked under the low arch, and knelt at the gate.

It didn't look like much. That was the point, presumably. A door of grey stone set into the cellar wall, unremarkable as any door, except that it had no handle, no hinges, no frame — it simply *was* wall on every side but the middle, and the middle was shut. Across its face ran the wards: lines of script older than any alphabet Kestrel knew, carved so fine they might have been scratches, glowing faintly the color of deep water.

Every morning, he renewed them. It wasn't complicated, once you knew how. He pressed his palm flat against the stone and let the small warmth in his chest — the only magic he permitted himself, the only magic he *had* — flow out along the old channels. The script brightened, drank, dimmed to its resting glow. Like feeding a fire that must never go out and must never flare.

Seven years. Two thousand five hundred mornings, give or take the ones he'd been sick or drunk or both. He'd gotten fast at it. Ninety seconds, palm to stone, and done.

Today, the gate drank slower.

Kestrel frowned. He pushed a little more warmth through, and the wards took it, but sluggishly, the way a tired man takes soup. One line of script near the door's lower corner flickered — actually flickered, a stutter of light like a candle in wind — before steadying.

"That's new," he told the gate.

The gate, as it had for seven years, declined to comment.

He stayed kneeling longer than usual, palm against the stone, listening with whatever sense it was that listened. The Order's courier had called it *the keeper's ear* and had died before explaining what that meant, which Kestrel had always considered rude. What he heard now was the same thing he always heard: nothing. A silence so complete it had texture, like felt. The old gods on the other side, asleep or absent or pretending. The wards humming their one long note.

But under the note, today, there was something else. Faint. Rhythmic.

Like something breathing.

He pulled his hand back and sat on the cold floor and made himself think about it properly, because panic was a luxury for people whose mistakes didn't end the world. The wards were old. Older than the Order that built the spire above them, according to the courier's notes, which were themselves older than the courier. Things that old grew tired. Tired things flickered. There was probably a perfectly boring explanation involving sediment and centuries.

There was also, he reminded himself, a child somewhere in this city who had prayed last week and been answered by something with too many voices. But that was across town, and the Inquisition was handling it, and the Inquisition handling things generally meant fire, and fire meant attention, and attention was the one thing Kestrel could not afford.

He climbed back upstairs, locked the trapdoor, and put the kettle on.

* * *

The afternoon brought regulars. Old Tam with his bad knee, buying the same lavender-and-tallow rub that did nothing for the knee but everything for the complaining about it. A young couple wanting a fertility charm, whom Kestrel talked down to a "general household blessing" while blushing on their behalf. A boy of maybe ten, sent by his mother for a fever-charm, paying with coins counted out one at a time from a sock.

"For the fever," Kestrel said, wrapping a twist of dried willow bark — actual medicine, the one honest thing in his inventory — "you'll want to boil this in water and have him drink it. Three times a day."

"The charm doesn't cure it?"

"The charm makes your mother feel like she's doing something. The bark makes your brother feel better. Both are important." He tucked an extra twist into the boy's pocket when he wasn't looking. "Go on."

Business was good. Business was always good, because hope was the one commodity that never glutted the market. Kestrel tallied his coppers, whistled tunelessly, and was reaching to close the shutters when the light changed.

Not dimmed — *changed*. The way it does when a cloud crosses the sun, except there were no clouds, and the change came from below, or behind, or inside, some direction that light didn't ordinarily come from. The dust motes in his shop hung still. The whistling kettle went quiet mid-note.

And somewhere — not far. A street or two. Close enough that the sound arrived without passing through any air at all, arriving directly in the marrow of him — a voice said:

*Please.*

A child's voice. Small, cracked, desperate. The kind of prayer that gets said into pillows and cracked floorboards because there's no one left to say it to. *Please, please, make her wake up, please, I'll be good, I'll give anything—*

Kestrel stood in his own shop with his hand on the shutter and felt the words pass through him like a fish through water, leaving no ripple, going *down*. Down through the floorboards, through the cellar, through ninety seconds of morning ritual and seven years of silence, toward the gate.

Toward the thing behind the gate that breathed.

*No,* Kestrel thought, with the full force of a man who had spent seven years practicing not being interesting. *No, no, don't—*

The wards flared. He felt it from the shop, felt it in his teeth, a bloom of heat like standing too near a forge. And then — quiet. Terribly quiet. The motes resumed falling. The kettle screamed back to life as if embarrassed.

Kestrel stood very still for a long moment.

Then he did the arithmetic he'd been dreading. A prayer, answered. Not by a saint, not by a spirit, not by any of the hundred small powers the Church pretended to catalog. By the only thing on the other side of his door. Something ancient, and hungry, and — if the courier's warnings were worth the paper they'd rotted on — something that had forgotten, over the long centuries, what prayers were *for*. What mercy was. What a fair price looked like.

*I'll give anything*, the child had said.

Children always said that. Children never understood what *anything* meant to a thing that had been starving since before the word *child* was invented.

Kestrel blew out the lamp, though it was barely dusk, and stood in the dark of his own shop listening to the ordinary sounds of Vharen settling into evening — cart wheels, a drunk singing, someone's supper burning. Ordinary sounds. The city had no idea. The city thought miracles were stories from the founding, safely dead.

He grabbed his coat and went out into the street, following the direction the prayer had gone, walking as fast as a charm-seller could walk without looking like a man running toward a miracle.

Which was to say: not nearly fast enough.

* * *

He found the house by the crowd.

Vharen gathered quickly around disaster; it was practically a civic virtue. A knot of neighbors stood in the lane outside a narrow row-house, murmuring in the particular hushed tone reserved for death and scandal, and Kestrel slipped among them wearing his best harmless expression — the one that said *charming local tradesman, nothing to see here* — and craned like everyone else.

Through the open door he could see a kitchen. A woman lay on the floor by the hearth, and a boy — younger than the fever-boy, six perhaps — knelt beside her, shaking her shoulder. Two neighbors were trying to pull him away gently.

She wasn't breathing. That much was clear even from the lane. But here was the thing that made the hair on Kestrel's arms stand up and stay standing:

The woman was *smiling*.

Not the smile of the dead. The dead relax; they go slack as dropped rope. This woman's face was arranged in an expression of perfect, beatific contentment, eyes half-open, lips curved, as though she had died mid-dream of something wonderful. As though whatever had answered her son's prayer had come to her first, gently, generously, and taken her somewhere lovely on its way to taking her.

And on the hearthstone beside her body, written in ash that hadn't come from any fire, in a hand too fluid and too old to be human, was a single line of script.

Kestrel knew it. Of course he knew it. He pressed his palm to it every morning of his life.

*Received in full*, it said. *With thanks.*

The crowd murmured. Someone said plague. Someone said the girl down the street had seen a light. Someone said fetch the Watch, and someone else said no, fetch the Inquisition, this was a matter for ash and pyre, and the boy was still shaking his mother's shoulder and saying *wake up, you promised, you promised* in a voice that was going to follow Kestrel for the rest of his life.

He backed out of the crowd. He walked home. He locked his door, went down to the cellar, and stood in front of the gate with his heart hammering and his palm itching to renew wards that suddenly seemed very thin indeed.

The flickering line of script had stopped flickering. All the wards glowed steady and strong and satisfied, like a fed animal.

"Whatever you took," Kestrel whispered to the door, "it wasn't yours to take."

The gate said nothing. But underneath the hum, faint and rhythmic and horribly patient, the breathing went on.

In the morning, he decided, he would need help. Real help. Which meant finding the one person in Vharen who knew what lay under the spire district — a smuggler named Doss Marrow, who owed Kestrel a debt measured in betrayal, and who would charge him for collecting it.

Kestrel climbed back upstairs, sat down at his counter among the clay beads and chicken feathers, and began, hands not entirely steady, to count his coppers.

Miracles, it turned out, had come back to Vharen.

And they were shopping.
