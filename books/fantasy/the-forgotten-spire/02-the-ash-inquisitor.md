## 2. The Ash Inquisitor

The miracle had killed a baker's wife, and Sera Vane was going to find out why.

She stood in the doorway of the bakery on Candlemaker's Row and let the smell of yesterday's bread sit in her lungs—yeasty, warm, ordinary. Ordinary things had a way of steadying her. She needed steadying, because the room beyond the doorway was not ordinary at all.

"Are you the ash woman?" asked a small boy in the corner, flour to his elbows, eyes enormous.

"Inquisitor," Sera said. "Sera Vane." She did not correct the *ash*. There was no point. Everyone in Vharen knew what happened to inquisitors who fell from grace: they were stripped of their white robes, given gray ones the color of cinders, and sent out into the world to do the Order's dirty work without the dignity of being called by rank. Ash women. Ash men. She'd earned the name three years ago and had stopped flinching at it two years ago, which she considered personal growth.

"The other one came first," the boy said. "Before you."

Sera went very still. "Another investigator?"

"A lady made of smoke." He said it matter-of-factly, the way children report impossible things, because they haven't yet learned which impossibilities are dangerous to mention. "She stood where you're standing and she cried, but the crying was wrong. It went up instead of down."

*Smoke that weeps upward.* Sera wrote it in her notebook anyway, because a cracked mind was still a mind, and half the truths in Vharen arrived wearing madness like a coat.

"Show me where your mother prayed," she said.

* * *

Marta Ellery had prayed at her kneading trough. That much the boy could show her—a scuffed square of floorboards between the oven and the window, still dusted with flour, as though the family couldn't bear to sweep it. Sera knelt there and pressed her palm flat against the wood and let herself feel what an inquisitor was trained to feel: residue.

Every prayer left something behind. That was doctrine, and doctrine was usually right, which was the most annoying thing about it. A prayer was a small act of reaching, and reaching left fingerprints. Most prayers left nothing but warmth—the spiritual equivalent of breath on glass. Some left more. And once in a generation, if the stories were true, a prayer left a *door*.

This one had left a door.

Sera felt it the moment her skin touched the boards: a coldness that wasn't temperature, a hollowness that wasn't emptiness. Something had reached back through Marta Ellery's prayer. Something had *answered*, and its answer had been so much larger than the question that the asker had simply—stopped.

"How did she die?" Sera asked the boy, gently. "Tell me exactly."

"She was making bread. She said, 'Saints above, I wish I had my sister's hands for this,' because Auntie Rill makes beautiful braids." His voice wobbled. "And then her hands started moving. Really fast. Faster than Auntie Rill. She made forty loaves. She was laughing. And then she—" He stopped. "Then she wasn't laughing anymore. Then she was just kneeling there, and the bread was perfect, and she was gone. Papa says her heart gave out. But hearts don't give out when you're laughing."

"No," Sera said quietly. "They don't."

Forty loaves of perfect bread sat cooling on racks along the wall, and Sera examined them with the dread of a woman reading a ransom note. They were flawless. Every braid identical, every crust the exact amber of a painter's ideal. That was the tell. Human work was never flawless; human work had thumbs in it and bad days and love expressed as lopsidedness. This bread had been made by something that understood *bread* perfectly and understood Marta Ellery not at all.

The old gods—or whatever wore their names these days—had never been good at mercy. They were good at *literalism*. Ask them for strength and they would hollow you out and fill you with stone. Ask them for hands like your sister's, and they would lend you hands until the borrowing cost everything else you were.

Doctrine said burn the house. Doctrine said the boy too, probably, or at least question him under the lamps, because answered prayers didn't happen to innocent households—they happened to corrupted ones. Sera had written that doctrine herself, in her younger years, in a treatise titled *On the Contagion of Grace* that had been required reading in the seminary for a decade. She remembered being proud of it. She remembered the specific satisfaction of sentences that ended in fire.

She looked at the boy, who was holding a lopsided loaf he'd made himself, badly, with his own small imperfect hands, and she heard herself say:

"You should keep making those. The crooked ones. They're better than hers."

He blinked at her. "They're worse."

"They're yours," Sera said, and then fled before she could say anything else treasonous.

* * *

Her office—if a rented room above a tannery with a desk missing one leg could be called an office—smelled permanently of leather and regret. Sera spread her notes across the desk and weighted the corners with candle stubs and tried to think like the inquisitor she used to be instead of the ash woman she was.

Three answered prayers in nine days. That was the pattern, and patterns were the whole religion of her profession. The first: a fisherman on the Salt Quay who'd prayed for calm waters and gotten them—for six hours, dead flat, no wind at all, and his boat drifting helplessly while he aged visibly, his hair going white before his crew's eyes, as though the sea had taken the years directly out of him as payment. The second: a moneylender who'd prayed for his debts to be forgiven and woke to find every ledger in the city recording him as having never existed—which sounded like mercy until you realized his wife no longer recognized him either, and he now wandered the Debtors' Ward asking strangers if they'd seen a man who looked like him.

And now Marta Ellery, who'd wished for her sister's hands and been emptied like a wineskin.

Each answer granted. Each answer *wrong*. Each answer costing the asker something vital—time, identity, self—and each prayer traced, when Sera mapped the locations on her chart of the city, toward the same quarter.

Lowmarket. The charm-sellers' district. The district everyone knew was harmless, because the charms sold there were nonsense—knotted red thread for luck, iron nails wrapped in wool against nightmares, little clay whistles that supposedly carried wishes to the saints. Superstition, the Order called it, tolerated the way one tolerates a child's imaginary friend. No real magic. Nothing worth an inquisitor's time.

Except that answered prayers were radiating outward from Lowmarket like ripples from a dropped stone, and Sera had spent enough years reading the Order's oldest files—back when she'd still been allowed in the archives—to know that ripples meant a source.

She pinned the map to the wall and drew lines from each prayer site inward. They converged, roughly, on a street called Tallow Lane, near a shop whose name she'd seen a hundred times on her patrols and never thought twice about.

*Halloran's Charms. Luck honestly sold.*

"Honestly sold," Sera murmured to the empty room. "What a strange thing to insist upon."

* * *

She went the next morning, out of uniform—gray traveling clothes, notebook in her satchel, no badge, because badges got doors closed and she needed this door open. Tallow Lane in the morning was all shutters opening and kettle-smoke and a knife-grinder calling out that he'd sharpen anything except grudges, which got a laugh from the fishwife across the way, and Sera felt the neighborhood settle around her like a cat deciding she was acceptable.

Halloran's Charms was the third shop from the corner, narrow-fronted, with a hand-painted sign and a window display that managed to look both abundant and apologetic. And behind the counter, arranging a tray of knotted threads with the concentration of a man defusing something explosive, was a tall, dark-haired man with careful hands and the kind of face that looked like it was used to being unremarkable on purpose.

"Morning," he said, without looking up. "If you're here about the luck-knots, I'm out of the red until Thursday. If you're here about the whistle, I'll be honest with you: it doesn't carry wishes anywhere except maybe downstairs, and downstairs is a cellar."

"And if I'm here about the answered prayers?" Sera said.

His hands did not stop moving. That was the first interesting thing. A guilty man's hands stuttered; a frightened man's hands froze. These hands kept laying out red thread in neat rows, steady as a surgeon's, while something behind his eyes recalculated everything.

"Answered prayers," he repeated pleasantly. "In this district? Madam, the most miraculous thing on Tallow Lane is Mrs. Ovett's stew, and even that's mostly turnip."

"Three in nine days." Sera leaned on the counter, close enough to watch his pupils. "A fisherman aged thirty years in an afternoon. A moneylender erased from every record in the city. A baker's wife who made forty perfect loaves and died laughing. All within a mile of this shop. You're telling me you've noticed nothing?"

"I'm telling you," Kestrel Halloran said, meeting her eyes at last, and his gaze was level and warm and utterly unreadable, "that grief makes people pray, and praying is free, and coincidence is cheaper than conspiracy. But you're welcome to browse. The iron nails are genuinely good against nightmares. Not magic," he added quickly. "Just iron. Iron's honest."

*Iron's honest.* Sera turned a nail over in her fingers and felt—nothing. Which was itself informative, because she'd warded her own doorframe with iron for years and knew exactly what properly prepared iron felt like against a sensitive palm, and this nail felt like hardware.

Everything in the shop felt like hardware. That was the second interesting thing. A charm-seller's stock should have a faint hum to it, even if the hum was only the seller's own belief soaked into the goods like sweat into a shirt. This shop was silent as a held breath. Either Halloran was the most honest fraud in Vharen, or—

Or he was very, very good at hiding something alive beneath a floor of dead things.

"Your cellar," Sera said lightly. "You mentioned a cellar."

"I mentioned that wishes don't go there." He smiled. It was a good smile. She hated how good it was. "Can I interest you in a luck-knot? On the house. For the road."

"I haven't told you where I'm going."

"You have the walk of someone going somewhere she doesn't want to arrive," he said, and handed her a knot of red thread anyway, and their fingers touched over the counter for half a heartbeat, and Sera felt—cold. Not the man's cold. *Beneath* him. Far beneath, deep down past the cellar, past the foundations, past anything a shop on Tallow Lane had any right to sit on top of: a coldness like the one she'd felt in Marta Ellery's kitchen, vast and patient and *hungry*, like a well that had finally noticed the sky.

She took the knot. She thanked him. She walked out onto Tallow Lane into the ordinary morning noise, and only when she was fifty paces away did she let her hands shake.

Because she knew that cold. She'd been raised on descriptions of it. Every inquisitor was. It was in the first chapter of the first book of doctrine, illustrated with a woodcut so old the monster in it had worn smooth into a smudge: *the cold beneath the world, where the old gods sleep behind the sealed gate, dreaming of the days when men prayed to them and men burned for the answering.*

Sealed. The doctrine was very firm on sealed. Sealed seven hundred years, sealed by the Nine Founders, sealed so thoroughly that the Order's own consecrated ground was built atop the seals like padlocks on a door nobody remembered the shape of.

But Marta Ellery's prayer had opened something. Three prayers had opened something. And the something was underneath a charm shop run by a man whose hands never shook and whose smile was a locked door of its own.

Sera stopped at the corner and looked back. Halloran stood in his shop doorway, watching her go, and he raised one hand—not a wave, exactly. More like a salute between soldiers on opposite sides of a war neither of them had chosen.

She raised her hand back before she could stop herself, and then stood there feeling like a fool, and then laughed at herself, alone on a street corner, which attracted the attention of the knife-grinder, who offered to sharpen her dignity for a copper.

"Keep your copper," she told him. "I may need the dull edge."

* * *

That night she wrote her report. Or rather, she sat before the blank page with her pen loaded and ready, and wrote three different openings, and crossed out all three.

The first began: *To the Prelate Ossen, keeper of the flame, from the ash woman Vane.* Formal. Correct. It reported the answered prayers, named the district, named the shop, named the man. It requested authority to detain and interrogate. It was the report the Sera of five years ago would have written in her sleep, and it would have ended with a chimney smoke and a name struck from the city rolls, and the boy on Candlemaker's Row would go to some aunt or none, and the forty loaves would be distributed to the poor as alms, because the Order wasted nothing, not even miracles.

The second began: *Prelate—I have found something the doctrine has no chapter for.*

The third was just: *Her hands were laughing.*

Sera put down the pen. Outside the tannery window, Vharen glittered, ten thousand hearthfires, ten thousand people saying their small safe prayers to saints who had never once, in all recorded history, answered—and weren't supposed to. That was the entire architecture of her faith: the gods were gone, the gate was shut, the silence was holy. Prayer was a letter written to a demolished address. It comforted precisely because it changed nothing.

And now something was answering. Badly. Hungrily. At a cost no doctrine had priced.

If she sent the first report, riders would come, and fires would come, and whatever fragile thing was happening under Tallow Lane would be burned open whether it was a wound or a door. If she sent the second, Ossen would come himself, and Sera had known Ossen for twenty years and loved him like a father and trusted him exactly as far as his sermons, which lately had developed a shine she didn't like, a hunger dressed as certainty.

And if she sent nothing—

She looked at the third page. *Her hands were laughing.* Underneath it, without quite deciding to, she added: *Something is answering the prayers, and it does not know what mercy is, and I think the man who sells honest charms knows why.*

She read it back. It was, by every standard of her order, heresy. It was also, by every standard she owned personally, simply true.

Sera Vane blew out the candle, folded the pages into her coat rather than sending them anywhere, and lay awake listening to the city breathe, telling herself she was only gathering more evidence before reporting.

She was a very good liar. She'd had to be, these last three years. The trouble was that lately the person she lied to best was herself, and the lie was getting thinner every day, and somewhere beneath Tallow Lane something ancient turned over in its sleep, and dreamed, and the dream leaked upward into the world like smoke that wept.

Tomorrow, she decided, she would go back to the shop. Not to arrest anyone. Just to talk. Just to buy a luck-knot she didn't need from a man she didn't trust, and see what shook loose.

It did not occur to her until much later that she had already chosen a side.
