## 3. What the Wards Remember

The trouble with keeping a secret for seven years, Kestrel Halloran had learned, was that the secret got bored long before you did.

He felt it now, in the hour before dawn, kneeling on the cold flagstones of his cellar with a cup of chicory going lukewarm beside him and a paintbrush behind his ear like a carpenter's pencil. The gate beneath his shop—the one no map acknowledged, no neighbor suspected, and no customer would ever believe sat forty feet below the shelf where he sold love-luck and safe-travel charms at three coppers apiece—was humming.

It hadn't hummed since the night he'd sealed it.

"Stop that," he told it.

The humming continued. It wasn't sound, exactly. It was more the way a tuning fork feels when someone strikes it across the room—something in Kestrel's back teeth and breastbone picking up a note that his ears insisted wasn't there. He'd spent seven years teaching himself to ignore that note. Seven years of careful, patient, unglamorous work: renewing the ward-lines every new moon, feeding them drops of his own blood mixed with lamp oil because blood was what the original builders had used and he saw no reason to improve on tradition, and above all never, ever listening.

Listening was how keepers died. His predecessor had told him that with her last coherent breath, which Kestrel had always thought was a bit dramatic of her, but then she'd also been dissolving into moths at the time, so perhaps drama was warranted.

He dipped the brush into the jar of ward-ink—a foul brew of soot, salt, and crushed ironweed that smelled like a blacksmith's regret—and touched it to the first line of the seal.

The line flared white.

Kestrel sat down hard on the flagstones. The ink should have drunk slowly, should have taken the whole night to settle into the groove the way it always did, the way it had two hundred times before. Instead it blazed up the wall of the gate like dawn breaking over a mountain range, and every other line caught fire in sequence, and the great stone door—which was not a door, he reminded himself firmly, it was a *seal*, doors invited things through, seals were just very committed walls—began to glow from within like a lantern held behind a hand.

"Oh," said Kestrel, "no."

The humming resolved into words.

*Keeper.*

The voice was not loud. That was somehow worse. It was the voice of something that had been speaking quietly for a very long time to an empty room and had only just noticed there was someone in it. It came from everywhere in the cellar at once—from the stones, from the ink, from the chicory, absurdly, as though even his drink had opinions now.

"I'm not listening," Kestrel announced to his cellar. "This is me, not listening."

*You are listening,* said the wards, with the mild precision of a schoolmaster catching a boy with his hand in the biscuit tin. *You have been listening for three nights. You dreamed of the sea that is not the sea. You woke with salt on your lips.*

He had. He'd assumed it was the herring pie.

"That's not—" He stopped, breathed, started again in the tone he used with customers who wanted their luck-charms to also cure baldness. "Look. We had an arrangement. I maintain you, you stay quiet, and neither of us has to think about the other until one of us dies. Preferably me, of natural causes, in bed, at a respectable age."

*The arrangement is failing.*

"I know." The words came out smaller than he meant them to. He looked down at his hands, at the brush trembling between his fingers, and made himself be honest, because apparently today was a day for it. "I know. Something's answering prayers out there. Something old and wrong. And every time it does, you—" he gestured at the glowing door, "—you get louder. I can feel it. Like a bucket with a hole in the bottom."

*Not a hole,* said the wards. *A mouth. The gate was built with two faces, Keeper. One faces here. One faces there. When they pray there, and something answers here, the wall between grows thin. We have been holding it. We are tired.*

"You're wards," Kestrel said. "Wards don't get tired."

There was a pause. When the voice came again, it sounded almost gentle, which frightened him more than anything else that morning.

*We were people once. Did you not know?*

Kestrel opened his mouth. Closed it. In seven years, the wards had never spoken. In seven years, he'd told himself they were architecture—clever architecture, ancient architecture, but architecture, the way a bridge is clever and a cathedral is ancient and neither of them asks you how your day went. He had been the keeper of a lock, and locks do not talk.

*Sit,* said the wards. *See.*

"I really don't think—"

*Sit, Keeper. There is little time, and you must understand what you are guarding, or you will guard it wrongly, and wrong-guarding is how your predecessor became moths.*

That was unfair. She'd become moths because of a completely different mistake involving a second seal she hadn't known existed. But Kestrel's legs were already folding under him, sitting cross-legged on the cold stone like a child at storytime, because some part of him—some part that had been listening for three nights whether he admitted it or not—wanted to know.

"Fine," he said. "But if this turns me into moths, I'm haunting you specifically."

*Noted,* said the wards, and he could have sworn they sounded amused.

* *

The vision took him the way sleep takes a tired man: all at once, and without permission.

He stood on a hillside under a sky the color of unpolished copper, and the hillside was covered in barley gone gold, and the wind moved through it in long silver strokes, and Kestrel knew the place.

That was the first horror: he *knew* it. Not recognized—knew, the way you know your own kitchen in the dark. This was the barley country above Vharen's eastern valleys, the terraced fields where the grain came from that his mother milled—

His mother.

He reached for her face and found a smooth place in his memory, the way a tongue finds a missing tooth. He knew she had a face. He knew he had loved it. He knew she'd hummed while she worked, off-key, a song about a miller's daughter and a fox, and he could remember the *humming*, could nearly hear it, and the face attached to it was a warm blur, a candle seen through fogged glass.

"No," Kestrel said aloud, in the vision, to nobody. "No, that's mine. That's my—"

*Watch,* said the wards, gently. *Please. Watch first. Grieve after. Grieving first is how keepers lose the thread.*

So he watched.

The copper sky brightened toward morning, and the barley country woke. A town in the valley below—white walls, red roofs, a bell tower with a green copper spire—began to smoke its chimneys. Farmers went out with sickles. Children ran along a lane chasing a dog, and the dog was brown and enormous and half wolf, and Kestrel knew its name was Bracken and that it belonged to everyone and no one and stole bread with the shameless expertise of a tax collector.

And the names. That was the thing that undid him. Every field had a name. Every well, every crossing, every crooked oak. The hill he stood on was called the Old Woman's Shoulder, because from the valley it looked like a bent back, and the stream below was the Laughing Water, and the town was called—

The town was called—

Kestrel stood in his own memory and found the space where the name should be, and the space was clean and swept and utterly empty, like a shop shelf the night before new stock arrives.

*You see,* said the wards, quietly. *It is already happening to you. It began the moment the prayers started being answered. The land forgets from the edges inward. First the far places. Then the small names. Then—*

"Then the people," Kestrel whispered.

*Then the people.*

The vision shifted. He was standing in the town square now, in the market he suddenly understood he had visited a hundred times as a boy, buying honey-cakes from a woman who pinched his cheek. The square was full. Market day. And yet the crowd made no sound—not the hush of reverence, but the silence of a painting. He walked among them and their faces were the same warm blur his mother's was, dozens of them, hundreds, a whole town of people he had known and loved reduced to candlelight behind glass.

Except one.

At the fountain in the center of the square stood a girl of maybe eight, and she was sharp-edged, real, *present*, in a way that hurt to look at. She was looking directly at him.

"You're the keeper," she said. Her voice was the only sound in the world. "You're late."

"I—" Kestrel's throat worked. "I don't know you."

"You will," she said. "Or you won't, and everything ends. The gods forgot us, keeper. Do you understand? Not abandoned. *Forgot.* There's a difference. Abandonment leaves a wound. Forgetting leaves nothing—not even the knowledge that something's missing. We only exist now because the gate remembers us. Because *you* remember us. Whatever's left of that."

"The answered prayers," Kestrel said. "The thing that killed the Ashwick child. Is that—"

"Hunger," the girl said simply. "When you're forgotten, you get hungry. They're reaching through the thin places, trying to taste the country they lost. But they've forgotten what tasting is *for*. They're drinking the land itself. Every miracle costs a field, a road, a name. They don't know. They can't know. Nobody taught them." She looked at him with eyes too old for her face, and said, in a smaller voice: "Somebody has to teach them. That's why the gate opens both ways, keeper. It was never a wall. It was a promise. Somebody promised them they wouldn't be alone forever, and promises need doors."

The copper sky flickered. The crowd blurred further, faces smoothing toward blankness, and the girl's edges began to soften too, and panic seized Kestrel's chest like a fist.

"Wait—who are you? What's your name?"

She smiled, and it was the saddest smile he had ever seen, and somewhere beneath the terror he thought: *I have seen that smile before. Whose smile is that? Where do I know that smile from?*

"You'll remember when it matters," she said. "We're good at waiting. It's the one thing we've gotten better at."

The square dissolved like sugar in rain.

* *

Kestrel came back to himself on the cellar floor with his cheek against the flagstones and the chicory spilled in a brown star beside his hand. Dawn light was coming through the high grate in a thin gray blade. His whole body ached as though he'd run a long way, and his face was wet, and he had no memory of crying, which somehow made it worse.

Above him, faintly, the shop bell chimed. Someone turning the sign, or the morning wind. Then footsteps overhead—Mrs. Tallow next door, banging her shutters open, the same racket she made every morning at the same time, bless her predictable heart. The world going on. The ordinary world, insisting on itself.

Kestrol lay still and tried to take inventory of himself, the way you count stock after a break-in.

His name: Kestrel Halloran. That was still there. Good start.

His trade: charm-seller, and underneath that, keeper. Present.

Vharen: present. The Order, the burnings, the ash-law: present, unfortunately.

His childhood street: present. Cobbled, crooked, called—

Called—

He lay on the floor of his own cellar and reached for the name of the street he had grown up on, the street whose cobbles he had skinned his knees on, and found the swept-clean space where it should be, and understood with a lurching, falling sensation that this was not a memory problem. Memory problems were for old men and bad headaches. This was a *country* problem. The land was forgetting itself from the edges inward, and he was inside the edges, and the forgetting had reached him.

His mother's face: gone. Her humming: still there, off-key, the miller's daughter and the fox. He clung to the humming like a man clinging to a rope over a drop.

"Right," he said aloud, to the ceiling, to the wards, to whatever was left of a town with a green copper spire. "Right. So. Either I'm mad—and I want it noted that I considered this option seriously—or my whole country is being eaten by homesick gods, and the only person who might know how the gate actually works is a smuggler who sold me to the Order seven years ago and whom I swore on my predecessor's moth-eaten grave I'd never speak to again."

The wards said nothing. Wards, having delivered their terrible news, apparently felt entitled to a rest.

Kestrel got up, joints cracking, washed his face in the rain barrel by the cellar steps, and climbed into the grey morning of his shop. He flipped the sign to OPEN out of pure habit, then stared at it, then flipped it back to CLOSED, because he was not going to sell luck-charms today, not when he now suspected luck was a finite national resource being siphoned through a hole in the world.

From the drawer under the counter, wrapped in oilcloth, he took out a battered brass compass that didn't point north. It pointed, instead, toward Doss Marrow—because Doss had stolen it from a hedge-witch who'd enchanted it to find "the person you most regret knowing," and Doss had kept it as a joke, and Kestrel had let him, because at the time it had seemed funny.

Seven years of dust fell off it when he picked it up. The needle swung once, twice, and settled, steady as a heartbeat, pointing east. Toward the river docks. Toward smoke and salt and cheap taverns.

Toward the man who had betrayed him, who owed him a debt measured in years and possibly in blood, and who was—this was the truly galling part—the only soul in Vharen who might know what the gate was *for*.

"He's going to be insufferable," Kestrel told the compass.

The needle did not disagree.

He put on his coat, pocketed the compass, and locked the shop behind him with hands that almost didn't shake. Overhead, the morning sky was the color of unpolished copper, and he pretended very hard not to notice.
