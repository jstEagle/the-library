## 4. A Bargain in Smoke

The trouble with needing a man you hate, Kestrel reflected as he picked his way down the harbor stairs, was that the hating didn't make him any less needed. It just made the walk longer, because you kept hoping to think of an alternative.

He had spent two days thinking of alternatives. He had consulted his ledgers, which were useless. He had gone down into the cellar and asked the wards—*asked them*, after seven years of silence they had finally opened their mouths and what they'd said was *you don't live here anymore, and neither does your country*—and the wards had been no help at all, unless you counted existential collapse as help. He had even considered going to the Order of the Cindered Flame and turning himself in, on the theory that anyone who burned people for a living probably kept good records.

That idea had lasted about four seconds.

Which left Doss Marrow.

Doss operated out of a smokehouse at the edge of the Saltquay district, a building that smelled permanently of herring and secrets, both of them past their prime. Kestrel remembered it from before—the before being six years ago, when he'd still trusted this man with his life and his name and, as it turned out, neither had deserved the honor. The smokehouse looked smaller now. Everything about that year looked smaller now, the way wounds do once they've scarred over and all that's left is the shape of them.

A boy sat outside on an upturned crate, gutting fish with the enthusiasm of someone who had recently discovered knives.

"I'm here to see Marrow."

"He's not seeing nobody."

"Tell him Kestrel Halloran's here to see him."

The boy looked up. Whatever he saw in Kestrel's face made him put down the knife, which Kestrel took as a compliment, and disappear inside. A moment later he came back out, walked around Kestrel at a careful distance, and sat back down on his crate without a word.

"That's it?"

"He says come in," the boy said, not looking up. "He also says—" the boy screwed up his face, recalling exactly, "—'tell him the door's open but I remember where every knife is.'"

"Warm as ever," Kestrel muttered, and went in.

* * *

Inside, the smokehouse was dim and gold-lit, hung with racks of fish and strings of drying peppers, and thick with a smoke that had soaked into the walls so long ago it had become part of the architecture. Doss Marrow sat behind a scarred table near the back, older than Kestrel remembered, broader too, with a beard he'd grown either for wisdom or disguise and salt-and-pepper in a way that suggested he'd stopped caring which.

"Well," Doss said. "The hermit comes down from his hill."

"You sold me to the Inquisition."

"Good morning to you too." Doss gestured at the chair across from him. "Sit. You look terrible."

"I've been told." Kestrel didn't sit. "Six years, Doss. Six years I've been hiding in a cellar like a rat while men with ash-censers searched my shop twice a year. Do you know what they do to gate-keepers? They don't burn us. Burning's quick. They take the wards apart one thread at a time and make you watch."

"I know what they do." For just a moment something moved behind Doss's eyes—something old and tired—and then it was gone, packed away with the practiced ease of a man who'd been packing things away his whole life. "I also know you didn't walk all the way to Saltquay to relitigate the past. You need passage, or you need cargo moved quiet, or you need information. And since you came yourself instead of sending word, it's information, and it's bad."

Kestrel pulled out the chair and sat after all. His legs had decided the matter without consulting him; he hadn't slept properly since the wards spoke.

"Something answered a prayer in Tanner's Row three nights ago," he said. "A child prayed for her father's cough to go away. Something heard her. The cough went away. So did the father, mostly—he's alive, technically, but there's less of him than there was. Less *substance*. Like a candle that's been burning from both ends."

Doss's hands, which had been sorting dried pepper pods into little piles, went still.

"And my wards are failing," Kestrel went on. "Not breaking—*failing*. There's a difference. Breaking is pressure from outside. Failing means whatever's holding them together is being drawn away. Something on the other side of that gate is hungry, Doss, and it's learned how to reach through, and it's feeding on—" he gestured vaguely, helplessly, at everything, "—on *here*. On the land itself. And here's the part I can't explain: the country the wards showed me, the one the gate was built to guard, doesn't exist. Not destroyed. Not hidden. *Erased*. No map has it. My own memories of home have holes in them the size of parishes. Somebody reached into the world and took a whole country out, and left the hole wearing its shape."

The silence stretched long enough that Kestrel could hear the peppers ticking as they dried.

"You know something," he said.

"I know lots of things. It's my trade."

"Doss."

"I know," Doss said slowly, "that you should talk to people who were there. Before. When the Spire was built, when the Order was young, when the gate was sealed the first time." He leaned forward. "And I know that those people don't gather in taverns, Kestrel. They're scattered from here to the Grey Marches, and most of them are dead, and the ones who aren't dead are hiding, same as you. But I know where some of them are. Or I did, six years ago. Routes change. Safehouses rot. But the network's still there, under everything, like roots under a road."

"The smuggling routes."

"My routes." Doss smiled without much humor. "You think I move herring? Herring pays for the boats. What moves through my network is names, Kestrel. People the Order wants erased. Bloodlines the crown wants forgotten. Books. Relics. Whole families, sometimes, spirited out of cities in the bottom of salt barges. I've been running the same operation for twenty years, and the reason it works is that nobody—not the Order, not the crown, not the gods themselves—knows it exists."

"Except you sold *me*."

Something flickered across Doss's face then, quick as a fish under ice, and was gone.

"Yes," he said. "I did."

"No explanation? No excuse? Just 'yes, I did'?"

"What would you like?" Doss spread his hands. "That I was coerced? I wasn't. That I regret it? Ask me again in twenty years. That I had reasons? I had reasons. They were mine, and they're not for sale today, and none of them will make your father's cough come back or your six years in a cellar un-happen. What I *will* tell you is this: the day I gave your name to the censer-men, I also made sure the tip arrived three days late. Three days, Kestrel. Long enough for you to get your mother's letters out of the shop and move the true ward-stones into the cellar wall. Check the stones. If they'd found the real ones, you wouldn't be sitting here. You'd be a smear on the ash-heap and the gate would be standing open."

Kestrel stared at him. The cellar wall. The stones that hummed faintly when he pressed his palm flat against them at night, the ones he'd always assumed he'd hidden well enough himself.

"You—" He stopped. Started again. "Three days isn't mercy, Doss. Three days is a man watching another man drown slow instead of fast and calling it kindness."

"It is," Doss agreed. "It absolutely is. Welcome to my entire moral philosophy. Can we get to the part where you tell me what you actually need?"

What Kestrel actually needed, it turned out, took a while to say, because saying it out loud made it real. He needed the network. He needed the scattered keepers and relic-mongers and half-mad archivists who might remember what the gate was *for*, because the wards had shown him only fragments—a spire, a door, a country with no name—and fragments weren't enough to act on. He needed to know whether the thing answering prayers could be starved, or bargained with, or killed, or whether it was simply going to drink the world dry through a straw made of desperate people's wishes.

And underneath all of it, unspoken, he needed to not be alone with this. Seven years he'd kept the gate alone, and it had nearly broken him, and now the gate was talking and the sky was falling and the only person in Vharen he could think to turn to was the man who'd betrayed him.

"There's a woman in Lowmarsh," Doss said, when Kestrel finished. "Old keeper-line blood. She'll know more than I do about what the gate was for. And there's a barge leaving Saltquay Thursday night if the tide's right, and it's right about half the time." He tapped the table. "But here's my price."

Kestrel braced himself. Coin, he could manage. Favors, worse. Information, worst of all.

"When that gate opens," Doss said, "however it opens, whenever it opens—I go through."

Kestrel blinked. "Through to *where*? There's nothing there. That's rather the point. The country's erased."

"Then I go through to nothing." Doss's voice was flat and certain in a way Kestrel had never heard from him. "You think I stay in this trade for the herring money? Twenty years, Kestrel, I've been moving people out of a country that eats them. Every person I've smuggled, every name I've saved—it's been bailing water out of a sinking ship with a teaspoon. If there's a door out—even a door out to a country that's been *erased*, even a door out to nothing—that's the first door I've ever seen that goes somewhere better. I want passage. Not for me alone. For everyone my network can carry before it closes. That's the price. Take it or leave it."

"You want me to promise you a door I don't know how to open, to a place that may not exist, for a fleet of refugees I can't protect."

"Now you're getting it."

"And if opening the door destroys everything on this side?"

Doss considered this with the seriousness of a man weighing herring futures.

"Then we'll all be equally destroyed," he said, "and I'll have died having finally shipped out. Which is more than most of us get." He stuck out a hand across the table, broad and scarred and steady. "Well, gate-keeper? Do we have a bargain?"

Kestrel looked at the hand. Six years ago he'd have taken it without a thought. Six years ago it had belonged to a friend, and friends' hands didn't sell you to the fire. This hand had. And yet this hand had also, apparently, held the censer-men off for three crucial days, and had spent twenty years fishing strangers out of the same sea that had nearly drowned Kestrel himself.

People were so much worse than stories, and so much better.

"If you betray me again," Kestrel said, taking the hand, "I will seal you into the gate itself. There are worse fates than being a hinge."

"Noted," said Doss. "Pleasure doing business."

They shook once, hard, and it settled nothing between them and everything, which Kestrel supposed was how the best bargains worked.

* * *

He stayed for supper, because Doss insisted and because the alternative was walking back across Vharen in the dark with his thoughts, and his thoughts were poor company these days. The fish was smoked and excellent. The boy—whose name, it emerged, was Pip, and who had been rescued from a censer-house orphanage at age six and had never entirely forgiven the world—served them with the sullen grace of the deeply indebted.

"So," Doss said, around a mouthful, "the prayer-thing. The hungry one. How many answered prayers you figure happen in a city this size on any given night?"

Kestrel frowned. "I don't know. Hundreds? Thousands. Most go unanswered, obviously. That's the natural order. Prayer's a letter sent to an empty house."

"Mm." Doss chewed. "And now the house isn't empty. And it's hungry. And every answer costs the land a little something." He set down his fork. "How long, Kestrel? Before the hunger gets loud enough that everyone notices? Before the Order notices, and decides it's *your* fault, and comes for the shop with fire?"

"A month," Kestrel said quietly. "Maybe less. The wards failed another notch this morning."

"Then we leave Thursday." Doss raised his cup. "To doors," he said. "May ours open before theirs do."

Kestrel drank, and the smoke curled around them, and far beneath the city, beneath the shop, beneath the cellar with its humming stones, something ancient turned over in its sleep and dreamed of being fed.

Neither of them knew yet that they were already being watched. Neither of them knew that a woman with ash-grey eyes had spent the afternoon asking questions in Tanner's Row, and that her questions had a way of arriving, sooner or later, at every door in Vharen—including one in Saltquay, including one beneath a charm-shop, including one that had been waiting seven years to be knocked upon.

But the lamp in Kestrel's window burned late that night anyway. Old habit. Keeper's habit.

Just in case.
