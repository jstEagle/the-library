## 5. The God-Touched Girl

The girl was praying when Sera Vane found her, which was not unusual. What was unusual was that someone appeared to be listening.

Sera had been in the Saltmarket since dawn, following the thread of the answered prayer from chapter house records that no longer officially existed—her rank had been stripped, but old habits and older contacts still fed her information like a leaking roof feeds a bucket. The prayer had come from a child in the weavers' quarter. A sick mother. And three days later, the mother had risen from her deathbed with color in her cheeks and no memory of being ill at all.

Miracles were supposed to be impossible. That was doctrine, and Sera had spent eleven years enforcing it with fire and paperwork in roughly equal measure.

She found the girl crouched behind a fishmonger's stall, hands pressed together so tightly her knuckles had gone white, whispering with the desperate fluency of someone who had learned that prayer was less a conversation than a plea shouted into a well.

"Please," the girl was saying. "Please, I only need one more. The baker's boy fell off the cart and his leg is bent wrong and they'll take it off if nobody—please. Please."

Sera stepped around the stall. "You should stop doing that."

The girl looked up. She was perhaps ten or eleven, small for it, with a smudge of flour on one cheek and eyes the gray of rain about to commit to something. She didn't startle. She didn't run. She looked at Sera's ash-gray cloak—the cloak of an inquisitor, even a disgraced one—and said, with the weary pragmatism of a much older woman:

"You're going to burn me, aren't you."

"That depends," Sera said, "on whether you keep praying."

The girl considered this. "Then yes. Probably."

* * *

Her name was Pell, though she offered it the way people offer a coin they expect to have stolen anyway. She had no parents she could name—or rather, she named them, and then frowned, and admitted she wasn't sure the names were right. She'd been passed between aunts and neighbors and finally the streets, and somewhere along the way she had discovered that when she prayed, things happened.

Not always. Not dramatically. But lately, always.

"The first time," Pell told her, sitting on the floor of a cell in the abandoned tithe-house Sera still technically had keys to, "I asked for rain because the garden was dying. It rained for nine days and drowned the garden anyway. So I learned to be careful." She held up a finger, lecturing. "You have to be specific. You can't just say *help*. Help is too big. You have to say exactly what you mean."

"And what did you ask for just now? Behind the fish stall?"

"The baker's boy's leg." Pell's chin came up, defiant in the way of children who have already decided the punishment is worth it. "It worked. I heard them say the bone went straight again before the surgeon even got there. They think it was a saint."

"It wasn't a saint."

"No," Pell agreed. "I don't know what it was. It feels like—" She searched for it, frowning. "Like a door opening somewhere far away. Like something leaning down to look through the crack. And it's *hungry*, miss. Not for me. For the asking. It wants me to keep asking."

Sera felt the cold slide down her spine that she had once been trained to interpret as the presence of evil and now simply interpreted as Tuesday. "And you kept asking anyway."

"The baker's boy would have lost his leg."

"Yes," Sera said quietly. "He would have."

Doctrine was very clear on this point. Unsanctioned prayer was heresy. Answered prayer was abomination. The girl should burn, and the thing answering her should be starved back through whatever crack it had leaned through, and Sera should write a tidy report and feel righteous about it.

Sera looked at the girl, who had used a miracle to save a stranger's leg and was now waiting calmly in a cell to be murdered for it, and found that the doctrine and her heart had stopped speaking to each other entirely.

"I'm going to ask you some questions," Sera said. "Answer honestly, and I promise you won't see a pyre today."

"That's what the last inquisitor said," Pell said. "Before he saw me do it."

"What happened to him?"

"He tripped," Pell said, with terrible innocence. "Down the whole staircase. I didn't even pray for it. I think maybe it just... agrees with me sometimes."

Sera stared at her. Against every regulation, against eleven years of training, she laughed—a short, startled sound she hadn't made in months.

"Don't do that again," she managed.

"To staircases?"

"To anything. Ever." She rubbed her face. "Gods help me. What am I going to do with you?"

"You could let me go," Pell suggested.

"I could not."

"You could let me go and also give me bread."

"*Pell.*"

"There's a bakery two streets over that throws out the end loaves at dusk," Pell continued, undeterred. "If you're going to imprison me, the least you can do is imprison me near bread."

* * *

The problem, Sera decided that night, pacing the tithe-house floor while Pell slept curled under her own ash-gray cloak like a cat appropriating territory, was that everything fit.

The answered prayer in the weavers' quarter. The rumors from the coast—fishermen swearing their dead nets had come up full. The wardens' reports of a village outside Vharen where a blighted field had come green overnight, and then the village itself had gone strange, people standing in doorways trying to remember why they lived there. Miracles blooming across the country like mold across bread, and everywhere the same faint wrongness underneath, the same sense of something leaning down to look through a crack.

Something hungry for the asking.

The Order's teaching was simple: the old gods were dead or gone, sealed beyond a gate no map remembered, and any voice that answered prayer was a demon wearing divinity as a mask. Burn the vessel. Starve the voice. Keep the world clean.

But Pell wasn't a vessel. Pell was a child who rationed miracles like bread ends and worried about baker's boys, and whatever leaned down to answer her had healed instead of harmed—had, so far, done nothing but good, wrapped in that faint wrongness like honey wrapped around a hook.

Unless the good *was* the hook. Unless every answered prayer took something. Sera thought of the village gone strange, the people in doorways. Thought of Pell naming her parents and frowning, unsure.

What did the asking cost? Who paid?

She realized she was standing over the sleeping girl, and that Pell had opened one eye.

"You're doing the thinking-out-loud face," Pell said. "You pace and your mouth moves. You should know that about yourself."

"Noted. Go back to sleep."

"Are you going to burn me tomorrow?"

The question was asked without drama, the way children ask about weather. It went through Sera like a blade finding the gap in armor.

"No," Sera said.

"You said that yesterday."

"And I meant it yesterday, and I mean it tonight, and tomorrow I will mean it then. Sleep."

Pell closed her eye. Then, into the dark: "You smell like ash. All inquisitors do. But yours smells like it's *old*. Like it happened a long time ago." A pause. "What burned?"

Sera stood in the dark of the tithe-house and did not answer, and after a while the girl slept, and Sera sat by the cold hearth until dawn, holding a question she couldn't put down: *what am I doing?*

* * *

What she was doing, it turned out, was treason.

She understood this fully three days later, when the Order came asking. A junior inquisitor—Brother Halloway, young, earnest, the kind of man who still believed the ledgers balanced—appeared at her lodgings with questions dressed as courtesies. A god-touched child in the Saltmarket. Witnesses described a woman in ash-gray speaking with her. Had Sister Vane—old habits of address die hard—seen anything?

"No," Sera said.

Halloway looked at her with those clear believing eyes, and Sera watched him decide to trust her, and felt the weight of that trust settle onto her shoulders like a yoke. He left. She stood in her lodgings surrounded by the wreckage of her former life—stripped insignia, confiscated texts, a single letter from Prelate Ossen that she had never answered—and understood that she had just lied to the Order to protect a heretic.

It should have felt worse. That was the alarming part. It should have torn at her the way heresy trials tore at her, all those years of watching people burn for smaller mercies than this. Instead it felt like setting down something heavy she'd forgotten she was carrying.

She went back to the tithe-house. Pell was sitting on the windowsill, watching the street, eating stolen bread with the untroubled conscience of the truly pragmatic.

"Halloway came to my rooms," Sera said. "They're looking for you."

"I know. There's a man who's been walking past the tithe-house twice a day. He has a listening-face." Pell hopped down. "So. Are we running, then?"

Sera blinked. "*We?*"

"Well, I assumed." Pell looked at her as if she were being very slow. "You're not going to burn me. You're not going to hand me over. You've lied to your own people, which I could tell because you came back smelling guilty, like burnt sugar. So either you're keeping me, or you're taking me somewhere else, and either way it's *we* now." She brushed crumbs from her dress with great dignity. "Also I can't run alone. I'm ten."

"You're a fugitive from the Holy Order of Ash."

"You're harboring one. We match."

Sera sat down slowly on the hearthstone and put her head in her hands, and discovered—to her genuine horror—that she was smiling.

"This is madness," she said to the floor. "Do you understand what I'm risking? Eleven years. My name. Everything I was."

"You were going to burn a little girl for saving a boy's leg," Pell said, not cruelly, just precisely, the way she was precise about prayers. "Maybe the everything-you-was needed losing."

Sera lifted her head. Outside, the evening light lay gold along the rooftops of Vharen, and somewhere in that city a gate she didn't yet know existed was failing ward by ward, and a charm-seller she didn't yet know she needed was counting his stock and feeling the ground go soft beneath him. None of that was hers to see yet. What was hers was a small gray-eyed girl watching her with the patience of someone who had already decided how this ended.

"If we run," Sera said, "we run properly. No markets, no begging, no praying where anyone can hear. And you do what I say."

"All right."

"And Pell—you tell me everything. Every prayer. Every answer. What it costs. If something leans down to look at you, you tell me *first*, before you ever lean back."

Pell nodded solemnly. Then: "Can we stop at the bakery?"

"*Pell.*"

"They'll throw the loaves out in an hour. It's practically charity."

Sera Vane, disgraced inquisitor, traitor to the Order of Ash, harborer of heretics, pulled her hood up over her ash-smelling hair and followed a ten-year-old out into the streets of a city that was beginning, very quietly, to forget itself.

Behind them, in the dark of the tithe-house, the cold hearth ticked as it cooled, and if anyone had been listening they might have heard—faint, far away, like a door drawn shut in another room—the sound of something disappointed that its favorite asker had gone.

* * *

They made the edge of the Saltmarket by full dark, and that was where the trouble found them—not the Order, but something stranger. A crowd had gathered at a shrine alcove where a guttering votive candle had gone out, and an old woman was kneeling in the muck, praying aloud for light, her voice cracking with the particular desperation of someone who has nothing left to lose by asking.

Pell stopped. Sera felt her stop, felt the small hand seize her sleeve.

"Miss," Pell whispered. "It's leaning down. Right now. It's right there, and it's looking at her, and it's so *hungry*—"

"Pell. Don't."

"But if I don't ask with her—if I don't ask *carefully*—it'll answer however it likes. It doesn't know how to be gentle unless someone shows it." The girl's gray eyes were wide and wet and absolutely certain. "Please. Teach me fast. How do I make it gentle?"

Above the shrine, unseen by anyone but the two of them, the darkness seemed to gather, attentive as a dog that has heard its name.

Sera looked at the kneeling woman, at the hungry dark, at the child tugging her sleeve—and reached for an answer she did not have.
