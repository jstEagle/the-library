## 6. Doors That Should Stay Shut

Kestrel knew someone was in his shop before he reached the top of the cellar stairs.

Not because of any sound—the intruder was too good for sound—but because of the charms. He kept a row of them above the doorframe, little knotted threads and river stones that did nothing at all except swing when the air moved. All twelve were perfectly still, which meant the door had been opened with a care that no honest person possessed.

He climbed the last step slowly, hands visible, and found Sera Vane sitting behind his counter like she owned it.

She'd made herself tea. That was somehow worse than the knife he could see at her belt. She sat with his good cup—his *good* cup, the one with the blue glaze—and watched him over the rim with the patient expression of a woman who had once burned people for a living and missed it only slightly.

"Your lock is terrible," she said.

"My lock is fine."

"It took me four seconds."

"That's because you're a criminal now," Kestrel said. "Disgraced inquisitors don't count as honest people either. The lock works exactly as intended on everyone else." He came around the counter, keeping the distance between them polite and the distance to the cellar door memorized. "You're supposed to be hunting whoever answered that prayer in Tanner's Row. I'd have thought you'd be halfway across the city by now."

"I was." She set down the cup. "I followed the trail here instead."

Kestrel felt the floor tilt under him, just slightly, the way it always did when the wards beneath his shop stirred. "There's no trail here. I sell luck-charms. They're decorative. A child could make better ones, and frequently does—I buy stock from children."

"The trail isn't magic," Sera said. "It's absence. Everything the prayer touched left a hole behind it. A hole shaped like a miracle." She stood, unhurried, and he noticed how carefully she moved, how she never turned her back on the cellar door even when she pretended to. "And every hole points here. Like water running downhill. Like something underneath this city is *drinking*."

The kettle on his stove began, very softly, to whistle. Neither of them looked at it.

"You've been down there," Sera said quietly. It wasn't a question. "Under the shop. I've watched this building for three days. You go down every night with a lamp and a ledger, and you come up looking like a man who's been arguing with someone who won't answer."

Kestrel weighed his options. Run—he wouldn't make the door. Fight—she'd been trained by the Order and he'd been trained by nobody, unless you counted seven years of hauling ward-stones as training, which, honestly, it half was. Lie—

He was tired of lying. It occurred to him, standing in his own shop with an enemy drinking his tea, that he had been tired of it for years.

"There's a gate under my shop," he said.

Sera didn't move. But something in her face shifted, the way ice shifts on a pond before it breaks—a crack running somewhere deep.

"A sealed one," he went on. "Older than Vharen. Older than the Order, though they built their spire on top of its memory because that's what the Order does, it builds on things and calls it faith. I keep the wards maintained. That's all. That's my whole life, Sera. I sell useless charms upstairs so I can spend my nights making sure nothing comes through that door."

"And the prayer in Tanner's Row?"

"Wasn't me." He said it flatly, and meant it, and hated that he needed to say it at all. "Something answered that child without going through my gate. Or—" he hesitated, and the hesitation cost him, because Sera caught it the way she probably caught everything, "—or through a door I don't know about. There shouldn't *be* other doors. That's rather the point of doors. One each."

Sera was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "Show me the girl first."

Kestrel blinked. "What girl?"

* * *

The girl was asleep in the back room of a chandler's shop on Candle Street, wrapped in Sera's own cloak, and Kestrel knew within three steps of seeing her that she was the most dangerous thing in Vharen.

She couldn't have been more than ten. Brown hair, chapped hands, a smudge of soot on one cheek like any other market child. But the air around her moved wrong. Not cold, not hot—just *attentive*, the way a room feels when someone is listening behind the wall. The charms in Kestrel's pocket, the ones that did nothing, were all leaning toward her like iron filings toward a lodestone.

"Her name is Pell," Sera said, arms crossed, watching him watch the child. "I arrested her two days ago for answered prayer. Standard procedure. She was supposed to go to the pyre yesterday morning."

"She's not ash."

"No." Sera's jaw worked, as if the next words physically hurt coming out. "I told the tribunal the evidence was contaminated. I told them I needed her alive for questioning. I signed a statement saying I would deliver her to the pyre myself after examination." She looked away. "Then I put her in a cloak and walked her out the side door of the Bastion like a smuggler with stolen lace, and I cannot tell you why, Kestrel Halloran, and that frightens me more than anything your gate has ever done."

Kestrel crouched by the sleeping girl. Up close, the attention in the air was stronger—it pressed against his skin like the moment before a storm breaks. Her lips moved in her sleep. Prayers, he realized. She prayed in her sleep. Small ones. *Let the bread rise. Let Da's cough ease. Let the dog come home.*

And around her, faint as breath on glass, the world *leaned in to listen.*

"She doesn't need the gate," he murmured. "She *is* a door."

"That's what I was afraid you'd say."

Pell woke the way cats wake—all at once, eyes open, body still. She saw Kestrel and scrambled back against the wall, and the candle on the table flared six inches tall, and every charm in Kestrel's pocket sang a single clear note that no charm should have been able to sing.

"It's all right," Sera said, and her voice changed entirely—went soft, went careful, went somewhere Kestrel had not expected a disgraced inquisitor to have. "Pell. This is the charm-seller I told you about. He's going to help us."

"Are you a priest?" Pell asked Kestrel. Her voice was hoarse, small. "The lady says I'm not supposed to pray anymore. But I can't stop. They just—they come out. Like sneezing."

Kestrel sat down on the floor, deliberately, at her level, the way you'd approach a spooked horse or a live grenade. "What do they feel like? The answers. When they come out."

Pell considered this with the gravity of a much older person. "Like pulling thread," she said. "Out of the world. And then the world has a hole where the thread was." Her brow furrowed. "A lady came to our street last month. Old, bent over. She asked me to pray for her son, gone to sea. So I did. And the next day he was home." She smiled, briefly, brilliantly, and it broke Kestrel's heart. "But the baker's wife forgot her own wedding. And the fountain on Cooper Lane went dry. And nobody remembers what the fountain was *for*, they just walk past the empty basin like it was always empty." The smile faded. "I didn't mean to take those things. The thread has to come from somewhere."

Kestrel looked up at Sera. Sera was already looking at him, and he watched the same arithmetic finish behind her eyes that had finished behind his.

"The land," he said quietly. "It's draining the land. Every answered prayer pulls something out of the world and pushes it... elsewhere. Into the gods' realm. That's why the wards are failing—not because something's battering the door. Because the whole country is being siphoned through a hundred thousand cracks like this one, and the pressure's shifting, and my gate is feeling it like a dam feels a flood."

"Then we stop her praying," Sera said.

"You can't stop a sneeze," Pell said, with the weary authority of experience. "I tried. I held my nose and everything."

"We bind her," Sera pressed. "The Order has rites—"

"The Order's rites are designed to feed things to the gods, not starve them," Kestrel snapped, and immediately regretted it, because Sera went very still.

"How do you know that?"

Because he'd read the founders' ledgers, down in the dark, seven years ago, on his first night as keeper. Because the gate hadn't been built to keep the old gods out. Because the Order had known, all along, exactly what answered prayers cost, and had spent three centuries burning the wrong people while the real leak ran underneath everything.

But that was a confession with a long tail, and he wasn't ready to give it. Not yet. Maybe not ever.

"I know things," he said. "It's a long story involving a lot of stairs."

Pell tugged his sleeve. When he turned, she was looking at him with those too-old eyes, and the attentive air around her had gone very quiet, very focused, the way it must have focused on the old woman's prayer.

"You're sad," she said. "Underneath. Like the fountain. Empty basin, but you remember what it was for." She tilted her head. "Who did you lose?"

Kestrel opened his mouth and found, to his genuine surprise, that he didn't know. There was a shape in him, a grief-shaped hole, worn smooth as a river stone from years of touching it—and no name inside it. No face. Just the certainty of loss, like a coat that fit someone once.

Behind him, he heard Sera's breath catch. She'd seen his face do whatever his face had done.

"The country," he said at last, and his voice came out strange. "I think I lost the country, Pell. I think we all did, a long time ago, and nobody noticed because the forgetting took the noticing too."

* * *

They sat around the chandler's back-room table until well past midnight, and by the end of it Kestrel had committed treason in front of a former inquisitor, which he suspected was either the bravest or stupidest thing he'd ever done, and possibly both.

He showed Sera the cellar.

She descended the stairs with her hand on her knife the entire way, which he chose to find flattering rather than insulting. At the bottom, the gate waited in its alcove of black stone—tall, seamless, older than the city's oldest bones—with the wards strung across it in lines of pale light that should have burned steady as stars.

Three of them were guttering.

"They've never done that," Kestrel said, and heard the fear in his own voice and didn't bother hiding it. Hiding things had gotten expensive lately. "Seven years, and they've never flickered. Whatever's happening out there—" he gestured vaguely upward, toward the city, the country, the whole forgetting world, "—it's reaching critical. The pressure's too high. If these fail—"

"What comes through?"

"I don't know. That's the honest answer. Something that prays *to us*, maybe. Something hungry enough to reach through a child's sneeze and drag a fountain dry. The old gods weren't kind, Sera. They weren't cruel either. They were *custodians*, and custodians who forget what they're guarding become something else entirely."

Sera stood before the gate a long time. In the ward-light her face looked younger and harder at once, stripped of the tribunal's certainty, and Kestrel understood that he was watching a woman's entire architecture of belief quietly collapse and rebuild itself in real time.

"I burned forty-one people," she said finally. "Over eleven years. I checked every case twice. I was *careful*, Kestrel. Careful was my religion." She touched the black stone of the gate, lightly, the way you'd touch a grave. "And the whole time, the real heresy was happening under a charm-shop, and the real miracle-workers were children, and the gods themselves were—" she laughed, short and bitter, "—were *leaking*. Through everything. Through everyone."

"Not everyone," Kestrel said. "Just the god-touched. Just Pell, and whoever else like her is out there, praying holes into the world."

"Then we find them." Sera turned, and her eyes had a new light in them—not the old righteous fire but something steadier, something that had survived the fire. "Before the Order does. Before Ossen does. Because if the Order learns what she is, they won't examine her, Kestrel. They'll *use* her. A girl who can pull miracles out of the world is worth more to them than a hundred pyres."

"You're proposing we run."

"I'm proposing we run *together*, which is different, and considerably more humiliating for both of us."

From the top of the stairs, a small voice: "Do I get a say?"

They both looked up. Pell stood at the cellar door in her borrowed cloak, having ignored instructions, orders, and presumably several locked doors between herself and the stairs. She looked at the gate with no fear at all—which frightened Kestrel more than anything else that night.

"I know that door," Pell said. "I've dreamed it. It dreams back, sometimes. It's lonely." She came down the stairs, bare feet silent on stone, and stopped beside Kestrel, and slipped her small hand into his large one as naturally as if she'd done it a hundred times. "Where are we running to?"

Kestrel looked at Sera. Sera looked at Kestrel. Somewhere above them, the city slept on, dreaming prayers into the dark, each one a tiny thread pulled loose from the world.

"East," Kestrel said, because it was the only direction he had. "There's a smuggler named Doss Marrow who owes me a betrayal, and if anyone knows the roads that aren't on maps anymore, it's him." He grimaced. "Fair warning: he'll want something awful in exchange. He always does."

Pell squeezed his hand. "That's all right," she said serenely. "So do I."

Kestrel looked down at her. "What could you possibly want?"

"To see the fountain work again," Pell said simply. "Somebody has to remember what it was for. Might as well be the people who broke it."

Above them, faint through seven floors of stone and timber, the third ward guttered—and this time, it did not come back.
