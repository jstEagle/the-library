## 7. The Country With No Name

Pell had never been east of Vharen before. She'd never been much of anywhere, really, unless you counted the cellar of the Sisters of Quiet Mercy, and she didn't, because the Sisters had made it very clear that Pell herself was not supposed to be counted among the things that existed.

So when Doss Marrow handed her the reins of a mule named Turnip and said, "Congratulations, you're a traveler now," she held onto those reins like they were the only true thing in the world.

Turnip was gray and smelled like a wet blanket left in a barn, and Pell loved him immediately.

They rode out through the eastern gate at dawn, four of them and one mule: Kestrel up front with his hood down like he wanted the wind to know his face, Sera sitting her horse the way she probably sat everything—straight-backed, jaw set, as if the horizon owed her money—and Doss bringing up the rear, chewing on a straw and narrating their journey to nobody.

"Day one," he announced. "The fugitives flee into the wilds. The mule is unimpressed."

"The mule is right to be," Sera said.

Pell laughed, and then caught herself laughing, and then felt strange about it, because laughing was not something that happened around her very often, and certainly not while the world behind them was coming apart at the seams.

Because it was. She could see it. That was the whole problem.

* * *

It started small, about an hour out from the walls. They passed a farmhouse—stone walls, slate roof, a dog chained by the well who barked itself hoarse at Turnip—and Pell looked back at it, the way she always looked back at things, memorizing without meaning to.

Then she looked forward again, and something tugged at her chest, and she turned around fully in her saddle.

The farmhouse was still there. But the *name* of it was gone.

She couldn't explain how she knew a farmhouse had a name, or how she could feel its absence, the way you feel a tooth missing with your tongue. But there it was: a smooth blank spot where something should have been, like a word rubbed off a page. The dog barked. The well stood. And whatever the place had been called—the thing people said when they pointed toward it over supper, the name carved somewhere on a lintel or a fence post—that was simply no longer anywhere.

"You're staring," Kestrel said quietly. He hadn't turned around. He didn't need to; she was learning that about him. He watched the road ahead but he saw everything, including her.

"There was a name," Pell said. "On that farm. It's gone."

Kestrel's shoulders did the thing they did when he was carrying something heavy and pretending it was light. "How gone?"

"Gone gone." She searched for better words and found none. "Like somebody licked their thumb and wiped it off the world."

Doss spat his straw into the ditch. "Charming image, little miss. Thank you for that."

"It's early," Kestrel said, mostly to himself. "Vharen's wards are still holding most of it back. It'll be worse the farther we go." He glanced at her, and his eyes were kind and tired in equal measure. "You'll see things the rest of us can't. If it gets too much, you tell me. Not Sera—she'll try to fix it. Not Doss—he'll try to sell it. Me."

"That's slander," said Doss.

"It's testimony," said Sera.

Pell nodded, and kept the reins loose, and did not tell him that it was already too much, and had been since the day prayers started answering her back.

* * *

By midday they reached a village called Brindleford—or it had been called Brindleford, once, though when Pell asked a woman drawing water from the trough what the village was named, the woman frowned like Pell had asked her to count to a number that didn't exist.

"Named?" the woman said. "It's just... here."

"Just here," Pell repeated.

"Well, it's not *there*, is it?" The woman gestured vaguely at everywhere else, satisfied with her logic, and went back to her water.

They stopped anyway, because Kestrel insisted on buying bread wherever bread was sold, and because Doss claimed that a man could learn more from a village baker than from a year of priests, which Sera said explained a great deal about Doss.

Pell sat on the mule cart's tailgate with her heels knocking against the wood, eating the heel of a loaf, watching the village the way other people watched fires.

She could see the forgetting happening. Not all at once—it came in pieces, like frost creeping across a window. A shutter would hang crooked and then hang *unexplained*, the crookedness forgotten even as the wood stayed warped. A child ran past chasing a hoop, and Pell heard the mother call a name, and then heard the name arrive at the child's ears and slide off, unheard, and the mother's mouth keep moving around a shape that meant nothing anymore. The child didn't turn around. Why would she? Nobody had called her.

"Pell." Kestrel crouched in front of her, breaking the loaf he'd bought into pieces he pretended were for everyone. "You've gone somewhere. Where?"

"They're forgetting each other," she said. "Not the buildings. Each other. Look at the well."

He looked. Two men stood at the well rope, talking, laughing. Ordinary. Except that every few sentences, one of them would pause—a tiny hitch, a blink—and reorient, like a man waking briefly on a boat. Pell could see the words falling between them, the ones with names in them, the ones with *remember when*. The two men laughed anyway. Laughter was the last thing to go.

"How long has this been happening?" Sera asked. She'd come up quiet, arms crossed, and her voice had that flat carefulness she used when doctrine and her own eyes disagreed.

"I don't know. I think—" Pell hugged the bread to her chest. "I think it's been happening for a long time. Slowly. Like water getting under a door. And now the door's open."

"And you can see it," Kestrel said. "All of it."

"All of it," Pell confirmed miserably. "Every bit. It's like being the only person at a funeral who knew the person who died."

Nobody had anything clever to say to that, which Pell appreciated. Cleverness would have been unbearable just then.

* * *

They camped that night in the ruins of a shrine—not ruined, exactly, Pell noted, but *forgotten*: the stones whole, the roof sound, and yet moss grown thick over the altar as if centuries had passed instead of years. No birds nested in it. Even ivy seemed uncertain whether it was allowed.

Kestrel built a fire small enough to hide from the sky. Doss produced sausages from somewhere on his person that Pell decided not to ask about. Sera took first watch without being asked, because taking watch was the one prayer she still permitted herself, and Pell understood that better than anyone would have guessed.

Pell lay with her head on Turnip's warm side—the mule had been promoted to pillow, over his mild objections—and listened to the others talk in low voices about roads and distances and how many days to the capital.

And she watched the road behind them unspool.

That was the only word for it. In the firelight, looking west, back the way they'd come, she could see the road doing something roads should not do. It wasn't disappearing. It was *loosening*—the ruts softening, the wheel-tracks fading, the milestones going blank one letter at a time, until the road wasn't a road to anywhere so much as a scar across grass that was slowly remembering it had never been cut.

And under the loosening, faint as breath on glass, she could see what was underneath. The old country. The one with no name. Fields that had been fields before this country grew over them like bark over a wound. Little lights moving in patterns that weren't villages and weren't stars. Something vast and patient turning over in its sleep beneath the ground, murmuring names in a language made entirely of longing.

One of the murmurs sounded almost like her name.

Pell sat up so fast that Turnip complained.

"What is it?" Kestrel was awake instantly. Of course he was. He slept like a man guarding a door, because for seven years he had been.

"It said my name." Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "Underneath. The old country. It knows me."

Kestrel came and sat beside her, close enough that she could feel the warmth of him, which was a strange warmth, banked and steady, like coals under ash. "What else do you hear?"

"It's not hearing exactly. It's more like..." She waved a hand helplessly. "Like when you know someone's standing behind you even though they haven't spoken. The whole land is standing behind us. And it's trying to remember why it knows our faces."

"Does it seem angry?"

Pell considered this seriously, because Kestrel asked serious questions and deserved serious answers. "No. Hungry. But not eat-you hungry. More like—" She thought of the Sisters' cellar, of the years of meals eaten alone, of praying every night to a god who never answered and slowly understanding that the silence wasn't cruelty, it was deafness. "Lonely hungry. The kind where you'd eat anything just to have someone at the table."

Kestrel was quiet for a long time. When he finally spoke, his voice was rough. "That's the truest thing anyone's said about them yet."

"You've heard them too?"

"No." He looked into the fire. "But I've been keeping a door shut on them for seven years, and I always hoped it was because they were monsters. It's easier to guard against monsters." He smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "Go back to sleep, Pell. I'll take your watch too."

"That's not fair to you."

"Neither is what you can see." He tapped her gently on the shoulder. "Sleep. That's an order from a charm-seller, which means it carries no legal weight, but I'd appreciate it anyway."

She slept. And dreamed of a table, long and empty, set for thousands, and a voice asking politely, endlessly, *is anyone coming, is anyone coming, is anyone coming.*

* * *

Three days east, the forgetting got teeth.

They came over a rise in late afternoon and found a town in the valley below—chimneys smoking, bells ringing the hour, laundry strung between windows like festival flags. A living town. Doss, who had a map that he consulted with great theatrical suspicion, declared it to be Harrowmere, market town, good inn, better cheese.

Except that when they rode down into it, the town was not there.

There was a valley. There was a river. There was a ruin—old stone, roof fallen in, a well choked with leaves—and a signpost at the crossroads with no letters on it at all, just four blank boards pointing confidently in four directions.

"But I *saw* it," Doss said, for the fourth time. He had dismounted and was walking the ruin's perimeter, touching walls, growing less theatrical by the minute. "Smoke. Bells. I heard a dog. I don't imagine dogs, Sera. Dogs are beneath my imagination."

"You saw what the land remembers seeing," Pell said quietly.

Everyone turned to look at her. She wished they wouldn't. Being the only witness was bad enough; being the *examined* witness was worse.

"It's like..." She struggled. Words were not her strong suit; answers were, and answers were currently the most dangerous thing she owned. "It's like a coat worn through at the elbows. From far away it looks fine. Up close, the threads are gone. The town isn't erased yet. It's just... only visible from far away, where the holes don't show."

Sera dismounted and walked to the well. She stood over it for a long moment, and Pell watched her inquisitor's mind working behind her eyes like a millwheel—turning evidence, grinding certainty. "How long," Sera said at last, "before the far view goes too? Before there's nothing left to remember seeing?"

Pell looked west, back along the road they'd traveled, at the smooth green places where farms and shrines and one entire bridge used to be.

"I don't think there's a rule," she said. "I think it depends on how much the gods eat."

Nobody liked that answer. Pell could tell, because Kestrel went still in the particular way he did when he was adding a weight to a load he'd already decided to carry, and Sera's hand moved to the sword she technically wasn't allowed to carry anymore, and Doss said, cheerfully and falsely, "Well! Cheese awaits in the next valley, surely," and got back on his horse.

Only Turnip seemed calm. Pell leaned against his neck and took comfort in the fact that at least one member of their company had no idea what was happening and intended to remain serene about it.

* * *

They made cold camp that night, no fire, because Sera said fires drew the Order's remnant and Kestrel said she was right, and Doss grumbled about sausage logistics. They lay in a fold of hills under a sky crowded with unfamiliar stars—which was itself wrong, Pell realized, because these were the same stars that hung over Vharen, and yet they looked rearranged, as if the sky, too, was having trouble keeping its facts straight.

She couldn't sleep. So she did what she always did when she couldn't sleep, the thing that had gotten her arrested, hidden, hauled across a dissolving country by three strangers who were becoming, terrifyingly, the closest thing to family she'd ever had.

She prayed.

Not to ask for anything. Just to listen. She'd learned that trick in the Sisters' cellar: pray empty, palms open, and sometimes something answered. Lately the something was always the same—the old country underneath, the lonely hunger, the murmur of names.

Tonight it was closer. Tonight it wasn't murmuring.

*Keeper,* it said, and the word rolled through her like a cart over a bridge, and she knew—with the absolute certainty that was her curse and her gift—that it wasn't talking to her.

It was talking to Kestrel, asleep ten feet away with his back to a stone, one hand curled loosely near the knife he never quite touched but never quite left.

*Keeper. We remember the door. We forget the keeper. Come remind us.*

Pell sat bolt upright, heart hammering, and found Kestrel already awake and looking at her across the dead campfire, his face lit by starlight gone strange.

"It spoke to you," he said. Not a question.

"It spoke *about* you." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "It called you Keeper. It said—" she made herself say it exactly, because exactness was the one thing she was for— "*We remember the door. We forget the keeper. Come remind us.*"

For a moment Kestrel didn't move at all. Then, very softly, in the voice of a man reading his own obituary, he said:

"It's never spoken before. Seven years, and the wards never said a word." He looked east, toward the misted horizon where a spire waited that only some of them could see. "It's not forgetting faster because it's starving, Pell. It's forgetting faster because it's *looking for me.*"

Behind them, far to the west, a bell rang in a town that no longer existed. Pell was the only one who heard it.
