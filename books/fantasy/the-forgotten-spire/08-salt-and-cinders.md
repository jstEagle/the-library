## 8. Salt and Cinders

Doss Marrow had three rules for a life well lived, and he recited them to himself every morning like a prayer to a god he didn't believe in. One: never sleep in the same bed twice. Two: never owe anybody anything you can't pay back in coin. Three: never, under any circumstances, tell the truth before noon.

It was well past noon, the barge was becalmed on a river that kept forgetting which direction it flowed, and Kestrel Halloran was sitting across from him sharpening a knife with the patience of a man who had all the time in the world and intended to use every second of it making Doss uncomfortable.

"He's going to keep doing that," Doss said to Pell.

The girl sat cross-legged on a crate of salted herring, turning one of Kestrel's luck-charms over in her fingers. The charm glowed faintly whenever she held it, which was not supposed to happen, and which Doss tried very hard not to think about. "Probably," Pell said.

"Until I talk."

"Definitely until you talk."

Doss looked at Sera Vane, hoping for an ally. The disgraced inquisitor stood at the rail with her arms folded, watching the shoreline where—last he'd checked—the village of Brindleford had been, and wasn't anymore, and might be again if the river felt generous. She did not look at him. That was its own kind of answer.

"You know," Doss said, "in most companies, when a man says *I'll explain everything, just get us downriver first*, that's considered a complete transaction. You got us downriver. We're square. Everybody goes home happy."

"We're not going home," Kestrel said. He didn't look up from the knife. *Shhk. Shhk.* "There's no home to go to. It's being eaten behind us. So you'll understand if I want the whole story, not the parts of it that were convenient for you."

*Shhk.*

"And I'd like it," Sera said quietly, still facing the water, "before we reach the ford. If there's going to be an ambush at the ford, I would prefer to walk into it knowing why."

That landed. Doss felt it land, right in the soft place under his ribs where he kept all the things he'd spent twenty years not thinking about.

He sighed, long and theatrical, because if he was going to do this he was at least going to make it look like a favor. "Fine. Fine. But somebody's getting me something to drink first, because this is a two-cup story at minimum, and I'm not telling it dry."

Pell scrambled off the herring crate before anyone could stop her, rummaged through the galley, and came back with a bottle of plum brandy that Doss had specifically hidden from her. She poured four cups with the solemnity of a priestess. Then she hesitated, looked at the bottle, and poured a fifth, setting it on the deck near the rail.

"For the village?" Kestrel asked softly.

"For Brindleford," Pell said. "In case it comes back and gets thirsty."

Nobody laughed. But nobody told her to pour it out either, and Doss found that his throat had gone tight over half a cup of nothing, so he took a drink of the actual brandy and got on with it.

* * *

"I was nineteen," he said. "Skinny as a whipstock, mean as a wet cat, and clever enough to know exactly how stupid I was. There was a woman in Corvess—my mother, if you're asking, though nobody who knew her would use that word—and she used to say, *Doss, you've got two talents. You can find anything, and you can lose anything. Pray you learn the difference before it matters.*"

He turned his cup in his fingers. The brandy caught the late light and threw it around.

"The Order found me first. Before the Inquisition split off, before all the burning—they were one thing then, the Order of the Spire, and they were looking for a boy who could find things. Not trinkets. Not smuggled silk. *Things.* Things that had been lost so thoroughly even their owners had forgotten owning them." He glanced at Pell. "You know how a thing can go missing and everyone just... stops asking about it? Like the missing itself is a coat hung over it?"

Pell nodded slowly. "Like the villages."

"Like the villages." His mouth tasted of salt and cinders, though the brandy was plum. "They called it retrieval work. I called it grave-robbing without the graves. I'd go into houses where a family had lost a daughter—not dead, *lost*, unwritten—and I'd come out with her hairbrush, her christening spoon, whatever she'd touched most. And the Order's artificers would hold those objects and read them, the way you'd read a letter, and they'd write down what they learned. Names. Faces. Whole streets of people that the world had stopped remembering."

"Why?" Sera asked. She'd turned from the rail at last. Her voice had the flat carefulness of someone standing on ice and listening for cracks. "Why collect them?"

"That's the part I didn't understand for years. That's the part I ran from." Doss drained his cup and poured another, because rules were rules but a man had to be practical. "You've all heard the doctrine. The gate beneath the spire keeps the old gods out. Sealed door, hungry things on the other side, brave wardens holding the line. Every child in Vharen learns it. Every burning I ever watched from a safe distance was justified by it."

Kestrel had stopped sharpening the knife. He was very still, in the way of a man listening with his whole body.

"It's a lie," Doss said. "Or—it's worse than a lie. It's a lie built on top of a truth, which is the sturdiest kind. The gate doesn't keep the gods out." He made himself say it plainly, because he owed these people plainness and had been paying them in installments for seven years. "The gate keeps a *promise* in."

"A promise," Kestrel repeated.

"An oath. A vow. Made by the gods themselves, back when they were young and the country—" he gestured vaguely eastward, toward the mist, toward the shapeless ache where a name should have been, "—back when the country still had a name. They swore to remember it. To hold every soul of it in their keeping, forever. That oath is bound inside the gate. It's the lock. And the wards aren't walls, Kestrel. They're *reminders*. Every ward-stone is a sentence of the vow, written so the gods can't forget it. The gate was never a prison for monsters. It was a knot tied around a promise, so the promise couldn't slip loose and couldn't be broken."

Silence, except for the river slapping the hull and forgetting, mid-slap, what it was doing, and starting again.

Sera spoke first, and her voice cracked along a fault line Doss recognized, because he'd heard it in his own head for twenty years. "Then the burnings. The doctrine. All of it—"

"Was scaffolding," Doss said. "Built by men who decided the promise was too heavy to carry and too dangerous to drop. You can't tell a frightened country that its gods are bound by law instead of love. So they gave the people a war instead. Gods outside, us inside, burn the witches. Simple. Clean. And every miracle the Order suppressed, every answered prayer they drowned in ash—that was maintenance. Because here's the ugly heart of it." He leaned forward. "A promise only holds while both sides keep it. The gods swore to remember. But somebody had to swear to *be worth remembering*. And the Order, somewhere back along the way, decided the country had failed its side of the bargain. Decided we were small, and cruel, and forgettable. So they started feeding the memory out. Bit by bit. Letting the gods forget us on purpose, so the gate would rot, so the vow would die of neglect, and then—" he spread his hands, "—no more promise, no more obligation. Free and clear. The biggest debt repudiation in history, and they dressed it up as holy war."

Pell had gone white. "The prayers," she whispered. "The ones being answered now, the hungry ones. That's the gate failing. That's the promise coming apart."

"That's the promise coming apart," Doss agreed. "And when it goes entirely, the gods won't invade. Worse. They'll wake up, look at this country, and feel *nothing*. No hatred. No hunger, even. Just the blankness you feel toward a stranger's dream. And everything the light touches will finish sliding out of the world, gentle as sand through fingers."

* * *

He'd expected anger. He'd rehearsed for anger—he was good at anger, it bounced off him like rain off waxed canvas. What he hadn't prepared for was Kestrel simply sitting there, knife forgotten, looking at him with an expression Doss couldn't file anywhere.

"How do you know all this?" Kestrel asked finally. "Truly. Retrieval work explains stolen hairbrushes. It doesn't explain the architecture of the gate."

And there it was. The real confession, the one under the confession, the one he'd been carrying so long it had grown roots.

"Because I built part of it," Doss said.

The river went quiet. Even it seemed to be listening.

"Not the stones. I'm no mason. The *finding*. When the Order raised the spire, the vow needed anchors—objects soaked in the country's memory, one for each great family of the vow, buried in the ward-foundations. Thirteen of them. And there was exactly one person alive who could locate things the world had already let go of." He laughed, and it came out wrong, thin as tin. "Me. Nineteen years old, and I found twelve of the thirteen. Spent four years at it. Held relics older than kingdoms in my hands and felt them *hum*, felt them glad to be of use, and I thought—I actually thought—I was saving something."

"What happened to the thirteenth?" Pell asked. Of course it was Pell. Children always asked the question the adults were politely not asking.

Doss looked at the fifth cup, the one for Brindleford, sitting full and untouched on the deck.

"There was a village," he said. "Up in the hills past Corvess. Salt-miners. The anchor for that region was a bell—the village bell, cast from salt-crusted ore, rung at every birth and every wedding and every funeral for nine hundred years. It had been lost. Unwritten. The Order sent me to find it, and I did, and then—" His hands had gone steady, which surprised him; he'd expected them to shake. "Then the Inquisition came to the village the same week. There'd been a healing. A miner's daughter, fevered, prayed over by the wrong sort of grandmother. The local prelate wanted a burning to make an example. And I stood in the square with the bell wrapped in sacking on my cart, and I knew—if I rang it, if I gave it back to them first, the sound would remind the whole valley of itself. Remind them so hard the prelate's ash-priests wouldn't be able to unmake it. Memory is the one fire can't catch, if it's loud enough."

"But you didn't ring it," Kestrel said. Not accusing. Just placing the stone on the table, face up.

"I sold it to the Order instead." The words came out level, and that was the worst part, that after all these years they could come out level. "Full price. Plus a bonus, because I told myself the Order knew best, told myself the vow needed completing, told myself every lie a clever man tells when the true one costs too much. They sank that bell in the foundation, and the village burned the next morning, and within a year nobody remembered there'd ever been a village there at all. The valley filled with mist. Good grazing land, the maps said, uninhabited." He picked up the fifth cup and drank it himself, because Brindleford hadn't come back yet and thirst was thirst. "Except it wasn't uninhabited, was it? It was *forgotten*. And I did that. Me, with my two talents. I finally learned the difference between finding and losing, and I learned it by being the best loser the Order ever employed."

* * *

For a long moment nobody moved. Then Pell slid off the herring crate, crossed the deck, and sat down beside him—not touching, just near, the way you'd sit beside a hurt animal you didn't want to startle.

"The bell," she said. "Is it still in the foundation?"

"In the spire's root, far as I know. Deepest anchor of the thirteen."

"And if it were taken out. If it were *rung*—"

"Don't." Doss's voice came out harder than he meant. "Girl, don't stand in a boat and hand a drowning man a rope made of maybe."

"She's right, though," Kestrel said slowly, and Doss watched the warden's mind working behind his eyes, watch him turn the idea over like a ward-stone, reading it. "If the anchors are sentences of the vow, and the vow is failing because the Order starved it—one restored anchor might slow the unraveling. Might even show us where the others are. Doss. You found twelve once."

"I was nineteen."

"You're fifty-something and ornery now. Presumably better at it."

"Fifty-*one*," Doss said automatically, and then cursed, because that was the trap, that was always the trap with Kestrel—you argued about the wrong thing and woke up having agreed to the right one. "Even if I could still do it—even if the talent hasn't rusted shut—the anchors are under the spire. Under the *gate*. Which means going to the one place in creation the Order wants us, the gods are starving toward, and I have spent twenty years running from."

"Yes," Sera said. She had that inquisitor's calm again, but it sat differently on her now, less like armor and more like a decision. "And Ossen will be there. My former mentor, feeding a congregation to the dark because he thinks starvation is salvation. If what you're saying is true, Marrow, then everything I burned, everything I believed, was scaffolding over a theft." Her jaw tightened. "I would like to be present when the bill comes due."

Doss looked around the little boat—at the warden who'd trusted him once and paid for it in seven years of hiding, at the heretic who'd committed treason for a child she'd known a day, at the girl who poured drinks for vanished villages—and felt the last of his deflections crumble like wet ash.

"One condition," he said. "When we get to the spire, and we dig up that bell, and it rings—" his voice caught, and he let it, because apparently today was a day for paying debts, "—somebody says their names. Out loud. All the names we've lost along the road. Somebody has to. That's how bells work. They don't make the memory. They just give it somewhere to live."

Pell reached over and took his hand. Her palm was warm, and under the warmth there was something else, something vast and patient, like the sea deciding to be gentle.

"Doss," she said, "look east."

He looked. The mist over the water had thinned, and through it—faint, impossible, unmistakable—a shape was rising against the evening sky. Tall. Narrow. Crowned with a light that no lamp had ever made.

The spire had noticed them coming.

"Well," Doss said, reaching for the bottle. "Damn it all. I *told* you this was a two-cup story."
