## 9. The Choir of Ash

Sera Vane had spent eleven years learning to read a battlefield the way other people read scripture: quickly, hungrily, and with the certain knowledge that most of what was written there was lies.

The road east of Drenn's Hollow was telling her three lies already.

The first lie was the quiet. Too quiet. Farmers in this country sang to their oxen, argued with their wives at full volume, and prayed at the tops of their lungs because prayer had started working again and nobody wanted to waste the opportunity. A silent stretch of road meant someone had made it silent.

The second lie was the crows. They sat in the hedgerow like an audience waiting for a play to begin, and crows only did that when there would soon be something to eat.

The third lie was Pell. The girl had gone still on the cart bench, her small hands pressed flat against her sternum, and she said, without opening her eyes, "There are men in the trees. They're singing."

"Singing," Kestrel repeated, from the driver's seat.

"Church singing." Pell's brow furrowed. "But wrong. Like they learned all the words and none of the meaning."

Sera felt the words land in her chest like stones dropped down a well. She knew that song. She had sung it herself, ten thousand times, in incense-thick halls with her voice braided among forty others. *The Ashen Choir*, the Order called it — the hymn they sang before a burning, the one that turned fire into sacrament.

"They found us," Doss said. He said it the way he said everything, flat and fast, already reaching under the cart seat for the crossbow he pretended wasn't there. "Told you the girl's prayers were a beacon. Told you. Nobody listens to Doss."

"You told us she was a walking bonfire, yes," Kestrel said. "You also told us the ferryman at Corrin's Ford owed you a favor, and we both remember how that went."

"He owed me a favor. He just also hated me. Those things can coexist."

"Everyone hates you. It coexists with everything."

Sera let them talk. It was what they did instead of panicking, and she'd learned in the last two weeks that this strange, bickering little caravan — a disgraced charm-seller, a smuggler with a face like a closed ledger, and a god-touched child who hummed when she was frightened — panicked badly enough without encouragement.

She stood up on the moving cart and looked back down the road.

They came out of the treeline the way she'd once taught novices to come out of a treeline: no shouting, no charge, just a slow unfolding, gray-robed figures stepping between the trunks until there were too many to count and the forest itself seemed to have grown a congregation. Thirty. Perhaps forty. At their center walked a tall man in white ash-gray vestments, his hands folded behind his back, his head bare despite the cold, as if the weather were a subordinate who would not dare.

Prelate Ossen.

Sera's stomach performed a slow, deliberate revolution.

"Oh," she said quietly. "Oh, no."

"What?" Kestrel didn't turn around. "How bad?"

"It's Ossen."

The talking stopped. Even the horse seemed to understand, ears swiveling back.

"Which one is Ossen?" Pell whispered.

"The man who taught me everything I know," Sera said. "And then some things I wish I didn't."

* * *

She had been nineteen the first time Prelate Ossen noticed her existence. She'd been scrubbing the flagstones of the Penitent Hall — a punishment for asking, in front of the whole novitiate, why the Order burned healers if healing was a gift of the Flame — and he had stopped beside her, looked down at her red knuckles, and said:

"Good questions are worth sore knees, Inquisitor-to-be. Ask better ones tomorrow."

He had never punished her curiosity. That was the trap of him, the beautiful, terrible architecture of him. Every other prelate wanted obedience; Ossen wanted understanding, and he would walk you to it himself, hour by hour, verse by verse, until you arrived at his conclusions and believed they were your own. He had held her hand over her first burning — not to force her to watch, but simply to stand beside her while she chose to. He had wept at her mother's funeral. He had promoted her twice before she was thirty and defended her, publicly, when the Synod questioned her methods.

And now here he came down a forgotten road in a country that was forgetting itself, leading forty singers toward a child, and Sera understood with terrible clarity that he had never changed at all. He had always been exactly this. She had simply been standing close enough that the shape of him looked like shelter.

"Options," Kestrel said, low. He'd finally turned around, reins loose in his hands, wearing the expression Sera privately thought of as his shopkeeper's face — pleasant, attentive, and calculating the exact value of everything including your soul. "We can run. We can fight. Or we can do whatever it is you're doing with your jaw right now."

"Clenching it."

"Very brave. Does it help?"

"Immensely." She exhaled. "Ossen doesn't travel with soldiers. He travels with believers. Forty of them won't raise a hand unless he asks them to, and he won't ask them to unless he decides we're lost causes. Which means—"

"We get a sermon first," Doss said grimly.

"You've met him?"

"I worked for the Order's supply lines for six years, love. I've sat through more Ossen sermons than you've had hot meals. Man once preached for four hours about the spiritual significance of grain tariffs." Doss checked the crossbow's string with his thumb. "I fell asleep. He remembered. For years."

"That does sound like him," Sera admitted.

"So we run," Kestrel said.

"We can't outrun them with a cart," Doss said. "And we can't abandon the cart, because the cart has—" he jerked his chin at Pell, "—the entire reason for this miserable expedition sitting on it."

Pell raised her hand slightly, the way she did in markets when she wanted to contribute and wasn't sure she was allowed. "Um. I could pray for fog."

"No," Sera and Kestrel said together.

"It answered before! Mostly!"

"*Mostly* is doing horrifying work in that sentence," Kestrel said. "Last time you prayed for rain we got a flood, a plague of frogs, and — I want to stress this — one extremely confused whale."

"The whale was fine."

"The whale was in a wheat field, Pell."

Sera almost smiled. Then the choir began to sing, and the smile died where it stood.

* * *

It was worse hearing it from outside. From inside the halls of the Order, the Ashen Choir was architecture; every voice was a stone and the hymn built walls around the faithful, kept the world out and the doctrine in. But from a cart on an open road, with the wind carrying it, the hymn sounded like what it had always secretly been: a hunger with harmony attached. Forty voices rising in perfect unison, praising the Flame that cleanses, the Ash that remains, the gods who must be fed so that the world might be spared —

Fed. She hadn't heard that word in the liturgy before. Not *appeased*. Not *honored*. *Fed.*

They'd rewritten it. Of course they had. Ossen had been rewriting it for years, she realized — every sermon a chisel-tap, every revision a half-step, until the Order's ancient terror of the old gods had been sanded smooth into something that could be walked toward instead of away from.

The cart rolled to a stop because Kestrel hauled the reins, and Ossen's procession halted thirty paces away, and for a long moment the only sound was the last note of the hymn hanging in the cold air like smoke.

"Kestrel Halloran," Ossen said.

His voice hadn't aged a day. Warm, resonant, the voice of a man reading your favorite passage back to you. His eyes found Sera and stayed there, and something in her chest — the part of her that was still nineteen, still kneeling on wet flagstones, still desperate for a mentor's approval — turned over like a sleeper.

"Sister Vane." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Or should I say *Vane*, plain and unadorned? I confess I grieved the loss of your rank. You wore it well."

"You're a long way from the Synod, Prelate."

"The Synod is ash, child. Most of it literally." He said it gently, the way one reports weather. "The gods are waking, and the Synod spent three hundred years teaching men to look away from them. I am doing what the Order was always meant to do. Standing at the door. Making arrangements."

"Arrangements," Kestrel said pleasantly. "Is that the church word for following a child across half a country with forty armed men?"

"Forty unarmed men," Ossen corrected, spreading his empty hands. "Though I notice your smuggler disagrees with our disarmament." His gaze slid to Doss, and something flickered there — recognition, and beneath it, contempt worn thin as gilt. "Marrow. Still alive. The Flame tests us all in different ways."

"Still alive, still handsome, still charging interest," Doss said. "Some of us don't need gods to be consistent."

Ossen smiled at that, genuinely, and Sera hated how much she recognized the smile. It was the one he gave to good arguments he intended to destroy.

"Here is what I know," Ossen said, and began to walk forward, slowly, alone, his congregation holding its breath behind him. "I know the land behind you is unraveling. I know villages wake unable to name their children. I know the old gods stir in their long fasting, and I know — because I have read the sealed archives, which you, Halloran, were never cleared to read — that the gate beneath your charming little shop was never a lock. It was a *larder door*. And larder doors exist to be opened."

"By whom?" Kestrel asked. His voice was still pleasant. His knuckles had gone white on the reins.

"By whoever loves the world enough to feed its makers." Ossen stopped ten paces off. Close enough that Sera could see the gray at his temples now, the deep lines around eyes that had once seemed ageless. "The gods are starving, and a starving god takes. It takes names, memories, roads, rivers, children's faces. Unless it is given. Freely. Abundantly. A congregation offered up in love buys a century of mercy. Two congregations buy five. And there are those among the faithful — many, many more than you would credit — who have heard my preaching and *wept with joy* at the chance to be of use."

Sera's blood went cold. "You've found volunteers."

"I've found *believers*, Sister. The difference matters to people like you." He tilted his head at her, and his voice softened into the register he'd used on her a hundred times, the one that made cruelty sound like curriculum. "You think I've fallen. I watched you decide that, the day you walked out of the Penitent Hall with your rank stripped and your certainty intact. But ask yourself, child — when the girl's prayers are answered, who answers them? Something vast and old and *hungry*. And when it hungers, it takes. From whom does it take? The powerless. The forgotten. People precisely like the ones you've taken under your wing." He gestured, gentle as a father, at Pell. "I am offering the powerful a way to pay the bill themselves. Tell me which of us serves the weak."

For one obscene moment, it almost made sense. That was the horror of Ossen; he was never wrong in the small print, only in the whole of it. Sera felt the old gravity of him pulling at her, familiar as a childhood home, and she saw Kestrel glance at her sideways — watching, she realized, to see which way she'd break.

She looked at Pell instead.

The girl had shrunk against Doss during the sermon, small hands fisted in his coat, but her chin was up and her eyes were fixed on Ossen with an expression no nine-year-old should own: the look of someone recognizing a shape from a nightmare.

"He means it," Pell whispered. "He really believes it. That's the worst part. I can hear it. Underneath the singing, they're all *glad*."

Something settled in Sera then — not courage, nothing so clean, but a decision arriving the way decisions do, fully formed, having apparently been made somewhere below the level of her consent.

"Prelate," she said, and stepped down off the cart, placing herself squarely between Ossen and the child. "You taught me that doctrine is a lantern, not a cage. Your words. When I was nineteen and scrubbing floors for asking too much."

A shadow of pleasure crossed his face. "You remember."

"I remember everything you ever taught me. That's the problem." She drew a slow breath. "You taught me that mercy without law is sentiment. You never once taught me what law without mercy is. So let me answer it for you now, since you seem to have misplaced the lesson. Law without mercy is forty grown men singing a hymn at a child. It's a larder. It's you, Prelate. You've become the thing the Order was built to stand against, and you did it one reasonable step at a time, and I am done being reasonable."

Ossen regarded her for a long moment. When he spoke again, the warmth was gone, and what remained was worse — patience.

"Then you've chosen," he said. "I hoped otherwise. Genuinely." He turned his head slightly, toward his congregation, and raised one hand. "Brothers. Sisters. The lost sheep have wandered onto the road. Bring them home. Gently, if they permit it."

The choir moved.

* * *

Later, Sera would try to reconstruct the fight and find it wouldn't hold a shape. Memory, she suspected, had been damaged by proximity to the gate's business, or perhaps battles simply refused to be remembered properly, out of self-respect.

What she kept were fragments.

Doss's crossbow bolt taking the lead chorister's staff out of his hands with a crack like the world's smallest thunderclap, followed by Doss shouting, "That's a warning! The next one's a *sermon*!" — which was, Sera reflected, the most Doss sentence ever uttered.

Kestrel vaulting off the cart and putting himself between two gray-robed brothers and Pell, arms spread, saying in his shopkeeper's voice, "Gentlemen, I'm afraid we're closed," and then doing something with his hands that made the air go thick and golden and heavy as wet wool, so that the two men waded toward him as though through honey, swearing.

Pell, pressed against the cart wheel, whispering prayers with her eyes screwed shut — and Sera screaming "Not the fog, not the fog, *anything but the fog*" — and the ground obligingly growing knee-high grass in a perfect circle around the cart, tripping three pursuers who went down in a tangle of robes and indignation.

And Ossen, standing apart from all of it, watching her.

He didn't fight. He simply walked toward her through the chaos, unhurried, hands folded, as though the battle were weather happening to other people, and Sera understood that this, too, was a lesson. *Look how calm I am. Look how certain. Certainty is armor.*

She drew the short blade she'd carried since the day she left the Order — carried and never used, carried out of habit, carried the way you carry a former life — and set her feet.

"Child," Ossen said, sorrowfully, closing the distance. "Put it down. You'll thank me in ten years. I thanked my teacher, in your exact position, and—"

"You're not my teacher anymore." Her voice shook. She let it. "You haven't been since the day you decided a congregation was a currency."

His hand shot out — faster than a man his age had any right to be — and caught her wrist, and for a heartbeat they stood locked, eye to eye, close enough that she could smell the incense that clung to him after all these years, sandalwood and ash.

"You were the best of them," he said quietly. "My finest student. This breaks me, Vane. Know that."

"No," Sera said. "It doesn't. That's what I finally figured out about you. Nothing breaks you. Things only *inconvenience* you." And she twisted free, drove her shoulder into his chest, and put the finest mind of the Order's generation into the ditch.

It was not a dignified victory. He grabbed her ankle on the way down and she went with him, and they grappled in the mud like drunks outside a tavern, and somewhere above them Doss yelled, "Are you *wrestling*? We're losing a battle and you're *wrestling*!"

Then a crossbow bolt buried itself in the road an inch from Ossen's ear — Doss's aim, deliberately wide, deliberately a message — and the prelate went still beneath her.

Around them, the fight was collapsing. Three of the choir lay groaning in Pell's grass. Two had fled into the trees. The rest had drawn back into a wary ring, and Kestrel stood at its center with his golden heaviness still thick in the air, breathing hard, blood running from a split lip.

"Go home, Prelate," Kestrel said. "Tell them the keeper says the door stays shut."

Ossen rose, mud-streaked, unhurried even in defeat, and Sera felt the true chill of him then — because he wasn't defeated. He was *revised*. She could see it happening behind his eyes: new variables entered, new verses drafted.

"There will be more of us," he said mildly, brushing mud from his vestments. "And next time, Sister, I will not ask gently. Nor will I come myself." He paused, and looked at her one last time, and for a single unguarded instant Sera saw what lived underneath the patience — not hatred, not zeal. Fear. Old, vast, carefully managed fear, the fear of a man who had stared into the hunger of the gods and decided the only sane response was to feed it before it fed on him.

"Take care of the girl," he said. "Whatever else you believe of me — take care of her. The gods mark what they mean to collect."

Then he whistled once, low, and his remaining choir melted into the treeline after him, and the road was theirs again, littered with abandoned staves and one very confused crow.

* * *

Doss was sitting against the cart wheel with his hand pressed to his side, and he was laughing, which was how Sera knew it was bad. Doss laughed at pain the way other men swore at it.

"Doss." She was beside him before she'd decided to move, peeling his hand away. The cut beneath was long and shallow — a chorister's blade, drawn across rather than thrust — but it bled with enthusiasm. "Why are you laughing?"

"Because I took a knife," he wheezed, "for a child whose guardian angel sets fires and floods and summons whales. There are men in the Order with *better odds*."

"Hold still." She tore a strip from her sleeve — the sleeve of the shirt Kestrel had bought her in Corrin's Ford, blue, the first color she'd owned since the gray — and bound the wound while he hissed through his teeth.

"You know," Doss said, quieter now, watching her work, "you could've gone with him. Back there. He'd have taken you. Rank, title, absolution, the whole basket."

"Yes."

"And you didn't."

"No."

"Why not?"

Sera tied off the bandage and sat back on her heels. Across the road, Kestrel was helping Pell down from the cart, and the girl was showing him the patch of miraculous grass with the enormous pride of a craftsman, and Kestrel was nodding along gravely, as though evaluating a fine vintage.

"Because when he talked about feeding the weak to the strong," Sera said, "he used the word *love*. And I looked at these idiots—" she gestured at the cart, at all of it, "—and realized I'd finally seen the word done properly. It looks like this. Badly. Loudly. With whales." She stood, and offered Doss her hand. "Come on. You're riding in the cart, and you're going to complain the entire time, and it's going to be wonderful."

Doss took her hand, winced upright, and grinned. "See, *this* is why I betrayed you that one time. You're very easy to follow into disaster."

"One time? You sold me out *twice*."

"The second time was a misunderstanding."

"You gave the Synod our route map, Doss."

"A *misunderstanding*," he repeated with dignity, and limped toward the cart.

Behind them, far down the road, faint as memory, the Ashen Choir began to sing again — a different hymn this time, one Sera didn't know, and she understood that Ossen was already composing, already revising, already walking toward whatever came next.

Pell tugged Kestrel's sleeve and pointed east, where the afternoon light had begun to fall strangely, bending, as though the horizon were a page someone was erasing.

"Kestrel," the girl said. "The road's doing the thing again."

Kestrel turned, and Sera watched the color leave his face as he looked at the place where, by every map she'd ever read, the town of Amberford ought to be.

"Drive," he said quietly, and snapped the reins.
