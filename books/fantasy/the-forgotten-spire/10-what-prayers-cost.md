## 10. What Prayers Cost

The farmer was dying in the middle of a road that had forgotten where it went.

Pell felt him before she saw him—a tugging behind her ribs, like a fishhook set gently in her breastbone and reeled once. She'd learned the feel of it over the past weeks of travel east: prayers came to her now the way smells came to other people, unbidden and unmistakable. This one tasted of dust and fear and something sweet underneath, like honey left too long in the sun.

*Please,* the prayer said. It didn't have words exactly, not anymore. The man was too far gone for words. *Please, please, please,* worn smooth as a river stone.

"There," she said, and pointed off the road's edge, where the wheat should have been golden but was instead the color of old porridge, because the land here had half-forgotten what gold looked like.

Kestrel reined the cart horse—Doss insisted his name was Buttercup, which nobody believed—down onto the verge. "Pell." He said her name the way he always did when he knew what she was about to do, which was the way you'd say *don't*. "We don't stop for every—"

"He's dying."

"Everyone's dying. That's what people do. Slowly, usually, and preferably somewhere we aren't."

But he was already climbing down from the bench, because Kestrel complained the way other men breathed, automatically and without meaning anything by it. Sera dismounted on the far side, hand near her knife out of habit, scanning the gray wheat for whatever had done this. Doss stayed on the cart, as was his policy regarding everything that might involve effort or danger or both.

The farmer lay in the dirt at the edge of his own field, and Pell could see immediately why the prayer had no words left in it. A scythe wound, badly bound, the bandages black. He'd cut himself harvesting a crop that wasn't quite real anymore, from a field that was forgetting how to grow, and he'd bled into soil that might not remember how to hold him.

His eyes found her and went wide. His lips moved. No sound came out, but the prayer sharpened, and this time it had a shape: *the girl who glows. I know what you are. Please.*

"I'm not going to hurt you," Pell said, kneeling. Behind her she heard Kestrel make a small noise like a kettle deciding whether to whistle.

She put her hands over the wound, the way she'd done a hundred times since they'd left Vharen, and she reached for the answer the way you reach for a jar on a high shelf—up on your toes, fingers stretched, trusting it'll be there.

It was there. It was always there. That was the problem.

Warmth poured through her palms and into the man's torn side, and Pell felt the familiar terrible arithmetic begin. Every answered prayer took something. Not blood, not strength—something finer than that. Today it took the memory of rain. Just like that, the word *rain* was still in her head but the feeling of it was gone, the drumming on the roof of the inn at Brindleford, the smell of wet dust, the way mud squelched between her toes when she was six and Sera—no, not Sera, someone else, someone before—had chased her through puddles. All of it, folded up and passed through her hands into a stranger's body, spent like a coin.

The wound closed. The color came back into the farmer's face, gray to pink to something approaching human.

He sat up gasping, clutching his side, feeling the smooth new skin beneath his ruined shirt. He stared at her. Then he began to laugh, a cracked, wheezing, terrified laugh, the kind people laughed when the alternative was screaming.

"Bless you," he managed. "Bless you, bless—" And then his face changed, because he'd looked past her at the field, at the wheat the color of porridge, and remembered what country he lived in now. "You shouldn't be here. Girl, you shouldn't have come. If the Order finds—"

"They won't," Sera said, coming up beside them. Her voice had its inquisitor flatness, but Pell had learned to hear the other thing underneath it, the thing that sounded like guilt wearing armor. "Get inside. Bar your door. Don't tell anyone what happened here."

"But my crops—"

"Your crops are leaving," Doss called down from the cart, cheerfully awful. "Best get used to it. Whole country's packing its bags, friend, and nobody asked if we wanted to come along."

The farmer scrambled up, staggered, steadied himself against Pell's shoulder without seeming to notice he was touching her—and then flinched away like her skin had burned him, though it hadn't, it never did, that was the joke of it, everyone expected the god-touched girl to burn and instead she just got colder every time, quieter, more full of holes.

"Thank you," he whispered, and fled toward his farmhouse, a smudge on the horizon that might have been a house or might have been the idea of one.

Pell stood in the road and tried to remember rain.

"Pell." Kestrel crouched in front of her, and his face had gone soft in the way it only did for her, the grumpiness folded away like a good coat. "What did it take?"

"The rain," she said. "I mean—the remembering of it. I know the word. I know it falls and it's wet and things grow. But I can't..." She made a shape in the air with her hands, trying to hold something that wasn't there anymore. "There's a hole where it was. A rain-shaped hole."

Kestrel closed his eyes briefly. When he opened them, he looked older, and Pell realized with a small cold shock that he was doing arithmetic too—counting her answers, tallying the holes, watching her fill up with absence one miracle at a time.

"How many?" he asked quietly. "Since Vharen?"

"I don't—"

"How many, love."

"Forty-one," she admitted. "Forty-two, counting him."

Kestrel exhaled through his nose, long and slow, the breath of a man watching a dam develop its first crack. Behind him, Sera had turned away to watch the road, arms crossed, jaw tight. Doss had suddenly become very interested in Buttercup's harness.

"Right," Kestrel said. "Camp. Now. We need to talk, and I refuse to have this conversation standing in a field that can't remember what color it is."

* * *

They camped in the lee of a wall that had forgotten it was a barn, so it was just a wall now, three sides of weathered stone standing in a wheat field with nothing to enclose. Doss built a fire anyway, because Doss held that a fire made anywhere into somewhere, and that a man who wouldn't light a fire in a bad situation deserved the bad situation.

Pell sat wrapped in a blanket with her knees pulled up, and Kestrel sat across the fire from her, and he had that look he got when he was about to teach her something, which was the same look he got when he was about to disappoint her, and after two months on the road she'd stopped being able to tell the difference.

"You're going to tell me to stop," she said.

"I'm going to tell you something worse." He fed a stick to the flames. "I'm going to tell you to keep stopping. There's a difference."

"That's the same thing."

"It isn't." He leaned forward. "Stopping is what happens when you run out of strength. Refusing is a choice, and choices cost something too, and I need you to understand that cost before you pay it, because nobody paid it for me until it was too late to matter."

Sera settled onto the log beside Pell—not close enough to crowd her, but close. Sera was getting better at that, at being near without looming. For an ex-inquisitor she was learning gentleness at a remarkable pace, mostly by apologizing for all the years she hadn't had any.

"Tell her about the gate," Sera said quietly.

"This isn't about the gate."

"Everything is about the gate, Kestrel. You've just gotten comfortable pretending otherwise."

Kestrel gave her a look, but it was a tired look, and he turned back to the fire. "Fine. Here's what nobody tells you about keeping a door shut for seven years, Pell. You stand in front of it, and behind it something is knocking, and knocking, and knocking, and every day you don't open it, you choose the suffering on your side of the door over whatever's on the other side. People starve within sight of it. People pray at it. And you let them, because letting them is the job." He poked the fire hard enough to scatter sparks. "And every single day, it costs you a piece of yourself, and you tell yourself that piece doesn't count because you gave it up on purpose."

Pell hugged her knees tighter. "That's horrible."

"Yes."

"So refusing is just... letting people die, but calling it a choice so you feel brave about it."

The fire crackled. Doss, wisely, studied his boots. Even Buttercup, tied to the ghost of a fence post, seemed to be listening.

"No," Kestrel said. "Refusing is arithmetic. Cold, ugly arithmetic, and I hate teaching it to you because you're eleven and you should be learning times tables and stealing apples, not this. But listen." He held up one hand, fingers spread. "Every answer costs you a piece of yourself. Forty-two pieces already. Some of them were small—a color, a smell, the name of a dog you once knew. Some of them weren't. I've watched you lose whole years, Pell. Last week you couldn't remember your mother's voice, and you cried, and then yesterday you couldn't remember that you'd ever cried about it, and that was worse. Do you see? The holes fill in with nothing, and nothing looks peaceful from the outside."

Pell's throat was tight. "So I should just let them die. The farmers. The children. That woman in Corrow Ford with the fever baby. I should walk past them and do sums."

"I think," Kestrel said carefully, "that you should learn to tell the difference between a prayer and a want. Everyone prays, Pell. They pray for rain and for their crops and for their team to win and for their mother-in-law to develop a rash. Most prayers are wants wearing holy clothes. The gods used to sort them—that was the whole point of the old system, the wards, the gate, all of it. A filter. Somebody had to decide which miracles the world could afford." He shook his head. "Now the filter's broken and everything pours through, and it's pouring through *you*, because you're young and open and the gods marked you before anyone could teach you to say no."

"And saying no feels like what?"

Kestrel was quiet for a moment. "Like holding a door shut while something knocks."

"Oh," Pell said. "Oh, that's rotten."

"Yeah," said Doss unexpectedly, from across the fire. "Welcome to adulthood, sprout. It's mostly that."

"Doss," Sera warned.

"What? She's going to find out eventually. Better she learns it sitting by a nice fire than—" He gestured broadly at the forgetting countryside, the porridge wheat, the whole unraveling east. "Than out there."

Pell looked into the flames and thought about the farmer. About the hook behind her ribs, the reel, the pull. About how good it felt to answer—she'd never told anyone that part, how *good*, like scratching an itch deep under her skin, like finally exhaling. And how the goodness lasted exactly as long as the warmth flowing out of her hands, and then the hole opened up where the gift had been, and the next prayer came along, and the itch started again, and—

"It's not kindness," she said slowly, testing the thought like ice on a pond. "Answering them. It feels kind. But it's... it's the hook wanting to be pulled."

Kestrel's expression did something complicated. "That," he said, "is the smartest thing anyone's said around this fire in weeks, and I include myself, which pains me."

"So how do I refuse? Actually refuse. Not just feel guilty about it."

"You practice." He reached across the fire and took her hand, and his palm was rough and warm and real, and she held on. "Small ones first. Prayers you can feel coming and let pass. Like building a muscle. And when the big ones come—the dying ones, the children—you ask yourself one question before you answer: *can this person be saved another way?* Medicine. Rest. Luck. A bandage and a strong opinion. Save your miracles for the moments when the world has genuinely run out of other options, because the world runs out fast, but you run out faster."

"And if there's no other way? If it's really the end, and only a miracle will do?"

Kestrel looked at her for a long moment, and the firelight moved in his eyes, and Pell saw that he was asking himself the same question about something else entirely—about a door, and a country, and a choice waiting for him at the top of a spire neither of them could see yet.

"Then you spend yourself," he said finally, "on purpose, with your eyes open, knowing the price. Never because the hook pulled. Only because *you* decided. That's the whole difference between a saint and a sieve, Pell. Saints choose."

"She's eleven," Doss muttered. "You're giving sainthood lectures to an eleven-year-old."

"She's an eleven-year-old who just diagnosed herself as a fishing lure. She can handle it."

Pell almost smiled. Almost. Then, from somewhere out in the dark beyond the firelight—out in the wheat that couldn't remember gold—she felt it.

A tug. Gentle. Insistent. A prayer, rising up from some house or hollow or ditch within a mile of them, threading through the night air straight to the hook behind her ribs.

*Please. Please. My daughter. Please.*

A child's prayer. A parent praying for a child.

Pell's breath caught. The warmth rose in her hands before she'd even decided anything, eager, automatic, the itch demanding to be scratched, and she clenched her fists in her lap and held it, held it, white-knuckled, the way Kestrel had described holding a door shut while something knocked.

"Pell?" Sera's hand landed light on her shoulder. "What is it?"

"There's a little girl," Pell whispered. "Half a mile that way. She's sick. Really sick. Her mother's praying." The warmth pushed against the inside of her skin, insistent, generous, *kind*, and Pell understood for the first time that it didn't care about her at all. "I can fix it. I can fix it right now, easy, like snapping my fingers. And it would cost me—" she searched for the shape of the price, and found it waiting, patient as tax season— "it would cost me the sea. I'd forget the sea. I've never even seen it, but I'd forget it anyway, and I'd never know what I lost."

Nobody spoke. The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark, a mother prayed with everything she had.

"Your choice," Kestrel said softly. "Not mine. Not the hook's. Yours. Whatever you decide, you decide it, and we live with it together."

Pell stared into the fire, fists clenched, tears sliding hot down her face, hating the arithmetic, hating the door, hating every god who had ever built a world where love came with a ledger.

And then, from the direction of the capital—from the east, from the Hollow City they were walking toward—came a sound none of them had heard before and none of them would ever quite be able to describe afterward: a vast, low, sighing groan, like the land itself exhaling, like a name being spoken backward.

The fire guttered. The wheat shivered though there was no wind.

Pell forgot, all at once and completely, what she had been crying about.
