## 11. The Hollow City

The capital announced itself the way a sick man announces himself: by smell.

Kestrel smelled it from the ridge road, two hours before they saw the walls—woodsmoke and tallow and something underneath both, faintly sweet, like fruit going soft in a bowl nobody remembered buying. He reined up at the crest of the hill and looked down at Corvane, city of ten thousand lamps, and felt his stomach do something complicated.

"That's it?" Pell asked, standing in the cart bed beside Doss, her hands on the rail. She was nine years old and had never seen a city larger than Vharen, and she had the particular stillness she got when she was seeing too much at once.

"That's it," Kestrel said. "Corvane. Pearl of the eastern provinces. Seat of the High Chancery. Home to the finest pie shop on this side of the mountains."

"You've been here before," Sera said. It wasn't a question. She sat beside him on the bench, ash-grey cloak pulled up against the wind, watching him watch the city.

"I grew up here."

Nobody said anything for a moment. The wind came off the plain and rattled the canvas over the cart's bed, where their supplies—and Doss's considerable collection of contraband—shifted and clinked like nervous cutlery.

"And?" Doss prompted, when the silence had gone on long enough to become rude. "Is there a story coming? Because I've been sharing a cart with you lot for three weeks and I've learned you all have stories, and they all come out eventually, usually at the worst possible time. Might as well pick your moment."

"It's not much of a story," Kestrel said. "I lived on Cordwainer's Row until I was eleven. My mother sold dyes. Then the Order took an interest in the neighborhood—there'd been a charm-seller two streets over who got careless—and we left in the night, and I don't remember much after that." He flicked the reins. "Let's go see what's left."

* * *

What was left was worse than he'd prepared for, and he had prepared for quite a lot.

The gate guards let them through without looking at them. That was the first wrong thing. Corvane's gates had always been watched by men who examined every face as though each one owed them money. These guards stood at their posts with their spears upright and their eyes fixed on the middle distance, and when Pell waved at one—a friendly wave, the kind that usually disarmed even the grumpiest customs man—the guard smiled at her with perfect warmth and no recognition whatsoever, as if she were a pleasant thought he'd have forgotten by supper.

Inside, the city was full. Markets running, bakeries baking, carts creaking, children shouting. Everything was happening. Nothing was *happening to anyone.*

"Look at the fishmonger," Sera murmured as they rolled down the Processional. A broad woman stood behind a stall of silver-bellied catch, calling prices in a strong voice. But her eyes moved across the crowd without landing on anything, and when a boy bought a herring and paid her properly, she counted the coins twice, frowning at them as though arithmetic were a language she'd studied years ago and mostly lost.

"They're not empty," Pell said quietly. She had gone very pale. "That's the horrible part. They're *full*. Full of everything except—" Her small hands gripped the rail. "Except the parts that made them themselves."

"The forgetting," Kestrel said. "It's not eating outward from the edges anymore. It's inside the walls."

"Then why aren't we forgetting?" Doss asked. He said it lightly, the way he said everything, but his knuckles were white on the cart rail. "We've been marinating in this curse for weeks. Villages dissolving behind us, roads unspooling, whole towns gone like candle smoke. Why are we the only four people in this country who can still remember our own names?"

"Because we're marked," Pell said. All three of them turned to look at her. She shrugged, embarrassed. "The gods marked us. You knew that. Kestrel keeps their door. Sera burned their name out of her own book. Doss—" she paused, tilting her head at him with the unnerving directness she sometimes had, "—Doss owes them a debt he hasn't told us about yet."

Doss opened his mouth. Closed it. "I'll tell you when we're not in public," he muttered, which was more admission than Kestrel had gotten out of him in three weeks, and filed it away.

They found lodging at an inn called the Gilded Hart, or possibly the Gilded Stag—the sign had been repainted so many times the animal underneath had become ambiguous—and Kestrel paid for two rooms with coins he counted twice himself, just to be sure he could.

* * *

He told himself he would wait until morning to go to Cordwainer's Row.

He lasted until the lamps were lit.

"I'm going out," he said to the common room generally, pulling his coat on. Sera looked up from the map she'd spread across their table, marking the districts where the forgetting seemed thickest. Doss was nursing a beer and pretending not to eavesdrop, which for Doss was practically a confession of deep concern.

"At night," Sera said. "Into the hollow city. Alone."

"I grew up here. I know the streets."

"That's exactly what worries me." She folded the map. "I'm coming."

"I don't need—"

"No," she agreed, standing, settling her cloak across her shoulders with the crisp economy of a woman who had once commanded garrisons and now commanded a party of four and a mule named Buttercup. "You don't. I'm coming anyway."

Doss drained his beer and stood too. "If you two are going to walk into the creepy district at night, somebody has to carry the lantern and make sarcastic comments, and Pell's asleep."

"She's not asleep," came Pell's voice from the stairs. "I'm coming too."

"Pell—"

"You always say I can stay behind," she said, coming down with her boots already on, "and then something happens and you're glad I was there. Every time. I'm keeping count. This is the sixth."

Kestrel looked at Sera. Sera looked at the ceiling, which was as close as she ever came to admitting someone else was right.

"Fine," he said. "But you hold my hand crossing the Processional. There are carts."

"There are *no* carts," Pell said. "There haven't been any carts since we entered the walls. Nobody remembers they're supposed to be driving anywhere."

Which was true, and which Kestrel chose not to think about.

* * *

Cordwainer's Row had not changed, which was the cruelest thing about it.

The cobblestones still dipped in the same places, worn by two hundred years of dye-carts. The same crooked chimney pots crowded the rooftops like old men leaning together to gossip. The lamplighter's post still stood at the corner—though the lamp atop it was dark, because the lamplighter, presumably, had forgotten he was the lamplighter.

And there, third door past the well: the house with the blue shutters. His mother's house. His mother's shutters, which she painted every spring, standing on a ladder and scolding him to hold it steady, *Kestrel, if I fall, at least have the decency to look sorry.*

The shutters were grey now. Weathered bare.

He stood in front of the house for a long time.

"Kestrel?" Sera's voice, quiet, beside him.

"I'm fine."

"You've been fine for three weeks. It's starting to sound like a lie."

He almost laughed. Almost. Instead he heard himself say, "Her name was Maren. Maren Halloran. She had—she laughed with her whole body, she'd throw her head back, she once laughed so hard at a joke about a bishop that she knocked a pot of indigo off the shelf and dyed our cat blue for a month. The cat's name was—" He stopped.

The street waited. The dark windows waited.

"The cat's name was Bishop," Pell said softly. "Because of the joke."

Kestrel looked down at her. "How do you know that?"

"I don't." Her face was pinched, unhappy. "It's just—it's sitting right there. Where the memory should be. Like a shape under a blanket. I can see the shape of it. I can't lift the blanket." She reached up and took his hand, small fingers lacing through his. "I think that's what it's like for everyone here. They can feel the holes. That's why the fishmonger counts her coins twice. She knows something's missing. She just can't say what."

Kestrel turned back to the house. And there—there it was, the thing he had been circling since the ridge road, the thing he had refused to set down and look at directly.

"Maren Halloran," he said again, testing the name like a loose tooth. "My mother. I remember her name. I remember the blue cat and the ladder and the indigo. I remember she sang while she worked, badly, always a half-beat behind the tune." His voice went strange and flat. "I cannot remember her face."

Sera put her hand on his shoulder. She didn't say *it will be all right*, because she was Sera, and she had spent twenty years learning exactly how often things were not all right, and she did not lie to people she respected.

"We'll get it back," Doss said instead, from behind them, uncharacteristically gentle. "Whatever's been taken. We're going to the spire, aren't we? We're going to open the door and make the gods give it back. That's the whole mad plan. So we'll get her face back. And the cat. And whatever else these starved old sky-lords have been snacking on."

"You believe that?" Kestrel asked. "Really?"

Doss was quiet a moment. When he answered, all the smuggler's polish had dropped away, and what was underneath was older and tireder. "I believe I've spent fifteen years telling myself I don't owe anybody anything. And I believe that stopped being true somewhere around the ford." He shrugged. "So yes. I believe it. Believing is cheaper than the alternative."

Pell tugged his hand. "Kestrel. Look at the window."

The upstairs window of the blue-shuttered house—grey-shuttered house—was lit. Candlelight, warm and wavering, and behind it a silhouette moving: someone pacing, someone restless, someone *home.*

His heart slammed once, hard, against his ribs.

"Someone lives there," Sera said.

"My mother sold this house when we fled. Or—did she? Did we flee? I remember leaving in the night, I remember—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eye. "I don't trust my memories anymore. Half of them might be mine and half might be the wards' leftovers, and I can't tell which is which."

"Only one way to find out," Doss said, and started toward the door.

"Doss—"

"What? Worst case, it's a stranger who doesn't remember us, and we apologize and leave. Best case—" He didn't finish it. He didn't have to.

Kestrel climbed the three steps to the door. His feet knew the way; the second step had always creaked, and it creaked now, faithful as a dog. He raised his hand to knock, and found it shaking, and hated it, and knocked anyway.

Footsteps inside. The shuffle-slap of house slippers. The latch lifted.

The door opened on a small woman with grey hair pinned up carelessly, holding a candle, squinting out at the night. She was perhaps sixty. She had laugh lines around her mouth, deep ones, the kind earned over decades.

She had no face he recognized at all.

"Yes?" she said. Polite. Wary. A stranger answering her door to strangers. "It's late. Do you want something?"

Kestrel's mouth opened. Every word he owned had fallen out of it.

"We're travelers," Sera said smoothly, stepping half in front of him. "Our cart lost a wheel-iron on the Processional. We wondered if we might buy a spare, or borrow a hammer. We understood a wheelwright used to keep shop on this row."

"Oh—" The woman's wariness softened into something apologetic. "There was a wheelwright, I think. Wasn't there? On this street?" She frowned, and the frown deepened, and Kestrel watched her reach for something and find nothing, watched the confusion bloom and then—this was the terrible part—watched it *smooth over*, her face relaxing into placid blankness like water closing over a dropped stone. "I'm sure someone here can help you. Goodnight."

She began to close the door.

"Wait," Kestrel said. "Please. One question. Just one." His voice cracked on it. "Do you know a song? About a bishop? A funny one? It would have been sung in this house, years ago, by a woman who laughed with her whole body—"

The woman looked at him for a long moment. Something flickered behind her eyes—not recognition, nothing so kind as that. More like a draft through a sealed room. A candle guttering in a house whose owner had forgotten it was lit.

"I don't remember any songs," she said gently, the way one speaks to the bereaved, though she couldn't have known why. "I'm sorry. I don't seem to remember very much of anything lately."

The door closed. The latch clicked. The candle-shadow behind the upstairs window kept pacing, pacing, pacing.

Kestrel stood on the step that creaked, in front of the house with the grey shutters, and did not cry, because some griefs are too large for crying, the way some doors are too large for knocking.

Pell wrapped both arms around his waist and held on. Sera stood at his shoulder, solid as a wall. Doss, for once, said nothing at all.

It was Pell who finally spoke, her face pressed into his coat, her voice muffled and fierce:

"When we open the door—I'm going to teach them her name first. Before anybody's. Maren Halloran. And then the cat. And then every single person on this street." She looked up at him, and her eyes were wet and absolutely certain. "I'm keeping count, Kestrel. Of everything they took. And I'm giving it all back."

Behind them, far across the sleeping city, a bell began to ring—an alarm bell, ragged and urgent, from the direction of the Chancery hill. And beneath its clangor, threading through the streets like smoke finding cracks, came a sound none of them had heard before and all of them understood at once:

voices. Hundreds of voices. The hollow citizens of Corvane, waking all together in the dark, calling out names they could no longer attach to faces—

—and among them, unmistakably, from the house with the grey shutters, a woman's voice calling *Maren? Maren Halloran? Why do I know that name?*

Sera's hand closed on Kestrel's arm. "The Chancery," she said grimly. "Something's happened at the Chancery. And I'd bet my ashes I know who's behind it."

Down the hill, against the burning skyline, a spire that was not yet visible to any of them waited like a held breath.
