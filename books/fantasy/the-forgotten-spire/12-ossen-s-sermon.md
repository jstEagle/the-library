## 12. Ossen's Sermon

Prelate Ossen had always believed that the finest sermons were delivered in silence, and so he said nothing while his acolytes bound Kestrel Halloran's wrists with ash-cord and led him up the steps of the ruined chapel. He simply watched, hands folded into his sleeves, the way a gardener watches a trellis take the weight of a vine it was built for.

The chapel had been a granary once, before the forgetting reached this far east. Now its rafters were hung with censers that smoked without fire, and its floor was strewn with gray sand that Ossen's people raked each morning into patterns resembling—vaguely, imperfectly—the wards of the old gate. Practice, he called it. Rehearsal for the true liturgy.

Kestrel stood in the center of it, wrists bound, chin up, wearing the expression of a man who has been dragged somewhere he considers beneath him. His coat was scorched at one shoulder from the skirmish at the ford. There was blood on his collar that might have been Doss Marrow's.

"Leave us," Ossen said.

His acolytes hesitated. They were good children, mostly—orphans of the hollowed villages, faithful in the way that only people who have lost everything can be faithful—but they hesitated anyway, because Kestrel Halloran had a reputation among them as the heretic who burned a warden's blessing out of the air with his bare hands. The story had grown in the telling. Stories did that now. Everything grew or shrank strangely these days; nothing kept its proper proportions anymore.

"I said leave us," Ossen repeated, gently, and they went.

* * *

For a long moment the two men regarded each other across the raked sand.

"You're shorter than I imagined," Kestrel said.

"You're exactly as tall as I feared." Ossen gestured to a bench near the cold hearth. "Sit. Your legs are shaking. It is not weakness; it is the cord. Ash-cord drinks what it binds. You'll feel steadier in an hour."

Kestrel did not sit. "Where are my friends?"

"The smuggler is being tended. The wound was deep but clean; my surgeon is better than most barbers, and more honest than all of them. The child is asleep. The girl—" something moved behind Ossen's eyes, there and gone, like a fish under ice, "—the girl is remarkable. Do you know she looked at me and saw my mother's name? A name I have not spoken aloud in forty years because I could not remember it until she said it."

"She shouldn't do that," Kestrel said quietly. "It costs her."

"Everything costs. That is rather the substance of my sermon, Keeper." Ossen let the title land between them like a stone into still water, and watched the ripples. Yes. Kestrel flinched. Seven years hiding behind charms and haggling, and still the old word found the flesh beneath. "Sit. Please. I promise you no conversion by force. I have never believed force makes faith. It only makes compliance, and compliance rots."

Kestrel sat. It was, Ossen noted with some satisfaction, the sitting of a man conserving strength for a fight, not surrendering to one. Good. He had no use for broken things. Broken things could not choose, and choice was the entire point.

"Do you know why I became an inquisitor?" Ossen asked.

"To burn people like me."

"To burn *answers*," Ossen said. "People were incidental. I spent thirty years hunting miracles, Halloran. Do you know what I found, in thirty years? Fraud. Sickness mistaken for sainthood. Grief mistaken for prophecy. Not one—not *one*—genuine intervention from any god, old or new. And I told myself this proved the gods were silent, and that silence was holy, and that our task was to keep the world quiet enough for ordinary people to live in without terror." He walked slowly along the raked lines of sand, his sandals whispering. "I was proud of that belief. It was a beautiful belief. And then, three years ago, in the province of Vell, I watched a prayer get answered."

Kestrel went very still.

"A farmer's wife," Ossen continued. "Dying of the red fever. Her husband prayed—not to anyone in particular, just prayed, the way drowning men beat water. And something answered. She lived. She stood up out of a deathbed whole and laughing, and everyone in that village wept and praised, and I—" he smiled, thin and terrible, "—I examined her. Professionally. Do you know what I found?"

"No."

"Nothing. No fever, no scarring, no memory of having been ill. But also—" he turned, and his voice dropped into the register he had used for decades over funeral pyres, "—no memory of her *sister*. A woman of thirty-one years, beloved, real, with a grave in the churchyard that now bore no name. The miracle had not healed the wife. It had *traded*. It took the sister's existence and poured it into the wife's survival, the way a miser melts down one ring to gild another. And no one in that village remembered the sister but me. Because I had written her name in my ledger two years prior, on an unrelated matter, and ink remembers what flesh forgets."

The censers breathed their smokeless smoke. Somewhere above them, wind worried the loose tiles of the roof.

"That is when I understood," Ossen said. "The gods are not silent. The gods are *hungry*, and they have been starving since the gates closed, and everything that has happened since—the forgetting, the unraveling roads, the mothers who cannot recall their children—it is not malice. It is appetite. And appetite, Halloran, is the most honest thing in creation. It does not lie. It does not corrupt. It does not take bribes or favor the rich or twist doctrine to suit the powerful. A hunger is perfectly, immaculately *just*. It takes according to need and gives according to capacity. Show me a human law that can say the same."

"And you've decided to feed it," Kestrel said. His voice was flat, but Ossen heard the tremor underneath, the way you hear water under floorboards. "Your congregation. At the spire."

"I have decided to offer them," Ossen corrected. "There is a difference, and the difference is consent. Every soul who walks with me to that summit walks willingly. They know what is asked. They believe—and I confess I taught them to believe—that their offering will restore law to a world dissolving into soup. Is that monstrous? A man donates his body to medicine after death; we call him generous. These souls donate themselves to the restoration of reality itself. We call them monsters?"

"They're people."

"They are *ingredients*," Ossen said, and then paused, hearing himself, and for one unguarded moment something old and tired crossed his face. "They are people," he amended, quieter. "That is the price. I have never pretended otherwise. I have only weighed it."

He crossed to where Kestrel sat and crouched, slowly, joints cracking, until they were eye to eye. Up close he smelled of cedar ash and old paper.

"But here is what I actually came to say, Keeper, and I will say it plainly because I am too old for seduction: I do not want your congregation. I want *you*. The gate answers to its keeper. It always has. When we reach the summit—and we will reach it, whether you walk there in cords or on your own feet—I intend to open that door properly, with the rites, with the keeper's hand on the mechanism, instead of kicking it in like burglars. For that, I need you willing."

"And if I'm not willing?"

"Then I will open it badly, and it will cost perhaps ten times what it should, and I will pay that cost in the coin I have, which is people. So consider, Halloran, how many lives your stubbornness is worth. Consider it honestly. You've been doing arithmetic like this your whole life—every charm you sold, every ward you fed, you were spending small mercies to prevent large horrors. I am merely proposing a larger ledger."

* * *

Ossen rose and paced away, giving him room, because a trap closes best when the animal believes the cage is the sky.

"You think I am a fanatic," Ossen said. "You would be half right. But look at yourself first. Look at what seven years of guarding has made you. A shopkeeper. A seller of trinkets to fools. You have knelt under this city for years feeding drops of yourself into those wards, alone, unwitnessed, unthanked—and do you know why? Not from hope. Hope would have opened the door. From *fear*. You are afraid of what comes through, so you hold the door shut with your own body, forever, and you call it duty because duty is fear wearing its Sunday clothes."

"That's not—"

"It is precisely so, and I know it because I wore the same garment for thirty years." Ossen's voice softened, and this was the cruelest thing he did all day, because the softness was genuine. "We are the same man, you and I. You guard a door because you cannot bear what is behind it. I feed what is behind it because I cannot bear the waiting. Both of us are terrified, and both of us have built cathedrals around our terror and called them principle. The only difference between us is that I have stopped lying to myself about it. Come stand beside me, and you may stop lying too."

Kestrel looked at his bound hands for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was low and rough.

"When I was nine years old," he said, "my mother took me to the gate. Showed me the wards. Told me that someday it would be mine, and that the keeping of it meant watching the world forget the country the gate protected, and loving that country anyway, without thanks, without witnesses. She said the worst temptation wouldn't be despair. It would be certainty. Someone would come along someday who knew exactly what was wrong with the world and exactly how to fix it, and they'd be almost right, and the 'almost' would be measured in graves." He raised his head. "She described you, Prelate. Forty years before I met you. Word for word."

Ossen held his gaze, and did not blink, and inside himself acknowledged, with the detachment of a scholar observing a specimen, that the boy's words had gone in like a splinter and that he would be digging at it for days.

"Mothers describe everyone eventually," he said. "Prophecy is cheap. Precision is expensive, and I offer precision. Think on it. We march at dawn."

He turned to go, and at the chapel door he paused, because fairness demanded it—he had always insisted on fairness, even toward the condemned.

"For what it is worth, Keeper: I hope you refuse me. A faith that needs no keeper's consent is only theft, and I have stolen quite enough already." He smiled, and it was almost warm. "But I hope it in the way I hope for rain. Wistfully. Without expectation."

* * *

Alone in the darkening chapel, Kestrel worked the ash-cord against the bench's rough edge and thought about his mother's face, which he could no longer see, and about certainty, which he could see all too clearly, wearing his own features in the polished black of the cold hearth.

And three streets away, in a locked room, Pell woke from a dream of a door opening, sat up in the dark, and whispered a name she had never heard before—the prelate's dead sister's name—and felt it go out of the world like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

By morning, Ossen's ledger would remember her.

No one else would.
