## 13. Betrayal at the Ford

Doss Marrow had spent forty years learning every way a man could get out of a bad situation, and the situation at the ford was shaping up to be the worst of his career.

The river came down out of the eastern hills in three braided channels, shallow enough to wade if you didn't mind cold that bit through your boots and deep enough at the middle channel to drown a horse that lost its footing. The far bank rose into pine shadow. Behind them—maybe two miles back, maybe less—the Order's riders had stopped pretending they weren't following.

"They've slowed down," Sera said, shading her eyes. She stood hip-deep in the shallows where she'd been testing the current, and even soaked to the knee she managed to look like someone giving orders to the water. "That's worse than speeding up."

"Slowed down means confident," Kestrel said. He had Pell up on his shoulders so her feet wouldn't freeze, which Doss thought was either very sweet or very stupid, depending on how the next hour went. "Confident means they think we're not going anywhere."

"Well." Doss clapped his hands together and tried for his best merchant's smile, the one that had talked customs officers out of opening crates full of things that would have gotten everyone hanged. "Good news, then. I know this ford. I know it *intimately*. We've been close, this river and I."

"You're stalling," Pell said from her perch above Kestrel's head.

"I'm *narrating*. There's a difference. Narration builds trust."

"What's the actual plan?" Sera asked.

Doss looked at the middle channel, where the water ran fast and dark over stones the color of old teeth. Then he looked at the treeline behind them, where any moment now six or seven men in ash-grey cloaks were going to come riding out with swords drawn and sermons loaded.

The actual plan was a thing he'd set up two nights ago, when they'd camped in the ruins of a shrine and everyone else had slept and he hadn't, because sleeping was for people whose pasts weren't armed and looking for them. The actual plan involved a cache of lamp-oil, a length of fuse-cord, and the old ferry house on the near bank, which was rotten as a pear but still held about forty gallons of the good stuff in its cellar, because Doss Marrow never used a road without leaving something useful along it.

The actual plan was also, and this was the part he'd been chewing on all morning like a gristle he couldn't swallow, a plan with exactly one exit in it. His exit. A fisherman's skiff tucked under the ferry house's broken dock, provisioned, oiled, ready. Downriver there was a marsh where the Order's horses couldn't follow, and past the marsh a coast, and past the coast a ship, and past the ship a whole world with no spire in it and no gods and no nameless country eating itself behind him.

He'd built that exit the same night he'd told himself: *if it goes wrong, you take the boat. You've done it before. Nobody will blame you.*

Except somebody would blame him. Somebody small, with god-touched eyes and a habit of saying thank you to inanimate objects, who had fallen asleep against his shoulder two nights running because apparently she'd decided he was safe, the little fool, and Doss Marrow had not been anyone's safe since before she was born.

"Doss?" Kestrel said. "You've gone quiet. That's usually expensive."

"I'm pricing my silence," Doss said. "It's steep today."

* * *

They got Pell across first.

That had been Kestrel's insistence, and nobody argued, because arguing with Kestrel Halloran about the girl was like arguing with a wall about being a wall. Sera went with her, sword drawn, watching the treeline. That left Doss and Kestrel on the near bank with the horses, the packs, and the growing sound of hoofbeats.

"Here." Doss pressed the fuse-cord into Kestrel's hand. "Ferry house cellar's full of oil. When we're across, light it. Whole bank goes up, they lose the crossing, we buy a day."

Kestrel turned the cord over. "And how do we get across? The horses can't swim that channel."

"The horses are a tax write-off. We swim."

"We—"

"There's a rope. Old ferry line, anchored both banks, still holds if you don't look at it too hard. Hand over hand, kick hard, don't think about the cold." Doss was already coiling it off the post. "I've done it four times. Well. Three times. The fourth time is why I know the channel so well."

Kestrel looked at him for a long moment, and Doss recognized the look, because he'd seen it coming for days now like weather on the horizon. It was the look of a man doing arithmetic.

"You built the fire trap," Kestrel said slowly, "and the rope, and the route downriver. When?"

"Two nights ago. While you were all sleeping like honest people."

"And the skiff under the dock."

The river made its noise. Somewhere back in the trees, a horn sounded—one long note, flat and certain, the Order calling the hunt closed.

"That's not an answer," Kestrel said.

"It's the only one I've got." Doss heard his own voice come out harder than he meant, so he softened it the only way he knew how, with the truth, which was the most expensive thing he owned. "Yes, Kestrel. There's a boat. There's always a boat. There has been a boat since the day I met you, because I am a man who keeps a boat, the way other men keep a knife or a lie. And every night since Vharen I've lain awake telling myself that when it comes to it, I'll take the boat, because that's what I do. I did it to you once already. Sold your name to the Order for coin and a head start, and I've told myself for seven years that a man can't change what he is."

"And now?"

"Now there's a child who thanks the rope for holding her," Doss said, "and I find I can't do the sums anymore. That's all. Don't make a speech about it. I'll be sick."

For once—and Doss would remember this later, in the bad hours and the good ones—for once Kestrel Halloran didn't make a speech. He just put his hand on Doss's shoulder, brief and heavy, and said, "Cross last, then. I'll hold the rope for you."

Which was such a small thing, and such an enormous thing, that Doss had to turn away and check the fuse-cord twice to keep his face in order.

* * *

The Order came out of the trees while Kestrel was halfway across the channel, hauling himself along the ferry line with the water tearing at his coat.

There were six of them, ash-grey cloaks, white sashes, and at their head a broad man on a dun mare whom Doss recognized immediately and had been dreading for a week: Brother Callow, who had taken Doss's confession seven years ago, written it all down in a fair hand, and then shaken Doss's wrist like a friend at the end of it. That was the thing about the Order that had always frightened Doss more than the burning—they were polite. They treated damnation like paperwork.

"Marrow!" Callow called across the water, cheerful as a market crier. "Still alive! The Prelate will be pleased. He does hate waste."

Doss stood on the near bank with the fuse-cord in his fist and did the fastest arithmetic of his life.

Here was the shape of it: Kestrel mid-channel. Sera and Pell on the far bank, Pell's small figure bright against the pines. Six riders between Doss and the water, and the ferry house at his back full of oil, and the skiff under the dock, and the marsh beyond the river that no horse could enter, and a whole soft warm world downriver where a man could forget himself professionally.

All he had to do was nothing. Stand here, raise his hands, give them the names. *Halloran, the gatekeeper. Vane, the heretic. The girl, the girl is the important one, the girl answers prayers.* Callow would write it down in his fair hand. Doss would ride out of this story alive, and the boat would rot under the dock unused, and he'd tell himself for another seven years that a man can't change what he is.

Callow dismounted and walked toward him across the shingle, easy, unhurried, sword still sheathed. Behind him the other five spread out along the bank, cutting off the shoreline both directions.

"You look tired, Marrow," Callow said. "This life doesn't suit you. Confession stands open. Full absolution, and the Prelate's protection. All it costs is—" he gestured pleasantly at the river, at the small figures on the far bank, at everything, really, "—the truth."

"The truth," Doss said. "Funny. I've been meaning to have a word with the truth myself."

He thought about the girl thanking the rope.

He struck the flint.

The fuse caught with a hiss and a spit of orange, and Doss turned and ran—not for the water, not for the skiff, but straight along the bank toward the middle channel, because the ferry house was going up in about thirty seconds and the blast would reach the dock and the dock was where the boat was tied, and he'd known that when he laid the fuse, hadn't he, he'd known it and laid it anyway, because a trap with no way out for the trapper wasn't a trap, it was a promise.

The world behind him turned orange and then white. The ferry house went up like a second sunrise, oil and rotten timber and forty years of Doss Marrow's careful exits, all of it blooming into flame, and the shockwave picked him up and threw him forward into the cold black water of the middle channel.

The cold hit him like a door. He went under, came up coughing, found the rope by blind luck and grace and grabbed it with both hands. Above him the sky was full of smoke. On the bank, the riders' horses screamed and wheeled; one went down in the burning shingle. Callow was shouting something about the girl, about taking the girl alive—

And then Doss saw the rider.

Not one of the five. One of the outriders who must have circled wide through the trees, come down to the far bank unseen, and now he was there, right there, ten feet from where Pell stood at the treeline with Sera twenty yards off and turning too slow, too slow, and the rider's blade already coming around, low, level, aimed at a child's chest.

Later, Doss would not remember deciding. He remembered the rope leaving his hands. He remembered the water, and the terrible long pull of the current, and his arms burning, and the far bank coming at him like a wall. He remembered hauling himself up the shingle on his elbows with his lungs full of knives, and he remembered getting between the blade and the girl with maybe half a heartbeat to spare, which was not enough time to draw anything or say anything clever, so he did the only thing left in his inventory.

He took it.

The blade went in under his ribs, angled, mean, a butcher's stroke, and the pain arrived a moment later like news from a distant country. Doss grabbed the rider's wrist with both hands and held on, because holding on was something he was good at, and shouted—actually shouted, voice cracking, undignified—"SERA!"

Sera arrived the way weather arrives. The rider came off his horse and did not get up in any way that mattered. The rest happened fast and far away: Kestrel dragging himself onto the bank, Sera standing over the fallen man, Pell screaming Doss's name in a voice much too big for her, the fire on the near bank roaring, the remaining riders pulling back into the smoke because the crossing was gone and the hunt was broken and the river ran red at the middle channel where a smuggler had let go of his rope.

Doss lay on the cold stones and watched the smoke climb and thought, with the strange floating clarity that comes after, *well. There goes the boat.*

* * *

Pell's hands were on him, small and desperate, pressing the wound, and her prayer was rising out of her like heat off a road—he felt it, everyone felt it, the air going taut and golden, the old hungry attention of the gods bending toward her, toward him, ready to answer, ready to take its price.

"No," Doss rasped, and caught her wrist. His grip was weaker than he wanted it to be. "No, sweetheart. Listen. Not for me. You hear me? Not for me. I've cost this family enough miracles."

"But you're—"

"I'm Doss Marrow. I'm hard to kill and harder to like. I'll manage." He grinned at her, and it hurt, and he grinned wider out of spite. "Besides. If you patch me up with god-magic, I'll owe the gods a favor, and I have never once in my life paid a debt on time."

A wet laugh came out of her, half sob. Good. Good, that was worth a rib.

Kestrel knelt on his other side, tearing his own shirt into bandages, and his face was doing something complicated. "You burned the boat," Kestrel said.

"I burned the boat."

"You burned your boat."

"Yes, Kestrel, I've *just* worked that out myself, thank you, the smoke was very instructive—"

"You could have crossed first. You could have taken the rope and gone and none of us would ever have known."

Doss looked up at the smoke, at the grey sky, at the pines swaying on the far shore of a country that was forgetting itself mile by mile behind them. He thought about the marsh downriver, and the coast, and the ship, and the whole soft world, all of it receding now like a tide going out, and found—to his genuine astonishment—that he didn't want it back.

"A man can't change what he is," he said. "Turns out that's true. So I changed what I'm *for*." He winced as Kestrel bound the wound. "Ow. Careful. Some of that's merchandise."

"Hold still, you impossible old fraud."

"That's *valuable* impossible old fraud."

Sera crouched at his shoulder, checking the bandage with a field surgeon's economy, and said quietly, "You'll live. Barely. You'll limp."

"Limping's fine. Limping's dignified. Gives a man an excuse." Doss let his head fall back on the stones and breathed, each breath a negotiation. Upstream, the ferry house was a black skeleton still smoking. Under its dock, sinking slowly into the cold brown water, went a fisherman's skiff, provisioned, oiled, and finally, blessedly, off the books.

Pell tucked herself against his good side, careful of the wound, and whispered, "Thank you for staying."

"Don't thank me yet," Doss muttered. "I'm a terrible patient. Ask anyone I've ever bled on."

But he put his arm around her anyway, because his arm was the one thing that still worked without complaint, and because some debts, he was learning, you pay forward.

It was Kestrel who spotted it first—a flicker at the treeline, upstream, where the smoke thinned. Figures. Not ash-grey. These wore no livery at all, a dozen shadows moving with the unhurried confidence of people who owned the forest, and at their center walked a tall shape in white that Doss had heard described in whispers all the way from Vharen to the hills.

Prelate Ossen had come to see the fire for himself.

And he was smiling.
