## 14. The Spire Appears

Pell had learned to count the days by Doss's complaints.

There were the morning complaints, which concerned his boots. There were the midday complaints, which concerned the road, the weather, the quality of the road *and* the weather simultaneously, and — since the wound at the ford — a new category of complaint concerning how everyone looked at him like he might fall over, which he resented deeply and also quietly arranged himself to be near Kestrel whenever the ground got steep.

But on the morning the mist came down off the hills like a held breath let out all at once, Doss said nothing at all. That was how Pell knew something was about to happen.

"Stop," she said.

The others stopped. They'd gotten good at that — stopping when she stopped, going when she went. It wasn't because she was in charge. It was because they'd figured out that she saw things first. Sera called it "the girl's advantage," which was a very inquisitor way of saying *she's marked and we're not, and I've decided to find that useful instead of terrifying.*

"What is it?" Kestrel asked. He had flour on his sleeve still, from the last village that remembered itself enough to have a baker. He never brushed it off. Pell thought he kept it there on purpose, the way you'd keep a stone from home in your pocket.

"The mist's wrong," she said.

They all looked at it. Mist, to most people, was mist. But Pell had spent eleven years being told her eyes were wrong — by priests, by neighbors, by one memorable aunt who had tried to wash them with holy water until they stopped seeing so much — and she had learned that *wrong* usually just meant *honest.* The mist wasn't lying around them. It was holding its shape. It stood in the valley below like water stands in a bowl, flat-topped and patient, and it did not drift, and it did not thin, and where the sun struck it, it did not steam.

"It's waiting," Pell said.

"For what?" Sera's hand had gone to the ash-marked hilt of her old inquisitor's blade, which she still carried even though carrying it was technically a crime in four jurisdictions and an act of blasphemy in the fifth. Sera had opinions about jurisdiction.

"For us," Pell said. "I think it's been waiting a long time."

* * *

They walked down into it together, because that was the kind of people they had become without ever deciding to.

Pell went first, because she always did now, and the mist took her the way bathwater takes you — warm, close, a little too intimate. Sound changed inside it. Her own footsteps sounded like someone else's footsteps, heard through a wall. She could hear Kestrel breathing behind her, and Doss's boots doing their complaining creak even though Doss's mouth had finally found mercy, and Sera's blade whispering against its scabbard with every step, because Sera drew it halfway the moment things got strange and pretended afterward that she hadn't.

Then the light changed.

It didn't brighten exactly. It *clarified*, the way a smudged window clarifies when someone breathes on it and wipes it clean. And Pell stopped walking, because ahead of them, rising out of the white like a bone rising out of sand, was the Spire.

She had imagined it a hundred times. In her imagining it had been tall and dark and terrible, a needle of black stone, a door with teeth. What stood before her instead was —

Old. That was the first thing. Old the way mountains are old, old the way the space under beds is old. It was made of a pale stone shot through with veins of gold, and the gold moved — slowly, slowly, like sap, like honey poured in winter. It rose so high that its top vanished not into cloud but into a softness above the mist, as if the sky up there had gone gentle. Its base was wide as a village square, and set into it, alone, unguarded, almost shy, was a door.

An ordinary door. Wooden. With an iron ring handle and a little awning over it, the kind shopkeepers put up to keep rain off their customers.

"That's not right," Kestrel whispered. He had come up beside her, and his face did something complicated. "That's my door. That's — that's the door of my mother's shop. In the capital. That awning, the ring, the scratch on the lower panel where I dragged a chair when I was six—"

He touched the scratch. His hand shook.

"It shows you your door," Pell said quietly. She didn't know how she knew. She knew the way she knew prayers were being answered, the way she knew a village had dissolved two valleys back — knowledge arriving whole, without permission. "It shows everyone their door."

Sera stared at the wood for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was careful, sanded smooth. "Mine was the novitiate dormitory. Third floor. There was a loose shutter I used to listen through." A pause. "I haven't thought about that shutter in twenty years."

Doss snorted from the rear, arms crossed over his bandaged side. "Mine's a tavern door in Corvess with a peephole drilled through it, and I'll thank the ancient horror not to bring that up in front of the child."

"You're the one who brought it up," Pell said.

"I'm wounded. I'm allowed."

Kestrel laughed — a small, cracked sound, but real. Then he looked at the door again and the laugh went out of him like a candle pinched. "Seven years I kept the gate beneath my shop. Seven years of wards and oil and silence, and I never once wondered why the Order built the spire looking like *home.*" He pressed his palm flat against the wood. "Because that's what it is. It's not a fortress. It's a threshold. And thresholds are supposed to look like somewhere you'd want to go."

"Or somewhere you can't help going," Sera said. "A door shaped like memory doesn't invite you. It *collects* you."

Pell reached past them both and took the iron ring.

"Pell—" Kestrel started.

"It's mine," she said. "Look again. Whose scratch is that?"

He looked. And Pell watched him understand: the door showed each of them their own door, but it was only ever one door, and it had chosen, this morning, in front of all of them, to be hers. A plain door from a house in a village that no longer appeared on any map, in a country that had forgotten its own name — the little house where she had knelt by her bed at five years old and prayed for her grandmother's cough to stop, and something vast and starving had leaned down through the dark and answered.

The door had always been hers. She was the only one of them the gods had marked twice.

She pulled it open.

* * *

Inside, time pooled like water, and Pell fell in.

Not fell — *stepped*, the way you step off a dock. The entry hall of the Spire should have been an entry hall. Instead it was ankle-deep in moments. Warm ones. Cold ones. Some of them moved across the stone floor in slow ripples, and where they touched her feet she felt them: a woman laughing in a kitchen that smelled of bread; a soldier's last breath, cold as well-water; a wedding; a funeral; a boy hiding under stairs counting to a thousand so the shouting upstairs would have time to stop.

"Careful where you stand," Kestrel said hoarsely. He was knee-deep already and hadn't seemed to notice. "These aren't ours. These are — everyone's. Everyone the gods ever answered. Every prayer that ever got through left a puddle."

"Charming architecture," Doss muttered. He was picking his way along a raised ridge of stone like a man crossing a stream, and doing it badly, favoring his hurt side. "No railings. No signage. In my professional opinion, whoever designed this place has never had to evacuate a building in a hurry."

"You're a smuggler," Sera said. "Evacuating buildings in a hurry is your entire profession."

"And I've *never once* had to do it through a floor made of other people's memories, Sera, thank you."

But Pell barely heard them. Because the pools weren't random. They flowed — gently, inevitably — toward the center of the hall, toward a wide spiral stair that rose up through the pale gold-veined stone, and the moments in the water were flowing the same direction, and among them, catching the light like oil catches fire, she saw moments she recognized.

Her grandmother's kitchen. The smell of rosemary and burnt porridge. The cough, worse in winter. Herself, small, kneeling, hands folded so tight her knuckles went white, praying with the desperate arithmetic of children: *if you make her better I will be good forever, I will give up sweets, I will never lie again, please, please, please.*

And the answer coming down. Not words. A *weight*. Something enormous turning its attention toward a five-year-old girl in a poor house in a forgotten country, and the attention itself being the miracle — the cough easing, the fever breaking, grandmother waking clear-eyed and calling her *little wren* — and Pell feeling, even then, even at five, something go out of her that never came back.

"There you are," she whispered to the pool.

"Pell?" Kestrel's hand settled on her shoulder, light as a bird. "You don't have to look."

"Yes I do." She crouched and let her fingers trail the surface. The moment rippled around her touch, and for one heartbeat she was five years old again and her grandmother was alive and the world had a name, and then the moment slid on, downstream, toward the stair, and she let it go. "It's not cruel," she said, surprised. "I thought it would be cruel. It's just... keeping them. Like a person keeps letters."

"The gods keep our prayers," Sera said slowly, "the way an archivist keeps evidence."

"Or the way a miser keeps coins," said Doss.

"Or the way a keeper keeps keys," Kestrel said, and nobody answered that, because none of them knew yet which it was, and the stair was waiting.

* * *

They climbed.

The stair spiraled up through the heart of the Spire, and as they climbed, the pools deepened. Ankle-deep became knee-deep became waist-deep, and the moments grew thicker, older, heavier. Pell felt them brushing past her like fish: plowmen praying for rain three hundred years ago; queens praying for sons; a leper praying simply to be touched once, kindly, before the end — and the answer, the terrible tender answer, some stranger's hand on his brow, and the cost of it deducted from some corner of the world that no one missed until the whole country had been spent coin by coin.

"That's what's happening," Sera said quietly, wading beside her. "This is the ledger. Every miracle ever granted, and next to each one—" she lifted a dripping hand from the water, and droplets fell upward instead of down, back into the pool, back toward the summit— "the payment. The land isn't draining into the gods' realm, Kestrel. It's draining *back*. All of it. Every prayer we ever borrowed against."

"Then we climb faster," Kestrel said.

They climbed faster.

And then the Spire began showing them their own.

Pell felt it happen rather than saw it — a change in the water, the way a current changes when you cross into new territory. The pools around the stair narrowed, deepened, went still and clear as glass, and in them she stopped seeing strangers.

She saw Kestrel stop dead three steps below her, staring down into a pool where a young man with flour-white hands knelt before a black iron gate in a cellar, swearing an oath in a language that made the stone flinch. She saw Sera turn her face away from a pool and then, after a long moment, deliberately turn it back — a younger Sera in gray robes, holding a torch, watching a burning, and the exact moment her certainty had begun, secretly, invisibly, to crack. She saw Doss stand over a pool for a long, long time without saying anything at all, which from Doss was louder than any complaint, and in the water was a younger Doss counting coins across a table from a younger Kestrel, and the coins were payment, and the payment was betrayal, and Doss's jaw worked and worked and finally he said, to the pool, to the boy in it, to nobody: "I know. I know. Get on with it."

And then Pell found her own pool, and it was not in the water at all.

It was on the wall. A niche in the pale stone, gold-veined, and inside it sat a moment rendered perfectly still: her grandmother's kitchen, complete, entire, preserved like a fly in amber. Grandmother at the table, little wren on her knee, teaching her a song. Not a prayer-song. Just a song, silly and sweet, about a peddler with too many hats. Outside the niche's frame, the real world went on — the stair, the water, her friends climbing — but inside the frame nothing moved, and Pell understood with a lurch that this was not a memory shown to her.

This was a memory *kept*. Set aside. Saved. Of all the moments in all the pools, someone had taken this one — a moment with no prayer in it at all, no miracle, no transaction, just a song about hats — and put it somewhere safe.

"Why this one?" she breathed.

"Maybe," Kestrel said softly, coming to stand beside her, "because it's the only thing left of a country that forgot itself. Maybe the Spire saves what the world drops." He looked at the frozen grandmother, the frozen child, and his voice went rough. "My mother used to sing that same song. Different words. Same tune. I'd forgotten I knew it until just now."

"The peddler's song," Pell whispered. "Everyone knows the peddler's song."

"No," said Doss quietly, from behind them. "Everyone *knew* it. There's a difference, and the difference is standing in this room." He limped closer, and for once there was no joke anywhere on him. "The Order built this place to keep a promise in. I told you that. I never understood what the promise was until right now." He nodded at the niche, at the song, at the small preserved warmth of it. "It's not keeping the gods in. It's keeping *us* in. Everything we were. Every song. So that when the land finished forgetting, there'd be one place left that remembered — and one person marked to carry it back out."

All of them looked at Pell.

She looked at the niche. At her grandmother's hands, forever mid-clap, forever keeping time. Terror went through her, cold and total — the weight of it, a whole country's worth of *remember me* stacked on the shoulders of one eleven-year-old girl who still sometimes sucked her thumb when she thought nobody was looking.

And underneath the terror, warm as banked coals, something else. Because her grandmother was in there. Not gone. *Kept.* And if Pell carried the memory up those stairs and gave it back to whatever waited at the top, then maybe — maybe — her grandmother would be kept everywhere again. In every kitchen. On every map.

"Well," Pell said, and her voice only shook a little, which she considered a personal triumph. "We'd better not drop it."

* * *

Near the top, the water ended.

The last flight of stairs rose dry out of the shallows, and the air changed — thinner, brighter, tasting faintly of ozone and old incense. Through a tall arched opening above them spilled light that was not sunlight, gold-shot and slow-moving, pulsing very slightly, like the glow off the veins in the Spire's stone. Like breathing.

Pell climbed until her head cleared the archway, and stopped.

The summit chamber was vast and round and empty, and it was not empty at all. The light pooled in the center of the floor in a wide, slow whirlpool of gold, turning, turning, and within it — shapes. Doors. Hundreds of doors, thousands, standing upright in the light like stones in a river, each one different, each one flickering between forms: a cottage door, a cathedral door, a cellar hatch, a garden gate, a shop door with a scratch on the lower panel. Doors within doors within doors, all of them the same door, all of them open a crack, and through every crack the same slow golden light seeped out like blood through a bandage.

At the far side of the chamber, silhouetted against the whirlpool, stood figures. Many figures. Robed. Still. Arranged in a half-circle with the neatness of people who believe very strongly in order.

One of them turned. Even at that distance, even across all that light, Pell knew the silhouette: broad, square-shouldered, head bowed in permanent benediction. She had seen it in Sera's nightmares often enough to recognize it awake.

"Sera," she whispered. "Your prelate is here. And he hasn't come alone."

Below her on the stairs, Sera went very still, and then very quiet, and then said, in the voice of a woman setting down something heavy she has carried a long way:

"Then we'd best decide quickly whose door we're knocking on."

Above them, the thousand doors all opened a little wider.
