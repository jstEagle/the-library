## 16. The Price of Names

Sera Vane had spent eleven years learning how to read doors.

The Order taught it as doctrine: a door was a confession. Every lock admitted fear; every bolt admitted something worth stealing or someone worth keeping out. She had stood before ten thousand doors in her career—cottage doors, chapel doors, the iron doors of confessionals where sinners whispered through grilles—and she had always known what waited on the other side before her knuckles touched wood.

The door at the heart of the Forgotten Spire told her nothing at all.

It stood in a chamber that should not have fit inside the tower's footprint, a hall of pale stone veined with gold like sunlight caught mid-pour. The door itself was old in a way that made the word *old* feel insufficient—the way a mountain is not merely tall. It had no handle. No hinges she could find. Its surface moved when she wasn't looking directly at it, grain shifting like water remembering it had once been a river.

And carved into its face, in letters that rearranged themselves whenever Pell wasn't watching (the girl had announced this cheerfully over breakfast, as though reporting on the weather), were words that Sera had now read forty times:

*A name freely given opens what no key can.*

"Freely given," Kestrel said behind her. He'd been saying it for two days, in exactly that tone—flat, careful, the tone of a man walking around a hole in the floor he'd already fallen into once. "That's the part I don't like."

"You don't like any part of it."

"I don't like most parts of it. That part least." He came to stand beside her, arms crossed, and Sera noted—not for the first time—that the man who had guarded a gate to the gods' realm for seven years stood before doors the way other men stood before spiders. "A name isn't a coin you hand over. It's not even a memory. It's the thread everything else hangs from. Give them your name and they can pull, and everything you are comes unraveling after it."

"And if nobody gives one?"

"Then the door stays shut, and Ossen feeds the gods until there's nothing left to feed them, and the country finishes forgetting itself." He said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. "So. Yes. Someone gives their name."

*Someone.* The word sat between them like a guest neither wanted to acknowledge.

Sera turned from the door. "Pell can't."

"Pell can't," he agreed immediately. "She's a child. Her name is still wet paint. It would take everything."

"Doss won't offer. He'd never offer—he'd sell us all passage and charge us for the rope—but if we asked him outright he'd say yes, and then he'd do it badly, out of guilt, and guilt makes people stupid at doors."

"Guilt makes people stupid everywhere," Kestrel said. "I have references."

"So it comes down to you or me."

The chamber was quiet in the particular way the Spire was quiet everywhere—not silent, but *attentive*, as though the walls were leaning in. Somewhere above them, time pooled and dripped. Somewhere below, Ossen was doing whatever Ossen did with his congregation of willing faithful, and every hour they spent deliberating was an hour the prelate spent feeding.

Kestrel looked at her. And Sera Vane, who had faced down heretics and prelates and once, memorably, a barn fire with a trapped child and no water, found herself unable to hold his gaze for longer than three seconds.

"Don't," he said quietly.

"I haven't said anything."

"You've been saying it since the ford. Since the choir fight. You've been saying it since the night you hid Pell instead of burning her, which, incidentally, was treason, and you did it with the expression of a woman clearing her conscience in advance." He stepped closer. "You've decided your name is already spent. You think ash owns it. So handing it to the gods feels like bookkeeping to you—moving a debt from one ledger to another."

"That is not—" She stopped. Started again, more honestly: "That is substantially what I think."

"Sera."

"It's *true*, Kestrel." Her voice came out harder than she intended, the inquisitor's voice, the interrogation-room voice, and she hated it. "I burned people. Do you know what my name is? Not symbolically. Literally. In four provinces, *Sera Vane* is what mothers invoke when children misbehave. *Behave, or the Ash Inquisitor comes.* My name belongs to smoke and screaming. If the gods take it, they're taking something that was never mine to begin with. I'm not sacrificing anything. I'm settling accounts."

The words rang in the attentive stone and died there.

Kestrel didn't argue. That was the worst part. He just looked at her with an expression she'd spent weeks learning to read and still got wrong half the time—something between grief and exasperation, the face of a man watching someone insist the cage door was painted on.

Then he said, "Do you know what my mother called me?"

Sera blinked. "What?"

"My mother. In the capital. On the street I showed you—the one with the crooked chimney pots, where I couldn't remember her face." His voice stayed level, but his hands had gone still in a way that meant effort. "I can't remember her face. I can't remember her voice. But I remember—I *know*, the way you know a scar—that she called me Kes. Just Kes. Nobody else ever has. And when I look at that door and think about giving it my name, here's what actually frightens me: not dying. Not unraveling. It's that if the gods take *Kestrel Halloran*, maybe they take *Kes* too. Maybe they take the last person who ever loved me enough to shorten my name, and I lose her twice."

The chamber hummed faintly, as if considering.

"That's what a name is," he said. "It's not yours alone. It's everyone who ever used it gently. You want to give the gods yours because you think it's a weapon someone else forged. But somewhere in Vharen—somewhere in the provinces you're ashamed of—there's a person who knew you before the ash. A mother, a sister, some child you taught to skip stones. They gave you a name too, Sera. They're holding half of it. You don't get to spend what isn't only yours."

Sera opened her mouth. Closed it.

Because the terrible thing—the thing that made her chest ache in a way no doctrine had ever reached—was that she could almost see the person he described. A kitchen. A woman's hands dusted with flour. A voice saying *Seri, come in from the rain,* and the memory dissolved even as she grasped it, because the land forgot, because everything forgot, because—

"I don't remember who called me Seri," she whispered.

"No," Kestrel said gently. "But somebody did. And when this is over, when the land remembers itself, you'll get her back. Both of them. The name and the voice. That's the whole point of what we're doing. We're not running from the gods—we're collecting what they owe."

*

They argued about it for two more days, which Doss later described as "the least romantic courtship I've ever witnessed, and I once watched two smugglers propose over a knife fight."

He said it from his cot by the eastern window, leg propped on a rolled cloak, mending a net with the patience of a man who had nothing left to prove and a limp to prove it with. Pell sat cross-legged beside him, sorting charms—real ones now, the kind Kestrel had finally stopped pretending were trinkets—and she looked up with the terrible directness of children.

"Why don't you both give half a name each?"

The room went still.

"What?" Kestrel said.

"A whole name is too much for one person. You said so. It unravels everything." Pell held up two charms, one blue, one green, comparing them against the light. "But the door says *a name*. It doesn't say whose, or that it has to be one person's. Names are made of pieces anyway. Seri. Kes. The parts mothers use." She set the charms down. "If you split it, maybe it doesn't unravel anybody. Maybe it just... stretches."

Doss snorted. "Child, doors don't work like—"

The Spire hummed.

Not the ambient hum. A different one—lower, resonant, coming up through the floor like a struck bell heard through the earth. The net slipped from Doss's hands. Pell's eyes went wide and silvered, the god-touched shine she got when the old things leaned close, and she said, in a voice that was hers and also slightly older than hers:

"*Two keepers. One door. Half the debt, doubled the bond.*"

The hum faded. Pell shook her head like a dog shedding water, blinked, and said, "Ow. I hate when they borrow me. It's like wearing someone else's boots."

Silence.

"Well," Doss said at last, into the ringing air. "Either that was prophecy, or the building agrees with the girl, and frankly I don't love either option."

Sera looked at Kestrel. Kestrel looked at Sera. And the thing that passed between them was not triumph or relief but something quieter and far more frightening: the realization that they had run out of reasons not to say the true thing aloud.

*

She found him that evening on the Spire's western balcony, if a ledge of mist-wrapped stone jutting over a country being unmade could be called a balcony. Below, far below, the land flickered—a village visible, then less than visible, then visible again, like a candle guttering. He was watching it the way he watched everything now: like a man memorizing a house he expected to burn.

"The plan is madness," Sera said, coming to stand beside him. "Splitting a name between two people. The wards might reject it. The gods might take it as an insult. Pell's interpretation could be wrong."

"All true."

"We could both unravel halfway and be stuck as half-people forever."

"Also true. Although," he added, with the ghost of the dry humor she'd learned to treasure, "some would argue we were already half-people. You with your ash, me with my missing mother. Between us we might make one functional person. It'd be efficient."

She laughed despite herself—a short, cracked sound—and then the laughter ran out, and they stood shoulder to shoulder in the cold, and the moment arrived the way such moments do: without announcement, without dignity, all at once.

"If we do this," Sera said, "and it works, and the door opens—what happens to us? To the name we share?"

"I've been thinking about that." He turned to face her fully. In the light of the lamp Pell had insisted they carry everywhere—"so the Spire knows we're people, not prayers"—his eyes were steady and unguarded in a way she had rarely seen. "Here's what I know. Seven years I kept that gate alone. I told myself it was duty. It was partly duty. But mostly I was hiding, and I told myself hiding was holy." He took a breath. "Then a disgraced inquisitor kicked my door in, dragged me across a dissolving country, insulted my charm-making, saved my life twice, and somewhere on the road east I stopped wanting to guard things alone."

"Kestrel—"

"Let me finish, because if I stop I'll start negotiating with myself again, and I'm very good at it." His voice roughened. "I love you, Sera Vane. Not the way the ballads say it—all thunder and sacrifice. Quietly. Like a lamp left burning. Like a door left unlocked. I love the inquisitor and the traitor and the woman who hides children from her own doctrine, and I will love whichever halves of us survive tomorrow, and if none of us survives, then I'll have loved you for exactly as long as I existed, and I regret none of it. There. Said plainly. No doctrine, no ash, no gate. Just that."

The wind off the forgetting-country pulled at their cloaks. Far below, a village guttered back into being, and Sera thought: *that's what he does. He makes things reappear.*

"I burned people," she said, because she owed him honesty even now. Especially now.

"I know."

"I may burn again, metaphorically. I'm difficult. I interrogate furniture. I once arrested a man for selling underweight candles."

"I've met the candles. He had it coming."

She laughed, and it broke something open, and before she could decide whether to be terrified, she said it back: "I love you. Plainly. Without metaphor, because I'm bad at metaphors and you deserve precision." She took his hand—scarred, charm-scarred, gatekeeper's hands. "Half a name, then. Half a debt. And when the land remembers itself, we find whoever called me Seri, and you find your mother's face, and we do it together. That's the bargain. That's the whole of it."

"That's the whole of it," he agreed.

Above them, deep in the Spire, the great door began—very softly—to glow.

*

They told the others at dawn.

Doss took it better than expected, which is to say he complained for a full quarter hour about the paperwork implications of shared collateral, then quietly pressed his lucky knife into Kestrel's palm and pretended he hadn't. Pell simply hugged them both, hard, and said, "I'll hold the other end of the thread. That's what I'm for. I'm the witness. Witnesses hold threads."

They climbed together through corridors where their own pasts pooled like standing water—Sera saw, briefly, a rain-soaked street and flour-dusted hands and did not look away this time—and came at last to the chamber of the moving-grained door.

The words carved upon it had changed.

*A name freely given opens what no key can. Two names, freely joined, open it wider.*

And beneath the words, new lines were surfacing in the gold-veined stone, letter by letter, like something rising through water:

*BUT THE DOOR IS NOT UNWATCHED. ANOTHER HAS REACHED THE SUMMIT. HE BRINGS THEM BY THE HUNDRED, AND THEY GO WILLINGLY, AND THE GODS ARE LEARNING TO BE HUNGRY IN A NEW WAY.*

Sera read it twice. Beside her, Kestrel went very still.

"Ossen," she said. "He's already here. He's been here while we argued about names."

Below them—or perhaps above; the Spire had stopped honoring the distinction—a sound began to rise through the stone. Not the hum of the attentive walls. Voices. Hundreds of voices, chanting in unison, in a cadence Sera knew from a hundred sermons she'd sat through with her jaw clenched.

Ossen's litany. The Litany of Lawful Flame.

And threaded through it, growing louder, growing *closer*, came the sound of footsteps—many, many footsteps—climbing toward the summit chamber, and the smell of incense, and beneath the incense something else, something older and worse:

Ash. Fresh ash. The kind that used to be people.

Pell grabbed Sera's sleeve, eyes silvered wide. "He's feeding them early," she whispered. "And the gods—they're starting to *like* it."
