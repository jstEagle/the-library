## 17. Ossen Ascendant

Prelate Ossen had climbed the spire for three days, and his knees had opinions about all of them.

He did not let the knees slow him. He had buried two wives, one heresy trial, and forty-one years of doubt beneath this cassock, and none of those had slowed him either. Doubt was the heaviest of the four, and he had carried it longest, and so when at last he crested the final stair and stepped out onto the summit platform — a ring of white stone open to a sky the color of old pewter — what he felt was not triumph.

It was relief. The particular relief of a man setting down a load he had convinced himself was holy.

"Well," said Brother Anselm beside him, breathing hard, "it's very tall."

"It is," Ossen agreed.

"And we're very high."

"We are."

Anselm was sixty, mild as porridge, and had once been the Order's finest accountant before God called him to worse work. He peered over the edge of the parapet with the expression of a man checking a ledger for errors. Far below, the mist churned around the spire's roots like milk in tea. Somewhere down there were the heretics — Halloran, Vane, the Marrow man, and the girl — climbing toward the same summit by routes Ossen had not bothered to learn. Let them climb. Climbing took time, and time was the one thing Ossen no longer intended to give anyone.

"Set the lamps," he said. "Wake the congregation. It is nearly evening, and we have an appointment with eternity."

* * *

The congregation numbered one hundred and twelve.

Ossen knew the number precisely because he had counted them himself, twice, on the road east, the way he counted everything now. Numbers were honest in a way doctrine had never been. One hundred and twelve souls who had heard his preaching in the hollow towns along the pilgrim road — towns where mothers could not remember their children's faces, where farmers sowed fields they would forget by harvest — and who had looked at the crumbling world and then at Ossen's calm gray eyes and said, *yes. Yes, if it will fix it. Yes, take me.*

They gathered now on the summit platform in their rough wool robes, holding candles that guttered in a wind that smelled of iron and rain-that-never-fell. They were not fanatics. That was the thing Sera Vane had never understood, back when she'd sat in his lecture hall taking notes with that fierce little frown, the thing the whole Order had never understood. Fanatics shouted. His people whispered. They were tired. Tired people will trade almost anything for rest, and Ossen was offering them the deepest rest there was: to be poured out like wine into the cupped hands of gods, and to have the world made lawful by the pouring.

"Brothers. Sisters." He raised his hands, and the wind quieted as though it too wished to hear. "You know why you've come."

A woman near the front — Marta, a baker from a village that no longer appeared on maps, whose bread Ossen had eaten every morning for nine days and which had been, frankly, excellent — lifted her candle higher. "For the law," she said softly.

"For the law," the congregation echoed.

"For mercy," someone added, and Ossen winced inwardly, just slightly, the way a man winces when a guest uses the wrong fork. Mercy. He had stopped believing in mercy somewhere around his second wife's deathbed, when he had prayed through three nights and received nothing but silence and the smell of tallow. But he did not correct the woman. A congregation may believe the ladder reaches heaven even if the carpenter knows it only reaches the roof. What mattered was that they climbed.

"The old gods starve," Ossen said. "That is the secret the Order has kept for six hundred years, wrapped in incense and Latin nobody reads. Not monsters. Not demons. *Custodians.* They held the world together once — held it the way a dam holds water — and we forgot them, and forgetting is a kind of theft, and the theft has come due. Every answered prayer you've heard tell of, every miracle twisted hungry — that is starvation, brothers. Sisters. That is what happens when the custodians have nothing left to hold the door with."

Anselm shifted beside him. Anselm still believed in mercy. Ossen found this touching, the way one finds a child's belief in the tooth fairy touching, and kept it the way one keeps such things: carefully, and without examination.

"So we feed them," Ossen said. "Not with lambs. Not with blood smeared on doorposts. With *selves*. One hundred and twelve willing souls, given freely, remembered fully — because that is the coin the old gods mint, do you see? Memory. Attention. To be seen by a god is to exist; to be forgotten is to dissolve. We go to them *remembered*, each of us carrying our whole life like a lamp, and we set our lamps on their table, and they will eat the light and rise from their long hunger and *hold the world again*."

Marta was crying. Several others were. Good tears. Clean tears. Ossen had learned to distinguish them during forty-one years of pastoral work, and these were the clean kind — grief for a world being left behind, not terror of what came next.

"And the world?" asked a young man near the back. Tam, a stonemason's son. "When they're fed. When they rise. What happens to us?"

Ossen smiled. This was the question. This was always the question, and he had an answer, and the answer was true, and the truth of it was the cruelest thing about him.

"The world becomes lawful," he said. "No more forgetting. No more villages sliding off maps. No more prayers answered by things that wear the shapes of answers. Law, without corruption. Order, without mercy's little bribes and exceptions. A world where a mother remembers her child because the *architecture of remembering* is sound again." He let the silence sit. "It will cost us everything. We are the mortar, brothers. Sisters. Mortar does not get to live in the house."

He had rehearsed that line for eleven years. It still landed. He watched it land, face by face, candle by candle, and felt the familiar cold satisfaction settle into his chest like a swallowed stone.

And beneath the satisfaction — small, deniable, filed away with the doubt — something whispered: *you have never once tested whether the dam works. You are betting one hundred and twelve lives on a theology you built alone in a dark room.*

Ossen told the whisper to be silent. The whisper, being his own, obeyed.

* * *

The feeding began at moonrise, or what passed for moonrise under that pewter sky — a pale disc like a fingernail paring, visible only in gaps in the cloud.

The ritual was simple, because Ossen distrusted complexity. Complexity was where heresy hid. He had the congregation stand in a ring around the summit's center, where the white stone was worn into a shallow depression, smooth as a bowl, older than the Order, older than any map. He had them each speak their own name aloud — full name, true name, the name their mother had used when they were small and in trouble — and then the name of everyone they loved, and then the name of the place they were born.

Names, names, names. A hundred and twelve lives recited into the bowl of stone.

And the stone *listened*.

Ossen felt it begin as a pressure behind his eyes, a leaning-in, the sensation of a vast attention turning toward the summit like a lighthouse beam. The mist below stopped churning. The wind died entirely. Even the candles stood up straight, flames rigid as needles.

Then the first of them went.

It was Marta. Of course it was Marta; she had been first to volunteer on the road, first to everything, a woman built of forward motion. She stepped into the bowl of stone, smiled at Ossen — actually smiled, and he would remember that smile for the rest of his life, however long the gods allowed it to be — and said, "For the law," and then she was not a woman anymore.

She was light. Warm light, gold-edged, rising off the stone like heat-shimmer made visible, and it streamed upward into the pewter sky and did not disperse. It went *somewhere*. Through whatever thin place the spire marked, into whatever dark room the old custodians had been starving in.

And the sky — Ossen saw it clearly, and would deny later that his hands shook — the sky *shivered*. Like a horse feeling a fly. Like a sleeper stirring.

One hundred and eleven remained. They did not flee. That was the terrible, magnificent thing. They watched Marta become light, and they did not run, because Ossen had spent nine days teaching them that dissolution was promotion, and they had believed him, and belief is the strongest material known to man or god.

"Next," Ossen said.

Tam the stonemason's son stepped forward, gave his name and his father's name and the name of the quarry town that no longer existed, and became light, and the sky shivered harder, and somewhere far below — Ossen felt it through the soles of his boots — something vast turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to wake.

By the twentieth soul, the clouds above the spire had begun to rotate. Slowly. Patiently. A great gray eye forming overhead.

By the fortieth, the light of the offerings no longer rose straight up; it bent, curving toward the eye like iron filings toward a lodestone, and Ossen stood in the center of it with his arms raised, drenched in borrowed radiance, and thought: *I am the hinge. I am the man who fed the starving and saved the world.* The thought tasted like communion wine and copper.

By the seventieth, Brother Anselm took Ossen's sleeve.

"Prelate." The old accountant's voice was very quiet. "May I show you a ledger entry?"

"Now is hardly—"

"Two lines." Anselm's mild face had gone the color of the mist. "Line one: seventy souls given. Line two: the sky is eating them. Prelate — I have watched ledgers my whole life, and I know a withdrawal when I see one. Nothing is coming *down*. Everything is going *up*. And the appetite—" his hand trembled toward the rotating clouds, toward the eye, toward the deep rhythmic pull that had entered the air itself, like the draft of an enormous opened mouth— "the appetite is growing faster than the gifts. That is not how salvation behaves, Prelate. That is how debt behaves."

Ossen looked at his oldest servant. Then up at the eye.

And for one unguarded moment — the first in forty-one years — Prelate Ossen doubted.

It lasted the length of a heartbeat. In that heartbeat he saw it all laid out plain, the way Anselm saw ledgers: the theology built alone in a dark room, the congregation as collateral, the gods not as custodians to be strengthened but as creditors to be paid, and payment, once begun, only *increasing* the size of the debt — and himself, Aldous Ossen, standing at the mouth of the machine he had built, feeding it the only currency it wanted, forever, because a creditor fed is a creditor owed more—

Then the moment closed. He was a man of the Order. Men of the Order did not stop halfway up a mountain.

"They are waking," he said aloud, and his voice rang steady across the summit, and eighty souls turned to him with trust shining in their faces, and he hated — briefly, privately, in the locked room behind his eyes — how easy it was. "Waking is painful, Anselm. Birth is a wound. Hold the line."

Anselm held the line. Anselm always held the line. But he did not step back from Ossen's side, and his hand stayed on his sleeve, light as ash, all the way through soul ninety.

* * *

At soul one hundred, the spire spoke.

Not in words. In *pressure* — a single pulse through the white stone, up through Ossen's sandals, into his teeth, and every candle on the summit flared green for an instant and went out. In the sudden dark, lit only by the streaming gold of the offerings, Ossen saw the far side of the platform clearly for the first time.

There was a door there.

It had not been there an hour ago. It stood free of the wall, freestanding, impossible — a plain wooden door with an iron latch, the kind found in kitchens and poorhouses, standing upright on the summit of a spire above the clouds. And it was *locked*. He could feel the lock the way one feels a headache: a knot of refusal in the air, dense and deliberate.

Someone had shut it. Someone had warded it against exactly what Ossen was doing, and recently — the wards still smelled of charm-smoke and market dust, of a shop counter and careful hands.

"Halloran," Ossen breathed.

The charm-seller. The gatekeeper. The little man with the little shop who had spent seven years pretending to sell luck while sitting on the hinge of the world. Of course. Of course he would come, and climb, and lock the door from the outside like a shopkeeper pulling down the shutter — as if a wooden latch could stand between the old gods and their meal.

Rage rose in Ossen, hot and unfamiliar, and he welcomed it, because rage was simpler than doubt.

"He's close," Anselm murmured. "If he's warded the door, he's minutes behind us. And Prelate — the girl. The god-touched child. If she reaches the summit while the offering is still open—"

"Then we finish before she arrives." Ossen turned to the remaining twelve. Twelve! So few. The arithmetic of it struck him with sudden horror that he converted, mid-breath, into resolve. "Brothers. Sisters. The last of the feast. Give your names."

They gave them. Eleven lights rose, bending toward the hungry eye, and the sky groaned like a ship in weather, and the great gray rotation tightened, and the whole summit trembled with the approach of something immense turning its attention toward the world for the first time in six hundred years.

One soul remained. A young woman, barely twenty, clutching her candle with both hands, looking from the bowl of stone to Ossen's face with a question in her eyes she had been too polite to ask for nine days.

"Will it hurt?" she asked.

And Ossen — who had buried two wives and forty-one years of doubt, who had fed one hundred and eleven souls to a debt he suddenly was not certain could ever be paid, who stood at the top of the world with his hands raised and his theology cracking like river ice — heard himself say, gently, warmly, in the voice that had talked a hundred frightened people into peace:

"No, child. It won't hurt at all."

She smiled, stepped into the stone, and became light.

And the eye above the spire opened all the way, and looked down at Prelate Ossen — at *him*, specifically, personally, with an attention like a falling cathedral — and he understood in that instant, with perfect clarity, that he had made a terrible error about who was being fed and who was doing the feeding.

Below him, faint through the stone, he heard footsteps on the stairs. More than one set. Running.

And the wooden door with the iron latch began, very quietly, to glow.
