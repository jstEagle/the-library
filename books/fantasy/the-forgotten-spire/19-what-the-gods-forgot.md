## 19. What the Gods Forgot

Pell had always thought that if she ever met a god, it would be like meeting the sun. Something vast and burning and impossible to look at directly, something that would know her name before she opened her mouth.

She had not expected them to be *hungry*.

Not hungry for her—she understood hunger for flesh and blood and soul-stuff well enough by now, having spent three weeks walking through a country that was eating itself. No. The old gods were hungry the way an empty house is hungry. The way a cold hearth is hungry. They were vast and patient and *starving*, and they had forgotten—not how to eat, but what eating was *for*.

"Again," said the one whose name was a long low note, like a bell rung underwater. Pell had decided to call it Lowbell, because thinking of it as *the second custodian of the western threshold* made her head ache in a way that felt like forgetting herself.

"Again," Pell agreed, and reached into the light.

The gate stood open behind her. Not the iron-and-oak door Kestrel had guarded under his shop floor—that had been only the smallest face of it, the keyhole version. This was the true door, the one at the spire's heart, and it was less a door than a wound in the world with good manners. Light poured out of it the way water pours from a tipped jug: steadily, endlessly, without any opinion about where it went.

Through that light came the prayers. They had always come through; that was the whole terrible secret of it. Every prayer ever answered had passed through this gate, and every answer had cost the land something—a field here, a memory there, a village's whole history unspooled into thread. The gods hadn't stolen anything. They'd been *given* things, over centuries, by people who didn't know they were giving, and the gods had eaten because starving things eat, and they had never once asked themselves whether they should.

Nobody had taught them. That was the part that broke Pell's heart open every morning. Nobody had ever taught them.

"Her name was Marta," Pell said, holding up the memory like a lamp. "Marta Ostrevva. She lived in a village called Brindle's End, which was east of the ford and south of the hills that look like sleeping dogs. She had four children and a bad knee that ached before rain. When her youngest caught fever, she prayed—not to you, you weren't listening then, but the prayer passed through anyway—and something answered, and the fever broke."

Lowbell's attention settled on her. It did not have eyes, but Pell felt it looking the way you feel weather changing.

"And the cost?" it asked. Its voice was the sound of a harvest coming in, if a harvest could speak.

"The cost was the village's well. It dried up. Everyone blamed the drought." Pell swallowed. "And then the village itself, later. It's gone now. But Marta isn't gone. I remember her. Her name was Marta Ostrevva, and she sang off-key, and she once gave her whole winter bread to a beggar who turned out to be a tax collector, and she laughed about it for years."

The light shivered. Somewhere in its depths, something turned over, slow as a whale.

"Why," said Lowbell, and the word came out wondering, "do you keep their names?"

Pell looked back at it. Behind her, at the edge of the light, Kestrel sat cross-legged with his hands flat on the stone, feeding the gate steadiness the way he'd fed it wards for seven years. Sera stood watch at the stair, ash-grey cloak thrown back, sword loose in its sheath because she'd finally learned that some doors you guard with your body and some you guard with your patience. And Doss lay propped against a fallen pillar with his bandaged side and a face like a man losing an argument with sleep, except he wasn't asleep, because he never quite slept when Pell was working. He just listened, and pretended not to.

"Because," Pell said, "a name is the smallest mercy there is. And small mercies add up. I've seen it happen."

* * *

It had not started that way. It had started with Ossen.

The prelate had reached the summit first—they all knew that now, would always know it—and he had brought his congregation with him: two hundred faithful in ash-white robes, kneeling in ranks around the spire's crown, singing. Their song was beautiful. That was the horror of it. It was the most beautiful thing Pell had ever heard, and it was a slaughter.

Ossen stood among them with his arms raised, feeding them upward into the light one soul at a time, and the gods—who did not understand what he was doing, who had never been *offered* anything before, who knew only hunger—began to accept.

"No," Pell had screamed, running up the last stair with Kestrel behind her. "No, stop, they don't know what they're taking—"

"They know exactly what they're taking," Ossen had said, turning to her with a smile of such serene certainty that Pell's stomach dropped. He looked *happy*. He looked like a man watching a dam open over a dry field. "Child, do you know what I spent thirty years learning? That mercy is chaos. That every answered prayer is a debt unpaid. These gods are law itself—law without corruption, law without favoritism, law without *mercy*, which is the flaw at the heart of everything mortal. I am giving them the raw material of a lawful world."

"You're giving them people."

"I'm giving them *order*." His voice rang off the stones. "Watch. Watch what they build."

And Pell had watched, because she couldn't help it, because she was the only one who could see it clearly—the way the offered souls went into the light and the light tried to make something of them and couldn't, because you cannot build order out of people who were given rather than chosen, because a gift taken by force curdles even in a god's mouth. The song faltered. One of the kneeling faithful lifted her head, confused, her prayer half-finished, and the light around her stuttered like a candle in wind.

The gods were choking.

"They can't digest it," Pell said. "Ossen, they can't—it doesn't work like that. You can't feed them a kingdom. You have to teach them. One name at a time. One *soul* at a time, freely remembered, freely given—"

"Freely." He spat the word like a seed. "Freedom is the disease. I am the cure."

Kestrel had stepped past her then. Pell would remember this forever, the way you remember the exact shape of a moment that changes everything: Kestrel Halloran, charm-seller, gate-keeper, a man who had spent seven years being small on purpose, walked up to the most dangerous man in the country and said, quietly, "You're right about one thing. They're custodians. They were set to guard something, and they forgot what. Do you know what it was?"

Ossen's smile flickered.

"It was us," Kestrel said. "They were guarding *us*. The whole forgotten country. Every name on no map. And they forgot, and their forgetting ate the land, and your answer is to hand them more to forget." He shook his head. "You're not curing the disease, Prelate. You're the fever."

And then—because Kestrel was Kestrel—he had turned away from Ossen entirely, as if the man were already finished, and knelt beside Pell in the light, and said, "Show them, little bird. Start with someone small. Start with someone true."

So she had. She started with Marta Ostrevva and her bad knee and her off-key singing, and the light had listened, and somewhere in the depths of the gate something very old and very starved had tasted a single human life, freely told, and found that it was *enough*.

One name. Then another. Then another.

* * *

By the third day, Pell had a rhythm.

Mornings she worked the near names—the ones still warm, the villages that had dissolved only weeks ago, their people scattered but alive, remembering nothing of where they'd come from. Those were easy, almost sweet. She'd hold a memory up to the light—"This is Hessel Fen. This is the bridge over the Aldwater, green with moss, and a boy named Tam fell off it twice every summer and was fished out both times by a girl named Brey who married him eleven years later"—and the light would take it, and somewhere far away a road would remember which way it ran, and a hearthstone would remember it had held a fire.

Afternoons were harder. The deep names. Countries under countries. People so thoroughly erased that Pell sometimes had to dig for them through layers of her own seeing, down in the dark place inside her where the god-touched gift lived, the place that hurt to use and hurt worse not to.

Evenings, she rested, and the others took turns sitting with her, and this was its own kind of work, though nobody called it that.

Doss sat with her on the fourth evening, his back against the pillar, his wounded side carefully arranged, a strip of dried apple in his fingers that he kept forgetting to eat.

"Do the farmers," he said. "When you give the gods a farmer. Does it hurt them? The gods?"

Pell considered. "I don't think they know how to be hurt yet. I think it's more like... you know when you've been hungry so long you forget you're hungry, and then someone puts bread in front of you, and suddenly you're shaking?"

"Aye." Doss's voice was rough. "I know that one."

"It's like that. Except the bread is a person's whole life, and they have to take it one crumb at a time or it kills them." She glanced at him. "That's what happened to Ossen's congregation. He tried to feed them a feast."

Doss was quiet a while. Below them, faintly, the spire hummed. Somewhere on the stairs, Sera was pacing her watch, boot-heels ringing steady as a clock.

"I sold you out once," Doss said abruptly. "Kestrel. Seven years back. Sold his name to the Order for passage money and a pardon that turned out to be written on ash." He wasn't looking at her; he was looking at the light. "He knows. Never said a word about it since the ford, when I burned our escape boat and took a blade meant for you. But he knows. And here's the thing, girl—the thing I can't get straight in my head." He finally ate the apple slice, chewing slowly. "He forgave me before I did the brave thing. Not after. *Before.* Years before. He just... left the door open and waited to see if I'd walk through it."

Pell smiled. "That's what the gods are doing now. Did you know that? Kestrel figured it out. They're not opening the gate wider. They're leaving it open *just enough*. Waiting to see who walks through."

"Huh." Doss considered this. "Gods learning patience from a charm-seller. World's gone strange."

"World's going *remembered*," Pell corrected. "Strange comes after."

* * *

On the fifth day, the light spoke back.

Not Lowbell—the other one, the deeper one, the one Pell privately called Hearthmother because its presence felt like standing near a banked fire. It had been silent through all the naming, receiving, weighing. On the fifth day, while Pell was mid-way through telling it about a shepherd named Col who had once sung to his sheep during a storm so they wouldn't panic, Hearthmother said:

"We had names too."

Pell stopped. Down in the light, shapes moved—vast, slow, the shadows of custodians turning toward their own past.

"We were named," Hearthmother said. "Before. By the ones who set us to guard. And then the ones who named us were forgotten, and we forgot ourselves, and hunger was the only thing left that made sense." A pause, long as a season. "Say ours back to us. If you can find them."

Pell closed her eyes and went down into the dark place, deeper than she'd ever gone, past the villages and the roads and the drowned bell-towers, down to the oldest stratum, where the land's first memories lay like river stones. And there—worn smooth, nearly invisible, but *there*—she found them.

She said the gods' names back to them.

It took a long time. Their true names were not sounds; they were seasons, duties, postures of care. One was the keeping of thresholds. One was the tending of boundaries between the living and whatever waits. One was simply *watching over*, the way a mother watches over a sleeping child—not to intervene, but so the child wakes safe.

When she finished, the light did not roar or blaze. It did something smaller and far more frightening.

It wept.

Rain began falling inside the spire, warm rain, salt-tasting, and every crack in the ancient stones ran with it, and Pell stood in the middle of it laughing and crying at once because she understood, suddenly and completely, what restoration was. Not a flood. Not a miracle. Just this: name by name, soul by soul, until the world could hold itself again.

* * *

Ossen came for her on the sixth day.

Later, everyone agreed it should not have surprised them. A man does not spend thirty years building a theology and abandon it because the sky starts crying. But by the sixth day his congregation was gone—released, unmade, gently returned to the light and thence to their lives, waking in distant towns with tears on their faces and no idea why—and Ossen stood alone at the top of the stair, robes grey with ash, and looked at Pell with an expression she would carry the rest of her life.

It was not hatred. It was *hunger*. The human kind. The kind that eats its owner.

"You've made them soft," he said. "Mercy is a leak in the roof. I watched this city drown in mercy, child. I watched judges take bribes and healers turn away the poor and priests grow fat. Law without mercy is the only honest thing left in the world, and you are teaching the law to *weep*."

"Yes," Pell said simply.

He blinked. "Yes?"

"Yes. I'm teaching them to weep. That's the whole lesson." She stepped forward, and the light parted around her like a curtain. "Ossen. Prelate. Teacher of inquisitors, maker of Sera Vane, burner of the innocent. I know your name too. Do you want to hear it?"

Something crossed his face—fear, hope, fury, all the weather of a lifetime—and Pell held up her hand, and in it she held his name the way she'd held Marta Ostrevva's, the way she'd held ten thousand names, complete and true: the orphan boy, the brilliant student, the young priest who had prayed so hard at his dying mother's bedside that something had answered, and the answer had cost a village, and he had spent fifty years building a doctrine to explain why that price was *just*.

"Your prayer was answered too," Pell said softly. "A long time ago. Your mother's pain ended. And a village paid for it. And you've been trying to make the arithmetic come out ever since." She let the name hang in the air between them, glowing. "It doesn't come out. It never comes out. That's not what mercy is *for*."

"Stop," Ossen whispered.

"Come and be counted," Pell said. "That's all any of this is. Come and be counted, and be forgiven, and start again. Even you. *Especially* you."

For one heartbeat—one long, suspended heartbeat—Pell believed he would.

Then Ossen smiled, and it was the old serene smile, and he said, "Law does not kneel," and threw himself into the light.

He fell the way a tower falls: slowly at first, then all at once, arms spread, robes streaming, mouth open on words that the light swallowed before they finished leaving him. Pell lunged forward—Sera shouted something, Kestrel was moving—but the gate was faster, and older, and wiser now. It did not devour him. It did not save him. It did the one thing Ossen had feared above all things, the thing his whole life had been built to avoid.

It *waited*.

The light held him at its edge, suspended, unanswered, while Hearthmother and Lowbell considered him with the full weight of everything Pell had taught them. And then, gently—so gently, with the exact tenderness of a mother watching over a sleeping child—the gods gave Ossen back every prayer he had ever refused to answer. Every dying hand he had pulled away from. Every mercy he had called weakness. All of it, all at once, in full, forever.

His scream lasted a long time. Or maybe no time at all. Time pooled strangely in the spire.

When it was done, there was no body. There was a fine grey ash drifting down through the warm rain, and Pell caught a little of it in her palm, and she did not curse it and she did not bless it. She just held it a moment, the way you hold anything that used to be a person.

"He wouldn't be counted," she said to the light.

*No,* the light agreed, sad as autumn. *Some will not. Count them anyway.*

"I will," Pell promised. "I'll count everybody."

* * *

After that, the work went fast, the way work goes fast once the thing you feared turns out to be survivable.

Pell named the drowned villages and they rose, wet and blinking, from the meadows. She named the unmapped roads and they unrolled across the hills like ribbons finding their spools. She named the capital street by street, and heard—faintly, gloriously—from far away, the sound of a hundred thousand people remembering their children's faces all at once, a sound like the biggest gasp in the world.

She named Kestrel's childhood street, and watched him go very still, and then walk to the edge of the light and stand there a long time, and when he turned around his eyes were red and he was smiling and he said, "Her name was Maren. My mother's name was Maren. She smelled like cedar and burnt sugar." And Doss put a hand on his shoulder and said nothing at all, which was exactly right.

She named Sera's first parish, the one she'd burned early in her career, back when she still believed, and Sera received it kneeling, and rose different.

She named the small things too, because the small things mattered: lost dogs, buried wedding rings, a treehouse in a chestnut tree, a recipe for plum cordial that three generations of one family had thought gone forever. Small mercies, scaling up. That was the whole architecture of the world, it turned out. Small mercies, stacked carefully, holding everything.

On the ninth day, the gate began to close—not with a slam but with a sigh, the way a great book closes when the story is properly finished. The light drew inward, gentle, unhurried. There was time. There was time for last names and last looks.

Lowbell spoke one final time. *Keeper's daughter,* it said—wrong, but warmly wrong, and Pell didn't correct it, because perhaps it was right in a way maps couldn't show. *What will you do now, with all those names in you?*

Pell thought about it. She thought about a shop in Vharen with a loose floorboard and a kettle that whistled off-key. She thought about a smuggler learning to limp upstairs without cursing, and an ex-inquisitor learning to cook, badly, and a charm-seller who had spent seven years being small and was about to become, in the gentlest possible way, enormous.

"I'm going home," she said. "And I'm going to keep counting."

The light accepted this. The light, Pell thought, had gotten very good at accepting things.

But as the gate drew down to its last bright seam—as the old gods gathered themselves to sleep behind a door that would from now on be guarded by memory instead of iron—Hearthmother paused, and leaned close, and said one more thing. Quietly. Almost shyly.

*There is one name left,* it said. *One we cannot read. It sits at the bottom of everything, older than us, older than the forgetting. We cannot see it. But you—you who see everything—*

The light flickered.

*Look down, keeper's daughter. Look at what is holding up the world.*

Pell looked.

And far below the spire, below the roots of the land, below the oldest stratum of names, she saw it: a single dark shape, folded small, holding the whole weight of the remembered world on its back—and it was beginning, very slowly, to stir.
