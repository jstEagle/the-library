## 21. A Shop With No Lock

The sign took three tries.

Kestrel had carved it himself, which was foolish, because his hands were ward-hands, not woodcarver's hands, and the letters came out lopsided as a drunk walking home. The first attempt read *HALLORAN'S CHARMS* with a *C* that looked like it had been struck by lightning. The second he burned, in a small ceremonial fire that Doss watched from the doorway with open delight and said, "Seven years of hiding magic under floorboards and now you're out here committing arson over typography."

The third one hung now above the door of the little shop on Candlemaker's Row: **KESTREL HALLORAN — CHARMSELLER**. Beneath it, smaller, added at Pell's insistence and painted in her careful, solemn hand: *All charms honest. No charm dishonest.*

"It's redundant," Kestrel had told her.

"It's a promise," she'd said, in the tone of a nine-year-old who had personally taught starving gods the names of ten thousand forgotten souls and did not intend to be argued with about promises. So it stayed.

He unlocked the door on the first morning of autumn, then stood there a moment with his hand on the latch, doing nothing at all, just feeling the strangeness of it. A lock. He had kept a lock on this door for seven years—not to keep people out, but to keep the smell of the gate's workings in, the ozone-and-iron tang that would have brought an inquisitor down the street faster than a scream. The lock had been part of the disguise. The whole shop had been part of the disguise. The harmless luck-charms, the little knotted cords and river-stones he sold to sailors and grandmothers, all of it theater, a screen behind which he crept down each night to feed the wards beneath the floor.

Now the wards were gone. Now the gate—*the* gate, the door between the world and its forgotten keepers—stood open in the way that mattered, which is to say it stood open in Pell's memory and in the gods' slow-learning hearts, and nowhere else. Now the country remembered itself. Maps had redrawn themselves overnight; cartographers were weeping in their workshops with joy or terror, depending on temperament. And Kestrel Halloran could sell charms for actual luck instead of metaphorical luck, and no one would burn him for it.

He turned the key anyway, out of habit, and then laughed at himself, and left the key in the door all day where anyone could see it.

That was the other thing. The new thing. The thing that made old customers pause on the threshold and stare: there was no lock worth the name anymore, and everyone knew whose shop this was. The man who had opened the Gate. The Keeper, some called him, though he hated it. *Kestrel*, most people called him, when they called him anything, because Vharen had decided after approximately four days of national awe that awe was exhausting and what it really wanted was to know whether he could fix a charm against rheumatism.

He could. That was the joke of it, the quiet miracle of it: everything he'd ever sold as theater worked now. The knots held. The stones hummed faintly if you listened right. A city's worth of harmless intentions, soaked in seven years of proximity to a god-door, had come true all at once, like seeds waking after a long winter.

His first customer of the morning was a fishwife named Brava who had bought a sea-charm from him every month for six years without once believing in it.

"Halloran," she said, setting a basket of silver-bellied mackerel on his counter as payment, "my husband swears the knot you gave him keeps the nets from tangling."

"They've been tangling less?"

"They've been tangling *impossibly* less. There's a knot in that net that shouldn't be possible. Old Tomas from the harbor guild looked at it and went pale and asked who tied it, and my husband said a charnseller on Candlemaker's Row, and now there are eleven men outside your door."

There were not eleven men outside his door. There were fourteen.

* * *

Doss moved in upstairs on a Tuesday, without announcing it, which was how Doss did everything.

Kestrel came up the back stair at dusk carrying a loaf and a pot of stew and found the spare room—the one that had stored dried herbs and, briefly, a smuggled crate of Vharen's finest illegal prayer-candles—occupied. A bedroll. A sea chest with a broken latch. A pair of boots by the window, one of them stuffed with straw where the leather had given out around the wound-scar. Doss himself sat on the windowsill with his bad leg stretched out, watching the street, looking for all the world like a cat that had lived somewhere forever and resented being noticed.

"You have a house," Kestrel said. "You have *three* houses. You told me so yourself, and I believed you, which I now realize was naive."

"Those houses are for business." Doss accepted the bread with the gravity of a man receiving a knighthood. "This is for living." He tore off a hunk, chewed, and gestured with it at the room, the stair, the general establishment. "Besides. Someone has to keep an eye on you. You'll give away the inventory out of guilt by winter."

"I gave away two charms today."

"See? Winter. You'll be destitute by midwinter and I'll have to resume smuggling just to feed us, and my leg can't take the rigging anymore, thanks very much for asking, it aches when it rains and it rained in the Ford and it rains in my dreams, if you want the full accounting."

Kestrel leaned in the doorway and let him talk. This was another new thing: Doss Marrow talked now. Seven years of deflection and charming lies and one catastrophic betrayal, and since the Ford—since the moment he'd burned his own escape boat and taken a blade meant for Pell—he talked like a man paying off a debt one confession at a time. The leg would heal crooked. The talking, Kestrel suspected, was the straightening.

"The physician says the scar will ache every year on the anniversary," Doss said, quieter now, turning the bread in his fingers. "She said it like it was a bad thing."

"What did you say?"

"I said good. I want to remember the day I stopped being the worst version of myself. It seems useful to mark it." He glanced up, quick and wary, the old look, the look that always preceded either a joke or a wound. "You could throw me out, you know. Everyone would understand. I sold you to the Order once. I'd have sold you twice, if the price had been right."

"The price wasn't right," Kestrel said. "I know. I've known for a while. You never wanted money, Doss. You wanted them to stop looking at you. And then Pell looked at you like you hung the moon, and suddenly there was something in the world worth being seen by." He shrugged. "People change houses. They don't usually change that fast. But you did."

Doss looked at him for a long moment. Then he said, thickly, "This stew smells like a crime," and that was the end of the sentiment for the evening, and that was fine too.

* * *

Pell called it home on a Thursday, casually, catastrophically, in the middle of dinner.

They ate at the big table downstairs among the charm-racks, because the upstairs table was too small for three and because Pell liked eating surrounded by the stock—"they're all listening," she'd explained once, "it's rude to make them wait in the dark"—and on this particular Thursday she was halfway through her second bowl of stew when she said, "Mara at school asked where I live, and I said here, and she asked if this is my home, and I said yes," and then kept eating as though she hadn't just reached into Kestrel's chest and rearranged something.

He set down his spoon.

Sera, across the table, did not set down hers, but her eyes came up over the rim of her cup, and there was a whole conversation in them, the kind two former enemies learn to have wordlessly: *did you hear that*, and *of course I heard that*, and *do something about your face before she notices*.

"That's good," Kestrel managed. "That this is your home."

"Well, obviously." Pell frowned at him, butter on her chin, exasperation radiating off her like heat off a forge. "Where else would I live? The Order's dormitory?" She made a face worthy of a much older child. "Sera says I'm technically still a ward of the Order until the reformation finishes, but Sera also says paperwork is just prayers written badly, so."

"Did she say that before or after she became the person rewriting the Order's entire legal code?"

"After. She says it a lot now. She says lots of things now." Pell speared a turnip. "She laughs sometimes. Did you know that? Before, I thought maybe she physically couldn't. Like a condition."

From the corner where Sera sat with her accounts—she kept the shop's books now, ferociously, having discovered that retail fraud offended her more than heresy ever had—came the sound of a page being turned slightly too hard.

"She has conditions," Kestrel agreed solemnly. "We're very fond of her conditions."

"I heard that," said Sera, not looking up.

"You were meant to," said Pell and Kestrel together, and Pell giggled, and the sound filled the shop the way lamplight fills a room, into all the corners, under all the shelves, into places that hadn't been bright in seven years.

Later, when Pell had gone up to the room that was hers now—she'd claimed it the first week, the little one facing east, "so I can see the spire-road," she said, though the spire itself was visible to almost no one anymore, folded back into mist and mercy—and when Sera had gone home to her rooms near the reconstituted Chapterhouse, Kestrel stood alone in the shop and did the thing he did every night now.

He walked the floor. Not checking wards—there were none to check—but checking the *quiet*. Pressing his palm flat to the boards where the gate had been, feeling for any tremor, any hunger, any wrongness in the answered things of the world. And every night, the same result: nothing but wood, and beneath the wood, earth, and beneath the earth, the deep patient presence of gods learning, slowly, soul by soul, what mercy was. Some nights he thought he felt them noticing him back. Not hungry. Curious. Like enormous cats.

"Goodnight," he told the floor, because it seemed polite, and because Pell had started the habit and habits, in this shop, had a way of becoming true.

* * *

Autumn deepened. Business boomed.

It turned out that a country remembering itself all at once needed an enormous number of charms. Charms for remembering lost names—those sold fastest, grief-stricken families lining up at dawn. Charms for forgetting, too, for those who'd been made to remember things the gods' restoration had dragged back into the light, and those Kestrel made reluctantly and priced high and always, always asked about first. Charms against nightmares, which the whole capital seemed to be having in sympathy. Charms for safe travel on roads that had unspooled back into existence and were, several travelers reported, *smug about it*.

Sera ran the counter on her days off from the Chapterhouse, terrifying customers into honesty with such efficiency that revenue rose forty percent. Doss ran the deliveries, limping cheerfully through Vharen with his delivery satchel, having appointed himself the shop's ambassador to every dock, den, and dubious cellar in the city, on the principle—which he announced at dinner, loudly—that "a reformed criminal knows where the sinners live, and sinners buy more charms than saints, it's practically doctrine."

And Pell ran the part no one else could run. On market days she sat by the window with a slate, taking requests, and when someone came in with a need too tangled for knotwork—a name half-lost, a memory with a hole in it, a grandmother who woke calling for a village that had only just come back—Pell would listen, and think, and say, "I'll ask," and later the answer would arrive, gentle and exact, and Kestrel would watch it happen and feel the same vertigo he'd felt standing before the open gate: *this child is a bridge between worlds, and she is eating jam toast.*

One evening in late autumn, a woman came in whom Kestrel knew. Maren Ostrey, a widow from the Row, who had bought a mourning-charm from him three years ago, back when his charms were lies and her grief was real. Her son had died at sea. Or—she'd thought so. The ship had been one of the erased ones, lost in waters that had themselves been forgotten, and when the land re-membered itself, so had the ship, and so had the crew, alive, stranded on a coast that had also just remembered existing, and her son had sent a letter that arrived last week, and she stood in the shop holding it and shaking.

"He wrote that he dreamed of me," she said. "Every night, these three years, he dreamed of me telling him I'd light a candle. And I did light candles, Master Halloran. I bought your charm and I lit candles and I didn't believe a bit of it, and he *heard*."

Kestrel looked at the little knotted cord she'd set on the counter, the one he'd sold her for two coppers and a kind lie. It glowed now, faintly, the way all his old stock did. Seven years of harmless intentions, parked next to a god-door.

"Maybe the lie was closer to the truth than either of us knew," he said.

She paid for a thanksgiving charm—one of the new ones, honestly made—and left lighter than she'd come, and Kestrel sat down on his stool and put his head in his hands and laughed until Sera came out of the back room to see what was wrong with him.

"Nothing," he said. "Everything. She got her son back because I was a fraud in exactly the right place."

"That's not what happened," Sera said, sitting on the stool beside him, in the way she'd learned to sit like a colleague instead of an inquisitor, knees apart, hands loose. "What happened is you kept a door for seven years and never once used it to run. The gods weren't answering her candles, Kestrel. They were answering *you*. Everything you did near that gate got... soaked in. You were the prayer. The rest was just rope."

He stared at her. "Has anyone told you that you've gotten disturbingly wise since leaving the Order?"

"Frequently. I find it suspicious and I'm investigating it." But she smiled when she said it, one of the new smiles, the ones that still surprised her own face, and Kestrel thought—as he thought most evenings now, sweeping up, banking the fire, hanging the *closed* sign on a door that no longer truly closed—that this was what he'd been guarding all along, underneath the gate and the wards and the lonely vigil. Not a door. A possibility. That the world could hold a place like this: a shop with no lock, a liar reformed, an inquisitor laughing, a god-touched child asleep upstairs with jam on her chin and ten thousand souls' worth of names tucked safely behind her ribs.

* * *

On the first frost-morning of winter, Kestrel opened the shop and found three things waiting.

The first was a queue of customers already forming in the cold, breath steaming, including—to his astonishment—a delegation of cartographers, who wished to commission a charm "for the maps, Master Halloran, so they stay remembered," and who had pooled their wages to afford it.

The second was a note from Sera, pinned to the door: *Chapterhouse vote passed. Magic instruction to be legalized by spring. They want you to teach the first class. I told them you'd say yes. Don't make a liar of an inquisitor.*

The third was Pell, already awake, sitting cross-legged on the counter in her nightclothes, which was against several rules, watching the eastern sky with an expression he'd learned to read the way sailors read weather.

"What is it, sprout?"

"There's a light on the spire-road," she said. "Far off. Just for a minute." She turned to him, and her eyes were wide, and she was smiling, but underneath the smile was the old loneliness, the witness's loneliness, the weight of being the only one who saw. "Somebody's coming, Kestrel. Somebody the gods marked. And they're going to knock on our door, and they're going to need..." She trailed off, frowning, reaching for words that hadn't been taught to her yet. "They're going to need a lamp left burning."

Kestrel looked past her, east, where the frost-fog lay over the hills and the road nobody else could quite see wound away toward a spire that was and wasn't there.

Then he went inside, and lit the lamp in the front window, and turned the sign to *open*—and left the door unlocked, the way it always was now, the way it would stay.

"Put the kettle on," he said. "Whoever they are, they'll be cold."
