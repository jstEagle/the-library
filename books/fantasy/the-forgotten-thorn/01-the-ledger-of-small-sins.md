## 1. The Ledger of Small Sins

The wind came down off Thornfell an hour before midnight, as it always did, carrying the smell of pine resin and old snow and something older still beneath both—stone, maybe, or the memory of stone. Ada Almeida stood in the doorway of the gatehouse with his ledger under his arm and watched the light die along the pass, and he thought, as he thought every night at this hour, that the mountains were the only things in the world that had never once lied to him.

Hollowgate was not much to look at, if you were the kind of person who judged a thing by its size. A cleft between two shoulders of the Thornfell range, perhaps forty paces wide at the narrowest point, walled on either side by masonry so old that no living kingdom claimed to have built it. The gate itself—two leaves of black iron banded with bronze, tall as three men—had not been opened in Ada's lifetime except by him, and he opened it every night, because that was the arrangement. The arrangement being: the crown of Vessar believed the pass sealed. The smugglers of the low country knew better. And Ada Almeida, keeper of Hollowgate for twenty years, kept his ledgers clean and his mouth shut and his head precisely low enough that neither side ever had reason to look at him twice.

He went back inside, lit the lamp over his desk, and opened the ledger to tonight's page.

The book itself was a work of quiet artistry, though no one would ever praise it. Two columns, ruled in his own careful hand. The left column recorded what the crown's auditors would see if they ever came—which they hadn't in eleven years, but Ada was a man who prepared for weather that might not come for a decade. *No traffic. Pass closed. Wind damage to north parapet, repaired.* The right column was smaller, the entries cramped, written in a shorthand of his own devising that would take a cryptographer a month to unpick.

He dipped his pen.

*Entry one.* The Tallow brothers, coming up from Corvale with six barrels of unmarked brandy hidden under honest salt-fish. Toll paid: two crowns and the fish, which Ada would salt and eat through the winter. He had waved them through at dusk, checked their cart's false bottom with the bored efficiency of long practice, and told them—if anyone asked—that they'd been turned away like everyone else. They would say the same. Everyone always said the same. That was the beauty of Hollowgate: it ran not on trust but on mutual blackmail, which was far more durable.

*Entry two.* A letter-carrier for the free cities' merchant houses, going south with dispatches sewn into her coat lining. Toll paid: nothing. She carried letters for Ada too, sometimes, when he could afford the postage—a book from Corvale twice a year, mostly, histories and geographies, because a man who lived alone above a mountain pass needed somewhere else to be, even if only on paper.

*Entry three* was where the night got complicated.

Ada set down his pen and looked at the lamp flame for a while.

A family. That was how he'd entered it in his head all evening, though the ledger demanded more precision than sympathy allowed. A man, a woman, two children—the boy perhaps eight, the girl no older than five—and an grandmother who walked with a stick and had spent the whole exchange staring at Ada with eyes like river stones, unblinking, as though she were trying to decide whether he was a man or merely a door that had learned to talk.

They had come up the south road at moonrise, footsore, the children half-asleep and slung across their parents' backs like sacks of grain. Fleeing something. Ada hadn't asked what; asking invited answers, and answers invited names, and names were the one currency he refused to hold. But he knew the shape of it. He'd known it for twenty years. War taxes, conscription quotas, a landlord who wanted more than rent, a neighbor who'd informed on someone—in the low country under King Vessar, the reasons came in only a few flavors, and all of them tasted the same.

The rule was simple, and Ada had built his whole life on the back of it: goods moved for coin. People moved for coin too—but coin wasn't enough for people. People required risk, and risk required that Ada care about the outcome, and caring was the first crack in any wall. So: goods, yes. People, almost never. Three times in twenty years he'd made exceptions, and each time he'd lain awake afterward listening to the wind and waiting for hoofbeats that never came, and each time he'd sworn never again, and each time the swearing had gotten easier, which was its own kind of shame.

"Please," the father had said. Not dramatically. Quietly, the way men beg when they've already begged everywhere else and have learned to expect the answer. "We have a little money."

"I know you do," Ada had said. "It isn't enough."

"It's everything we have."

"Yes," Ada had said, and hated how steady his voice came out. "That's why I'm giving it back."

He had turned them away at the threshold of the pass, in the blue moonlight, with the great iron gate standing shut behind him like a held breath. The woman had not said anything at all. She had just looked at him—looked *through* him, the way people look at walls—and gathered her daughter closer, and the family had gone back down the south road the way they'd come, small and slow against the dark, until the mountain folded them out of sight.

The grandmother had been last. She had stopped at arm's length from Ada, close enough that he could smell woodsmoke in her shawl, and she had studied his face for a long moment with those river-stone eyes.

"You count everything," she had said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't quite an accusation. It had the tone of someone reading a sign.

"That's my job," Ada had said.

"And do you count yourself among the things?"

He had not answered that. He had watched her go, leaning on her stick, and the question had gone with her, or part of it had, and now here he sat with his lamp and his pen and his beautiful, terrible ledger, and the right-hand column waited for entry three, and Ada found—for the first time in years, and it frightened him more than any rider ever had—that he did not know what to write.

In the end he wrote: *Family of four and one elder. Turned away. No toll taken.*

Then, after a moment, because the ledger was the only place in the world where Ada Almeida told the truth, he added in the smallest hand he could manage:

*Should have let them through.*

* * *

He locked the ledger in the iron box beneath the floorboards, banked the fire, and did what he did every night after the last entry: he walked the gate.

It was a ritual by now, worn smooth as a stair-stone. Lamp in hand, keys at his belt—three keys, though only one mattered, the long black one that fit the ward-lock at the gate's heart—he made the circuit of the pass. North wall first, checking the mortar against frost-heave. Then the parapet, then the watch-niche where a soldier of the crown was supposed to stand and never did, because the crown had decided a century ago that Hollowgate needed no garrison. *The pass is sealed,* the old decrees said. *The gate cannot open. Let the mountain keep its own door.*

The mountain, Ada thought, keeping its own door very well indeed, thank you, with considerable help from a bribed keeper and a lock that opened for exactly one key.

At the southern postern he paused, as he always did, and pressed his palm flat against the stone.

This was the part of the ritual he'd never told anyone, the part he barely admitted to himself: the gate-warding. When Ada had come to Hollowgate at nineteen—young, alone, glad of a posting where no one would ever look at him—he'd been given the black key by the previous keeper, a dried-out old man named Hask who'd handed it over with the words *"Don't lose it, don't sell it, don't wonder about it,"* and died the following spring with suspicious convenience. And in twenty years of turning that key every night, Ada had learned things about it that Hask had never mentioned. That the lock drank warmth from his hand. That the gate hummed, very faintly, when he touched it—a note below hearing, felt in the teeth. That he did not age the way other men aged. His hair had gone grey at the temples early and then simply stopped; his hands were a working man's hands, scarred and strong, but they were the same hands he'd had at thirty, and he was fifty-one years old, and there were nights when he caught his reflection in the rain barrel and felt the cold slide down his spine that had nothing to do with the wind.

Hask had said *don't wonder about it.* Ada had obeyed for twenty years. Wondering was a door, and doors, in his experience, only ever opened onto trouble.

But some nights—like tonight, like this night, with a grandmother's question still lodged under his ribs like a splinter—he stood with his palm on the stone and wondered anyway. What the warding was. Who had built it. Why the gate seemed, on certain evenings, when the wind came down off the high snows, to be less like a machine and more like a sleeping animal, breathing slow, aware of him in some dim and patient way.

Tonight the stone was warm under his hand. Warmer than the air, warmer than it should have been, and Ada pulled his fingers back and told himself it was residual heat from the day, though the day had ended hours ago and the rock had been ice-cold at supper.

"Old gate," he said aloud, because talking to it was another part of the ritual, the embarrassing part, the part that made him feel less like a keeper and more like a man who talked to doors. "Nobody home. Go back to sleep."

The gate did not answer. Gates didn't. But the hum under his palm lingered a heartbeat longer than usual before fading, and Ada stood in the dark with his lamp and felt, distinctly and without evidence, *watched*—not by anything hostile, which would at least have been familiar, but by something with the quality of attention that a dog gives a sleeping master. Patient. Waiting for him to wake up to something.

"Nonsense," Ada said, and went inside, and bolted the door, and lay down without undressing, as was his habit, because a keeper of Hollowgate slept light or not at all.

* * *

He woke past midnight to knocking.

Not at the gatehouse door—at the gate itself. Iron knuckles on iron, unhurried, spaced evenly, the knock of someone who knows they will be heard and is content to wait. Ada sat up in the dark with his heart going hard and listened. Smugglers didn't knock. Smugglers whistled the three-note signal from the treeline and waited for the postern. Soldiers knocked, but soldiers came in daylight, with banners, and there had been no soldiers at Hollowgate in eleven years.

The knock came again. Same rhythm. Patient as tide.

Ada took the lamp and the black key and went out into the cold, and the cold was wrong—not bitter, not sharp, but soft, a damp mildness utterly impossible at this altitude in this season, and when he breathed it he smelled, faintly, absurdly, of summer meadows, of hay cut and drying, of a garden he could not place and yet recognized the way you recognize a face in a dream.

Frost covered the gate.

Not the rime of mountain cold—this was lacework, deliberate, fern-patterned, blooming across the black iron in spirals and whorls that no weather had ever made, silver-white and fine as spun sugar, and it was growing as Ada watched, spreading outward from the center of the left-hand leaf in slow fractal blooms, and at the heart of each bloom, tiny and perfect, was a shape like an eye, or a keyhole, or a flower, depending on how he tilted his head.

The knocking had stopped. Of course it had. There was no one there. There had never been anyone there; the south road lay empty under the stars for as far as the lamplight reached, and beyond the lamplight, empty still.

Ada stood before his gate with the black key in his fist and did not run, because running from your own gate was the act of a man who had already lost it, and whatever else Ada Almeida was—coward, collaborator, keeper of small sins—he had not lost Hollowgate in twenty years and did not mean to start tonight.

"Who's there?" he said. His voice came out steadier than he deserved.

The frost finished blooming. The whole gate now wore its silver lace, and in the silence Ada heard—or felt, or dreamed, the distinction had become academic—a sound like wind moving through a very deep canyon, far below the world, and within that sound, shaped by it the way a shape is shaped by water, a voice. It did not speak so much as it *arrived*, fully formed, in the space behind his ears:

*"Almë."*

Ada's breath stopped.

Not *Almeida*. Not the name on the crown's rolls, the name he'd worn like a coat for twenty years, the safe dull name of a safe dull man. *Almë.* The name his mother had called him across a kitchen, once, in another life, in a village that no longer appeared on maps—a name he had not heard spoken aloud in forty years, a name he had buried so deep he'd nearly convinced himself he'd invented it, and here it was, spoken by the wind through stone, gentle as a hand on a child's head.

"No," Ada said. His voice cracked on it. "No. You're the wind. You're the mountain settling. That's all this is."

The frost began, slowly, to melt—not dripping, but sinking into the iron, absorbed, drunk, and where it sank the metal gleamed clean and bright as the day it was forged. The warmth in the air faded. The smell of hay and summer thinned and was gone, and the ordinary cold of Thornfell came back down the pass like a debt collector, and Ada stood alone at his gate with a dead name ringing in his skull and his hands shaking so badly he had to grip the lamppost to keep the light steady.

He told himself, walking back to the gatehouse, that he would write it off. Fatigue. A dream walked upright. Twenty years of solitude finally collecting interest. He told himself he would sleep, and wake tomorrow, and log no traffic, and be exactly the invisible man he had spent two decades building, plank by plank, sin by sin.

He wrote nothing in the ledger. For the first time in twenty years, Ada Almeida could not decide which column the truth belonged in.

And in the morning, when he stepped outside with his tea, he found the frost had returned—only a single patch this time, small as a spread hand, on the gatepost beside the road where travelers paused to rest. And in the frost, drawn in lines fine as spider silk, unmistakable, patient, was a single word:

*TOMORROW.*
