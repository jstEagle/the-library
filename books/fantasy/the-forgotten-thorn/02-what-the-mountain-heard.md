## 2. What the Mountain Heard

The frost came in the wrong month.

Ada Almeida noticed it the way he noticed everything at Hollowgate—sideways, without seeming to notice at all, the way a man notices a change in wind while pretending to study his ledger. It was late in the Year's Waning, three weeks past the last honest frost and well into the season when the pass should have smelled of thaw and wet stone and the first stubborn green things pushing up between the cobbles. Instead, when he stepped out of the gatehouse at dusk to light the passage lamps, his breath hung white in front of him, and the flagstones wore a skin of ice so thin and perfect it looked painted on.

He stood very still for a moment, lamp unlit in his hand, and listened.

Nothing. Wind through the throat of the pass, the groan of the great gate's iron hinges as it settled against its frame, the distant complaint of a mule in the waystation stable. The ordinary music of twenty years.

"Old stone lies about the weather," he told himself quietly, and lit the lamps, and went back inside to finish the day's entries.

That was the trouble with Hollowgate, he thought later. It taught you to explain everything. Twenty years of explaining was how a man survived there—every odd sound was settling rock, every flicker of light was swamp gas off the low meadows, every stranger's too-sharp question was simple curiosity. You explained, you logged, you kept your head down, and the years slid over you like water over a stone until the stone forgot it had ever been anything but smooth.

He was halfway through reconciling the toll receipts when the knock came.

Not the heavy double-rap of the watch, not the merchant's impatient triple beat. A soft knocking, hesitant, like someone who had been told they weren't welcome and had come anyway. Ada set down his pen, checked that the strongbox was latched out of habit, and opened the gatehouse door onto the blue dark.

An old woman stood on the threshold stones, wrapped in a shawl the color of wet ash. She was small, bent at the shoulders like a drawn bow, with a face full of deep seams and two eyes that were still startlingly clear, the pale gray of river ice. Behind her, a handcart loaded with what looked like bundles of dried herbs sagged against the gatepost—the eastern one, the old one, the one carved with spirals that predated the road, the kingdom, possibly the language.

"The pass is closed after dusk," Ada said. Standard words, standard tone, mild as milk. "Waystation's got bunks if you've coin. If not, I can point you to the shrine alcove—it's dry."

"I'm not crossing," the woman said. Her voice was thin but steady. "I know better than to ask that. I only wanted—" She stopped. Looked at him for a long moment with those ice-colored eyes, and something in the look made Ada's skin prickle, though he couldn't have said why. "You've kept this gate a long time."

"Long enough." He didn't confirm numbers. Numbers invited questions. Questions invited records, and records traveled.

"My mother crossed here once," the woman said. "Before the war of the succession. She said the keeper then was a hard man, but fair. She left an offering at the post, for safe passage. They used to do that, in those days. Leave offerings." Her gaze drifted to the gatepost, to the worn spirals. "Nobody leaves offerings anymore. Nobody remembers what the posts are *for*."

"They're for holding up the arch," Ada said flatly. "Superstition rots timber faster than weather. If you've no business crossing, mistress, the alcove is—"

"I have business," she said. "Just not with you."

And before he could decide whether to be offended or simply tired, she shuffled past him—he let her, because stopping her would mean touching her, and touching meant reports, and reports meant attention—down to the eastern gatepost. She knelt on the frosted stone without a wince, which told Ada her knees were either younger than the rest of her or long past caring. From her shawl she drew a small clay cup, poured into it a measure of something from a flask—honey mead, by the smell that reached him even across the courtyard, sweet and old—and set it at the base of the post.

Then she began to pray.

Ada had heard prayers at his gate before. Hundreds of them, over the years. Refugees whispering to whatever gods they'd carried out of burning towns, merchants muttering to saints of roads and profit, soldiers asking any listening thing to let them go home whole. He'd learned to tune them out the way you tune out rain. Prayer at Hollowgate was weather.

But this was different, and he knew it was different within three words, because the woman wasn't asking. She was *naming*. Her voice dropped into a cadence so old it made the hair on his arms lift—a rising-falling rhythm, call and answer, the shape of a liturgy—and the names she spoke were names Ada hadn't heard since he was a boy kneeling beside his sister in a kitchen while their grandmother burned herbs and murmured the same syllables under her breath.

*Thennmar who sleeps in stone. Vessia of the nine waters. The Thorn-Crowned, the Root-Fast, the Ones Who Wait.*

Dead gods. Forgotten gods. Gods the new faith had buried so thoroughly that saying their names aloud in most of the free cities would get a man fined, and in Vessar's territories, worse. Ada hadn't spoken them himself in forty years. He found now that his lips were moving silently, shaping the responses without his permission, the answers rising out of him like water out of a cut root.

*Who wait beneath. Who wait within. Who hold the door from underneath.*

The temperature fell.

It wasn't gradual. There was no wind to carry it, no cloud to explain it. One breath the night was merely cold; the next, cold poured out of the ground itself, out of the mountain's bones, rolling across the courtyard in a silent wave that turned the puddles by the trough to glass and silvered every surface. Ada's lamp guttered and went out. The woman went on praying, unhurried, as though this happened every day, as though perhaps for her it did.

And the frost bloomed.

That was the only word for it. Ice spread from the base of the gatepost in ferning patterns—not the random lace of ordinary frost but deliberate, structured, *meaningful*, spirals chasing themselves outward across the flagstones in patterns that matched the carvings on the post exactly, matched them the way an echo matches a shout. The spirals climbed the post itself, filling the worn grooves of the ancient carving with white fire, and the old symbols—which Ada had looked at every day for twenty years without seeing, the way you stop seeing your own hands—stood out suddenly, luminous, legible.

*Who hold the door from underneath,* the prayer finished. *Remember us who remember You.*

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on the eardrums.

Then the voice came, and Ada stopped being a gatekeeper and became, for one suspended moment, a boy again—seven years old, standing in a doorway, watching something he was never supposed to see.

It was not loud. That was the worst of it. It came from everywhere and nowhere—from the post, from the ground, from the mountain entire—a sound like wind moving through a cavern system miles deep, like stone slowly speaking, and it said:

***"Almë."***

His childhood name. Not Ada, the name he'd built for himself out of anonymity and clean ledgers. *Almë.* The name his grandmother had called him across the kitchen, the name Lira had chanted when they played at the stream, the name that had gone into the ground with everything else of his childhood, buried so deep he sometimes doubted he'd ever owned it.

Nobody living knew that name. Nobody living *could* know it.

The old woman rose stiffly, collected her empty cup, and turned to face him. In the light of the frost-glow her eyes were almost colorless.

"There," she said softly. "It remembered. First time in seventy years anyone's bothered to ask it to." She studied him, head tilted. "It knows you, gatekeeper."

"It's mistaken," Ada heard himself say. His voice came out level. He was distantly proud of that. "Old gods get confused. Like old men."

"Mm." She gathered her cart handles. "My grandmother served at a shrine below the southern face, before the sealing. She taught me the old forms. Said someday the mountain would wake hungry, and someone would need to know how to feed it." She paused at the edge of the courtyard, a small gray shape against the enormous black mass of Thornfell rising behind the gate. "It isn't hungry tonight. Tonight it's *homesick*. There's a difference. Homesick is worse."

She walked away down the south road, cart wheels creaking, and the dark took her, and Ada did not watch her go because he was looking at the gatepost.

The frost was already retreating, spirals fading, the carvings sinking back into their twenty years of invisibility. But at the base of the post, where the cup had sat, a single pattern remained: a small ring of ice, perfect and unmelting, though the night around it was warming by the moment. And inside the ring, written in characters of frost finer than any pen stroke, two words that were already beginning to weep into water:

*ALMË WAITS.*

"No," Ada said aloud, to the post, to the mountain, to whatever vast sleeping thing had just brushed its attention across him like a finger across a map. "No, you've got the wrong man. There's nothing waiting. There's nothing here at all."

The mountain, naturally, did not answer. Mountains never answered. That was the entire arrangement, the foundation stone of his whole careful life: the world above was deaf and blind and busy, and the world below was sealed, and Ada Almeida stood in the crack between them collecting coins and keeping his name out of every ledger but his own.

He picked up his dead lamp. He walked back to the gatehouse. He shut the door, barred it, and sat down at his desk, and picked up his pen, and looked at the open ledger, and discovered that his hands were shaking so badly he couldn't form a letter.

He set the pen down. Pressed his palms flat against the desk until the trembling dulled to a quiver. Breathed the way his grandmother had taught him—slow in, slower out—while the lamp oil smell and the ink smell and the cold radiating off his own clothes all insisted that this was real, that it had happened, that something under the mountain knew a name he'd spent fifty years killing.

*Being seen* was the one thing. The one unforgivable thing. Everything else at Hollowgate could be managed—bribes managed, smugglers managed, even guilt managed, folded small and filed under *later*. But to be seen was to be counted, and to be counted was to be spent. He'd learned that young. He'd built a fortress out of being nobody, and tonight something had walked straight through the walls without troubling to open the gate, and had called him by the name of the boy who'd once believed he mattered.

He thought, absurdly, of Lira. Of her gap-toothed grin, her habit of talking to stones because she was sure they listened. *They do listen,* their grandmother always said. *That's the problem.*

Lira, who had gone into the ground seventy years ago, if the arithmetic he refused to do was done honestly. Lira, whose name was the hole in every family story Ada allowed himself to remember.

He cleaned the lamp, relit it, and forced himself through the rest of the receipts, line by line, because routine was rope and rope was how you climbed out of holes. When the last entry was made he banked the fire, checked the bars twice, and lay down in his narrow bed staring at the ceiling beams.

Somewhere past midnight, half-dreaming, he heard it again. Faint. Patient. Wind through stone, far below, shaping two syllables with terrible gentleness.

*Almë.*

He pressed his face into the pillow and did not answer.

But in the morning, when he went out to sweep the courtyard, he found the ring of frost at the gatepost still unmelting in the full sun, and inside it, where the words had wept away, new letters had formed overnight, small and neat and unmistakable:

*SHE REMEMBERS THE GATE.*

Ada stood in the bright cold morning with his broom in his hands, invisible no longer, and understood with sick certainty that the life he had built was already over—that somewhere below, something had begun counting toward him, and that counting, once started, did not stop.

Three days later, a rider in royal colors came up the south road, carrying surveyor's papers and the king's seal, asking questions about passes and doors.

But that, as Ada would write grimly in a ledger no one would ever read, is getting ahead of the entry.
