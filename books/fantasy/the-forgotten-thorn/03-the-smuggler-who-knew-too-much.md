## 3. The Smuggler Who Knew Too Much

Sefa Vane had three rules, and she'd broken all of them by noon.

Rule one: never work the same road twice in a season. Rule two: never drink with a man who owed you money. Rule three—and this was the one that mattered, the one her mother had carved into her before she could read—*never be interesting.*

She was sitting in the back room of the Drowned Bell in Corvale, drinking with a man who owed her forty crowns and had just become very, very interesting.

"Say it again," she said. "Slowly."

Tomas Quill was a surveyor's assistant, which was a respectable way of saying he drew maps for men who owned things. He was also a coward, which made him useful, and a gossip, which made him dangerous. Right now both qualities were sweating out of him into his untouched beer.

"I shouldn't have told you anything," he said.

"You haven't told me anything yet. You've told me beer foam." She leaned across the table, close enough to smell the ink on his fingers. "Vessar's people are in the Thornfells. Charting passes. Asking questions. About *what*, Tomas?"

He glanced at the door. The back room of the Drowned Bell was empty except for them and a cat that belonged to no one, but he glanced anyway, the way guilty men do, as if the walls had ears they might report to.

"The sealed door," he whispered.

Sefa kept her face still. Inside, something cold turned over.

There were a hundred doors in the Thornfell mountains that smugglers called sealed—the collapsed mine at Greyhollow, the walled-up shrine at Cinders Pass, the cave old Marta swore ate her brother in '61. Every one of those stories was worth coin to the right listener. But there was only one door that mattered, only one that had a name nobody said above a whisper, and it sat behind the gate of Hollowgate, where a gray little man named Ada Almeida kept ledgers cleaner than any honest bookkeeper's and asked no questions that cost him extra.

Sefa had been through Hollowgate maybe sixty times. She'd paid her toll, logged her false names, watched Ada's eyes slide over her cargo without ever quite seeing it. He was the best kind of official: one who had decided long ago that not-knowing was a form of virtue. She'd always assumed the arrangement would last forever, because arrangements like theirs were built on mutual boredom, and boredom was the most durable material in the world.

Apparently not.

"What sealed door?" she said lightly. "There's dozens."

Quill gave her a look that said he knew exactly what she knew, which was worse than ignorance. "The one under the mountain. The one the old stories call the seventh lock." He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. "They're paying twenty crowns a head for information. Twenty! For *stories*. Old women's tales about gods buried under Thornfell, about a bargain struck before the kingdom had a name. Captain Dreven himself is running the survey. You know what that means?"

"It means someone important wants something," Sefa said, "and doesn't want his name on the wanting."

"Dreven doesn't take surveys. Dreven takes heads." Quill finally drank, a long nervous pull. "They've got crews on every pass from here to the Salt Reach. Measuring. Sketching. And every crew has a list of questions, and every list ends the same way: *has anyone seen the sealed door, and does anyone keep its key.*"

The cold thing in Sefa's chest turned again, harder.

*Does anyone keep its key.*

Ada Almeida kept a great iron ring of keys on his belt, and everyone assumed they opened gates and storehouses and the occasional stubborn lockbox. But Sefa had once, drunk on victory and cheap wine after the Kestrel Run, asked him what the smallest key opened—the little black one, worn smooth as a river stone, that he touched sometimes when he thought no one was looking. He'd gone still in a way she'd never seen before or since, and said, *Nothing you can afford,* and changed the subject so completely and so gracefully that she'd almost believed she hadn't asked.

She'd filed it away the way she filed everything: under *later*, in the drawer marked *never*.

Later had arrived.

* * *

She left Quill with his beer and his guilt and walked out into Corvale's afternoon, which smelled like fish, tar, and the particular desperation of a city pretending everything was fine.

It wasn't fine. Anyone with eyes could see it wasn't fine. The harbor was half-empty because Vessar's navy had started "inspecting" merchant ships, which meant taking them. The grain price had doubled since midsummer. In the square by the customs house, criers were reading the King's new edict—*all passes through the Thornfells to be registered and garrisoned for the security of the realm*—to a crowd that listened the way sheep listen to wolves: attentively, and without any belief that listening would help.

The free cities had spent three hundred years being free mostly by being beneath notice. Corvale didn't have an army; it had a militia that drilled twice a year and a treasury that existed mainly to bribe whoever threatened to invade. It had worked. It had worked beautifully, right up until a king came along who didn't want tribute, didn't want fealty, didn't want anything except *everything*, and had the soldiers to collect it.

Sefa cut down Fishgut Alley, took two turns she didn't need to take, and stopped in the shadow of a cooper's awning to think.

Here was what she knew, laid out in her head like contraband on a blanket:

One. Vessar's surveyors were charting every pass into the Thornfells, which meant he intended to move armies through them, which meant the free cities' last natural wall was about to stop being a wall.

Two. They were asking about a sealed door under the mountain, and offering real money for real answers, which meant the door mattered more than the passes. Armies were the excuse. The door was the errand.

Three. The only person alive who might know anything true about that door was a gray little gatekeeper who had spent twenty years making himself invisible, and whose smallest key he touched like a wound.

And four—if she was smart, and she was, and if she wanted to stay alive, and she did—she would forget all of this, take the Kestrel Run south, and be in Marshport by winter selling silk to people who didn't ask questions.

Sefa stood in the shadow and let herself want that, honestly and completely, the way you let yourself look at a full purse before giving it back.

Then she thought about Ada Almeida touching that little black key, and about the first time she'd come through Hollowgate, seventeen years old and alone, with a forged pass and stolen goods and terror sweating through her shirt, and how the gray man behind the desk had looked at her for one long moment—not at her papers, at *her*—and then stamped the pass and said, in a voice like dry paper, *"Mind the frost on the north steps. It lies."*

It had. She'd have broken her neck otherwise. She'd never known how he knew. She'd never asked. That was the deal: he saw too much and said nothing, and she noticed too much and said nothing, and between the two silences they'd built something that was almost trust, if trust could survive on almost.

King Vessar's men were going to come to Hollowgate with their lists and their questions and their twenty-crown smiles, and when polite failed they'd come with less polite things, and Ada Almeida—who had survived twenty years by being nothing to no one—was going to discover that invisibility only works while nobody's looking for you specifically.

Sefa sighed, loudly, at the injustice of caring about anyone.

* * *

Hollowgate at dusk was the prettiest ugly place in the world, if you had a smuggler's eye. The pass narrowed to a throat barely wide enough for two carts, walled in by cliffs the color of old bruises, and the gate itself was a slab of black iron set in stone so ancient the masons' marks had weathered to hieroglyphs. Torches burned in brackets shaped like thorns. Above the arch, worn nearly smooth, some forgotten hand had carved words in a script older than the kingdom. Sefa had once asked a scholar friend what it said. The friend had gone pale and refused to translate, which Sefa had found extremely funny and now, walking toward it, did not.

Ada was at his desk in the gatehouse, as always, a lamp turned low beside him, a ledger open, his pen moving in small precise strokes. He looked up when she came in, and his face did the thing it always did—nothing at all—but his eyes flicked past her shoulder to the empty road behind her, then back, and she saw him register that she'd come alone, at dusk, unannounced, with no cargo.

"Sefa." He set down his pen with care, the way other men holstered weapons. "You're off your road."

"Roads change." She dropped onto the stool across the desk, uninvited, and helped herself to the dried apples in the chipped bowl he kept for officials he wanted to bore. "I hear the King's taken up cartography."

Something happened to the lamplight. Or to his face. It was very small either way.

"Kings do many things," Ada said.

"These kings send surveyors. Crews on every pass into Thornfell. Twenty crowns a head for stories about old doors." She chewed an apple slowly, watching him. "And one question on every list, asked quiet, at the end, like it hardly matters. *Does anyone keep the key to the sealed door.*"

For a long moment the only sound was the torches outside, popping in the wind that funneled down the pass. Ada's hands were folded on the ledger, and they were perfectly steady, and Sefa, who had spent her life reading hands the way priests read scripture, understood that the steadiness was the tell. Nobody's hands are naturally that calm.

"Where did you hear this?" he asked.

"Tomas Quill. Surveyor's assistant, drinks at the Drowned Bell, owes me forty crowns, and is now so frightened he'll pay me the forty to never speak to him again." She leaned forward. "Dreven's running it personally. You know the name?"

"I know the name." Dreven. The King's left hand. The man who had dissolved the Council of Reth by having the councilmen arrested mid-session, politely, with tea served. "How long?"

"Crews have been in the foothills a month. The lists went out two weeks ago. If I heard in Corvale, then—" she spread her hands— "the mountain already knows, Ada. The mountain's known since the thaw."

Ada looked at the ledger in front of him. Sefa had seen that ledger a dozen times over the years, glimpsed it sideways: columns of names, dates, tolls, notes in his tiny exact hand. A record of every sin that passed his gate, priced and forgiven. He touched the page now the way he'd touched that little black key, and she felt the floor of the conversation tilt, because those weren't the same gesture. They'd never been the same gesture. They were the same *wound*.

"There's a story," Ada said at last, and his voice was different—flatter, older, like something long buried shifting under weight, "that the Thornfells aren't a mountain range. They're a grave. That the old gods didn't die when the new faith came. They were locked away, under the deepest part of the range, and the lock has seven tumblers, and each tumbler was set with a soul freely surrendered, and the door has stayed shut for three hundred years because everyone who knew the terms is dead."

Sefa kept very still. "Is that the story?"

"That's the story." He met her eyes, and for the first time in all the years she'd known him, there was nothing careful in his face at all. What was underneath was so tired and so fierce that she had to make herself not look away. "Three days ago, an old woman prayed at my gatepost. And something answered her. Frost in high summer, Sefa. A voice in the stone. It said a name I haven't heard since I was six years old." His jaw tightened. "I told myself it was wind. I'm done telling myself that."

The cold thing in Sefa's chest had stopped turning. Now it was simply cold, all the way through, the clean cold of a decision arriving ahead of the person making it.

"Vessar doesn't want passes," she said. "He wants the door."

"He wants what's behind it. Power enough to end the war in a week and own what's left forever." Ada closed the ledger. The sound was very soft and very final, a book shutting on twenty years. "When his captain comes—and he'll come, within the fortnight—he'll ask for my keys and my records, and I will lie to him, because lying is the one trade I've truly mastered. But lies buy days, not safety. And I am not going to sit behind this desk counting the days until the mountain opens whether I will it or no."

"So what does the great gatekeeper intend?" she asked, and hated how gently it came out.

"I intend to go under the mountain," Ada Almeida said, "and steal back what was sold there. And I need a crew for a descent no one returns from, which means I need people who've already lost everything, and are angry enough about it to spend what's left." Almost—almost—a smile, dry as dust. "I'm told you know such people."

Sefa sat back. Outside, the wind came down the pass with a sound like a voice trying out syllables, and the torches bent, and somewhere far below their feet—she'd have sworn it, later, under oath—something deep in the stone exhaled.

"No one returns from this," she said. "You said it yourself."

"Yes."

"And you're asking anyway."

"I'm asking *you*," Ada said. "The rest I'll ask tomorrow."

Sefa looked at the gray little man behind the gray little desk, the invisible man, the keeper of clean ledgers and small sins, and understood that she was watching the exact moment his twenty years of hiding ended—and that the strangest feeling in her chest, under the fear, under the cold, was relief. Because if Ada Almeida was done being safe, then safety was dead everywhere, and she'd rather die in interesting company than live bored in a kingdom with a crown made of graves.

"Well," she said, standing, pulling her coat straight. "Lucky for you, I know a defrocked priestess who drinks like it's a holy sacrament, a tunnel-digger who hasn't spoken in nine years, and a pickpocket child who worships you for reasons neither of you understands." She headed for the door, then paused, hand on the frame, and didn't turn around. "Forty crowns, Ada. You owe me forty crowns for this conversation."

"I don't recall borrowing anything."

"You borrowed my quiet life." She stepped out into the dark of the pass, where the frost was already blooming on the north steps, out of season, spelling something in the starlight that she chose not to read. "Consider the debt called in."

Behind her, in the gatehouse, she heard Ada Almeida open a fresh page in his ledger and begin, for the first time in twenty years, to write down something that wasn't a sin.
