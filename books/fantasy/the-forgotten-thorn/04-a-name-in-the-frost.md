## 4. A Name in the Frost

The frost came back on the third night.

Ada noticed it the way he noticed everything at Hollowgate: sideways, without seeming to look. He was closing the toll ledger when the candle flame leaned toward the window as if pulled by a thread, and beyond the glass, the gatepost had gone white from crown to base—a sheath of ice in the middle of harvest season, when the nights still smelled of cut hay and warm dust.

He set down his pen. The ink blot spread into a small black flower on the page.

*Not again,* he thought, and knew even as he thought it that it was a lie. It would happen again. It was already happening again.

He took the lamp and went out into the yard, boots crunching on frost that should not exist, breath fogging in air that should have been soft. The mountains stood black against a sky salted with stars, and Thornfell—the great shoulder of it, the pass threading its throat—caught the moonlight along its eastern ridge like the edge of a drawn blade.

The gatepost was old ironwood, older than the road, older than the toll house, older than any record Ada kept. His predecessor had told him it was planted by the first keepers, back when Hollowgate was a shrine and not a business. Ada had spent ninety years touching that post every morning of his working life, and it had never once been anything but wood.

Now it glittered. Now it *breathed*, almost—frost crawling up in slow fern-patterns, blooming and fading and blooming again, like something exhaling through the grain.

"Who's there?" he said. His voice came out steadier than his hands felt. "I'm the keeper of this gate. State your business."

The wind moved through the pass behind him, low and long, and for a moment it sounded less like wind than like a voice trying to remember how to be one. Frost crackled. Somewhere up-slope, a stone shifted with a sound too deliberate to be erosion.

Then, clear as a bell rung inside his own skull:

***Alm.***

Ada went very still.

No one had called him that in seventy years. No one alive had ever called him that. It was what his mother had shouted across the yard at supper time, what Lira had chanted when she wanted to annoy him—*Alm, Alm, slow as a snail*—a child's name, a family name, a name that had been buried with everyone who'd used it.

He turned slowly, lamp raised, and the yard was empty. The road ran down toward the valley, silvered and vacant. The road ran up into the pass, swallowed by dark. Nothing moved but the frost-ferns on the gatepost, growing, dying back, growing again.

"You're dead," Ada said to the night. "All of you are dead. Everyone says so. The priests say so. The histories say so."

The wind said nothing. But the cold pressed against his chest like a hand laid flat over his heart—not hostile. Patient. The way you'd press a hand to a door you were deciding whether to knock on.

He stood there until the frost began to melt, which took until well past midnight, and then he went inside and did not sleep. He sat at his desk with the ledger open before him and wrote nothing, and the ink dried in its pot, and the candle burned down to a stub, and somewhere in the walls the old timbers ticked and settled like a house full of sleeping people.

In the morning, the gatepost was dry and ordinary, and Ada touched it as he always did, and pretended his fingers weren't searching for cold.

* * *

He lasted four days before he went looking.

That was the thing about being invisible for ninety years—it made a man an excellent listener. Smugglers talked in front of him because they'd decided long ago that the gatekeeper was furniture. Pilgrims prayed aloud at the post because he was scenery. And in ninety years of scenery, Ada had assembled, without ever meaning to, a private archive of everything the world had forgotten about the old gods.

He started with the oldest thing he owned besides himself: a box under the floorboards of his bedroom, containing his mother's prayer cards, rescued from their farmhouse when he was eleven years old and the soldiers came. Seven cards, seven gods, painted in faded vegetable dyes by some village artist whose name was lost. The Gatekeeper of Crossroads. The Mother Under Hill. The River That Remembers. On the back of each card, in his mother's careful hand, a prayer.

He hadn't read them since he was a boy. He read them now, sitting on his narrow bed with the shutters closed, and found that his mouth remembered the words before his mind did. They rose up whole, in his mother's cadence, in her flat valley vowels, and by the third card his eyes were burning and he had to stop.

*Lira used to sing them,* he thought. *She turned them into skipping rhymes. Mother scolded her and then hummed along.*

Seventy years, and the grief was not a wound anymore; it was a room in his house, furnished and dusted, that he simply never entered. He opened the door now and stood on the threshold and could not make himself go in.

Because here was the arithmetic he had avoided for seven decades: Lira had been taken—or sold, or given, the stories disagreed—to seal a bargain beneath Thornfell. She had been nine years old. And Ada had been thirteen, hiding in the meal barrel while men in gray robes argued with his father in the kitchen, and he had heard everything, and he had done nothing, because he was thirteen and afraid, and afterward everyone who might have punished him or forgiven him was gone.

And Ada himself—

He got up and crossed to the little mirror above the washstand, the one with the cracked corner, and looked at the face in it. A face in its early thirties. Brown hair going gray at the temples, no more than that. Hands unspotted, unbent. Ninety years of winters at three thousand feet, and not one of them had touched him.

Gate-warding magic, the first keeper had called it, when Ada asked why he didn't age. *The gate needs a living anchor. It borrows your years and pays them back in keeping.* A fair trade, Ada had thought once. Then a convenient one. Then, in the long middle decades, a sentence.

Now, holding his mother's prayer cards, he understood it differently. The gate hadn't just kept him young. It had kept him *here*. Anchored, like a boat tied to a dock, while the whole world rowed away without him. Every person who could have called him Alm was dead. Every witness to what happened in that kitchen was dead. The gray-robed men were dead. His parents were dead. The village was a ruin with a new name.

Which meant—

The realization arrived quietly, the way frost arrives, and it chilled him far more than the voice had.

*If the god beneath the mountain is waking, and it knows my childhood name, then it remembers. And I am the only one left who does.*

The last witness. Not to history—histories had scholars. The last witness to *her*. To the sound of Lira laughing through the meal-barrel lid. To the exact weight of her asleep against his shoulder on the cart ride they never finished. If she existed anywhere—if some part of her waited in whatever court the old stories placed beneath the stone—then Ada Almeida was the only living soul who could say her name and mean it.

He put the cards away. He blew out the lamp. He lay in the dark and listened to the mountain breathe.

* * *

On the fifth night, he stopped pretending.

He banked the fire, took the lamp, and walked out to the gatepost carrying his mother's prayer card for the Gatekeeper of Crossroads, because if you meant to speak with a god, it seemed prudent to open with the correct paperwork.

The frost was already there, waiting, ferning up the ironwood in white lace. The air had that held-breath quality it had had before. Ada planted his feet the way he did when facing down armed men at the toll bar, which was absurd, and also exactly right.

"I know you're there," he said. "I don't know what you are. The old faith says the gods died when the locks were sealed. The priests say the mountain is just a mountain." He turned the card over in his fingers. "But mountains don't learn children's names."

The wind came down the pass, and this time it shaped itself around words, most of them. What reached him was less language than weather-with-meaning, and yet he understood it the way he understood his own heartbeat:

***Lira.***

His knees nearly went. He caught himself on the gatepost, and the frost bit his palm, sweet and sharp as snowmelt.

"Don't," he said hoarsely. "Don't say her name unless you mean to tell me where she is."

***Under.*** The word fell like a stone down a well. ***Beneath. Kept. Sealed. Seventh.***

"Seventh lock." Ada's mouth was dry. He'd heard smugglers whisper about the sealed door for years—drunk talk, ghost talk, the kind of story men told to make the dark interesting. Seven souls for seven locks, someone had said once, and another had laughed and said, *and the seventh was a child, they say, but that's just the wine.* "You're telling me the stories are true. All of them."

***True.*** The frost flared, every fern on the post standing out in silver relief, and for one instant Ada saw—not with his eyes, he was sure of that, but saw nonetheless—an impression of vastness folded into stone, of seven lights sunk deep in the dark like coins at the bottom of a well, six dim and one bright, one bright and *small*, and around it a patience so old it had worn grooves in eternity.

Then it was gone, and he was a man gripping a frozen post in his own yard, weeping without having decided to.

"She was nine," he said. The words came out of him like splinters, one at a time. "They took her out of her bed. My father signed something—I heard the quill. I hid. I was a coward and I hid, and I have hidden for ninety years, and you—" his voice cracked clean in half— "you have been down there with her. In the dark. For seventy years. Why now? Why tell me *now*?"

The answer came slow, each syllable a separate exhalation of cold:

***The lock. Cracks.***

***He comes. The thief-king. With keys of blood.***

***Before he reaches her—***

The voice frayed, wind losing its shape, and the last of it was barely a touch of frost against Ada's cheek, almost gentle, almost pleading:

***—witness. Speak her name where it can be heard. You are the last who can.***

Then the night was only night. The frost began, slowly, to melt.

* * *

Ada did not go back inside. He walked the length of the wall instead, all the way around the toll yard, past the stables and the shuttered waystation, his boots ringing on frozen mud, doing the thing he did best: counting costs.

Vessar. The name had been arriving all autumn like weather reports from a distant storm—surveyors in the passes, charters signed, soldiers wintering closer every month. Sefa had mentioned, two visits back, that the king's men were asking questions about Thornfell specifically. About *the sealed door*. Ada had filed it away as one more danger among many, one more reason to keep his head down, his ledgers clean, his opinions priced and purchasable.

Keep your head down. Ninety years of that. It had kept him alive, and it had kept him alone, and it had let a nine-year-old girl rot in the dark beneath the mountain while he grew old without aging, comfortable in his sentence, calling it survival.

He stopped at the corner of the wall where he could see the whole pass laid out under the stars: the road climbing north into Thornfell's throat, the black mass of the mountain, the faint seam high on its flank where, if the stories were true, a door had been sealed with a child's soul and forgotten by everyone except the dark.

Everyone except him.

If Vessar reached that door first—with his keys of blood, with an army at his back—then whatever power the seventh lock held would belong to a man who had never once hesitated to spend other people's lives. And Lira—

Lira would become a crown jewel. A battery. A footnote in a tyrant's coronation.

Whereas if Ada spoke her name—if he gathered whatever ragged crew it took and went down beneath the mountain after her—then he would finally stop hiding. Ninety years of invisibility, ended in a single act of visible, unforgivable trespass. Vessar would know his face. The priests would know his name. There would be no more gate to hide behind, because he would have abandoned it.

He looked at his hands in the starlight—young hands, borrowed-time hands—and found they were not shaking.

"They'll come for the gate anyway," he said aloud, to the mountain, to the god, to himself. "A king who wants what's under Thornfell doesn't leave the only pass unguarded. So my choice isn't whether the storm gets here. It's whether I'm still standing at my window watching it when it does."

The wind stirred, and though it carried no words, it felt—for the first time in ninety years—like agreement.

Ada went inside, pulled his traveling strongbox from under the bed, and began, methodically, to count what ninety years of small sins had bought him. Coin, yes. But also names. Favors. Debts owed to him by people who moved through the world's cracks, the ones who knew how to go down into places that swallow people.

At the top of the list, written in his neat keeper's hand, he put a single word:

*Sefa.*

Three days' ride to Corvale, if the roads were kind. Less, if he rode like a man who had finally stopped being careful.

He packed the prayer cards last, wrapping them in oilcloth, tucking them against his heart. Then he blew out the lamp, shouldered his pack, and stepped out into the frozen yard, leaving the toll house dark behind him for the first night in ninety years.

Above the pass, Thornfell stood silent against the stars—but as Ada crossed the threshold of the gate he had guarded his whole unnatural life, he could have sworn the mountain exhaled, long and slow, like something turning over in its sleep to watch him go.
