## 5. The Priestess Without a God

The wine in Corvale was cheap enough to drink without tasting and expensive enough to ruin you anyway, which Yara Vess had decided was the most honest thing left in the free cities.

She sat at her usual table in the Drowned Bell—the one in the corner where the lamplight gave out and the plaster sweated in the damp—and she drank with the methodical patience of a woman performing a duty. That was what it had become, over the six years since the Order of the Ninefold Gate had been dissolved by decree and its priestesses scattered like ash on a wind that smelled of politics. Drinking was a rite now. She performed it nightly, with proper reverence, facing no particular altar.

"You're starting early," said Marette, the barkeep's daughter, setting down a fresh cup unasked.

"I started late," Yara said. "It's nearly dusk."

"It's barely past noon."

Yara looked at the window, high and grimy near the ceiling, where the light did indeed have the thin gold quality of early afternoon rather than the bruised amber of evening. She considered this information. She declined to be changed by it.

"Then I'm pacing myself," she said, "which is more than can be said for this city."

Marette laughed the way young people laughed at her—kindly, dismissively, the laugh you give a madwoman or a beggar or an old soldier whose wars are over—and went back to the barrels. Yara wrapped her hands around the cup and let the day continue its slow collapse around her.

The Drowned Bell filled as the afternoon wore on. Dockworkers from the river quarter, their forearms striped with rope burns. A pair of wool merchants arguing about tariffs with the passion of men who had never once feared anything worse than a bad season. Two students from the Athenaeum, wearing the new fashion of skepticism like a badge—Vessar's fashion, imported from the capital along with his surveyors and his edicts and his handsome, terrible certainty that the world could be administered.

Yara listened the way she always listened, half-drunk and wholly attentive, because old habits were the last things to die and hers had been trained into her before she could read. The students were talking about the mountain.

"—not superstition anymore, that's my point. The Crown has *surveyors* there. Actual surveyors. You don't chart passes for nothing."

"They're charting passes because of the war. Supply routes."

"Through Thornfell? No army has crossed Thornfell in three hundred years. There's one pass, and it's got some mad gatekeeper sitting on it, and everyone says the mountain itself is wrong up there. Roads that move. Streams that run backward." He lowered his voice with the delicious self-consciousness of a boy saying something forbidden in public. "My uncle drove freight through the lowlands two years ago. He swears he heard singing under the ground. Under it."

"Singing."

"The old kind. The kind they burned the orders for knowing."

Yara turned her cup slowly on the scarred wood. The old kind. As if there were kinds of singing, as if the songs had not all been one song, sung in rounds by nine orders across nine provinces, each verse assigned to a different house of the faithful so that no single order ever held the whole of it. She had known her verse. The Order of the Ninefold Gate had kept the third verse, the one about doors and thresholds and the gods who wait at crossings for someone to invite them through.

She had sung it every morning for eleven years. Then the decree came, and the sanctuaries were stripped, and the Mother Superior had stood in the empty chapter hall and told them all to go home, and Yara had asked—she still remembered asking, still remembered the sound of her own voice cracking on it—*home to where?*

The Mother Superior hadn't answered. She'd just looked at Yara with an expression Yara had spent six years trying to drink out of her memory: pity, yes, but underneath the pity something worse. Agreement. The old woman had thought the decree was correct. Had thought the gods were dead and the orders were corpses pretending to breathe, and that mercy meant letting them stop.

Yara finished her cup and signaled for another.

That was the thing nobody understood about being defrocked. It wasn't the losing of the title. Titles were words; she'd been called worse by better. It wasn't even the losing of the work—the prayers, the rites, the long patient tending of shrines that fewer people visited every year. She could have done that work alone, unnamed, in ditches, if she'd believed in it.

It was the losing of the *being seen*. For eleven years she had walked through market towns in her gray-and-silver robes and people had looked at her and seen a function in the world—a keeper of thresholds, a woman who knew the names of the powers at the edges of things. When she prayed aloud at a crossroads, the prayer landed somewhere. Not because anyone answered. Because the shape of her standing there, robed and certain, told the frightened that the world had edges and the edges had wardens.

Now she was just a drunk in a corner of the Drowned Bell, and when she prayed—which she still did, some nights, kneeling on the floor of her rented room with her forehead against the boards, hating herself for it—no one saw, and the words went nowhere, and the silence afterward had teeth.

The light through the high window went from gold to copper to nothing. Lamps were lit. The students left. The dockworkers sang something obscene about a harbor master's wife. Yara drank and watched the door, though she could not have said why. She always watched the door. Another habit that would not die: a priestess of gates watches every threshold, waiting for whatever comes through it.

What came through it, at the hour when the rain started, was a child.

A girl, maybe ten, soaked to the skin, barefoot, standing in the doorway with the rain streaming off her hair and her eyes wide and fixed—not on the room, not on the barkeep, but on Yara's corner. On Yara herself, with an intensity that made the hair rise on Yara's arms.

The room went on around them. Nobody else seemed to notice the girl at all, which should have been Yara's first warning, and would have been, six years ago, when her senses were clean. But she was six years deep in wine and grief, and so she simply watched, frowning, as the girl crossed the room—leaving no wet footprints, she noticed distantly, no footprints at all—and stopped at her table.

"You're the gate-keeper's daughter," the girl said.

"I'm no one's daughter." Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Go home, little one. You'll catch your death."

"I don't have a home. Neither do you. That's why it has to be you." The girl held out her hand, palm up, small and pale in the lamplight. "You have to look at your hand."

And Yara—who had spent six years perfecting the art of not looking at anything, of letting the world slide past her like water off oiled cloth—looked at her hand.

Her left palm was burning.

Not alight, not aflame—there was no fire, no smoke—but burning the way a brand burns, the way a name is pressed into a steer's hide, and rising out of the skin in lines of silver-white heat was a sigil she had not seen in six years and would know blindfolded, dead, or damned.

Nine lines radiating from a single point, crossed by a circle that did not quite close—an open ring, a threshold, a door drawn as the old faith drew it: never shut, never fully open, always *becoming*. The mark of the Ninefold Gate. The mark that had been inked onto her palm with ash and holy water at her ordination, and that had faded to nothing the morning after the decree, leaving her skin blank as any common woman's.

It was not inked now. It was *burning*, from inside, silver-white and precise, and it hurt, and it was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen.

"No," Yara said. Out loud. To a child who was not there, in a tavern full of people who heard nothing. "No. It's gone. We're ended. Whatever you are, you've made a mistake."

The girl tilted her head, and for a moment—only a moment—her eyes were not a child's eyes. They were old the way stone is old, the way winter is old, and behind them Yara felt something vast turn its attention toward her, the way a sleeper turns toward a sound.

*We do not make mistakes,* the girl said—or did not say; the words arrived inside Yara's skull like frost forming on glass, pattern by pattern. *We waited. Waiting is most of what we are, now. And then, tonight, at a gate in the mountains, someone prayed, and we remembered how to answer.*

"A gate in the mountains." Yara's mouth was dry. The cup in her hand might as well have been empty air; she had forgotten it existed. "Thornfell."

*Hollowgate.* The word carried weight, carried *history*, carried the sense of a place long watched and recently woken. *There is a keeper there who has forgotten what he keeps. And there is a debt beneath the stone, seventy years unpaid, and a man coming to collect it who will make the world a locked room. You were made for doors, priestess. Yours is the third verse. Sing it.*

"I can't." It came out of her before she could dress it up, raw and true. "I don't—I haven't sung since—my order is dead, the sanctuaries are plowed fields, there's no one left who knows the responses, I am—" her voice broke, and she hated it, hated the six years of wine sitting right there behind her tongue, audible to anyone with ears, audible to a god. "I'm not what I was. I'm a drunk in a corner. You've come to the wrong woman. There are no women left. There's no one left."

The girl regarded her for a long moment. Rain ran down outside the door; none fell here, on this impossible child with her dry bare feet.

*Yes,* the presence said, and there was something in it that was almost gentle, almost sorrowful, the tone of a parent watching a child insist on a wound. *That is what you believe. But we remember you at nineteen, kneeling in the snow at the threshold shrine at Vael's Crossing, praying for a dying mule driver no one else would pray for. We remember every prayer you ever gave us, Yara Vess. We kept them. All of them. Even the ones you thought fell into silence.*

"We kept them," Yara whispered. "Where? Where were you?"

*Asleep. Sealed. Bound beneath the stone by our own bargain, our own cowardice, seven locks and seven souls paid to hold them—and we are sorry, we are so sorry, for what that cost, and the cost is coming due.* The child's face flickered, and beneath it, for half a heartbeat, Yara saw something enormous and weary and radiant, a presence like a cathedral full of candlelight, and then it was a girl again, small and wet-eyed and waiting. *The seal is failing. The man who means to seize it is named Vessar, and he is already sending his people to the mountain. You have a choice, priestess. You always had a choice. That is why we are asking instead of commanding.*

"And if I say no?"

*Then we will find another. Or we will fail. Gods have failed before.* The girl smiled then, and it was a child's smile, crooked and hopeful and heartbreaking. *But you are watching the door. You have always been watching the door. We thought you might want to be more than the one who watches.*

The sigil on her palm flared white-hot—one pulse, like a struck bell—and began to fade, sinking back beneath the skin, leaving behind a faint silver shimmer, a ghost of the mark, visible only when the lamplight caught it at the right angle. Like a scar. Like a promise.

The girl was gone. Between one blink and the next, simply absent, and the doorway behind her held nothing but rain and the blue-gray dark of evening, and the dockworkers' song rolled on, and Marette was laughing at something by the barrels, and the whole world continued in its ignorance as if nothing had happened at all.

Yara sat very still for a long time.

Then she looked down at her cup, at the wine she had been drinking with such dutiful devotion, and pushed it away to the far edge of the table, out of reach, the way you banish a lover you have finally caught betraying you.

Her hands were shaking. She let them shake. She was done hiding things from herself; she'd hidden from herself for six years and it had gotten her exactly here, to a corner table in a drowning city, waiting for a sign she'd sworn she'd stopped believing in.

*Someone prayed, and we remembered how to answer.*

At a gate in the mountains. A keeper who had forgotten what he kept. She didn't know who he was. She found, examining herself honestly, that she already pitied him, and already, absurdly, wanted to meet him—wanted to stand in front of another keeper of thresholds and see whether the sickness in her own vocation lived in his too, or whether somewhere in the world the work had survived intact in someone else's hands.

She rose. The room tilted and settled. Six years of wine did not leave a body in an evening, and the walk to her rented room would be its own small penance, and tomorrow the shaking would be worse before it was better. She knew the road. She'd walked harder ones.

At the door she stopped, and turned, and knelt—right there, on the filthy boards of the Drowned Bell's threshold, in front of the dockworkers and the lamplight and Marette's startled face—and pressed her marked palm flat against the floor.

"Third verse," she said quietly. Her voice cracked on the first word and steadied on the second, and by the third it sounded, for the first time in six years, like her voice. "Doors and thresholds. The gods who wait at crossings. *I remember.*"

The silver sigil pulsed once beneath her skin, warm as a held hand.

Outside, the rain was easing, and away to the east—toward the mountains, toward a gate she had never seen and a keeper she had never met—the clouds had broken, and between them stood the moon, full and white and watchful as an open eye.

Yara pulled her hood up and walked into the street, toward the stables at the edge of the river quarter, where a horse dealer who owed her a favor would be closing up for the night.

She was going to need a horse, supplies, and a map of Thornfell.

And if the god was honest—if any of it was real—then somewhere ahead of her, at a lonely pass in the mountains, a man was about to discover that his gate had been listening all along.

She only hoped she reached him before the king's people did.
