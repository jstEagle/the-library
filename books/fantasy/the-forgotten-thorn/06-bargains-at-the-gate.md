## 6. Bargains at the Gate

The captain of Vessar's vanguard arrived three days after the frost, and he arrived the way bad news always arrived at Hollowgate: on good horses, with clean boots, and with the particular patience of a man who had never once been told no.

Ada watched them come up the pass from the gatehouse window—six riders in the king's slate-and-silver, banners furled against the wind, and a seventh rider trailing behind with a scribe's satchel bouncing against his hip. A scribe. That was worse than soldiers. Soldiers wanted passage or plunder, both of which Ada knew how to price. A scribe wanted *records*.

He set down his tea, which had gone cold anyway, and took a moment to arrange himself. Twenty years of practice went into it: shoulders loosened but not slouched, face arranged into the mild, faintly apologetic blankness of a minor functionary whose whole ambition was to be forgotten by supper. He checked the ledger shelf out of habit. He had already moved the real one—the true ledger, the one with twenty years of bribes logged in his own neat hand—into the loose stone behind the hearth. What sat on the shelf now was its twin, duller and dumber, the book of a man who saw nothing and wrote down less.

*You are no one,* he told himself in the old litany. *You are the gate that opens and closes. You are weather.*

But the frost on the gatepost had said his name. *Adaric.* A name no living person used, a name his mother had shouted across a courtyard seventy years ago, back when his hair was brown and his knees didn't ache in the cold. And gods, he thought as the hoofbeats grew louder, did his knees ache now. The gate-warding kept him alive; it had never promised to keep him young.

He went out to meet them.

The captain reined in beneath the arch and looked around with the expression of a man appraising a horse he intended to buy cheap. He was perhaps forty, square-built, with the sunburned patience of someone who had marched a long way to stand somewhere unimpressive.

"Gatekeeper." He did not dismount. "I'm Captain Renn Vosse, third vanguard, in service to King Vessar, rightful sovereign of the Thornfell marches and the free territories beyond." The titles came off him like coins counted aloud. "By order of the crown, I'm to take custody of this post's keys, seals, and records."

Ada blinked at him with the gentle confusion of a man who had misheard something simple. "Records," he repeated.

"Traffic logs. Toll receipts. Correspondence. Anything touching the mountain."

"The mountain." Ada scratched his jaw. "Captain, I keep a toll book and a complaint box. The complaints are mostly about my soup."

Vosse's eyes narrowed a fraction—not suspicion yet, just the irritation of a man who had expected faster obedience. "The king's surveyors have charted every pass into Thornfell. This is the only one with a sealed door beneath it. You'll understand that makes your little book interesting to us."

"Would that it made it interesting to anyone else," Ada said, and let a small, tired smile through. "Twenty years, Captain. Do you know what comes through Hollowgate? Wool. Salt. Smugglers who pay me to look at the sky. Pilgrims going to shrines nobody remembers the names of. Once, a man transporting bees. There were *forms* for the bees." He turned and gestured up at the gatehouse with its sagging eaves. "Come inside. Warm yourself. I'll show you every page I own, and you can tell me if any of it is worth a march from the capital."

It was, he reflected as Vosse dismounted and handed his reins to a trooper, almost too easy. Men like Vosse had decided what Ada Almeida was before they'd finished riding up the road—a gray little clerk in a gray little pass—and men like Vosse never revisited their decisions. Invisibility wasn't a gift. It was a craft, and Ada had been apprenticed to it since before this captain was born.

Inside, he poured wine that was more water than wine, set out bread, and opened the false ledger under the captain's eye while the scribe copied pages in a corner, quill scratching like a mouse in a wall.

Vosse read slowly, thoroughly, and Ada let him, watching over the rim of his cup with a heart that beat slow and even from long discipline. Here was the trick of lying well: you did not lie. You gave the truth a haircut. Every entry in the false book was real—wool, salt, the bee man—just not all of it. Omission was the honest man's forgery.

"Your tolls are low," Vosse said at last.

"I charge what the road will bear. The road bears very little."

"And smugglers? Every gatekeeper on this border takes bribes. It's practically a toll itself." He said it without malice, almost companionably, testing.

"Some do," Ada agreed mildly. "I wouldn't know where to find one if I wanted to. They don't leave cards."

Something flickered behind the captain's eyes—the frustration of a fisherman feeling a tug he couldn't name. He turned a page. "There's a seal on the door below. Old script. My orders say the crown requires access to everything pertaining to it. Keys, gatekeeper. Where are the keys?"

And there it was, laid bare at last: not traffic, not taxes. The door.

Ada refilled the captain's cup with the motion of a man buying time to think, and thought fast beneath the blandness of his face. There were keys, of course. Three of them, iron and green with age, hanging on a nail behind his bed for twenty years because no one had ever asked, because the door below the gate had been sealed when his grandfather's grandfather still drew breath, sealed with a bargain everyone had agreed to forget. Seven souls for seven locks, the god had whispered in the frost. Six names remained. One had been paid.

His sister's.

If Vosse took those keys, some scribe in the capital would carry them down into the dark, and whatever slept beneath Thornfell would wake to the smell of a usurper's ambition. Or worse—it wouldn't wake. It would simply open, the way a wound opens.

"The keys," Ada said, and sighed, and became suddenly, profoundly old. "Captain, I'll tell you what I told the last crown officer who asked, eleven years ago, and I'll save you the trip he made to the archive at Drenmark first." He held up one finger. "There are no keys."

Vosse's jaw tightened. "Don't waste my time."

"Waste it gladly, but listen." Ada leaned forward, lowering his voice into the register of shared professional misery. "The door predates the office. When the Almeidas were given the wardenship, the sealing was already done—by the old faith, by the kings before the kings, by whoever. The passage down doesn't lock. It's *bound*. There's no keyhole. There's a rite, and the rite died with the priesthood, and the priests' order was broken up—" he waved a hand vaguely eastward, toward Corvale, "—oh, forty years ago? More. If your surveyors found a door down there, it's a door the way a tombstone is a door. You can knock all you like."

He watched the lie land. It was a good lie, built on true bones—the old faith *had* been broken, the priesthood *had* died out, and the binding *was* older than any key. Only the conclusion was false, and conclusions were cheap.

Vosse stared at him for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he smiled, and it was the smile that frightened Ada more than anything else that day, because it was patient.

"My orders anticipated difficulty," the captain said. "Not yours specifically. Difficulty generally." He stood, buckling his sword-belt with a small adjustment. "So here's what happens instead. I'm leaving four men here. They'll assist with your records. All of them. Going back thirty years, and then further, if the archive at Drenmark has anything. You'll remain in your post—you seem attached to it—but the gate answers to the crown now, Gatekeeper. Consider yourself retained."

Retained. The word sat in Ada's chest like a swallowed stone. Four soldiers billeted in his gatehouse, reading his life page by page, and the true ledger six inches from their morning fire behind a loose stone.

"Of course," he heard himself say, warm and defeated and cooperative. "I'll have bunks made up."

* * *

They searched the gatehouse on the second day. Not officially—officially they were "inventorying"—but Ada came back from checking the trapline to find a trooper standing at his hearth with a pry bar, and the loose stone sitting on the floor like a pulled tooth.

The true ledger lay open in the captain's hands.

Ada stood in the doorway and performed the arithmetic of his remaining life in the space of a breath. Twenty years of entries. Names, sums, dates. Sefa appeared eleven times under three different aliases. A dozen smugglers. Two priests of the old faith, sheltered in the cellar during the purges, marked only as *guests, wool merchants*. And in the earliest pages, in younger ink: payments received from a woman named Maren Almeida, his mother, for goods unlisted—which was how a desperate family had once bought silence, and how a seven-year-old girl had been carried past this very gate in a merchant's cart, sold to strangers who spoke softly of the court beneath the mountain, while her brother watched from a window and was told she'd gone to be married in the low country.

Vosse read for a long time. The scribe copied. The other soldiers watched Ada with the flat attention of dogs waiting for a command.

"You're more interesting than your soup," Vosse said finally.

"Everyone is," Ada said. "That's the tragedy of record-keeping."

"These names will hang, you understand. Collaborators with smugglers. Harborers of proscribed clergy." He closed the book gently, which was somehow worse than slamming it. "Unless."

"Unless," Ada echoed.

"Unless the keeper of this gate proves useful in the matter of the door. The king believes there's a power under Thornfell, old man. A bargain, a binding, something the old faith died protecting. Whoever claims it ends the war before it starts. Help us reach it, and your ledger burns. Refuse—" he glanced around the shabby room, at the cold tea, the thin blankets, the nail behind the bed where three iron keys hung in plain sight, because Ada had decided two nights ago that hiding them would damn him faster than showing them, "—and it goes to the magistrates in Corvale, along with your head, I expect, given how many of these names are still alive."

There it was. The wall at the end of twenty years of careful walking.

Ada looked at the keys on their nail. He looked at the ledger. He thought about an old woman praying at his gatepost, and frost blooming out of season, and a voice like wind through stone saying *Adaric* in his mother's accent, saying *Lira*, saying *the seventh lock is waking and the wrong hands are reaching for it.*

He had spent two decades being no one. Being no one had kept him safe, and safe had kept him useless, and useless had kept his sister in the dark.

"Give me a month," Ada said.

Vosse raised an eyebrow.

"A month to prepare what you'll need," Ada said smoothly, and the lie assembled itself in his mouth out of true parts, the way all his best ones did. "The binding responds to the old rites—I told you, the priesthood is gone, but their *writings* aren't. Corvale has them. Confiscated at the suppression, moldering in a magistrate's cellar. If your king wants that door handled rather than knocked upon, someone has to go and get the correct words, and someone has to translate them, and neither happens quickly." He spread his hands. "Kill me now and you have a dead clerk and a locked door. Wait a month and you have a gatekeeper who knows the pass, the roads, and every tongue the old faith wrote in."

It was mostly true. That was the beauty of it. He *did* know the tongues. He *did* know the pass. And there *were* writings in Corvale—or there would be, if a defrocked priestess hadn't drunk them all away, in which case she would do nicely in place of them.

Vosse studied him the way he'd studied the gatehouse: appraising, discounting, deciding. "A month," he said. "One rider goes to Corvale with you—no, don't argue, that's the offer. Sergeant Ilves. He reports to me weekly. Miss the week, and the ledger travels without you." He tucked the true ledger under his arm—collateral, hostage, leash—and walked to the door. At the threshold he paused. "You lied well, gatekeeper. For what it's worth. But nobody keeps a lonely pass for twenty years out of love of tolls. Everybody guarding something is hiding something. I just haven't decided which way round it is with you."

He left. The door swung. Ada stood alone in his gatehouse with four enemy soldiers making themselves at home, one sergeant shadowing his every step, and a deadline ticking like water dripping in a cave.

* * *

That night he wrote the message.

It took him an hour to compose eight lines, because eight lines was all a smuggler's cipher could carry safely, and because every word had to be weighed like coin. He wrote it small, on scrap, in the trade-cant Sefa had taught him years ago against exactly this kind of night:

*Door below is waking. Crown knows. Has my books and my neck. Need crew for descent—priest if you can find one, digger who knows deep ways, someone young and quick and stupid enough to say yes. Month, maybe less. No one comes back from under Thornfell. Pay is everything I have, which is nothing. Come anyway.*

He read it five times. Then he added a sixth line, crossed it out, and left it crossed out, because some truths were too heavy even for ciphers: *My sister is down there.*

At midnight he went out to check the traplines, as he did every night, and Sergeant Ilves trailed him at a respectful distance, because the crown now supervised even his rabbit snares. At the third snare, by the split boulder where the smugglers' cache hollow lived, Ada knelt in the snow, freed a hare that wasn't there, and slipped the folded paper into the crack behind the stone, under the flat marker rock that generations of runners knew to lift.

Then he stood, brushed off his knees, and looked south down the pass, where the mountains went black against the stars and somewhere beneath them his sister waited, unchanged, unlamented, unclaimed by anyone but a god of frost and a brother who had watched a cart carry her away.

*Come anyway,* he had written. It was not a plan. It was a prayer, addressed to a sharp-tongued woman with quick hands and a debt of eleven favors outstanding, and it was the first prayer Ada Almeida had offered in twenty years that he actually meant.

Behind him, at the gatepost, unnoticed by the sergeant, unnoticed by anyone, the frost had bloomed again—out of season, out of reason—in the shape of an open door.
