## 7. The Crew of Broken Things

Sefa Venn had stolen from kings, priests, and one very memorable dowager empress who'd chased her through three districts in nothing but a nightgown and her husband's sword. She had never, until now, tried to steal people.

"You want me to find you *who*?" she'd said, two nights ago, in the back room of the Drowned Lantern while Ada Almeida sat across from her looking like a man who had rehearsed his lines and lost them anyway.

"A crew. For a descent."

"Into Thornfell."

"Under it."

"And you're asking me because—"

"Because you know everyone the world threw away," Ada said, "and you know which ones are worth keeping."

She should have laughed. Instead she'd looked at his hands—steady on the table, always steady, that was the trick of him—and seen the tremor he was hiding inside his sleeves. That was when she knew this wasn't a job. This was a wound with legs.

So now she stood in the rain outside Corvale's Temple Ward, watching a defrocked priestess lose a fistfight with a bottle.

* * *

Yara Solenne drank like she was trying to drown something specific, and the something kept swimming. Sefa found her at the Broken Chalice, third table from the wall, where the light didn't reach and neither did hope. The sigil was still there—Sefa could see it glowing faintly through the woman's sleeve even from the doorway, a seven-pointed star burning on her palm like a coal that refused to die.

"You're Yara Solenne," Sefa said, dropping into the opposite chair.

"I'm nobody." The priestess's voice was gravel soaked in wine. "Whoever told you different is selling something."

"Sefa Venn. I'm selling something."

"Then buy me another bottle and go away."

Sefa signaled the barkeep for two. When they came, she pushed one across and said, "The old gods woke up."

Yara's hand stopped halfway to the cup. Just for a heartbeat. Then it kept moving, and she drank half of it in one pull. "Drunk already," she muttered. "Impressive pace, even for me."

"At Hollowgate. At the gatepost. Frost in high summer, and a voice out of the stone." Sefa leaned forward. "And your hand's been burning for six days. Don't tell me it hasn't. I watched you flinch when you reached for the cup."

The silence stretched long enough that Sefa heard the rain ticking on the shutters, the groan of the tavern's beams, somewhere upstairs a couple arguing about money—the whole wet machinery of Corvale grinding on.

"The order burned my name off their rolls eleven years ago," Yara said finally. Her thumb moved over the sigil without her seeming to notice, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. "I preached the old faith in an empty chapel until the empty chapel got tired of me too. Whatever's waking under Thornfell—" she laughed, ugly and short, "—it has terrible taste in messengers."

"It chose a gatekeeper who's been invisible for twenty years and a smuggler who can't stay out of trouble. I'd say its taste runs to the discarded."

Something moved behind Yara's eyes then—not hope, hope was too clean a word for it. More like a door creaking open in a house she'd boarded up. "What do you want from me?"

"There's a door under the mountain. Behind it, souls. One of them belongs to someone Ada lost. We're going down to take it back, and the gods themselves are clearing the road for us. I need someone who remembers the rites. Someone who knows what the old faith actually *said*, before the new temples burned the books."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll drink your share and leave you the bill."

Yara stared at her. Then, slowly, she turned her palm upward on the table, and the sigil flared—bright enough to throw shadows, bright enough that the barkeep glanced over and quickly found something else to look at. The light pulsed once, twice, like a heartbeat remembering itself.

"Eleven years," Yara whispered, "I begged it to leave me alone. And now it won't stop *shining*." She closed her fist, and the light went out, and when she looked up her eyes were wet and furious and more alive than they'd been all night. "When do we leave?"

* * *

Dov was harder.

Not because he was hidden—everyone in the quarry district knew about the mute digger who'd been cast out of the Underclan for a crime no one would name. He slept in the mouth of an abandoned adit above the slag heaps, wrapped in a cloak that had been gray since before the war. The hard part was that he didn't want to be found, and a man who doesn't want to be found and knows the tunnels better than the rats is a man you don't find so much as get *permitted* to see.

Sefa came bearing bread, salt pork, and chalk—a digger's currency. She set them down on a flat stone at the adit's entrance and waited, hands visible, the way you do with things that might be wild animals or might be kings.

He emerged after an hour. Of course he did. He'd been watching her the whole time; she'd felt it between her shoulder blades like weather.

He was younger than she expected. Thirty, maybe. A face carved out of the same stone he'd spent his life cutting, hands like root systems, and eyes that did all the talking his tongue wouldn't. He looked at the food. Then at her. Then he picked up the chalk and wrote on the stone:

**WHY**

"Honest question." Sefa crouched, keeping below his eye line—not submission exactly, but courtesy. "There's a sealed door under Thornfell. Old clan-work, older than the Underclan maybe. We need to get through it. And there's no one alive who reads tunnel-marks better than the exiles they were stupid enough to throw away."

He wrote: **THE CLAN SEALED IT. THE CLAN FORGOT WHY. THAT IS WHY THEY FEAR ME. I REMEMBERED.**

Sefa felt the hair rise on her arms. "You've seen the door."

**MY GRANDMOTHER'S GRANDMOTHER CARVED ITS WARNING. SEVEN SOULS FOR SEVEN LOCKS. SHE TAUGHT ME THE MARKS BEFORE THEY TOOK MY VOICE FOR ASKING ABOUT THEM.**

"Took your voice—" Sefa stopped. Recalibrated. The Underclan didn't exile people for crimes. They exiled them for *knowledge*. "They cut it out?"

He nodded once, without self-pity, which was somehow worse.

**IF YOU OPEN WHAT THEY SEALED,** he wrote, **I WANT TO BE THERE WHEN THE CLAN LEARNS THEY WERE WRONG.**

"That's not revenge?"

He considered. Then: **IT IS CORRECTION.**

Sefa stuck out her hand. He looked at it for a long moment, then shook it, and his grip was careful in a way that told her he knew exactly how much force lived in those fingers.

**WHEN DO WE DIG,** he wrote, and almost—almost—smiled.

* * *

Pell found *them*, which figured.

They'd regrouped at the Drowned Lantern to plan the route north, and Sefa stepped out into the alley to check for tails and found a scrap of a girl going through her coat pockets with professional tenderness.

"Oi," Sefa said.

The kid froze—one hand still in Sefa's pocket, holding Sefa's spare knife, holding it *correctly*, by the blade flat, ready to use or discard. Fifteen, maybe sixteen. Sharp face, sharper eyes, hair chopped short with what looked like a fish-gutting knife. A city sparrow with river mud still on her boots.

"You're good," Sefa said. "That's my good knife and you had it out before I finished turning around."

"You're Sefa Venn." The girl didn't run, which was either very brave or very stupid, and Sefa was starting to suspect both. "Everyone in the Warrens says you're the best. And you've been buying rope and lamp oil and cold-weather rations for three days, and nobody buys cold-weather rations in Corvale unless they're going to Thornfell, and *nobody* goes to Thornfell." Her eyes were doing arithmetic. "Except now somebody is. And it's you. And the gatekeeper from Hollowgate was here two nights ago—I saw him, everyone saw him, he looks like he's made of locked doors—and if *he's* going under the mountain—"

"Slow down. What's your interest?"

"My interest—" and here the bravado flickered, just once, like a lamp in wind, "—is that I'm coming. Take me or I follow you anyway. I'm lighter than your packs, quieter than your shadow, and I can open any lock in this city except the ones that matter to me."

"Why would I take a child under a mountain?"

"Because I'm not a child, I'm an investment." But her voice cracked on it, and Sefa saw the truth underneath: three days of hungry watching, a kid who'd decided that these strange adults were the first interesting thing to happen to her life, and who had nowhere else to put her hope.

"Where are your parents?"

"Where's yours?" Pell shot back. Then, quieter, looking at the mud on her own boots: "Don't have any. Had a crew, on the river. They went south for work last spring and never sent for me. So."

Sefa sighed the sigh of a woman watching herself make a decision she'd already made. "Rule one: you do what I say, when I say it. Rule two: if I say run, you run and don't ask which direction. Rule three—"

"What's rule three?"

"Nobody gets left behind. Not ever. Not for speed, not for safety, not for anything. You sign onto that, you're crew. Break it, and you're cargo I dump at the first crossroads."

Pell's face did something complicated—too much feeling for a kid who'd learned to keep her face empty. "Crew," she repeated, tasting the word.

"Crew."

"Does the gatekeeper know? About me?"

"He'll know."

And that was the strange thing, Sefa thought later. The kid lit up—not at the adventure, not at the danger, but at *that*. At the idea of being known to Ada Almeida. Sefa filed it away. There was a story there, some debt the gatekeeper didn't know he'd paid, and she'd get it out of the girl eventually, somewhere on the long cold road.

* * *

They gathered in the back room of the Drowned Lantern on the last night, five people around a scarred table with a map of Thornfell spread across it, and Sefa looked at what she'd assembled and thought: *this is what a disaster looks like.*

A drunk priestess whose god had come back from the dead to haunt her palm. A mute digger who communicated in chalk and grudges. A pickpocket who worshipped a man she'd never spoken to. And herself, a smuggler with more enemies than coin, about to lead all of them into a hole in the world that the clans had sealed with souls.

Ada arrived last, hooded against the rain, and the room changed when he entered—she watched it happen. Yara straightened. Dov's chalk paused mid-word. Pell went pink and pretended to study the map.

"Well," Sefa said. "Here's your crew of broken things, gatekeeper. A fallen priestess, an exile, a thief, and me. If the mountain doesn't kill us, the embarrassment might."

Ada looked at each of them in turn, and Sefa had spent twenty years reading faces and had never seen anything like what crossed his—something between grief and gratitude, as if he'd been handed back pieces of himself he hadn't known were missing.

"They're not broken things," he said quietly. "They're the only things that were never bought."

Yara snorted. Dov wrote something on his slate and turned it around: **HE ALWAYS TALK LIKE THIS?**

"Only when he's terrified," Sefa said, and Ada didn't deny it.

They planned until the candles guttered—routes, signals, contingencies, Dov sketching tunnel-markings in chalk dust while Yara recited fragments of rites and Pell memorized everything with that frightening quick mind. And near dawn, when the others had gone to snatch a few hours of sleep, Sefa caught Ada at the door.

"One condition I didn't mention," she said. "If this goes wrong—if the mountain keeps us—you make sure the kid gets out. Whatever it costs. Promise me."

"She's your crew."

"She's your *believer*. Didn't you wonder why a Warrens pickpocket worships the most boring man in two kingdoms?" Sefa held his gaze. "Ask her on the road. It's a good story. It's the kind of story that reminds you why we're doing this."

Ada stood in the doorway a long moment, rain silvering behind him. "Thank you, Sefa. For finding them."

"Don't thank me yet." She jerked her chin toward the map, toward the black mass of Thornfell inked at its heart. "Because here's what I couldn't put on the table in front of the kid. My contact on the mountain roads sent word at midnight. There are soldiers moving north. Vessar's vanguard, a full column, marching on Hollowgate."

Ada went very still. "How fast?"

"If they push hard? Four days." Sefa pulled her coat off the peg. "So we don't leave at dawn, gatekeeper. We leave *now*—and pray whatever gods just woke up under that mountain like us enough to clear the snow."

Outside, the rain was turning to sleet, and far to the north, beneath the stone, something ancient began—very softly—to laugh.
