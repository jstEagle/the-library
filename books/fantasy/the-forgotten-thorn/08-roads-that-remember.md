## 8. Roads That Remember

The smugglers' road began where the king's road ended, which was to say: at a deadfall of pine and rot that no honest traveler would look at twice.

Ada dismounted and put his shoulder to a slab of granite that had been leaning against its neighbor since before Vessar was born. The stone moved with the groan of an old man rising from a chair, and behind it opened a gap barely wide enough for a mule—black, root-hung, smelling of wet earth and old smoke.

"You're joking," Sefa said.

"I never joke about roads." Ada swung back into the saddle. "The king's surveyors charted every pass with a name on a map. This one has none. It's called the Hollow Way by people who don't write things down, and it runs under Thornfell's shoulder for eleven miles before it sees sky again."

Pell peered into the dark with the naked hunger of a sixteen-year-old who had never met a hole he didn't want to be inside. "Eleven miles underground."

"Under *mountain*. There's a difference, and you'll feel it in your teeth." Ada glanced at Dov, who sat his borrowed pony like a man born to it, silent as always, eyes already reading the tunnel mouth the way a priest reads scripture. "You'll lead when we're under. Your people cut these galleries."

Dov nodded once. It was more answer than he usually gave, and Ada had learned to count each nod like coin.

Yara came last, leading her mule, her hood up against a wind that hadn't decided yet whether it was autumn or winter. She had not spoken much since Corvale. The sigil on her palm—her dead order's burning wheel—had faded to a faint pink scar, but she kept flexing the hand as if checking whether it still hurt. It did. Ada could tell by how often she looked at it.

"The god spoke to you again?" she asked quietly, falling in beside him.

"Through a stream this time. Two nights ago." He kept his voice low, beneath the wind. "It said *left at the split birch, then down where the water forgets the sun*." He almost laughed. "Twenty years I've known these roads, and now a river is giving me directions."

"Does it frighten you?"

Ada considered lying. He was very good at lying; he'd built a life out of it, brick by careful brick. But Yara had a way of waiting through lies like weather.

"Yes," he said. "Not the voice. The fact that it knows my name. That it wants me found." He nudged his horse toward the gap. "I spent twenty years making sure nothing in this world could find me. Now everything can—the gods, the king, all of you. And the strange thing is—" He stopped himself.

"Say it," Yara said.

"It feels less like being hunted than like being *invited*." He shook his head. "That's worse. Invitations can be declined. Hunts end."

They went into the mountain.

* * *

The Hollow Way was older than any kingdom, older perhaps than the gods' long sleep, though Dov's carvings suggested his ancestors had argued otherwise. The gallery walls were marked with spirals and handprints and lines of script no living scholar could read, worn shallow by ten thousand years of passing shoulders. In places the ceiling rose to cathedral height, lost in black; in others it pressed so low they rode bent double, breath loud in their own ears.

Dov led with a lantern and a confidence that needed no words. Twice he stopped, laid his palm flat against stone, and listened—and once he turned them down a branch so narrow Pell asked, half-joking, if anyone had ever come out the other side. Dov looked back at him and smiled for the first time since Ada had known him. It changed his whole face. It did not make him less frightening.

They camped in a chamber where a spring rose straight out of the floor, warm as blood, steaming gently in the cold air. While Pell dozed against his pack and Sefa cleaned her knives with the devotion other people gave to prayer, Ada knelt at the water and cupped some in his hands.

The whisper came up through his palms like warmth through a window in winter.

*South fork sleeps. Take the dry throat. The watchers above are blind, but their dogs are not.*

He drank. The water tasted of iron and something sweeter underneath, something like memory.

"It talks to you through anything wet, doesn't it," Sefa said behind him. Not a question.

"Stones, streams, frost. Once, horribly, through a bowl of soup."

"And you're just—fine with that. A dead god using your dinner as a mouthpiece."

"They're not dead. That's rather the point of all this." Ada wiped his hands on his coat. "And no. I'm not fine with it. But I've stopped expecting to be fine with anything."

Sefa turned her knife over, watching firelight run along the edge. When she spoke again, her voice had lost its usual blade. "My grandmother used to leave cream on the doorstep for the gate-wardens. Little household gods. Everyone said it was superstition, and everyone left the cream anyway." She shrugged. "Maybe we always knew. Maybe knowing is just what you call it when it gets inconvenient."

Before Ada could answer, Yara cried out in her sleep.

She was curled near the spring, and her whole body had gone rigid, the burned sigil flaring red through her glove as though lit from within. Pell woke with a start. Dov was on his feet before anyone else, lantern raised.

Ada reached her first and took her burning hand. "Yara. Yara—you're dreaming."

Her eyes snapped open, white-rimmed. "They're coming," she gasped. "Not here—not yet—but they know the road exists. The god showed me. Riders. Six of them. They'll reach the far mouth of the Hollow Way by tomorrow's dusk, and they'll wait there, because someone told them someone would come out." She stared at him. "Someone sold us, Ada. Someone in Corvale knew what crew left, and why."

The chamber went very quiet. Even the spring seemed to hush.

Sefa stood slowly, knife still in hand, and looked around the circle of faces with the flat professional gaze of a woman doing arithmetic. "Six riders don't find a nameless tunnel mouth by accident. Somebody drew them a map." Her eyes settled nowhere in particular, which was somehow worse than if they'd settled on someone. "So let's all think carefully about who knew our route."

"I told no one," Yara said.

"I told my fence in Corvale I'd be gone a month. Not where." Pell's voice cracked slightly. "Nobody asks me things anyway."

All eyes went, inevitably, to Dov.

Dov met them without flinching. Then he crouched, took a stick of charcoal from his belt, and drew on the flagstones: a rough map of the free cities, a line to Corvale, and beside it—a symbol. A circle crossed by seven bars. Ada recognized it from the tunnel walls: the mark of the digger clans, the people of the under-roads.

Then Dov pointed at the symbol, and at himself, and made a slow cutting motion across his own throat.

"He's saying his clan would kill him before they'd sell a road," Sefa translated softly. "Roads are blood to them." She exhaled. "Which leaves the four of us, and I'll thank you all to notice I'm insulted nobody even glanced at me twice."

"You're too useful to suspect," Ada said. "It's your most reliable quality."

Something that was almost laughter moved through the group—thin, exhausted, but real. Trust, Ada thought, wasn't a wall you built. It was a bridge you kept deciding, moment by moment, not to burn. He looked at each of them: the priestess with her burning hand, the mute digger, the boy thief, the smuggler counting exits. Broken things, all of them. Held together by nothing but the decision to keep holding.

"We don't go out the far mouth," he said. "There's another way. Nobody uses it because nobody knows it exists—I only know because I bribed a dying man for it twenty years ago and swore on his grave I'd never tell." He paused. "I'm telling you now. That's either trust or stupidity, and I've given up telling them apart."

* * *

They broke camp before dawn and walked into the deep dark, where the mountain pressed close enough to touch on both sides.

The alternate way was not a road so much as a wound—an old collapse that Dov's ancestors had shored up with timber black with age, threading between two galleries like a splinter of nothing. They went in single file, leading the mules by voice and touch, the lanterns hooded to slits. Hours passed. Ada lost track of whether his eyes were open. The dark was total and soft, like being held.

And through it all, the whispers followed.

They came from the stones themselves now, faint as breath: *low arch ahead, mind your head* — *the third beam is rotten, pass wide* — *not far, keeper, not far.* The land wanted them through. Ada had spent his whole life believing the world was indifferent at best, hostile at worst, and here was the ground itself murmuring encouragement like an old nurse. He didn't know what to do with it. He filed it away with all the other things he didn't know what to do with, a growing pile.

Near midday—or what he guessed was midday, time being a rumor down here—they heard the dogs.

Faint, high, echoing down some distant gallery: the belling of hounds, and beneath it the knock of hooves on stone. Vessar's outriders had not waited at the far mouth after all. They had found a way in.

"They're in the south gallery," Sefa breathed, pressing her ear to the rock. "Two, maybe three hours behind. The dogs have our scent off the camp."

Dov was already moving, hands flying through the quick gestures his clan used when speech was death. *Split. Two ways. Dogs follow loudest group.*

"No splitting," Ada said. "We stay together or we die apart, and I refuse to die apart. It's the one luxury I've got left."

Dov held his gaze a long moment. Then he nodded, and pointed deeper into the wound-road, and ran.

What followed, Ada would remember afterward in fragments: the timber beams flashing past like the ribs of some enormous dying animal. Pell's breathing, ragged and stubborn, and Sefa's hand at the boy's collar hauling him onward when he stumbled. Yara running with her burning palm held out before her like a lamp, the sigil blazing now bright enough to light the passage, the god pouring strength through it into her legs. And everywhere the whispers, urgent now, no longer gentle: *faster, faster, the hollow comes, the hollow comes.*

The hollow came.

The passage ended at a chasm—a gallery torn open by some ancient quake, forty feet across and bottomless, the far side a black lip of stone with no visible way across. Behind them the dog-cry swelled. Ahead, nothing.

"There's no bridge," Pell said, in the small voice of a boy discovering that stories lie.

"There's always a bridge," Ada said, and hated how much he sounded like a man guessing. "The smugglers called this the King's Mistake. They said there was a crossing that only held for those the mountain favored." He stepped to the edge. Far below, air moved—cold, vast, alive. "I always assumed that was poetry."

The frost bloomed under his boots.

It spread from his feet across the broken edge of the chasm like lace, like fern, like writing—white fire crawling out over the void, thickening, braiding itself into a span of ice that gleamed in Yara's light. It should not have held. Ice does not hold. But this ice sang, a thin high note like a struck glass, and Ada understood suddenly that it was not ice at all. It was attention. The mountain, looking at them, and choosing.

"Go," he said. "Quickly, lightly, and do not look down, because it's rude."

One by one they crossed—Pell first, arms out, walking the singing span with a pickpocket's balance; then Sefa with the mules, soothing them across with a smuggler's murmur; then Dov, who paused halfway and pressed his palm to the ice in something unmistakably like reverence. Yara went last but one. Ada crossed last, and as his boot left the far lip, the span behind him shattered into snow that fell silently into the dark, taking the sound of the dogs with it.

On the far side, they leaned against the stone and breathed.

"That," Sefa said finally, "was not poetry."

"No."

"You're going to tell me what you are, eventually. What the gate made you. What the gods think you are." She wasn't accusing. She was tired, and curious, and afraid in the specific way of people who have bet their lives on a stranger. "Because men don't walk on prayers, Ada. Keepers keep books. You just wrote one in ice."

Ada looked at his hands. They were steady now. They hadn't been steady in weeks, and he realized he'd stopped missing the steadiness of the man who used to hide behind them.

"When we're through," he said. "When Lira is out. I'll tell you everything I know, which is less than you'd hope and more than you'd like." He pushed off the wall. "For now—there's a shepherd's stair ahead, cut into the cliff face, that comes out above the tree line north of the pass. From there we can see the king's road. And I want to see it."

"Why?"

"Because three days ago Vessar's vanguard was a week behind us." Ada felt the words go cold in his mouth. "And the god stopped whispering directions an hour ago. It started whispering warnings instead."

They climbed.

The stair delivered them, near dusk, onto a shelf of granite under a sky the color of a healing bruise. Below, spread along the valley floor like spilled ink, torches. Hundreds. The vanguard of Vessar's army, moving on Hollowgate—moving on the gate whose keys hung, at this moment, uselessly in Ada's pocket, whose warding was failing because its keeper had abandoned his post.

Refugees were on the road too, tiny in the failing light, streaming east ahead of the soldiers. Some would make the gate in time. Some wouldn't. And all of it—the timing, the route, the army at his door—traced back to a nameless tunnel mouth and six patient riders, and someone in Corvale who had drawn them a map.

Sefa stood beside him, following his gaze down at the torches, and when she spoke her voice was stripped of everything sharp.

"Whoever sold us," she said, "sold a lot more than us."

Below, at the head of the column, a banner caught the last light: the crowned thorn of House Vessar, riding straight for the gate.

And on Ada's palm, unasked, frost wrote a single word:

*Hurry.*
