## 9. The Free Cities Burn Their Candles

Pell had stolen his first candle at six years old, and he'd stolen a hundred since, but he had never seen anything like Corvale burning them.

They were everywhere. In the windows of the chandler's row, in the hands of beggars and bankers alike, in iron brackets bolted to the harbor pilings so the whole waterfront glowed like a festival that had forgotten what it was celebrating. White candles, tallow candles, one enormous pillar of wax taller than Pell himself standing in the square before the Council Hall, its flame as big as a bonfire. People pressed their palms to it—not close enough to burn, just close enough to feel the heat—and whispered.

"What are they doing?" he asked Sefa.

"Praying." She said it like a curse word. "Old way. You light a candle for something you've lost or something you want back. Haven't seen it done proper since I was a girl." Her eyes moved along the crowded street, cataloguing exits the way she always did, and her jaw was tight. "It means they're scared."

The city smelled of hot wax and salt and fear. Above the rooftops, on the headland where the citadel stood, signal fires burned in a line—three of them, then four while Pell watched, each new flame answered by a distant flicker from the next hill eastward. The chain of beacons ran all the way down the coast toward Vessar's territory, carrying news faster than any horse: *he is coming, he is coming, he is coming.*

* * *

Sefa had business with a fence named Orla who operated out of a dye-house that reeked of fermented indigo, so she took Dov and left Pell to run the resupply list with Yara. He suspected this was a test. Everything Sefa did lately felt like a test, ever since the ambush on the smugglers' road, ever since Pell had thrown a rock at an outrider's helmet instead of running like she'd told him to. She hadn't praised him for it. She'd grabbed his collar afterward and said, *Next time you run,* and her voice had cracked in the middle of the sentence, which had frightened him more than the soldiers had.

So now he walked through Corvale with Yara and the supply list, trying very hard to be useful.

Yara walked like someone expecting to be recognized. Hood up, shoulders hunched, though half the city wore hoods against the sea wind. She kept her right hand fisted closed—the hand with the sigil burned into the palm—and when people brushed past her in the crowd, she flinched like they might see straight through her skin.

"The candles," Pell said, because silence made him itch. "Is it true? That the old gods answer them?"

"They used to." Yara's voice was flat. "Before the locks. Before my order sealed them away and called it mercy." She stopped at a shrine.

It was small—a niche in a wall near the fish market, older than every building around it, carved with seven interlocking rings worn nearly smooth by centuries of touching thumbs. Someone had set three candles inside it. Fresh ones, the wax still soft, the flames steady in the windless alcove.

Yara stared at it for a long time. Then she reached out, almost without seeming to decide to, and touched her thumb to the lowest ring.

The candle flames all leaned toward her at once, like flowers turning to sun.

She snatched her hand back. Nobody else seemed to notice. The market roared on around them—fishwives shouting prices, gulls screaming, a child crying about eels—but Pell had seen it, and Yara knew he'd seen it, and for a moment they just looked at each other.

"Don't tell Ada," she said.

"He'd want to know."

"He'd want to hope." She pulled her hood lower. "Hope's heavier than you think, boy. It breaks backs."

* * *

They heard the Council debate because there was nowhere in Corvale you could avoid hearing it. Speakers had been set up on the steps of the Hall, and criers carried the arguments out into the streets like vendors selling disaster by the pound.

*"The free cities stand together—"*

*"Together we burn! Alone we bargain!"*

*"Vessar asks only recognition of his crown—"*

*"He asked the same of Vellin. Vellin has no harbor now."*

Pell climbed onto a barrel to see over the crowd. On the steps, robed councilors argued beneath the great candle-pillar, and above them hung the banner of the Concord—the linked-hands sigil of the five free cities, faded from forty years without a war worth flying it for.

An old man beside Pell was explaining to anyone who'd listen: "My grandfather saw the last king try to take the passes. You know what stopped him? Not armies. The mountain did. The old gods did. One night of frost and his whole vanguard turned back frozen stiff in their saddles." He shook his head. "People forget what's sleeping under Thornfell. Maybe that's the problem. Maybe somebody woke it up wrong."

Pell's stomach went cold. *Somebody.* Ada had said the god spoke his childhood name. Ada had said the seventh lock was cracking. What if it wasn't Vessar waking things up wrong? What if it was them?

He found Yara at the edge of the crowd, listening with her whole body, the way she listened to everything—as if words were debts being called in.

"They're going to surrender," she said quietly. "Two cities already have. Look at the banners." And he looked: among the Concord's linked hands, here and there, flew the black thorn-crown of House Vessar, hung from windows like apologies. "Corvale's debating terms tonight. If they kneel, the others follow. And then there's no free cities for us to come back to. No gate worth holding."

"But we're getting Lira," Pell said. "That's still—that matters, doesn't it? Even if—"

"It matters." Yara said it fast, fierce, and then seemed surprised at herself. She looked down at her bandaged palm. "It's the only thing that's mattered in twenty years. Don't let me talk you out of it."

That was when the mob found the shrine.

* * *

It started with shouting at the far end of the fish market. A knot of men pushing through the stalls, maybe thirty of them, armed mostly with boat hooks and torches, led by a broad-shouldered man in a dyed red sash—one of the Concord militia, by the colors, though no officer marched with him. They moved with the ugly purposeful drift of a crowd that had been arguing about surrender all day and needed somewhere to put the fear.

"There!" someone shouted. "There's another one!"

They meant the shrine. The little niche with the seven rings and the three candles.

A woman was kneeling before it. Old, wrapped in gray wool, feeding another candle into the bracket with shaking hands. She must have been deaf to the mob's approach, or too deep in prayer to hear it, because she didn't move until the first torchlight fell across her face.

"You shouldn't light these," the red-sashed man said. His voice carried, pitched for the crowd. "You know what the priests say. Every candle you burn for the buried gods is a door you crack open. You want what's under Thornfell loose in your streets? You want your children taken like in the old stories?"

"They never took children," the old woman said. Her voice was thin but it didn't waver. "They *kept* what was given. There's a difference, boy. My mother taught me the difference."

"Your mother's dead and her gods are dead with her." He gestured, and two men wrenched the candles from the bracket and ground them underfoot. Wax smeared across the seven rings like spilled fat. "Council's voting surrender tonight. Last thing we need is the king thinking Corvale's full of god-worshippers giving him excuses."

The old woman knelt again and began gathering the broken candles, cradling them like injured birds.

And Pell saw Yara step forward.

"No," Pell breathed, grabbing for her sleeve. "Yara—"

She was already moving. She walked into the open space between the old woman and the mob with her hood thrown back and her chin up, and everything about her was different—straighter, louder, lit from inside. She looked like a priestess. Pell had never once thought of her that way, and now he couldn't think of her any other way.

"That shrine is older than your Council," Yara said, and her voice rang off the market walls. "Older than your charter, older than your king's grandfather's grandfather. Those rings are a promise, not a door. Your ancestors cut them into the stone so no one would ever forget what was paid to keep the world whole. And you're scraping wax over it because you're afraid."

"Afraid keeps us alive," the red-sash man said.

"Afraid makes you kneel early." She raised her right hand, the bandaged one, and tore the wrapping away.

The sigil blazed on her palm. Seven interlocked rings, glowing like coals under skin, throwing light up across her face. The crowd made a sound like a wave sucking back from shore. Even the torches guttered.

"Defrocked," Yara said, letting them see it, letting them see *her*, all of her, the drunk and the exile and the last of her order. "Struck from the rolls. My own faith threw me out. But the gods haven't." She closed her fist and the light leaked between her fingers. "You want to surrender to Vessar? Kneel. But don't you dare put out these candles and call it peace. Some doors should stay shut. This isn't one of them."

For a heartbeat, nobody moved. The old woman looked up at Yara with wet eyes. The red-sashed man's jaw worked. Behind him, the crowd shifted—Pell could feel it turning, the way mobs turn, unpredictable as weather.

Then a stone came out of the crowd.

It missed Yara's head by a hand's width and cracked against the wall above the shrine. And the mob broke forward all at once, and the market became a riot in the space of three heartbeats.

* * *

Later, Pell could never piece the order of events together properly. Memory gave it to him in flashes, the way nightmares do.

Yara shoving the old woman behind her. The red-sashed man swinging a boat hook and Sefa appearing out of nowhere—she'd been drawn by the noise, or by instinct, or by whatever smuggler sense told her trouble was profitable—to catch the shaft on her forearm and drive her knee into his gut. Torches scattering. A stall going over, dried cod sliding everywhere underfoot. Dov moving through the chaos like a boulder through a stream, silent, immovable, putting his wide back between the mob and the shrine while Yara dragged the old woman clear.

And Pell himself, doing the thing he was best at. Slipping through legs and elbows, small hands finding purses and knife-sheaths and the drawstrings of torch-oil flasks, unburdening the mob one thief at a time. He cut a belt. He dumped a quiver of bolts into the harbor. He lifted a militiaman's whistle right off his neck and blew it in three sharp blasts—the watch signal for *fire in the granary quarter*—and half the mob peeled away to go loot a fire that didn't exist.

By the time the real watch arrived, blowing whistles of their own, the five of them were three rooftops away, breathless, in the shadow of a chimney stack, watching the crowd below disperse into sullen knots.

The old woman had gotten away. So had the shrine—scorched, wax-smeared, but standing. And in the dark of the niche, impossibly, one candle flame still burned. Small and stubborn. It had not been lit by any hand Pell had seen.

Nobody said anything about that.

* * *

They sat on the rooftop while Corvale argued itself hoarse below. Somewhere in the Council Hall, the vote on surrender was happening right now; Pell imagined he could feel the city holding its breath.

Sefa counted the takings from Pell's impromptu looting—two purses, a silver hairpin, a knife with a good edge—and pushed most of it back into his hands. "Resupply money. You've got quick fingers when you're not being stupid."

"That's the nicest thing you've ever said to me."

"Don't get used to it." But she was looking down at the market, at the scattered torches and the trampled fish, and her voice lost its edge. "Twenty years I've worked these cities. Bribed their guards, dodged their taxes, laughed at their councils. Never cared who sat in the Hall. Kings, concords, whatever—it's all the same tollbooth with different paint." She turned the silver hairpin over in her fingers. "Tonight some idiot with a sash tried to smash an old woman's candles, and I broke his ribs for it, and I don't even know why I care."

"I know why," Pell said.

Everyone looked at him. He hadn't meant to speak. He picked at the mortar between the roof tiles, gathering the thought the way he gathered coins—quickly, before anyone noticed.

"It's not about the vote. Or Vessar. It's—" He pointed down at the shrine, at the small stubborn flame. "That old lady wasn't praying for the war to end. She was praying for something she lost. Everybody down there with a candle, they're all praying for something they lost. And Vessar wants to take the rest." He shrugged, embarrassed. "Ada's sister is down there under the mountain. Somebody took her. That's what we're actually fighting. Not the king. The taking."

Silence on the rooftop. Dov, who never spoke, reached over and put one huge hand briefly on Pell's head, ruffling his hair like a brother might. Yara was rewrapping her palm, and when she spoke, her voice was rough.

"When did you get wise?"

"I'm a pickpocket. I know what people carry." He grinned, and it mostly held. "Everybody's carrying something they can't put down."

Below them, the great doors of the Council Hall opened. Light spilled onto the steps, and a crier's voice rose over the city, cracked with exhaustion:

*"The Concord of Corvale votes—no surrender! The gates close at dawn! By order of the Council, every soul able to bear arms—"*

The rest was drowned in cheering and screaming, both at once, joy and terror wearing the same face. Beacon fires leapt up along the headland, passing the word east: *Corvale stands. Corvale stands.*

Sefa stood up. "We leave tonight. Now, before they seal the gates and start conscripting everyone who can hold a spear—including smart-mouthed boys and defrocked priestesses." She was already moving to the roof ladder. "Supplies are bought. We've got what we came for."

"We have more than that," Yara said quietly. She looked at her palm, at the sigil glowing faintly through the fresh wrapping, brighter than Pell had ever seen it. "The gods are answering. All across the coast, if those candles mean anything. They're answering, and they're getting stronger, and Vessar knows it—that's why he's marching now." She met Ada's absent gaze in some middle distance only she could see. "We have to reach the court before he does. Before the seventh lock gives. Because if it cracks on its own, with no one down there to speak for the living—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Pell had heard enough whispers on the road to fill in the shape of it: seven souls for seven locks, six names carved in stone, and the seventh scraped away. Something under Thornfell was already awake. Something was already counting.

They dropped from the rooftops into the dark and made for the north road, five shadows moving against the tide of a city lighting its candles—and far behind them, at the edge of hearing, Pell would have sworn the harbor bells rang once with no hand on the rope.
