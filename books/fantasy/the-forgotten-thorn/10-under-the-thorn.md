## 10. Under the Thorn

Stone first.

Always stone first. Before names, before fire, before the soft wet noise of people breathing in the dark — stone. Dov put his palm flat against it and let it speak the way it had spoken to his mother's mother's mother, back when the clan still had a name to carve.

*Cold. Old. Awake.*

He signed it anyway, out of habit, though no one behind him read hands. *We go down. The stone knows us.*

Sefa's torch guttered and spat. "You're sure this is a way through," she said, in the tone that wasn't a question but wanted to be answered like one.

Dov looked at her. Looked at the crack in the mountain's flank, barely wide enough for Pell's narrow shoulders, weeping a thread of water that smelled of iron and deep places. He shrugged. Then he turned sideways and went in, because shrugging was easier than explaining that his people had dug these veins before Thornfell had a king, before it had a gate, before anyone thought to write down what was traded beneath it.

The others followed. He heard them: Ada's measured breath, Yara's boots scraping, Pell's quick frightened patter, then steadying, then quick again. Five heartbeats in the dark. Six with his own.

Seven, if you counted the one the mountain kept.

* * *

The tunnel widened by degrees, the way tunnels did when they had been made on purpose. Dov ran his fingers along the walls as he walked, reading. Rough-hewn here — the old work, pick-marks like bird tracks. Then smoother. Then, three hundred paces in, smooth as river ice, and warm.

Warm stone underground was wrong. He stopped. Put both palms flat. Felt the faint pulse under the rock, slow as a sleeping ox, patient as winter.

*It remembers us,* he signed, forgetting again that no one watched. But Ada had come up beside him, close enough that the torchlight caught the lines of his face, and Ada said quietly, "What did you feel?"

Dov held up one hand: *warm*. Then pressed it to his own chest: *alive.*

Ada went very still. "Good," he said, in the voice of a man deciding not to say something else. "Keep going."

They kept going.

* * *

The carvings began where the warmth began.

At first Dov took them for water stains — dark spirals, looping whorls, the kind of marks damp leaves in a stream leave on rock. Then the torch swung and the shadows moved and the spirals became letters, and the letters became rows, and the rows became a wall of writing older than any alphabet Sefa could fence or Yara could pray in.

But Dov knew it. Not the words — those had crumbled out of living mouths two generations ago — but the *shape* of it. The deep-cut certainty of it. His clan had cut letters like this into the support beams of their last free tunnel, the night before the soldiers came and the name was taken from them.

He stood before the wall a long time.

Yara came to stand beside him. She didn't touch the carving. Priests' hands, even defrocked ones, knew better than to grab at holy things. "Can you read it?" she asked.

He shook his head. Then he traced the first line with one finger, slowly, and spoke the only word of the old tongue his grandmother had left him, the word she'd used for the mountain itself:

"Thorn."

The syllable fell into the tunnel and did not echo. The stone took it. Somewhere far below, the pulse under the rock skipped — once — like a sleeper turning toward a familiar voice.

Pell made a small sound. "It heard you."

Dov nodded. *It always hears. That is the problem with mountains.*

* * *

They camped in a chamber shaped like a swallowed bell, ceiling lost in black above, floor worn glass-smooth by feet long dead. Sefa counted out hardtack and dried plums with a miser's precision. Ada sat apart, turning something over in his fingers — a scrap of frost, Dov realized, a little curl of ice that should have melted hours ago and hadn't, glowing faintly like a captive star. Ada stared at it the way men stare at wounds.

Dov ate. Watched. Counted exits, as his blood demanded: two, the way they'd come and a fissure in the far wall breathing cold air upward. Good. A digger always knew his ways out, even from a place with no way out.

Pell crept over and sat cross-legged beside him, close but not touching, the way the boy did everything — wanting warmth, afraid to ask. "Dov." Whispered. "The walls. What are they saying?"

Dov considered how to answer without a voice. In the end he took the boy's hand, turned it palm-up, and drew on it with one blunt finger: seven small circles in a row. Under six of them he scratched six short strokes. Under the seventh he drew nothing at all — just smoothed the skin flat, empty.

Pell frowned. "Six... things? And a hole?"

Dov tapped each circle, then pointed up, toward the surface, toward the world of gates and ledgers and kings. Then he pointed down, twice, deeper.

"Souls," Yara said softly, from across the fire. She had been watching. Defrocked priests watched everything; it was all they had left of the god. "Six souls given, six locks set. It's in the oldest liturgies, the ones my order burned. *Seven souls for seven locks.* The bargain that put the old gods under the mountain." Her mouth twisted. "We were told there were only ever six. That the seventh was a scribe's error, a duplication, a myth."

"And now?" Sefa asked around a plum pit.

"Now we're walking through the proof that it wasn't."

Dov looked at the seventh circle he'd drawn on Pell's palm. Empty. He closed the boy's fingers over it, gently, the way you'd close a hand over an ember.

*Not empty,* he thought. *Scraped.*

He had seen scraped names before. On clan stones, when the soldiers came. You did not erase a name by chiseling it away — chiseling honored it, gave it a wound, a grave. To unmake a name you *scraped* it, polished it smooth, so that the stone forgot it had ever been written. So that even grief had nowhere to hold.

The wall back there had six names cut deep and proud.

And where the seventh should have been, the rock was smooth as ice.

Someone had wanted the mountain itself to forget.

* * *

Sleep came thin and shallow. Dov dreamed the way he always dreamed: in stone.

In the dream he was small again, in the last tunnel, and his grandmother was pressing his hand to a beam and signing into his palm, slow, so he'd feel every letter: *We dig because the mountain lets us. We stay because we keep its secrets. The day we sell one, the day we speak one, the stone will close its ears to us forever.*

In the dream he asked her — children in dreams can ask anything — *what secret was worth seven souls?*

And his grandmother's hands, which had never once trembled in life, trembled in the dream, and she signed: *Ask the ones who bought it.*

He woke with his heart slamming and the fire burned to coals and Ada standing at the mouth of the downward passage, listening.

Dov rose and stood beside him. Listened too.

Far below — so far it might have been his own blood — something was singing. Not words. Not music, exactly. More like the sound a cave makes when it's deciding whether to be a grave or a home.

Ada's jaw was tight. "You hear it too."

Dov nodded.

"It knows we're coming."

Dov considered lying, for the man's comfort. Diggers did not lie; it was the one law left to the nameless. He raised his hand instead and signed, slowly, so Ada would follow: *It has always known. We are the late ones.*

Ada read his hands the way he read everything — carefully, completely, missing nothing. Something moved behind his eyes, some ledger closing. "Then let's not be later still."

* * *

The second day under the thorn, the walls began to bleed light.

Not torchlight. Not the gray moss-glow of deep caves. This light came *out of the stone itself*, veined through it like gold in quartz, pale and memory-colored — and it was not steady. It flickered in rhythms almost like breathing. Almost like *waiting*.

Yara walked with her palm pressed to the wall for twenty paces at a time, and each time she lifted it her face was wetter. "These aren't veins of ore," she said hoarsely. "I know what my order taught me about the locks, I know what the liturgy says the prison looks like from outside. This is—" she stopped, started again. "Souls give off light. Where they're kept. It soaks into whatever holds them."

Pell had stopped walking entirely. He was staring at a patch of glow at eye height, and his young face had gone strange and old. "There's a face in it," he whispered. "There's — Dov, there's people in the walls."

Dov looked. He wished he hadn't. The light was not uniform; it gathered, pooled, and in the pooling were shapes — not bodies, nothing so cruel as bodies, but *presences*, drifting slow as lanterns on a flood, pressing toward the warmth of living flesh the way moths press toward flame. Not attacking. Just coming close. Just *looking*.

One of them drifted near Pell and stayed there, keeping pace beyond the stone, and its light was the color of a specific afternoon, and Pell's breath went ragged. "That's my—" He couldn't finish. Dov put a hand on the boy's shoulder and steered him onward, and the presence drifted after them for a hundred paces, and then, gently, like something remembering manners, let go.

Sefa had gone quiet in the way that cost her most. She walked with her knife drawn and her knuckles white and her eyes fixed forward, refusing the walls, refusing the light, refusing the soft shapes that gathered at the edge of her refusal. "Don't look at them," she said, to no one, to everyone. "Whatever you do. Don't look at them like they're people."

"They *are* people," Yara said.

"That's why don't look."

Dov looked. Someone had to. Someone whose voice had been taken from him already, who knew what it was to be carried in a body the world refused to read. He met the drifting lights as they came, one after another, and gave each one the same thing: a nod. Small. Level. *I see you. You were someone. The stone has not forgotten, even if it was made to.*

And the lights — he would swear this later, would swear it on the only thing he had left, his hands — the lights *bowed*.

* * *

On the third day they reached the gallery of names.

It opened without warning, the passage simply exhaling them into a hall so vast the torches died in it, and the soul-light rising from below was enough to see by — enough to see *everything*, and Dov understood in one sick lurch why the light had grown stronger with every step. They had been walking down the throat of the prison itself. The gallery was its ring. Its collar. The seam where the lock met the door.

Carvings covered every surface. Floor to impossible ceiling, spiraling inward, and the spirals all converged on the far wall, on a single panel as tall as a cathedral door, and Dov's legs carried him toward it because his legs belonged to the stone and the stone was calling.

Seven columns. Seven recessed panels, each the size of a coffin lid.

Six bore names.

He read them the way his grandmother had taught him to read stone — not with eyes but with fingertips, tracing each cut letter while the others stood behind him in a silence that had weight and temperature. The names were in the old tongue, the dead one, the scraped-out one, but the stone translated for him the way it had always translated for his bloodline, pouring meaning up through his palms:

*First given: the shepherd Oren, who loved his flocks more than his years.*

*Second given: the twins Mael and Maren, who asked to go together and were permitted.*

*Third given: the queen's daughter, unnamed here by her mother's mercy.*

*Fourth given: the digger Vash, of the clan without a name, who volunteered so his people would be spared.*

Dov's fingers stopped.

Stopped on the fourth panel. On the mark beneath the name — a spiral crossed by three bars. The sign of the tunnel-clans. *His* sign. Cut into this wall centuries before the soldiers, before the scattering, before the namelessness — cut by hands that had held picks the way his hands held picks, calloused in the same places.

*Vash. Of the clan without a name.*

So the namelessness was older than the soldiers. Older than the shame they'd been handed. His people had paid it here, at the root of the world, and paid their name along with their blood, and no one above ground had ever known, had buried the debt so deep even the debtors' children inherited only the hole where the honor used to be.

Something hot ran down his face. He let it.

"Dov." Ada's voice, very quiet. "What does the fourth one say?"

Dov pressed his whole forearm against the panel, and signed one-handed, haltingly, the signs his grandmother had used for the oldest griefs: *Mine. My people. Paid first among the living clans.*

Behind him, Yara made a sound like a prayer breaking. Sefa swore, low and vicious, at kings and gods and the general unfairness of stone. Pell just said, "Oh," in a small crushed voice, and Dov felt the boy's forehead come to rest between his shoulder blades, briefly, a moth-touch of warmth, and then withdraw.

*Fifth given: the priestess Solenne, who went gladly, believing it a wedding.*

*Sixth given: the child Lira Almeida, sold by contract, sealed by oath, mourned by one.*

Dov heard Ada stop breathing.

He turned. Ada stood before the sixth panel with his hand hovering an inch from the carved letters, not touching, as if touching might wake something or as if he feared the stone would know him — and the frost-scrap he carried was glowing in his other fist like a dying star, and his face was the face of a man reading his own death warrant in a language he'd hoped to forget.

"Lira," he said. Just the name. It fell into the gallery and the soul-light flickered, all of it, every drifting shape in every wall turning at once toward the sound like flowers toward sun.

*Seventh given—*

Dov faced the last panel.

Or the place where it should have been. Because the seventh recess was there, coffin-sized, waiting, its borders cut clean as the others — but its face was smooth. Smooth as river ice. Smooth as a scraped beam in a stolen tunnel. No name. No mark. Not even the ghost of a chisel-stroke. Only stone polished so long and so lovingly that it shone, and in its shine the soul-light gathered and swam, and Dov saw shapes moving in it, many shapes, and none of them would hold still long enough to be a face.

Someone had unmade the seventh name. Not hidden it — *unmade* it, scrubbed it from the memory of the mountain itself, so thoroughly that even the stone that had recorded the giving of six souls could not recall the seventh.

And yet the lock was there. The seventh lock, seated in its frame, whole and hungry and *empty*.

Waiting to be filled.

"The bargain isn't finished," Yara breathed. Her defrocked hands had found one another and were gripping hard, knuckle to knuckle, the way they must have gripped in vestments long ago. "Six paid. Seven owed. That's why the court keeps the souls, that's why the locks hold — the seventh was never—"

She stopped. They all heard it at the same moment.

Below them, past the gallery floor, past the last of the stairs that the light revealed winding down into the shining dark — footsteps. Measured. Unhurried. Armored. Many.

And threaded through the ring of iron, faint but unmistakable, a human voice speaking the old tongue with a court-bred accent, reciting something with the cadence of a contract being read aloud.

Sefa's knife was out. Pell had gone gray. Ada's fist closed around the frost-scrap until the light of it bled between his fingers.

Dov put his palm flat against the scraped seventh panel one last time.

Under the polish, deeper than any chisel had reached, the stone said one thing to him, in the wordless way stone speaks to diggers, and it said it clearly:

*They are coming to pay.*
