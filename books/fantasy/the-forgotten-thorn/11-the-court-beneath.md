## 11. The Court Beneath

The last of Dov's tunnels ended at a wall that was not a wall.

Ada felt it before he saw it—a pressure against his skin like the moment before a storm breaks, the same wrongness he'd known for twenty years in the gate-warding stones at Hollowgate, but older here, deeper, as if the whole mountain were holding its breath. He raised a hand and the others stopped behind him: Sefa with her knives loose in their sheaths, Pell pressed close to her shadow, Yara with her burned palm held against her chest like something she'd been told to carry and not open.

Dov stood beside him, silent as always, and touched the stone.

It gave way like fog.

Not opened—*parted*, folding back from itself in slow ripples, and beyond it there was light where no light should be, three hundred feet beneath the roots of Thornfell, light without fire or sun.

"Gods below," Sefa breathed. "Literally."

"Stay together," Ada said. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "Touch nothing unless I touch it first. And whatever you see down there—" He hesitated. He had meant to say *whatever you see, don't believe it*. But Yara was watching him with those tired, clear eyes, and he heard himself say instead: "Whatever you see, remember it's real. That's worse."

He stepped through.

* * *

The Court Beneath did not look like anything men had built, because men had never built anything to last this long.

They came out onto a ledge of black glass that curved away into vastness, and above them—not sky, never sky—the cavern roof glittered with what Ada first took for stars until he looked closer and understood they were memories. Whole scenes hung suspended in the dark like frost-flowers on a window: a woman laughing in a wheat field, golden and small and impossibly far away; a boy running along a riverbank; an old man's hands folding a paper bird over and over. Fragments of lives that had once been prayed upward through stone, caught and kept.

"They're prayers," Yara said softly. She had come up beside him, and her voice had changed—stripped of its tavern rasp, stripped even of its fear. It was the voice of a girl in a temple, decades ago. "All the prayers ever spoken to them. They didn't just hear us. They *kept* us."

"Is that mercy or hoarding?" Sefa asked.

No one answered, because ahead of them the ledge broadened into a road, and the road was paved with names.

Ada knelt and brushed his fingers across it. The stone was warm, faintly, like a cheek. Carved characters ran beneath his palm in lines so old they had worn soft, and though he could not read the script, he knew—he knew the way you know your own heartbeat—that every name carved here had once been spoken aloud by someone who loved it. The road itself was made of being remembered.

"The bargain," Yara murmured. "Seven souls for seven locks. This is where they walked it."

"Then let's walk it quick," Sefa said. "Places like this, you don't want to give them time to notice you."

But the Court had already noticed. Ada felt it the way he'd felt the old woman's prayer at his gatepost—a turning of attention, vast and slow, like a whale passing beneath a rowboat.

The souls began to drift toward them.

* * *

They came up out of the deep places of the cavern like lanterns released from a pond—points of soft gold light rising through the dark, dozens of them, then hundreds, each one trailing faint ribbons of memory behind it. Ada watched one pass close overhead and inside its glow he saw a harvest festival, complete and entire: fiddles, cider, a girl with flour on her nose. Another carried a deathbed, gentle and dim, an old woman's hand letting go of a younger one.

Pell made a small sound. Ada glanced back and found the boy staring upward, tears running freely down his face, and when Ada followed his gaze he understood: one of the lanterns held a woman singing. Not anyone Pell could have known. A lullaby in a language dead a thousand years. But some part of the boy recognized it anyway, some part of everyone would have, because these were the songs underneath all songs.

"Don't reach for them," Yara said quietly. "They're not lost. They're *kept*. There's a difference, and it matters."

"How can you tell?" Pell whispered.

"Because I spent ten years telling myself my god had abandoned me." Her burned palm flexed open. The sigil on it glowed now, a thin silver line like a vein of ore. "Abandoned things go quiet. These are waiting."

The road descended in long shallow curves, and with every step the air grew warmer and sweeter, thick with the smell of bread baking somewhere and rain on hot stone and cut grass—every good smell Ada had ever known, layered together, which was exactly what made it wrong. Grief smelled like this too, he thought. Grief remembered everything sweet.

Sefa walked with her hand on her knife and her eyes moving constantly, cataloguing exits that probably didn't exist. Dov walked with his palm flat against the name-carved stone the whole way, reading it, or greeting it, Ada couldn't tell. Once, Dov stopped before a stretch of the road and went very still, and Ada saw that among the thousands of worn names, one section had been carved more recently—within a living memory, maybe two—and that the script matched the markings in Dov's ancestral tunnels. Clan names. Tunnel-diggers' names, given freely to pave the gods' road.

Dov touched each one. Then he straightened, nodded once—to the road, or to whoever had carved it—and walked on.

* * *

The Court itself announced its presence with silence.

One moment the cavern hummed with drifting souls and remembered birdsong; the next, all of it drew back and stilled, the lantern-souls hanging motionless in the dark like a congregation, and ahead of them the road opened onto a plain of silver water so still it reflected the memory-stars perfectly, so that they seemed to walk between two skies.

And on the far shore, lit by nothing and needing no light, stood the court of the buried gods.

It was beautiful. That was the first thing, and Ada hated how much it moved him. Towers of pale stone rose in shapes that hurt to follow, spirals that turned both directions at once, arches that framed views of moments rather than places—through one arch he saw spring, actual spring, a hillside of white blossoms trembling in wind. Banners hung from the towers, but the banners were woven from voices; he could hear them faintly, threads of hymn and argument and laughter, the sound of a civilization that had gone on existing in the only way left to it.

And it was wrong. That was the second thing, and it took him longer to name. Everything was beautiful the way a portrait is beautiful—flattened, finished, *kept*. Nothing in the Court grew. Nothing decayed. The blossoms behind the arch never fell. The banners sang the same bars of the same hymn, over and over, lovely as a music box and as trapped.

"It's a museum," Sefa said flatly. "They've been pickled."

"They've been *imprisoned*," Yara said. "There's a difference, and it matters." But her voice shook.

At the near end of the silver water, a bridge of black glass arched across, and halfway across the bridge, waiting, stood a figure Ada would think about for the rest of his life—which, knowing what he now knew about the length of his life, might be a very long time indeed.

* * *

She was seventeen.

She stood with her weight on one hip the way she always had, arms crossed, chin tipped up in that half-defiant, half-amused tilt that had driven their mother to despair and made strangers fall a little in love with her in market squares. Dark hair braided back. A smudge of ash on one cheekbone, because she had never once in her life managed to keep her face clean past noon.

Lira.

Ada stopped walking. The world narrowed to the width of the bridge and the height of a seventeen-year-old girl, and seventy years fell away from him like a coat slipping off his shoulders.

He had been twenty-three when they took her. He had held her hand while their father signed the contract, because someone had to, because she'd asked him to, because she'd been brave enough for both of them and he had been a coward enough to let her be. *It's just a door, Ada,* she'd said. *I'll knock on it and walk through it and it'll be fine.* And then the priests of the new king had sung their sealing-song, and the mountain had swallowed her, and Ada had walked back down out of Thornfell and begun the long business of forgetting how to be seen.

Seventy years. He had guarded a gate for twenty of them and hidden for fifty before that, kept unnaturally young by the warding-magic he'd never asked for, and in all that time he had built himself a life with no windows. And here she was, unchanged, unaged, wearing his sister's face like a lamp still burning in a house everyone else had abandoned.

"She looks—" Pell started.

"Don't," Ada said.

The figure on the bridge tilted her head. And Ada's heart, which had survived seventy years of careful disuse, broke cleanly in half—because the tilt was right, the angle was right, but the eyes were wrong. They were Lira's eyes and they were not. They held the flat sweet depth of the lantern-souls, the kept-ness, the museum light. Whatever stood on the bridge wore his sister the way the Court wore its memories: perfectly, and from outside.

"That's not her," Yara said gently. "That's what the Court remembers of her. The soul is real—it has to be, it's one of the seven—but the shape it wears..." She trailed off. "I'm sorry, Ada."

"I know," Ada said. "I know what it is."

He walked forward anyway. Of course he walked forward. He had crossed half a kingdom and most of a mountain, and there was no force under heaven or beneath it that could have stopped his feet.

The bridge rang faintly under his boots, a note like a struck bell. Behind him he heard Sefa mutter a curse and follow, then Pell, then the soft tread of Dov and the whisper of Yara's robes. His crew. His ridiculous, broken, magnificent crew, following him toward a ghost because he asked them to without asking.

Up close, the thing-that-was-Lira resolved into terrible detail. The ash smudge. A chipped tooth she'd gotten falling out of the pear tree. The exact way her braid frayed at the end because she refused to let anyone redo it. Seventy years of memory, preserved by a grieving mountain, rendered flawless.

"You're the gatekeeper," she said. Her voice was Lira's voice, warm and rough. "You keep the door at the top of the world."

"I do," Ada said. His throat ached around the words.

"My brother was supposed to come find me." She smiled, and the smile was the worst thing yet, because it was kind. "He said he would. Did he?"

The question went through Ada like a spear. He thought of all the years he had not come, all the years he had told himself the bargain was sealed and sacred and irreversible, all the years of ledgers and bribes and looking away. He thought of the lie he had told her at the end—*it's just a door, it'll be fine*—and the lie he had told himself every day since—*there was nothing to be done.*

"He's here now," Ada said.

Something flickered in the borrowed eyes. A hairline crack in the museum glass. The figure frowned—Lira's frown, the suspicious one—and leaned closer, and for one instant the lantern-light behind her face guttered like a candle in wind, and beneath it Ada glimpsed something else: not a memory, but a presence. Something vast and patient and *aware*, looking out through his sister's shape, studying him.

Studying the warding-mark on his hand. The gatekeeper's mark, the one that had kept him young, the one that marked him as the last living signatory of the oldest contract in the world.

"Oh," the figure said, and the voice changed as it spoke, Lira's warmth giving way to something older, layered, many-throated, the sound of the Court itself finding its tongue. "Oh, *you're* the little brother. The one who kept the door."

Behind Ada, Yara's sigil flared silver-bright. The lantern-souls throughout the cavern all turned at once, hundreds of remembered faces orienting toward the bridge like flowers toward a sun.

"We have waited a long time for a keeper to come down," the Court said through his sister's mouth, gently, terribly. "Tell us, gatekeeper. Have you come to free us—or to buy?"

* * *

On the far shore, past the towers of woven voices, a second light appeared. Cold where the souls were warm. White where they were gold.

It came down a stair that had not been there a moment ago, descending from nowhere into the heart of the Court, and Ada knew its color the way he knew the taste of ash. He had seen that white light on Vessar's surveyors' instruments, on the captain's seal, on the warrants nailed to gates across the free cities.

Someone else had found the way down.

And they were not alone.
