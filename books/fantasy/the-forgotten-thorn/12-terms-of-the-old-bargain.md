## 12. Terms of the Old Bargain

Yara had spent eleven years learning how gods went silent. She had stood in a burned temple in Corvale with ash on her tongue and her order's sigil scraped from her wrist, and she had learned that silence was not absence—that the old faith's deepest teaching was that the gods did not leave, they were *taken*, and that prayer was not a request but a knock on a door someone else had locked.

She had never expected to be standing inside the room behind the door.

The Court Beneath opened around them like a cathedral made of held breath. There was no sky, only a vaulted dark so high that the light of the soul-lanterns drifting through it faded before it reached anything like a ceiling. The lanterns themselves—souls, Ada had said, and his voice had cracked on the word like ice on a pond—hung at varying heights, some near enough to touch, some so distant they might have been stars. Each one burned a different color. Each one, when Yara let herself look too long, seemed to burn with a face.

She kept her eyes forward. A priestess who stared at souls became a priestess who wept, and she could not afford to wept. Not yet. Not while there was work.

The floor beneath their feet was not stone. It was *writing*. Vast lines of script flowed and shifted under them like current under ice, characters older than any alphabet Yara had studied in her novitiate, older than the free cities, older perhaps than the mountains themselves. Where she stepped, the writing dimpled, as if recognizing weight.

"Careful where you walk," Sefa murmured beside her. The smuggler's bravado had thinned since the descent; she kept one hand on Pell's shoulder the way a sailor keeps a hand on rigging. "I don't read whatever this is, but I'd bet my boat it reads us."

"It's reading me," Yara said quietly. Because it was. With every step, the script beneath her boots flared faint gold, and she knew—she *knew*, in the place where her dead order still lived despite the defrocking, despite the drink, despite eleven years of telling herself otherwise—that what flared was her sigil, mirrored back at her from the floor of the world. Her palm still bore the mark that had appeared the night the gods first stirred: the seven-pointed seal of the Gatekeepers, burning cold.

The steward was waiting for them.

She supposed it was a steward because there was nothing else to call it. It stood—or rather, it *was positioned*—at the center of a dais that had not been there a moment before, assembled out of the same flowing script, letters climbing over one another to form a shape approximating a body. It was tall and narrow, robed in something that looked like vellum left out in rain, and where a face should have been there was a smooth expanse of parchment across which words surfaced and sank like fish rising to feed.

When it spoke, its voice came from everywhere the writing was.

**"Petitioners."**

Yara stepped forward before Ada could. This was hers. Whatever else had been taken from her—the temple, the title, the god she'd served since childhood—this was the work she had been made for, and no bishop's decree could unmake it.

"We come to redeem a soul," she said. "One of the seven taken under the old bargain. Lira Almeida, sold seventy years past by—" she hesitated, "—by those who had no right."

The parchment face rippled. Words swam up: *terms, terms, terms, sealed, sealed, sealed.*

**"All souls here are sealed by right,"** the steward said. **"The right of contract. Contracts do not concern themselves with rights of the contracting parties. You mistake this court for a court of justice. It is a court of agreement."**

"I know the difference," Yara said. "Believe me. I have lived my whole life under agreements I didn't write." She lifted her marked palm. "But your own law recognizes redemption clauses. Every bargain struck by the old faith carried one. I am the last of the order that witnessed them. My sigil is recognized."

For a long moment the Court was silent except for the drift of souls, and Yara heard—in the silence, underneath it—a sound like a great many people breathing very far away. She refused to think about what that meant.

**"The sigil is recognized,"** the steward said at last. **"Redemption is possible. State the claimant."**

Ada moved up beside her. Up close, she could see what the descent had done to him: he looked less like a gatekeeper now and more like a man standing at the edge of his own grave and finding it familiar. Seventy years old by any honest count, and looking forty, and carrying both numbers in his eyes.

"Lira Almeida is my sister," he said. "I'm the claimant."

**"Claimant identified. Terms follow."**

The script on the floor surged toward them—not threateningly, but with the terrible eagerness of a thing that has waited a long time to be read aloud—and Yara felt the words arrive not in her ears but in her understanding, whole and untranslatable and perfectly clear:

*A soul may be redeemed by an equivalent freely given. Equivalent in kind. Equivalent in weight. Freely given means offered without coercion, without deception, and without reservation. Partial payments are not accepted. Regret after payment does not void the contract. There is no appeal. There has never been an appeal. There will never be an appeal.*

"There it is," Sefa said flatly. "There's always a price."

"There's always a *fair* price," Yara corrected. "That's worse."

*Equivalent in kind.* She turned the phrase over the way she'd once turned scripture, hunting the seam in it. A soul for a soul. That was the plain reading. Someone in their company would have to stay. Or die. The distinction, she suspected, was thinner down here than it was above.

"No," Ada said. He said it fast, the way a man slams a door. "No. Nobody pays but me. I've been doing the arithmetic since the frost spoke my name. I've had seventy extra years my sister never got. If equivalence is the rule, then my life is more than equivalent—it's *overdue*."

"You don't get to decide that alone," Pell said. The boy's voice cracked in the middle, and he pushed through it anyway. "You're not—you don't get to just *decide*—"

"Pell." Ada's voice gentled. "Not now."

"Yes now! We didn't come all this way so you could trade yourself like a—like a toll!" The boy's fists were shaking. Sefa put a hand on his neck, steadying him the way you'd steady a spooked horse, and said nothing, which from Sefa was itself a kind of argument.

Yara watched Ada's face and saw the thing she had seen in a hundred dying penitents: a man who had already signed himself over and was merely waiting for the ink to dry. Twenty years of small sins logged in clean ledgers, and underneath all of it, this—one enormous sin he intended to commit against himself and call it justice.

Before she could speak, the steward's voice cut through them like a blade through wet paper.

**"A competing petition stands before the Court."**

Every soul-lantern in the vast dark flickered at once. Yara felt it against her skin, a shiver passing through the air like the moment before lightning.

**"Envoy of the Crown of Vessar. Present your instrument."**

They turned together.

He had been there the whole time. Yara was certain of it now—certain and sick with it. A man in traveling grays, unremarkable, forgettable, the sort of face you lost within minutes of seeing it, standing at the edge of the dais with the patient stillness of a professional. One of Vessar's surveyors, or something worse than a surveyor. In his hands he held a scroll case of black lacquer, and when he drew out the document inside, the script of the floor rose toward it like a starving thing.

"By authority of King Vessar, rightful sovereign of the Thornfell reaches and all passes thereto," the envoy said. His voice was pleasant. That was the worst part. It was the voice of a man reading a lease. "The Crown petitions the Court for transfer of the seventh lock's power, per the original terms of the bargain, with consideration rendered as follows."

He unrolled the scroll. Names. Hundreds of names, written in a fine clerk's hand, each one annotated with a date and a place. Yara's stomach dropped before her mind caught up.

"He's offering soldiers," she said. "Dead soldiers?"

"Offering *promised* soldiers," the envoy corrected mildly. "Men of the vanguard currently crossing the pass above. Their names are enrolled in this instrument. Upon completion of the rite, their souls become the property of the Court, in satisfaction of the Crown's obligation." He smiled—a small, tidy smile. "The king finds this arrangement economical. The men, I'm told, volunteered."

"They're marching to war," Sefa snarled. "They don't know what they volunteered for."

"The instrument does not require their knowledge. Only their enrollment." The envoy glanced at the scroll. "Consent was given by their officers. The Court will find the signatures valid."

Yara stepped between the envoy and the dais, her marked palm raised, and the sigil burned so hot she smelled her own skin. "This is *abomination*. These are living men!"

"They are conscripts," the envoy agreed pleasantly. "Which is to say, they were never asked about anything. The king asks nothing new of them now. And the Court—" he inclined his head toward the steward, courteous as a merchant closing a sale, "—the Court cares for validity, not virtue. Isn't that so?"

The parchment face rippled. Words surfaced: *valid, valid, valid.*

Yara had spent eleven years believing the old gods were better than the church that had abandoned them. Standing here, she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the Court beneath the mountain was neither good nor evil. It was a *machine*. It ground forward on the fuel it was fed, and it did not care whether the fuel was freely given love or a tyrant's forged ledger. Justice outside any law, she thought. That was what they had descended to find. But there was no justice here at all—only terms, and whoever brought the best ones.

Unless.

Her heart was pounding. Eleven years of drinking had not killed the scholar in her, and the scholar seized on the clause the way a drowning woman seizes a rope. *"Freely given means offered without coercion, without deception, and without reservation."*

"The soldiers' consent is coerced," she said loudly. "They serve under penalty of death. Enrollment by their officers is deception—they believe they march to battle, not to soul-surrender. By the Court's own definition, the consideration is invalid."

The floor-script stilled. For the first time, the steward's attention—vast, indifferent, ancient—seemed to focus entirely on her, and Yara felt it like a hand pressing down on the crown of her head.

**"The petitioner cites the redemption clause."**

"I cite *all* the clauses," Yara said. "I was trained in them. My order wrote half of them. Check your instruments. Check the founding contracts. The Gatekeepers' seal carries interpretive authority—I invoke it."

The silence stretched. Somewhere far above, muffled by leagues of stone, something boomed—once, twice. Dov, at the rear of their company, went rigid and signed something quick and urgent with his hands. Pell translated in a whisper: "The tunnels are shaking. Something's coming down the mountain."

Vessar's army. Reaching Hollowgate. Reaching the door Ada had left behind him.

The envoy's pleasant expression did not change, but something behind it did—a recalculation, swift and cold. "The Court will verify the instruments," he said smoothly. "Verification takes time. Time favors patience."

"Time favors whoever's army arrives first," Sefa muttered.

**"Verification commences,"** the steward said. **"During verification, all petitions are suspended. Petitioners will remain."**

"Remain?" Ada's head came up sharply. "Above, my gate—"

**"Remain,"** the steward repeated, and the word settled over them like snowfall, like a sentence, like a door closing. The script beneath their feet brightened into a ring that enclosed the whole company, gentle and absolute as a cage drawn in light.

Yara looked at the ring. At the envoy, already seating himself on the dais steps with the composure of a man prepared to wait out a siege. At Ada, whose hands had gone white around the strap of his pack, whose eyes were fixed upward toward a gate twenty miles and half a mountain away, where refugees were running and soldiers were marching and everything he had spent twenty years guarding was coming apart without him.

Then she looked at her own palm, where the sigil of her dead order burned steady and gold.

They had come seeking justice and found a courtroom instead, and the courtroom did not care who was right—only who could pay, and whether the payment was clean. Vessar's offer was dirty. Hers—*theirs*—would have to be cleaner. And the only clean currency in this entire dark country was the one thing none of them wanted to spend.

*Equivalent in kind. Freely given.*

She caught Ada's eye across the ring of light. He was already doing the arithmetic again. She could see it moving behind his face like water under ice.

"Not you," she said quietly. "We'll find another way."

"There isn't another way," Ada said. "There's only who pays."

And far above them, muffled and rhythmic now, the mountain began to drum with the footsteps of an army.
