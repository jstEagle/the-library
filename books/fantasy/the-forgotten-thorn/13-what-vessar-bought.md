## 13. What Vessar Bought

The courier had been dead for two days when Sefa found him, and he'd been a fool for at least twice that long.

She knew it the moment she saw him lying in the gully below the Widow's Stair—a royal courier's grey-and-gold riding coat on a man traveling alone through smugglers' country, no escort, no decoy, nothing but a fat leather dispatch case chained to his wrist like he thought the mountains cared about chains. The crows had found him first. They always did. They were honest that way.

"Amateurs," she muttered, crouching over him. "Vessar sends amateurs into Thornfell."

Dov stood a few paces off with his arms crossed, saying nothing, which was how Dov said most things. His dark eyes went from the body to Sefa and back, patient as stone. He'd been the one to spot the rider from the ridge, three hours ago, weaving down the goat track where no legitimate traveler had any business being. Pell had wanted to go down immediately—*I could've picked his pockets before he hit the ground*—but Sefa had made them all wait in the rocks and watch, because the first rule of her trade was that anything worth taking was worth studying first.

The second rule was that dead men told the best secrets.

"Keys," she said, holding out her hand without looking up. Pell produced the courier's ring of them, grinning, having lifted them during the brief, undignified struggle that had ended the man's career as an obstacle. Sefa worked the chain lock free of the swollen wrist, tried not to think about how recently it had been a living one, and opened the case.

Inside: oilskin packets, wax-sealed, maybe thirty letters. She cracked the nearest one and read by the flat grey light of the overcast afternoon, and the longer she read, the stiller she became.

"Sefa?" Pell's voice, too loud for the gully. "What is it?"

She didn't answer. She read the letter again, then a third time, because the first two times she'd been certain she was misreading it.

*His Majesty's pleasure is that the survey continue without pause. The seventh seal is confirmed beneath the Thornfell massif. Note well: His Majesty does not seek the gods' liberation. His Majesty seeks their subjugation. The lock is not a door to be opened but an instrument to be worn. Once bound to the crown, the old powers will answer to the throne as hounds answer to the hand that feeds them.*

"Hounds," Sefa said aloud. Her voice came out wrong, scraped thin. "He wants to put the mountain on a leash."

Dov crossed to her side and read over her shoulder. He couldn't read—not more than road signs and price marks—but he didn't need to. He watched her face instead, and whatever he saw there made him go very quiet, quieter than usual, which for Dov was saying something.

She read on. Letter after letter, the same clerk's neat hand, the same cold arithmetic. Vessar's surveyors weren't charting passes for armies. Or rather—they were, but that was the small ambition, the visible one. The army marching on the free cities was leverage, a fire lit under the world so that everyone would be looking at the flames while the king's men did the real work below. The correspondence spoke of *the descent party*, of *the envoy's terms*, of *payment rendered to the court beneath*. Payment. As if souls were coin. As if there were enough souls in any army to buy what the Court kept locked away—and then she found the letter that said yes, there were, that the envoy had offered exactly that, a tithe of the conquered, ten thousand names from the free cities' rolls once the cities fell, freely surrendered by treaty or taken by force, and either way *freely given* in the only sense the steward beneath the mountain would recognize.

Sefa had been a smuggler for nineteen years. She had run salt past taxmen and children past slavers and once, in a winter she didn't talk about, plague medicine past a quarantine line because the quarantine was a lie and people were dying. She had seen greed in every shape it came in. But this—this was a man who looked at the oldest prison in the world and didn't see suffering to be ended or power to be feared. He saw a collar. He saw himself holding the leash.

"He doesn't want to wake them," she said. Her hands had started shaking somewhere around the fourth letter, and she let them. There was no one here to see weakness except family, and family got the truth. "Ada thinks—Yara thinks—we're racing him to free the gods before he can use them. That's not it. He wants them awake. He wants them *bound*. Seven locks, seven gods, and he means to wear the last one like a crown and make the other six heel." She looked up at Dov. "Your tunnels. Your clan's carvings. Seven souls for seven locks. You know what happens if he gets the seventh?"

Dov's jaw tightened. He signed something fast and sharp, a flurry of hands, and Pell translated from where she sat on a rock, her grin long gone.

"He says the locks aren't just doors. They're the gods' true names, tied up in knots. Whoever holds a knot holds the name. Holds the name—" Pell swallowed. "Holds the god."

"Holds the god," Sefa repeated. "And Vessar's envoy is already down there making the deal. While we're sitting in a gully reading dead men's mail."

* * *

They burned nothing and buried nothing; there wasn't time for decency. Sefa took the dispatch case, took the courier's boots because hers were falling apart, and took one last look at his face—young, maybe twenty-five, a farm boy's cheekbones under the crow's work—and felt nothing she could use. Somewhere in Corvale a mother would wait for a son who'd stopped writing. That was the war arriving, she supposed. It arrived one missing person at a time until it arrived all at once.

They climbed back toward the hollow where they'd left Yara and Ada. The smugglers' road wound up through black pine and fog, and Sefa walked it fast, angry, the case banging against her hip. Behind her, Pell kept pace with the tireless, irritating energy of the young, and Dov moved like water over stone, silent even uphill.

It was Pell who finally asked it. "If the envoy's already offering Vessar's bargain—the souls, the army-tithe—what do we have? What can we possibly give the steward that beats ten thousand souls?"

"Nothing," Sefa said. "We give them something better than quantity. We give them quality." She didn't know if that was true. She hoped it was. Hope was the currency of fools and smugglers, and she'd been rich in it her whole life.

Ada was waiting at the hollow's mouth, and the sight of him stopped her mid-stride, because Ada Almeida never waited anywhere. Ada occupied space the way furniture did—present, useful, unremarked. But now he was standing in the fog with his shoulders squared and his eyes fixed on the pass below, and he looked less like furniture and more like a drawn blade someone had mistaken for a walking stick for twenty years.

"You've heard it too," Sefa said.

"The mountain's been talking all morning." His voice was level. Too level. "Stones humming. The stream by the lower ford running backward against the grade. Yara says the gods are waking whether anyone frees them or not—the lock's cracking on its own, and everything under Thornfell is getting louder." He turned. "What did the courier carry?"

She handed him the case. "Read the third packet first. Then the ninth. Then I want you to tell me about the pass."

Ada read fast—he was the only one of them who read fast, another gift of his unnaturally long gate-warding years—and Sefa watched his face do what faces did when a man learned the size of the thing hunting him. First the frown of concentration. Then the stillness. Then, worst of all, the calm, because Ada went calm the way other men went violent.

"The vanguard," he said quietly. "How many?"

"Letters say three thousand through the pass by week's end. The main host behind them." She paused. "Ada. Your gate. You left it—"

"I know what I left." The words came out flat as slate. "I barred it from the inside and walked away. Twenty years I kept that door, and I told myself it mattered, that keeping the ledger clean and the passage quiet was its own kind of virtue. And the moment I leave, it stands open for the first army that wants it." He laughed, and there was nothing funny in it. "No. Not open. Worse. Open *because* I left. The warding was me, Sefa. All those years, I was the lock. And I unlocked myself."

Pell shifted uncomfortably. "You couldn't have known—"

"I could have known anything if I'd ever once looked up from my ledgers." Ada closed the case. "Three thousand soldiers crossing Hollowgate. Refugees ahead of them, if they're lucky, and if they're not—" He stopped. Sefa watched him wrestle with it, the guilt and the fury tangling together until she couldn't tell which was driving. "Vessar counted on this. On me being small. On the gatekeeper who takes bribes and keeps his head down. My whole life of careful invisibility, and it turns out I was building his road for him."

"Don't do that," Sefa snapped.

Ada blinked. In twenty-odd years of their uneven alliance, she could count on one hand the times she'd raised her voice at him.

"Don't you dare do that," she said again, stepping closer. "You don't get to take Vessar's sins onto your own books. That man bought an army with other people's souls. He sent an envoy to purchase ten thousand lives from a court of hungry ghosts, and you're standing here acting like the crime is that you failed to personally stand in front of three thousand soldiers with your little iron key. I've spent nineteen years moving contraband under the noses of men like Vessar, and I'll tell you what I know: the guilty love it when the decent ones feel responsible. It's how they win. Guilt makes you slow, Ada. Fury makes you fast. Choose."

For a long moment nobody spoke. Fog drifted through the hollow. Somewhere below, faint as a heartbeat, the stones hummed.

Then Ada smiled—a small, crooked, real thing, the first she'd seen on him since the descent began. "Nineteen years," he said. "And you've never once told me what to do."

"Somebody has to. You're terrible at it." She jerked her chin at the case in his hands. "So. We know what he bought. We know what he's paying. Which means we know exactly what we're selling against, and I've spent my whole life beating richer buyers to a deal. We go down faster. We go down meaner. And when the steward asks us what we offer—" she held up the courier's letters, "—we offer him the one thing Vessar's gold can't buy."

"Which is?"

"A choice made by people who aren't spending anyone else." She turned to the others. Yara had emerged from the tents, pale and grim, sigil-burn still shadowing her palm; Dov stood ready, rope coiled over one shoulder; Pell was already kicking dirt over their cold fire. "Break camp. We move tonight, by the deep road. Dov—I need your tunnels, the ones your clan sealed. All of them."

Dov met her eyes. Something passed across his face—fear, and under the fear, a fierce and ancient pride. He signed, short and decisive.

Pell translated, voice steady: "He says the sealed ways remember his family's name. He says it's time they remembered why."

* * *

They descended as the light died, single file down a crack in the mountain that no map had ever carried, and above them—far above, invisible, unstoppable—the vanguard of King Vessar's army crossed the pass at Hollowgate where a gatekeeper used to sit with his ledgers and his lamp.

Sefa felt it happen. She couldn't have said how—some shift in the mountain's weight, some groan in the stone too deep for ears, or maybe just the old gods' attention rolling overhead like storm-front, following the soldiers' march with something that was not yet anger but was learning the shape of it. Pell stumbled and caught herself against the tunnel wall. Yara whispered a word in a dead language. Even Dov paused, head cocked, listening to the bones of the earth.

"They're through," Ada said softly, behind her. Not a question.

"Yeah," Sefa said. "They're through."

She kept climbing down into the dark, toward the Court beneath, carrying a dead man's letters and a live woman's fury, and she made herself one promise in the black: whatever Vessar had bought, whatever price he'd named, she would be there when the bill came due—and she intended to be the one holding the pen.

Below them, far below, something in the mountain began, very faintly, to sing.
