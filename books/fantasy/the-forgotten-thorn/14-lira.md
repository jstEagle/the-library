## 14. Lira

The lanterns did not hang. That was the first thing Ada understood, standing at the edge of the Court's great hall of memories: they *drifted*, each soul a soft-glowing vessel suspended in air as though the room itself were water and the dead had simply learned to float. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Some burned white and steady, some flickered like candles in a draft, some pulsed slow as sleeping hearts.

The light they gave was not the light of fire. It was the light of *remembering*—warm where it touched the walls, and the walls answered it, showing things that had happened long ago and far away: a wedding on a hillside, a funeral in the rain, a woman kneading bread while her children argued underfoot. The Court beneath Thornfell was lit by everything its prisoners had ever loved.

Ada walked among them and tried not to look at any of them too closely.

Behind him, Yara kept pace, her palm still faintly marked with the sigil that had burned itself there in Corvale. Sefa ranged ahead with Pell, the boy's eyes enormous, his usual patter strangled to silence by the sheer weight of the place. Dov had stayed at the tunnel mouth—someone had to hold their way open, and Dov held things the way mountains held things—but his absence felt like a hand released from Ada's sleeve.

*Seventy years,* he thought. *She has been here seventy years.*

He had done the arithmetic a hundred times since the god in the frost had spoken Lira's name. Seventy years since their mother had carried a child down into the dark and traded her for a bargain that kept the old gods locked away and the world above safe. Seventy years since Ada—nine years old, hiding behind the woodpile, told only that his sister had gone to live with their aunt in the lowlands—had watched the mountain swallow his family whole.

He was fifty-one years old. He looked thirty. The gate-warding magic had seen to that, knitting him to Hollowgate so thoroughly that time had begun to treat him as furniture rather than flesh. Twenty years as keeper. Before that, other keepers, other names, all of them men who had learned to be invisible. He had thought himself patient. He had thought himself good at waiting.

He had never once, in all those decades, let himself wonder what Lira would look like now.

Because he knew. He had known since the frost spoke her name, and knowing had been a knife he carried sheathed against his ribs through every mile of the descent.

Souls here did not age.

"Keeper." Yara's voice was low. She nodded toward the far end of the hall, where the drift of lanterns thinned and the memory-light fell softer, pooled like lamplight in a sickroom. "There. Do you feel it?"

He did. He had felt it since they entered—a pull, gentle and insistent, like a hand tugging his coat hem. Not the gate-magic he had lived inside for twenty years. Something older. Something that knew his blood.

A single lantern hung apart from the others, drifting in a slow orbit around nothing, around a space shaped exactly like a girl.

Ada stopped walking.

His legs simply declined to continue, and for a moment he stood in the middle of the Court of the buried gods, a man who had lied beautifully to a king's captain and stared down smugglers and outlasted forty winters at the coldest pass in the world, unable to take one more step.

Yara said nothing. She only put her hand briefly on his shoulder—the sigil flaring warm through his cloak—and then withdrew, giving him the road.

He took the step.

Then another. Then he was close enough to see it clearly, and every prepared word he had carried down the mountain dissolved like breath on glass.

The lantern was glass, or something like glass—blown smooth, unmarked, its surface holding the drifting light the way a cup holds water. And inside—

Inside was a girl of seventeen.

Not a portrait of a girl. Not a memory of one. A soul, whole and luminous, wearing the shape it had worn when it was taken, and Ada knew that shape the way he knew his own hands. The stubborn set of the jaw. The cowlick at the crown that their mother had fought every morning with a wet comb and lost every morning without regret. The small scar on the left eyebrow from the fence rail she had sworn, weeping with laughter, she had *definitely* cleared.

"Lira," he said.

It came out as almost nothing. A shape of breath.

The lantern brightened.

He felt it happen—not saw, *felt*, the way you feel someone turn toward you in a dark room—and the light inside the glass gathered, quickened, and leaned toward him like a face coming close over candleflame.

And then the Court gave her a voice.

It did not come from the lantern, exactly. It came from everywhere and nowhere, the way the god's voice had come through the frost at his gatepost—wind through stone, memory given sound. But it was *hers*. Seventeen years old and bright and slightly impatient, the voice of a girl who had once talked her way past a toll collector by inventing a tax on left shoes.

"You're new," she said. "You're not one of the stewards. You're—" A pause, and he could hear her puzzling, the light inside the glass turning slowly, considering him. "You feel like the gate."

"The gate," Ada managed.

"Hollowgate." The name came out of her with such simple warmth that his chest cracked along a seam he had kept sealed for seventy years. "I remember the gate. I remember—" The light pulsed, searching. "I remember standing at the post. The tall one, the crooked one, with the iron band. Someone was holding my hand. It was cold. Snow, maybe. And there was singing. Mother was singing. And then—"

The light dimmed, flickering like a candle guttering.

"And then nothing," Lira said. "Just this. Just the lights. They're kind here, mostly. The ones who've been here longest don't talk anymore, but the newer ones do. We wait. I don't know what we're waiting for, but it doesn't hurt. Nothing hurts here. That's the strange part. You'd think it would hurt."

Ada found that he had knelt. His knees had simply gone, and he was crouched before the lantern like a man before a shrine, which perhaps he was, which perhaps he had always been.

"Lira," he said again, and this time the name carried, and the light steadied.

"Yes."

"I need to tell you something, and I need you to listen to all of it before you answer me. Can you do that? All of it. Even the parts that will hurt."

The light went very still.

"Nothing hurts here," she said again, but slower this time, and he heard the question enter it. "Does it have to?"

"No," Ada said. "You can refuse. You can send me away. I'll go. I swear it on the gate itself—I'll go, and I won't come back, and no one will make you hear anything you don't choose to hear." His voice caught. He let it. He was done with smoothness; he had spent twenty years lying beautifully and it had bought him nothing but a clean ledger and an empty house. "But I can't lie to you twice."

"Twice?" The light turned. "Have you lied to me before?"

"No," Ada said. "But everyone else has. For seventy years."

Silence filled the hall—not empty silence but *attentive* silence, the whole drifting congregation of souls seeming to lean in, the memory-light on the walls dimming respectfully, as if the Court itself knew what was about to be said and wished to give it room.

Behind him, he heard Yara murmur something to Pell—*stay back, stay quiet*—and then even that faded, and it was only Ada and the lantern and the girl inside it.

"My name is Ada Almeida," he said. "I was born at Hollowgate, in the keeper's house below the pass, seventy-nine years ago. I had a mother who sang off-key and pretended not to know it. I had a father who died on the wall in the winter of the wolves. And I had a sister."

The light trembled.

"She was eleven when they took her. No—" He stopped. Corrected himself, because he had promised no lies, not even the comfortable kind. "No. She was taken at seventeen. But I was nine, and I was told she'd gone to live with our aunt in the lowlands, and I believed it, because children believe what the grown people tell them because the alternative is unbearable. I believed it for six years. Until I was fifteen, and I found my mother's ledger."

"Mother kept ledgers?" A ghost of the old brightness, the old wry turn. "Father always said she couldn't count past the number of loaves in the oven."

"She kept this one hidden. Under the floor of the pantry, wrapped in oilcloth. It wasn't a ledger of coin." Ada's hands were shaking. He let them shake. "It was a ledger of debts. Ours. Our family's. Going back generations. And the last entry was in her own hand, and it said: *One soul, freely offered, to seal the seventh lock. The mountain keeps its word. The mountain keeps her.*"

The lantern did not flicker. It burned very, very steadily, the way a person goes still when the ground opens under them.

"There was a bargain," Ada said. "Made before either of us was born, made by kings and priests who are all dust now. The old gods were to be bound beneath Thornfell, and the binding required seven souls, freely given, one for each lock. Six were given by the great houses, who got land and titles for them and wrote the whole thing out of their histories. The seventh—" His throat closed. He forced through it. "The seventh lock was the deepest, and it needed a soul given freely, without payment, and the court beneath chose our family, because our family had kept the gate for a thousand years and the mountain trusted us. And my mother—"

He had rehearsed this part on the road. In the dark, watching Sefa's fire, listening to Pell snore. He had rehearsed it a dozen ways, looking for the version that would be merciful.

There wasn't one. There was only the truth.

"My mother climbed down into the mountain with you," he said, "and she gave you to them. Freely. Because if she didn't, the locks would fail, and the gods would wake unfettered and furious, and the world above would burn. She chose the world over you. Or she told herself she did. She wrote in the ledger that night, at the bottom, in letters so small I needed Yara's lens to read them: *Forgive me. I will wait at the gate until one of us is dust.*"

The light inside the glass had begun, very softly, to shake.

"She waited four years," Ada said. "She stood at the gatepost every day, rain or snow, and she sang the songs you liked, off-key, and then one winter she didn't wake up. And I—" His voice broke entirely, and he let it, kneeling in the hall of the dead beneath the world. "I took her ledger, and I took the keeper's oath, and I spent the next fifty-four years being the most careful, most invisible, most *useless* man at the coldest pass in creation, taking bribes and logging sins and telling myself I was keeping the gate safe, when really I was just keeping myself from ever having to think about you. About what she did. About what I—"

"What did you do?" Lira asked. Very quietly. The wind-through-stone voice gone thin and young.

"Nothing," Ada said. "That's what I did. I did nothing. For fifty-four years. I knew what the ledgers meant and I never opened the floor of the pantry again. I knew the mountain had a door and I never asked what was behind it. I let you be a story I didn't finish reading." He lifted his head and met the light with his eyes, because she deserved a face saying these words, even if the face was wrong, even if it was younger than hers should have been. "My name is Ada Almeida. I'm your brother. I'm fifty-one years old and I look thirty because the gate's magic wouldn't let me age, and I think—some nights I think—it wouldn't let me age because some part of me knew I'd have to come down here someday and say this to your face."

The hall was silent.

The lantern hung motionless in the air between them, and Ada knelt, and waited, and understood with terrible clarity that whatever happened next belonged to her—that after seventy years of being kept, of being a debt and a lock and a line in a hidden ledger, this one thing at least would be her choice.

"You're Ada," she said at last.

"Yes."

"You were *small*." Wonder crept into the voice, cracking it. "You had a—there was a dog. A horrible little dog. It bit Father and Father loved it anyway. You cried when it died. You cried for *days*, and I told you it had gone to live with our aunt in the lowlands, and you believed me, because—"

"Because children believe what the grown people tell them," Ada whispered.

"Because the alternative is unbearable." The light flared, and it was almost a laugh and almost a sob and the Court's strange acoustics carried both. "Oh. Oh, that's cruel. That's *cruel*, that we both—she used the same lie on us both, and it worked on us both, and—" The light turned, rapid, agitated, a girl pacing in a room made of glass. "Is it true? All of it? The locks, the bargain, the—am I a *lock*, Ada? Am I keeping gods underground right now? Is that what I am?"

"You're my sister," Ada said. "That's what you are. What was done to you is another matter, and it was wrong, and I have come down into the dark with three fools and a defrocked priestess to unmake it. But I won't touch that lock unless you tell me to. Do you hear me? The steward can offer his terms and the king can send his armies and the gods themselves can demand their freedom, but nothing happens to you that you don't choose. Not this time. Not ever again."

The light stilled.

For a long moment there was only the soft drift of the other souls around them, the memory-light moving on the walls—a harvest festival somewhere, a ship launching, hands braiding hair—and Ada knelt in the hush and felt seventy years balanced on the head of a pin.

"If I leave," Lira said slowly, "what happens to the locks?"

"They fail," Yara said from behind him, gently. Ada hadn't heard her approach; the priestess moved like a woman accustomed to sacred spaces. She knelt at Ada's side, her marked palm open, showing the sigil. "Or they can be replaced. There are rites. My order was made for them, though we forgot, or were made to forget. A soul freely given can hold a lock as well as a soul stolen. Better. Locks built on theft always crack eventually. Your lock has been cracking for seventy years already, child. The mountain has been humming about it in its sleep."

"So someone else would have to pay," Lira said. "Someone would have to give what I was made to give."

"It's being discussed," Ada said.

"By whom?"

"Everyone," said a third voice, and Sefa dropped into a crouch on his other side with the total lack of ceremony of a woman who had never knelt reverently in her life. Pell hovered behind her, pale, clutching his stolen crowbar like a rosary. "Loudly. The priestess wants to volunteer, which is insane. The big quiet one wants to offer his clan's name, which I didn't know was a currency, but apparently it is. And the kid—" she jerked a thumb at Pell, "—has informed us, repeatedly, that nobody is allowed to die, which is adorable and logistically useless."

"Not him," Pell said, to the lantern, with fierce sincerity. "Whatever the price is. Not him."

Lira's light turned from face to face, and Ada watched her take them in—the smuggler, the boy, the priestess, himself—and he saw something in the glow shift, deepen, gather weight.

"You came down here for me," she said. "All of you. Into the mountain. For a girl you never met."

"Don't undersell it," Sefa said. "We also came for the loot. There's loot, right? There's always loot."

"Sefa," Ada said.

"There's *always* loot."

But her voice was rough, and her eyes were wet, and Ada knew—as he had known since Corvale, since the crew assembled, since the roads remembered them—that none of them had come for loot.

Lira drifted closer. Close enough that the light of her washed over his face, warm as a kitchen, warm as a summer evening at the gate, warm as a hand holding his in the snow.

"Brother," she said, trying the word out, and the hall seemed to hold its breath. "I want to say yes. I want to say it so badly I can feel it in—do I have bones? I can feel it in my bones. But I'm the seventh lock, and locks don't get to want things, and if I say yes and it breaks something—if the gods wake angry, if the world burns because I wanted my *name* back—"

"It won't," Ada said. "And if it might—we'll find another way. I swear it. But Lira, listen to me. You have been paying this family's debts for seventy years. You have paid enough for ten lifetimes. Whatever comes next, the debt stops *here*. Tonight. With me. That's why I came. Not to free the gods. Not to stop the king. To tell my sister that the ledger is closed."

The light trembled once, twice—

And then, from far above, through miles of stone, through the roots of the mountain, came a sound.

Low. Rhythmic. Wrong.

Drums. Or boots. Or both.

Sefa was on her feet instantly, head cocked, and Ada saw the color drain from her face in the lantern-light. "That's not echo," she said. "That's marching. That's an army on the stairs, and there's only one army stupid enough to march down a hole in the world."

"Vessar," Yara breathed, and her sigil flared red.

The steward's chime sounded somewhere deep in the Court, urgent now, no longer courteous. The drifting souls began to stir, agitated, their memory-lights strobing across the walls—battles now, floods, fires, the fears of the dead.

Lira's light pressed close against the glass, against him, and her voice came fast and frightened and absolutely clear:

"Ada. Whatever they're offering him—whatever he's come to buy—don't let him use me. And don't you dare pay for me with yourself. I just found you. I have seventy years of questions and I am *not* spending them on a brother who trades himself away the second things get hard. Do you understand me? Family doesn't spend family."

Above them, the drums grew louder.

And somewhere in the deeps of the Court, the seventh lock began, very softly, to sing.
