## 15. The Price Named Twice

The steward did not have a face. It had a ledger.

Yara understood this within the first minute of standing before it, in the long hall beneath the Court where the walls were made of stacked memory—harvest scenes, wedding feasts, funerals, all of them glowing faintly like coals banked under ash. The creature that presided over the Court's contracts was tall and narrow as a door, its skin the grey of old vellum, and where a face should have been there was only a slow turning of pages, each one inscribed in a hand so fine it looked like frost.

It had listened to their request without interruption. That, at least, was courteous. Everything else about it made Yara's palm itch where the sigil still burned.

"You wish to redeem a soul," the steward said. Its voice came from everywhere in the hall at once, from the walls themselves, from the memories of harvests and weddings. "This is permitted. Redemption is the oldest clause in the oldest contract. But you must understand the terms as they were written, before your kingdoms had names."

"We understand equivalent exchange," Yara said. She kept her voice level. Behind her she could feel Ada standing very still, the way he'd stood still at his gate for twenty years, and she knew—that terrible priest's intuition she'd thought she'd buried with her order—that he had already decided something.

"Equivalent," the steward repeated, and the pages turned. "A soul freely given for a soul taken back. Not offered in desperation. Not traded under threat. Not bargained away by another on the giver's behalf." A pause, and somewhere in the walls a funeral memory dimmed, as if the hall itself were listening harder. "Freely given. The words do more work than you imagine, priestess. I have watched mortals lie to themselves for ten thousand years. They are very bad at it, down here, where the light is honest."

"And if no one gives freely?" Sefa asked from Yara's left. Her arms were crossed, her knives hidden but present the way they always were, an argument waiting to happen. "Then what? You keep her?"

"The soul remains. As it has remained for seventy years." The pages turned again. "The Court does not punish. The Court balances. Your king's envoy understands this better than you do. He arrived with terms already drafted."

Yara's stomach dropped. "Vessar's envoy is *here*?"

"Two corridors away," the steward said placidly, "drafting a contract for the seventh lock. He offers souls by the thousand—soldiers who signed oaths they did not read, criminals condemned by courts that never weighed them, debtors sold by their creditors. None of it freely given. All of it valid." The pages rustled, almost like laughter. "The law does not require goodness, priestess. Only consent, however poisoned its well."

"That's monstrous," Pell said. The boy's voice cracked in the middle of it, and he flushed, but he didn't take it back.

"Yes," said the steward. "It is also binding."

* * *

They withdrew to a side alcove—a hollow in the memory-walls where a single harvest scene glowed around them, some farmer's joy at a wheat field centuries dead—and argued in whispers, because sound carried strangely here and none of them trusted the corridors.

"It's me," Ada said.

"No," said four voices at once.

He looked at them, and Yara saw him do the thing he must have done at his gate a thousand times: arrange his face into competence, into calm, into the mask of a man merely managing logistics. "Listen to me. I'm the obvious choice. I've lived twice as long as any of you should have—the gate-warding fed years into me that were never mine. I've been borrowing time for decades. Giving it back isn't sacrifice. It's arithmetic."

"It's not arithmetic," Yara said sharply. "You've heard the steward. Freely given means freely given, and you're standing there rehearsing your own funeral like it's a customs inspection. That's not freedom, Ada. That's just guilt wearing a clean coat."

"I watched my sister's face down that corridor," he said quietly, and the mask slipped, just for a heartbeat, and what was underneath was so raw that Pell looked away. "Seventy years old and seventeen forever. My mother died waiting at a gate her son wouldn't open for free. My father drank himself into the ground rather than speak her name. I am the last of the Almeidas and I have spent twenty years being nobody on purpose. If anyone's soul is worth less here—"

"Don't." Sefa's voice cut like her knives. She stepped forward, and Yara saw something in her face she'd never seen before—not the smuggler's grin, not the sharp-eyed calculation, but fury with grief underneath it, old and structural, like rot in a foundation. "Don't you dare finish that sentence. I've hauled cargo past your gate for eleven years, Almeida. You think I didn't know what you were? Every smuggler on the Thornfell roads knew. The man who kept perfect ledgers of his own sins so the tax collectors would never look closer. We used you. And you let us, because being used meant being left alone." She jabbed a finger at his chest. "And now you want to make yourself the price because you've decided your life is the cheapest thing on the table. That's not generosity. That's the same invisibility with a fancier name."

"Sefa—"

"The mountain has a debt." She said it fast, before he could speak, the words tumbling out like contraband spilled from a torn sack. "The old debt. Older than the guilds, older than the passes. Smugglers' law, first law: *the mountain keeps what it keeps, and we keep what we owe.* Every crew that ever ran the Thornfell roads paid tithe to the deep places—one coin in ten, left at the cairns, for luck, for passage, for the things below that let us be. My grandmother paid it. Her grandmother paid it. Ten generations of smugglers paying into an account nobody thought would ever be drawn on." She pulled something from inside her coat: a small iron token, black with age, stamped with a spiral. "This is a tithe-mark. First mark of the first crew. It's been in my family since before Vessar's line had a crown. The debt is real, and debts can be assigned. So I'm assigning it. Take the smugglers' account, steward. Ten generations of tithes against one soul. Let's see whose balance is bigger."

Yara felt hope rise and immediately distrust it. "Sefa, that's—"

"A gesture," Sefa admitted. "I know. I don't know if it's enough. But it's *mine*, and it's true, and it's not a man deciding he deserves to die because he once turned away a refugee family." Her voice broke on the last word, and Yara realized she knew that story too. Of course she did. Smugglers told stories about the gatekeeper of Hollowgate the way sailors told stories about lighthouses.

* * *

It was Dov who moved next.

He had been silent since the descent began—he was silent always, his tongue taken or never given, his answers carved in air and gesture—but now he stepped forward and knelt, right there on the memory-lit floor, and pressed both palms flat against the stone.

The stone answered.

Yara felt it through her boots, a low hum, and the harvest scene around them flickered. Dov looked up at the steward, then at each of them in turn, and began to sign—slow, deliberate, formal, the signing of someone reciting rather than conversing. Yara couldn't follow it. But the walls could.

The memories shifted. Harvest became digging. A tunnel opened in the glow around them, rendered in light: narrow, ancient, lined with carvings, and figures moving through it—Dov's people, the clan of diggers exiled from the deep roads a century ago, their names struck from every register above and below. The carvings on the tunnel walls showed hands. Hundreds of hands, pressed to stone, passing something down.

"He's offering his clan's name," Ada said hoarsely. He alone, Yara thought, seemed able to half-read the signing; maybe the gate-warding magic had given him more than years. "The name they lost when they were exiled. The name that was scraped off the seventh lock." His voice shook. "Dov, no. Names are all they have left. You can't—"

Dov turned to him. And signed, short and hard, three gestures that even Yara could read because they were universal: *I choose. Me. Mine.*

The hum in the stone deepened. Somewhere far above, Yara imagined, the mountain itself was leaning in to listen.

* * *

And then Pell stepped forward, and everything in Yara that was still priestess wanted to grab him and throw him behind her.

He was sixteen. He was skinny and sharp-faced and he picked pockets with his left hand and cried at night when he thought the firelight hid him, and he had followed Ada Almeida through half the underworld with the dumb blazing devotion of a boy who had found the one adult who ever looked at him like he was a person instead of a purse. He stood there in the harvest-light with his fists clenched and his chin up, terrified and refusing to show it, and he said:

"Not him."

"Pell—" Ada started.

"*Not him.*" Louder this time, cracking again, and he didn't care. "You don't get to. Any of you. This isn't—I don't have a clan name to give, I don't have a family debt, I don't have anything, I'm a pickpocket, okay, I know what I am. But I know what he is." He pointed at Ada, and his hand was shaking, and he didn't care about that either. "He turned me away at his gate two winters ago. Freezing, starving, caught stealing bread in Hollowgate village. He should've called the watch. Instead he gave me soup and a night in the gatehouse and put me on the road south with coin in my pocket, and he logged it in his ledger—*one sin, charity*—like helping somebody was a crime he had to confess." Pell's eyes were wet and furious. "Nobody had ever written me down before. Like I counted. Like I was a *name* and not a nuisance. So no. Nobody's buying his soul. Not the steward, not the mountain, not the gods, not him. If someone goes into that contract, it's me, because I'm the one who owes him, and I say *not him*, and I'll say it until the mountain falls down."

The silence afterward was enormous.

Yara found she was crying, quietly, without drama, the way she hadn't cried since the day they stripped her vestments. Because this—this ragged circle of a smuggler with her grandmother's iron, a mute exile kneeling on stone, a thief with nothing to offer but refusal—this was the thing her order had spent five hundred years trying to build and never managed. Congregation. Communion. People bound not by doctrine but by the stubborn, irrational insistence that no one at the table gets eaten.

She wiped her face and stepped to the center, and raised her hand, and let the sigil burn openly where all of them—and the walls, and whatever listened beyond them—could see.

"Hear me," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "I was defrocked for asking questions. For reading the sealed commentaries. For saying aloud that our order had forgotten what it was *for*." She turned to the corridor where the steward waited. "Now I remember. We weren't priests of the old gods. We were priests *of the gates*. Keepers of thresholds. Our whole rite, the one they burned the books over—it was a rite of exchange. Standing between worlds and making sure the toll was paid fairly. That's why the sigil woke on my hand when I met a gatekeeper. It wasn't calling me back to faith." She laughed, wet and disbelieving. "It was calling me back to *work*."

"Yara," Ada said softly. "Whatever you're about to offer—"

"I'm not offering my soul," she said. "I'm offering my office. Steward! Come balance your books, because I'm invoking the Rite of the Threshold, and under that rite the officiant stands in the exchange—not as payment, but as *guarantor*. My life held in bond until the bargain is honored. That's in your oldest clauses. Check your pages."

The pages turned. Faster now. The hall hummed.

And then, from the corridor behind them, footsteps—measured, polished, unhurried. A figure in royal blue resolved out of the memory-light, flanked by two soldiers whose eyes had the flat shine of men who had signed things they hadn't read.

Vessar's envoy smiled at them all, warm as a knife going in.

"Forgive the intrusion," he said. "But I believe we're bidding on the same soul."

Behind him, far up the dark throat of the tunnel, the mountain shuddered—and somewhere above, Yara knew without knowing how, the seventh lock had begun to crack.
