## 16. Vessar at the Gate

The first thing Ada felt was the absence.

He had carried the gate's weight for twenty years without knowing it was a weight—the way a man carries a stone in his boot so long his foot reshapes itself around it. Now, three days beneath Thornfell, in air that tasted of memory and old contracts, he reached for that familiar pressure and found nothing. The warding was gone. Not broken. *Unheld.* Like a hand letting go of a rope it had gripped so long the rope had grown into the skin.

He stopped mid-stride on the causeway of the Court Beneath, souls drifting past him like pale lanterns on a slow tide, and pressed his palm flat against the living rock.

*Oh,* he thought. *Oh no.*

"What is it?" Yara asked. She had her sigil-burned palm raised toward the steward's distant dais, still negotiating terms they all suspected were lies.

"The gate." His voice came out flat, which was how he lied best, and this was not a lie but it sounded like one even to himself. "I left it open."

"You left it *unguarded*," Sefa corrected from behind him, sharpening the word like she sharpened everything. "There's a difference, gatekeeper, and I'm guessing we're about to learn what it costs."

Ada closed his eyes. Twenty years of small sins logged in neat columns, and here at last was the great one, the sin with no ledger line wide enough to hold it: he had walked away from the only door between an army and the free cities, and the door did not know how to stay shut without him.

Far above, through a mile of mountain, something began to groan.

* * *

Captain Edran Vael had served King Vessar for eleven years and had learned to read his sovereign the way sailors read weather—by what the horizon refused to show. The king rode at the head of his vanguard up the last switchback to Hollowgate, and he was smiling, and that frightened Edran more than any storm ever had.

"There it is," Vessar said.

The pass opened before them like a wound in the mountains. And there stood the gate—and Edran had heard the stories, everyone had, of the black thorn-iron doors that no ram could mark and no lockpick could whisper to, sealed by a magic older than the kingdom—but the doors stood *ajar*. Just a crack. A hand's width of darkness breathing cold air into the summer morning, frost feathered across the stone threshold in July.

"Your Majesty," Edran said carefully, "the reports said the keeper never leaves his post. Never. In twenty years."

"Then perhaps," Vessar said, "someone finally made him an offer worth leaving for. Or a threat." He dismounted, boots crunching on frost that should not exist, and laid one gloved hand against the door. Edran watched the king's face do something terrible and delighted. "It's still warm. The binding. Like a hearth someone just walked away from." He turned, and his smile was the worst thing Edran had seen in eleven years of war. "Sound the advance. We camp inside the pass tonight."

"And the refugees, sire?"

Vessar glanced down the road, where the dust of a hundred fleeing families hung in the heat haze—farmers and free-city folk who had heard the army coming and run for the mountains with whatever they could carry.

"Let them through," the king said pleasantly. "Every soul that passes through my gate is a soul I can count. And I will want an accurate count."

Edran gave the order. Behind them, ten thousand soldiers began to climb.

* * *

Marta Osei had been running for six days with her two daughters and her dead husband's loom-shuttle in her pocket, and when she saw the gates of Hollowgate standing open she did not believe it, because nothing in this war had been given freely yet.

But the gate was open. Frost on the stones in high summer, and a cold clean wind pouring out of the dark like breath, and beyond the threshold the smugglers' road descending into the mountain's heart, lit—she would swear this until she died—lit by small drifting lights that moved aside to let people pass.

"They're going under," whispered the woman beside her, an old Corvale tanner with bleeding feet. "Under the Thornfell. That's a grave-road, girl. Nobody comes back from under the mountain."

Marta looked at her daughters. Nine and four. The four-year-old was asleep standing up, swaying like a reed. Behind them, far below on the plain, the vanguard's banners had come into view, red as open meat.

A light drifted close to Marta's face. It was warm. It pulsed once, twice, like a heartbeat, like a patient thing saying *yes, this way, yes.*

She took her daughters' hands and walked into the mountain.

Behind her came the tanner, then a family of stonemasons, then forty more, then hundreds—a river of the desperate flowing into the dark, guided by lights that had no business being kind, while above them the seventh lock, buried deep in the roots of the world, began—very slowly, very quietly—to crack.

* * *

Dov felt it first because Dov felt everything first; it was the curse of his clan, the tunnel-sense, the stone speaking through the bones of the feet.

They were crossing the Court's outer gallery when he stopped so abruptly that Pell walked into his back. He crouched, laid both palms flat against the floor, and listened with his whole body the way other men listened with their ears.

His hands said: *cracking.*

Not near. Far above. A seam opening along something vast and old, hairline-thin, spreading the way ice spreads on a pond before it goes. And threaded through the cracking, faint as a pulse in a wrist, he felt footsteps. Thousands of them. Boots on mountain stone, marching in step, marching *down.*

He grabbed Pell's sleeve and pointed up, then made the clan-sign for *breaking*, then the sign for *many*, then—he hesitated, because he had never used this sign outside his mother's house—the sign for *coming home wrong.*

Pell went white. "Ada!" the boy shouted, voice cracking across the gallery. "ADA!"

* * *

Ada was standing before Lira's lantern when the boy's shout reached him.

He had told her everything. She had chosen to hear it. And now she drifted close to him, seventeen forever, wearing the face from the one portrait their mother had owned, and she was asking him questions about rain—whether rain still smelled the same, whether the gatepost still held the notch where she'd carved a crooked little bird—and every answer he gave was a knife he was grateful for.

Then Pell's voice, cracked with fear, and Sefa swearing in three languages, and the whole Court shuddering like a struck bell.

The lights dimmed. Every soul-lantern in the vast cavern flickered at once, and in the sudden half-dark Ada saw them clearly for the first time—not ornaments, not ghosts, but *people*, thousands upon thousands of the taken dead, and every one of them had turned to look upward, toward the mountain's crown, toward the sound.

The sound was a crack. Then another. Rhythmic now. Patient. Like a lock being worried by a thief who had all the time in the world.

"The seventh lock," the steward said. It had appeared among them without walking there, contract-flesh gleaming, its voice carrying no alarm at all, which was somehow worse than screaming. "It responds to approach. To intention. Someone above intends to claim it." Its head rotated toward Ada with the smooth precision of a well-oiled hinge. "Keeper. Your gate stands unbound. You are aware of this."

"I know," Ada said.

"You are aware that your king marches with ten thousand souls, and that the bargain names seven, and that a crown may be forged from a lock only if the lock is *broken* rather than *opened*?"

"I know," Ada said again, and found that his hands had stopped shaking for the first time in twenty years. Fear had finally spent itself and left something harder underneath. "How long?"

The steward considered. "For the lock? Days. For your world?" Its smile was a clause, not a kindness. "That depends entirely on what you are willing to pay, and you have not yet finished naming your price."

Above them, faint as memory, the mountain groaned again—and this time, through a mile of stone, Ada heard something else. Voices. Human voices, echoing down the throat of the pass. An army, singing a marching song as it descended into Hollowgate, and the gate that should have barred them stood open as a host's door, and the refugees who had fled into the dark were now trapped in the same throat as the wolf.

Lira drifted to his side. She did not remember him, not truly, not yet—but she took his hand anyway, the way you take the hand of someone standing at a ledge beside you.

"What do we do?" she asked.

Ada looked at his sister, at Sefa with her knives already loose in their sheaths, at Yara with her burning palm raised like a torch, at Dov signing *follow me* toward a crack in the wall too narrow for soldiers, at Pell standing guard over all of them with a stolen kitchen knife and no sense whatsoever of his own mortality.

"We go up," Ada said. "All the way up. If Vessar wants the lock—" and here he smiled, and it was Sefa's smile, sharp and borrowed, the smile of a man done hiding— "he'll have to break it. And breaking things is the one trade I've spent twenty years learning to stop."

The mountain groaned. The lock cracked. Somewhere above, a king put his shoulder to the door of the world.

And the gate, unheld, unguarded, unloved for the first time in twenty years, stood open to everything that came.
