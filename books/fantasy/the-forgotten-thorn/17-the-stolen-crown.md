## 17. The Stolen Crown

The mountain began to scream before we heard the boots.

It was not a sound so much as a pressure—a groan in the stone that rose up through our knees and settled behind our teeth, like the whole of Thornfell had been asked to bear a weight it had never agreed to carry. Yara's hand flew to her palm, where the sigil still burned faintly beneath the skin, and her face went the color of old ash.

"That's not an earthquake," she said.

"No," I said. "That's an army."

We had perhaps an hour—perhaps less—between the moment the steward's contract-scales tipped and the moment Vessar's vanguard reached the Court. Sefa had wanted to run for the upper tunnels. Pell had wanted to fight. Dov had simply stood at the mouth of the passage he'd held open for us since the descent began, his broad hands flat against the rock, reading the tremors the way other men read weather.

I had wanted to stay with Lira.

Her lantern-light was still near me, drifting at shoulder height like something that had forgotten it was ever anything but light. She had chosen to hear everything—the bargain, the seventy years, the sister who had grown old without her—and she had not fled from it. She had only said, *Then don't leave me here,* and I had promised, with the gate-warder's oath I hadn't spoken aloud in twenty years, that I wouldn't.

Now the mountain screamed, and every promise I had ever made came due at once.

* * *

They came down through the great stair on the far side of the Court—the one the steward called the King's Approach, carved in an age when kings still knelt to gods instead of robbing them. We saw them first as torchlight bleeding down the spiral, dozens of flames reflected in the memory-glass walls until it looked as though the whole mountain were burning.

Vessar descended last.

I had seen his likeness on proclamations nailed to gateposts across three provinces: a square-jawed man with careful hair and a soldier's economy of movement. The likeness had flattered him. In person he was smaller, harder, with the look of a man who had spent his whole life climbing stairs and resented every one of them. His armor was black-lacquered steel worked with silver thread, and around his throat he wore a collar of pale stone that hurt to look at—the seventh lock, or a piece of it, torn free and carried like jewelry.

Behind him walked the envoy.

I had met the envoy before, though not in this life. It wore the shape of a tall woman in courtly gray, and its face was beautiful the way contracts are beautiful—precise, symmetrical, utterly without mercy. When it smiled at the steward across the Court's memory-lit hall, the steward—who had negotiated with empires and outlasted their bones—took one step backward.

"You brought that thing down here," Sefa breathed beside me. "Into the one place it can't lie."

"It doesn't need to lie," Yara said grimly. "Not anymore."

Vessar did not pause to admire the Court. That was the first thing I understood about him, watching from the shadow of a fallen pillar while Lira's light trembled against my chest: he was not a man awed by wonders. He was a man cataloguing them. His eyes went from the drifting soul-lanterns to the seven thrones of living stone to the steward itself, and in each he saw only a resource unclaimed.

"The lock," he said, and his voice carried through the Court the way a hammer carries through a chapel. "All of it. Now."

"King of dust and iron," the steward said, and its voice was the sound of scales settling, "the terms were named. A soul freely given. You offered an army's worth, taken, not given. The Court does not—"

"I'm not here to negotiate." Vessar lifted the collar of pale stone from his throat, and the screaming in the mountain changed pitch—rose, sharpened, became almost articulate. "My envoy has studied your law for eleven years. There is a clause. There is always a clause. *What is freely surrendered may be claimed by conquest.* Your gods wrote it themselves, back when they thought no mortal would ever be fool enough to march an army into hell to rob them."

The envoy stepped forward and spread its hands, and where its shadow fell, the memory-glass walls went dark.

"The seventh lock was surrendered," it said. "Freely. By the gods themselves, when they sealed the bargain. It has been held in trust by the Court for seventy years." Its smile widened by a precise degree. "And the Court has just been conquered."

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way disasters do—one breath everything is standing, the next the world is a list of things falling.

Vessar drove the lock-stone into the floor of the Court, into the seam between the seventh throne and the root of the mountain, and spoke a word that Yara later told me was older than her order, older than writing, a word the priests had been made guardians of precisely so it would never be spoken. The stone drank the word and began to glow—not warm gold, not the patient amber of the soul-lanterns, but a hard white light like frost seen from inside.

The rite had begun.

Every lantern in the Court shuddered. I felt it happen to Lira before I saw it—a lurch, a pull, her light dragging sideways toward Vessar's white fire like a leaf toward a drain. Around the hall, hundreds of souls felt the same pull. They had drifted in the Court for decades, centuries, dim and dreaming, and now they woke all at once, and their waking was terror.

"They're binding them," Yara shouted over the rising howl. "The rite turns souls into instruments—weapons! He'll wear the gods like armor!"

Sefa already had her knives out, which was absurd, which was magnificent. "Tell me you have a better idea than stabbing a king in a room full of ghosts."

"I have Dov," I said.

He was already moving. While the rest of us had watched the king, Dov had watched the walls—and the walls were failing. Cracks spiderwebbed up through the memory-glass, and through them poured not water but memory itself, raw and undirected: a harvest festival, a funeral procession, someone's grandmother singing, all of it bleeding out of the broken glass like light from a shattered lamp. The tunnels above us were collapsing floor by floor, and the passages Dov's clan had dug and mapped and sung to for forty generations were coming down.

Dov put his back against the main arch—the one that led to the smugglers' road, the one that led home—and set his shoulders under the falling stone, and held.

I have seen men hold doors against soldiers. I have never seen a man hold a door against a mountain. He could not speak—he had not spoken since Corvale, since the tunnel that took his voice along with his clan—but the sound he made then was not silence. It was a low continuous note, a digger's note, the note his people used to sing stone steady, and the arch above him bent and groaned and did not fall.

"Dov—" Pell started toward him.

"Hold the others!" I caught the boy's arm. "He's holding the way out. Someone has to hold the people going through it!"

* * *

We fought our way across the Court because there was no other direction worth going. The white light of the rite was spreading from the seventh lock in veins, and everywhere it touched, souls were dragged down into it—pulled out of their drifting, compressed, made small and hard and bright. Weapons being forged. I saw a lantern that might have been a farmer's wife crushed into a point of light sharp enough to cut. I saw what might have been a child.

Lira pulled against my hands. "Ada." Her voice was still seventeen, still hers, but frayed now at the edges. "It hurts. Why does it—"

"Because a thief is trying to steal you," I said, "and I am going to be extremely rude about it."

Sefa reached Vessar's flank first, using the chaos—souls scattering like startled birds, the steward's contract-forms whirling through the air like a flock of furious paper—as cover. She got two knives into the gap beneath his pauldron before his guards closed ranks, and if he had been any other man, any merely mortal tyrant, that would have been the end of it.

But the rite was already feeding him. The white light ran up through the lock-stone and into his hands and along his arms, and when he turned and struck her—backhanded, almost casual—she flew ten feet and hit a throne of living stone and did not get up quickly.

"Sefa!" Pell screamed.

Vessar looked at his own glowing hands with something like wonder, and then with something much worse: satisfaction. "Eleven years," he said, to the Court, to the gods, to us. "Eleven years of surveyors and scholars and envoys, and everyone told me the old gods were dead, that the locks were legend, that the door beneath Thornfell was a smuggler's story." He laughed, short and joyless. "Dead things make the best weapons. They can't complain."

"You're wrong," Yara said.

She had come forward alone, past the guards, past the dying light, and stood between Vessar and the seventh lock with her burned sigil raised, and for one moment the whole Court seemed to lean toward her.

"They're not dead," she said. "They're *listening*. They have always been listening. That's what my order was for—not to serve them. To hear them." Her voice cracked, and I understood that she was speaking to gods she had believed had abandoned her, in words she had rehearsed drunk and despairing in a hundred gutters. "And right now they can hear everything you're doing to their people."

The mountain answered.

Not with the scream this time. With a word.

No one spoke it. No one needed to. It came up through the floor and out of the walls and off the shards of memory-glass, a single syllable of such vast, slow anger that the torches guttered and the guards fell to their knees and even the envoy's smile flickered—for the first time in eleven years of study, the envoy looked uncertain.

Vessar did not kneel. That was his tragedy, I think, and ours: he was the kind of man who heard a god's voice and reached for a lever.

"Bind it," he ordered, and drove both hands down onto the lock-stone, and the white light surged—

—and the Court turned on everyone.

* * *

I cannot tell it in order, because it did not happen in order. It happened all at once, the way a dam breaks.

The thrones of living stone opened their eyes. The memory-glass walls gave way entirely, and seventy years of stored memory flooded the Court—every prayer ever whispered at Hollowgate, every name ever spoken at a gatepost or a crossroads, all of it at once, a flood of human voices crying out of the dark. The soul-lanterns scattered upward in a storm of light, tearing free of the rite's gravity, and where they passed, the white veins of Vessar's working cracked and dimmed. The guards broke and ran for the stair and found the stair gone—swallowed by the collapse roaring down from above. The steward rose to its full height, taller than I had ever seen it, its contract-scrolls unfurling into wings, and its voice when it spoke was not one voice but seven.

"*THE BARGAIN IS BROKEN.*"

The ceiling came down in the east gallery. The floor tilted. And through all of it, Dov held the arch.

I saw him clearly in that moment, and I will see him clearly until I die: a mute exile with his spine bent under a mountain, singing his clan's steadying note into the stone, blood running from his nose and ears with the effort, refusing—with the same stubborn, wordless will that had carried us through forty miles of ancestral dark—to let the way home close while any of us were still on the wrong side of it.

Pell reached Sefa and got her arm over his shoulders. She was conscious; she was cursing; therefore she was alive. "Go!" she rasped at me. "Get her and go!"

But Lira's light had been caught again—snared at the edge of the rite, half-drawn into the white, her voice thinning to a thread. "Ada—"

I did the arithmetic every gate-warder learns: what can be saved, what must be abandoned, what the crossing costs. Twenty years of ledgers. Twenty years of weighing lives in columns.

I tore the page out.

I ran into the white light after my sister, into the rite itself, into the space where Vessar's stolen power was trying to compress a soul into a weapon, and I wrapped my arms around a lantern made of light, and I pulled.

It should not have worked. Mortal flesh does not argue with god-binding rites. But the light did not resist me—it *recognized* me. Seventy years ago a frightened girl had been sold through a gate, and the last thing she had seen was her brother's face, and some part of her had kept that face the way a drowning swimmer keeps a star. The rite pulled. I pulled harder. And Lira—Lira chose. I felt her choose, felt her soul turn from the white fire toward my arms the way a compass turns north, and the binding snapped like a rotten rope.

We fell together out of the light, onto the tilting floor, and she was in my arms—weightless, luminous, laughing or sobbing or both.

"Got you," I said, which was a lie, because nothing was gotten, nothing was safe, everything was breaking.

Above us, Vessar screamed at his envoy to stabilize the rite, and the envoy did something to the lock-stone that made the whole mountain flinch, and the white light gathered itself for a second surge. Yara was on her feet at the heart of the chaos with her arms raised and her sigil blazing like a brand, buying seconds with prayers her order had died preserving. Dov's note climbed higher, more desperate, the arch above him shedding stone like a molting bird. Sefa was upright somehow, knife in her teeth, hauling Pell toward the arch. The steward's seven voices were naming debts in a language that made the dark itself flinch.

Everything was breaking. Everything was breaking at once, and we had seconds, maybe less, and one chance left—the rite Yara had been born for, the seven names, the price freely given, the thing we had crossed the world to attempt and were nowhere near ready to perform.

"Lira," I said, holding her light against my heart as the white fire rose. "I need you to listen one more time. And then I need you to let go of me."

The mountain leaned in to hear her answer.
