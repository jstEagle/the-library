## 18. Seven Names Spoken

The seventh lock hung in the dark like a wound in the world.

Ada had no better word for it. It was a door that was not a door, a seam of black glass suspended in the vast throat of the cavern where the Court's memory-light pooled and guttered. Cracks spiderwebbed across its surface, thin as hair, weeping a cold that made his teeth ache. Through those cracks he could hear it—the sound of something enormous turning over in its sleep, dreaming of sky.

And above it, wreathed in stolen light, stood King Vessar.

He had come down into the mountain the way a man wades into a river he believes is shallow—certain of his depth, certain of his footing. His envoy flanked him, gray-robed and faceless beneath its hood, holding the contract-scroll that had bought an army's worth of souls. Vessar himself wore the crown.

Not a crown of gold. Ada could see that now, from across the shattered floor of the Court, crouched behind a fallen pillar with Sefa's knife warm in his hand. The seventh lock's power had poured itself into Vessar when he seized it, and it had taken the shape of a circlet of black thorns, each one tipped with a point of light—a soul, Ada realized, a little lantern of someone's life, threaded onto the king's brow like beads on a wire. There were hundreds of them. Thousands. They flickered in time with his breathing.

*He's wearing them,* Ada thought, and the horror of it went through him colder than the frost ever had. *Every light on that crown is a person.*

"Yara," he breathed. "Tell me you can do this."

Beside him, the priestess had gone very still. Her palm—the one that had burned with her order's sigil for weeks now—was raised before her face, and the mark upon it blazed like a coal. Around them, the Court's drifting souls had begun to turn toward Vessar like flowers toward a poisoned sun.

"I can do this," Yara said. Her voice was steady, which frightened Ada more than trembling would have. Steady meant she'd stopped hoping to survive it. "But the rite needs names. Seven names, spoken true, by blood-kin or by choice. The order made me for this, Ada. They just never told me I'd be the last one left to say them."

"Then we say them." He looked past her: Pell, small and fierce, pressed flat against the pillar's other side, clutching the stolen envoy-seal they'd need to unbind the contract; Sefa, bleeding from a gash along her jaw, grinning anyway, because Sefa grinned at death the way she grinned at customs officers. And behind them all, at the mouth of the passage, Dov—holding the collapsing tunnel open with his bare hands and his whole silent will, stone groaning against stone above his bowed head.

Dov caught Ada looking. The mute tunnel-digger did not smile. He simply nodded once, the way a man nods who has already said everything he means to say.

*Seven names.* Ada closed his eyes and let the ledger open in his mind—not the clean ledgers of Hollowgate, but the other one, the one he'd kept for twenty years without ink: every sin logged, every name remembered. Six souls sold to seal six locks. Six names scraped into the tunnels' ancient walls, and a seventh scratched away—but not erased. Not from him. He had carried it since he was a boy too young to understand what the whispering at the gatepost meant, since the night his mother pressed Lira's hand into his and said *watch the gate, Ada, watch it always*, and he had watched, and the gate had kept him young while it took everything else.

"The rite," he said aloud. "What happens to the one who speaks?"

Yara met his eyes. She didn't lie. He loved her a little for that, in the way you love people at the end of things.

"The price must be freely given," she said. "A soul for a soul. A life for a life. The lock takes what's offered—or it takes what it wants. That part was never written down."

"Convenient," Sefa muttered.

"Holy writ rarely is."

Above them, Vessar's voice rolled through the cavern, amplified by the crown until it seemed to come from the mountain's own throat. "People of the buried dark! Hear your king! The old bargain ends tonight—and a new one begins, sealed in my name. Kneel, gods of Thornfell. You have a master now."

The cracks in the lock widened. Something behind them screamed—not in pain, Ada thought. In fury. The memory-lights of the Court dimmed, guttered, flared.

"Now," Yara whispered. "While he's binding it. Once the rite completes, nothing wakes again—ever. Everything sleeps under him forever." She turned to Pell. "The seal. When I begin, you get to the contract-scroll and break the envoy's mark. Sefa—" 

"I know my job." Sefa rolled her shoulders, spat blood onto the black glass floor, and smiled her terrible smile. "Loud and stupid and impossible to ignore."

"That's not what I was going to say."

"It's what you were thinking."

* * *

They went like a thrown knife.

Sefa first, straight out into the open, screaming Vessar's name and a string of insults so inventive that two of the king's honor guard broke formation simply to look at her—which was the point, because Pell came off the pillar low and fast, a shadow with a stolen seal, skimming the cavern wall toward the envoy and the scroll. Ada ran with Yara at his back, her voice already rising into the old cadences, the ones carved into Dov's ancestral tunnels, the ones her dead order had died rather than surrender.

*"By gate and threshold, by door and key—"*

Vessar saw them. Of course he did. The crown turned his gaze into searchlight, and light lanced down—black fire threading with soul-light—and struck the ground where Ada had been half a heartbeat before. He felt it pass close enough to part his hair. He did not stop running. Twenty years of standing still had taught him exactly how much he hated it.

*"By the seven who slept, by the seven who wait—"*

Soldiers poured from the flanking galleries, Vessar's vanguard, the men who had crossed the pass Ada left unguarded. Ada saw their faces as they charged—young, mostly, afraid, following a king who promised them the world—and he felt no hatred, only a terrible tired recognition. They were refugees too, in their way. Fleeing toward the wrong thing.

Sefa met the first rank alone, and it should have been impossible, and it was not, because Sefa fought like a woman settling accounts. She moved through steel the way she moved through a market crowd—all elbows and angles and cheerful profanity—and behind her Pell reached the envoy, slammed the stolen seal against the contract-scroll, and *twisted*.

The scroll screamed. Every soul-thread on Vessar's crown shuddered at once, and the king staggered, and in that stagger—in that single graceless human moment—Ada saw him plainly: not a colossus, not a legend. A man who had been afraid of being small his whole life and had decided the cure was to make everyone else smaller still.

*"Hear us, keepers of the deep!"* Yara's voice cracked across the cavern like ice splitting on a spring lake. *"The bargain is called! The debt is named! Speak the seven, blood-keeper, or be silent forever!"*

Blood-keeper. Her. Him. The last witness.

Ada stopped running.

He stood in the open floor of the Court beneath the mountain, between an army and a god-prison, and he opened the ledger of his life.

* * *

"Lirael Almeida," he said.

His sister's name came out of him the way a splinter leaves flesh—with pain, and then with an awful lightness. Across the cavern, the soul-lanterns stirred. One of them, the one wearing a seventeen-year-old girl's face, turned toward the sound of her brother's voice, and though she could not remember why, she began—very softly—to hum the gate-song their mother used to sing.

"First of seven. Sold, not lost. Remembered."

The first crack in the lock blazed white.

"Sefan of the Marrow Roads," Ada continued, and Sefa's head snapped around, her grin gone, her eyes wide. "Smuggler. Debt-carrier. The mountain's own child, freely named."

"You absolute *bastard*," Sefa breathed—but the old smugglers' debt to the mountain, the one her grandmother's grandmother had sworn and no one had ever been able to pay, rose up out of her like a tide answering the moon. She planted her feet in the middle of the battle and let it move through her. "Fine. Fine! Named. Freely given, you hear me, you great sleeping rock? *Freely given!*"

The second crack burned gold.

"Dovren, son of the Underclan." At the tunnel mouth, Dov straightened under the weight of the stone he held, and offered the only thing his people considered greater than silence: the clan-name itself, the name that made them a people, given so that others might live. The third crack burned green.

"Pell." Ada's voice caught. The boy was mid-sprint back toward them, seal in hand, and he skidded to a halt and stared. "Pell of nowhere, Pell of the gate. Who chose us. Freely chosen, freely naming."

"I don't even know what I'm agreeing to!" Pell shouted—but he was crying, and he thrust his fist into the air anyway, and the fourth crack burned red.

Four lights now blazed along the lock's fracture-lines, and Vessar understood, and roared, and threw everything. Black fire fell like weather. Yara stepped into it, arms spread, her sigil blazing, and the fire broke around her the way water breaks around a bridge piling—because her order had been built for exactly this, made and maimed and finally murdered for the sake of this one hour, and their last priestess stood in it unbowed.

*"Speak, Ada!"* she screamed over the roar. *"The last two! Now, or never!"*

"Fifth." He was shaking. He let himself shake. "Yara of the Broken Order, who found her god again in the dark. Freely named."

White-gold light. The lock screamed. Behind its cracks, the sleeping vastness had begun to press outward, and the whole cavern trembled, and dust fell like snow from a sky that had never seen weather.

One name left.

Ada looked up at the lock, and then—at the last—he looked at the lantern with his sister's face. She had stopped humming. She was watching him with Lira's eyes, the exact grey-green of them, and though she remembered nothing, something in her knew. Mothers wait at gates. Little brothers watch them. Some things live deeper than memory.

"Sixth," Ada said, and his voice did not break, because he had spent twenty years learning how to hold a voice steady while everything behind it broke instead. "Lira Almeida, who was a person before she was a price. Whose name was scraped from stone but never from me. I remember you. I have always remembered you. Come home."

The sixth crack blazed silver, and the lantern that wore his sister's face filled with light like dawn filling a window, and somewhere far above them, in a world of wind and thorn, every gatepost in the mountains bloomed with frost.

"And the seventh?" Yara's voice, gentle now, terrible now. "Who pays the seventh?"

Ada stepped forward. Away from Sefa, who was shouting his name. Away from Pell, who was screaming *not him, not him, not him* in a voice that would live in Ada's memory longer than any prayer. Away from all of it, toward the lock, alone, the way he had always been alone—except that this time he chose it, and choosing made it different, made it holy.

"Seventh," he said. "Ada Almeida. Gatekeeper. Brother. Coward, once. Keeper of a ledger of small sins, which I hereby close." He spread his arms, and offered it—the long unnatural life, the twenty years the gate had stolen for him and lent him back, the decades the warding had folded into his bones like interest on a debt. "Take it back. All of it. Freely given. Let my sister's sacrifice end, and let mine begin, and let the count come even at last."

The lock drank him in.

Cold first, then light, then a pulling so vast it felt less like dying than like being *read*—every page of him turned at once, every entry weighed, every small sin and smaller kindness tallied by something patient beyond patience. Ada felt his years leave him the way frost leaves glass in the morning: silently, beautifully, irreversibly. He thought: *so this is what it costs.* He thought: *worth it.* He thought of Lira humming at a gate, and of Pell's fist in the air, and of Sefa swearing love at the top of her lungs, and he thought—

*Let the gate stay open.*

And the seventh lock shattered.

* * *

There was no sound. There was a *before* the sound, one white instant in which the whole world held its breath—and then the mountain itself exhaled, and the sound arrived everywhere at once, a chord so deep it was felt in the marrow rather than heard, seven notes woven into one, and the old gods woke whole.

They rose out of the broken lock like seasons rising out of winter. Ada, falling to his knees, his hands already going grey and papery, his borrowed years draining away into the dark—Ada saw them and wept without shame. Figures of stone and storm and root and river, vast past sense, crowned in living light, unfolding upward through the cavern until their heads were lost in the dark overhead. The Court's memory-lights knelt. The drifting souls sang. Even the soldiers of Vessar's vanguard dropped their weapons and fell prostrate, undone by the sheer animal fact of divinity after so long an absence.

Vessar alone remained standing. Because of the crown.

"No," the king snarled, staggering under the weight of his stolen light. "No—you are *mine*—I hold the bargain—I hold the *lock*—"

He held nothing. The gods turned their attention upon him the way winter turns upon a late flower, without malice and without mercy, and the crown began to come apart.

Thread by thread.

That was the horror and the beauty of it—that they did not strike him down. They *unpicked* him. Each soul-bead on the crown of thorns loosened, gently, precisely, lifted free of the black metal like a knot untied by careful fingers, and rose, and became itself again: a small light, a person, a name. Hundreds of them spiraled upward through the cavern, a slow ascending constellation, freed. And with every thread removed, Vessar grew smaller—not only in stature but in substance, the power draining out of him, the grandeur, the amplifying terror of his voice, until he was only a man in fine boots standing in the ruins of his ambition, screaming at gods who no longer required his permission to exist.

The last thread left the crown. The thorns crumbled to ash. And Vessar, King of the Thornfell reach, usurper, buyer of souls, stood empty-handed in the dark before the waking world.

The greatest of the seven—the one shaped like a mountain wearing a mantle of night—bent its head and regarded him for a long, long moment.

Then it spoke, in a voice like the closing of a door, and what it said, Ada did not hear clearly, and would spend the rest of his life—however long that proved to be—grateful not to have.

Vessar's scream followed the gods' servants down into the deep places, and the mountain closed over it like water.

* * *

Ada lay on the black glass floor and waited to end.

It was taking longer than he'd expected. His body felt ancient—his hands were an old man's hands, spotted and trembling, his breath a thin thread—but the dark had not taken him. The light was fading from the shattered lock, sinking into the stone, and he was still here, still watching, still *counting*, because apparently even at the end of the world he kept ledgers.

Footsteps. Running. Then Pell was there, dropping to his knees, grabbing Ada's shoulders with hands that shook. "No—no no no, you're okay, you're okay, you gave it, it took it, it's *done*—"

"Pell." Ada's voice was a ruin. He smiled with it anyway. "Stop shouting. You'll wake the gods."

"They're already awake, you mad old—"

"Then they'll hear me." He turned his head. Yara knelt on his other side, her sigil dimming at last to embers, tears cutting tracks through the dust on her face. Sefa stood over them both, bloody and swaying, staring down at Ada with an expression he had never seen her wear: naked, undefended grief.

"Stupid," she said thickly. "Stupid, brave, ledger-keeping bastard."

"Yes," Ada agreed.

The light gathered.

It came up out of the broken lock one final time—not vast now, not terrible, but quiet, a warmth like sun through a doorway—and it settled over him, and Ada felt it *consider* him. The freely given price. The offered life. The scales, weighing.

And then, in voices that were seven voices and one voice, the gods answered.

*"Well given,"* they said. *"Well spoken. But the bargain was ours before it was yours, gatekeeper—and we are done with bargains that eat children. We are done with prices. Keep your years. We return what was lent."*

Warmth flooded him. It hurt—it hurt like spring hurting frozen ground—and Ada felt it moving through him, the grey retreating from his hands, the strength pouring back into his ruined breath, decade after decade returning like birds to a roost at dusk. Not all of it. He sensed that instantly, with a keeper's instinct for accounts: some portion had been spent, truly spent, and would not come back. He would age now. He would grow old, honestly, at the ordinary speed of men. He found, to his astonishment, that he wanted to laugh.

Across the cavern, another light descended—smaller, brighter, wrapped in silver—and settled over the lantern that wore his sister's face. Lira's soul-lantern glowed, and glowed, and did not fade, and did not rise, and did not go out.

She looked down at her own hands, at the light running through them like sap, and then she looked at Ada, and her brow furrowed—the smallest, most heartbreakingly human gesture in all that vast and holy place.

"Do I know you?" Lira asked.

"Not yet," Ada said. "But you will."

Above them, the gods were already turning toward the long climb to the surface, toward the world of gates and crossroads and prayers, and the mountain was opening ways that had been shut for seventy years, and somewhere far overhead, Ada knew, frost was blooming on a thousand gateposts at once.

Yara pulled him to his feet. Sefa caught his elbow. Pell refused to let go of his sleeve at all.

"Come on," Sefa said, and her voice cracked, and she pretended it hadn't. "Let's go see what the world looks like with the gods awake in it."

They climbed toward the light—together, all of them, out of the dark and up toward the gate—and behind them, faint but unmistakable, the mountain began, very softly, to sing.
