## 19. What the Gods Decided

Yara had spent her whole life learning how gods spoke. Her order's first lesson, scrawled in a novice's hand on the inside cover of every prayer book: *the gods do not shout; they echo.* She had believed it once, before the silences, before the defrocking, before the wine. She had stopped believing it entirely the night her palm burned with her dead order's sigil.

She believed it now, standing in the shattered heart of the Court Beneath, because the wakened gods were echoing everywhere at once.

The seventh lock lay in pieces around them like the ribs of some enormous fallen beast—shards of pale stone each taller than a man, humming still, though the power that had lived in them was gone. Through the cracks they had left behind poured light that did not behave like light. It moved the way memory moves: sideways, all at once, arriving before its cause. Souls that had scattered during Vessar's rite drifted back down through the broken ceiling of the world like snow falling upward, settling into the dark water of the Court's central pool, and where each one touched, the pool rang like a struck bell.

Yara's knees had given out somewhere between the rite and the waking. She knelt now on wet stone, her burned palm pressed flat against the ground as if she could steady herself through it, and watched seven shapes assemble out of the light.

They did not look like the statues in her order's ruined temples. The statues had been kind: robed figures with gentle faces, hands open, made small enough for human comfort. What stood—or rather, what *occupied*—the space above the pool bore no robes and no single face. Each of the seven was a suggestion, a pressure, a place where the air refused to be ordinary. One was the shape of a doorway seen from the wrong side. One was winter deciding whether to end. One was so old it looked less like a god than like the idea of ground, of stone remembering being lava. Yara's training supplied their names one by one, in liturgical order, her lips moving without sound: *Vethra of the Threshold. Omma of the Long Snow. Karach-Under-Root. The Sisters of the Turning Year. The Ferryman, whose name is not spoken but sung. And the Seventh—*

And the Seventh, who had been locked longest, who had been a lock themselves, and who now stood unbound among their siblings wearing a form borrowed from the youngest soul in the room: Pell's face, fourteen years old and terrified, looking out at the world from inside something vast.

"Pell," Sefa said sharply, grabbing the boy's shoulder. "Pell, blink twice if you're still in there."

The Seventh turned its borrowed head toward her, and smiled with Pell's mouth, and said in a voice like a bell heard through a mountain, *The child is here. We are only borrowing the shape he offered. He offered it very bravely.*

"He's fourteen," Sefa said.

*He is ours now,* the Seventh said, gently, terribly, *as all things at crossroads are ours. But we have not come to keep him. We have come to settle accounts.*

Yara felt the words land in her chest like stones dropped into a well. Settle accounts. She was a priestess without an order, without a god, without anything left to lose except the people kneeling beside her in the wet dark—and she understood, with the cold clarity that had survived her faith, that accounts could be settled in either direction. The old gods had been imprisoned for seventy years by a bargain bought with a child's soul. They had just watched a king try to forge them into weapons. Justice, from beings this old, might look nothing like mercy.

*Bring forward the keeper,* said Vethra of the Threshold, whose voice arrived from every doorway in the cavern at once, including, Yara realized with a chill, the ruined archway through which they had entered, and the crack in the world above, and—she would swear to this until she died—the door of her mother's house in Corvale, thirty years gone.

Ada rose.

He rose the way old men rise, which was wrong, because Ada Almeida was not an old man—he had been gatekeeper of Hollowgate for twenty years and looked barely forty—but the gate-warding magic had been paid for across his spine and his years, and everyone in the Court knew it, and now the gods knew it too. He stood straight anyway. He had Lira's lantern-soul cradled against his chest like something rescued from a fire, and he did not set it down, and Yara loved him a little for that, and feared for him a great deal.

"You know why I came," Ada said. His voice was rough. It carried anyway. "You know what I want."

*We know what you offered,* said Karach-Under-Root, and the ground itself seemed to lean closer. *You spoke your own name last among the seven. You offered your long unnatural life as the freely given price. That offer stands. That offer has weight. The bargain requires a soul freely given, and yours was given most freely of all—by a man who kept our gate for twenty years and never once prayed to us for himself.*

"I didn't do it for you," Ada said.

*No,* said the Ferryman, and there was something in the unsung name that sounded almost warm. *You did it for her. That is why it counts.*

The light shifted. Yara felt the judgment turning like a millstone beginning to move, slow and enormous, and she understood suddenly that she was watching something no priest had witnessed since the founding of her order: not a prayer answered, not a sign sent, but a *ruling*. The gods were deliberating. Out loud. In front of witnesses.

Because, she realized, that was the point. That had always been the point. Her order had been made for this—not to serve gods in secret, but to stand witness when gods judged in the open. Every defrocked priestess, every burned temple, every discarded prayer book had been a thread pulled loose from this moment. She was not here despite her fall. She was here because someone had to see it done properly.

She got to her feet. Her legs shook. She stood anyway, the way Ada had, and when the nearest god-shape inclined toward her—a question, or perhaps permission—she said, "I witness. In the name of the Order of the Open Hand, which you remember even if the world doesn't."

*We remember,* said Omma of the Long Snow. *Witness, then, daughter of a dead order. Here is what we have decided.*

* * *

They returned Ada's years first.

It was not dramatic. That was the thing Yara would carry with her always, the detail she would tell and retell until drunk novices in some rebuilt temple begged her to stop: it was not dramatic at all. The Ferryman simply extended something that was not a hand toward Ada, and the twenty years of gate-warding debt lifted off him like frost melting off a windowpane. He staggered. He caught himself on a shard of the broken lock. When he straightened, he was—there was no other word—younger. Not transformed, not glowing. Just younger. The deep lines around his eyes softened to the lines of a man in his forties who had slept badly for a decade instead of a man who had been slowly spent like coin. His hair, salted gray at the temples for as long as anyone had known him, darkened at the roots and kept going, color flowing back into it like ink into water.

Sefa made a sound that was half laugh, half sob. "Twenty years," she said. "You mean to tell me you've been carrying twenty years this whole time and you never mentioned it?"

"It didn't come up," Ada said. His voice cracked on the second word. He put his hand to his face, feeling the changed geography of it, and Yara saw him arrive somewhere complicated: grief and relief tangled past separating, because the years returned were the years taken from Lira's side of the ledger, and no arithmetic made that fair.

*The debt was ours to repay,* said Vethra of the Threshold. *The gate-warding was a working laid upon our prison door. You maintained it without knowing what you maintained. We do not leave such debts standing. Not anymore.*

"Not anymore," Yara repeated, before she could stop herself. "That's the second time you've said it that way."

All seven shapes turned toward her. The temperature of the cavern did not drop, exactly, but the attention of things that old has a weather of its own, and Yara stood in it and did not kneel.

"The bargain sealed you away," she said. "Seventy years ago. Seven souls for seven locks, and the world forgot you, and my order forgot you, or worse—we remembered and called it heresy." Her burned palm throbbed. "So whatever you decide next, decide it out loud. Because if you go back to whispering, if you go back to being a story priests get defrocked over, then seventy years from now there'll be another Vessar, and another court beneath another mountain, and another child sold, and no one standing where I'm standing. Decide in the open. Bind yourselves where we can see the binding."

Silence. The pool rang softly, a soul settling home.

Then the Seventh—still wearing Pell's young face, still smiling that impossible gentle smile—said, *This is why we woke her palm and not another's.*

*Here is the third ruling,* said Omma of the Long Snow, and the snow-god's voice fell over the cavern like the first quiet after a storm. *Hear it, witness. Hear it, all of you. We are done with courts beneath mountains. We are done with bargains sealed in the dark and paid for by children. From this night forward, we bind ourselves to the living world—not as kings, not as owners, but as neighbors. Wherever there is a gate, we will hear what is prayed at it. Wherever two roads cross, we will listen. Whoever knocks at a threshold honestly will not knock unheard.*

"Answered?" Sefa asked, sharp as ever, arms crossed, chin up, refusing to be awed. "You'll answer prayers? All of them? Because I've got a list—"

*Not all,* said the Ferryman, and something ancient and sad moved through the unsung name. *A god who answers everything is only a machine. We will answer as gates answer: opened to those who knock in good faith. We will answer as crossroads answer: showing the way to those willing to choose one. Small mercies mostly. Sometimes large ones. Never for a price again—only for the asking, and only for the passing through.*

Yara's throat closed. Seventy years of silence, and her order's whole purpose—*to stand at thresholds and carry voices across*—was being restored, amended, made real, by the gods themselves, spoken aloud before witnesses. She thought of her sigil burning on her palm. She thought of every drunk night in Corvale when she'd cursed a dead faith. She found she was crying, and did not care, and wrote it down later in the book she would begin that year, the new prayer book, the first line already forming: *On the night of the waking, I asked the gods to judge in the open, and they did, and the first thing they chose to be bound to was a doorway.*

* * *

Lira they kept in the living world.

The lantern-soul in Ada's arms brightened as the Sisters of the Turning Year drew near—one of them wore the shape of spring deciding, the other autumn relenting, and together they handled the soul the way midwives handle a newborn, with competence and tenderness and no sentimentality at all.

*She was taken at seventeen,* said the Sister of Spring. *She has been seventeen for seventy years. A soul cannot return to a life interrupted; the life is gone, the body is gone, the mother who waited at the gate is long beyond our reach or anyone's.*

"I know," Ada said. His voice was wrecked. He held the lantern tighter. "I told her. Last night, in the dark, I told her everything. She heard it. She—" He stopped. Started again. "She asked about our mother. I couldn't lie to her twice."

*No,* said the Sister of Autumn, softly. *And so she chose, and her choosing matters. A soul that hears the truth of its own taking and consents to be moved—that soul carries no wound into what comes next. Most stolen souls are not so lucky. Yours was. Remember that when the grief comes, keeper. Grief will come anyway. Let it sit at the table. Do not let it cook.*

Yara almost laughed, wet-eyed, at that—old gods speaking like grandmothers—and saw Sefa bite down on the same reaction.

*She will be reborn,* said the Sister of Spring. *In the living world. Young, as souls reborn are young. With new memories, as all the reborn have. She will not remember you, Ada Almeida. This is not cruelty. It is the same mercy every living thing receives: to begin unwounded.*

"And if she walks past my gate someday," Ada said, "and doesn't know me?"

*Then you will know her,* said the Sister of Autumn, *and you will have a choice, and we are bound now to thresholds, so we will be watching how you make it.* The god's attention gentled. *We think—I say we, we seven, who have just learned to say we again—we think you will make it well.*

The lantern flared once, softly, like a candle catching, and was gone—not extinguished but *departed*, drawn up through the broken ceiling of the world, through the crack above Thornfell, out into the night air and the rain and the long road toward wherever rebirth waits. Ada stood holding empty arms. Sefa put a hand on his shoulder and left it there. Pell—the real Pell, returned to himself the moment the Seventh released its borrowed shape, blinking and confused and asking what happened, *what happened, did I miss it*—did not understand why the smuggler and the gatekeeper both looked at him like that, and would not understand for years.

* * *

Vessar they gave to the mountain.

The king had been dragged down from the shattered lock by his own envoy, who had abandoned him the instant the rite failed—envoys of the Court knew better than anyone what happened to those who defaulted on contracts below. He knelt now at the edge of the pool, chained in bindings of contract-light that his own signed agreements had become, and he was still talking. That was the worst of it, Yara thought. Even now. Even kneeling before seven wakened gods in the ruins of his ambition, he was negotiating.

"—an equitable arrangement, clearly there are terms to be discussed, I hold legitimate claim, the survey was commissioned under crown authority—"

*You attempted to bind us,* said Karach-Under-Root, and the whole cavern groaned, stone remembering anger. *You took the seventh lock, which was our body, and began a rite to wear it as a crown. Speak to us of claims again and we will discuss what you have earned.*

But it was the Ferryman who stepped forward—or became foreground, in the way of things that have no fixed position—and the Ferryman's voice, when it came, was the voice of every river deciding, and it was calm, and it was the coldest sound in the cavern.

*The law of men would execute you,* the Ferryman said. *Execution is quick, and quickness is a mercy, and we owe you no mercy. But we owe you justice, because you are a creature of crossings too—you crossed into our court, you crossed every boundary that mattered. So hear your sentence, King Vessar of the Usurped Throne.*

*The mountain showed us mercy once. It held us gently for seventy years while the world forgot us. It kept us safe. It kept us whole. We give you the same mercy.*

The floor opened.

Not violently. The stone simply parted the way water parts, without malice, without haste, and Vessar sank to his waist in rock that closed around him like a hand around a caught bird, firm and unhurried and utterly indifferent to his screaming. The contract-chains dissolved into the stone with him, every agreement he'd signed below becoming part of his imprisonment, every clause a layer of the dark.

*You will be held,* said the Ferryman, *as we were held. Gently. Safely. Whole. For a very long time. The mountain does not hate you, king. That is what will break you. The mountain does not feel anything at all.*

His voice went under. The stone sealed smooth above his head, leaving no mark, no monument, no grave—just unbroken rock, somewhere beneath Thornfell, holding a man who would live a long unnatural life with nothing to look at and no one to negotiate with. Yara had imagined vengeance all her life in hot colors, fire and blood and triumphant shouting. This was none of those. This was colder and cleaner and it settled something in her that twenty years of wine had never touched.

"That's it?" Pell whispered. "That's just—it? He's just *down there*?"

"For a very long time," Yara said. "Longer than kingdoms. Longer than grudges." She looked at the smooth stone where a king had been. "Law could never have done this. Law needs a rope and a crowd and a name for the crime. The mountain just needed the truth."

* * *

The gods began to rise.

Yara felt it before she understood it—a lightening, a loosening, the Court Beneath exhaling after seventy held breaths. The seven shapes thinned, spread, poured themselves upward through the broken lock and the cracked ceiling and the miles of stone, going out into the world they had chosen to rejoin. Vethra of the Threshold went last, and paused at the ruin of the archway, and looked back—at Yara, at Ada, at Sefa and Dov and Pell, at the pool of settling souls, at all of it.

*One matter remains,* the Threshold said. *The gate above. Hollowgate stands unwarded, and the war above has not ended because a king is buried. Armies do not stop for miracles they haven't noticed yet. Refugees do. There are many of both on the roads tonight.*

"We know," Ada said. He was already moving, already reaching for his coat, twenty years of habit pulling him toward his post like a tide. "I'm going back."

*We know,* said Vethra, and the doorway-shaped god did something Yara had never known a god to do, in all her training, in all her order's oldest texts.

It bowed.

*Keeper of Hollowgate. Priestess of the Open Hand. Smuggler. Digger. Thief-child. We will meet you at the gate. Look for frost where frost should not be, and listen at the posts, and know that when you open what you built to guard, you do not open it alone.*

The light went up and out, and the Court Beneath was quiet for the first time in seventy years—quiet and clean, the pool ringing softly, the broken lock lying honest in pieces, nothing hidden anywhere.

Sefa broke the silence, because of course she did. "Right. Well. I don't know about the rest of you, but I've just been blessed by seven gods and I'd very much like a drink and a surface walk." She jerked her head toward the tunnel Dov was already holding open, silent and steady as ever, lantern raised. "Gatekeeper. You coming home?"

Ada looked around the Court one last time—at the place where his sister had waited seventy years, at the stone that held a king, at the pool where stolen souls were finding their way back to the world. Then he shouldered his pack and turned toward the passage up, toward the pass, toward the gate standing open and unguarded under the stars.

"Yes," he said. "There's a war to let people through."

And far above them, at the top of the long climb, the first travelers on the night road reported afterward that frost had bloomed along the gateposts of Hollowgate out of season, delicate and white and unmistakably deliberate—like a signature, like a promise, like something old and patient finally, gladly, coming home to wait by a door.
