## 21. Homecoming

Pell had learned to read the road from the top of the gatehouse, and what he saw on the morning the frost came back was a figure walking up the pass with a bundle on her shoulder and no fear in her at all.

That alone made him look twice. The war's aftermath still bled down every road into Hollowgate—carts with their wheels burned off, families carrying children who slept through everything because sleeping through everything was safer than waking. Everyone who came up that pass came hunched, watching the ridgelines, flinching at birdsong. But this woman walked like the road belonged to her. Straight-backed, unhurried, dark hair loose in the wind, a traveler's cloak the color of wet slate. She stopped once, maybe fifty yards out, and stood looking at the gate for so long that Pell leaned over the parapet to make sure she hadn't frozen there.

She wasn't looking at the gate, he realized. She was looking at the gatepost. The old one, the cracked one, where the frost bloomed now most evenings like breath on cold glass.

Then she smiled—not a big smile, just a small private one, the kind you give to something only you can see—and kept walking.

"Someone's coming," Pell called down, and heard Ada's boots on the stairs before he finished saying it. He always heard them now. In the months since the mountain woke, since the lock shattered and the gods poured out of it like light from a broken jar, Ada had stopped moving like a man trying not to be noticed. It was strange, still, seeing him take up space. Strange and good.

"Traveler?" Ada asked, coming to stand beside him at the wall. The wind pulled at his gray-streaked hair. His face had lines in it now—real lines, earned ones, the years the gods had given back settling into him like rain into dry ground. He looked older than Pell had ever known him to look, and younger too, somehow, both at once.

"Woman. Alone. Walking like she owns the pass."

Ada looked. And Pell felt him go still beside him—a stillness so complete it was louder than shouting.

"That's—" Ada's voice caught on something. "That's Lira."

Pell knew the name the way he knew his own heartbeat. He'd heard it whispered over campfires on the smugglers' roads, spoken once into the dark beneath Thornfell when Ada thought everyone slept: *Lira, I'm coming back for you.* He'd seen Ada stand in front of a soul-lantern wearing a seventeen-year-old's face and offer his own life to buy it free. He'd watched the gods give the soul back to the world instead—reborn, new-made, memories washed clean—and watched Ada nod and say *thank you* with a voice like a snapped wire, and mean it, and grieve anyway.

"She's alive," Pell said stupidly, because his chest was too full for anything clever.

"She's walking up my road." Ada's hands were flat on the stone of the parapet. Pell saw the tremor in them and pretended he didn't. "Seventy years, Pell. Seventy years she was a lantern in the dark, and now she's—" He stopped. Breathed. When he spoke again his voice was steady, the gatekeeper's voice, the one that could lie beautifully to captains and tell hard truths gently. "She doesn't know me."

"No."

"And she mustn't—" He turned, and his eyes were wet and fierce all at once. "I won't be a ghost standing in her doorway. I won't hand her seventy years of someone else's grief and call it welcome. If she comes here, she comes as a stranger, and if she stays—" His jaw worked. "If she stays, she stays because this place is hers to stay in. Not because she owes a dead man her past."

"You're not dead."

"I was, for seventy years." He almost smiled. "I'm learning to be otherwise. Go down and open the gate, would you? Before she has to knock."

Pell went. At the bottom of the stairs he paused, because below, in the yard, Sefa was already there—of course she was, Sefa heard everything on that mountain before it happened—leaning against the well with her arms crossed and an expression Pell had learned to translate as *I know exactly what's coming and I have opinions.*

"Dov's already put water on," she said. "Don't ask how he knows. He always knows."

* * *

Lira Almeida stopped three paces inside the gate and looked around as though confirming something with herself.

Pell had been assigned—had assigned himself—the duty of greeting her, mostly because Ada had suddenly discovered urgent business inspecting the eastern wall and Sefa had shoved Pell forward with both hands. Up close, the resemblance was staggering and also not there at all. She had Ada's dark eyes, or Ada had hers; the same stubborn set to the mouth. But where Ada carried himself like a man who had spent decades being careful, this woman moved like someone who had just been born and found the world interesting.

"Welcome to Hollowgate," Pell said. "I'm Pell. I do gates, mostly. And doors. And occasionally roofs."

"Hollowgate." She said the name slowly, tasting it. "I've been walking toward it for six days. I don't remember deciding to. I woke up on the coast road near Corvale with sand in my boots and this name in my mouth, and every crossroads since, something's told me which way to go." She glanced back at the gatepost, where a lace of frost was spreading across the old wood in the full warmth of morning. "The stones talk here, don't they. I can hear them. Is that normal?"

"It's getting more normal," Pell said. "Since the mountain woke up. The gods answer prayers at gates and crossroads now. Yara says it's the oldest covenant there is—you leave an offering, they leave a kindness. Mostly they just listen, honestly. People pray a lot when they're walking away from a war."

"I prayed at a shrine two days ago." Her brow furrowed. "I didn't know any gods' names. I just said, *whoever's listening—I want to go home.* And the candle flame bent toward the mountains." She looked at Pell, and there was something raw under the steadiness. "Except I don't have a home. That's the thing. I have a name and a direction and a feeling I can't explain. Like homesickness, but for somewhere I've never been."

Pell's throat hurt. Behind her, at the well, Dov had gone very still, water jug in his hands, watching her with the soft grave attention he gave to anything wounded. Sefa had pushed off the well and was pretending to fuss with a harness buckle, and doing it badly.

"Well," Pell managed. "You should eat something. We've got stew. There's always stew—Dov makes it, he doesn't talk but he communicates, you'll see. And the keeper here—"

"The keeper." Something flickered across her face. "I dreamed about a keeper. On the coast road. An old man at a gate counting coins by lamplight, and he looked up at me like I was a debt he'd been carrying." She shook her head, embarrassed. "Sorry. That's grim. You asked me nothing and I'm telling you my dreams."

"No," Pell said quickly. "Tell us everything. Tell us all of it."

Which was when Ada came out of the gatehouse.

Pell watched it happen and would remember it the rest of his life: Ada crossing the yard at an ordinary pace, an ordinary man in a patched coat, stopping an ordinary distance away. Every line of him screaming *nothing unusual here.* Only Pell, who had spent a year learning to read him, saw the effort of it—the way his hands stayed loose at his sides, the way he didn't step closer, didn't reach out, didn't do any of the ten thousand things Pell could feel him wanting to do.

"Welcome to Hollowgate," Ada said. "I'm Ada. I keep the gate."

"Lira." She offered her name like a gift and took his hand when he offered his. "Your gatepost is talking to me. Should I be worried?"

"The frost likes some people." Ada's voice was perfectly level. "It liked my sister, once."

Something crossed her face—confusion, recognition, a door opening onto a room she couldn't find. "Why does that feel—" She pressed her fingers to her temple. "I'm sorry. I keep having these echoes. Things I shouldn't know. I dreamed about this yard last night, except the gate was shut and there were ledgers everywhere, and someone was turning people away in the snow, and I was so angry at him—" She blinked, looked at Ada, laughed a little helplessly. "At you, actually. Isn't that strange? I was angry at you in a dream."

"Yes," Ada said softly. "That sounds like her."

"My lord?"

"Nothing." He cleared his throat. "Stew first. Echoes after. Dov—hear that? Stew."

Dov was already ladling bowls. He set one in Lira's hands and looked at her for a long moment, and then he did something Pell had never seen him do for a stranger: he touched two fingers to his own heart, then to hers. *Kin,* in the tunnel-clans' silent speech. Lira frowned like she was trying to remember a word from a language she'd never learned—and then she signed something back, clumsy and instinctive, and Dov's whole face broke open.

"What did I just say?" Lira asked.

"*Home,*" Dov wrote later, on his slate. "*You said home.*"

* * *

They gave her the small room above the stable, the one with the window facing the pass, and she stayed one night, and then another, and then she stopped counting, and so did everyone else.

It was strange, Pell thought, watching it happen. Grief didn't leave the yard—it just learned to share the table. Ada never told her. Not that week, not that month. He sat across from her at supper and listened to her stories about the coast road and the talking stones and the dream of the ledger-keeper, and he laughed in the right places, and twice Pell caught him in the doorway afterward, alone, pressing the heel of his hand against his eyes. Sefa started keeping a bottle of the good Corvale brandy behind the flour sacks for those nights, and never once mentioned it, and Ada never once thanked her, and between them that was a whole conversation.

Yara came up from Corvale at midsummer, robes clean, sigil bright on her palm, to consecrate the rebuilt shrine by the gatepost. She took one look at Lira, one longer look at Ada, and said nothing at all—which from Yara was a sermon in itself. She blessed the gate, blessed the road, blessed the frost, and when Lira asked her, shyly, whether the gods ever gave things back, Yara said: "They gave back more than they took, child. That's the scandal of it. Grace always overspends its budget." And Lira laughed, and Yara held Ada's eye over her head for one long moment, and whatever passed between them, Ada stood straighter after.

Sefa and Dov built their house that autumn on the rise east of the gate, close enough to shout to, far enough to have their own smoke. Sefa ran cargo up and down the reopened passes—honest cargo now, mostly, though Pell had his doubts about the third crate in every shipment—and Dov carved the lintel himself, in the old clan signs: *ALL WHO KNOCK ARE KIN.* Refugees coming up from the burned valleys learned to look for that smoke the way sailors look for lighthouses.

And Lira learned the gate.

Nobody taught her; that was the uncanny part. She simply began appearing at the post at dusk when travelers needed tending, knowing which families needed blankets and which needed silence, knowing how to quiet a frightened horse, knowing the exact words that made a weeping child laugh. The frost bloomed thickest wherever she stood. Once Pell found her standing in the yard at midnight, arms wrapped around herself, staring up at Thornfell.

"I dreamed about the mountain again," she said, without turning. "There was a court under it. Lights everywhere, like lanterns. And someone was holding my hand, telling me it was time to go home, and I was scared, and then I wasn't." She turned. "Do you think dreams can be memories?"

Pell thought about a lantern with his friend's sister's face. Thought about a man offering his life in the dark. "I think," he said carefully, "that some things are true even when we can't remember them. And that remembering isn't the only way of knowing someone." He hesitated. "The keeper here. Ada. Do you like him?"

"He's the easiest person to be quiet with that I've ever met." She said it simply, like reporting the weather. "Isn't that odd? I've known him two months and I already know how he takes his tea and which stair creaks and that he hums when he thinks no one's listening. Some old song. He doesn't know he does it." She smiled, and it was Ada's smile, and Pell had to look away. "He feels like home. All of this does. Like I left something here and came back for it."

"Maybe you did," Pell said. "Maybe it'll turn up."

* * *

On the first night of winter, a year almost to the day since the lock shattered, the frost bloomed across the whole gatepost at once, silver and intricate as lace, and every traveler in the yard went quiet without knowing why.

Ada lit the lamps. Lira brought the stew. Sefa and Dov came down from the rise with a jug of something illegal, and Yara arrived on the evening coach as if summoned, which perhaps she was. They ate at the long table in the gatehouse, all of them, refugees and keepers and gods-touched alike, and the windows steamed, and outside the wind came down off Thornfell singing through the stones in voices that sounded almost like names.

Lira sat next to Ada. She'd taken to sitting next to Ada. Neither of them remarked on it.

"There's a song," she said suddenly, to the table, to the firelight. "The one he hums. I finally remembered where I know it from. My mother used to sing it—or—no." She frowned, hand at her temple. "Someone's mother. Someone sang it waiting for someone to come home." She looked at Ada, and the firelight moved in her eyes, and for a moment—just a moment—something ancient and patient looked out of her face. "Was it you? Were you waiting for someone?"

The table went still. Pell forgot to breathe.

Ada set down his spoon. Pell watched him choose—watched the whole shape of the choice move across his face, seventy years of grief and one year of discipline and all the love he'd carried like a coal in his chest since the dark beneath the mountain. He could claim her. One sentence and she was his sister, the debt paid, the loss named. One sentence and he handed her every weight she'd been mercifully spared.

"No," Ada said gently. "But I think whoever it was, they're home now."

Lira studied him for a long moment. Then she reached over and, very matter-of-factly, tucked the blanket more snugly around his shoulders, the way you do for family, the way you do without thinking.

"You should hum it properly sometime," she said. "For whoever was waiting."

Outside, the frost climbed the gatepost and did not melt, and far up the pass, where the road bent toward the mountain, the stones murmured to each other in the dark—and Pell, watching Ada's face in the lamplight, saw him mouth two words to no one, to the frost, to the listening gods:

*Thank you. Stay.*

And the frost, very softly, bloomed once more.
