## 3. What the Inquisitors Burn

The village of Hallowmere sat at the edge of the map like a crumb at the edge of a plate—unimportant, easily brushed away, and therefore, Rafael had always believed, safe. He had come for salt pork and lamp oil, the ordinary arithmetic of a man passing through, and he had meant to be gone before noon.

He was still in the market square when the wagons came.

There were three of them, painted grey as old ash, drawn by horses whose eyes had gone flat and pale, like coins left too long in water. The men who rode them wore no livery he recognized, which was itself a kind of livery; every order that mattered in Veyland had learned to dress like nothing at all. But Rafael knew them anyway. He had seen their kind once, seven years ago, from a great distance, through rain, and he had told himself then what he told himself now: *not your war. Not your name. Keep walking.*

They were not burning anything yet. That was the first strange thing. He had expected fire—inquisitors were supposed to burn—but these men were collecting. They moved door to door with the unhurried courtesy of tax collectors, and they carried ledgers instead of torches, and into those ledgers they wrote down everything they took.

Rafael stood in the shadow of the chandler's awning and watched.

An old man surrendered a bundle of letters tied with faded ribbon. A woman handed over a christening gown, yellowed to the color of weak tea. Two soldiers pried up a gravestone from the churchyard with crowbars, grunting, and loaded it onto the second wagon beside a dozen others, all laid flat like slabs of meat. On each stone, before it went into the wagon, one of the inquisitors—a thin man with ink-stained fingers and a voice like dry paper—would read the name aloud, softly, almost tenderly, and then write something in his ledger, and the stone would go blank.

Not cracked. Not defaced. Blank. Smooth as river ice, as if no chisel had ever touched it.

Rafael's hand drifted to the strap of his pack and gripped it until the leather creaked.

*Keep walking.*

He had gotten very good at keep walking. Seven years of it. He knew how to be furniture, weather, background noise. He knew how to stand in a market square while history was carted away three wagonloads at a time, because a man who had erased himself could not afford to have opinions, and a man with no opinions had no business interfering when other people's names were being scraped off stones.

The thin inquisitor turned toward the chandler's shop.

Rafael bought his lamp oil without meeting anyone's eyes, counted out exact coin, and was halfway down the lane toward the east road when he heard the singing.

It came from the last house on the square, a low stone cottage with geraniums in the window, and it was an old song—the kind mothers sang, the kind with no author anymore, worn smooth by generations of mouths. An old woman stood in her doorway singing it, holding onto the frame with both hands, and facing her on the step was a broad-shouldered man in a farmer's coat, laughing, trying to shush her, embarrassed the way grown sons are embarrassed by their mothers.

"Mother, they're only taking the *things*, not us—"

"You'll want it when I'm gone," she sang over him, sweet and cracked. "You'll want to know I kept it."

She went inside and came back with a small wooden box, and Rafael, watching from thirty feet away, saw the inquisitor's face change. Saw the thin man set down his ledger. Saw him smile.

"That," the inquisitor said gently, "is exactly the sort of thing we've come for."

"It's her wedding veil," the son said. His laugh had died somewhere. "And my father's letters, and—" He stopped, because the inquisitor had opened the box, and whatever was inside it, the inquisitor looked at it the way a starving man looks at bread.

"Maera Colm," the inquisitor read, from something written within. "Who loved Tomas Colm, who died in the flood year. Who bore a son, Denny." He looked up. "Denny. That's you?"

"I'm Denny," the farmer said, and there was already something wrong with his voice, a thinness, a doubt, as if the word had been asked of him rather than offered.

"We'll take good care of it," the inquisitor said, and closed the box, and wrote in his ledger, and the old woman swayed on her feet like a woman struck.

"No," she said. "No, wait. Wait, I had—I was going to give him—" She looked around, lost, her hands opening and closing on air. "I had something. For my son. Where is my son? Denny?" She looked straight through the broad-shouldered man standing in front of her. Looked *through* him, the way one looks through a fencepost, through a tree, through anything that has never been a person at all. "I had a son. Didn't I? I had a little boy. Where did I put him down?"

"*Mother.*" The farmer caught her arms. "*Mother, it's me.*"

She screamed. That was the worst of it—not the forgetting but the scream after, the sound of a woman finding a stranger's hands on her body, a stranger's tears falling on her face, a stranger calling himself her child in a voice she did not know. She fought him. She fought him the way you fight off an animal in the dark, all instinct and no thought, until two soldiers pulled her back and she stood there trembling in the doorway of her own house, among her own geraniums, asking the air in a small polite voice whether anyone had seen her boy, he'd be about forty now, she'd know him anywhere, she'd know him *anywhere.*

Rafael did not remember crossing the square.

That was the thing he would turn over later, in the dark, on the road, unable to sleep: that there was no decision in it at all. Seven years of discipline, seven years of counting days and keeping his head down and paying any price for silence—and none of it held. His body simply moved, the way a bowstring moves, and the storm that lived coiled and starved beneath his skin came up his arm like water finding a crack.

He did not call lightning. Lightning was a confession; lightning lit the whole sky with his signature. What he did was smaller, finer, the first trick his mother's people had ever taught him: he reached into the iron nail-heads of the nearest wagon wheel, into the cold sleeping metal, and woke them—one spark, no bigger than a moth, passed from nail to nail to nail along the wagon's rim until it found the axle grease and the dry wood of the bed where the ledgers lay.

The wagon caught fire with a soft whump, like a breath let out.

Flames took the ledgers first. Paper loves fire; paper has been waiting for it all its life. The thin inquisitor shouted and lunged for the burning books and snatched his hand back, and in that moment of chaos Rafael did the only other thing he could think of: he let the spark leap the gap between the wagon and the old woman's memory—or rather, he let it leap toward the inquisitor's ledger-page where her name had been written, and he *pulled*, the way one pulls a thread, and felt something tear loose from the page and fly free, unclaimed, unowned, back into the smoke.

Whether it reached her, he never knew. There was no time to see. The soldiers were turning, the horses screaming, the thin inquisitor rising with his ink-stained fingers spread wide and his flat pale eyes fixed—not on the fire, not on the wagon, but on Rafael, on the space where Rafael stood, with an expression of dawning, delighted recognition, like a collector spotting a piece he had been hunting for years.

"There you are," the inquisitor said.

Rafael ran.

* * *

He ran the way water runs, downhill and without glory—through the pig-yard, over a wall slick with moss, along the drainage ditch behind the tannery where the stink covered his scent from anything that might follow by nose. His lungs burned with a fire older than the wagon's. His arm still tingled to the elbow, humming, *full*, and that was the terror of it, that was the thing that made him stumble on the ditch bank and catch himself against a fence post and hang there gasping like a landed fish:

It had felt *good.*

Seven years since he had touched it, and the power had not diminished. It had waited. It had lain coiled under all his careful gray nothingness, patient as a seed, and the moment he opened the smallest crack it had surged through him with the gratitude of a long-starved dog, and every nerve in his body had sung *yes, yes, again, more*—

And underneath the yes, quiet as a knife between ribs, another voice, one he had spent seven years drowning: *this is how it starts. This is exactly how it started last time.*

He made the tree line. He made the shepherd's path above the village. From there, chest heaving, hands braced on his knees, he allowed himself one look back.

Hallowmere smoked gently in the afternoon light. The wagon-fire had been put out—of course it had; they were not amateurs—but the market square was full of people standing very still, and the grey wagons were being repacked, and on the doorstep of the last house the old woman sat wrapped in a blanket, and beside her knelt the broad-shouldered man, still talking to her, still saying *mother, it's me,* with the terrible patience of a man who has decided he will say it every day for the rest of his life if he has to, even if she never once believes him.

Rafael watched until his eyes blurred.

Then he turned uphill, into the pines, and walked, and the walking was the old walking, the practiced walking, the keep-walking that had kept him alive for seven years. Except that now, with every step, the hum in his arm reminded him: *they saw. He saw. There you are.*

Inquisitors marked what they touched. He didn't know how—he suspected the thin man hadn't known either, that the marking was the Veil's work, done through its servants the way a spider feels through its web—but he knew it in his bones, in the place where knowledge lived that had no evidence and no argument and was never once wrong. Something of him had stayed behind in that square. A thread, fine as spider silk, running from his spine down the hill, through the smoking village, into a ledger that had not burned after all.

By nightfall he had put ten miles between himself and Hallowmere, and the thread still pulled, faint as a fisherman's line, and he had stopped twice to check his own forearm in the firelight, half expecting to find writing on it.

On the third night, camped in the ruins of a watchtower with the wind coming off the Thornfell passes, he dreamed of lightning for the first time in years. In the dream it fell from his own hand, and it curved, mid-air, the way a hawk curves, deliberate and hungry, and he woke with his heart slamming and his arm raised toward the ceiling of broken stone, sparks crawling on his fingertips like insects, and he had to sit in the dark for an hour breathing slowly, slowly, until they died.

*One spark,* he told himself. *One. You paid for it, you'll pay for it, and now you bury it deeper than before.*

But he caught himself, in the gray hour before dawn, flexing his fingers. Testing. The way a man tests a tooth that aches.

And far behind him, down in the valley, he could feel it—faint, patient, pleased—the pull of the thread, drawing tight.
