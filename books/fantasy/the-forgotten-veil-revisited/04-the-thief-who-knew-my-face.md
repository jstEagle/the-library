## 4. The Thief Who Knew My Face

Sera Vane had stolen eleven hundred memories in her career, and she had learned long ago that the work was easiest when she thought of people as houses.

A mind was a structure with rooms. Some were locked; most were not. People left their grief on the kitchen table like unwashed dishes. They kept their first kiss in a drawer with the buttons they'd never sew back on. You slipped in through whatever window they'd forgotten to bar, you took what the client had paid for—or what the Veil's agents had marked—and you left before anyone noticed the shape of the absence. The best thefts were ones the victim never felt at all. She prided herself on that. *He won't even miss it*, she'd tell clients, and it was almost always true.

The man walking the cliff road below her was going to be difficult, and she knew it before she ever touched him.

She'd been watching him for two days from ridgelines and hedgerows, the way she watched everything: patiently, hungrily, with a thief's arithmetic running constant in the back of her skull. He traveled alone. He moved like a man who had practiced being unremarkable until the practice became bone—shoulders loose, pace even, eyes down but never *down*, always flicking to the high ground, the treeline, the blind curves. He wore road-dust gray and carried nothing that gleamed. In Thornfell, where half the kingdom's smuggled goods passed through hands that didn't exist, he was known by no name at all, which was itself a kind of signature. Nobody erased themselves that thoroughly by accident.

And the Veil wanted him. That was the part that made her mouth go dry.

The contract had come through the usual channels—a dead-drop letter written in ink that shifted color when she wasn't looking directly at it, which meant Veil-bound, which meant the price would be obscene and the questions would be few. *The storm-caller on the coast road. Take everything. Leave the body breathing.* Everything. Not a memory or a handful—not the standard surgical extraction—but the whole house, emptied to the studs. She'd done total harvests twice. Both times the person left behind had been a soft-eyed stranger wearing a familiar face, smiling politely at their own children.

Sera told herself the coin was reason enough. It was more money than ten years of ordinary thieving. It would buy her out of the trade entirely, buy a cottage somewhere the Veil's reach went thin, buy silence and salt air and an ending.

She told herself that all morning. By late afternoon, watching him pick his way along the cliff path with the sea booming far below, she stopped believing herself, and she hated that.

*One job*, she reminded her reflection in a rain pool that morning. *One job and you're done.*

---

The cliff road was the only way north from Thornfell's coast unless a traveler fancied three extra days through passes that had lately begun to misbehave—villages appearing on no map, roads that bent back on themselves like question marks. Sera didn't know yet why the geography was rotting. She knew only that the Veil's appetite had changed flavor in recent months, from memories of people to memories of *places*, as if it were eating the kingdom's setting before its cast. That frightened her in a way she refused to examine.

She chose her ground carefully. A quarter mile ahead of him, the path narrowed where the cliff had shed a shoulder of rock some winter past—a chokepoint six feet wide, the sea four hundred feet below, no room to fight and nowhere to run. She got there first, settled into the shadow of a leaning boulder, and did what she did best: she waited, and she opened herself.

Memory-thieving wasn't magic, exactly, though the Veil-bound liked to call it that to make themselves feel holy. It was older than magic and hungrier. It was a door in her that had been open since birth, a door other people didn't have, and over the years she'd learned to walk through it while standing perfectly still. She let her awareness spill out along the road like oil finding cracks, thin and patient, until it brushed the edges of his mind.

Even at that distance, through that gossamer contact, she flinched.

Most minds, touched from outside, tasted like their contents. Fear tasted like fear. Guilt like rust. This man's mind tasted like a *storm cellar*—like something enormous sealed behind thick walls, muffled, pressing, alive. And beneath the seal, faint as a heartbeat through stone, something crackled. Her skin prickled with static that wasn't there. The hairs on her forearms lifted.

*Storm-caller.* So the letter hadn't lied. Seven years the rumors said he'd been quiet, seven years since whatever catastrophe had driven him into erasure, and still the power sat in him like weather waiting for permission.

Fine. She'd taken things bigger than weather. She'd taken a bishop's faith once, whole, and he'd thanked her afterward because the doubt had gone with it.

She waited for him to enter the narrows.

He came slow, the way careful people come to chokepoints—pausing at the mouth of it, reading the rock overhead, checking the scree for disturbed stones. For one absurd moment she was certain he'd seen her, certain the whole elegant plan was already ash, and her hand closed around the knife at her hip without her deciding anything. But then he exhaled and stepped in, committing himself to the six feet of path with the void yawning below, and Sera rose out of the boulder's shadow behind him and said, in the flat professional voice she used for opening negotiations:

"Don't turn around. Don't reach for anything. You're going to stand very still, and this will be over before you know it happened."

He went still. Good. Most of them did, at first.

"Who sent you?" His voice was low, roughened by disuse, the voice of a man who spoke maybe fifty words a day. "The crown? Corvel?"

"The crown doesn't know I exist." She drifted closer, boots silent on the shale. "That's rather the point of me."

"You're a taker." He said it the way other men said *leper*. "I can feel you at the edge. Get out."

"I'm afraid getting out is the last thing I do." She was close enough now, close enough to touch, and she raised her hand toward the back of his skull the way a surgeon raises a scalpel—no malice in it, just procedure. "Here's how this goes. I'm going to open a door, and I'm going to walk through your rooms, and I'm going to take what I was paid to take. If you fight me, it hurts. If you don't, you'll wake up tomorrow missing some years and never quite know which ones. Everyone I've ever done it to says the same thing afterward—" she let her fingertips hover a breath from his hair, "—they say they don't remember me at all."

"I'll fight," he said quietly.

"They all say that too."

She touched him.

---

The door in him opened the way doors open in dreams—all at once, and onto somewhere impossible.

Sera had prepared herself for a fortress. For grief bricked over, for rage banked and banked again, for seven years of deliberate darkness. She had prepared herself to move fast and take ugly, because total harvests were butchery and she'd accepted that, she'd counted the coin against the sin and found the coin heavier, God help her.

What she fell into instead was a hallway.

And the hallway was *hers*.

Not similar. Not reminiscent. Hers—the actual corridor of the actual house where she had grown up, the smuggler's cottage above Greywater Bay, with the water stain on the ceiling shaped like a leaping fish and the third floorboard from the stairs that sang a flat note when you stepped on it. She was standing inside Rafael Amberdale's memory, and the memory was her childhood home, rendered in perfect, merciless detail: the smell of tar and her mother's dried lavender, the sound of the sea through shutters that never quite closed, the scratch marks on the doorframe where her father had measured her height every solstice with a boning knife.

*No.*

She tried to pull back. Thieves learned early that you never lingered—you dipped, you took, you surfaced—but her body wouldn't answer. The memory had hold of her the way deep water holds a swimmer who has looked down.

She turned, in the dream-logic of the intrusion, and there was a girl in the corridor. Small, maybe eight, dark-haired, barefoot on the singing floorboard, frozen mid-sneak with a heel of bread in her fist. The girl looked up at her and Sera's heart stopped, because she knew that face. She saw it every time she dared look in a mirror for longer than shaving required. She had not seen it at eight years old in twenty-two years, because she had sold those years—sold them in pieces, one desperate season at a time, until the girl was a stranger described to her by others.

But here the girl was. Here the bread was. Here the lavender was.

And the memory wasn't hers.

It couldn't be hers. It was *his*—she could feel the ownership stamped through it like a watermark, feel the edges of Rafael Amberdale's consciousness all around this room the way you feel the walls of a house at night. Which meant—

Which meant he had been there. Inside her childhood. Inside the cottage above Greywater Bay, in the years she'd sold away, in the rooms she could no longer enter even in her own head.

*How?*

The girl moved. Set the bread down on a windowsill, deliberate as a ritual, and looked up at Sera with eyes that held something no eight-year-old should manage—an adult's weary recognition.

"He keeps it safe," the girl said, in Sera's own child-voice. "He doesn't know whose it is. He just knows someone lost it, and he keeps it."

Then the corridor folded like paper, and Sera was falling out of him, gasping, stumbling backward across the shale with her hand pressed over her mouth.

---

Rafael Amberdale had turned around at some point. She didn't know when. He stood at the narrow waist of the cliff path with the wind pulling at his coat, watching her come apart, and his face was doing something complicated—alarm braided with a strange, terrible gentleness.

"What did you see?" he asked.

"Nothing." The lie came out cracked down the middle. She steadied herself against the boulder. The sea boomed below, indifferent. "Nothing. I missed. It happens."

"You're shaking."

"It's the wind."

"Sera Vane." He said her name and her whole body went cold, because she had never given it to him, because no one on this road knew it, because—she understood a half-second later—he'd read it off her in the instant their minds had touched, the way she read street names off strangers. Fair was fair. Thieves got robbed too. "You went into my head and came out crying. What did you see?"

"I told you. Nothing." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, furious at the wetness there, furious at him, furious most of all at the thing blooming under her sternum that felt disgracefully like *tenderness*. He had kept it. This man, this stranger, this target—he had walked through some ruined corner of a life that wasn't his and found a child's stolen years lying in the wreckage, and instead of selling them, instead of using them, he had *kept them safe*. Who did that? What kind of person hoarded another person's childhood like contraband, guarding it for a owner he'd never met?

Someone dangerous, she thought. Someone the Veil would very much want dead.

Her hand was still on her knife. She made herself look at it, and then at him, and do the arithmetic she was famous for. The contract said take everything, leave him breathing. She was the best in the trade; if she committed, she would succeed. He was strong, but strength meant nothing against the door in her. One touch. Ten seconds. Done forever, and the cottage in Greywater Bay bought and paid for.

Except.

Except that if she emptied him, whatever he was keeping—that corridor, that girl, that bread on the windowsill—would go with the rest. Total harvests took everything. There was no surgery delicate enough to cut around a single memory in a storm cellar like his. She would be the one who destroyed it. She would walk away rich, and the girl with the bread would be gone, and no one—no one in all the world—would have those years anymore except the Veil, which ate them, which kept nothing, which only consumed.

"You're deciding whether to kill me," Rafael said. Not a question.

"I'm deciding whether you're worth the trouble of not killing." She straightened. Put the wind back in her spine where it belonged. "Here's what happens now. You're going to forget this conversation. Not all of it—I'm not good enough to be neat on a moving target, and honestly I don't care if you remember my face. But you'll forget the important part. You'll forget that I was ever inside."

"And the contract?" Something flickered behind his eyes—wariness, yes, but underneath it, incredibly, concern. For *her*. "You were paid. They'll know you failed."

"They won't." She was already backing toward the upper path, putting rock between them, because her hands were not steady and she refused to let him see. "As far as the Veil will ever know, Sera Vane reached into the storm-caller's mind and found nothing worth taking. A hollow man. An erased man. Isn't that what you've spent seven years pretending to be?"

He didn't answer. The wind filled the silence with the sound of the sea working the cliffs below, patient as erosion.

"Why?" he asked finally. Just that. Why.

Because you have my childhood in your cellar, she didn't say. Because touching you was like coming home to a house I burned down myself. Because in nineteen years of theft no one has ever *kept* anything of mine, and I don't know what to do with a man who guards what he steals.

"Because I missed," she said. "It happens. Even to the best."

She turned and climbed, taking the goat track upward two-handed, and she did not look back, because looking back was how thieves died—she'd learned that in her first year, from a mentor whose name she had sold along with everything else.

But halfway up, her fingers found the knife at her hip, and she realized she had drawn it during the harvest without knowing—drawn it and then, at some point she could not recall, put it away again. Unhurting. Unused.

She had never once, in eleven hundred thefts, put the knife away.

Below her, small against the vast gray sweep of the cliff road, Rafael Amberdale stood a moment longer in the wind. Then he continued north, toward the capital, toward the drowned archive he didn't yet know existed, toward the prophecy with his name circled in dead ink.

Toward the throne room where something wearing a king's face had already marked his approach.

Sera watched him go until the path bent and hid him. Then she sat down hard on the cold rock, took out the contract letter, and watched the shifting ink rearrange itself into new words as the Veil registered her failure:

*You looked. Now it knows you looked. Run, little thief.*

She ran.

* * *

Three days later, on the road through the collapsing passes, she stepped out of the treeline in front of him again—mud-spattered, sleepless, and lying with every breath.

"You're being followed," she said. "By worse than me. And whether you trust me or not, you're about to learn why that matters."

Rafael Amberdale looked at her for a long moment, and Sera saw him decide—saw the exact instant a man who trusted nothing chose, against all his own arithmetic, to make an exception.

She distrusted herself completely for making him do it.
