## 6. Seven Years of Weather

The safehouse smelled of tallow and old rain, and Rafael Amberdale counted the days the way other men counted coins.

Seven years. Two thousand five hundred and fifty-seven mornings since he had last let the storm through his skin. He kept the tally in a ledger with no name on its cover, one small mark for each day, and some nights—most nights—he ran his thumb down the columns like a man reading letters from someone who would never write again.

Outside, Thornfell's autumn pressed against the shutters. The mountains did their slow work of eroding, the river did its patient work of forgetting, and Rafael sat in a room with no name over the door and did the only magic left to him: he remembered nothing out loud.

He had been mending a saddle strap when the itch began.

It always started the same way—a prickling along the forearms, as if the skin remembered being sky before it remembered being flesh. A pressure behind the eyes like weather deciding. He set down the awl and breathed the way the monks of the Ashen Order had taught him, back when he'd been someone worth teaching: in for four, hold for seven, out for eight. Count the cracks in the floorboards. Name them, if you must. *One. Two. Three.*

Do not call the storm.

*Do not call the storm.*

He had written those words on the inside of his own eyelids so thoroughly that he sometimes saw them when he slept.

* * *

Seven years ago there had been rain too, but a different kind—the warm, fat rain of the lowlands in late summer, the kind that made the whole city of Vellmar smell of wet stone and frying onions. Rafael had been twenty-four and invincible, or near enough that it amounted to the same thing. The storm lived in him like a second heartbeat, and he had never once thought to be afraid of it.

Magistrate Corven Hale had been the kind of corruption that wore a clean collar. He took bribes from the grain syndicates while the mill districts starved, and when a miller's boy named Tam had asked why, publicly, in the square, Hale had him flogged until the questions stopped. Rafael had gone to the magistrate's townhouse on a night of gathering thunder with justice in his mouth and lightning in his hands, and he had told himself—he still told himself—that what he meant to do was measured. A warning. A mark burned into the doorframe above the man's head. The storm aimed like an arrow when you were calm, and Rafael had been calm. He had been *certain*.

He had not been alone.

Elias had come with him because Elias came everywhere with him, because they had been inseparable since the academy years, because Elias loved storms the way sailors love the sea—from the deck, laughing, arms spread—and because Elias had said, standing in the rain with his dark hair plastered to his forehead, *"Rafe, wait,"* and stepped forward at exactly the wrong instant, at exactly the moment Rafael loosed.

Rafael had spent seven years trying to remember whether Elias had stepped into the bolt or whether the bolt had reached for him. It was the question he built his whole exile around and could never answer, the stone at the bottom of the well he dropped everything else down after it. Because there was a third possibility, the one that woke him at the hour before dawn sweating and silent: that the storm had simply been too big, that his certainty had been arrogance, that he had never once in his life checked the size of what he carried until it was already leaving his hands.

The lightning had struck Elias through the chest and thrown him eleven feet across the courtyard, and Rafael had run to him through the rain and held him and felt, beneath his palms, the terrible ordinary weight of a body that no longer answered to its name.

Elias had died looking at him. That was the part Rafael could never burn out of himself, though he had tried every way a man can try. Not in pain. In *recognition*. As if even at the end, even killed by it, Elias had known exactly whose storm it was, and had not—could not have—blamed him, and somehow that was worse than blame. Blame would have given Rafael something to argue with for the rest of his life.

Instead there had only been the rain, and the magistrate's door standing open, and Corven Hale's face going white in the lamplight, untouched, unmarked, alive.

Rafael had not struck him again. He had knelt in the courtyard holding the man he loved and done nothing, and the doing-nothing had lasted seven years.

* * *

In the present, the itch climbed from his forearms to his shoulders.

Rafael got up and walked the safehouse the way he did every night, touching the things that anchored him: the iron poker (iron hated storm-work; holding it was like gripping a hand that argued back), the chipped cup Elias had never drunk from because it had never belonged to him, the ledger with its columns of marks. Outside, the wind had changed. It came off the peaks now instead of up the valley, and it carried something under the smell of pine and coming snow.

Smoke. Woodsmoke, but wrong—too sweet, too uniform, the smoke of fires lit all at once by people moving in formation.

He went to the gap between the shutters and looked down the mountain road, and there they were: four riders in grey, moving without lanterns, which no honest traveler did on the Thornfell passes at night. Veil-bound. He had seen them twice from a distance since the border village—the old woman who had forgotten her son mid-embrace, the single spark he had let loose, the relief and terror of it like blood returning to a dead limb. He had fled knowing they marked him. He had hoped, in the coward's arithmetic he permitted himself, that the marking would take time to follow.

It had taken nine days.

They stopped at the bend where the road met his track, and one of them dismounted and stood very still, head tilted, like a hound tasting air. Even at this distance Rafael felt it—the sensation of being *read*, of something paging through him looking for a particular word.

Then the rider raised his head, and though the night was moonless and the distance great, Rafael heard the voice as clearly as if its owner stood at his shoulder.

*"Rafe."*

His whole body flinched. His hand found the wall.

*"Rafe, it's cold out here. Let me in."*

It was Elias's voice. Not an imitation—not quite. The Veil ate memory, and what it ate it could wear, the way it wore King Aldric's crown, the way its inquisitors wore the shapes of men. Somewhere in the drowned dark of that thing's hoard hung the memory of a man's voice saying his name in the rain, and now the thing had fished it out and put it on like a coat.

*"You always did leave the door open for me. Even when you swore you wouldn't."*

"Get out of my head," Rafael whispered. Saying it aloud was a mistake—he knew it was a mistake—but grief is not a disciplined soldier. Grief breaks formation and runs at the enemy screaming.

*"I'm not in your head, Rafe. I'm in your past. There's a difference. Come down here and I'll show you. I have all of him. Every year. Every morning. I even have the parts you've forgotten—you've forgotten so much, haven't you? Seven years of not-looking. Do you remember the color of his coat? You don't, do you. But I do. Grey wool, silver buttons, a tear in the left cuff he never mended because you liked it."*

Rafael's breath came ragged. The color of the coat. He had lost the color of the coat. He had lost it sometime in the fourth year, quietly, the way teeth go loose, and he had never told anyone because there was no one to tell, and now this thing recited the loss back to him like a bill presented for payment.

Grey wool. Silver buttons. The torn cuff.

Was it true? Was any of it true? That was the poison in it—the Veil didn't need to lie, because a grieving man cannot check his own heart's inventory. It could tell him Elias had green eyes or brown, that he laughed high or low, that his last word had been *wait* or *Rafe* or nothing at all, and every version would fit the wound.

That was how it farmed them, Sera Vane had said on the cliff road, before either of them trusted the other: grief was the richest soil the Veil knew. A man who would sell anything to feel his dead again. A kingdom full of such men, once the archives emptied and nothing remained but the ache.

*"Come down,"* the voice said, softer now, kinder, which was crueler. *"You've punished yourself enough. Seven years, Rafe. Don't you think he'd want—"*

"Don't." The word cracked out of him with force enough to rattle the shutter. "Don't you *dare* tell me what he'd want."

Because that was the thing, wasn't it. Elias would have wanted him to live. Elias would have wanted him to forgive himself, to marry some patient miller's daughter, to grow apples, to be soft and ordinary and unhaunted. And Rafael had refused—all of it, every mercy—for seven years, because refusing was the only punishment that fit a crime with no sentence, and because as long as he hurt, some part of him could pretend the hurting was loyalty.

The Veil knew that. The Veil had read him like a ledger.

*"Or,"* the voice said, and now the kindness drained out of it, leaving something vast and patient underneath, something older than the mountains it spoke from, *"you could come down and take him back. We keep everything, storm-caller. Nothing we eat is destroyed. He is here. He is whole. He is waiting. All you have to do is stop counting the days."*

And there it was. The offer under the taunt, the hook under the bait. Stop counting. Stop the ledger. Let go of the arithmetic of absence and drown in the archive where everything stolen was kept forever, perfectly preserved, perfectly hollow—memories like fish in dark water, beautiful and dead.

Rafael looked at his hands. They were shaking, and beneath the skin of them the storm pressed like a fist against a door.

For one black moment—one heartbeat, no more—he wanted it. Wanted it with a purity that frightened him more than the riders on the road. To stop counting. To stop waking. To walk down into that sweet dark water and find Elias waiting with his torn cuff and his sailor's laugh and never ask again whether the bolt had reached for him.

Then his eye fell on the ledger. On the marks. Two thousand five hundred and fifty-seven small strokes of ink, each one a day he had chosen the hard, dry, empty world over the easy flooded one.

If he went down there, the counting stopped. And if the counting stopped, then the seven years meant nothing. Then Elias had died for nothing, and worse—had been *used* for nothing, worn like a mask by a thing that ate kingdoms.

"No," Rafael said aloud, to the voice, to the night, to himself. "No. He's mine to remember. Not yours. You don't get him."

The silence on the road stretched long. When the voice came again, it was the inquisitor's own—flat, grey, official.

"Surrender the book, storm-caller, and the crown's mercy is broad."

"The book," Rafael repeated. So that was the secondary object. The prophecy with tonight's date circled in dead ink, the page he had burned and the words that had grown back on his forearm like frost. They wanted it. Which meant it mattered. Which meant, whatever else was true, the dying fence in Thornfell had sold him something worth killing for.

He moved through the safehouse fast and quiet, the way he moved through everything now, unseen, unnamed. Blanket. Knife. Iron poker—no, leave it, iron slowed the hands and tonight his hands might need to be quick. The ledger he hesitated over, then wrapped in oilcloth and bound inside his shirt, against his sternum, where the itch of the storm was loudest.

At the back wall he pulled loose the stones he'd mortared loosely two winters ago, and slid into the cold throat of the old smuggler's tunnel, the blind passes he knew better than the backs of his lying hands.

Behind him, the safehouse door splintered. Boots. Torchlight sweeping walls that held nothing worth remembering except, folded on the table, a single page torn from a blank ledger, on which Rafael had written in a careful hand:

*Two thousand five hundred and fifty-eight.*

Let them carry that to their master. Let the thing in the crown chew on it.

He crawled into the dark of the mountain, and the mountain closed over him like a hand, and somewhere below—days below, weeks below, if the maps in his head still pointed anywhere at all—there was a capital, and a throne, and a drowned archive full of everything the Veil had ever eaten.

Including, if the voice on the road had told the truth about anything, a man in a grey coat with a torn left cuff.

Rafael crawled toward the dark and told himself he was running from the inquisitors.

He was almost able to believe it.
