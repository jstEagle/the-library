## 7. The Road Through Thornfell Closes

Rafael felt the mountain change before he saw it.

They had been riding since the grey hour before dawn, following a goat track above the Ashenmere valley, and somewhere in the last mile the world had gone quiet in the wrong way. Not the hush of snow or the held breath before a storm—this was a silence with edges to it, as if the air itself had been trimmed. The pines along the switchback below still stood where pines should stand, but their shadows fell at angles that disagreed with the sun.

He reined in. Beside him, Sera's mare stamped once and refused to go further, ears flat, and Rafael found he could not blame her.

"What is it?" Sera asked. She had learned, in three days of hard travel, not to waste words on *are you sure* or *what's wrong*. She asked only what she needed answered.

"The light," he said. "It's wrong."

She looked up at the sky, then down at the trail, and he watched her do the same arithmetic he had just done—the sun sitting low over his left shoulder when it should have been climbing behind them, the shadows leaning east when they had been leaning west an hour ago. Her face did something complicated and then settled into grimness.

"Maps," she said, and pulled hers from her saddlebag.

The map of Thornfell was one of the finest pieces of cartography in the Nine Holds—Rafael had smuggled half a dozen of them himself, rolled in oilcloth between bolts of silk, and he knew every line of it the way a priest knows scripture. He leaned across as she unrolled it against her pommel.

The mountains were gone.

Not faded. Not smudged. *Gone*—the parchment where the Ashenmere range should have been was smooth and blank as an egg, the ink simply absent, and where the pass road had been drawn in careful dotted strokes there was now nothing but clean paper running from one edge of the sheet to the other. Even the border of the map itself had softened, the neat rule-line wavering like a shoreline at low tide.

Sera turned it over. Checked the back. Ran her thumb across it as if the ink might be hiding.

"It ate the map," she said quietly.

"It ate the *mountains*." Rafael looked up again at the peaks around them—still there, still real, snow catching the strange slanted light—but the word *still* sat uneasily in his mouth. "The map isn't a picture of Thornfell, Vane. It's a memory of it. Ten thousand surveyors, a thousand years of people walking these passes and writing down what they saw. That's what the Veil eats. And when it finishes eating—" he nodded at the blank parchment— "there won't be anything left for the mountains to be made of."

"You're saying it can erase a place."

"I'm saying I've watched it erase a woman's son while she was holding him. A valley is smaller than that."

* * *

They rode faster after that, and the world got worse.

The first village they came to wasn't there. Rafael knew this because he had slept in it twice—Harrowmere, a cluster of slate-roofed houses around a millpond, famous for nothing except that its innkeeper watered the ale and its stables were warm—and yet the road ran straight through empty meadow grass, unbroken, as if no cart wheel had ever cut a rut there. There was no ruin. No foundation stones. No well. Just grass, tall and indifferent, bending in a wind that smelled of nothing at all.

Sera dismounted and walked the ground where the millpond should have been, boots sinking slightly into soft earth that might have been pond mud or might have been ordinary soil, and there was no way left in the world to know which.

"Harrowmere," she said, testing the name. "I stole a memory out of Harrowmere once. A cooper who'd seen something he shouldn't have. I remember the millpond. Ducks on it." She frowned, pressing her palm flat against the grass. "I remember ducks, Rafael. If the village is gone, why do I remember the ducks?"

"Because you're a thief," Rafael said. "You keep things. That's the whole trade." He said it without thinking, and then heard what he'd said, and saw her hear it too—that he had just described her hoard of *his* memories, the ones he'd confronted her about, the ones she claimed she'd been keeping safe. Something passed between them, brief and uncomfortable, and neither of them picked it up. There wasn't time. There was never any time anymore.

"Then we navigate by what we keep," Sera said.

"By instinct, you mean."

"By whatever hasn't been eaten yet."

So Rafael took the lead, because navigating by instinct was the one craft he had practiced harder than any other. Seven years of moving unseen through blind passes teaches you things maps never could: how a trail smells different an hour before rain; how goats will always tell you where the safe footing is if you watch their ears; how the land remembers water even when everything else forgets, because water goes where gravity tells it and gravity has never once lied. He stopped consulting roads entirely. Roads were arguments written by men, and men's arguments were the first thing the Veil devoured. Instead he read the mountains themselves—the grain of the stone, the lean of the trees away from the prevailing gales, the way snowmelt stained the rock in long dark tears that marked every channel water had ever loved.

Twice that day the road tried to kill them. Once it bent gently, persuasively, toward a col that Rafael's gut told him ended in a two-hundred-foot drop—the trail looked perfectly sound, graded and graveled, and it was only when he noticed there were no hoofprints, no droppings, no bones, no litter of any kind along its whole length, that he understood no living thing had ever walked it, because it had never existed until the Veil needed a road that led nowhere. The second time, a bridge stood over a gorge where his memory insisted there had only been a ford, and Sera started onto it before he caught her arm.

"How do you know?" she demanded.

"I don't. That's the point. I don't *know* anything anymore, so all I've got left is the shape of my doubt." He pointed at the bridge's far end, which terminated in a sheer cliff face with no path continuing from it. "That road doesn't go anywhere. It doesn't want to go anywhere. It wants us in the middle of that gorge when whatever built it decides to stop pretending."

She looked at the bridge a moment longer. Then she took a stone from her pocket and threw it, underhand, onto the planking.

The stone went through the bridge as though the bridge were smoke, dropped forty feet, and shattered on rocks below.

Neither of them said anything. They forded the gorge in knee-deep freezing water instead, and Rafael pretended not to notice that Sera's hands shook while she wrung out her stockings, and she pretended not to notice that his did too.

* * *

The pass closed at dusk.

They had made for the Greywether—the high saddle that was the only way through the spine of Thornfell into the lowlands, the only route to the capital that didn't run through country the Veil had already begun digesting. Rafael had crossed it eleven times in seven years. He knew the way up it the way he knew the back of his own hand, which was, he was beginning to understand, a phrase that no longer meant very much.

But the Greywether was simply not there.

Where the saddle should have opened—a broad, wind-scoured notch between two shoulders of granite, visible from miles off—there was instead a wall. Unbroken rock, sheer and seamless, rising four hundred feet from talus to sky, with no seam, no crack, no fault line, no suggestion that a pass had ever threaded it. It wasn't rubble. It wasn't a landslide. It was as if some enormous mason had laid stone over the gap in the mountains the way you'd lay a plank over a hole in a floor, and done it competently, and meant it to stay.

Sera dismounted and put her hand against it. "This is new stone," she said. "Feel it. No lichen. No weathering. It's like—it's like the mountain grew a scab."

"The Veil erased the pass," Rafael said. His voice came out flat and strange. "And the world filled in the hole, because the world can't abide a hole where its own memory says there shouldn't be one. The Veil takes the *idea* of the Greywether, and the rock closes over it like water over a drowned man."

"Is there another way?"

He stood looking at the wall for a long time. Behind them, somewhere down the valley, the light was doing the wrong thing again—thinning, going grey at the edges, the dusk arriving not west-to-east as dusk should but all at once, everywhere, like a lid coming down. Somewhere in that greying distance, he knew, inquisitors were riding. Corvel's hounds. They had marked him in the border village and they had not lost the scent, and every hour the world got smaller and smoother and easier to search.

"There's a way," he said finally. "Through the Undercroft. The old miners' galleries under the Greywether. But the entrances—" He stopped. Rubbed his face. "The entrances were mapped. Everything was mapped. And the maps are blank, and the Veil has probably eaten the galleries themselves, and even if it hasn't, I haven't been down there in nine years and my memory of them is—" he laughed, short and bitter— "my memory of everything is exactly as trustworthy as yours."

"So we're trapped."

"We're trapped unless the pass opens."

"And how does a pass open, Rafael? Do you propose to storm-blast four hundred feet of granite? Because the last time you called lightning, it killed—" She caught herself. Too late. He saw her catch it, saw the wince move through her like a shudder through a rope.

"No," he said, very evenly. "I don't propose that."

Silence. Wind moved through the grass of the meadow where Harrowmere wasn't, making a sound like the sea, like something vast breathing in its sleep.

Then Sera said, "There's another way to open it."

He turned. She had gone pale—not frightened-pale, but the particular bloodless look of someone about to spend something they cannot afford, and Rafael had spent enough of his own life to recognize the currency.

"The Veil eats memories," she said. "Fine. But it also *trades* in them. Everything it takes, it keeps—that's what the Archive is, that's what the drowned library under the capital is, a vault of everything it's swallowed. And the thing about a vault—" her jaw tightened— "is that things can be taken back out. By someone who knows how to steal."

"You're talking about robbing the Veil."

"I'm talking about giving something back. The pass exists in the Archive right now—every memory of everyone who ever crossed the Greywether, hanging in that black water. If enough of those memories come back—if the world *remembers* the pass hard enough—the rock can't stay closed. Memory is load-bearing, Rafael. It's the only thing holding any of this up." She was unbuckling a case at her saddle now, a slim black-leather case he had seen her sleep with curled against her chest like a child, and his stomach dropped before she even opened it.

"That's your stock," he said. "Your harvest. Your whole livelihood."

"My whole livelihood is stolen goods," she said, "and most of it's worthless. A cooper's shame. A magistrate's first kiss. Forty years of somebody's Tuesdays." She flipped the case open, and inside, nested in velvet, lay small glass vials, each one holding a curl of silver-grey luminescence that moved like a fish in dark water. Memories. Dozens of them. The collected weight of strangers' lives. "None of it matters. None of it holds anything up." Her finger stopped on one vial near the center, set apart from the others in its own fitted slot, and Rafael saw her hesitate over it the way you hesitate over a door you know leads somewhere you can't come back from.

"That one's not worthless," he said quietly.

"No."

"It's yours."

She didn't answer, which was an answer. She lifted the vial out, and in the failing light the memory inside it glowed faintly against her palm, and for a moment her face was young and unguarded and utterly miserable.

"A harbor town," she said, as if explaining to a customs officer. "South coast. Before I was anyone. My mother worked the fish market and she used to sing this song about the tide coming in, and I couldn't tell you the tune anymore, but I remember—I remember standing on the dock with her sleeve in my fist, and the smell of brine and tar, and being *known*. Being somebody's daughter. That's what's in here. That's the oldest thing I own." She looked up at him, and her eyes were dry and furious with themselves. "The Veil took the town two years ago. Erased it, the way it erased Harrowmere. Nobody remembers it but me, because I'm a thief and thieves keep things. Which means this—" she raised the vial— "is the last copy of a place that ever existed. And if I pour it into the Greywether, along with the rest, the mountain will remember being open, and the pass will reopen, and we'll get through tonight instead of dying in this meadow waiting for Corvel."

"And you lose it forever."

"I lose it forever," she agreed. "And I'll be nobody's daughter again. I'll be exactly what I was before I stole it back—which is nobody at all." She smiled, and it was the worst smile Rafael had ever seen, bright and brittle as river ice. "So don't you dare thank me. Thanking me makes it noble. It's not noble. It's arithmetic. You're worth more to the unbinding than one dock and one dead woman's singing, and I have decided to be ruthless about the arithmetic, and if you make this sentimental I will put the vial back and we'll both die stupid."

Rafael looked at her for a long moment.

Seven days ago he would have said he trusted no one alive. Six days ago a memory-thief had put her fingers against his temple and found her own childhood hiding inside his head, and he had trusted her less than no one. And now here she stood in a meadow where a village used to be, offering up the last proof of her own origin so that a man she had every reason to abandon could keep stumbling toward a throne room and a monster, and Rafael understood—with the same instinct that read mountains, the sense that had never once lied to him—that she was not performing. That underneath the thief's arithmetic there was something she would deny to her grave, and that it was the same thing he had buried seven years ago under ash and exile, and that its name was not arithmetic at all.

"Vane," he said. "After the capital. When this is done. I'll find your harbor town."

"It doesn't exist."

"Neither did I, for seven years. Things come back." He held her gaze. "Things come back when somebody refuses to let them go. That's the whole lesson of this entire cursed kingdom."

Something moved behind her eyes—quick, startled, quickly mastered. She looked down at the vial.

"Stand back from the rock," she said, voice rough. "And whatever happens, don't let go of the horses."

* * *

She walked to the base of the false stone and knelt, and uncorked the vial, and poured.

The memory came out slow, like honey, like light given weight—a ribbon of silver that unwound across the grass and crawled up the face of the wall, and where it touched, the stone *shivered*. Then she opened the case and tipped the whole of it out, dozens of vials, strangers' Tuesdays and first kisses and shames, a decade of harvested lives spilling across the roots of the mountain, and Rafael felt the air pressure change the way it changes before a summer storm, felt his suppressed power stir in its seven-year cage and press against the bars, wanting to help, wanting to sing.

The wall remembered.

It happened the way ice remembers being water. The seamless granite went translucent, then fluid, then briefly, horribly, showed them what was inside it—the ghost of the Greywether pass, the true saddle, the wind-scoured notch, hanging in the solid rock like a fish in dark water—and then the false stone sighed and sloughed away in sheets of grey dust, and wind came through the reopened pass carrying the cold clean smell of the high snowfields, a smell from a part of the world that still existed.

Sera stayed kneeling a long time among the empty vials.

When she finally stood and turned, her face was composed and her eyes were red-rimmed and she said, in a perfectly level voice, "Not one word."

"Not one word," Rafael agreed.

He handed her her reins. For a moment their hands overlapped on the leather, and neither of them pulled away, and the wind through the pass smelled like snowmelt and like a harbor town that only one person in the world would ever remember again.

Then, from the valley behind them—far off, but not nearly far enough—came a sound that froze the blood in Rafael's veins despite everything: a horn. Long, low, patient. The hunting note of the Veil-bound, blown once, and then, after a pause, blown again, closer.

"They found Harrowmere's ghost," Sera said. "Or what's left of it. Ride?"

"Ride."

They drove their horses up through the reborn pass as the last light failed, and Rafael did not look back at the meadow, or at the dust of a hundred stolen memories settling over the grass. But as the walls of the Greywether rose on either side of them, real and ancient and remembered, he became aware of a faint warmth against his forearm—the place where the prophecy's words had appeared in dead ink—and when he pushed back his sleeve, the final line had changed.

New letters were surfacing through his skin, fresh as wounds, in handwriting he now knew better than his own:

*The pass is open. So is the way down. What waits beneath the capital has been reading over your shoulder since Thornfell — and it knows you are coming.*

Below it, in smaller script, almost gentle:

*Trust her anyway.*
