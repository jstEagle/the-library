## 8. An Audience With Nobody

The capital had a smell Rafael had forgotten he knew—river silt and tallow smoke and the green rot of the canal district in late summer—and the forgetting of it was its own small wound. He stood at the mouth of the Saltgate tunnel with Sera's hood drawn over his hair and let the smell do what smells do: reach past every wall a man builds and touch the rooms underneath.

*You were here before,* the city said. *You have walked these stones.*

He did not answer it. He had spent seven years learning not to answer.

"Keep your eyes down past the fountain," Sera murmured beside him, her voice pitched under the noise of cart traffic. "The Veil doesn't watch faces. It watches attention. A man who looks too long at anything is a man who remembers something, and remembering is contraband now."

"And you? You look at everything."

"I'm licensed," she said, with a thin smile that didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "I steal for them when it suits me. That's our way in, if you can stomach it."

He could not stomach it, which was why they were doing it anyway.

They came up out of the tunnel into the merchant quarter, where the morning market was in full cry, and Rafael made himself walk through it slowly, cataloguing the wrongness the way he'd once catalogued blind passes in the Thornfell hills. The wrongness was not loud. That was the horror of it. The market was *busy*, cheerful even—a woman haggling over plums, a boy chasing a dog between stalls, a fiddler working a tune that snagged at some door in Rafael's chest and would not open. But every stall sold the same three things: bread, cloth, oil. He counted four spice merchants whose tables held nothing but salt. A bookbinder's shop displayed empty covers, spines without pages, arranged like trophies. When he looked too long at the bookbinder, the man smiled at him with the wide, untroubled smile of someone who has never once wondered about anything.

"There used to be a fountain here," Rafael said quietly. "Didn't there?"

Sera glanced at the dry stone basin at the square's center, choked with weeds, its carved mermaid worn faceless. "There's a fountain there now. Whether there ever was one—" She shrugged. "Ask the mermaid."

They crossed the square. Behind them, faintly, Rafael heard the fiddler stop mid-phrase, as though the tune had been taken from him, and start again on a different song entirely—one Rafael was almost certain he'd just heard the fiddler play an hour ago, except it couldn't have been an hour, because they'd only just arrived.

*The kingdom is being eaten from the edges inward,* Sera had told him on the cliff road, weeks ago now, in another life. He was beginning to understand that the edges were not places. The edges were everywhere. The Veil did not devour a country the way fire devours a forest, from one border to another. It devoured the way rot devours fruit—softly, from within, leaving the skin whole until the day the whole shape collapses in your hand.

*

The palace rose above the terraced gardens like a jaw of white teeth, and Rafael hated how well he remembered the way to it. His feet knew the streets. Seven years of exile, and his body still turned left at the cooper's yard because the left turn avoided the hill, and the hill was cobbled badly, and once—some lost afternoon, some lost company—he had complained about those cobblestones to someone who had laughed.

He stopped walking.

"You knew this city," Sera said. Not a question.

"I don't know what I knew." He started walking again, faster. "That's rather the point of me."

The plan was hers, and it was elegant in the way of all her work: simple, ruthless, built on theft. Every quarter, the crown held an open court—a relic of old Aldric's populism, she said, kept alive by the thing wearing him because an audience of kneeling subjects was the richest feeding ground in the kingdom. Petitioners came in their hundreds with grievances, and the Veil drank the room's attention like wine, and no one who left the throne room could afterward say precisely what they had seen there. They remembered being satisfied. They remembered bowing. They remembered nothing else, and they went home grateful.

Petitioners required papers. Papers required a name.

So Sera stole them a name. In the shadow of the customs house she lifted a merchant's writ of petition from a fat wool-trader's satchel—the man would find a blank paper in its place and spend the rest of the day vaguely certain he'd already been to court, already been heard, already been answered—and she pressed the writ into Rafael's hands along with a wax seal warm from her palm.

"Your name today is Odo Ferran, wool factor, Thornfell district." She watched his face as she said it, watching for what it cost him. "A dead man's name. I checked. No one will grieve the borrowing."

"A dead man's name," Rafael repeated. "You do know how to flatter a fellow."

"It's safer than your real one." Her voice went flat and careful. "Which we are not going to speak inside those walls. Not whispered, not thought loudly. Do you understand me? The Veil hears names the way spiders hear vibration. Yours especially. There is a thread running through a century of paper with your name knotted into it, and if you so much as hum it in that building, everything we've done ends in that throne room."

"I understand."

"Do you?" She caught his sleeve. For a moment, under the customs-house eaves, her gray eyes were very close and very serious, and Rafael was struck again—as he had been struck on the cliff road, and in the collapsing pass, and every night since around their small fire—by the strangeness of being known by her. She looked at him like a person reading a letter she'd written herself long ago and half forgotten. "Rafael. I have seen what happens to people the Veil takes an interest in. Interest is not the right word. *Appetite.* You are not walking into its hall as a petitioner. You are walking in as a meal that learned to use a fork. So when I tell you to keep your head down and your mouth shut and your storm leashed—"

"My storm is bound," he said. "It's been bound for seven years."

"Yes," Sera said softly. "And I've felt it straining against the binding every mile since Thornfell, like a horse tied too short. So has whatever lives in that palace. Bound power isn't silent power. It's a bell that's stopped ringing but still hums when you touch it." She released his sleeve. "Don't let anyone touch the bell."

*

They joined the queue of petitioners at the western stair an hour before midday, and Rafael discovered that the worst part of infiltration was how little of it felt like infiltration. The line shuffled forward through colonnades of white marble, past guards in Aldric's blue and silver, and every guard's face wore the same smooth contentment, the same settled calm, as though fear itself had been recalled from circulation like clipped coin. A young woman ahead of them rehearsed her petition under her breath—something about a boundary wall, a neighbor, a deed—and then, halfway through the rehearsal, stopped, frowned, and began again from the beginning with different words, as though the first version had been taken from her while she spoke it.

The throne room doors opened, and the sound that came out was the sound of a congregation.

Inside, the hall was vast and dim and full of kneeling bodies, hundreds of them arrayed down the central aisle in rows, foreheads bowed to the mosaic floor, and the air had a texture Rafael felt against his skin like standing near deep water. Cold rose off the floor. The great windows had been shuttered—shuttered, in daylight, in a king's own hall—and the only light came from a hundred candles burning without flicker, each flame perfectly still, as still as painted light, as still as something remembered rather than lit.

And at the far end, on the raised dais beneath the empty canopy of state, sat King Aldric.

Or the shape of him.

Rafael had never seen the king in person. He knew the face from coins and from the mural in the Thornfell chapel of the coronation twenty years gone—young then, broad-shouldered, famous for laughing. The figure on the throne had the face still, but time had done something obscene to it, or been prevented from doing anything at all. It was Aldric as a coin is Aldric: correct, idealized, frozen at the moment of greatest authority. The skin had the matte perfection of candle-wax. The hands resting on the arms of the throne were folded in exactly the pose of the coronation mural. Even the robes hung in sculpted folds that had not shifted, Rafael realized as he watched, in all the minutes he had been looking.

The courtiers stood flanking the dais in two shining ranks, and they were smiling. All of them. Chamberlain and heralds and ladies of the privy chamber, smiling with serene, identical pleasure, and when the herald called the next petitioner forward, the smiles widened by a precise fraction, all together, like a tide.

"Odo Ferran," the herald intoned, "wool factor, of Thornfell."

Sera's elbow found Rafael's ribs, gentle as a knife going in. *Go.*

He walked the aisle alone. Sera remained among the crowd, hooded, one more bowed head, and Rafael felt her absence at his shoulder like a missing tooth. The cold came up through the mosaic as he neared the dais, cold with a pulse in it, slow as a sleeping heart, and the nearer he came the more certain he grew that the stillness of the candle flames was not decoration. Nothing in this room moved because movement was memory's oldest language, and the thing on the throne had forbidden the language.

He knelt. The stone bit his knees. He bowed his head and stared at the mosaic beneath him—interlocking circles of blue and gold, a pattern of linked rings—and understood, with a lurch, that the rings had once held pictures. Faces, maybe. Kings and founders. They had been smoothed to abstract geometry, worn down not by knees but by *eating*.

"Speak, Odo Ferran, wool factor." The voice from the throne was Aldric's voice, or nearly—it had the warmth, the famous warmth—but underneath the warmth ran a second sound, a sound like wind moving through an empty house, like pages turning in a room where no one stands. "The crown hears all its children. What does the child ask?"

Rafael had prepared a petition. Boundary dispute, grazing rights, the dull meat of ordinary grievance. He opened his mouth to deliver it—

—and the thing on the throne breathed in.

It was a small sound. In the vast hush of the hall it should have been nothing. But Rafael had spent seven years listening to weather, to the held breath before lightning, to the particular silence a sky makes when it is deciding, and he knew a gathering when he heard one. The figure on the throne drew breath the way a diver draws breath before surfacing from somewhere very deep and very dark.

Rafael raised his eyes.

He had been told not to. He raised them anyway, because he was Rafael Amberdale, and self-preservation had always been the least of his talents.

The king's eyes were looking directly at him.

Not the wax mask. Not the coin-face with its serene, vacant benevolence. The *eyes*—and they were wrong, they were terribly, humanly wrong, wet and bloodshot and wide with a horror that had been living behind them for ten years, and in the instant before Rafael could look away, the eyes *moved*. They darted toward the smiling ranks of courtiers, back to Rafael, down to the king's own folded hands, and the fingers twitched—two fingers of the right hand, tapping once, twice against the armrest in a rhythm that was not random, that was *deliberate*, that was a man signaling across a distance no one had ever signaled across before.

Then the mask closed over the face like water closing over a drowning man.

The serenity returned. The smile returned, mild and royal. "You may rise, wool factor," said the voice with the wind in it. "Your petition is granted. The crown is pleased. The crown remembers."

*The crown remembers.* And behind the words, faint as a knock through a wall, Rafael heard—or imagined he heard, and would spend the rest of his life deciding whether he had imagined it—a second voice, buried, cracked, screaming without sound:

*Help me.*

*

He did not remember rising. He did not remember turning. He retained, afterward, only fragments: the cold of the mosaic through his trousers; the candle flames motionless as he passed them; a courtier's smile passing over him like a searchlight and finding nothing worth keeping; Sera's hand appearing at his elbow in the colonnade outside, steering him, her grip harder than affection.

They walked four blocks before either of them spoke. Then Rafael stopped in the lee of a shuttered chandler's shop and put his hand flat against the wall, because the ground had developed opinions about spinning.

"You saw it," he said.

"I saw a king on a throne."

"Sera." He turned and gripped her shoulders, and she let him, which frightened him more than the throne room had. "His eyes. For one second. One second and then it sealed over like ice on a pond. He tapped his fingers. Twice. Deliberately. He *looked at me*, Sera, and it wasn't the Veil looking. The Veil looks through things. This was a man looking out from inside his own skull, begging." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it and let it crack. "He's awake. He's been awake the whole time. Ten years, locked in his own body, watching it smile and kneel and eat his kingdom, and everyone in that hall walked out *satisfied*."

Sera was quiet for a long moment. Around them the street flowed on—a baker's boy with a basket, two guards strolling, a woman shaking out a rug—all of it normal, all of it hollow, a play performed by actors who no longer knew there was a script.

"The Veil keeps its vessels conscious," she said at last, and her voice had gone low and rough. "It's not cruelty. Or—it is, but it's also appetite. Fear tastes better than peace. Despair seasons the memory. A vessel that has given up is bland. A vessel that *hopes*—" She shook her head. "It keeps them hoping. It keeps them reaching. Every year, King Aldric finds one second, one petitioner, one pair of eyes, and reaches out. And every year the Veil lets him, because the reaching is the sweetest part of the meal."

"Ten years." Rafael's hands had closed into fists without his permission. Storm-light itched along his knuckles, under the skin, under the binding, a bell humming. "Ten years of that. And the prophecy—the book, the date, my name—" He stopped. Started again. "The Veil chose him because a king's memory is a kingdom's memory. Everything the realm ever saw, it saw through him. Eat the king and you eat the country's whole past in one sitting." He was thinking aloud now, fast, the way he used to think aloud in another life, in lecture halls and war councils, in front of people who listened. "But it means the rite, the unbinding—it means he can be reached. If he's conscious, if he can signal, then he can be *anchored*. A possession needs a mind to possess. As long as Aldric holds on to himself in there, the Veil can't fully digest him. It's why it hasn't finished. It's been keeping him alive the way you keep a cow alive."

"Elegant," Sera said dryly. "And horrifying. Well done."

"There's a drowned archive under this city." He watched her face carefully as he said it. "You told me. Where it stores what it eats. Memories suspended in the water like fish." He swallowed. "If Aldric's true self—his memories, the ones that make him *him*—if any part of that is stored down there instead of destroyed, then he isn't just a prisoner. He's a prisoner with ransom hidden in the vault. We don't need to kill the thing wearing the crown, Sera. We need to make the crown fit the man again. Give him back enough of himself to tear free from the inside."

For a moment she said nothing, and in the silence Rafael heard the city breathing around them, millions of small forgettings, a kingdom dissolving politely in its sleep.

"That archive is a graveyard," Sera said finally. "I've skimmed its edge once, years ago, for a commission I still don't talk about. The water remembers you while you're in it. It offers you back every good thing you've ever lost, and the price of taking one is standing still, and the price of standing still is everything." Her eyes came up to his, gray and level. "People don't drown in it, Rafael. They *stay*. Happily. Forever."

"Then we'll wade carefully."

"You're mad."

"Almost certainly." He looked back the way they had come, up the hill toward the white jaw of the palace, where a man sat smiling inside his own stolen face, tapping two fingers against an armrest, counting seconds, waiting ten years for someone to count back. The itch of the storm moved under Rafael's skin like a tide answering a moon. Seven years he had starved it. Seven years he had paid any price for silence.

But silence, he understood now, had never been safety. Silence was just the feeding made quiet.

"He begged," Rafael said. "One second, and he spent it begging. Not for death. I know the difference. I've wanted death, and that wasn't it." He pulled Sera's hood straight over her hair, an odd, almost tender gesture that surprised them both. "He asked for help. So we're going to give it to him. Find me the way down, thief. Tonight, if it can be done tonight."

Sera studied him for a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes—fear, yes, but underneath the fear, reluctantly, unmistakably, hope.

"It can be done tonight," she said. "God help us both. It can be done tonight."
