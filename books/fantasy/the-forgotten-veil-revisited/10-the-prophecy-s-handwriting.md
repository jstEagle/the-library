## 10. The Prophecy's Handwriting

The safehouse Sera had chosen was a collapsed watchtower on the shoulder of the Grayfell ridge, its upper rooms open to the sky like a broken tooth. Rain came through the wound in the ceiling in a fine, patient drizzle, pooling on the flagstones, and Rafael sat with his back against a wall that still remembered being part of something whole, and did not sleep.

He had not slept since the cliff road. Since her hand had touched his temple and she had flinched as though his mind were a stove left burning. She was below now, in what remained of the ground floor, ostensibly keeping watch, actually—Rafael suspected—keeping distance from him. He could not blame her. He was keeping distance from himself.

The book lay on his knees.

It was heavier than it should have been, the way guilt is heavier than the acts that make it. Dead ink, the dying fence had called it, and Rafael had assumed he meant the color—a brown so old it had gone the shade of dried blood, of things written by hands that no longer existed to be ashamed or proud of what they'd set down. But dead ink meant more than that. Dead ink meant ink that refused to fade, refused to run when the rain found it, refused to behave like anything written by living men.

Tonight's date was circled in it. Had been circled in it for a hundred years.

And beside the circle, in that same impossible brown: *Amberdale.*

His name. The one he had buried so carefully, paid so much to bury, starved and smuggled and gone nameless seven years to bury—and there it sat in a book older than his grandfather, waiting for him like a trap waits for the foot.

Rafael turned pages by candlelight. The tower dripped. Somewhere below, Sera shifted her weight, and the sound of it was the only proof the world still contained other people.

*Read it properly,* he told himself. *If you're going to be damned by a book, at least read the damnation.*

The early entries were rambling, half-prophecy and half-confession, the handwriting of someone writing fast in a cold room. They spoke of the Veil in terms Rafael now recognized—the thing born of the failed rite, the century of feeding, the slow fusion with the crown—but they spoke of it with a familiarity that unsettled him. Not the familiarity of a scholar. The familiarity of a victim.

*It does not take memories all at once,* one entry read. *That is the mercy everyone believes in, and it is false. It takes them the way water takes a shoreline. You will argue with yourself about whether you ever owned a red coat. You will lose.*

Rafael looked down at his own hands in the candlelight. Hands that had not held a pen in seven years, because pens left evidence and evidence left trails. But they remembered the shape of holding one. His fingers curled around nothing, an absent quill, and the muscle-memory of it made his stomach turn.

Because the handwriting in the book was slanting slightly leftward on the downstrokes. Because the ascenders were cut short, impatient, the writer always hurrying toward the end of the word before the beginning of it had finished meaning anything. Because Rafael had been taught his letters by a mother who pressed too hard and broke three nibs a week, and he had inherited her hand entire, every impatient stroke of it—

No.

He set the book down on the flagstone. Stood. Walked to the broken window and let the rain hit his face until it hurt with cold.

Coincidence. Scripts ran in families; he'd seen it among the caravan folk, fathers and sons whose signatures might have been pressed from the same seal. A hundred years was long enough for a hand to travel down a bloodline and come out looking like his. That was all. That was the sane explanation, and he clung to it the way a drowning man clings to driftwood that is visibly, obviously on fire.

Then he went back inside, took a piece of charcoal from his pack, and did the thing he had been refusing to do since Thornfell.

He wrote his own name.

Not *the name he went by*, the gray nothing the caravans knew—not the alias worn smooth as river stone. His true name, the one the book had spoken, the one he hadn't written since the night the lightning came down. His hand shook so badly the letters came out crooked, but they came out, and he set them beneath the open page of the prophecy, side by side in the candlelight, and made himself look.

The *A*s matched. Not similar—matched. The same slight forward lean, the same crossbar placed a hair too high, the same tiny hook where the pen lifted. The *d* with its shortened ascender. The *l* that ended in a downward flick, as though the writer were already turning away from the word before finishing it. Stroke for stroke, tremor for tremor, the handwriting of a man a century dead and the handwriting of a man sitting in a ruined tower with rain in his hair were the same hand.

Rafael sat down very slowly, because his legs had informed him they were no longer interested in standing.

"It's me," he said aloud, to the empty room, and heard how absurd it sounded. "I wrote this."

Except he hadn't. He would swear it on any grave you cared to name—he had never seen this book before Thornfell, never put ink to these pages, never written a word of prophecy in his life. And yet the hand was his, unquestionably, the way your own face in a mirror is unquestionable even when you don't recognize the expression.

Which left only one explanation, and it was worse than forgery, worse than madness, worse than everything.

Someone had pulled him out of time. Some version of him—older, or displaced, or caught like a fly in the Veil's first feeding a hundred years ago—had lived through all of this already, or was living through it still, somewhere outside the ordinary river of days. And that version had spent a century writing warnings backward across the years, in his own unmistakable hand, addressed to a man who would not be born for generations.

*I am the letter,* Rafael thought, and the thought arrived with the calm of shock, the strange clarity that comes after the worst has already happened. *I am the message I've been carrying.*

* * *

He read the rest of the book that night, properly this time, and the entries changed as they went—or rather, they deepened, the way a voice changes when the speaker stops performing and starts confessing.

The middle entries were practical. Warnings about the inquisitors, about Corvel by name, about the drowned archive and the shoals of eaten memory. Instructions that read like they were written by someone who knew exactly which mistakes Rafael would make, because he had made them:

*You will want to burn the book after the first entry. Burn it anyway. It comes back. Everything true comes back; that is the only advantage we have over it.*

Rafael touched his forearm, where the burned page had reappeared in the morning, written on his skin in dead ink. Yes. Everything true came back.

Another entry, later, the handwriting deteriorating, the letters leaning as though wind were pushing against them:

*It will show you him. Understand this now, while you are still able to understand anything: it will build him out of stolen memory and set him in front of you, perfect, warm, saying your name in his voice, and it will be the cruelest thing that has ever been done to anyone, and half of you will kneel. Plan for the kneeling. Forgive yourself in advance. Then stand up.*

Rafael closed his eyes. He did not ask who *him* was. He knew who *him* was. There was only ever going to be one *him*, and the fact that a version of himself a century distant knew it too—that the grief was so structural to Rafael's soul that it survived time itself—was somehow the most devastating sentence in the whole book.

But it was the final entry that stopped his heart.

It was shorter than the others. The handwriting had steadied again, the way a man's hand steadies when he has passed beyond fear into decision, and the ink was darker here, laid on thick, each letter pressed deep enough to dent the page from the back. As if the writer had wanted to be certain. As if the writer had known this was the line that mattered.

There was no date. Just four words, and then a fifth line beneath them, and Rafael read them perhaps forty times, and each time they landed like the first.

*Do not trust the one who returns your name.*

Beneath it, smaller, almost an afterthought, almost gentle:

*She is not lying. That is what makes it unforgivable.*

Rafael sat with the book open on his knees and listened to the rain fall through the broken roof, and felt the trap close around him with a click so quiet he might have imagined it.

*She.* There was only one *she*. There had only been one *she* since the cliff road, since her fingers had brushed his temple and found her own childhood folded up inside his memory like a letter she'd mailed to the wrong address. Sera Vane, who claimed to remember him from a life he couldn't recall. Sera Vane, who flinched from his mind and lied badly about missing. Sera Vane, who was downstairs right now, keeping watch, keeping distance, keeping—what?

Keeping something of his. He had known it since the archive talk, in the way you know a wound before you look at it. She had taken pieces of him on that road; he'd felt the small thefts like coins lifted from a pocket. And the future-him—the trapped him, the him who had paid a century of exile for the privilege of warning—was telling him, in his own handwriting, in ink that could not lie:

*Do not trust the one who returns your name.*

Rafael looked at the words until they blurred.

Here was the cruelty of it, the exquisite engineering of it: the warning did not tell him what she would do. It did not say *she will betray you to the Veil* or *she serves Corvel* or *she will sell your memories in the night markets of some city that no longer exists.* It said only: do not trust the one who returns your name. Which meant the betrayal, whatever it was, would wear the face of a gift. Would arrive at the moment of greatest need, shaped exactly like salvation. The knife would come disguised as the rope.

And the second line—*she is not lying*—was somehow worse than the first. Because if she were simply a liar, he could hate her cleanly, walk away, be done. But a person who tells the truth and betrays you anyway—that is a person who means it. Who loves you, maybe, or needs you, or is following orders written in her own dead ink by someone she cannot refuse. The future-Rafael had known her too. Had trusted her too, once, enough to write *she is not lying* with a steady hand. And had still written the warning above it.

What had she done to him? What would she do?

The candle guttered. Downstairs, Sera's footsteps crossed the floor—slow, restless, the pacing of someone who also wasn't sleeping—and stopped directly beneath him, as if she could feel his attention through the floorboards. For a long moment neither of them moved. Then her footsteps resumed, and Rafael realized his hand had drifted to the charcoal, and that without deciding to, he had begun to copy the final entry onto a scrap of paper, over and over, the way a man worries a loose tooth.

*Do not trust the one who returns your name.*

The terrible arithmetic of it settled over him like the rain through the roof: he needed her. Every road from here led down, into the capital, into the drowned dark, and she was the only guide he had who knew the way and could survive it. He could not leave her behind and he could not, now, fully turn his back to her. The prophecy had not freed him from her. It had only made sure that whatever happened next, he would see it coming—and be unable to stop it.

Betrayal anticipated before it can occur. That was the future-self's real gift, Rafael understood suddenly. Not the warnings about Corvel, not the maps of the archive. This. The knowledge that the blow was coming, so that when it fell, some small core of him would remain un-surprised, and therefore unbroken. His future self could not save him. But he could make sure that when the moment came, Rafael would recognize it—and choose, knowingly, with open eyes, instead of being chosen for.

*Forgive yourself in advance,* the book had said about the kneeling. Perhaps that was the whole philosophy of the thing. Walk into the trap knowing it is a trap, because the alternative is not walking at all, and the kingdom's past is drowning either way.

Rafael fed the last of the candle to the flame and watched it drown.

Below, the footsteps stopped again. And this time, through the gap in the rotted floorboards, her voice came up to him—quiet, careful, pitched for the dark:

"You've been reading it all night." A pause. "There's an entry near the end. Four words. I know you found it, because you haven't come down."

His blood went cold and stayed there.

"How do you know what's in it?" he called back, and hated how much the question cost him—how it told her, immediately, everything.

Silence. Long enough to count raindrops in.

"Because," Sera Vane said at last, "I'm the one who taught him to write it."

* * *

Rafael came down the broken stair with the book held against his chest like a shield, and found her standing in the doorway of the ground room, silhouetted against the gray pre-dawn, arms crossed, watching him with an expression he had never seen on her before and could not name. Not guilt. Not quite fear. Something older than both.

"Say that again," he said.

"I taught him." Her voice was steady, but her hands weren't; he saw her thumb worrying the scar on her palm, the thief's habit of a woman counting something invisible. "The one who wrote the book. Your handwriting isn't a coincidence, Rafael—I know, because I held the pen with him. A hundred years ago. Or—" she exhaled, and the breath shook on the way out, "—or five years from now. Time doesn't sit still in the places the Veil has chewed. He was pulled out during the first feeding, and I found him, and I kept him alive long enough to write it all down. Every word. I carried the book out of that place myself."

"And the last entry." Rafael's voice came out flat, dead level, the voice he used for negotiating passes full of soldiers. "Did you carry that one out too?"

"Yes."

"Then you read it."

"Yes."

"And you brought me to it anyway." He took a step closer, and the candleless dark between them seemed to tighten. "Knowing what it says. Knowing what it says about *you*."

Sera didn't move. Rain-light silvered the edge of her jaw, and when she spoke, each word was placed like a coin on a table, exact and irrevocable.

"I brought you to it because you deserved to read it before you decided. That's the difference between me and the Veil, Rafael—it takes your choices and calls them fate. I give them back to you, even the ones that ruin me." Her thumb stilled on the scar. "So decide. Right now, in this room, with the book in your hands. Trust me or don't. But decide knowing that when the moment comes—the moment he wrote about—you'll have to choose faster than you can think. And I need you to know that whatever you choose tonight..."

Her voice finally, finally wavered.

"...I'll still be walking down into that dark with you. Either way. That's the part he couldn't warn you about."

Outside, far to the south, beyond the ridge, a light kindled in the black where no light should be—one point, then two, then a slow procession of them, winding up the valley road like a rosary being counted.

Torchlight. Many torches. Moving with the unhurried certainty of hunters who already knew where their quarry slept.

Corvel had found the tower.

Rafael looked at the torches, then at the woman the prophecy had warned him against, standing in the doorway between him and the only stair—and understood, with terrible clarity, that the next choice was already smaller than it looked.

"We run together," he said, "or we die apart. Decide faster than you can think."

Behind them, in the dead-ink book, faint and fresh as morning frost, a new line began to write itself.
