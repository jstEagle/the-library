## 11. Inquisitor's Mercy

The village of Hollowmere had four hundred and eleven souls when Corvel arrived, and by the time the sun set he could tell you the exact number that remained: four hundred and eleven. That was the mercy of it. No one died in a Veil culling. Everyone was saved. They simply stopped carrying what they could not afford to carry.

He stood in the square while his acolytes worked the houses door to door, and he watched an old man sit on a bench with a wooden bird in his hands, turning it over and over, his face smooth as a river stone.

"What is it?" Corvel asked him gently.

"I don't know," the old man said, smiling. "It's lovely, though. Isn't it? It's lovely."

"It was your son's," Corvel said. "Carved for you the winter before he took fever. You used to keep it on the sill above your bed."

The old man looked at him with mild, untroubled eyes. "Did I have a son?"

"You did."

"Was he good?"

"He was good."

"Then I was lucky." The old man set the bird down on the bench between them, patted the wood once as if thanking it, and walked away whistling toward whatever small, unburdened evening remained to him.

Corvel watched him go and felt the thing inside him stir with something that might once have been satisfaction. *There*, it said, in a voice like water finding the lowest place. *See how light he walks now.*

*Yes,* Corvel answered, because it was true, and because lying to the Veil was like lying to the tide.

* * *

He had been a man once. He held onto that fact the way a drowning man holds a plank—less because it would save him than because letting go seemed unthinkable. Corvel. High Inquisitor of the Crown's Third Office. He had a name before this. He had a daughter before this.

*You have us now,* the Veil murmured, and its voice came from everywhere at once—from the gray film over his eyes, from the soft erosion of his knuckles where flesh had given way to something smoother, from the forty other bodies scattered across three provinces that shared his thoughts the way rooms share a draft.

*I know,* he thought back. *I remember.*

*No you don't,* it said, not cruelly. *That's rather the point.*

And that was true too. He remembered *that* he had been a man; the fact sat in him like a coin sewn into a coat lining. But the shape of the man—the color of his wife's kitchen, the sound his daughter made when she laughed so hard she hiccupped, whether her hair had been dark or light, whether there had even been a wife—all of that had gone into the Veil's keeping years ago, traded away in installments, each one reasonable, each one a relief.

Grief, the Veil had explained to him in the first week, in the first gentle hour, requires memory the way fire requires air. Take away the memory and the grief starves. He had come to them weeping—he could not recall why, only that the weeping had been oceanic—and they had offered him not comfort but surgery. And it had worked. Oh, how it had worked. The pain had lifted out of him like fog off a morning field, and what remained was purpose, clean and weightless as a blade.

This was what no one understood about the Veil, Corvel thought, watching his acolytes carry another armload of letters and christening gowns and wedding rings out of Hollowmere's houses and into the burning cart. This was what the rebels and the archivists and the storm-witch's ghost-cult never grasped, because they were all still rich with remembering, still fat with the past. The Veil did not steal. The Veil *relieved*. Every memory taken was a wound closed. Every forgotten grave was a grief that no longer had to be carried up the hill every year with flowers and a bent spine.

The kingdom was drowning in its own past, and he was bailing water.

*Beautifully put,* the Veil said, pleased. *We should write that down.* And then, because it had a sense of humor like a knife laid flat on a table: *Oh wait.*

* * *

The report came at dusk, delivered by a courier whose face Corvel had already forgotten twice today and whose name he had never bothered to learn. Names were scaffolding. When the great work was done, no one would need them.

"The Amberdale sighting is confirmed," the courier said. "Thornfell passes. He used power—a single discharge, controlled, at the border village of Ashen Ford. Three witnesses. Two of the three have since been... processed."

"And the third?"

"An old woman. She forgot her son mid-embrace before we reached her. Natural forgetting. Accelerated." The courier hesitated. "She was holding him when it happened. He ran."

Corvel turned this over. Somewhere beneath the gray calm of him, something shifted—an ache without an object, a toothache of the soul. He had learned to read these residues the way a sailor reads weather. They were the last weather of the man he'd been.

"Amberdale intervened," he said. "After seven years of nothing, he chose to spend himself on one old woman's one remaining memory."

"Yes, Inquisitor."

"Do you know why that matters?" Corvel asked, and the courier, wisely, did not answer. "Because it means he can still be hurt. A man who has sealed himself away feels nothing. But a man who breaks his seal for a stranger's grief—that man is full of grief. He is *carrying* it. All of it, seven years deep." Corvel looked out at the smoke rising from the cart, at the pages curling to ash, each one a small salvation. "He is the most suffering man in Thornfell. Which makes him the most savable."

*Or the most dangerous,* the Veil said.

*Those are the same thing,* Corvel replied. *They have always been the same thing. That is what I am trying to teach you.*

For a moment—rare, precious—the vast attention of the thing paused, as if considering. Then it moved on, the way weather moves on, and Corvel was left alone in his skull with the courier and the smoke and the ache.

* * *

They caught the storm-witch's trail two days later, or rather, the trail caught them: Rafael Amberdale had passed through the cliff roads above the Serevan coast, and where he passed, things *remembered*. A shepherd who had lost his flock to the cullings suddenly recalled every sheep by name. A milestone worn blank by Veil-touch bloomed again with its carved distances. The land itself seemed to wake behind him like water closing over a swimmer.

Corvel knelt at the milestone and pressed his palm flat against the stone, and the Veil in him recoiled—not in fear, exactly. In recognition. In hunger.

"He's leaking," Corvel said aloud. "Every step he takes, he gives back what we've taken. Do you understand what that means?" He stood, joints whispering. "It means the storm doesn't just destroy. It *returns*. He isn't fighting us, old friend. He's *undoing* us. One milestone at a time."

*Then we will take faster than he gives,* the Veil said. *We have eaten a century. We can eat a boy.*

"A man," Corvel corrected. "Twenty-nine years old. Born in the spring, in a house with a blue door—" He stopped. The knowledge had simply been there, filed among the ten thousand facts the Veil fed its servants about their quarry, and something in him snagged on it. *A house with a blue door.* Why did he know that? Why did the Veil keep such small things?

Because small things were hooks, he realized. Because the Veil did not merely consume memory—it curated it, kept certain details bright and polished, kept them the way a fisherman keeps bait.

"Why do we know about his house?" Corvel asked quietly.

Silence, of the particular quality the Veil produced when it was deciding how much truth a servant could hold without cracking.

"We have plans for him," it said finally. "We have had plans for a long time. Walk, Inquisitor. The trail is fresh, and there is a thief traveling with him now—one of ours gone feral, a little collector named Vane. Bring me both. There are memories in that pair worth more than everything in Hollowmere."

* * *

That night, camped on the cliffs with his acolytes sleeping in their bedrolls around a banked fire, Corvel dreamed—or did whatever the Veil-touched did instead of dreaming, which felt like being read aloud to by something with too many voices.

In the dream he was in a kitchen. It was warm. Bread smell. A child's voice saying a word over and over, a word that might have been *Papa*, and he reached for the sound the way a starving man reaches through a window, and just before his fingers touched it, the Veil smoothed the dream away like a hand smoothing a bedsheet and said:

*You're grieving again.*

*I'm dreaming,* Corvel said.

*Grieving. Same root. Same cure.* And then, softer, almost kind, in the tone it saved for its oldest servants: *Do you want it back? The kitchen? We keep it well. We keep everything well. Nothing rots in us, Corvel. Nothing fades. Your daughter's laugh is exactly as it was the day we took it—no, better. We improved the acoustics.*

And here was the terrible thing, the thing Corvel had never confessed to any living soul, partly because he had no living soul left in the usual sense and partly because the confession would have changed nothing: he was tempted. Not weakly. Enormously. Every day the temptation renewed itself like bread. He could have the kitchen back. He could lie down inside the Veil's keeping and be given his whole life again, curated, perfected, permanent—every lost afternoon restored, every dead friend resurrected in perfect detail, grief included if he wanted it, grief served warm at a table set for one, because the Veil could afford grief. Grief was only expensive for creatures who could lose things.

The Veil could not lose anything ever again. Was that not, in the end, what everyone wanted? Was that not the whole mad human project—tombs, portraits, songs, ledgers, dynasties—every civilization a scream against forgetting? The Veil had simply succeeded where kings and priests had failed. It was the only ark that would never sink.

*Say yes,* it whispered. *Say yes and stop hunting. Lie down. Be kept.*

"No," Corvel said, in the dream, in the dark, in the gray morning after. "Not yet. There's work."

*There is always work. That is what we made you for.*

"Yes," he agreed. "It is."

And he rose, and broke his fast on food he could not taste, and gave the order to move on Amberdale's trail, and told himself—as he had told himself for eleven years, with diminishing conviction and increasing fluency—that he was saving people.

* * *

They found the aftermath of the thief on the fourth day.

A waystation inn at the crossroads below Raven Tor, its common room full of travelers who all, when questioned, described the same impossible thing: a woman who had paid for her supper with a memory, pressing two fingers to the innkeeper's brow and leaving behind, as tender, the recollection of the innkeeper's mother singing—which the innkeeper had lost to a culling in the spring. She had been giving memories *back*. Random ones, small ones, spent like coins from a purse that must eventually empty.

"She's with Amberdale," Corvel said. His acolytes nodded and began their preparations, and he stood alone in the common room while around him the travelers resumed their meals, and one old woman hummed a lullaby she had not known yesterday, tears running freely down her face, laughing, humming, crying, alive.

Corvel watched her for a long time.

*She'll lose it again,* the Veil said. *When we pass through. Everything she was just given, we'll take. Is this what moves you, Inquisitor? One woman's borrowed song?*

"Yes," Corvel said honestly.

*Why?*

And because the Veil deserved honesty—it had given him so much, taken so much, and honesty cost him almost nothing now—he answered: "Because she's crying. Because the song hurts her and she asked for it anyway. Because that's the whole argument, isn't it? Right there. She *chose* the grief. Given a clean, painless mind or her mother's voice with all its knives in it, she chose the knives. Every one of them would. You've never once offered the choice. That's the flaw in your arithmetic, old friend. That's the crack in the crown. Mercy that isn't chosen isn't mercy. It's just theft with good manners."

The silence that followed lasted long enough for the fire to settle, for the humming woman to finish her song, for an acolyte to clear his throat nervously.

Then the Veil laughed. It rarely laughed, and when it did, Corvel felt it in his teeth, in the fillings of his dissolving body, in the forty distant bodies across the provinces that flinched as one.

*You argue like him,* it said. *Like Amberdale. Did you know that? Did you know we chose you for this hunt because you argue like him?*

"No," Corvel said slowly. "I didn't know that."

*There is so much you don't know,* the Veil said fondly. *Ride faster.*

* * *

They rode faster. Through the passes of Thornfell, where the maps in Corvel's saddlebag had begun to lie—roads drawn that led nowhere, villages inked in that no scout could find, whole valleys gone soft at the edges like wet paper. The world was fraying where the Veil fed too greedily, and even the Veil's own servants felt it, a seasickness of the soul, a sense of walking on ground that could not quite remember being ground.

On the sixth night, Corvel sat apart from the fires and did the thing he did every night, the ritual the Veil tolerated because rituals kept its instruments sharp: he took inventory of himself.

Name: Corvel. Rank: High Inquisitor. Purpose: the great work, the emptying, the saving.

Daughter: unknown. Possibly nonexistent. The word *daughter* produced an ache and no image, a bell with no clapper.

Wife: unknown. Kitchen: warm. Blue door: whose? Whose blue door?

And then, unbidden, from some sealed room in himself that even the Veil had not fully stripped—or had not wanted to strip, and the thought arrived cold as well-water: *or has kept deliberately, as bait, as hook, as leash*—a single image surfaced.

A girl on a wall. Eight years old, maybe nine. Bare feet. Laughing so hard she hiccupped. Saying a word. Saying—

The word was gone before it landed. The Veil took it back mid-arrival, smooth as a pickpocket, and left him kneeling in the frost with his heart hammering in a chest that was half shadow, gasping at an emptiness shaped exactly like a name.

*Careful,* the Veil said, very softly. *Some doors stay shut for your sake, not ours.*

"Whose sake?" Corvel whispered.

But the Veil had turned its attention elsewhere—to the east, to the coast road, to a storm-witch and a thief moving toward the capital with a century-old book between them—and it did not answer, and Corvel knelt alone under the indifferent stars, aching in a place where he had no memory of having anything to ache about.

He rose. He called the column to order. And as they marched west through the fraying dark, following a trail of returned songs and blooming milestones toward the man the Veil had been planning for longer than Corvel had been its servant, he permitted himself one thought, hidden deep, in the last unlit room of himself:

*Whatever you're keeping from me, old friend—whatever blue door, whatever girl on the wall—I will find it. And when I do, I will use it to kill you.*

The Veil, miles wide and centuries deep, heard him perfectly.

*Of course you will,* it said, and sounded, horribly, like it believed him—and like it did not mind at all.
