## 12. What Sera Took

The prophecy's last line had not left him since the drowned archive.

*Do not trust the one who returns your name.*

Rafael read it again by the light of a shuttered lamp in the safehouse above the tannery—Sera's choice of shelter, not his, which was its own kind of warning now that he thought about it. The words sat on the page in his own handwriting, or something close enough to it that the difference felt like looking at a reflection a half-second too late. He knew the slant of that *r*. He knew the way he crossed his *t*s like fence posts. A century ago, some version of him had dipped a pen and written this sentence the way a man writes a will: knowing it would be read too late to do any good.

He closed the book.

Across the room, Sera Vane sat on an upturned crate with her boots off and her knives laid out on a cloth in front of her, cleaning them with the unhurried devotion of a woman saying her prayers. She did everything with that same economy—the way she ate, the way she slept sitting up against a wall, the way she watched doors. He had told himself for weeks that it was simply what she was: a professional. A thief of memories, precise as a surgeon and about as warm.

But the prophecy had rearranged every gesture into evidence.

"You've been staring at me for an hour," she said without looking up. "Either you're going to kill me or you want to ask me something. I'd prefer the first, honestly. It'd be simpler."

"Would it?"

"For you? No." She set down the whetstone. "For me, yes."

He turned the book over in his hands, spine toward her, and watched her face when she saw which page he'd marked. To her credit—and he hated how much of her behavior now required crediting—she didn't flinch. But something moved behind her eyes, quick as a fish under ice, and Rafael had spent seven years learning to read water.

"The final entry," he said. "*Do not trust the one who returns your name.* Written by me. Or a me. A hundred years before either of us was born." He stood. His knees ached; they always ached now, though he couldn't remember when that had started, and the not-remembering itself had begun to feel less like age and more like theft. "So I'm going to ask you plainly, Sera. What have you taken from me?"

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below them, the tannery vats breathed their sour breath through the floorboards.

"That's not a question you ask a memory-thief lightly," she said.

"I'm not asking lightly. I'm asking at knifepoint." He hadn't drawn one. He didn't need to; the storm under his skin had been answering for him since the shrine, a low pressure behind his ribs that made lamplight gutter when his pulse climbed. It guttered now.

Sera looked at the flame. Then at him. And then she did the last thing he expected.

She laughed—not cruelly, but with a tiredness so old it had gone smooth, like river stone. "Finally. Do you know how long I've been waiting for you to ask? Weeks, Rafael. You walked around with a hole in your head the size of a life and never once wondered where the edges went."

"I wondered."

"No. You *feared*. There's a difference, and the Veil counts on it." She rose, and for once she didn't reach for a knife. She reached instead into her collar and drew out a cord, and from the cord a small object no bigger than a walnut—a sphere of smoked glass banded in tarnished silver, the kind of thing a pawnbroker would sell for a copper and a scholar would kill for. "You want to know what I took. Fine. But you should understand first that taking is the only word you have for it, and it's the wrong one."

"Thieves love redefining their trade."

"Yes," she agreed. "We do. Because everyone else defines it for us." She held the sphere out on her open palm between them. "Touch it."

"Touch it and what? Wake up in a ditch with my childhood missing?"

"Your childhood isn't in a ditch. Your childhood is in here. Along with—" she paused, and for the first time since the cliff road, since the moment she had touched his mind and found her own face waiting there, she looked uncertain, "—along with quite a lot else."

He didn't move. The storm pressed against the backs of his teeth. "Say it plainly. All of it. How long."

"How long have I been siphoning you?" She met his eyes. "Since Thornfell. Since the night you bought a dead man's book and burned a page that wouldn't stay dead. You slept twelve hours in that loft above the fence's shop, and I sat beside you with my hand on your temple and I lifted—" she exhaled, "—pieces. Small ones. The smell of rain on a particular road. A woman's voice singing badly. The weight of a dog asleep on your feet. Things small enough that you wouldn't feel the absence, because that's the craft, Rafael. That's the whole craft. Nobody misses what's taken gently."

The lamp went out. Not dramatically—it simply gave up, the flame shrinking to a blue bead and dying, and the room fell into the brown half-dark of shuttered afternoon. Rafael realized the darkness had come from him, from the pressure in his chest, and he made himself breathe until the wick caught again on its own, trembling.

"You've been robbing me since the beginning," he said quietly.

"I've been *emptying* you," Sera said, and her voice changed, lost its dryness, became something urgent and low. "There's a difference and it's the whole point, so listen to me. The Veil eats. That's all it does, all it has ever done—it takes and it digests and what it takes is gone, gone, gone, unrecoverable, unless someone got there first. I got there first. Every piece I lifted from you, I lifted because I could feel the Veil's attention on you like a hand hovering over a purse. Corvel knows what you are. The crown knows. They've known longer than you have, and every hour you carried those memories loose in your skull, you were carrying them through a country full of pickpockets with inquisitor's writ."

"So you stole them to protect them."

"I stole them to keep them *existing*." She closed her fist around the glass sphere, and Rafael saw, just before her fingers hid it, a faint light moving inside the smoke-colored depths—slow shapes, drifting, like fish in dark water. Like the drowned archive in miniature. "A memory in a living mind can be eaten. A memory in here cannot. This is a vault, not a purse. Everything I've taken from you is still yours. I never sold a grain of it. I never looked at more than I had to. I hoarded you like contraband because that's exactly what you are—the single most dangerous object in this kingdom, wrapped in skin, and too careless to guard yourself."

The silence after that was long. Rain had started somewhere above them, ticking against the roof tiles, and Rafael found himself absurdly grateful for it—for weather, for the sky doing openly what he had done in secret for seven years.

"Why?" he asked finally. "Not the craft answer. Not the Veil answer. Why you. Why spend months carrying a stranger's past around your neck?"

Sera's jaw tightened. He watched her decide, watch the calculation run and fail and run again, and then she said, "Because I found myself in there. On the cliff road, when I touched you—I told you I recoiled. I lied about why. I didn't recoil because it hurt. I recoiled because among your memories was a girl of maybe eight years old, standing in a doorway in a house I have been trying to find in my own head for twenty years, and she was waving at *you*. You were there, Rafael. In whatever came before I was Sera Vane, professional thief, woman with no history worth stealing—you were *there*, and I have been trying to remember that house since before I understood that other people could remember theirs." Her voice frayed at the edge. "I kept siphoning you because every time I touched that vault, I got another inch of myself back. So yes. Call it theft. It was also the most selfish thing I've ever done, and I've done some truly spectacular things."

* * *

They sat across from each other on the floor, the sphere between them on the cloth where her knives had been, and she showed him.

She taught him how to press two fingers to the glass and think of a question rather than an answer—*the vault answered questions, not commands*, she said, as if it were a horse and not a prison—and the first thing he asked for was small, deliberately small, a test of the water's depth. *What did she take first?*

The glass warmed. Light coiled. And the smell hit him before anything else: woodsmoke and wet wool and the particular green rot of autumn apples, and he was standing in a farmyard he had no name for, watching a woman—he knew her, he *knew* her, the knowledge arrived whole and unbearably specific—beat a rug against a fence rail while a dog circled her ankles. Ordinary. Utterly ordinary. And he wept at it, silently, furiously, because he had not known it was gone until it returned, and its return revealed the shape of the absence the way a key reveals the shape of a lock.

"That one," Sera said softly, watching him. "That was the first. I took it the second night, when you talked in your sleep. You said a name I didn't recognize and then you apologized to the dark for an hour. I figured whatever let you sleep through that was worth keeping somewhere safer than your head."

He surfaced from the farmyard gasping. "How much is in there?"

"Of you?" She hesitated. "More than I told you. More than I told myself, at the start. It grew. Every time the Veil's agents came close, I took a little more. When Corvel's hunters reached the border villages, I took the year you spent learning to sail. When we crossed the closed passes, I took your mother." She said it flatly, refusing to dress it. "Her face, her voice, the lullaby she sang wrong on purpose to make you laugh. It's all in here. Nothing is lost. But nothing is *yours*, right now, except what I give back—and I know exactly what that makes me in your eyes."

"It makes you the warden of my life."

"It makes me the only reason you still have one to ward."

He wanted to hate her for it. He reached for hatred the way a man reaches for a familiar tool in a darkened room, and his hand kept closing on nothing, because the truth underneath her confession was worse than treachery and better than kindness: she had treated his past as precious goods, and he had treated it as a burden to be abandoned. Seven years he had spent throwing himself away piece by piece, and this woman had followed behind him gathering the pieces out of the mud.

"What else," he said. His voice came out rough. "Show me the rest. All of it."

"Rafael—"

"*All of it.* If you've been hoarding me like contraband, then I'm inspecting the cargo."

Something flickered across her face—fear, actual fear, the first he'd ever seen on her—and that frightened him more than anything she might have hidden. She turned the sphere in her fingers, and inside it the drifting lights seemed to gather, to press against the inner curve of the glass like faces at a window.

"There's one more thing," she said. "And I need you to hear the whole of it before you look, because if you look first you'll burn this room down around us and I won't blame you." She steadied herself. "Three nights ago, while you scouted the aqueduct, I went deeper than I've ever gone. Past the sailing years, past your mother, down to the oldest stratum—the sealed place, the wound, the night you bound your power. I wasn't going to take anything. I swear that on whatever I have left of myself. I was mapping the edges so that when the time came to unbind you fully, I'd know what I was anchoring." Her hand was shaking now; he could see it, and Sera Vane's hands did not shake. "But the vault doesn't hold what I put in it. It holds what I *found*. And at the bottom of that night—the night of the lightning, the night you killed him—there was something already there. Something that didn't come from you. Something that had been *placed*."

The storm rose in him without permission. The lamp died again, and this time the dark stayed, and the only light in the room was the sphere in Sera's palm, glowing now like a captive dawn.

"Placed," he repeated. The word tasted of copper. "By whom?"

"I don't know yet. But Rafael—it wasn't aimed where you aimed it. Your bolt *curved*. Someone fed your aim to something, bent it mid-flight, and the proof of it has been sitting at the bottom of your grief for seven years, buried under the guilt, and the guilt—" her voice dropped to almost nothing, "—the guilt may be the only part of that night that was truly yours."

The rain stopped. The whole world seemed to stop, the tannery stink and the ticking roof and his own heartbeat, everything suspended in the brown dark around that small burning light.

Seven years. Seven years of exile, of erasure, of counting days since the storm—built on a strike that had never flown true. He thought of the magistrate he had meant to kill. He thought of the man he had loved instead, falling, and the terrible arithmetic of a curved bolt, and he thought: *someone did this. Someone chose.*

"Give it to me," Rafael said.

"Not here. Not all at once—if I pour the whole memory into you unanchored, it will take you apart. We do it properly, the way I'd restore any stolen goods: slowly, with both of us holding the line." She rose, pocketing the sphere, and for a moment she was silhouetted against the shuttered window, thin and hard-edged and utterly steady again. "Tomorrow. At first light. I'll give you back the night you've built your whole life on—and you're going to learn whose hands were on the lightning besides your own."

Outside, far off across the sleeping city, thunder answered—though there had been no lightning, and no cloud, and Rafael understood that the storm had heard its name spoken.
