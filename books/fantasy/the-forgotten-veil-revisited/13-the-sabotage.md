## 13. The Sabotage

Sera had stolen a thousand memories and returned none of them, and she had told herself for a thousand reasons that this made her honest rather than cruel. A thief who keeps her ledger clean. A thief who never lies about the price. But she had lied to Rafael Amberdale in every way that mattered—about the cliff road, about the way his mind had felt under her fingers, about how long she had been carrying pieces of him like coins sewn into a hem—and tonight the ledger came due.

She sat across from him in the ruined chapel above the tree line, where they had gone to ground after the passes closed behind them. Rain worked at the broken roof in slow percussion. He had not spoken since she'd said *I have something of yours*, and the silence between them had weight and temperature, like a stone pulled from deep water.

He was looking at the fire, not at her. That was deliberate, she knew. Rafael Amberdale did everything deliberately; it was the only way a man survived seven years of refusing to be himself.

"Before I give it back," she said, "you should understand what taking a memory looks like from my side."

"I don't care what it looks like from your side."

"You will." She turned her hands over, palms up, the old gesture—the one memory-thieves used when they wanted to show they held nothing sharp. "Every memory has a seam. Where it was cut loose, there's a scar on the mind, a place where the shape doesn't match the hole. Most people never notice their own seams. They build around them. But you—" She hesitated, and hated herself for hesitating, because hesitation was a kind of confession. "You have a seam the size of a cathedral door, and you've spent seven years calling it grief."

His jaw tightened. "Say what you took."

"Not took." The word came out sharper than she intended, and she let it stay sharp, because softness would be worse. "Kept. There's a difference, and you're going to hate me either way, but I'd rather you hate me accurately." She reached into the inner pocket of her coat, where the vial rode against her ribs the way it had ridden for three years, warm as a swallowed coal. "The Veil can't eat what it can't find. So I hid you. In pieces. Scattered through glass and salt and my own head, where its inquisitors don't look because they think everything in a thief is already stolen goods."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I have that won't make you walk out into the rain." She set the vial on the flat tombstone between them. Inside it, the memory moved—a curl of silver light, denser than smoke, turning slowly like a fish deciding which way the current ran. "This one isn't yours alone. It belongs to both of us, though neither of us knew it until now. It's the night your lightning curved."

* * *

She had taken it eleven days after the strike, in the aftermath-city of Vaelmoor, when the magistrate's household was being quietly dissolved by men in gray coats and everyone with a talent was fleeing or dying. Sera had been twenty-two and hungry and professionally amoral, working the crowd outside the burned courthouse, harvesting whatever grief was loose enough to lift. And then she had brushed against a young man sitting on the courthouse steps with his hands still smoking, staring at nothing, and the memory had come off him like a burning page.

She hadn't meant to keep it. Keeping was against every rule she lived by. But she had touched it, and inside it she had seen—

"Corvel," she said now, and watched Rafael's face do something complicated. "Not the High Inquisitor. Not yet. Corvel the clerk. Corvel the man, thirty years old, standing in the gallery of the magistrate's court with a Veil-lens cupped in his palm like a coin he'd been paid."

Rafael's voice came out flat. "I killed a man in that courtroom."

"You aimed at Magistrate Halloway. Yes. I know. I've seen it more times than I can count—I've lived inside that second for three years, Rafael, I know the angle of your arm better than you do." She uncorked the vial, and the smell rose out of it: ozone and wet slate and something older, the smell of the Archive itself. "Do you remember what you felt just before you loosed?"

"No." His hands had gone white on his knees. "That's the point. I remember aiming true. I remember knowing—*knowing*—the bolt would take him through the heart. And then it didn't. Seven years I've told myself the storm slipped. Storms slip. That's what storms are."

"Storms slip," Sera agreed. "Yours was pushed."

She tipped the vial, and the memory poured out onto the space between them—not light exactly, not sound, but the thing memories are before minds dress them in either: a pressure, a presence, a room reassembling itself out of nothing. The chapel filled with the ghost of the courtroom. Dust motes hung in shafts of window-light that no longer existed. And there, at the center of it, stood a younger Rafael Amberdale with storm-light crawling up his forearms, and his face was not the face of a murderer. It was the face of a man doing arithmetic he despised and trusting the sum.

Sera spoke over the vision, low and level, narrating the way she'd narrate a theft, because narration kept her steady. "Watch the gallery. Third row. Gray coat. See the lens? Veil-glass, cut before the rite failed, one of maybe two dozen left in the world. When a memory-thief uses one, we call it *reading*. When the Veil's servants use one, we call it *feeding*. Same act. Different appetite."

In the ghost-courtroom, Corvel raised the lens. His lips moved—she'd lip-read the words a hundred times, in a hundred sleepless replays, until they were worn smooth as river stones: *curve left, curve left, curve left.* Not a command. An instruction to the world. The Veil did not command; it suggested, and reality, being lonely, agreed.

And Rafael's bolt—which in every other telling of this moment went straight and true—curved.

It curved like a thing alive. It bent through the air with a grace that had no business belonging to lightning, and it struck Tomas Ellery through the chest instead, the man standing at Halloway's shoulder, the man whose only crime that day was love.

The vision collapsed. The rain came back. The fire spat.

Rafael did not move for a long time. Sera had seen men receive terrible news before—had, in fact, delivered terrible news as a trade—and she knew all the shapes grief took when it arrived late. Denial wore a smile. Rage wore fists. But Rafael simply sat, very still, with the particular immobility of a man discovering that the floor of his life had been false for seven years, and beneath it, another floor, and beneath that, someone else's design.

"The crown," he said finally. His voice sounded scraped. "You said orders from the crown."

"Corvel wasn't freelancing. The lens was court-issue. The instruction came down through three hands, all of them palace, all of them already half-Veil even then—this was early, Rafael, the first feeding was barely a decade gone, the thing was still learning to wear people." She wrapped her arms around herself. The firelight made her shadow enormous on the chapel wall. "Halloway was bait. You were the blade. The whole trial, the corruption, the public reckoning—it was staged so that a storm-mage with a grudge would be standing in a courtroom with a clear shot and a full heart. The Veil didn't want Halloway dead. Dead men are cheap. It wanted *you*. It wanted your grief. Grief is the richest food there is, and yours was going to be a banquet."

"And exile." He laughed, and there was nothing in it. "Seven years of silence. Seven years of counting days. It designed that too."

"It designed all of it. Your self-erasure wasn't penance, Rafael. It was *ripening*. Every year you refused your name, you got easier to erase entirely. The Veil doesn't need to fight a man who's already disappearing." She leaned forward. "The prophecy book. The date. Your name in dead ink. None of it is coincidence, and none of it is mercy. It chose you a century ago—or rather, it chose the version of you that got pulled out of time during the first feeding, and it's been choosing you ever since, forward and backward, like a hand moving chess pieces in both directions at once."

He stood up so fast the tombstone rocked, and the vial rolled, and Sera caught it without looking, an old reflex.

"A pawn." The word came out of him like something torn. "My whole adult life. The trial. Tomas—" His voice broke on the name and he forced it straight again, brutally, the way he forced everything. "Tomas died because some thing in a crown decided I'd be useful later. And I spent seven years punishing myself for a murder I didn't commit. I built my entire life on top of a lie and called it atonement."

"Yes."

"Don't *yes* me." He rounded on her, and for one heartbeat the storm was right there under his skin, visible, the air going thin and metallic, and Sera understood why kingdoms had once feared the Amberdale name. "You knew. Three years. You carried the proof in your pocket for three years and let me go on believing I'd killed him."

"I carried it because if I'd given it back sooner, the Veil would have taken it!" Her own voice cracked now, and she let it. "Don't you understand yet? Everything I stole from you, I stole *twice*—once from your head, once from theirs. The moment you knew the truth, the truth became findable. Memories leave traces, Rafael. Grief leaves a scent. The inquisitors have been following your guilt like hounds follow blood, and the only reason they haven't found you is that your guilt is *in here*—" she pressed the vial against her sternum, "—and not in you. I am not your thief. I'm your hiding place. And hiding places get burned when the person they're hiding walks out into the open, which is exactly what you're about to do, because I can see it in your face, and I need you to hear me before you do it."

He was breathing hard. The storm-light guttered, held, sank back beneath his skin. Outside, the rain thickened, as if the sky were listening.

"They'll pay for it," he said quietly. "Corvel. The crown. The thing underneath them both. Whatever it costs."

"It will cost memories. Deep ones. You know that now—you've felt the first draft of the price since the shrine." She stepped toward him, close enough to see the exhaustion under the fury, the seven years of it, the sheer grinding cost of a man carrying a grief that was never his to carry. "So before you unbind anything, before you swear any oath, you take this back. All of it. Every piece I hold. Because you can't fight a war for the past while your own past is sitting in a thief's coat pocket."

"Why?" The word was almost gentle, which was worse than anger. "Why did you do it? Really. Not the ledger answer. Not the professional answer. Why does a woman who sells memories for bread carry a stranger's grief for three years through a kingdom that's forgetting its own name?"

Sera looked at him, and the truth rose in her like floodwater, and for once in her life she did not steal around it.

"Because when I took that memory off the courthouse steps, I saw myself in it." Her voice dropped. "A girl in the gallery. Dark hair, gray coat, watching. You never noticed me—you were a little busy—but you were *there*, Rafael, and I was there, and I have checked that memory a thousand times trying to make sense of it, because I have no childhood. No mother, no father, no first snow, no name-day. My earliest memory is a courthouse in Vaelmoor and a man with smoking hands. Either someone stole my whole life before I was old enough to miss it—" she held up the vial, "—or someone put me in yours. And I don't know which possibility frightens me more."

The rain drummed. The fire sank to embers. Rafael stared at her, and she watched the fury drain out of his face and leave behind something rawer and far more dangerous, the thing she had dreaded and hoped for in equal measure since the cliff road.

Hope.

"Then we stop running from the question," he said. "Tonight. Give me all of it—every piece you kept. And then show me the road to the mountain shrine, because I'm done letting other people aim me."

Sera uncorked the vial and held it out, and in the ember-light the memory inside turned toward him like a compass finding north.

"One condition," she said. "When the storm starts taking your memories tomorrow—and it will—you hold on to the ones worth paying. Let the rest go. But the truth of tonight, you keep. Promise me."

"I promise," Rafael Amberdale said, and took back the first hour of his stolen life.
