## 14. Storm Oath

The shrine had no name anymore.

Rafael knew this because Sera told him so, twice, on the climb—once as a fact and once as a warning. *The Veil got here first,* she said, standing in the doorway of the little stone sanctuary wedged into the shoulder of the mountain like a swallow's nest. *Whatever this place was called, whoever kept it, whatever god it answered to—all of it's gone. Eaten. The stones remember nothing but their own weight.*

He ran his hand along the doorframe anyway. Old granite, lichen-crusted, cold as a dead man's cheek. There had been carvings once; he could feel the ghosts of them under his palm, grooves worn shallow by centuries of pilgrim fingers before the wearing stopped. His fingers found the shape of something that might have been a lightning bolt, or might have been a river, or might have been nothing at all now that the meaning had been devoured.

*Good,* he thought, and hated himself for thinking it. A hollow shrine was the right place for what he meant to do. You did not perform an amputation in a house of healing.

Inside, the sanctuary was one round room with a firepit at its center and a ring of flat stones around it where worshippers had once knelt. The roof was half-collapsed, letting in a wedge of grey afternoon sky, and through that wedge he could see the peak above them, snow-streaked, indifferent. Wind moved through the broken rafters and made a low sound, almost like breathing.

Sera built a fire while he stood in the doorway like a man hesitating on the edge of cold water. She didn't urge him. She'd learned that much about him on the road from Thornfell—that urging was a door slammed in her face, that he came to things in his own time or not at all. Instead she talked, quietly, about nothing: the quality of the kindling, the way mountain wood burned faster than lowland oak, the particular stubbornness of flint. Filling the silence the way you'd fill a room with furniture so no one could hear how empty it was.

He loved her for it and did not tell her so. He was not sure, anymore, which of his feelings were real and which had been planted, and until he knew, he kept them all locked in the same box.

* * *

The unbinding was not complicated. That was the cruelest part of it.

Seven years ago, binding his power had taken him three days of ritual, blood, and agony—a knot tied through his own marrow, sealed with grief so dense it might as well have been lead. He had done it weeping, in a cellar in a town whose name he could still say, over a body he could still see when he let himself, which was rarely, which was never if he could help it.

Unbinding took a sentence.

That was the whole secret they never told children who dreamed of storm-magic: the power was never truly bound. It was only *asked* to wait. And all it required to stop waiting was for its keeper to say, aloud, in his own voice, *I release myself.* The storm did the rest. The storm had always been patient. The storm had seven years of patience banked like a debt accruing interest.

"You don't have to do it here," Sera said, when the fire was burning and the light through the roof-gap had gone the color of old pewter. "You don't have to do it at all. I want that said plainly, once, so you can't tell yourself later that I pushed you."

"You didn't push me." He knelt at the ring of stones. The granite bit into his knees through his trousers. "The Veil pushed me. Corvel pushed me. Whoever fed my aim to that thing seven years ago pushed me. You're just—" He searched for the word and found it and disliked it. "—the person standing next to me."

"Someone has to be."

"Why?" He looked up at her. Her face was firelit on one side, shadowed on the other, and he thought—not for the first time—that she had the kind of face that made both look intentional. "Why does someone have to be? What happens if I'm alone?"

She held his gaze without flinching, which he had come to understand was her native language. "You know what happens. You've felt it already, that spark in the village. One controlled flicker and it nearly took your knees out. This is—" She gestured at the sky through the broken roof, at the pressure sitting on the mountain like a hand. "This is seven years of held breath. When you let it go, it won't be a flicker. It'll be everything at once, because you've taught it that everything is what waiting means. And everything, Rafael, has a price. You know the price."

"The memories."

"The memories." She crouched across the fire from him, forearms on her knees, close enough that he could see the small scar through her left eyebrow that he had never asked about. "The storm doesn't take randomly. It takes what's *charged*. What's lit up. And right now, the most charged thing in you is—" She stopped. Started again, gentler. "It's your grief. It's him. It's your mother. It's every memory with weight behind it. The storm will drink from the deepest wells first, because they're easiest to reach."

"So I lose him twice."

"You might lose him forever." No flinch. No softening. She gave him the wound whole, the way a surgeon gives a diagnosis. "Or you might lose something else entirely. That's the truth of it—I don't know, and neither do you, and anyone who tells you they know is selling something. But I know this: the Veil is eating a kingdom's worth of remembering while we sit on this mountain. Every day we wait, there's less world left to save. So the arithmetic is yours to do. I just wanted you doing it with your eyes open."

He looked at the fire for a long time.

Then he said, "If I start to go—if I forget why I'm doing this—you have to talk me back. Not with magic. With details. Names, faces, small stupid things. The mind holds on to specifics when it loses everything general. My mother used to—" His throat closed. He worked past it. "My mother used to sing while she kneaded bread. Off-key. She'd get worse when she was happy. If I forget her face, tell me about the singing."

"I don't know anything about your mother."

"You will," Rafael said. "I'm going to tell you everything first. While I still have it to give."

* * *

So he spent the last hour of his bound life giving it away.

He sat cross-legged by the fire and he poured himself out like a man emptying a pack before a river crossing, and Sera listened with her whole body, leaning forward, interrupting only to pin down details. *What color were her hands?* Flour-white at the knuckles, brown everywhere else, from years at an outdoor loom. *Which eye did your father have?* Only the left; the right had gone white when Rafael was four, from a splinter of oak, and he'd worn the blindness like a joke, telling everyone he'd given half his sight to see his son being born. *The lover's name?*

And here Rafael stopped, and the wind through the rafters filled the silence, and Sera waited, and finally he said the name aloud for the first time in seven years, and it did not kill him, which surprised him more than anything else that day. It just hurt, the way setting down something very heavy hurts your arms after you've carried it so long the weight became your posture.

He told her about the magistrate. About the bolt. About the curve—the wrongness of it, the impossible bend that Sera's recovered memory had explained, the sabotage, Corvel's human hand feeding his aim to the hungry dark. He told her about the cellar and the binding and the seven gray years of caravans and false names and counting days.

And then, because there was nothing left to lose by it, he told her the small things. That he was afraid of deep water and had never learned to swim. That he liked the smell of rain on hot stone better than any perfume. That he wrote letters in his head for seven years and sent none of them, and that the letters had gotten shorter each year until the last one was only three words long, and those three words were—

"Don't tell me those," Sera said quietly. "Keep those. Give me everything else, but keep those. A man should walk into a fire holding one thing that's only his."

* * *

Night came down the mountain like slow water. The fire burned low. Through the roof-gap, stars appeared—one, then five, then a scatter of them, hard and cold and ancient, and Rafael thought: *some of those lights are already forgotten. Some star out there has been eaten, and its light still travels, and no one below remembers what it was named for.*

He rose. His legs were steady, which seemed obscene.

"How do I—" he began.

"Just speak it," Sera said. "You've known the words since before you could read. Everyone does. That's the horror of it. The power isn't hidden in you. It's only polite."

He almost laughed. It came out as something between a laugh and a cough.

He spread his arms. Not for drama—for grounding. Palms open, the way you'd stand in a high wind, the way his teacher had shown him a lifetime ago in a schoolyard under a summer storm, back when magic was a game played with sparks and laughter. He felt the knot inside himself, the seven-year seal, and it was exactly where he'd left it, patient as a dog at a door.

*I release myself,* he thought.

He said it aloud.

The world went white.

Not light—*sound*, first, a crack that wasn't heard so much as undergone, a detonation inside his own skeleton, and then the light, pouring up out of him through his opened palms, through his eyes, through the seams of him, seven years of dammed storm finding its old riverbed all at once. The fire flattened. The ring of kneeling-stones shivered in their sockets. Above, through the broken roof, clouds boiled across the stars like ink dropped in milk, and the air went metallic, and every hair on his body stood up in homage, and Rafael Amberdale stood in the center of his own returning power and screamed—not in pain, though there was pain, but in *relief*, which was worse, relief so total it felt like dying.

And then the price came due.

He felt it as a tug. Gentle, at first. Almost tender. Something reaching into him the way you'd reach into a drawer for a folded shirt—and it drew out a memory of his father teaching him to whittle, and the memory simply *wasn't there anymore*, and worse, he couldn't tell what had been taken, only that a drawer somewhere in him now stood empty, its absence shaped like nothing at all.

"No," he gasped. "No—wait—"

The storm did not wait. The storm had waited seven years. It reached deeper.

His mother's kitchen. The smell of yeast and woodsmoke. Her singing, off-key, getting worse when she was happy—he could feel the memory glowing, charged, lit up like a lantern, exactly as Sera had said, the deepest well, the easiest reach—and the storm closed around it and began to *drink*—

Her face went first. Not erased—*smudged*, like a thumb dragged across wet ink. He could feel the shape of her, the warmth of her, the flour and the loom-brown hands, but the face above them was going soft, going pale, going to a blur, and he grasped for it the way you grasp for a dream on waking and came away with nothing, and the terror of that was absolute, total, a black vertigo, because a man could survive losing his power but losing her face was losing the first country he'd ever lived in—

"—flour-white at the knuckles!"

A voice. Cutting through the whiteout like a blade through canvas.

"Brown everywhere else—from the outdoor loom! She sang off-key and got WORSE when she was happy, you told me that, you told me that an hour ago and I believed you because you cried when you said it—"

"Sera—" His voice came out shredded.

"She kneaded bread! Yeast and woodsmoke! Your father had one white eye—the RIGHT one—an oak splinter, and he joked about it, he said he'd given half his sight to see you born, and you LAUGHED at that joke your whole childhood, you laughed every single time—"

The storm pulled harder. He felt another drawer opening—his childhood home, its walls, its crooked door—

"Crooked blue door!" she shouted. "Your house had a crooked blue door that stuck in winter and your mother painted it three times and it never fixed and you said—" Her voice cracked, steadied. "You said it stuck because the house didn't want to let you leave. You said that. Those were your words. Come back and tell me I'm right. Come back and tell me I'm right, Rafael!"

And he *did*. He clawed back along her voice like a rope thrown into floodwater, hand over hand, detail over detail, and the storm fought him for every inch—it was not malicious, that was the terrible thing, it was only *hungry*, the way weather is hungry, the way rivers are hungry—but her voice was anchored in the world outside him, in stone and fire and night air, and the storm could not eat what was spoken aloud, could not devour a thing that two people held between them.

He came back to himself on his knees, palms pressed flat to the freezing granite, shaking, soaked in sweat despite the mountain cold, and above him the clouds churned and grumbled but did not strike, and the power sat in him like a live coal wrapped in flesh—present, awake, *his*.

"Say it again," he rasped. "All of it. Everything I told you. Say it until I can say it with you."

So she did. For what remained of the night, Sera Vane sat across a dying fire and recited him back to himself—his mother's hands, his father's eye, the crooked blue door, the off-key singing, the name he'd spoken once and would not speak again tonight—and each time he repeated a detail, it solidified, the smudge sharpening back toward a face, the drawer refilling, not fully, never quite fully, but enough. Enough to live on.

Near dawn, exhausted, wrung out, he understood what he had agreed to, really understood it, in his bones: this was what power would be now. Not the clean joy of his youth. A transaction. Every deep casting a withdrawal. Every storm a slow amputation, and Sera beside him with her ledger of details, sewing him shut a piece at a time.

"Worth it?" she asked quietly, when the eastern sky had gone the color of ash and pearl.

He looked at his hands. Sparks crawled lazily between his fingers, small and tame as fireflies, and did not hurt.

"It will be," he said. "When I spend it on something."

As if the mountain had been waiting for the words, the far southern horizon flickered—once, twice—silent heatless lightning, too distant for thunder. Sera stood, shading her eyes, and Rafael rose beside her, and they watched together as the flickering resolved into something neither of them had ever seen: storms breaking over the capital, over a city that should have been a week's ride away, pulsing like a heartbeat under a bruise.

"That's not weather," Sera said.

"No," Rafael said. The coal in his chest stirred. "That's the Veil. It knows what I've done. And it's started eating the city *all at once*."

He was already moving for the door.
