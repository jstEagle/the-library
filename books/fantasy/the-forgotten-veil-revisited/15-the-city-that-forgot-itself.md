## 15. The City That Forgot Itself

The baker's name was Odo Fenwick, and at half past four in the morning he could no longer read his own hands.

He stood in the dark of his kitchen with a flour-sack slumped against his hip and the recipe book open on the scarred oak table, the one his grandmother had written in a hand like bird tracks, and the words had gone strange. Not faded—strange. The letters were all still there, black and patient in their rows, but they refused to mean. *Honey* looked like honey. It looked like a drawing of a sound. He knew what honey was; he could taste it if he thought hard enough, golden and slow off the spoon. But the word sat on the page like a stranger at a funeral, dressed correctly, belonging to nothing.

"Grandmother's hand," he said aloud, to hear something true in the room.

Except—he frowned—the book wasn't his grandmother's. Was it? He turned back to the flyleaf. There had been an inscription there once; he was almost sure there had been an inscription, a line in that same bird-track script, *for Odo, who will rise early all his life.* Now the page showed only a faint brown shadow where ink had been, the ghost of writing pressed into the paper like a face into a pillow.

His wife had died three winters ago. He knew this the way you know a load-bearing wall is behind the plaster: not by seeing it, but by the shape of everything else. Her name would not come. He stood in his kitchen saying *her name will not come* over and over, and each repetition made the absence smoother, more comfortable, until by the time the ovens needed lighting he had stopped reaching for it at all, and the grief lay down inside him like a dog told to stay, and he baked bread for a city whose name he also could not quite find, and the bread came out perfect, because his hands remembered even when he did not.

That was the cruelest arithmetic of it, though Odo would never have the words to say so: the Veil did not take skill. It took story. A man could knead dough forever without knowing why the kneading mattered.

* * *

On the western wall of the capital, a soldier named Brakka Voss stood her watch and wondered why she was standing it.

She was nineteen years old and had been a soldier for six years, or so said the sergeant, and the uniform fit her shoulders the way a thing fits when it has been fitted many times. Below her the city spread out gray-gold in the dawn smut of cookfires, ten thousand rooftops, and beyond the walls the orchards, and beyond the orchards the hills where the enemy—

Where the enemy who?

She gripped her spear. Her hands knew the grip. Her feet knew the sentry's walk, heel-to-toe along the parapet, and her eyes swept the treeline in the old pattern, left to right, far to near, because that was how eyes were supposed to sweep a treeline when you were looking for—

She laughed, alone up there, a short ugly laugh with no humor in it. Looking for what? Bandits? She couldn't remember a bandit raid in her lifetime. War? There hadn't been a war. Had there been a war? The wall under her boots was forty feet high and older than anything else she'd ever touched, and someone had built it, and people only built walls like this for one reason, and the reason was gone from her head as cleanly as a name scraped off a door.

Sergeant Ilves came along the parapet and stopped beside her, and they stood together in the wind, two young women guarding a wall against nothing, and neither of them asked the other why, because asking felt dangerous, the way poking a bruise feels dangerous.

"Pretty morning," said Ilves.

"Pretty morning," said Brakka.

Below them, at the gate, a merchant argued with a customs clerk about a tariff neither of them could produce the rule for, and the argument went on and on because both men kept feeling, in their bones, that there *was* a rule, that the rule mattered, that somewhere a ledger existed which would settle everything—and the ledger's pages had been blank since Tuesday, and no one had noticed, because noticing requires remembering what was written before.

* * *

Rafael Amberdale came through the south gate at midmorning wearing a carter's coat and another man's boredom.

He had expected ruin. He had expected panic—barricades, screaming, the animal chaos of a city that knows it is being eaten. What he found instead was worse: order. A city going about its business with a hole in the middle of itself, the way a man with a tooth knocked out keeps smiling, keeps talking, tongue worrying the gap every few minutes and finding nothing there, again, always nothing there.

He walked the Grainmarket Street slowly, hood down, hands in pockets, and he watched.

A woman was teaching her daughter to skip rope outside a chandler's shop, chanting a rhyme—*one for the silver, two for the gold*—and losing her place in the middle, every time, coming back around to *one for the silver* with a small puzzled frown, and starting again, and the girl laughing, thinking it was a game, the rhyme that never got anywhere.

A barber swept hair off his step and paused, broom still, staring at the row of painted signs above the shops across the way. Rafael followed his gaze. The signs were wrong. Not damaged—wrong. One read simply **THE SHOP**. Another, above a place that smelled unmistakably of tallow and smoke, bore only the word **THINGS**. The barber shook himself like a wet dog and went back to sweeping, and Rafael understood that the sign-painters themselves must be forgetting the names of the businesses they painted, that the erasure had moved upstream into language itself, into the act of naming.

At a fountain in the square, an old man sat feeding pigeons and telling anyone who would listen about the flood. "The year the river took the lower town," he said, tapping his stick. "I was a boy. Water to the second floor. My father carried me on his back through the bell tower arch." People smiled politely and moved along. Rafael stopped and listened, and his blood went cold, because he knew the capital's history now, had spent three days with Sera drilling it into him from the Archive fragments they'd recovered, and there had been no such flood. No flood, no lower town drowned, no bell tower arch. The old man was not misremembering. He was remembering something that had been *given* to him—a replacement, a counterfeit past, minted fresh by the Veil to fill the hole it had dug, because a hole that shows its edges makes people dig, and the Veil could not abide digging.

The pigeons ate. The old man smiled at the sun. And Rafael stood in the middle of the square with his heart hammering and understood, finally, viscerally, what Sera had been trying to make him understand for weeks: this was not a war for territory. Armies could take cities and give them back. This was a war for the past itself, and the past could not be retaken, because no one alive would know it needed retaking. You cannot liberate a prison whose inmates have been taught to love the walls.

He bought a meat pie from a stall because buying things made him invisible, and while the vendor wrapped it the man chattered cheerfully about his stall—"been in my family forever, don't know how long, don't know who started it, funny, isn't it"—and laughed, and Rafael laughed with him, and the laughter tasted like ash.

* * *

In the temple district, a priest named Anselm lost his god at noon.

It happened between one bell and the next. He was vesting for the afternoon office, handling the robes with the ease of thirty years, when he reached the altar and found the statue there and could not say who it depicted. Stone face, stone hands, stone mercy. Beautiful. Anonymous. He checked the liturgy book and found the hymns intact but the deity's name replaced throughout with a dash, a long patient horizontal stroke, hundreds of them, one where every holy name should be, so that the entire service read like a sentence with its heart cut out.

He knelt. He prayed—to whom? To the dash. To the absence. And here was the terrible grace of it: the praying still worked. Something in him bowed, and the bowing eased him, because devotion outlives its object the way a riverbed outlives the river. He wept without knowing what for, and rang the bell, and the congregation came, and they sang the hymns with the dashes in them, and it sounded, to anyone passing in the street, exactly like worship.

Rafael passed the temple at the noon bell and heard the singing and had to stop, hand braced on cold stone, because for one vertiginous moment he could not remember why he had come to the capital at all. The song reached into him and found the same soft places the Veil had found in everyone else. His purpose wobbled. His name—that buried name, the one he'd paid seven years to keep silent—wobbled worse. For a heartbeat he was nobody, a carter in a borrowed coat, standing in a city that had forgotten itself, and the peace of it was obscene. How easy. How *easy* it would be to put the coat on permanently, to let the last of Rafael Amberdale drain away into the general forgetting, to be a man with a meat pie and no debts, no dead lover, no storm coiled in his chest like a debt of its own.

Then he thought of Sera's voice saying his name—not the false one, the real one, the one she'd been carrying like contraband—and the wobble steadied. Someone remembered him. As long as one person held the thread, he was not yet eaten.

He walked on. Behind him the hymn swelled, gorgeous and hollow, praising the dash.

* * *

By evening the gaps had grown teeth.

Rafael saw it happen in a tavern called the Copper Kettle, in the kind of low-ceilinged room where news traveled faster than anywhere official. A drover stood up on a bench and announced that the road east was closed, that the village of Marrow's End had vanished—not burned, not flooded, *vanished*—that he'd ridden the road twice in a week and the second time there was a hill where the village green had been, an old hill, mossy, unremarkable, as if it had always been there, and his own memory of the village was already going soft at the edges, already smoothing itself over the way water smooths sand, and he was frightened, gentlemen, he was properly frightened, and would anyone else say they remembered Marrow's End?

Silence. Then, one by one, faces around the room performed the same small collapse: the furrowed brow, the searching pause, the shrug. Nobody remembered Marrow's End. Why would they? It had never existed. The drover looked around at all those pleasant blank faces and Rafael watched the exact moment the man understood he was alone with his memory, the only witness of a whole world, and that within a few days he would doubt himself too, and then the hill would have always been there for everyone, forever.

Someone laughed at him. Just one drunk, but others smiled, and the drover sat down, and Rafael's hands curled into fists under the table so hard his knuckles cracked.

This was the strategy. He saw it now, whole, the way you see a pattern in a carpet once someone points out the border. The Veil didn't need to fight the city. It needed only to let the city forget it had ever been worth fighting for. A kingdom with no past raises no defenders. A people with no history cannot be occupied, because occupation requires a before and after, and the Veil was patiently dissolving the *before*. In a generation there would be no archives to burn, no heirlooms to confiscate, no graves to scrape clean. The work would be finished and self-maintaining, a forgetting that forgot itself.

And the crown sat at the center of it all, smiling, wearing a king.

* * *

He climbed to the roof of the tannery he'd rented a cot in and looked out over the capital as the lamps came on, district by district, a constellation of small stubborn fires.

From up here the city looked whole. Smoke rising straight in the windless dusk, bells ringing the hour, lamplighters working their rounds with the competence of habit. Ten thousand lives being lived in the ordinary way. And underneath, everywhere, the quiet hemorrhage: recipes unreadable, gods unnamed, villages unmade, mothers' names gone smooth as river stones. The Veil did not roar. It *tidied*. It was the world's most patient housekeeper, removing clutter no one would miss.

Sera appeared beside him at the roof edge without sound, the way she always did, and for a while neither spoke.

"The eastern districts are nearly gone," she said finally. "The Archive fragments I've been carrying—I checked them tonight. Three of them are blank now." Her voice was level, but her hands weren't; she had them laced together tight enough to whiten. "Memories don't survive in a stolen mind any better than in a living one, if the Veil gets close enough. It's eating backward through everything that remembers. Including me."

"Then we go down tomorrow," Rafael said. "Into the Archive. Tonight, if you'd rather."

"There's something else." She turned to look at him, and the lamplight from below caught the underside of her face, and he saw that she was afraid in a way he hadn't seen before—not for herself. "The blanks in my fragments aren't random. They're arranged. I laid them out and the gaps form a sequence, a countdown—it's the same pattern that's in the prophecy book, the marginalia your future self wrote in the deep stacks. Rafael, the erasure isn't spreading. It's *converging*. Everything the Veil has taken, every memory in every head in this city, is being pulled toward one point, one date, one hour." She swallowed. "Tomorrow at moonrise. Whatever happens in the throne room—whatever we do down there—we do it before the moon clears the palace spire, or there won't be a past left to save."

Below them, the bells rang the hour, sweet and certain, counting down toward a midnight the city could no longer fear because it could no longer remember what midnight meant.

Rafael watched the moon rise fat and pale over the eastern wall, one night short of full, and felt the storm stir in his chest like something waking.

"One day," he said.

"One day," said Sera.
