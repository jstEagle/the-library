## 16. Corvel's Last Human Hour

The Veil did not sleep, and so neither did Corvel.

He stood at the window of the inquisitors' quarters in Thornfell's garrison, watching the last light drain out of the mountains, and he tried—gently, patiently, the way one coaxes a splinter from a child's palm—to remember his daughter's face.

He could not.

He knew that he had a daughter. The knowledge sat in him like a stone in a shoe: small, hard, impossible to ignore. He knew her name had begun with *M*. He knew she had been seven when the rite took him, or perhaps nine—the numbers slid away whenever he reached for them, like fish in dark water. He knew he had loved her more than his own breath. That much the Veil had left him, perhaps as a kindness, perhaps as a hook. Grief without memory was a wound that could not close and could not be examined; it simply bled.

*You are sad,* the Veil murmured through the lattice of his thoughts, in its voice like ash settling on snow. *Why are you sad? There is nothing to be sad about. Sadness requires a before. You have no before. You have only now, and now is clean.*

*I had a daughter,* Corvel answered, for the ten-thousandth time.

*Did you?* And the doubt came flooding in, warm and anesthetic, the sweet fog of forgetting, and Corvel clung to the stone-in-the-shoe feeling with both hands because it was all he had left of being a man.

Below, in the garrison yard, his inquisitors were burning the day's harvest. Letters, mostly. A wedding ledger. A cradle, carved with a family name that no longer belonged to anyone who could read it. The smoke rose straight in the windless evening, gray against gray, and Corvel felt the familiar satisfaction—the rightness of it—warring with the familiar wrongness, and both feelings were so old now that they had worn smooth as river stones.

The Amberdale man was close. He could feel it the way a scar feels weather. The storm-signature hung over Thornfell like the smell of rain: faint, suppressed, but unmistakable to what Corvel had become. Three days ago a border village had reported a single spark, controlled, deliberate—a candle-flame where once there had been a bonfire—and Corvel had known, with a certainty that bypassed thought entirely, *he is coming back to himself.*

Good, some buried remnant of the man whispered. Let him come. Let someone fight it.

No, said everything else. Find him. Burn what he loves. There is nothing he loves. There is nothing anyone loves. Love is only memory wearing perfume.

A knock at the door. Not the Veil's knock—it made no sound at all—but a human knuckle on human wood, hesitant.

"Enter."

It was a junior inquisitor, one of the half-touched ones whose eyes still reflected light properly. "My lord Corvel. There is... a visitor. At the gate. Alone. Unarmed." The boy's throat worked. "He asked for you by name. Your *old* name."

The name went through Corvel like a blade of ice. He had not heard it spoken aloud in eleven years. It sounded, in the boy's mouth, like something dug up from a grave—muddy, smaller than he remembered, absurdly human.

"Describe him," Corvel said, though he already knew.

"Tall. Dark-haired, going gray at the temples. Walks like a soldier who's trying to forget how. He said—" the boy swallowed— "he said you'd know why he'd come. He said to tell you: *the well at Merrow's Cross.*"

* * *

Corvel walked down to the gate alone, and every step of the way the two halves of him argued.

The man remembered Merrow's Cross: a village of forty souls in a green fold of hills, a well with a roof of slate, and a girl named Maren—*Maren*, the name arriving whole and sudden, like a bird through a window—who used to drop pebbles down the well to hear them splash, counting each one, and Corvel had lifted her onto his shoulders afterward and she had pulled his hair and called him *Dada* and—

And the Veil slammed shut around the memory like a hand closing over a candle.

*That is contraband,* it said, cold with something almost like fear. *Where did that name come from? Who gave it back to you?*

"He's here," Corvel said aloud, to the empty stairwell, to the thing inside him. "He's come to give me back my daughter. And you cannot stop me from hearing him out."

*I can unmake you,* the Veil said, and there was no threat in it, only arithmetic. *You are ninety-one percent mine. Do not mistake the remainder for a self.*

But it let him go to the gate. Curiosity, Corvel thought. Even gods have it. Or perhaps it wanted to see what Rafael Amberdale would offer, so it could take the offering and burn it and teach them both the futility.

Rafael stood outside the portcullis in the blue dusk, hands open and empty at his sides, and up close Corvel could see what the years and the exile had done to him: a man built out of careful silences, holding himself together the way a dam holds water. His eyes were the worst part. They were the eyes of someone who had already lost everything and was now negotiating with the loss itself.

"You got my message," Rafael said.

"I got your message." Corvel's voice came out rusted. When had he last used it for anything but commands? "You're either very brave or very stupid, coming here. I have six inquisitors within a shout. I have orders to take you apart thread by thread and feed the threads to—"

"To it. Yes." Rafael didn't flinch. "I know what you are now, Corvel. I've seen what it makes of its servants. But I also know what you were." He stepped closer, and the guards at the wall tensed, and Corvel raised one finger and they held. "Merrow's Cross," Rafael said quietly. "The well with the slate roof. A girl who counted pebbles."

The world stopped.

Not literally—the torches still guttered, the wind still moved—but Corvel felt something crack open in his chest that had been sealed since the rite, and through the crack poured Maren: Maren at four with jam on her chin, Maren asleep against his shoulder on the road home from market, Maren the last morning, waving from the doorway, shouting *come back soon, Dada*, and he had said *always*, he had said *I promise*, and then the crown's men had come with their diagrams and their promises of purpose and he had gone into the rite-chamber a father and walked out a—

"Dada," Corvel said. Out loud. In front of everyone.

His knees went. He caught himself on the portcullis bars, and the iron was cold and real and the pain in his palms was real and none of it mattered, none of it mattered at all, because his daughter's name was alive in his mouth again after eleven years in the dark, and he was weeping, great ugly human sobs that shook the thing's lattice inside him until it shrieked its cold alarm.

*Stop this,* the Veil hissed. *Stop this NOW.*

"She had a birthmark," Corvel gasped, gripping the bars, looking up at Rafael with streaming eyes. "On her wrist. Like a little—like a swallow in flight. Is she—did you find her—is she alive?"

Rafael's face did something complicated. Pity and guilt and resolve. "She's alive, Corvel. She's married. She has a son. She remembers you—she's one of the few who does, because the Archive kept what you gave up. Her memory of you is *in there*, whole, waiting." He pressed his hand flat against the portcullis, palm to palm with the monster on the other side of the bars. "I can't get it out alone. But you—" his voice broke slightly— "*you* could walk in. What's left of you. The part it hasn't eaten. If any of it is still you, you could carry her memory out yourself. No theft. No price. Just a father, taking back what was stolen."

For one shining moment—one heartbeat, maybe two—Corvel was not mostly Veil. He was Edran Corvel, captain of the border watch, widower, father of Maren who counted pebbles, and he was going to say yes. He felt the yes rising in him like water finding its level, inevitable, joyous, and he opened his mouth to speak it—

The Veil punished him.

There was no better word. It did not rage; rage was human. It *corrected*. Corvel felt the lattice seize the memory of Maren—the name, the birthmark, the well, the waving girl in the doorway, the whole bright shoal of her—and drag it up into the open air between himself and Rafael, where it hung visible for one terrible instant: a shimmering ribbon of golden light, dense with images, humming with love, and Rafael made a sound like a man watching a child fall.

Then the Veil burned it.

Not quickly. Deliberately. The ribbon blackened from the ends inward, curling like paper in a fire, and Corvel heard—everyone in the yard heard—a sound like a voice singing that rises and rises and then stops mid-note. The golden light guttered, shrank, and went out, and the ash of it drifted down between the portcullis bars and settled on Corvel's hands, and it was warm, briefly, the last warmth of his daughter, and then it was nothing at all.

*Maren,* Corvel thought. *Maren Mar—*

Gone. The stone in his shoe was gone. The grief remained—heavy, sourceless, a mourning with no corpse—and that was the true cruelty, Corvel understood dimly, kneeling in the ash of his own heart. The Veil never took the grief. Only the reason for it. It wanted its servants grieving forever, so they would believe, as it believed, that memory was the enemy. That forgetting was mercy.

"There," the Veil said through his mouth, and Corvel felt his lips move without his permission, felt his voice flatten into that ash-and-snow tone. "Now you see, storm-caller. This is what defiance costs. He wept for a name, and the name is cinders. Would you like to know what your dead lover's memory weighs, in the Archive? Would you like to watch me burn that too, page by page, while you—"

"No," Rafael said.

It was very quiet. The yard had gone utterly still—the guards, the junior inquisitor, the dying torches—all of them staring at the ash on the ground, at the thing wearing their lord's face, and Corvel, deep down in the nine percent, screaming soundlessly against the walls of his own skull.

"No," Rafael said again, and the air above the garrison began, very softly, to rumble. Clouds that had no business existing gathered over Thornfell in the space of three breaths, and lightning lit them from within like a lantern behind skin. "You've just made a mistake, and I want you to understand it fully, so I'll say it plainly." His eyes had gone pale, storm-pale, and the first drops of rain struck the dust of the yard like thrown stones. "Eleven years ago you helped take everything from me, and I swore off the storm rather than become what killed him. I meant to keep that oath. But you just burned a father's love in front of me to prove a point." Thunder rolled, close enough to taste, metallic on the tongue. "So here is my counterpoint."

He raised one hand, and the lightning came down—not striking, not yet, but *waiting*, coiling around his fist like a living thing, and every guard in the yard fell to their knees, though whether from terror or awe even they could not have said.

"The next memory you burn," Rafael said, "I will make you feel. All of it. Every stolen century in that drowned archive of yours—I will open the floodgates and drown you in what you've eaten, and we will discover together whether a god of forgetting can survive remembering."

And then he was gone—not fled, simply *withdrawn*, stepping backward into the rain and the gathering dark, leaving the garrison in stunned silence and the ash of Maren Corvel scattered across the mud like grey snow.

* * *

Deep inside, in the nine percent, in the small cell where the man still lived, Edran Corvel knelt in the ruins of himself and found, to his astonishment, that he was not empty.

The Veil had burned the memory. But it had burned it *here*, in front of witnesses, in front of the storm-caller—and grief without memory was a wound, yes, but wounds could be turned to purposes. Somewhere in the drowning dark of the Archive, his daughter's memory of *him* still floated, whole and golden, untouched. The Veil had shown him exactly where it kept its treasures. And it had just taught him, in the cruelest lesson of his long half-life, that there were things worse than disobedience.

There was being its instrument while it did this.

*Rafael Amberdale,* Corvel thought, into the dark, knowing the storm-caller might hear, hoping he would. *When you breach the Archive—and you will, I've seen the prophecy's handwriting, I know how this ends—look for the ninth shelf in the flooded stacks. The one the others avoid.*

*What remains of me will meet you there.*

Above him, the Veil seethed through his veins like frost through mortar, sensing—dimly, for the first time in a century—that something inside its best servant had just changed allegiance.

And far off across the rain-lashed hills, thunder answered.
