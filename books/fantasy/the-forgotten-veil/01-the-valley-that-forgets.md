## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The beacon-stones woke before I did.

They always did — a faint hum through the floorboards of the keeper's cottage, like bees dreaming in a wall. Most people in the Veiled Valley never heard it. Most people slept straight through the hour before dawn while the stones drank the last of the night's dark and turned it into that soft amber glow we all took for granted, the way you take for granted air, or bread, or the fact that the mountains stay where you put them.

I lay in my narrow bed and listened to them hum, and I hated how much I loved it.

That was the thing about the valley nobody said out loud: it was beautiful the way a held breath is beautiful. Perfect. Suspended. Waiting for something to go wrong.

I dressed in the dark out of habit — no lamp needed when your blood remembered lightning — and pulled on the keeper's coat, too big for me still, sleeves rolled four times at the wrist. Old Marta had worn this coat for thirty years before her hands gave out and the council handed the beacon-farm to the exiled girl with the dangerous name. *Galloway*, they'd said, like it was a diagnosis. Like it was something you caught.

Outside, frost had come early again. Third time this month. The valley didn't have real seasons anymore — hadn't since before I was born — just a long mild nothing that the ward-smiths of the old days had stitched across the passes like a blanket. The Veil, they called it. Not the wards themselves but the whole arrangement: the valley wrapped in quiet, magic sliding off its skin like rain off oiled canvas. Nothing magical got in. Nothing magical got out.

Including me.

I walked the rows between the beacon-stones as the sky went from ash to pearl, checking each one the way Marta taught me. Palm flat against the stone's flank, count the pulse. One-two-three-four, steady as a sleeping heart. Good. Next row. One-two-three-four. Good. Forty-one stones in all, standing in their long furrows like a crop of grey teeth, each one drinking the valley's ambient stillness and breathing out enough warmth and light to keep the farms from freezing, the wells from icing, the children from ever learning what a thunderstorm was.

The children here thought storms were stories. I used to be one of those children. Then I turned nine, and the sky over the eastern ridge answered me back, and my mother sold my sister's soul to buy me a one-way exile to the only place in the world where I could never do it again.

One-two-three-four. Good stone.

I knelt by the last beacon in the north row, the old one, the crooked one, and pressed my palm to its flank, and that was when I felt it — not the hum, not the pulse, but underneath both, thin as wire drawn through wet cloth:

A wrongness. Out past the border. Out past the Veil.

It came to me the way weather comes to an old soldier's knee. My storm-sense had been dormant eleven years — starved, muzzled, buried under valley-quiet so thick I sometimes pretended it was dead. But it wasn't dead. It never died. It just waited, coiled somewhere behind my sternum, and every few months it twitched toward some pressure change no instrument in the valley could measure, and I would stand in a field feeling the shape of a storm two hundred miles away, and I would hate myself for the little thrill of it. The hunger. The way my whole body leaned toward the horizon like a dog hearing a whistle no one else could hear.

This wasn't a storm. Storms had texture — rotation, charge, the fat electric weight of them. This was thinner. Sharper. A scraping, like something being filed down on the far side of the world. Or on the near side of the border.

I pulled my hand off the stone fast, wiped it on my coat as if the sense were something sticky that might spread.

"Morning, Oscar."

Tam Vess stood at the gate of the beacon-farm with his milk cart, breath steaming, face doing what Tam's face always did — trying to look casual and landing somewhere nearer to wary. He was the closest thing I had to a friend, which mostly meant he talked at me while I worked and pretended not to notice that I flinched whenever anyone touched me.

"Morning."

"Heard frost on the high fields again. Council's saying it's nothing."

"The council says everything is nothing." I latched the gate behind me and took the jug he offered because refusing would start a conversation about why I refused. "Frost three times in one month isn't nothing. Frost isn't supposed to happen at all inside the Veil."

Tam shrugged the shrug of a man who had decided, decades ago, that the valley's peace was load-bearing and he wasn't going to poke it. "Elder Sarn says the stones are cycling. Normal fluctuation."

"Elder Sarn also says the war ended because everyone got tired," I said. "Elder Sarn says a lot of things."

"You could bring it up at assembly."

"I could." I wouldn't. The last time I'd stood up in the meetinghouse and said *something's wrong with the border stones, I can feel it* — without explaining how I could feel it, because explaining meant confessing what I was — Elder Sarn had looked at me with that soft, terrible patience and said, *Oscar, dear, the valley forgets so that you don't have to remember.* And the room had nodded. And I had learned my lesson: in the Veiled Valley, the safest thing to be was nobody, and the safest thing to know was nothing.

We walked down the lane together, Tam's cart wheels crunching frost that shouldn't exist. The village unrolled around us in its usual morning order: baker's chimney first, then the well-chain squealing, then old Fen's rooster, who crowed at exactly the wrong moment every day and had done so for so long that everyone had adjusted their lives around his error rather than fix him. It was that kind of place. Nothing broken enough to mend. Nothing sharp enough to cut.

Nothing alive enough to bleed, whispered the thing behind my sternum, and I told it to shut up.

* * *

Assembly was called for noon anyway, which surprised everyone, including, by the look of him, Elder Sarn.

I found out why at the gate.

There was a stranger at the gate.

You have to understand what that sentence means in the Veiled Valley. People came to the valley sometimes — refugees, wanderers, the occasional trader willing to cross the passes for our preserved fruit and our famous indifference. They came through the southern gate, they surrendered anything that hummed or glowed or whispered to the ward-wardens, and they settled or moved on. That was the ritual. That was the order of things.

But this man hadn't come through the southern gate. He was standing at the *northern* gate — the one that faced the mountain, the one that hadn't opened in living memory — and he was standing there openly, hands visible, travel-worn coat stiff with road dust, looking up at the gate-stones the way a surgeon looks at a wound.

And the ward-wardens were letting him.

I stopped at the edge of the square, market basket forgotten on my arm, and watched. The stranger was young-ish, maybe thirty, brown skin gone grey with dust, black hair cropped short and unevenly, like he'd cut it himself with a knife. There was a burn scar on his left hand, old and bad, pulling two fingers into a half-curl. He carried no pack. Just a leather satchel slung crosswise, and a pair of brass-rimmed lenses pushed up on his forehead that caught the noon light and threw small coins of it onto the gate-stone above him.

He was talking to Warden Cettis, and even from forty feet away I could hear the shape of his voice — low, clipped, a city accent ground smooth by years of somewhere else — and I could hear the words, because the square was very quiet, because everyone in the Veiled Valley had stopped pretending to be busy.

"—not asking to enter the sanctuary," the stranger was saying. "I'm asking about the border stones. The northern line specifically. When did you last inspect them?"

Cettis's jaw did the thing jaws do when a question has landed somewhere tender. "The stones are maintained according to the founding charter. Which is to say, stranger, that they are maintained, and that how is not a matter for outsiders."

"When," the stranger repeated, with the patience of someone who had asked the question many times in many places and been lied to in most of them, "did you last *inspect* them? Not maintain. Inspect. Walk the line. Put your hand on the stone and read it."

Something cold walked down my spine wearing my sister's footsteps.

Because I knew the answer. Everyone who tended beacons knew the answer, though none of us said it: the border stones weren't inspected. They were *trusted*. The charter said the Veil was permanent, self-sustaining, woven by the great ward-smiths of the founding generation into a pattern that needed no tending, and the valley had believed it the way you believe in the floor beneath your feet. You didn't inspect the floor. You just walked.

But I had put my hand on a beacon-stone that morning and felt a scraping on the far side of the border, and here was a dust-covered stranger asking, in front of the entire assembled village, the exact question I had been forbidden to ask.

Elder Sarn came forward through the crowd, white-bearded, unhurried, radiating the particular calm of a man whose authority has never once been genuinely challenged. "Friend, you've walked a long way to ask strange questions. The Veiled Valley keeps its own counsel. If you're seeking refuge, the southern gate—"

"I'm not seeking refuge." The stranger turned, and his eyes swept the crowd — quick, professional, cataloguing — and for half a heartbeat they snagged on mine, and something flickered in his face. Recognition? No. Worse. *Confirmation.* Like he'd found the thing he'd been told to look for and was disappointed by how easy it was. "I'm seeking the truth about these wards, and I'm running out of places to look for it. So I'll ask plainly, and then I'll leave you to your forgetting." He raised his voice slightly, not shouting — projecting, the way you learn to in big rooms. "In the last month, has anyone noticed frost where frost shouldn't fall? Lights guttering? Animals restless? Dreams of cold?"

The silence that followed had a texture. I felt it press against my skin.

Because yes. Yes to all of it. The frost on the high fields. The beacon-hum gone thin some nights, like a singer reaching for a note. Old Fen's dog howling at the northern ridge for three nights running until Fen tied him up. My own storm-sense twitching toward the border like a compass needle with a grudge.

Nobody said any of it. That was the covenant of the valley, unwritten and absolute: we traded memory for safety, questions for quiet, and the trade had held for sixty years, and no dusty stranger with brass lenses was going to make us default on it now.

Elder Sarn smiled his gentle smile. "No, friend. Nothing of the kind. The valley is at peace."

The stranger looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked past him — at the crowd, at the faces studiously arranged into blankness — and I watched him understand, all at once, exactly what kind of place this was. His expression didn't change, but something behind it closed, like a door.

"Of course it is," he said quietly. "Forgive me. I'll trouble you no further."

He shouldered his satchel and turned south, toward the southern gate, toward leaving, and the crowd began — visibly, audibly — to exhale. Cettis started issuing instructions about reopening the market stalls. Sarn murmured something soothing to the widow Hask. The machinery of normalcy ground back into motion, swallowing the interruption whole.

And I stood there with my market basket and my hidden storm-sense and my eleven years of practiced silence, and I made the second-worst decision of my life.

I followed him.

* * *

I caught up at the bend in the south road, where the valley's fields give out and the pines take over and you can see, on a clear day, the pass beyond the border stones — except you can't, actually, because the Veil blurs everything past the boundary into a soft grey smudge, like breath on glass. He heard my boots and turned with his hand already inside his satchel, and then saw what I was — a girl of twenty, keeper's coat, basket of turnips — and the hand came back out empty.

"You forgot your turnip money," I said, because apparently my mouth had chosen cowardice.

"I didn't sell any turnips."

"No. You didn't." I set the basket down in the frost-killed grass. My heart was going hard, harder than it had any right to, and behind my sternum the dormant thing had lifted its head and was listening. "Frost on the high fields, three times this month. Beacon-hum gone thin on the nights the wind comes out of the north. Fen's dog. My—" I stopped. Swallowed the confession whole. "Yes. All of it. Everything you asked about."

He studied me for a long moment. Up close his eyes were a strange pale hazel, almost yellow, and tired in a way that sleep doesn't fix.

"They'll say you're lying," he said. "Or mad. Or both. They said the same in the last six valleys I've visited."

"I know what they'll say."

"Do you?" He stepped closer, and lowered his voice, and the tiredness in his face burned off all at once, replaced by something harder and much older. "Then let me tell you what I've seen, girl, and you can decide whether they'll call you mad for believing it. Six valleys. In each one, the same story: the border wards are failing. Not aging — *failing*. Scorched from the outside. Cut, deliberately, precisely, by someone who knows the founding patterns better than the founding smiths did. Someone is unwinding the Veils of the world one valley at a time, and every valley I've reached has already forgotten how to be afraid." His jaw tightened. "Yours is the seventh. And yours is the only one where the wards were built *right* — which means whoever cuts them will have to work at it. Weeks, maybe. Maybe less."

The cold walked down my spine again, wearing her footsteps, and this time it brought the memory with it: my palm on the beacon-stone at dawn, the thin wire of wrongness, the scraping, filing sound of something being undone.

"The northern stones," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved. "They run along the old quarry road. Nobody goes there. Nobody's gone there in years."

"I know. I checked the approach last night." He paused, and I watched him decide something — weigh me, measure me, find me heavier than I looked. "The scorch marks start forty paces east of the third marker. From the outside. Whatever your elders believe, whatever your charter promises — your valley's walls are being eaten, and the eating has already begun."

Behind my sternum, the dormant storm turned over in its sleep and, for the first time in eleven years, growled.

"What's your name?" I asked.

"Sefa Anwar." He said it like a man handing over a coin he couldn't afford. "Ward-smith. Formerly."

"Lior— " No. I caught it. I always caught it. "Oscar. Oscar Galloway."

Something moved behind his eyes at the name — quick, quickly buried. *Everyone* reacted to the name Galloway; it was the price of it. But his reaction was different. Not fear. Not recognition of a scandal. Something more like grief, arriving ahead of schedule.

"Galloway," he repeated softly. "Well. Then you of all people should understand what I'm about to say." He glanced back up the road, toward the village, toward the meetinghouse where Elder Sarn was even now smoothing the morning back into place. "Your valley is going to vote to ignore me. They're going to vote for peace and quiet and the charter, because that's what valleys do, right up until the walls come down. And when the walls come down—" He stopped himself. Shook his head. "Go home, Oscar Galloway. Forget you spoke to me. It's kinder."

He picked up his satchel and walked south, into the pines, and the grey blur of the Veil swallowed the road behind him.

I stood in the frost-killed grass for a long time, holding a basket of turnips, listening to the storm behind my ribs.

Then I turned north — not toward the village, not toward the farm, but toward the old quarry road, toward the border stones, toward the third marker and the forty paces east of it.

Kinder, he'd said.

I'd been kind for eleven years. I was done.
