## 2. Wards Like Old Bones

The elder's house smelled of dried yarrow and old decisions.

Oscar stood in the doorway of Maren Ostrova's kitchen with her cap in her hands and the words she'd rehearsed all morning dying one by one on her tongue. Elder Ostrova sat at her table the way she sat everywhere — like a stone that had decided to be a woman and never quite committed to it. Her tea had gone cold. It always went cold. She drank it anyway, because waste was a sin in the Veiled Valley, along with curiosity, ambition, and weather-talk after sundown.

"You're certain," Ostrova said. Not a question. A verdict waiting for its paperwork.

"The border stone past the mill," Oscar said. "The one by the birch stand. I walk that line every morning checking the beacons, and yesterday the frost wouldn't take on the north face of it. The ground around it's warm. In February." She made herself hold the old woman's eyes. "The wards are failing."

For a long moment there was only the tick of the stove cooling and somewhere outside the flat, patient sound of snow sliding off a roof.

"Ground warms near stones all the time," Ostrova said. "Roots rot. Rabbits burrow. The world does things, Galloway, without asking our permission first."

"Not like this. The beacon-farm's been drawing thin for three weeks. You've seen the harvest tallies. The light's down a fifth since midwinter, and we haven't changed anything—"

"We changed plenty." Ostrova set down her cup with a click that ended conversations. "We changed the rotation. We changed the compost. We changed the girl who runs the farm, if you'll recall, after the last one burned out." Her eyes did not soften when they landed on Oscar; they never softened anywhere. "You want me to send men to the border to poke at rocks in the dead of winter because the dirt feels warm to you."

"I want you to send someone who can *read* them. There hasn't been a proper ward-inspection in eleven years. Maybe longer."

"And there won't be." Ostrova rose, joints cracking like green wood in a fire, and began gathering her ledger from the shelf as though Oscar were already gone. "This valley survives because it doesn't go looking for trouble at its edges. The wards were laid before any of us were born. They held through the Sundering, through the Gray Years, through your arrival, God forgive us all. They will hold through a warm patch of ground."

"They're *weakening*—"

"They are *old*," Ostrova said, turning, and for one unguarded instant something moved behind her face that was not dismissal at all. Something tired. Something that looked almost like fear wearing an elder's shawl. "Everything here is old, child. Old wards, old bones, old promises. We do not disturb what is old, because the last time this valley disturbed anything, it took forty years to stop screaming." She opened the door. Cold air poured in, smelling of snow and woodsmoke and nothing else, the way everything smelled of nothing else here. "Check your beacons. Feed your lights. And leave the border stones alone."

The door closed. Oscar stood on the step a while, cap crushed in her fist, watching her breath hang in the air like a small ghost that couldn't find its way onward.

*Old bones,* she thought. *That's exactly what I'm afraid of.*

* * *

She waited three days, which she counted as a kind of heroism.

Three days of doing everything right. Tending the beacon-farm rows where the glass spires drank their thin ration of valley-light and gave back the pale glow that kept the fields from freezing. Eating supper at the common hall and laughing at Hesta Brandt's story about the goat in the chapel loft. Saying goodnight to people whose names she knew by heart and whose histories she knew not at all, because no one in the Veiled Valley talked about before, and after twenty years Oscar had learned that asking was the fastest way to become a stranger again.

Three days of feeling it, though. That was the thing nobody else seemed able to feel — or willing to admit feeling. Under her sternum, in the place where her storm-sense lived coiled and dormant like a snake someone had charmed into stillness, there was a pressure. A wrongness in the air the way there's a wrongness in a room just before a shelf gives way. The valley's magicless quiet had always pressed against her like cotton wool. Lately it pressed like a hand.

On the third night, the pressure spoke her name.

Not aloud. Nothing so dramatic. But she woke at moonset with her heart slamming and the taste of ozone on her tongue — real ozone, or memory of it, the smell of sky about to break — and she lay in her narrow bed listening to the silence and understood that she was done waiting for permission.

She dressed in the dark. Wool layers, two pairs of socks, her father's old oilskin coat that she'd brought to the valley folded in a child's bundle twenty years ago and never had reason to wear until now. She took the lantern but didn't light it. She took the iron pry-bar from the tool shed, which was not theft because she fully intended to return it, and told herself the lie comfortably.

The valley slept the way it always slept: completely, obediently, like children after a long day of being told what to think. Snow squeaked under her boots. The beacon-farm glowed faintly behind her, rows of dimming glass, and she did not look back at it because looking back was how you talked yourself out of things.

The border road ran northwest along the stream, past the mill with its frozen wheel, past the last field, into the birch stand where the trees grew white and close as ribs. The wards ran along the valley's rim like a fence made of nothing — you couldn't see them, most days you couldn't feel them, but every child in the valley learned the line of stones the way other children learned prayers. Here a mossy granite knee. There a standing slab taller than a man. Forty-one of them, the oldest said, though no one had ever bothered to count twice.

Oscar counted.

The first twelve were fine. Sleeping stones, cold and indifferent, wearing their snow caps like old men in nightshirts. The thirteenth made her stop.

No snow on its north face.

She crouched, held her bare palm above the granite, and felt it — not warmth exactly, but the absence of cold, a wrongness like a word missing from a sentence. The stone should have been radiating the deep mineral chill of February. Instead it breathed. Slowly. Faintly. Like something sleeping too hard.

She moved the lantern closer — then stopped herself, and struck no light, and simply put her hand flat against the stone.

And felt it hum.

Barely. A vibration below hearing, below feeling, rising up through her palm into the marrow of her arm and settling somewhere behind her breastbone where her storm-sense stirred and turned over like a dog dreaming. The ward-stone was working. That was the horror of it. It was working *hard*, straining at something, a plow-horse leaning into a load far heavier than any plow.

She followed the line. Fourteen. Fifteen. Sixteen, tilted, its base cracked in a way that might have been frost-heave except that frost-heave cracks outward and this crack ran *inward*, toward the valley, as if something outside had pushed. Seventeen —

Seventeen was scorched.

She knelt in the snow and stared and her mind refused it the way a hand refuses a hot pan. The north face of the stone — the face turned away from the valley, turned toward the empty white world beyond the veil — was blackened. Not burned the way fire burns wood, mottled and uneven. Scorched the way lightning scars a tree: one clean, branching, deliberate mark, silver-black, running from the stone's crown to its root. And around the mark, in a ring, the granite had gone smooth. Polished. Melted and refrozen like candle wax.

Nothing in the valley could have done this. Nothing in the valley had enough magic left to light a candle, let alone scar a ward-stone laid before living memory.

Which meant it had come from outside.

From the world she'd been told, her whole exile-long life, could not touch her.

Oscar sat back on her heels in the snow and found that her breath had gone shallow and fast and that her hands, bare in the killing cold, had gone strangely calm. That was the old shame waking, she knew. The thing she'd spent twenty years starving. Somewhere beneath her fear, some animal part of her was *interested*. Was saying: *yes, this, finally, something true.*

"Third one from the birch stand," said a voice behind her. "There's another past the stream bend, worse. And the anchor-stone at the ridge has been hollowed clean through."

Oscar came off her knees and spun, pry-bar up, lantern swinging — and the stranger stepped out of the dark between the birches as if he'd been part of the night all along.

He was older than she'd guessed at the gate, fifty perhaps, with a grey-shot beard trimmed short and deep lines around eyes that had the particular flatness of men who had seen too much to perform surprise. He wore a traveler's coat stitched with pockets — dozens of them, bulging with tools and vials and things that clinked — and across his back rode a leather roll the length of a forearm that she would learn, later, held his chisels. His breath smoked. His boots were crusted to the knee with other valleys' snow.

"You," she said. "The gate-watcher said you'd gone south."

"I went south. Then I came back, because going south was stupid." He nodded at the scorched stone, unhurried, unbothered by the pry-bar she hadn't lowered. "You found it yourself?"

"I check the beacons. I walk the line." She heard how defensive it sounded and hated it. "Who are you? Really. Ward-smiths don't wander into the Veiled Valley by accident. Nobody wanders in by accident. The veil doesn't allow it."

"No," he agreed. "It doesn't. Which is why my being here should worry you more than those stones do." He crouched beside the scorch-mark, unhurried, and drew from one of his impossible pockets a lens of smoky glass on a wire frame. He held it to the mark, angled it toward the moon, and whatever he saw made his mouth go thin. "Sefa Anwar," he said, without looking up. "Journeyman of the Wardwrights' Guild, formerly. Formerly of several things." He glanced up at her then, and his eyes were the color of wet slate. "You're the Galloway girl. The one they keep."

"The one they *keep*," Oscar repeated. "That's one way to say exiled."

"That's one way to say protected. Whether it worked is another question entirely." He rose, pocketing the lens, and looked down the ward-line into the dark where the stones ran on, patient and wounded, toward the ridge. "Twenty years ago these wards were the finest work in the northern reaches. Whoever laid them wove the veil into the bedrock itself — no seam, no hinge, no door. Nothing got in. Nothing got out. Nothing *could*." He paused, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped into something quieter and much worse. "Someone has cut hinges into them anyway. From the outside. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Oscar's grip tightened on the pry-bar until the cold iron burned. "Tell me plainly."

"These weren't worn down. They weren't neglected. They weren't failed." Sefa Anwar turned to face her fully, and in the blue dark his breath rose between them like smoke from a doused fire. "They were sabotaged. Deliberately. Skillfully. By someone who knew exactly how they were built — which is a very short list of people, girl, and I am on it." He looked back at the blackened stone, and for a moment the flatness cracked and she saw underneath it something raw and old and grim. "Whatever is coming through those hinges isn't coming by chance. It was invited. It was *engineered*. And it isn't finished."

The wind moved through the birch stand, and every white trunk creaked at once, and far off — so far off it might have been imagination, might have been the dormant snake in her chest turning over again — Oscar felt the pressure behind her sternum swell by a single degree.

"How long?" she asked. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved.

Sefa Anwar considered her. Considered the pry-bar. Considered, perhaps, the strange calm of a woman who had grown up inside a cage and never once been told why.

"If the elder sends men tomorrow? Perhaps a season. If she does what elders do—" he shrugged, and the shrug contained whole libraries of disappointment, "—then less."

"Then we don't have time for her." Oscar slung the lantern onto her hip and turned down the ward-line, toward the stream bend, toward the fourth scorch-mark and the hollowed anchor-stone beyond it, toward the thing coiling tighter and tighter beneath her ribs that was no longer shame at all. "Show me the rest."

Behind her, in the dark, the ward-smith made a sound that might have been a laugh, if laughter could be worried.

And above the veil, above the valley, above the whole sleeping world — the stars began, very quietly, to go out.
