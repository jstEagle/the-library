## 3. The Night Without Stars

Oscar had learned to read the valley's silences the way other people read weather.

There was the silence of morning, when mist sat heavy on the beacon-farm and the glass globes along their iron cradles caught no light at all, because there was never any light to catch. There was the silence of the market square, where the elders spoke in careful, rounded sentences that had been sanded smooth of anything dangerous. And there was the silence of evening, when every door in Hollowmere closed at the same hour as if the whole town were one animal settling into sleep — a silence Oscar had spent eleven years pretending was peace.

Tonight the silence was wrong.

She stood in the doorway of the beacon-house with her hands wrapped around a cup of barley tea gone cold, watching the sky. The first stars had come out early, sharp and close over the eastern ridge, and she had counted them without meaning to. Counting was an old habit. Her sister had taught her the constellations before either of them could write, back when Wren's hair smelled of woodsmoke and their mother still sang in the kitchen, back before the word *sold* had entered their lives like a stone dropped into a well.

*That one is the Lantern-Bearer,* Wren had said, small fingers tracing lines between the stars. *And those three are the Sisters Walking. They're always looking for each other.*

*I'm looking right at you,* Oscar had said, six years old and certain of everything.

The memory arrived uninvited, the way it always did on clear nights, and Oscar drank her cold tea and let it pass through her like weather. That was the discipline of the valley: feel it, name it, let it go. Grief was just another kind of storm, and storms did not reach here. Nothing reached here.

Except tonight, something was reaching.

It started as a pressure behind her eyes — the old dormant storm-sense, the thing she kept folded up and buried like contraband, stirring in its sleep. She hadn't felt it in years. In the Veiled Valley it simply didn't function; the wards dampened everything, magic and memory and grief alike, which was precisely why her mother had chosen this place for her exile. But now the sense flickered awake like a limb remembering itself, and what it told her made her set down the cup with a click too loud in the empty room.

The air tasted of scorched iron.

Not from her hearth. Not from the forge, which had banked its fires hours ago. It was coming from outside — from beyond the ridge, beyond the fields, from the direction of the border stones Sefa Anwar had stood beside two nights ago with his ruined hands and his terrible certainty: *They didn't fail. Someone opened them from the outside, and someone let them be opened from within.*

Oscar pulled on her coat and went out into the dark.

The beacon-farm stretched below her in rows, forty glass globes on their iron cradles, each one meant to hold a captured fragment of storm-light that would never come. The valley had built them generations ago, back when it still believed the veil might lift. Now they were ornaments. Now they were how Oscar paid for her bread — tending lights that lit nothing, polishing glass that would never shine.

She walked the rows because walking was better than standing still, and because the pressure behind her eyes was building into something with edges. Above her, the stars burned on, indifferent. The Lantern-Bearer. The Sisters Walking.

She was halfway down the third row when the nearest beacon-globe hummed.

Oscar stopped. Globes did not hum. Glass held nothing, resonated with nothing, in a valley where resonance itself had been wardsmithied out of the world. But the hum was real — low, rising, a note she felt in her teeth — and as she watched, frost bloomed across the globe's surface in feathering whorls, spreading from the crown of the glass downward like breath on a winter window.

In autumn. In a valley that had not seen true frost in living memory.

"No," she said aloud, to the globe, to the night, to no one. "No, no—"

The hum spread. Globe by globe, down the rows and across the farm and out toward the town, she heard it take hold — dozens of voices, then hundreds, a chorus of glass singing a single terrified note. Frost raced across the fields. Her breath came out white. And overhead—

Overhead, the stars began to go out.

Not fading. Not clouding over. Going out — snuffed, all of them, in no order she could find, east and west and zenith, as if a vast hand were closing them one by one like lamps in a house where everyone had gone to bed. The Lantern-Bearer vanished. The Sisters Walking vanished, mid-stride, forever looking for each other.

Oscar stood in the middle of her dead beacon-farm under a sky being erased, and the last star — the one low on the southern horizon that Wren had called the Keeper, the one that watched over travelers and lost things — held for a heartbeat longer than all the rest.

Then it, too, went dark.

The world became a blackness so complete it had texture. Oscar heard herself breathing. Heard the frost crackling as it climbed the beacon-cradles. Heard, distantly, the first screams from Hollowmere — doors banging open, dogs howling, the elder Tam Voss's voice cracking across the square calling for lanterns, for calm, for order, for all the small rituals people cling to when the actual world comes apart.

And beneath it all, beneath the screaming and the glass-song and the groan of iron cradles contracting in sudden cold, Oscar felt the wards break.

She had never felt them before — that was the point of them, that was the mercy of this place — but she felt them go, felt it in her sternum like a door slamming open inside her own chest. Eleven years of silence ended in a single instant, and everything the valley had held back came pouring through the breach: the wind off the high passes, the taste of distant rain, the vast slow pressure of weather systems wheeling over mountains she'd forgotten existed. Her storm-sense, unburied, unfettered, drank it all in and screamed.

She fell to her knees between the beacon rows. Cold poured over the valley like water finding its level — not wind, not weather, but cold itself, moving with intent, sliding down from the broken border stones and filling the hollows, the cellars, the spaces between heartbeats. Where it touched the ground, the earth turned grey and rang faintly, like porcelain.

Get up. *Get up.* She got up.

The town needed — she didn't know what the town needed. Lanterns. Doors barred. The children from the low houses brought up to the meeting hall. She knew these procedures; they drilled them twice a year against floods and rockslides, though no drill had ever imagined this. She was running for the path when the voice came.

It came through the dark the way sound carries over frozen lakes — impossibly far and impossibly close at once, arriving inside her ears rather than through them.

*"Oscar."*

She stopped so hard she nearly went down again.

She knew that voice. She had known it before she knew her own name. It was thinner than she remembered, frayed at the edges like cloth worn through, but it was *Wren's* voice, unmistakably, impossibly, her sister's voice saying her sister's-name-for-her, the shortened one, the private one, the one no one else alive had ever used.

*"Osca. Can you hear me? Osca, it's so cold here—"*

"Wren?" Her voice came out as a child's voice, small and cracked. "Wren, where are you?"

The darkness offered nothing back. The frost crept up her boots. Somewhere behind her the beacon-globes sang their dying note, and somewhere ahead the town blundered through its blackout, and Oscar stood alone on the frozen field with her heart tearing cleanly in half — because the voice was impossible, because the voice was a cruelty, because the voice was *real*, she would have staked her soul on it being real, and because if it was real then everything else was true too: the sabotage, the scorch-marks on the outer stones, the Hollow Court, the deal her mother had made eleven years ago in a room that smelled of ink and lilies.

*Sold.* Her sister's soul, sold to buy a child's safety. And now the buyer had come to collect, or to gloat, or simply to let the cold do its work while a dead girl's voice called her name across the ruins of the only safe place left in the world.

*"Run, Osca,"* the voice said, and now it sounded frightened, and that was worst of all, because Wren had never once sounded frightened, not even at the end. *"Don't listen to it. Don't follow the cold. Run—"*

The voice cut off like a snuffed candle.

Oscar ran — not away, though some sane part of her screamed to run away, but toward the voice, toward the northern fields, toward the direction the cold poured from, stumbling between the beacon rows with tears freezing on her face and her dead storm-sense roaring in her ears like a sea let out of a bottle.

Behind her, the first beacon-globe shattered.

* * *

Sefa Anwar had spent thirty years learning to fear specific sounds, and he had a taxonomy of them.

There was the sound of a ward failing honestly — a sigh, a slump, the tired exhale of old craft giving out. He'd heard it a hundred times on the borders he'd built and the borders he'd abandoned. There was the sound of a ward breaking violently, which was less a sound than a pressure, a flat concussion you felt in the fluid of your spine. And there was the sound he heard now, standing in the doorway of the waystation inn as the sky above Hollowmere emptied itself of stars: a long, rising harmonic, glass and stone and earth singing together, that he had heard exactly once before in his life.

The night the Hollow Court took the Sunspire. The night he ran, and thirty thousand people did not.

"Everyone inside!" he shouted, though the inn's common room was already full of panicking villagers and the words were wasted breath. "Away from the windows! Get to the interior walls!"

Nobody listened. Nobody ever listened. A woman clutched a clay lamp whose flame had gone strange and blue. Two farmers argued about whether the stars had fallen or been hidden, as if the distinction mattered. Old Tam Voss stood in the middle of the square in his nightclothes bellowing for calm with the desperate authority of a man who has led a town for forty years and understands, tonight, that leadership is a costume.

Sefa pushed through them into the street and looked north.

He couldn't see the border stones from here, but he could see what they were doing — or rather, what was being done through them. The cold came down the valley in a visible tide, a grey translucence rolling over the fields like floodwater, and where it passed, the world changed register. Grass stiffened. Fence posts whitened. The very dark seemed to thicken, as if the absence of stars had weight and the weight was settling.

*Sabotage,* he thought, with the cold clarity that had always been his gift and his curse. *Full breach. They've been working the flaw since summer — maybe longer. Since I built it.*

Because he had built it. That was the truth he carried like a swallowed coal: the Veiled Valley's wards were his work, laid down twenty-two years ago for a fee he'd never spent and a guilt he'd never put down, and he had built them strong enough to keep a storm-child safe and flawed enough — one flaw, one hairline seam in the northern anchor, invisible, deniable, *survivable*, he'd told himself — so that the Court would believe him loyal and let him walk away. He had told himself the flaw would never matter. He had told himself a great many things.

Now the flaw was a wound, and the wound was a door, and through the door came winter with a purpose.

"The girl," he said aloud, and started running.

He found her at the edge of the beacon-farm, or rather he found the aftermath of her: a line of shattered globes glittering like spilled ice, frost thick as fur on the iron cradles, and Oscar Galloway on her knees in the middle of it with her head thrown back and her arms slightly away from her body, and around her the air *moving*. Not wind. Movement without wind, pressure without source, the raw grammar of weather trying to assemble itself around a woman who had spent eleven years learning to be ordinary.

Sefa stopped. Every instinct said approach slowly. Every instinct also said *run*, and he was too old and too guilty to run anymore.

"Galloway." His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "Oscar. Look at me."

Her head turned. Her eyes were wrong — not glowing, nothing so mercifully theatrical as glowing, but *lit from behind*, pupils blown wide, and in their depths flickered something he recognized from mirrors in his worst years: the reflection of a sky full of storms that hadn't happened yet.

"It called me," she said. Her voice was scraped raw. "It used her voice. Sefa, it used *Wren's voice*, it said my name, it said—"

"I know."

"You don't know. You can't—" She surged upright, and the pressure around her spiked; the nearest intact globe cracked from crown to base with a sound like a knuckle popping. "It's her. It's *her*, she's alive, she's in there, they have her and she's *awake*—"

"Or it wants you to believe she is." He said it gently, and hated himself for it, and said it anyway, because it was true and because lies had gotten thirty thousand people killed once already. "The Court doesn't need her soul to wear her voice. A soul-anchor holds an echo. What you heard may be her. It may be bait. Tonight, in this, they are the same thing."

Oscar stared at him. Snow had begun to fall — real snow, dry and fine, falling upward in the eddies around her body before surrendering and drifting down. Behind her eyes, the storm-light guttered, fought, held.

"Then I'll go to it either way," she said. "If it's her, I'm bringing her home. If it's bait—" Her jaw tightened, and for a moment she looked so much like the stories he'd heard of her mother that Sefa's chest ached. "Then I'll tear the hook out of the water with my hands."

The pressure around her collapsed, suddenly, like a held breath released. She swayed. He caught her arm — her sleeve frosted instantly under his palm, then thawed — and together they stood in the ruin of the beacon-farm while Hollowmere screamed softly behind them and the snow came down thicker, straighter, colder than any natural snowfall, falling in perfect parallel lines as if measured.

Above the northern ridge, where the border stones stood, a light had appeared. Not warm. Not bright. A grey luminescence, pillar-shaped, patient, like a door held open by something that had all the time in the world.

And out of the dark between the town and that light, thin and carrying and horribly sweet, the voice came again — not to Sefa, he understood that immediately, it slid past him like smoke past a window, it was not for him and never had been:

*"Osca. You came. You always come. Come north, little storm. Come and see what Mother bought."*

Oscar made a sound Sefa had only heard once before, from his own throat, on the night the Sunspire fell — grief and terror fused into a single note, the sound a person makes when two unbearable truths arrive holding hands.

The grey light pulsed, once, like a heartbeat.

And every remaining beacon-globe in the valley shattered at once, forty voices of glass crying out together, and in the ringing silence after, Sefa Anwar took Oscar's frozen hand and said the only true thing left to say:

"We go north. Tonight. Before the cold finishes the job — and before whatever called you decides to stop asking."
