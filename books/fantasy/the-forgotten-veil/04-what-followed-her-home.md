## 4. What Followed Her Home

The beacon-house had not been built for refugees.

Sefa Anwar stood in the doorway and watched the valley's people crowd into a structure meant to hold maybe thirty souls, holding now closer to ninety. They sat on the floor among the dormant beacon-stones, wrapped in every blanket the farm could spare, breathing steam into the dark. Someone had lit the great hearth, but the fire burned strange tonight — low, blue at its edges, as though it too were afraid of what waited outside.

He counted them the way he'd once counted wards: methodically, without mercy. Ninety-one. Three missing. The Hollis brothers, who had gone out to check their goats when the sky went black. And a girl named Tam Vey, twelve years old, whose mother kept standing at the door as if her body might go fetch the child back by sheer refusal to sit down.

Outside, the snow fell upward.

That was the detail Sefa couldn't stop looking at. Through the doorway's narrow frame he could see the flakes rising from the ground into a sky with no stars in it, no moon, nothing — a ceiling of absolute black, like ink poured over glass. Snow did not fall upward. Not anywhere. Not in any weather he had ever catalogued across forty years of studying how the world held itself together.

Except under the influence of Hollow work.

He pressed his back against the doorframe and made himself breathe. *You knew this was coming,* he told himself. *You've known for eleven years. You built the flaw yourself.*

*You just never believed they'd come collect.*

"Master Anwar."

Elder Corrin Ash stood beside him, a small woman gone gray and stooped, her hands folded in that way she had — the way of someone who believed that stillness was authority. She had refused him three times today. Refused to send runners to inspect the border stones. Refused to wake the valley's one remaining warden, old Brammel, who slept through everything these days. Refused, most of all, to say aloud the word everyone in this room was thinking.

*Wards.* The word was *wards.*

"Elder," Sefa said.

"You've been staring at that snow for an hour."

"I've been staring at worse things for longer." He turned to face her fully. In the hearthlight his reflection ghosted across the window glass behind her — a lean man of fifty-odd, frost-gray beard trimmed short, hands scarred in patterns that any ward-smith would recognize as burn-scars from a forge that ran on something hotter than coal. "Corrin. I need you to listen to me now, and I need you to do it quickly, because whatever is happening at your border is not going to wait for your council to finish being careful."

Her chin lifted. He watched her decide to be offended, watched the offense falter, watched fear win. That was the order of things in the Veiled Valley, he was learning. Everything here moved slowly except terror.

"The stranger asks for trust on his first night," she said quietly.

"The stranger has been asking since the gate. You gave me soup instead of answers. I ate the soup. Now give me the answers' chance to matter." He kept his voice level. He had learned, in another life, in another court, that panic was contagious and calm was too, and that the smith who stayed level while the metal screamed was the smith who lived. "Your border stones are scorched. I saw the marks myself before full dark, on the western face of the third stone from the pass. Do you know what scorched wards mean?"

"They mean—"

"They mean someone stood outside your valley and *unmade* them. Deliberately. With skill. This was not decay, Elder. Decay leaves rot-patterns, soft edges, slow failure over years. What I saw had edges like a knife cut. Someone carved your safety open the way you'd gut a fish, and they did it from the outside because they wanted you to feel safe right up until the moment you didn't."

Corrin's folded hands had gone white at the knuckles. "Who," she said. Not *how*. Not *why*. Just *who* — and beneath it, the question she couldn't bring herself to shape: *who would bother?* Because that was the poison of the Veiled Valley, Sefa thought. Its people had been taught for generations that they were too small, too magicless, too forgotten to be worth harming. Safety had curdled into insignificance, and insignificance into faith.

"Hollow Court," he said.

The word dropped through the room like a stone through still water. He felt heads turn along the benches. A baby started crying somewhere near the hearth and was hushed with desperate tenderness.

"That name is a story," Corrin said. "A story grandmothers tell."

"Yes. So is 'the long winter.' Stories are usually memories wearing coats." He glanced past her, into the room, and found the face he was looking for.

Oscar Galloway sat against the far wall with her knees drawn up, a blanket around her shoulders that she wasn't really wearing so much as hiding inside. Her sister's shawl — he'd noticed it at the gate, noticed again now, the faded embroidery of swallows along its hem. She was watching him with an expression he recognized from mirrors: the look of someone doing arithmetic with grief, trying to make the numbers come out to something survivable.

She had heard something, last night, when the stars went out. He'd seen her face when the screaming started — not the crowd's screaming, the other thing, the voice that only she seemed to hear. He had spent one night in this valley and already understood more about Oscar Galloway than she likely understood about herself. Some of it, he suspected, she'd understand better if someone finally told her the truth about the wards.

About *his* wards.

*Not yet,* he cautioned himself. *One confession at a time. Confessions are like stitches — pull them all at once and the wound opens wider than before.*

*But you will tell her. Today. Before you lead her anywhere.*

Because that was the other thing he'd realized, standing in the snow that fell upward: he was going to have to lead her somewhere. The voice she'd heard meant the Court hadn't merely passed through. It meant an anchor. And anchors could be found.

* * *

They put the children in the inner room, which was really just the seed cellar with its racks cleared, and the adults arranged themselves around the hearth in the loose defensive geometry of people who had never defended anything. Sefa stood where he could see the door. Old habits. Old, old habits.

"We should go south," Corrin was saying. "Tonight, if we can manage it. Take the southern track to Lowmark—"

"Lowmark is four days' walk," said Doss Merrick, the miller, a big man whose calm was the loud kind. "With children. Without supplies. Through open country, in the dark, in winter that came in a single night. We'd lose half of them on the road."

"And we'll lose all of them here!"

"Will we?" It was Oscar who spoke. Her voice came out rough, unused, like a hinge that needed oiling. She hadn't spoken much since the stars died. "What happened at the border — does it follow us? Or did it want the valley specifically?"

Everyone looked at Sefa. He let the silence sit for exactly long enough, then stepped into the firelight.

"It wanted the valley," he said. "Or rather — it wanted what the valley was hiding. Which, I suspect, none of you know you were hiding."

Murmurs. Corrin's eyes narrowed. "Speak plainly, Anwar."

So he did. He laid it out the way he'd once laid out designs for clients who paid in coin and souls alike: the Veiled Valley was not naturally veiled. It had been warded — warded comprehensively, warded by a master, warded with a signature so deep in the stone that three generations had grown up believing the quiet was geography rather than craft. The valley suppressed magic. All magic. Every gift born inside its borders went dormant, and every gift carried in went silent at the treeline.

"That's why we're safe," someone said. "Nothing magical can reach us here."

"No," Sefa said. "That's why you're *invisible*. There's a difference, and it matters tonight. A veil doesn't stop a blade. It stops the world from noticing there's anything here worth a blade. Your valley is a smothered candle in a very large dark house. And last night, someone walked through the house and found the candle anyway — because they didn't need to see it. They built it."

The murmuring stopped. In the new silence, Oscar Galloway uncurled from the wall and stood, slowly, the shawl sliding off one shoulder.

"What do you mean, *built*?"

Here it was, then. Earlier than he'd planned. Sefa looked at her — at the storm-gray eyes, at the restless coiled quality of her stillness, at the way the firelight seemed to bend very slightly toward her the way iron filings bend toward a lodestone — and understood that he had run out of partial truths.

"I mean," he said, "that I know the design of your wards because I designed them. Eleven years ago. And I know they failed last night because I built the failure into them."

* * *

The uproar lasted four minutes. He counted that too. Four minutes of Doss Merrick shouting, of Corrin demanding he be put out into the snow, of a woman near the hearth saying *I knew it, I knew we shouldn't have fed him* — and then, cutting through it all, Oscar's voice, flat and cold as the new frost:

"*Why.*"

And the room, strangely, obeyed her. Quiet fell the way it falls before lightning strikes.

Sefa told them. He told it plainly, because he had rehearsed plainness for eleven years and had never once found an audience until now. He had been a ward-smith of the Hollow Court — not a herald, not a knight of the frozen ranks, but a craftsman, which was somehow worse. The Court did not conquer. It *contracted*. It traded in seasons and souls, and its genius was that it almost never had to take anything. People sold. Villages sold. Kingdoms sold, in the end, one winter at a time. And every contract needed sealing, and every seal needed a smith.

"I was the best they had," he said. "I tell you that not as boasting but as warning. Everything I build holds. Including the flaw." He spread his scarred hands. "Eleven years ago a woman came to the Court and bought a veil. A valley where her younger daughter's magic could never wake, could never be sensed, could never be *taken* — because the elder daughter had already been taken, or something near enough to taken, and the mother had learned what the Court's attention does to a family. She paid with—" he paused, chose precision over mercy, "—she paid with what she had. And I built her valley. And because I was already planning to leave, because I had already begun to hate what my hands made, I built a flaw into the wards. A seam. A place where the veil could be opened from outside, someday, by someone who knew the pattern. My insurance. My escape route, if the Court ever decided a defector's knowledge was worth reclaiming."

"Oscar," Oscar said.

He nodded. "Oscar. Your mother bought you a cage and called it a sanctuary, and I forged the bars, and I left the lock broken on purpose, and last night the Hollow Court used my lock to walk into your home." He let his hands fall. "I came back to undo it. I've been traveling toward this valley for two years. I arrived yesterday. I was one day late."

The silence afterward had texture. He felt it settle on the room like ash.

It was Corrin who broke it, and to her credit — and Sefa would remember this — she did not order him out. She looked at him for a long moment with eyes that had buried a husband and two of her own children in this valley's long quiet, and she said, "Then undo it. Whatever's left to undo."

"There isn't," Sefa said. "The wards aren't damaged, Elder. They're *spent*. What happened at the border wasn't a break-in. It was a harvest. They pulled the veil down thread by thread and took the power stored in it — eleven years of suppression, all that sleeping potential, siphoned off in a night. That kind of power doesn't dissipate. It gets *used*." He looked at the black window, at the snow falling upward. "You asked me earlier why anyone would bother with a forgotten valley. Here's why. Your valley has been sitting on a reservoir of stolen potential for a decade, and last night the Court came to drain it. Something is being forged under the mountain. Something that needs power the way a crown needs gold. And I don't think your valley was the only source. I think it was one of many."

"But the voice." Oscar had come closer without seeming to move, the way weather comes closer. Her hands were fists at her sides. "When the stars went out. I heard—" She stopped. Started again, quieter, and the quiet was worse. "My sister's voice. Wren. She's been dead nine years. I heard her say my name like she was *right there*, like she was calling through a wall."

Sefa exhaled slowly. This was the part he had dreaded, and the part that mattered most.

"When the Court takes a soul," he said, "it doesn't destroy it. Destruction would be waste, and the Court wastes nothing. Souls are *stored*. Anchored. Bound into vessels — glass, stone, sometimes living flesh, sometimes places — and held as currency, as fuel, as leverage. A soul that's anchored can't speak. Can't reach across the dark. Unless—" he held up one finger, "—unless the anchor is disturbed. Moved. Cracked. Or unless something tears open nearby that lets the sound leak through." He gestured at the window, at the starless sky, at the whole ruined architecture of the night. "Something tore open last night. And you heard your sister."

"She's alive." The words came out of Oscar like something ripped. "Wren is *alive*."

"Wren is *kept*," Sefa corrected gently. "There's a difference, and you need to hold onto it, because hope that isn't precise gets people killed. But yes. If you heard her voice, then her soul exists, and it's anchored somewhere within reach of that tear. Probably close. Probably—" he closed his eyes and let the old training speak, the terrible fluency he wished he'd never learned, "—probably under the mountain. Where the forging is."

Doss Merrick said, "Under the mountain," the way a man says *at the bottom of the sea*.

"The Hollow Court's seat," Sefa confirmed. "Three weeks' travel north, through country that's going to get much worse before it gets better. Assuming it gets better at all."

"Then it's impossible," Corrin said. Not cruelly. Almost kindly. "Child, whatever you heard—"

"It's not impossible." Oscar's voice had changed. Sefa heard the shift and recognized it with a chill he hadn't felt in years: it was the sound of a decision crystallizing, the exact pitch of a soul committing itself. He had heard kings make that sound. He had heard the Hollow Queen make it, once, across a negotiating table, and had gone home and started planning his defection the same night. "She called *me*. Nine years dead and she called my name. If there's even a chance she's in there — aware, waiting — then someone has to go."

"The valley needs—"

"The valley is leaving south tomorrow; I heard the vote forming before you called this meeting. Good. Go. Be safe. I hope the thaw finds you." She turned to Sefa, and up close he could see the exhaustion carved under her eyes, the grief, and underneath both of them something older and harder that exile had been quietly tempering for fifteen years without anyone noticing — least of all her. "You said the anchor can be found. You said *hers to find*. You said that to Elder Ash at the door. I was listening."

Of course she had been. Sefa almost smiled. "I did."

"Then find it with me."

He looked at her for a long moment. Around them the survivors of the Veiled Valley murmured their fear and their plans, and the fire burned low and blue, and outside the snow continued its impossible climb into a sky with no stars. He thought about the flaw in the wards, and the flaw in himself, and the eleven years of penance that had just, unexpectedly, been given a shape and a direction.

Guilt, he had learned, was a currency too. The Court had taught him that. But spent rightly — spent *forward* — it was the only coin he had left that was worth anything.

"I'll need to teach you things," he said. "Fast. Ugly. Things about your own magic that this valley made sure you never learned. And I'll need you to remember, when the lessons hurt, that the man teaching you built the cage you grew up in."

Oscar Galloway pulled her sister's shawl tight around her shoulders and looked at him with eyes like the sky before a storm breaks.

"Good," she said. "Then you can start by telling me exactly how it breaks."

Far to the north, beneath the mountain, something vast turned over in its sleep — and in the beacon-house doorway, Sefa felt it through the soles of his boots, faint as a heartbeat through a wall.

Three weeks, he thought. *If we're lucky.*

He had stopped believing in luck the day he'd signed his first contract. But he picked up his pack anyway.
