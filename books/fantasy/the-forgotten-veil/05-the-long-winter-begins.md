## 5. The Long Winter Begins

The meeting hall had never held so many people, and it had never felt so empty.

Oscar stood at the back wall with her arms crossed tight over her chest, watching the valley argue itself into motion. Two hundred souls packed between the timber beams and the soot-stained rafters, breath fogging in the cold that had crept in through the shattered wards and refused to leave. Someone had lit every lamp in the building, and still the corners stayed dark, as if the night outside had found a way to seep through the walls.

Elder Maren stood at the front on the low dais where she'd presided over harvest festivals and naming days for as long as Oscar could remember. She looked smaller tonight. Older. Her voice, when she raised it, cracked like ice underfoot.

"—the southern passes are still open. Barely. The frost hasn't reached the Kettle River yet, but it will. Every day we wait is a day the road narrows."

"And go where?" That was Tam Broadbelt, a farmer with a voice like a landslide. "South to what? We don't know anyone past the ridge. We don't know *anything* past the ridge. None of us has left the valley in twenty years."

"Then we'll learn," Maren said. "The wards are broken. The stars are gone. Whatever kept this place safe—" she faltered on the word, and Oscar saw her jaw tighten around it, "*—kept* us safe, it's gone. There's no shame in surviving."

"There's shame in freezing on some stranger's road," someone called from the middle of the crowd.

"There's death in staying," Maren answered, flat and final.

Oscar tuned out the argument that followed. She'd heard its shape already, in fragments all morning — at the well, at the beacon-farm, in the doorway of the provisioner's shop where she'd gone to trade for lamp oil and heard the same conversation happening three times in three voices. *We should go. We can't go. What about the fields? What about the dead? What if it follows us?*

Her eyes found Sefa Anwar instead.

He stood near the eastern wall, apart from everyone, the way he'd stood apart since the night the sky went black. He wasn't listening to the debate either. His gaze was fixed on the ceiling beams, but Oscar knew him well enough now — three days of hard travel together, three nights of campfire confessions that neither of them had fully intended to make — to know he was looking through the roof, through the dark, toward the mountain.

Toward the thing that had eaten the stars.

He caught her watching and gave a small shake of his head. *Not here. Not now.* She looked away.

At the front, the vote was being called. Maren didn't bother with formalities; there was no time for tokens or tallies. "All who would go south, stand."

The room groaned — benches scraping, bodies rising, coats rustling. Oscar counted without meaning to. Most of them. Nearly all. Old Hesta rose on her cane with her granddaughter's help. Tam Broadbelt stood like a felled tree righting itself. Even the provisioner, who had sworn an hour ago that he'd die in his own shop, got to his feet with his hat clutched to his chest like a prayer.

"All who would stay."

Three people stood. A widow named Cora who hadn't spoken a full sentence since her husband died. A boy of maybe sixteen who immediately looked terrified of his own courage. And old Pell, the gravedigger, who folded his arms and stared straight ahead as though daring the room to ask him why.

Maren nodded slowly, and something in her face said she'd expected exactly this arithmetic. "We leave at first light. Take what you can carry. Leave the rest for whoever comes after." Her voice caught on that last part, just slightly. "May whatever gods are left watch over us."

The hall emptied slowly, a river of murmured fear draining out into the frozen dark. Oscar stayed against the wall until the last of them were gone, until only the lamps and the silence remained, and then she pushed off the timbers and crossed the room to where Sefa waited.

"You're not going south," she said.

It wasn't a question, and he didn't treat it like one. "No."

"Neither am I."

"I know." He studied her face in the lamplight, and she watched him decide whether to argue. He decided not to. Three days was enough to teach a man that arguing with Oscar Galloway was like arguing with weather. "You felt it again this morning."

It wasn't a question either. She nodded anyway.

The thread. That was what she'd started calling it, because she couldn't call it anything else without admitting things she wasn't ready to admit. It had begun the night the wards broke — a pull behind her sternum, faint as a fisherman's line tugging somewhere far offshore. It pointed north and east, always north and east, toward the mountain that sat on the horizon like a closed fist. Toward the voice that had called her name in Wren's voice while the world came apart.

Every morning since, it had pulled a little harder.

"It's stronger today," she said. "Like it knows I'm listening."

Sefa rubbed his jaw. In the lamplight, the burn-scar along his forearm — the one he'd never explained, the one shaped like a ward-sigil half-finished — caught the light. "A soul-anchor does that. If your sister's soul is bound somewhere, and if there's enough of you in the binding to make a claim..." He trailed off, shaking his head. "I've read about this. I helped build things like this. I've never known anyone standing on the living end of the line."

"Lucky me."

"Oscar." He said her name carefully, the way you set down something fragile. "If we follow that thread, we're walking toward the Hollow Court. Not away from it. Toward. Everything I know about them says that's suicide with extra steps."

"Everything you know about them says they took my sister's soul and turned off the sky." She heard her own voice come out low and even, colder than she'd meant it, and didn't take it back. "I'm not going south to plant turnips in someone else's war while my sister hangs in a glass jar under a mountain."

He held her gaze for a long moment. Then, quietly: "I wasn't going to stop you. I was going to ask which pass you meant to take. The low road floods with meltwater by midday. We should use the ridge trail."

Something loosened in her chest — not quite gratitude, but adjacent to it. "We?"

"I told you I came back to undo my work." He picked up his pack from the bench beside him, slinging it over one shoulder with the ease of long habit. "My work is under that mountain. Seems I'm walking the same direction regardless."

* * *

They left before dawn, ahead of the exodus, taking the ridge trail east while two hundred refugees wound south along the valley floor with their carts and their children and their bundled lives. From the height of the ridge, Oscar stopped once and looked back.

The valley lay below in the grey pre-light, and it looked exactly as it always had — the terraced beacon-farms, the slate roofs, the millpond holding a skin of new ice. Twenty years of her life, arranged neatly beneath her like a map of someone else's. She had hated this place for most of those years. She had needed it like a lung. Both things were true, and both things were useless now, because the valley was leaving, and she was not, and the thread behind her sternum pulled tighter with every step she took away from it.

She realized, standing there, that she couldn't remember her sister's laugh anymore. Only the fact of it — that Wren had laughed, easily, constantly, before the court took her — but the sound itself was gone, worn smooth by two decades of exile. All she had left was a voice calling her name through a broken ward-line, and a pull like a hook behind her ribs.

It would have to be enough.

She turned north and didn't look back again.

* * *

The veil ended three miles past the ridge, though nothing marked it. No stone, no sigil, no line drawn in the frost. But Oscar felt it the way you feel a doorframe against your shoulder in the dark — a change in pressure, a threshold in the air itself.

For twenty years, the Veiled Valley had been the one place in the world where her magic could not reach her. The wards had smothered it, pressed it down into some sealed cellar at the base of her spine, and she had learned to live with the silence the way you learn to live with a limp. She'd told herself she was grateful for it. Safe from the storms. Safe from the court that had sold her sister to buy her exile. Safe from herself.

Beyond the veil, the silence ended.

It happened between one step and the next. One moment she was walking through knee-deep snow under a starless sky, and the next, the cellar door in her chest blew open.

Oscar went to her knees in the drift.

It wasn't pain, exactly. It was *pressure* — the whole weight of the sky suddenly leaning on a door that had been barred shut since she was seven years old. She could feel everything. The cold fronts stacking up over the northern peaks like freight cars on a grade. The dry wind dragging ice crystals off the high passes. A storm system three hundred miles west, coiling over the sea, hungry and enormous and *aware of her*, the way a dog is aware of a whistle only it can hear.

"Oh," she breathed, and her breath came out silver and shaking. "Oh, that's—"

"That's what I was afraid of." Sefa crouched beside her, snow soaking through his trousers, his eyes wide. "Twenty years of pressure with nowhere to go. You're not a kettle with the lid off, Oscar. You're a kettle that's been welded shut and thrown on the fire."

Above them, clouds were gathering.

There had been no clouds when they crested the ridge — the sky had been that awful, uniform black, starless and moonless and clean. Now, in the space of ninety seconds, a knot of cloud had formed directly overhead, spiraling slow and deliberate, like water finding a drain. Like something answering a call it had been waiting years to answer.

"Make it stop," Oscar gasped. "How do I make it stop?"

"You don't. You *aim* it." Sefa grabbed her shoulders, forcing her to look at him, his grip bruising through her coat. "Listen to me. Storms don't obey anger. They don't obey fear. They obey *want*. So want something small. Want the wind to move that snowdrift. Nothing else. Small want, small storm."

The pressure in her chest was unbearable now, a scream building behind her teeth. She could feel the clouds drinking from her, feeding on the leak, growing fat and dark. Somewhere far to the west, the sea-storm had turned toward her like a compass needle finding north.

*Small want.*

She thought of the snowdrift. Just the drift. Just the wind moving powder from one place to another, harmless, ordinary, a thing any winter morning might do without magic at all.

The gale hit them sideways.

It tore the drift apart — and the next one, and the one after that, and then it took the whole hillside, a white wall of screaming powder that lifted Oscar off her feet and slammed her down ten yards downslope. Lightning walked across the clouds overhead in jagged, silent steps, blue-white light strobing the ridge. The thunder arrived a heartbeat later and it didn't sound like thunder; it sounded like the sky tearing open along a seam that had been stitched too long.

And underneath the terror — underneath the whiteout and the roar and the taste of copper in her mouth — Oscar felt something she had not felt since childhood.

Joy.

Wild, terrible, unquenchable joy. Because the power wasn't pressing down anymore. It was *moving*, pouring through her like a river through a broken dam, and every nerve in her body sang with it, and for three glorious seconds she understood why the storms had always come when she was angry, why they'd come when she was seven and grieving and full of lightning with nowhere to put it —

The wind died.

Not gradually. All at once, like a hand closing around a bell. Snow hung in the air for a moment, suspended, glittering in the afterlight of the fading lightning, and then fell straight down, soft as ash. The clouds unraveled. The silence rushed back in, vast and ringing.

Oscar lay on her back in the trampled snow, chest heaving, blood trickling from her nose, laughing.

It hurt to laugh. Everything hurt. Her muscles felt wrung out, her bones ached like she'd run a hundred miles, and the joy was still there under the pain, stubborn as a coal.

"That," Sefa said, dropping down beside her, breathing hard, "was the smallest storm I have ever seen destroy a hillside."

"I aimed it."

"You aimed it at *everything*." He wiped snow from his face and studied her — the nosebleed, the wild eyes, the grin she couldn't suppress. Something in his expression shifted, softened, worried all at once. "You felt good. Didn't you."

She didn't answer. She didn't have to.

"Oscar. Listen to me." He leaned close, and his voice dropped into the register he used for the things that mattered. "That feeling is how the Court finds people like you. That feeling is how they *make* people like you. The heralds, the hollowed ones — every one of them started as someone who touched the storm and liked it. Power isn't the trap. Loving the power is the trap."

The grin faded. She sat up slowly, wiping her nose with the back of her glove, and looked north.

The mountain stood against the starless horizon, blacker than the sky around it, and the thread behind her sternum pulled so hard now it was less a tug than a hand fisted in her ribs. *Come,* it said, in a voice that was almost Wren's. *Come, come, come.*

"I'll learn control," she said. "On the road. However long it takes. But I'm not stopping, and I'm not going south, and I'm not pretending I don't love it, because I do, and lying to you about that would be worse than scaring you."

Sefa was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, he was looking at the mountain too.

"My grandmother used to say the difference between a storm-caller and a storm is that one of them can be sorry afterward." He stood, offered her his hand. "Try to be the sorry kind."

She took his hand and let him pull her up. Her legs shook. Her whole body shook. But the thread pulled, steady and insistent as a pulse, and when she started walking north, her feet found the rhythm of it without being asked.

Behind them, far to the south, she could just make out the tail end of the refugee column winding down toward the Kettle River — a thin dark line of people carrying their whole world on their backs, fleeing the winter, fleeing the dark, fleeing her.

Oscar Galloway watched them go, and let them.

Then the wind rose at her back — gentle this time, almost tender, pushing her forward like a hand between the shoulder blades — and she walked into the long winter to get her sister back.
