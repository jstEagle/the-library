## 6. Road of Frozen Bells

The first village had no name left on its sign.

Whatever letters had once hung there had been pried away or rotted off, and the board swung on a single rusted hinge above the road, creaking in a wind that shouldn't have existed. That was the thing about the Hollow Court's frost — it didn't kill the wind. It killed everything the wind touched, and then let the wind keep moving through the wreckage like a hand rifling through a dead man's pockets.

Oscar pulled her coat tighter and stopped at the edge of the square.

"Another one," she said.

Sefa came up beside her, his ward-smith's satchel clanking softly against his hip. He didn't answer right away. He never did, with these villages. He'd stand there with his jaw working, reading the frost the way other men read scripture, and whatever he found in it made him older every time.

The village was small — maybe thirty houses arranged around a well and a shrine to some harvest god whose statue had been sheathed in ice so thick it looked like glass fruit. The frost here wasn't white. It was faintly blue, faintly *wrong*, catching the grey daylight and bending it into colors that didn't belong to winter. Frost that color didn't come from weather. It came from contracts.

"No bodies," Oscar said. She'd learned to look for them first, these last two weeks on the road. The Court's frost took warmth, not life — that was the horror of it. People could survive inside it for a long time, moving slow, thinking slow, burning whatever they had left to burn.

"They're inside," Sefa said quietly. "They're always inside."

He was right. They found them in the first house they checked: a family of five sitting around a cold hearth, wrapped in every blanket they owned, breathing shallow and gray-lipped. Alive. Barely. The mother's eyes tracked Oscar as she moved through the room, but her mouth wouldn't form words. The frost had gotten into the small muscles first — fingers, lips, the fine machinery of being human.

"The bells," the father managed, when Oscar chipped ice from the window and let in what little light the day offered. His voice sounded like it was being dragged over gravel. "You have to ring the bells."

"Bells?" Oscar looked at Sefa.

But Sefa had gone very still, staring through the doorway toward the village square, where a squat stone bell tower rose against the sky. Its bell hung silent, furred with blue frost.

"Oh," he said, and the word came out flat and sick. "Oh, no. It's a tithe-village."

* * *

Sefa explained it while they walked back to the square, his breath smoking, his hands jammed under his arms. He'd been doing more of that lately — explaining, confessing, unpicking the Court's machinery thread by thread as if every revelation were a payment on a debt he'd never finish owing.

"Some villages couldn't pay the Court in coin or souls," he said. "So they paid in warmth. There's an old rite — a contract sealed in a bell. You cast your hearth-fire's heat into the bronze, all of it, the whole village at once, and the Court takes the bell's toll and leaves you alive. Colder every year. But alive." He stopped at the tower's base and looked up at the frozen bell. "The bell rings when the next payment is due. When it rings, everyone who hears it gives up another measure of warmth whether they consent or not. The contract remembers for them."

"And if no one rings it?"

"The frost collects anyway. All at once." He met her eyes. "These people are on their last measure, Oscar. Look at them. If that bell rings tonight—"

It rang at dusk.

Not because anyone pulled the rope. The rope had frozen solid weeks ago. The bell simply *rang*, one deep, cracked note that rolled out over the rooftops and through Oscar's chest like a fist closing around her heart — and she felt it happen, felt the warmth drain out of the air itself, out of her own skin, streaming invisibly toward the tower like water finding a drain. For three seconds the whole world went silver-cold and silent.

Then it passed, and the village was a little deader than it had been at noon.

Oscar stood in the square shaking, her storm-sense — dormant, muffled, useless behind the valley's old veil even now that the veil was gone — straining against something it couldn't touch. This wasn't weather. Weather she understood. This was *accounting*. Somewhere beneath the mountain, something had just entered this village's suffering into a ledger and turned the page.

"We can't fix this," she said. Her voice cracked on it. "There's nothing to fight. There's no one to fight. It's a *contract*, Sefa. How do you fight a piece of paper?"

"You don't," Sefa said. He'd sunk down onto the tower steps, his head in his hands. "That's how they win. Not with armies. With arithmetic. Every village between here and the mountain has signed something, Oscar. Sold something. Or sold *someone*. By the time we reach the Court, half the world will have helped build our prison, and none of them will think of themselves as traitors. They'll think of themselves as survivors." He laughed, short and bitter. "I know. I was one."

She wanted to argue. She wanted to tell him there was a difference between a starving village trading its hearth-heat and a ward-smith who built the flaw the Court walked through. But standing in that square, watching a mother carry her children blankets she couldn't warm them with, Oscar found she didn't have the conviction. Judgment was easy from inside walls. From the road, it just looked like arithmetic too.

They spent the night carrying families to the shrine, packing them together around the harvest god's frozen feet, pooling what body-warmth remained. It was triage dressed as kindness. In the morning, four of the villagers would not wake, and Oscar would close their eyes and hate herself for feeling relieved that it was only four.

* * *

The second village still had its name: Bellharrow, burned into a wooden arch over the road, the wood around the letters scorched black as though the sign itself had tried to scream.

Bellharrow was worse. Bellharrow had fought.

Oscar saw it from the ridge before they descended — the barricades in the lanes, the shattered windows, the scorch-marks where someone had burned furniture for heat that hadn't been enough. And hanging from the arch, swinging gently in the dead wind, were the bells. Dozens of them. Small ones, hand-bells and cow-bells and the tinny bells from a child's toy cart, strung on wire across the road in rows.

"Why—" Oscar started.

"A warning system," Sefa said grimly. "When the Court's collectors come, the bells ring. Everyone hides. Sometimes it works." He ducked under the lowest wire without touching it. "Sometimes it doesn't."

Inside, Bellharrow was silent in a way that made the first village seem loud. No smoke from any chimney. No footprints in the frost. Doors stood open down the whole length of the main street, each one exhaling that faint blue glow from within, like mouths breathing out something colder than breath.

They found the first body in the third house. Then the second in the street. Then a third, and a fourth, and Oscar stopped counting because counting made it real and not-counting let her keep moving. These people had refused the bell-tithe. Had rung their warning bells and hidden and starved and frozen rather than feed the ledger one more entry. Their corpses were bent and dark, frozen from the inside out, and their faces —

Their faces weren't peaceful. That was the lie the ballads told about freezing to death. These faces were furious, and afraid, and human all the way to the end.

"They chose," Oscar said. Her voice came out strange. "They chose death over the deal."

"They chose," Sefa agreed. "And now they're dead, and the tithe-villages are alive, and you can stand here and tell me which one was right." He crouched beside a fallen man — young, a farmer's calluses, a wedding band — and closed his eyes with a gentleness that didn't match his words. "Go on. Tell me which one was right."

Oscar couldn't. That was the rot at the center of it, the thing she was beginning to understand about the world outside the Veiled Valley: the Court never offered good choices. It only ever offered *smaller* evils, priced fairly, payable in installments. And everyone who paid — everyone — became, in some tiny way, its clerk.

"I hate them," she said finally. "I hate them so much I can't even hate the people who deal with them. That's the trick, isn't it? That's the whole trick."

Sefa looked up at her, and for a moment something moved behind his eyes that might have been gratitude, or grief, or both wearing the same face.

"That's the whole trick," he said.

* * *

They heard the child before they saw anything.

A sound in the blue dusk of the third day — thin, rhythmic, mechanical. A bell. Not ringing in warning. Ringing the way a child rings a bell: over and over, without rhythm or reason, because the hand needs something to do when the heart has run out of instructions.

Oscar broke into a run before she decided to.

The sound led them off the road to a farmhouse set back among dead orchards, its windows glowing faint blue, its door ajar. Inside, in a kitchen crusted with frost, a boy sat on the floor beside a cold stove, ringing a small brass hand-bell against his knee. Clang. Clang. Clang. He was perhaps eight years old, wrapped in a man's coat, his lips gray, his eyes enormous and dry — past crying, dried out from the inside like everything else in this country.

Beside him, laid out neatly on the floor with their hands folded on their chests, were three bodies. A woman. A man. An old, old woman. Arranged with a care no eight-year-old should possess.

The boy looked up at Oscar and kept ringing the bell.

Clang. Clang.

"Hey," Oscar said. She knelt slowly, the way you'd approach a spooked animal, holding out her hands. "Hey. I'm Oscar. What's your name?"

The bell slowed. Stopped. The boy stared at her with those vast dry eyes.

"Ilo," he said. His voice was a whisper, rusted from disuse. "My name is Ilo."

"Ilo. Okay. Ilo, we're going to get you somewhere warm. Can you walk?"

He didn't answer. Instead he looked down at the bell in his hands, turning it over, and said, in that same rusted whisper:

"They said if I kept ringing it, they'd know I was still alive. Mama said. Before she went to sleep. She said, *keep ringing it, someone will come.*" He lifted his eyes to Oscar's. "Nobody came. Nobody came for eleven days. I counted. I rang it eleven days."

Something in Oscar's chest tore along a seam she hadn't known was there.

She gathered him up — he weighed almost nothing, a bundle of bones and coat — and he didn't resist, didn't cling either, just lay against her shoulder with the bell still gripped in one fist, pressed against her collarbone like something he intended to hold until the world gave him a reason to put it down.

Behind her, Sefa stood in the doorway looking at the three bodies laid out so carefully, and Oscar watched him mouth something — a word, or a prayer, or a number in a ledger only he could see.

* * *

They made camp that night in the farmhouse, because Ilo couldn't be moved far and because, Sefa argued, the Court's collectors had already taken their toll here; the house was spent, worthless, overlooked. Oscar got a fire going from the last of the orchard wood, and the boy watched the flames with an expression she recognized and hated seeing on a child's face: hunger without hope. He'd learned already that good things ended. Hope was just the pain of waiting for it.

"There was a man who came," Ilo said suddenly, as the fire caught. "Before. In the autumn. He wore gray, and his breath didn't make clouds." He turned the bell over in his lap. "He sat at our table and ate our bread and he was very polite. He showed Mama papers. He said the winter was going to be long this year, longer than any winter, and that the Court could help. He said everybody else was signing." His small thumb traced the bell's rim. "Papa said no. Papa said we don't deal with things that eat souls. Mama cried. Then it got cold, and the crops died, and the baby —"

He stopped. His jaw worked.

"We had a baby," he said. "Her name was Nettie. She's not in the house. Mama carried her to the church before she went to sleep, so she wouldn't be cold in the ground. Mama was strong like that. Right up to the end."

Oscar's arms were around him before she knew she'd moved. He let her hold him, stiff and unyielding as a fence post, his heartbeat fluttering quick and shallow against her ribs.

"It's not fair," he whispered into her coat. "Papa was *right*. Doing the right thing is supposed to work. That's what Papa said. That's what the priest said." His voice climbed, cracked. "If being right doesn't work, then what's the point? What's the *point* of anything?"

And Oscar — who had been exiled for the crime of existing, whose sister's soul had been sold by their own mother, who had watched safe villages sign themselves hollow and brave villages freeze for their principles — had no answer that wasn't a lie.

So she gave him the truth instead, or the closest thing to it she owned.

"I don't know what the point is," she said. "I think maybe there isn't one until we make it. I think the Court counts on everyone deciding it's pointless, because pointless people are cheap." She pulled back so he could see her face. "Your papa was right, Ilo. Being right cost him everything, and he was still right. That's not nothing. That's the only thing they can't buy."

Ilo stared at her for a long moment. Then, slowly, he uncurled his fingers from the bell and set it on the floor between them — the first time, she suspected, he'd put it down in eleven days.

Across the fire, Sefa met her eyes over the boy's head. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to. She could read it there plainly enough: *This is what they do. This is what they've always done. And this is why we keep walking.*

* * *

They buried the family at dawn, in the hard blue light, because Ilo asked. Oscar dug while Sefa carved what markers he could, and Ilo laid the brass bell on his mother's grave, then picked it up again at the last second and stuffed it in his pocket. "For later," he explained, daring them to object. Neither of them did.

They walked out of Bellharrow as the sun struggled up through a sky the color of dirty wool. Behind them the village shrank into the frost; ahead, the road ran north through hills that grew starker and stranger with every mile, the blue wrongness of contracted frost thickening in the low places like fog with an appetite.

Somewhere ahead — a day, maybe two — the road crossed a river gorge, and Sefa said there was a tollhouse there, one of the Court's way-stations, where travelers could buy passage and provisions.

"Buy with what?" Oscar asked. "We have nothing."

Sefa was quiet for so long that she stopped walking and turned to look at him. He was staring north, at the mountains just beginning to show themselves above the hills — black peaks under a bruised sky, and above the tallest of them, where stars should have been, nothing at all. The hole in the night where the stolen stars used to hang.

"Tollhouses don't take coin," he said at last. "Not out here. Out here, passage costs a memory. One per traveler, weighed and measured at the door." He glanced sideways at her, and there was something careful in his expression, something almost apologetic. "You'll want to decide beforehand which ones you can afford to lose, Oscar. Because the tollhouse won't ask. It just reaches in and takes what it judges cheapest."

Oscar's hand drifted, without her meaning it to, to the satchel at her side — where Wren's memory-fragments lived, the stolen shards of her sister's soul that had been guiding her north since the night the stars went dark. Fragments that sometimes whispered to her in her sleep. Fragments that were the only part of Wren left in the world.

Ilo, walking between them with his bell silent in his pocket, looked up at her with those vast dry eyes.

"What if you don't want to forget anything?" the boy asked.

Ahead, through the grey morning, faint and patient, a bell began to toll.
