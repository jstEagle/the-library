## 7. The Tollhouse of Small Mercies

Ilo smelled the tollhouse before he saw it — woodsmoke and tallow and something underneath, something sweetish and wrong, like fruit rotting in a warm room. He had learned to trust his nose on the road. His nose was the only part of him the frost hadn't taken a tithe from yet.

"It's real," he said, stopping at the crest of the hill. "The tollhouse. I thought it was a story my mother told to keep me from wandering."

Below them, tucked into the crook of a frozen river, stood a long building with a steep roof bowed under snow. Lanterns burned in its windows — actual fire, actual light — and smoke rose from its chimney in a fat, unbroken column. Around it, for half a mile in every direction, the world lay white and silent and dead. But here: warmth. Here: mercy.

"Everything's a story until you're hungry enough," Sefa Anwar said. He stood with his hands jammed under his arms, breath streaming from his beard in gray ribbons. The ward-smith looked older every day Ilo saw him, as if the road were unwinding him thread by thread. "Tollhouses are old magic. Older than the Court, maybe. They used to take coin."

"And now?" Oscar Galloway stood a little apart from them, the way she always did, as if she were still deciding whether she belonged to her own company. Wind moved through her hair even though there wasn't any wind. There never was around her anymore — not since the gorge, not since she'd started learning what lived in her blood.

"Now they take something worth more than coin," Sefa said. "Something everyone carries and no one can spare."

Ilo hugged his arms against his chest. He knew what the tollhouses took. Everyone in the frozen country knew, the way everyone knew which neighbors had signed and which had starved. You paid a memory. One memory per traveler, chosen by the house, not by you. That was the part people didn't say out loud — that you didn't get to pick what you lost. The house chose. The house always knew best what you could live without.

Or so they claimed.

"My village paid differently," Ilo heard himself say. The words came out flat, rehearsed, the way he'd been saying them since the night the frost took everything. "We sold our warmth. All of it. In one contract." He looked at Oscar because she was the one who'd pulled him out of the snowdrift where his village used to be, and because looking at Sefa made his stomach hurt. "So I know about paying. I just never paid this way."

Oscar's jaw tightened. She did that whenever he mentioned the contracts — not with pity, which he could have borne, but with that fierce, banked fury of hers, as if someone had wronged *her*. As if she'd decided his village's arithmetic was an insult she intended to answer for.

"We don't have to go in," she said.

"Yes, we do." Sefa nodded down the slope. "Look at the light. Look at the smoke. That's a working hearth, girl — a true one, fed by the house itself. We haven't slept warm in nine days. Your hands are cracked. The boy's lips are gray. And tomorrow we reach the foothills of the mountain, and after that there won't be another lit window between here and the Court's door." He exhaled slowly. "One memory each. That's the toll. People walk out of these places. People survive them."

"People walk out of them *missing things*," Ilo said.

"Yes," Sefa agreed simply. "That's what surviving costs now."

* * *

The tollhouse door was oak, older than any tree Ilo had ever seen, carved over every inch with figures whose faces had been worn smooth by centuries of hands pushing past them. A brass bell above the lintel rang once when they entered — a small, clear sound, absurdly gentle, like a shopkeeper's welcome.

Inside, it was warm enough to make Ilo's fingers ache. The great room stretched back farther than the building's outside walls should have allowed, lined with shelves, and the shelves were lined with jars.

Hundreds of jars. Thousands.

Each one held a faint glow, some gold, some blue, some the pale green of foxfire, and they pulsed — softly, arrhythmically, like a room full of sleeping hearts. Ilo had seen soul-glass once, from a distance, in the caravan of a Court factor who'd passed through his village before the signing. But these weren't souls. These were smaller. Dimmer. Humbler.

Memories. Shelves and shelves of other people's yesterdays.

"You're staring," said the tollkeeper.

She sat behind a counter of black wood at the far end of the room, an old woman wrapped in so many shawls she seemed less a person than a landscape — hills and valleys of dyed wool, with a face rising out of them like a moon. Her eyes were the strange part. Not blind, exactly. But unfixed. Looking at Ilo, at Oscar, at Sefa, and also looking somewhere behind all of them, at something they couldn't see.

"The ledger keeps itself," she said, before anyone had spoken. "Sit. Warm yourselves. The toll comes due before the door opens again, never fear. No one leaves colder than they came."

There were benches near a hearth the size of a wagon bed, and the fire in it burned without wood — or rather, Ilo realized with a lurch, it burned *on* wood that never got shorter. Logs that had been burning for years, perhaps, giving and giving and never diminishing. Everything in this house was like that. Everything gave and kept.

They sat. A kettle appeared — Ilo never saw who brought it — and poured tea that tasted of honey and pine and, faintly, of nothing at all, like a flavor remembered rather than tasted.

"How does it work?" Oscar asked. She held her cup in both hands, and Ilo watched her deliberately loosen her grip, deliberately slow her breathing. She'd been doing that since the gorge. Holding herself like a dam holds a river. "The taking. Does it hurt?"

The tollkeeper's moon-face turned toward her. For a moment those unfocused eyes seemed to sharpen, seemed to look *into* her, and Ilo saw the old woman go very still, the way a dog goes still when it scents something on the wind.

"What a full cup you carry," the tollkeeper murmured. "What a heavy, crowded head." Then the stillness passed, and she was merely an old woman again, pouring herself tea with a hand that trembled slightly. "It doesn't hurt. That's the cruelty of it, child. If it hurt, folk would fight it. It feels like setting down a bundle you forgot you were carrying. You walk out lighter. You walk out grateful. And it's only later — sometimes years later — that you reach for the thing that's gone and find the space where it was shaped like an absence." She sipped. "The house chooses kindly. I promise you that. The house has been choosing kindly since before your grandmother's grandmother paid her first toll."

"Who built it?" Sefa asked quietly. "Who *keeps* it?"

"The ledger keeps the ledger." The old woman smiled, and it was the smile of someone reciting words worn smooth as the carvings on the door. "Ask your next question, smith. I can smell the questions on you like smoke."

Sefa's eyes went to the shelves. To the jars. "When the Court falls — if it falls — do these go back? Do the memories return?"

For the first time, the tollkeeper laughed. It was a terrible sound, dry as pages turning.

"Oh, smith. Oh, you dear ruined man. Nothing goes back. That's not what ledgers do. Ledgers record. What's written is written." She set down her cup. "But drink your tea. Rest your feet. The toll comes due at dawn, and dawn is four hours off, and I'll not have it said the Tollhouse of Small Mercies rushed its guests."

* * *

Ilo didn't mean to sleep, but the warmth pulled him under like a current. He dreamed of his mother's kitchen, of bread and cardamom, of his cousin Vess laughing with flour on her nose — and woke with wet eyes and no reason for them, and lay staring at the ceiling beams while the others slept, counting the glowing jars along the nearest shelf.

Forty-one on that shelf alone. Forty-one things people had walked in carrying and walked out without.

He found Oscar awake too, sitting by the dying edge of the hearth-light, turning something over in her hands. A strip of leather, braided — a bracelet, child-sized, worn nearly through.

"That's hers," Ilo said softly. "Your sister's."

Oscar flinched. Then, slowly, she nodded. "Wren made two. One for each of us. Before—" She stopped. Before their mother sold her. Before the court bought a little girl's soul and shipped her sister beyond the veil like contraband. Ilo knew the shape of the story; he'd pieced it together from fragments on the road, from the way Sefa went quiet whenever Wren's name surfaced, from the way Oscar sometimes pressed her hand flat against her sternum as though something inside her were knocking to get out.

"She's alive," Ilo said. It wasn't a question. He'd felt the truth of it radiating off Oscar for days, a heat stronger than any hearth. "In the mountain. You're going to get her back."

"I'm going to try." Oscar wound the bracelet around her wrist, over the fading rope-burn scars, and knotted it tight. "And if trying isn't enough, then I'm going to burn the mountain down trying."

Ilo believed her. That was the frightening thing. He'd watched her call lightning out of a sky that had forgotten how, watched storms gather around her grief like iron filings around a magnet, and he believed her completely. He was glad, in a distant way, that such a person was on his side. He was more frightened, in a nearer way, of what she'd become by the time they reached the Court's door.

"Sleep," she told him. "Dawn comes early. And apparently it takes things with it."

* * *

Dawn came gray through the frost-furred windows, and with it came the tollkeeper, moving between them with a small silver bell that made no sound.

"One at a time," she said. "The house will choose. Hold still. Think of nothing, or think of everything — it makes no difference. The ledger reads deeper than thinking."

Sefa went first. He insisted on it — *if the house takes something vital from me, better you learn it while we can still turn back* — and Ilo watched from the bench as the old woman laid two fingers against the ward-smith's temple and rang her silent bell.

Sefa swayed. Blinked. Straightened his shoulders with the deliberate care of a man checking that all his parts were still attached.

"Well?" Oscar asked.

"I don't know." He frowned, patting his pockets, patting his own chest, hunting for the shape of the absence. "That's the trick of it. You can't miss what you can't remember missing." His frown deepened. "Though — hm. I had a speech prepared. About the valley wards. Why I built the flaw into them. I've been composing it for days, waiting for the right moment to tell you both." He looked up, and there was something raw and lost in his face. "I remember that I had a confession. I remember that it mattered. I cannot remember one word of it."

The tollkeeper wrote something in a book bound in gray leather, her pen scratching like a mouse in a wall. "The house chose kindly," she repeated. "A confession can be relearned, smith. Some burdens are kinder carried twice."

Then it was Ilo's turn.

He stood. His legs felt like borrowed things. He crossed the floor to the counter, and up close the tollkeeper smelled of tallow and cold stone, and her unfocused eyes drifted across him like searchlights finding nothing.

"Small thing," she murmured, almost tenderly. "Small life. The house will be gentle."

Her fingers touched his temple. The bell rang its soundless ring.

And Ilo felt — nothing. Exactly as promised. A settling, like snow sliding off a branch. A lightness. He waited for the horror, the gaping hole, the scream stuck in his throat.

Nothing came. He felt fine. He felt *good*, God forgive him. He felt lighter.

He walked back to the bench and sat down beside Oscar, and the tollkeeper's pen scratched, and the jars glowed on their shelves, and somewhere in that vast silent library of other people's yesterdays, one more small light had been added to the collection.

* * *

Oscar went last.

Ilo was watching her — he'd appointed himself her watcher somewhere on the Road of Frozen Bells, without asking her permission, without telling her — and so he saw everything. The tollkeeper's fingers at her temple. The soundless bell. The long pause, longer than either of theirs, as if the house were reading her the way you'd read a difficult letter, twice, three times.

Then Oscar gasped.

It was a small sound, quickly swallowed, but Ilo saw her whole body flinch around it, saw her hand fly to her wrist — to the braided leather there — and grip it hard enough to blanch her knuckles.

"What did it take?" Sefa asked sharply. "Girl. What did it take?"

"I don't—" Oscar's brow furrowed. She looked genuinely baffled, and that bafflement was worse than grief would have been. "Nothing. I feel fine. Lighter, like you said. Like—"

"Your wrist," Ilo said quietly.

She looked down. Her thumb was rubbing the braid in small, automatic circles, the way you'd touch a scar, the way you'd worry a rosary. She stared at it. Turned her hand over. Stared harder.

"Why am I wearing this?" she said.

The room went very quiet. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"It's your sister's bracelet," Ilo said. "Wren's. You told me last night. She made two, one for each of—"

"I know the story," Oscar said. And then, slower, with the careful diction of someone walking across ice: "I know the story. Mother sold her. The court bought her. I was exiled to keep me safe. I've known it since I was six years old." Her hand hadn't stopped moving on the braid. "But I don't know why I know it. It's like — it's like reading a report. Like someone else's history that happens to have my name in it." She looked up at Sefa, and her eyes were wide and bright and terribly calm. "Last night I was holding this and I was *aching*. I remember that I was aching. I can't remember what the ache was made of."

Sefa rose from the bench very slowly. He crossed to the tollkeeper's counter, and his voice, when it came, was the flattest Ilo had ever heard it — flat the way ground is flat right before it opens.

"What did the house take from her?"

The old woman's pen paused. Her moon-face tilted. "The house chose kindly," she said.

"*What did it take.*"

"A memory of the sister," the tollkeeper said. "One memory. A single evening, by the weight of it. Recent, emotionally speaking. The house takes what can be spared, smith. The house is very old and very good at knowing what can be spared." She turned her unfixed eyes on Oscar with something that might, in a younger face, have been apology. "You'll find you remember that you loved her. You'll find you remember everything important. Only one evening is gone. One evening out of a childhood. Small mercies."

"But she doesn't know *which* evening," Sefa said.

"No one ever does."

Oscar stood. She was very still, in the way Ilo had learned meant danger — the stillness before weather. Outside the frosted windows, clouds were gathering that hadn't been there moments ago, boiling up over the white hills like smoke from a buried fire.

"Give it back," she said.

"I can't, child. The ledger—"

"Give. It. Back." Thunder rolled somewhere beyond the hills, soft and distant, a sound the frozen country had almost forgotten. The jars on the shelves began to tremble in their rows, chiming faintly against one another, hundreds of stolen memories rattling like teeth. "I have given up enough. My home. My name. My *magic* is chained behind a veil your kind probably built. I am not giving up one single hour of my sister. Not one. Give it back or I will pull this house apart rafter by rafter and read every jar myself until I find mine."

The tollkeeper did not flinch. That was the horrible thing — she didn't flinch at all. She simply folded her hands atop the gray ledger and regarded Oscar with those bottomless eyes, and said, gently:

"Storm-child. Sit down. Listen to an old woman who has watched ten thousand travelers pass through this door. You cannot bully a ledger. You cannot threaten arithmetic. What is written is written." She opened the book and turned it around, and Ilo craned to see — and wished he hadn't. The page was filled with entries in a hand like frost-ferns, and Oscar's entry sat fresh and gleaming among them:

*Galloway, O. — one (1) evening, paid in full. Receipt issued.*

"Receipt," Sefa said. His voice had gone strange. "What receipt? Where?"

The tollkeeper reached beneath the counter and produced a slip of paper, thin as onion skin, glowing faintly gold at the edges. "Every payment issues a receipt. House rule. Older than the house." She held it out to Oscar. "Keep it safe, storm-child. You'll need it someday. When you reach the place where all the ledgers are kept — the great one, under the mountain — receipts can be honored. Debts can be contested. It has been done before." Her eyes drifted, unfocused, toward the south and east, toward the black shape of the mountain that even now Ilo could feel pulling at the horizon like a hole pulls at cloth. "If you're going there anyway. Which I suspect you are. Which the ledger already knows."

Oscar took the receipt. Her hand had stopped trembling; the thunder outside had settled into a low continuous growl, like a dog lying at the door. She looked at the slip of paper, then at the shelves of jars, thousands of them, thousands of evenings and faces and names that someone, somewhere, was keeping.

"Who writes in that book?" she asked. "Really. Not the house. Not the ledger. Who's keeping the accounts?"

The tollkeeper smiled her dry, page-turning smile.

"Child," she said, "who do you think? Every tollhouse, every contract, every village that sold its warmth and every soul bought in glass — it's all one ledger. It's all been one ledger for a thousand years." She closed the book with a soft, final sound. "The Hollow Queen doesn't just forge winters, storm-child. She keeps books. And she is very, very good at arithmetic."

They left within the hour, into the gathering dark of a sky that Oscar's grief was already bruising purple. Ilo walked last, and at the threshold he turned back once. The tollkeeper stood in her doorway, silhouetted against the warm light, watching them go.

"Safe roads," she called. "Mind the foothills. And travelers —" her voice dropped, almost kind, almost sorry — "the next toll you pay won't be collected by a house."

Behind them, far to the north, beneath the mountain, something ancient turned a page.
