## 8. Court Beneath the Mountain

Sefa had walked this road before. That was the thing he could not say aloud, not yet, not while the girl slept with her back against a frozen bell and the child Ilo curled against her side like a burr. He had walked this road before, and the last time he had come down it in the opposite direction, with frost on his boots and a contract signed in his own blood and no idea — none at all — of what he was carrying home.

He crouched at the edge of the ridge and let the others sleep a little longer.

The mountain filled half the sky now. It had been visible for two days as a gray smudge on the horizon, and each hour it grew more definite, more architectural, until it stopped looking like geology at all and started looking like intention. The peak was wrong. Mountains wore their snow like fur, uneven, wind-scoured, alive. This one's snow lay smooth as poured wax, unbroken from summit to shoulder, and where the rock showed through it was not gray but black, black as cooled iron, black as the inside of a closed mouth.

And it breathed. That was the worst part. Sefa had watched it for an hour before he admitted what he was seeing: a slow pulse of vapor rising off the southern face, regular as a sleeper's chest. Warm air going out. Cold air going in. The mountain was inhaling winter and exhaling nothing.

Behind him, Ilo stirred. The boy woke the way children in dead villages woke now — all at once, silently, eyes open before body moving, hands already searching for whatever could be held. He found his stick of chalk. He always found his chalk first. Then he crawled over to Oscar and pressed himself against her ribs, and only then did he look up at Sefa and speak.

"Is that it?"

"That's it."

"It doesn't look like a court."

"No," Sefa said. "Courts have doors."

* * *

They made the lower slopes by midmorning, if midmorning still meant anything under a sky the color of dirty pewter. The stars had not returned. Six days now without them, and Sefa had caught Oscar counting the dark twice a night, lips moving, as though she could shame the heavens back into place through sheer arithmetic. It would not work. He knew exactly how many stars were missing, because he knew who had counted them out of the sky, and into what vessel, and why.

The road here had been a road once. You could see its bones beneath the frost — paving stones laid by hands that had believed in permanence, milestone stubs, the drowned arch of a waystation gate. Now everything wore the same white shroud. They passed a farmhouse with its door standing open and frost furring the lintel like white mold, and inside, Sefa saw, the table was still set. Three bowls. Three spoons. A loaf gone to stone under glass. Whatever bargain those people had struck or failed to strike, they had left in a hurry, or been taken in one, and there was no way anymore to tell which.

Ilo stopped at the gate and looked at the bowls for a long time.

"We can't take anything," Sefa told him gently. "Nothing from a house that paid."

"Why?"

"Because the Court keeps ledgers, and everything in a paid house is already entered in them. Touch it, and you're touching ink." He hated how easily the old vocabulary came back to him. Ledger. Entry. Ink. Seven years trying to forget the grammar of the Hollow Court, and it lived in his mouth like a second tongue.

Oscar came up beside him, breath steaming, storm-grey eyes fixed on the mountain. She had not slept well. None of them had, but she wore it differently — not as exhaustion but as voltage, a fine tremor in her hands she kept closing into fists. Since the gorge, since the training, since Wren's voice had come through the soul-thread saying *don't come*, she had been a wire pulled too tight.

"Tell me again," she said. "All of it. Not the version you gave me on the road. The real one."

Sefa looked at the mountain instead of at her. "Which part?"

"Start with what they are. Actually are. You keep saying *the Court* like it's a government. Governments have borders. This thing ate the sky."

So he told her. Walking, because it was easier walking, easier to say terrible things when your legs were doing something honest.

"They started as people," he said. "That's the part nobody believes. Three hundred years ago they were a guild — season-smiths, weather-wrights, the folk who kept the almanacs true. When the old kingdoms fell apart, someone had to decide where the rain went, who got the long summer, whose harvest would fail so another's wouldn't. It was supposed to be temporary stewardship. Triage." His boot crunched through frost crust into older frost beneath. "But triage becomes administration, and administration becomes ownership. Somewhere in the second century they stopped asking who deserved the warm years and started selling them. A decade of summer for a city's debt. A mild winter for a border redrawn. And when coin ran short — because coin always runs short — they found the currency that never does."

"Souls," Oscar said flatly.

"Souls." He nodded toward the drowned farmhouse. "Not just taken. Traded. Every village we've passed that still has warmth bought it somehow. The Court doesn't conquer. It *underwrites*. It lets you sign yourself into ruin one clause at a time, and it never lies to you, not once, not ever. That's the horror of it. Everything you lose, you'll find your own name next to, in your own hand."

"And the stars." Her voice cracked slightly on the word. "The crown. Explain the crown."

Sefa stopped walking. Ahead, the road bent around a shoulder of black rock, and beyond the bend he could see the first of the Court's works — pillars, distant and thin as needles, marching up the mountainside in a spiral. He made himself explain it anyway, because she needed to hear it from him before she saw it, and because saying it aloud was the only penance he had that cost anything.

"A soul is a small light," he said. "Every ward-smith knows it; it's the first lesson and the last. Small, but it burns at a temperature nothing else reaches. Stack enough of them together and you don't get a bigger flame. You get a different kind of fire entirely — one that burns *time*." He turned to face her fully. "The stars aren't lights, Oscar. They're the oldest souls there are. First-fire. The world's original deposit, locked away where no one could spend it. The Court has spent three hundred years building the machinery to make a withdrawal."

"The crown."

"The crown. Forged, not worn — not yet. They're hammering starlight into a ring of permanent winter, and when it closes, it won't be a season anymore. It'll be a law. Snow that falls forever because somewhere under that mountain, forever has been purchased outright." He heard his own voice go quiet. "I helped design the forge that shapes it. Thirty years ago. I thought I was building a kiln for tempering wards. By the time I understood what the plans fed into, my name was in the ledger too, and the exit clauses were—" He stopped. "There are no exit clauses. That's the whole design."

Oscar was silent for a long moment. Wind moved across the slope, carrying grit and cold and, faintly, a sound Sefa had been pretending not to hear for a day: singing. Very far away. Very many voices, flattened into one note, like wind through wires.

"You're going to tell me the rest eventually," she said. "About the flaw in the valley wards. About what you buried."

"Yes."

"When we're closer. When it matters more."

"When it can't be avoided," he agreed, and she almost smiled, and the almost was enough to keep him upright.

* * *

They camped that night in the shell of a tollhouse — not the Tollhouse of Small Mercies, that was days behind them, but a cousin of it, same architecture, same purpose. The Court's roads all ran through the same chokepoints, and every chokepoint had a booth where passage was weighed. This one stood empty, its toll-plate frosted over, its memory-jars racked and shattered. Someone had broken in months ago and smashed every jar. Sefa knelt among the glass and read the story in it: freed memories evaporating as mist, half-forgotten names scratched into the counter by some debtor's last act of defiance.

Ilo traced one of the names with his chalk, careful not to touch it. "Did it help? Breaking them?"

"For a night," Sefa said. "Then the Court sent collectors, and the collectors don't need jars. They carry the ledger in their heads." He looked at the boy, at the chalk-stained fingers, at the way the child catalogued ruin the way other children collected river stones. "You knew a tollhouse keeper once, didn't you. Before."

"My aunt." Ilo didn't look up. "She took one memory from me. Just one. She cried while she did it. She said it was better she took it than the heralds, because she'd only take fair price." He finally raised his eyes. "Was she complicit?"

The question hung there, and Sefa felt Oscar go very still by the fireless lamp.

"Everyone under this sky is complicit," he said finally. "The Court built a world where the choices are all bad and then sold us the badness at interest. Your aunt chose the smallest cruelty available. That's not innocence. But it isn't monstrosity either. It's arithmetic." He hated the answer even as he gave it, because it was the answer the Court wanted everyone to reach — *we are all just numbers doing sums* — and yet it was also true, and truth was the only weapon he had left that didn't belong to them.

Ilo nodded slowly, accepting it the way children accept heavy things, without pretending they're light.

* * *

It was near midnight when the heralds came up the road.

Sefa heard them first — or rather, heard the absence of them, the way the mountain's distant singing cut off all at once, like a door closing on a choir. He was on his feet before his mind finished the thought, hand on Oscar's shoulder, pressing her down behind the tollhouse wall. Ilo folded himself into the shadow of the shattered jars, small as a sack of laundry.

They came up the frozen road in single file, and Sefa's stomach turned over, because he had not seen one in thirty years and they had not changed, not one hair of their terrible design.

Heralds of the Hollow Court had been people. That was the first thing. They had the proportions of people, the gait of people, and they moved with the particular care of bodies being operated rather than inhabited. Their skin was the grey-white of paper kept too long in water. Where faces should have been there was smoothness, featureless except for a single seam running temple to jaw — the seam along which the soul had been removed, neatly, surgically, the way a seed is cut from fruit. They wore livery of undyed wool, and each carried a staff tipped with a small glass bell, and the bells did not ring. They *leaned*. As the heralds passed, the bells tilted toward the tollhouse, toward the three living hearts hiding in it, straining like compass needles.

Sefa held his breath. Beside him, Oscar's whole body had gone rigid, and he could feel it — the pressure building in the air around her, the static crawl along his arm hairs, the storm-sense reaching for her power the way a drowning man reaches for a rope. If she sparked now, if one thread of lightning left her skin—

*Don't,* he mouthed. *Want it quiet. Want it quiet.*

Her eyes, huge in the dark, fixed on his. And slowly, slowly, the pressure drained away, the air going flat and dead, and Sefa understood that she had done the hardest thing in her young life: she had wanted silence more than she wanted to strike, and the storm had obeyed.

The heralds passed. Eleven of them. At the last one, the lead herald paused — just a hitch in its stride, half a heartbeat — and its glass bell swung once toward the tollhouse and then, deliberately, away. Then the file moved on up the road, toward the mountain, and the singing resumed far above, and the night exhaled.

"They knew," Oscar whispered. "That last one. It looked right at us."

"It looked at the tollhouse," Sefa said. "There's a difference. Heralds don't see. They *audit*. And an audit only flags what's already in the ledger." He let out his breath. "We're not in it. Yet."

"But we will be."

"Oh, yes. The moment we cross the threshold, our names write themselves. That's how the mountain works. It's not a fortress, Oscar. It's a book, and everything that enters gets recorded." He settled back against the wall, staring up at the black peak against the starless sky, at the spiral of needle-pillars climbing its flanks, each one crowned with a cage of glass that held — he didn't let himself count, but he knew — a fragment of stolen heaven.

"Then we'd better make sure," Oscar said quietly, "that when our names go in, they go in as a problem."

Despite everything — the heralds, the mountain, the thirty years of buried guilt sitting on his chest like snowpack — Sefa laughed. It came out ragged and surprised, and Ilo looked up at the sound as though the world had briefly spoken a language he'd forgotten.

Sleep did not come easily after that, and when it came, it brought the old dream: the forge plans spread across a table, his own hand drawing the flaw into the valley wards, and a voice like cool water saying *thank you, smith, your account stands in good order.* He woke before dawn with frost on his eyelashes and Oscar already awake, watching the mountain breathe.

"There's a path," she said. "On the north face. Where the snow doesn't lie smooth. Look."

He looked. She was right — a seam of disturbed white, winding up toward a dark cleft in the rock. Old mine workings, maybe, or a service way the Court's own people used. A way in that wasn't a door, wasn't a threshold, might not trigger the ledger.

Or might be exactly the kind of trap the Court wrote into every contract: the clause that looks like mercy.

"Tomorrow," Sefa said. "We rest today. Tomorrow we climb." And high above them, faint as a memory of music, the mountain sang on — thousands of voices, thousands of souls, and somewhere in that flattened chorus, he was almost certain, one voice that sounded like a girl named Wren, calling a name that was not *don't come* anymore, but something new.

Something that sounded, terribly, like *hurry*.
