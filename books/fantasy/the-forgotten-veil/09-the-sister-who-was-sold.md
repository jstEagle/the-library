## 9. The Sister Who Was Sold

The fire had burned down to a red memory of itself when the dream came again.

Oscar knew it was a dream because Wren was twelve in it, and Wren would be twenty-three now if she were anything at all. She stood in the doorway of their old room in the capital, wearing the blue dress with the crooked hem, the one their mother never got around to mending because there was always something more urgent — a petition, a debt, a name to whisper into the right ear at court.

"You're dreaming," Wren said, in the way of dreams, stating the obvious as though it were a kindness.

"I know."

"Then look closer."

Oscar looked. The room behind her sister was wrong — not the warm clutter of childhood but a corridor of pale glass, and behind each pane something moved that might have been a face. The cold came through the dream like water through a cracked hull.

"Wake up," Wren said, and her voice split into two voices, and one of them was the voice from the night the stars went out, calling Oscar's name across the ruined wards. "Wake *up*—"

She woke with frost on her eyelashes.

That was new. That was very new. The abandoned tollhouse they'd sheltered in for two nights had a roof, mostly, and a hearth Sefa had coaxed back to life, and Ilo slept curled against the far wall like a comma of a child, breathing small and even. But the air near Oscar's face glittered, fine crystals forming and falling, forming and falling, and her breath came out silver.

Her magic, leaking. Even here, three days south of the valley, three days into a world going white at the edges, the storm in her blood was seeping through cracks she hadn't known she had.

Sefa sat by the hearth, awake, watching her with those ward-smith's eyes that measured everything twice. "You dreamed about her," he said. Not a question.

"How could you possibly—"

"Frost on your lashes. It happens when the thread pulls hard." He fed a splintered chair leg to the fire. "My mother used to say grief has a temperature. I thought it was poetry. Then I started working soul-anchors, and I learned it was engineering."

Oscar sat up, pulling her cloak around her shoulders. Outside, the wind moved through the frozen pines with a sound like distant bells — the whole road north had that sound now, villages' worth of bells frozen mid-ring, and she'd stopped asking Sefa what it meant because his face when he answered was worse than not knowing.

"Tell me about the contracts," she said.

He looked up.

"The Hollow Court trades in souls. You said it yourself, the first night, before you told me anything else. Trades in souls *and seasons*. So tell me how a trade works. Tell me what my mother paid." Her voice stayed level, which surprised her. "Because I've spent eleven years believing she sold my sister to save herself. And last night Wren told me to look closer, and I am so tired, Sefa, of looking at the same picture and seeing the same lie."

The fire popped. Ilo stirred but didn't wake.

"All right," Sefa said quietly. "But you should eat something first. This story goes down easier with bread."

"It doesn't go down at all. Eat it with me anyway."

---

Eleven years ago, in a house in the capital that smelled of ink and cold tea, their mother had come to Oscar's room at an hour no honest person kept.

Oscar remembered it in fragments, the way you remember anything you've spent years trying not to remember: the candle guttering; her mother's hands, usually so composed, worrying the ring on her right index finger; the packed satchel already waiting by the door.

"You leave at dawn," her mother said. "There will be a carriage. You won't see who drives it."

"Where am I going?"

"Somewhere your magic can't follow you."

Oscar had been eleven and furious and flattered all at once — furious because exile, flattered because *your magic*, spoken of her storm-sense as though it were already a thing of consequence, a thing worth hiding a child over. She hadn't asked the question that mattered. Children never ask the question that matters; they ask *why me* instead of *what did it cost*.

"Why me?" she had asked.

Her mother had knelt then, which she almost never did, and taken Oscar's face in her cool hands, and said: "Because you are loud, my love. You are loud in a world where loud things get collected."

And then — this was the part Oscar had buried deepest, the part that surfaced only now, in a freezing tollhouse with frost still melting off her lashes — her mother had wept. Not the pretty kind. The ugly, silent kind, tears running off her jaw onto the floorboards, while her expression stayed perfectly, terribly calm, as though the crying belonged to someone else entirely.

"Whatever you hear about me," she said, "whatever they tell you I did — remember that I did it *first*. Remember that whatever came after, I stood in front of it."

Then she kissed Oscar's forehead, left the candle burning, and closed the door.

Oscar never saw her again. Three weeks later, word reached the valley where the carriage had delivered her: Lady Villalobos dead of a fever, estate seized by creditors, and — mentioned only in passing, a footnote in someone else's ruin — the younger daughter unaccounted for. Not dead. Not sold, as far as any record showed. Just *unaccounted for*, the way things go unaccounted for when someone powerful signs something quiet.

For eleven years Oscar had built her life on top of that footnote. Her mother sold Wren. Her mother chose survival over love, or cowardice over courage, or — on Oscar's worst nights, alone on the beacon-farm with the valley's suffocating silence pressing down — her mother simply loved one daughter more, and the loud one was easier to spend than the quiet one.

It was a complete story. It had kept her upright for a decade.

And it was wrong.

---

"She didn't sell Wren to save herself," Oscar said. The bread sat untouched in her hands. "Did she."

Sefa turned his cup slowly, once, the way he did when he was choosing how much truth a moment could hold. He'd been doing that a lot since the wards fell. Since he'd confessed, in a voice like gravel underwater, that he had once served the Court whose signature he now read in every shattered stone.

"No," he said. "I don't think she did."

"Then tell me what she did. All of it. Don't arrange it kindly."

He set the cup down. "The Court doesn't take souls the way thieves take purses. They *trade*. Every contract is an exchange — something given, something received, both sides bound. And the exchange rate is never fair, because desperation sets the price, and desperate people don't haggle." He looked into the fire. "Your mother went to them. That part of the story you've always believed is true. She sought out the Hollow Court, and she signed something, and Wren's soul is in their vault because of her signature. None of that changes."

"But why."

"Because of you."

The word landed like a dropped stone.

"There are records," Sefa said. "I kept records, in those years. Contracts, copies, the ledger-work nobody thinks a smith needs to bother with. There was a contract drawn up eleven years ago — I've seen the fragment. Two clauses. The first clause: the soul of Wren Villalobos, surrendered entire, held in perpetuity." His jaw tightened. "The second clause: in exchange, the storm-calling bloodline of the house of Villalobos shall be veiled — sealed beyond the reach of the Court, its bearer hidden where heralds cannot walk, for as long as the veil stands."

Oscar heard the words. She understood each one individually. Together they refused to make sense, the way a familiar room refuses to make sense when you wake in it somewhere else.

"That's not a sale," she said finally. "That's—"

"A purchase. Your mother bought your exile. The Veiled Valley isn't a prison, Oscar. It's a vault *she built* — or paid the Court to build, or paid the last free ward-smiths to build, I've never been sure which. The valley's wards aren't there to keep you in. They're there to keep the Court from ever collecting the rest of what your blood carries." He met her eyes. "Wren's soul wasn't the payment. Wren's soul was the *price of admission*. The thing the Court demanded before they'd agree to hide you at all."

The fire crackled. Somewhere outside, a frozen bell rang once in the wind and went silent again.

"And me?" Oscar's voice came out strange, thin. "What was I in the transaction?"

Sefa was quiet for a long moment. When he answered, he chose the words like a man walking on river ice.

"You were the reason for the contract. But you weren't the beneficiary." He rubbed his eyes. "Think about what the Court actually wanted. A storm-caller coming into her power in the capital, at court, surrounded by rivals and collectors and queens who buy weather. Your mother saw what was coming for you — maybe she was warned, maybe she just understood the arithmetic better than anyone gave her credit for. And she couldn't fight the Court. Nobody fights the Court. So she did the only thing left. She gave them something they wanted *more* than you."

"Wren."

"Wren. A soul, clean and young and unclaimed — the rarest currency they deal in. In exchange, they wrote you out of the world. Veiled you. Made you invisible to every herald and collector and hungry queen in existence." His voice roughened. "You weren't the payment, Oscar. You were the *shadow* the payment cast. Everything your mother did — the exile, the secrecy, the cold goodbye — none of it was abandonment. It was the shape protection takes when it can't afford to look like love."

Oscar stood up. She didn't decide to; her body simply refused to keep sitting inside the story.

"That's worse," she said.

"Oscar—"

"Don't." She pressed the heel of her hand against her sternum, as if she could hold the breaking in. "Don't you see? If she sold Wren to save herself, I could hate her. Hating her was *simple*. Eleven years, Sefa. I built everything on hating her. And now you're telling me she traded one daughter's *soul* for the other daughter's *safety*, and called it motherhood, and died before anyone could ask her whether it was worth it—" Her breath fogged the air between them. "She let me believe I was the unwanted one. She let me carry that. Because if I'd known, I would have gone back. I would have marched into the Court myself at twelve years old and offered them everything, and they'd have taken it, and both of us would be glass on a shelf."

"Yes," Sefa said softly. "That's exactly what she was afraid of."

Ilo had woken at some point without either of them noticing. The child sat against the wall, knees drawn up, watching them with the particular stillness of children who have learned that adults' worst moments are not for interrupting.

"My village made a contract too," Ilo said.

Both of them turned.

"Not for souls. For warmth. The elders signed, and the frost stopped at our borders, and everyone pretended not to know what the frost was doing to the places past our borders." Ilo's voice was flat, careful, older than its years. "Everyone knew. Everyone let it happen. Because the alternative was us." A pause. "Was that evil?"

"No," Oscar said, before she could think, because some answers live below thinking.

"Then maybe your mother wasn't either." Ilo shrugged, small and devastating. "Maybe nobody's evil. Maybe everybody's just arithmetic."

The child lay back down and was asleep within minutes, the way children can, carrying horrors like stones they've learned to sleep on.

---

Oscar walked out into the snow.

Not far — fifty paces, maybe, to the lip of the ravine behind the tollhouse, where the world fell away into white and the frozen bells of the road rang faintly below. The sky was still starless. Three nights now, and the dark overhead felt less like absence and more like intention, like a held breath.

She tried to hate her mother. She reached for it the way you reach for a wall in the dark, out of habit, expecting it to be there.

It wasn't.

In its place was something far more unbearable: the image of a woman kneeling on floorboards, crying silently, saying *remember that I stood in front of it* — and meaning, all along, *I stood in front of it by putting you behind it*. Protection disguised as abandonment. Sacrifice disguised as betrayal. And underneath it all, the cruelest mercy of all: letting Oscar believe herself unloved, because a daughter who believed herself unloved would stay safely in exile, while a daughter who knew the truth would run straight into the jaws of the thing that had eaten her sister.

*You are loud, my love. Loud things get collected.*

"Well," Oscar said to the starless sky, and her breath came out in a plume of silver that the wind caught and tore apart. "You should have let me be collected."

The words came out with weight. Real weight. The snow around her feet shivered, lifted a half-inch, hung there trembling — and the air pressure dropped so fast her ears popped, and somewhere beneath the mountain, miles north, she felt the soul-thread pull taut like a harp string finding its note.

Fragments of Wren surfaced, unbidden. Not memories — *impressions*, carried on the thread the way scent is carried on wind. Glass. Cold light. Watching, always watching, through panes that never quite blurred. And threaded through it, fainter than heartbeat:

*Don't.*

Oscar's fists clenched. Snow swirled upward around her in a slow spiral, defying the wind, obeying something older than wind.

"No," she said aloud, to the thread, to the mountain, to the mother who had decided eleven years ago what Oscar was allowed to sacrifice. "You don't get to choose for me either. None of you do."

The spiral collapsed. The snow fell back. And in the ringing silence after, Sefa's hand settled on her shoulder — light, careful, the touch of a man who had learned exactly how dangerous she was and chosen to stand beside her anyway.

"We move at first light," he said. "The thread strengthens every hour. Whatever's pulling you — it's close enough now to feel us too."

Below them, far down the frozen road, a bell rang.

Then another.

Then a dozen, all at once, though there was no wind — a sound like the world clearing its throat, or like something vast turning its attention toward two small figures standing at the edge of a ravine, and beginning, unhurried, to walk toward them through the snow.
