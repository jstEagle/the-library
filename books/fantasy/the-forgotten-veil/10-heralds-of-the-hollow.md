## 10. Heralds of the Hollow

The storm found Oscar before she knew it was coming.

That was the shame of it — three days beyond the veil, and her storm-sense still arrived late, like a guest who'd forgotten the address. In the valley she had spent twenty years pretending the silence in her bones was natural, a scar from childhood, nothing more. Now, with the whole sky gone dark and the frost climbing the mountains like a slow tide, that silence felt less like a scar and more like a locked door she'd been standing in front of her entire life without once trying the handle.

She tried it now, walking at the rear of their small procession, Ilo's small hand tucked in hers. *Reach,* Sefa kept saying. *Not with your eyes. With whatever it is you've been strangling since you were six.* So she reached — gingerly, the way you touch a stove to learn how hot it is — and what she felt made her stomach drop.

Weather. Not above them. *Behind* them.

Something was moving through the frozen country at their backs, and it was not wind, because wind did not have intent. It pressed against the edge of her awareness like a thumb against a bruise: heavy, patient, wrong. The air behind them tasted of iron filings and old snow, of cellars and shut eyes.

"Sefa." Her voice came out flatter than she wanted. "How fast can heralds travel?"

He stopped so abruptly that Ilo walked into his pack. He didn't turn around right away. When he did, his face had gone the color of the ice on the river they'd crossed that morning — not pale exactly, but *translucent*, as if something inside him had stepped back from his own features.

"Where," he said.

"South-southwest. Maybe two miles. Maybe less." She flexed her fingers, feeling for the pressure again, hating how much it felt like reaching into a stranger's pocket. "Three of them. I think. It's hard to count something that doesn't want to be counted."

Sefa looked at the sky — the starless, moonless, utterly black sky that had not changed in nine days — and then at Ilo, who was watching them both with the terrible attentiveness of children who have already learned that adults lie to soften things.

"How long have you known how to do that?" Sefa asked quietly.

"Since last night."

"Last night."

"You said reach. I reached." She lifted her chin. "I'm a fast study when the alternative is dying."

Something flickered across his face — pride, maybe, or its close cousin, fear — and then he was all business again, unshouldering his pack, pulling out the battered tin compass he consulted even though its needle had spun uselessly since the stars went out. "There's a gorge ahead. Old riverbed. The walls will break up their line of sight and muffle whatever they use instead of sight. If we move now—"

"They'll just follow us into the gorge," Oscar said.

"Yes." He met her eyes. "But they'll follow us *downhill*, and downhill is where storms are born."

---

They made the gorge in under an hour, moving at the pace of a child's shortest legs, which meant Oscar spent the whole walk doing arithmetic she hated: how far could they get if they left Ilo hidden somewhere? How much faster without the boy's mittened hand in hers? And every time the answer came back *miles*, she pushed the thought down where it couldn't breathe, because the day she started calculating children as weight was the day she became the kind of court that sold souls by the pound.

The gorge was a wound in the plain, thirty feet deep, its walls layered slate and frozen clay, its floor a ribbon of ice where the river still ran underneath, muttering. They followed it north, boots crunching, breath hanging in flags behind them. Ilo had stopped asking questions an hour ago, which frightened Oscar more than any question could have. Children went quiet when they were conserving themselves. Children went quiet when they had learned that answers cost too much.

The pressure behind them grew.

Oscar felt it the way you feel a headache starting — not pain yet, but the *shape* of pain, arriving. Three points of cold attention, sliding along the rim of the world. She tried to describe it to herself and failed; the closest she could come was the sensation of being watched through frosted glass by someone who had all the time in creation.

Then the light changed.

It didn't brighten. Nothing had brightened since the Night Without Stars. But the blackness ahead of them *organized* itself, gathered into shapes, and three figures stood on the gorge rim above them, silhouetted against a darkness darker than themselves.

Oscar had seen the first herald only as a glimpse, days ago, on the Road of Frozen Bells — a tall shape in the distance, motionless among the frost-killed trees, gone when she blinked. Now she saw them properly and wished she hadn't.

They were human-shaped the way a coat is body-shaped. Long, grey, seamless robes that moved without wind. No faces that she could find — just smooth expanses where faces should be, and yet she felt, with absolute certainty, that they were looking directly at her. At *her*. Not at Sefa, not at Ilo. The attention of all three settled on her like snowfall, soft and total and suffocating.

"Hollow Court," Sefa breathed. He put himself between them and Ilo, hands raised, palms out — an old gesture, Oscar realized, a ward-smith's gesture, the posture of a man who had once stood in rooms where such things were negotiated. "They won't speak first. They're waiting to see if we run. Running tells them everything."

"What does it tell them?"

"That we're afraid. That we're mortal. That we can be herded." His voice was steady but his hands weren't. "Stand still. Say nothing. They're here for you, Oscar. They've been tracking your magic since the veil broke — a storm-caller loose in the world is a beacon, and they don't need wards to read one."

*I'm a beacon.* The words landed in her chest and sat there, cold. All her life she had hidden, dimmed, buried — and the one time she let herself burn, the whole dark sky turned to look.

The center herald took a step down the gorge wall. Its feet found purchase on sheer slate as though the stone had been asked politely. When it moved, the air around it frosted in patterns — spirals, sigils, contracts written in rime — and Oscar's storm-sense screamed a warning she had no vocabulary for.

And then, beneath the warning, something else. A thread. Thin as spider silk, running from her sternum toward the mountain, toward the south — and the heralds stood *across* it. Standing between her and Wren.

Every rational plan she had — stand still, say nothing, be a pebble — evaporated.

"No," she said aloud, and her voice came out doubled, herself and something vaster speaking through the same throat.

The heralds stopped.

The air changed pressure. Her ears popped. Somewhere beneath the ice, the buried river stuttered in its course, and Oscar felt — rising through her boots, through her bones, through twenty years of dammed-up silence — the storm.

It wasn't summoned. Summoning implied choice. It simply *arrived*, the way grief arrives, the way her sister's name used to arrive unbidden in quiet rooms: all at once, everywhere, hers. Clouds she couldn't see in the starless dark churned overhead. Static crawled up her arms and lifted the fine hairs there. The temperature dropped so fast her next breath crackled.

"*Oscar.*" Sefa's voice, sharp with a new kind of fear. "Whatever you're doing — stop. You don't know what you're calling. You don't know where it will *land*—"

She didn't know. That was the truth of it, the ugly truth she'd been outrunning since the valley gates fell: she had spent her whole life suppressing this thing and had never once been taught to hold it. It was a sword she'd carried sheathed for twenty years, and now it was out, and her hands were shaking, and the blade did not care whom it found.

Lightning took the gorge.

Not a bolt — a *web*, branching white fire that leapt from cloud to wall to ice, and the world went silent and enormous. She heard nothing after that, not the thunder, not Sefa shouting, not the buried river boiling through cracks her lightning split in the stone. She saw the heralds stagger, robes smoking, faceless heads thrown back — saw one of them simply come apart, unravelling into threads of grey that the wind took — and then her vision cleared enough to see the other thing.

Ilo.

The boy had been standing near the gorge wall when the lightning webbed down it. He was on the ground now, on his side, and the slate around him was fused glass, and there was a smell in the air that Oscar knew from the beacon-farm, from the day a strike had hit the drying shed — scorched wool, scorched hair, scorched—

She was moving before she decided to move. She slid down the ice on her knees, gathered him up, and his eyes opened — thank every forgotten star, his eyes opened — dazed, unfocused, one pupil blown wide. There was a burn along his jaw and down his neck, angry red, and his little fist was clenched around the hem of her coat with the desperate strength of someone holding onto the only solid thing in a world that had betrayed him twice.

"I'm sorry," she said, and her voice was wrecked, half thunder still. "Ilo, look at me, I'm sorry, I'm sorry—"

His mouth moved. No sound came out. The lightning had taken his hearing along with everything else, at least for now, and his face crumpled as he understood it — a child discovering a new way the world could take things from him — and Oscar held him while he cried silently, which was somehow worse than any sound.

Above them, the remaining heralds withdrew. No retreat she could see; they simply thinned, like ink in water, until the darkness was only darkness again. They had seen what they came to see. A storm-caller, untamed. A weapon that wounded its own people.

A weapon that could be *aimed*.

---

They made camp in a hollow beneath an overhang, as deep in the gorge as shelter allowed. Sefa treated Ilo's burns with salve from a tin that was running low, working in silence, his movements precise and gentle. Ilo sat very still and watched the man's hands, and occasionally his eyes drifted to Oscar, and each time they did, something in her chest tore a little further.

She sat apart, knees drawn up, watching the starless dark. The storm had left her wrung out and humming, every nerve a struck string. She could still feel it in her teeth — the power, vast and eager, coiled just below her skin like a river under ice. *Come back,* it seemed to say. *Let me out again. I was only warming up.*

That was the horror of it. Some part of her had *enjoyed* it.

Sefa finished with the salve, wrapped Ilo in both blankets, and got him lying down against the packs. Then he crossed the hollow and crouched in front of Oscar, and she saw that the gentleness was gone from his face. What remained was harder and older and much more tired.

"We need to talk about what happened."

"I saved us."

"You nearly killed him." He said it quietly, without heat, which made it worse than shouting. "Another foot, Oscar. One foot to the left and we're burying a child tomorrow morning. You didn't aim that storm. You didn't shape it. You opened a door and let a hurricane through and prayed it would land on the monsters."

"It *did* land on the monsters."

"One of them. Two walked away. And they walked away knowing exactly what you are and exactly how sloppy you are." He ran a hand over his face. "I've seen storm-callers before. In the Court's service, decades ago. They were leashed with iron and years of training, and even then, even *then*, the Court kept them away from anything they valued. You are carrying more raw power than any of them had, and you have the control of a frightened girl, because no one ever taught you, because everyone who should have taught you was too busy hiding you."

The words hit the bruise dead center. "You think I don't know that?"

"I think you don't act like you know it." He leaned closer. "I watched you today. When the heralds appeared, you didn't think about Ilo. You thought about the soul-thread. About Wren. The storm answered *that* — your wanting, your fury — and Ilo was simply standing in the geography of your grief."

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. Most true things aren't." He glanced back at the sleeping boy. "Here is what happens now. Tomorrow we train. Every morning, every evening, however many miles we make. You learn to call a single spark and put it out. You learn to feel weather without becoming it. You learn the difference between wanting and raging, because storms obey want — rage just aims them at whatever's nearest."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you leave." He said it flatly, no cruelty in it, which was somehow the crueler kindness. "Not because I'm punishing you. Because I cannot march a child into the Hollow Court's front door behind someone whose power might kill him before the guards get the chance. I won't do it. I've buried enough people to the arithmetic of good intentions."

Oscar looked past him at Ilo — asleep now, breath shallow but even, the burn salve gleaming on his jaw. *Sold its warmth to the Court for survival,* the villages had. *Complicity everywhere, judgment nowhere easy.* And here she was, the judge with lightning in her fists, having judged no one but nearly executed a child anyway.

"When my magic came," she said slowly, "in the valley, when I was small — my mother didn't teach me. My father didn't either. They were terrified of it. Of *me*. And then the court came for Wren instead, and I told myself for twenty years that exile was protection." Her laugh came out cracked. "Maybe they weren't protecting the valley from me. Maybe they were protecting me from ever learning what I am. Because what I am is *this*."

Sefa was quiet a moment. "Or," he said, "they were cowards, and cowardice dressed itself up as love, the way it always does. Either way — you're not six years old anymore, and no one is going to hide you from yourself. Not the valley. Not your mother. Not me." He stood, joints cracking in the cold. "Sleep. Dawn comes early, and your first lesson is going to hurt worse than the heralds did."

He rolled into his blankets. Oscar stayed sitting, watching the dark, feeling the storm turn over beneath her skin like something dreaming.

*Don't come,* Wren had said, clear as chapel bells, three nights ago through the soul-thread. Oscar had told herself it was despair talking, a broken thing begging not to be hoped for. But lying in the dark with the smell of scorched wool in her nose and a burned child breathing ten feet away, another reading arrived, unwelcome and patient:

Maybe Wren hadn't been saying *don't save me.*

Maybe she'd been saying *don't come until you can control what you bring.*

Oscar closed her eyes. Beneath her ribs, faint but unmistakable, the soul-thread pulled south — and tonight, for the first time, she thought she felt impatience in it. Not Wren's.

The mountain's. Something down there had noticed the storm in the gorge. Something down there had liked what it saw.

And far off, at the edge of her new sense, weather was gathering that had no business existing in a world with no sun — a vast, slow spiral of cold, turning above the mountain like a crown deciding whose head it belonged on.
