## 11. Lessons in Thunder

The gorge had been a river once. Sefa could still read its bones — the smooth-worn walls, the terraces of stone where waterfalls had stepped down in some warmer century, the pale silt lines drawn across the rock like rings in a felled tree. Now it was a corridor of ice, a quarter mile of frozen silence between two shoulders of the mountain, and the wind that funneled through it made a sound like a choir holding one long, dying note.

He had chosen it for exactly that sound.

"Again," he said.

Oscar Galloway stood thirty paces down the gorge from him, boots planted on blue ice, her breath tearing out of her in white rags. She was thinning. He'd been watching it happen for three days now — the way she drew on the storm the way a starving woman draws on her own muscle, burning herself as fuel because no one had ever taught her there was another way. Her knuckles were split. There was frost in her eyebrows that hadn't melted since morning.

"I *am* doing it," she said.

"You're doing what you've always done." He kept his voice level. He had learned, in his years beneath the Hollow Court, that the worst teachers were the ones who shouted; shouting gave the student something to push against, and pushing was the whole problem. "You're angry at the storm until it answers out of fear. That's not calling. That's provoking."

"What's the difference? It comes either way."

"The difference," Sefa said, "is whether you can tell it where to go."

She glared at him. Even half-frozen and furious, she had a presence that reminded him uncomfortably of the heralds — not the hollowed ones, but the ones who had been made from people who refused to be emptied. He turned that thought over and set it aside. Later. Everything was later, now.

"Show me again," she said, grudging.

So he did what he had sworn he would never do again in front of another living soul: he reached into the air and asked it for something small.

It was not magic the way Oscar's valley had taught her to fear magic — no flash, no thunderclap. The wind in the gorge simply changed its mind. The long dying note bent, softened, became a low even breath moving from wall to wall, stirring the frost off the stones in little silver ghosts. Sefa held it there, gentle as cupped water, and let her watch.

"I'm not commanding it," he said. "I'm wanting it. There's a difference, and the Court never understood it, which is why they have to steal power instead of growing it. Want is a door. Anger is a battering ram. You can get through a door with a ram, but you'll never close it behind you."

"And if I don't want anything?" she asked quietly. "What if all I want is to find my sister?"

The wind shivered. Not from him.

Sefa crossed the ice toward her, slow, deliberate, the way you approached a spooked animal or an unstable ward. Up close he could see how tired she was — the bruised skin under her eyes, the tremor in her hands she kept trying to hide by making fists. Three days since the heralds' ambush. Three days since her uncontrolled storm had thrown Ilo against a rock face hard enough to crack a rib, and Sefa had stood between them afterward and told her, in words he'd rehearsed and hated, *learn control or leave.*

She had stayed. He still didn't know if he was glad.

"Then we start there," he said. "Want is honest. So be honest. What do you actually want, right now, this second? Not the grand thing. The small true thing."

Oscar looked past him, up the gorge, to where the sky showed as a strip of iron-grey between the walls. No stars. There were no stars anywhere anymore, and every hour they didn't act the crown beneath the mountain grew heavier, the winter grew deeper, and somewhere in the dark Wren Galloway's soul sat in glass, awake, aware, watching.

"I want to hear her voice again," Oscar said. "Just once. Just to know she's—"

The storm answered before he could stop it.

It came up out of the ground, not the sky — a groan through the ice, a shudder along the whole length of the gorge, and the frost-ghosts rose off the walls in a spiral around her like startled birds. Sefa felt his teeth ache. The pressure of it shoved him back two steps on the slick stone, arms out for balance, and Oscar stood in the middle of it with her eyes wide and wet and her hands open at her sides, not fighting it, not shaping it, just *wanting*, and the storm wanted with her so hard the ice screamed.

"*Down,*" Sefa said. "*Oscar — down —*"

She heard him. He saw her hear him, saw her grab for the door instead of the ram, and the spiral slowed, wavered, collapsed inward and went out like a candle pinched between fingers. The silence afterward rang worse than the noise. Somewhere above them a shelf of snow let go and slid down the far wall in a long soft sigh.

Oscar was shaking. "I didn't call that."

"No," Sefa said. "You meant it, though. That's worse. That's better." He rubbed his jaw. "That's the lesson. Your want is louder than your anger ever was. Which means when you learn to aim it, you'll be stronger than anything I trained at Court. And it means—" he hesitated, then said it plainly, because she deserved plainness, "—it means you're dangerous to us until you do. Ilo cracked a rib because your grief wanted out and had nowhere to go."

"I know what I did."

"Do you?" He hadn't meant it as cruelty. It came out anyway, and he let it stand, because the truth was a toll neither of them could keep refusing to pay. "Then here's the last piece. Storms obey want. Souls obey want too. You've been feeling the thread since the night the stars died — pulling at you like a fishhook. You've been fighting it because you're afraid of what it costs to follow. But there's a way to touch it without following. To listen without answering."

"How?"

"Stand still," Sefa said. "And want nothing but her."

* * *

She closed her eyes.

He watched her find it — watched the change come over her face the way weather comes over a horizon, first a darkening, then a shape. The thread. He had seen soul-work done in the Court's deep rooms, had built anchors himself in the years he did not talk about, and he knew what it looked like when a living person touched a stolen soul across the distance: like watching someone press their palm flat against the other side of a frozen lake, feeling for a hand that might not be there.

The wind died completely. The gorge went so quiet he could hear the ice settling, tick and groan, tick and groan, the mountain's old joints complaining in its sleep.

Frost bloomed outward from Oscar's boots in a perfect ring.

"Sefa," she whispered. Her eyes were still closed. "I can feel her. She's — oh, gods, she's *close*, she's closer than I thought, she's—"

"Listen," he said softly. "Don't pull. Don't answer. Just listen."

And Oscar Galloway, standing in a dead riverbed under a starless sky, heard her sister speak for the first time in fifteen years.

Sefa couldn't hear it. He only saw it land — saw her whole body flinch as though struck, saw her hand come up to her mouth, saw the tears freeze in her lashes almost before they fell. He saw her lips move, shaping a name. *Wren.* He saw her take one step forward, then another, toward the mountain, toward the dark, dragged by a hook no one else could see—

"Wren—"

And then Oscar stopped. Stopped so abruptly she swayed, as if the thread had gone slack, as if the voice on the other end had pulled away.

Her eyes opened. They were the grey of the sky and absolutely empty of everything except what she'd just heard.

"She said," Oscar began, and her voice broke, and she started again, flat and careful, reciting it like a ward-formula, like something that would hold its shape if she didn't breathe on it wrong. "She said, *Don't come.*"

The words dropped into the gorge and lay there on the ice between them.

"That's not possible," Sefa said slowly. "The Court keeps them asleep. If she's awake enough to speak, she's awake enough to—"

"To mean it." Oscar's jaw tightened. "She knows I'm coming. She can feel me the way I feel her. And she said *don't come*, Sefa. Fifteen years. Fifteen years I thought she was gone, sold, silent — and the first thing she says to me is *don't come.*"

"There are reasons a prisoner says that," Sefa said carefully. "Reasons that aren't what they seem. Bait. Leverage. They might be using her voice—"

"They're not." She said it with terrible certainty. "It was her. It was *her*, the way she used to say it when Mother sent us to bed and I'd try to sneak into her room. *Don't come, Oscar.* Like I was eight years old again." A sound escaped her that was almost a laugh and was mostly grief. "Like I was always going to follow her somewhere and she was always going to tell me not to."

"Then why are you smiling?"

Oscar touched her own mouth as if surprised to find it curved. She looked down at the ring of frost, at the ghost of the storm still trembling in the air, and when she spoke again her voice had changed — steadier, harder, lit from underneath like ice over running water.

"Because she's alive. Because she's awake. Because she lied to me, which means she thinks lying will work, which means she thinks there's still something left to protect." She turned to face him fully. "Wren spent her whole life telling me not to come after her. Every time. Every single time, right up until the night they took her. And every single time, Sefa, I came anyway. And every single time, she was glad."

The wind moved through the gorge, low and even, a held breath.

"So that's the lesson, isn't it?" Oscar said. "Storms obey want. Fine. Then teach me to want *loudly*. Because tomorrow we walk into the mountain, and I am going to break every promise she ever made me keep."

Sefa looked at her — this exiled, exhausted, half-frozen woman who had just been warned away by the only voice in the world she loved — and felt the old familiar weight settle onto his shoulders, the weight of knowing exactly what came next, because he had helped build the machine at the end of this road and he knew, better than anyone breathing, what waited inside it.

"Don't come," he repeated quietly. Testing the words. Hearing, in them, the echo of every warning he himself had ignored, once, in a life he was still paying for.

"Tomorrow," Oscar said, already turning toward the camp, toward Ilo, toward south and the drowned shafts and the dark. "At dawn. Teach me the rest."

Above the gorge, in the iron-grey strip of sky, the clouds began — very faintly, very briefly — to glow.

Not lightning. Something older. Something answering.

Sefa stared up at it, and his blood went cold, because he recognized the pattern in those clouds, the geometry of the glow, and it was not weather at all.

It was a summons. And it was addressed to her.
