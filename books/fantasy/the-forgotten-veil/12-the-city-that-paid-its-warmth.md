## 12. The City That Paid Its Warmth

The city announced itself by smell.

Ilo smelled it before he saw it: woodsmoke and bread and roasting meat, the warm animal stink of too many bodies in too little space. After three weeks on the Road of Frozen Bells, where every village had been a mausoleum of frost and every chimney cold as a grave-marker, the smell hit him like a slap. His mouth watered so suddenly it hurt.

"Smoke," Sefa said, reining up beside him. The ward-smith's voice was flat, careful, the way it always went flat when his mind had already run ahead to the worst answer. "Real smoke. From real fires."

Oscar pulled her hood back. Her face was wind-scoured and gray-lipped, but her eyes went bright at the haze rising over the hills ahead. "A city that size should have burned through its stores months ago."

"It did," Ilo said quietly.

They both looked at him. He hadn't meant to say it out loud. It had just come out, the way things sometimes came out of him now — knowledge he didn't remember learning, arithmetic he'd watched his mother do at their kitchen table with a stub of pencil and a ledger she hid under the floorboards when the collectors came.

"You know this place," Oscar said. Not a question.

"Carrowmere." The name tasted like old porridge and worse memories. "My mother's sister lived here. Aunt Vess. She used to send us honey-cakes at midwinter." He stared at the smoke plumes, fat and lazy and *wrong* against the white sky. "Carrowmere signed early. Before the frost even reached the valley roads. My mother said—" He stopped. Swallowed. "My mother said Carrowmere would sell its own shadow if the price was right. She said it like a joke. Nobody laughed."

Sefa's jaw tightened. "If they're burning wood for warmth and cooking meat in the open, they have a contract. A standing one." He turned his horse toward the road. "Contracts leave signatures. I want to see what kind."

"And I want bread," Oscar said, and there was something almost fierce in how ordinary it sounded, as if wanting bread were an act of defiance against the whole dying world.

Ilo followed them down toward the gates, and all the way he kept doing the math he couldn't stop doing. A city of eight thousand souls. Ten years of summer, bought and paid for. And somewhere in the fine print, written in a hand that never showed itself, the price.

* * *

The gates of Carrowmere stood open.

That was the first wrong thing. Every settlement they'd passed since the stars died had been barred and barricaded, doors nailed shut, watchmen with crossbows trembling on the walls. But Carrowmere's gates swung wide, and the guards beside them weren't holding weapons. They were holding scales.

Little brass ones, the kind fishmongers used. Each traveler who entered set whatever they carried onto one pan — a knife, a ring, a bag of dried apples — and the guard studied the balance, consulted a slate, and either waved them through or named a price.

"They're weighing *possessions*," Oscar murmured.

"No," said the nearest guard, who had heard. He was young, pink-cheeked from the warmth radiating off the braziers behind him, and he smiled with the easy confidence of someone who had not been afraid in a very long time. "Weighing is free. Entry's free too, if you've nothing worth taking. We're not monsters." He gestured them forward with genuine courtesy. "Welcome to Carrowmere. Last warm city in the world, some say. Mind you don't mention the weather indoors — it's considered poor manners."

Inside the walls, Ilo stopped breathing for a moment.

Market stalls. Actual market stalls, canopied in dyed canvas, piled with root vegetables and salted fish and wheels of pale cheese. Children ran shrieking between the rows playing a game with knucklebones. An old woman argued cheerfully with a butcher about the price of tripe. Music drifted from somewhere — a fiddle, badly played, joyfully played. Steam rose from cook-shops in fragrant columns, and the light of a hundred lanterns glowed amber against the early dusk, and above it all, where the stars should have been, there was only black.

It was like walking into a painting of the world as it used to be, hung slightly crooked on the wall of hell.

"How," Oscar breathed. Her storm-sense must have been screaming; Ilo could see the tension in her shoulders, the way her fingers curled like she was gripping invisible lightning. "How are they *warm*?"

Ilo didn't answer. He was looking at the doorways.

Every doorway in the main square had a small brass plate screwed beside it, polished bright, each engraved with the same words in different hands:

*This household stands in good standing with the Court of Seasons.*

And beneath the words, a number. Some plates read 1. Some read 2. One grand house near the fountain bore a 7, its plate framed in silver.

"What are the numbers?" Oscar asked.

Ilo's voice came out very small. "Shares."

Sefa had gone still in the way he did when he was assembling something terrible out of small pieces. "Shares of what?"

But Ilo couldn't answer that either, because at that moment a woman stepped out of the crowd and said his name.

"Ilo?"

She was older than he remembered, heavier, wrapped in a fox-fur coat that probably cost more than his village's entire winter harvest. Her hair was pinned up with lacquered combs. Her cheeks were round and rosy and well-fed. But he knew the shape of her mouth, because it was his mother's shape, because it was the family shape, and his stomach dropped through the frozen ground.

"Aunt Vess," he said.

* * *

She took them home. Of course she did. Family was family, even now — *especially* now, she said, squeezing his arm hard enough to bruise, saying he was skin and bone and frost-bitten and *where is your mother, child, where is Marisol*, and Ilo said *dead, the frost took her, the frost took everyone,* and Vess made a sound like a hiccup and then walked faster, as if speed could outrun grief.

Her house was the third-warmest place Ilo had ever been, after the bakery ovens of his childhood and the moment before the Hollow Court's heralds froze his village. A fire crackled in every hearth. There was a roast on the table. There was *fruit* — actual oranges, their skins bright as little suns, sitting in a bowl like an accusation.

Oscar stood in the doorway of the parlor and looked at the oranges for a long time. Ilo watched her decide not to say anything. He loved her a little for it.

Vess fed them. She wouldn't be refused. She piled their bowls and refilled them and talked the whole time — about the harvest festival they'd managed to hold last autumn, about her son Tam who had an apprenticeship with the glasswrights, about how bad things were *out there*, truly terrible, she'd heard whole towns gone silent, and wasn't it fortunate, wasn't it providence, that Carrowmere had always been prudent. Sensible. Forward-thinking.

"The council signed before the first frost," she said, ladling gravy. "That's the thing people don't understand. Everyone else waited until they were desperate. Desperation gets you terrible terms. We negotiated from strength. Eleven percent below the regional standard, and a fixed term — ten years, guaranteed summer, no escalations. Your mother never understood finance, sweetling. She thought saving was something you did with grain."

Ilo put down his spoon. "What did we buy the summer with?"

Vess's ladle paused, just for a heartbeat, above the tureen.

Then it resumed, smooth as water. "Payments," she said. "Installments. The Court structures everything in installments these days, very modern, very humane. Nothing anyone needs to worry their head about at dinner."

"Shares," Ilo said. "The numbers on the doors. Shares of what, Aunt Vess?"

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked. Vess set the ladle down with exaggerated care, and when she turned to him her face had rearranged itself into kindness, the particular soft kind cruelty wears when it wants to be mistaken for love.

"You're tired," she said. "You've walked through the dead lands and seen horrors, and you're sitting in a warm house eating hot food, and your grief is looking for someone to bite. I understand that. I forgive it." She touched his cheek. Her hand was warm. Everything about her was warm. "Eat your supper, Ilo."

He ate his supper. He hated himself for it, and he ate it anyway, because his body had spent three weeks starving and bodies don't care about morality. Across the table, Oscar watched him with an expression he couldn't read, and Sefa watched Vess the way he watched wards — looking for the flaw, the seam, the place where the lie was joined to the truth.

* * *

They were given beds. Real beds, with feather mattresses and clean linens that smelled of lavender. Ilo lay in the dark listening to the house settle around him, warm as a living animal, and he did not sleep.

Near midnight, he heard footsteps on the stairs. Soft ones, practiced ones. He rose and followed them down through the kitchen, past the banked fire, to the cellar door, which stood ajar with candlelight leaking through.

His aunt was counting.

Not money. She sat at a scarred worktable with a ledger open before her — a ledger Ilo recognized, because it was the same make as the one his mother had hidden under the floorboards, heavy black covers, cream pages ruled in columns — and she was moving small brass tokens from one dish to another. Each token was stamped with a number.

The cellar door creaked under his hand and she looked up. To her credit, she didn't startle. She just looked at him for a long moment, and then moved a second chair out from the table with her foot.

"Sit, if you're going to stand there judging me," she said. "Judging is free. Everything else costs."

He sat. The ledger lay open between them. He looked at it, because he couldn't not look.

Columns of names. Hundreds of names, in dozens of different hands, added across years. Beside each name, a date. Beside each date, a number. And beside each number, in a smaller, neater script — the Court's script, Ilo realized, the same elegant slanting hand from the contract-tokens the tollhouse had accepted — a single word:

*Rendered.*

"Aunt Vess," he said. "What is this?"

"Household accounts." She said it steadily, but her thumb was rubbing the edge of the page, back and forth, back and forth. "Every household in good standing keeps one. It's how the payments are tracked. You think the Court sends bill-collectors? No. We keep our own books. Self-reporting. Very efficient." She turned the ledger toward him. "Do you know what a share is, Ilo? Since you asked at dinner, since you're going to keep asking until someone tells you. A share is a unit of warmth-credit. One share equals one person's seasonal contribution."

"Contribution of *what*."

"Of themselves." Her voice didn't waver. That was the worst part. It didn't waver at all. "When the council signed, the contract specified renderings in installments. Quarterly, adjusted for population. A rendering is—" She stopped rubbing the page. "A person, Ilo. A rendering is a person. Their soul goes to the Court, and their body... the body goes into the furnaces under the mountain. Soul-fire burns hotter than coal. Cleaner. The Court uses it to forge, and we get our ten years of summer, and the arithmetic works out to roughly four hundred renderings per decade for a city our size. Four hundred, spread across eight thousand households. Most families never lose anyone. Most families contribute zero shares their entire lives. The numbers fall on—" she waved a hand, "—the unregistered. Transients. The elderly without family to advocate. Criminals. Outsiders."

"Outsiders," Ilo repeated. "Like travelers at the gate."

"Weighed on entry," Vess agreed softly. "Assessed. If you carry nothing of value, you're not worth a rendering — the processing costs more than the yield. But if you're young and healthy and alone..." She shrugged, and the fox fur rippled on her shoulders. "The guards mark you. The collectors come within the week. It's nothing personal. It's *arithmetic*."

Ilo looked at the ledger. At the names. He found, near the bottom of the current page, three entries in fresh ink, dated two days ago. Travelers' names he didn't recognize. Assessed value: moderate. Status: pending.

"You knew my mother sold me," he said. "Didn't you. When the collectors came to our village. You knew what a rendering was, and you let her sign."

Vess flinched. It was the first true thing her face had done all night. "Your mother sold *half* of you," she said. "Half-shares. A child's soul split pays less than a whole one, but it lasts longer — the Court renders halves slowly, over years. It was the only way to save anything. If she'd given them all of you, you'd have been furnace-smoke by midwinter. Instead you got—" she gestured at him, at all of him, "—this. Years. A life, most of one. Don't you dare sit in my warm cellar and tell me she was wrong."

"She sold my *soul*."

"She kept you *alive*!" And there it was, finally, the crack in the ledger-voice, the arithmetic breaking open to show the terrified woman underneath. "You think I don't know what it cost? You think I sleep? I signed the household register myself, Ilo. My name is on the contract. Do you know what my share-count is? Zero. Do you know why? Because Tam needed his mother, and the neighbors needed someone to organize the rotation, and someone has to *keep the books* or the Court sends assessors who count *wrong*, who take children from good families instead of drifters no one will miss. Someone has to hold the pen so the pen doesn't fall into worse hands. That's not monstrosity. That's *bookkeeping*. That's keeping the number as low as the number can go!"

"Four hundred people," Ilo whispered. "Per decade. You keep the number low."

"Four hundred people," Vess said, "or eight thousand. Those are the choices. There is no third column. I have looked. For eleven years I have looked, and there is *no third column*."

The candle guttered. In the silence, Ilo became aware of a fourth presence in the cellar — Oscar, standing at the top of the stairs, having heard everything, her face lit half gold and half shadow. Behind her, Sefa. Neither of them spoke.

Vess saw them and closed the ledger with a snap. "Well," she said, recovering, smoothing her coat. "Now you know. So. Will you report me? To whom? The world outside is dying, and this city will be warm for another nine years, and my son will grow up." She stood, and gathered the candle, and paused beside Ilo long enough to press something into his hand — a brass token, stamped with the numeral 1. "Household guest-share," she murmured. "It marks you as accounted-for. Wear it or don't. But if collectors come, show it."

She climbed the stairs. At the top she stopped, without turning around.

"Your mother's ledger is buried under the hearthstone of our old house in Fenwick," she said. "If it survived the frost. She wanted you to have it someday. She said you'd need to see the sums yourself before you'd believe them." A pause. "She was right about you, Ilo. She was right about almost everything. That was her tragedy."

Then she was gone, and Ilo sat in the warm cellar holding a brass share of himself, and Oscar came down the last steps and crouched beside his chair.

"Ilo," she said gently. "The names in that ledger. The pending ones. The travelers marked at the gate."

He looked up. He already knew. Of course he already knew — the arithmetic was simple, and simple sums were the only kind the world had left.

"There are three of us," he said. "Young, healthy, traveling together. No local registration." He turned the token over in his fingers. "We're not guests, Oscar. We're inventory."

Above them, through the floorboards, came the sound of the front bell — deep, silvery, patient — ringing once at midnight, though no hand had touched it.

Sefa appeared in the stairwell, and his face in the candlelight was the color of ash. "That's not the doorbell," he said. "That's a summons-knell. I've heard it before, in another life." He looked at each of them in turn. "The Court's agent is already inside the city. And I think it knows exactly what walked in through its gates tonight."
