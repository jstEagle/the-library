## 13. A Bargain in the Dark

The city of Vell had a way of making Oscar feel like she was walking through someone else's dream.

It was warm here — the only warm place left in a hundred miles — and that warmth had a texture to it, thick and faintly sweet, like air over a candle flame. The streets were clean. The fountains ran unfrozen. Children played in the square outside the inn where they'd taken rooms, and their laughter sounded almost normal, except that it never quite reached the edges of the sound, as if the cold beyond the walls were drinking it.

Everything in Vell was paid for. Oscar could feel it now, the way she'd learned to feel weather: a low pressure behind her sternum, a wrongness in the seams of things. Ten years of summer, bought and paid for, and somewhere beneath the sweetness of the air she could taste what had been traded for it.

She stood at the window of their room on the third floor, watching lamplighters work the street below with long brass poles, and tried not to think about the tollhouse. Tried not to think about the memory she'd handed over for passage — one of Wren, she knew that much, though which one she couldn't say, because the shape of it was gone from her mind like a tooth pulled from her jaw. She kept running her tongue along the gap where it should have been.

"You're doing it again," Sefa said from the corner, where he sat cross-legged with his tools spread out around him, re-grinding the edge of a ward-chisel by lamplight. "Counting your memories."

"I'm not counting them."

"You are. You do it every night since the tollhouse. You go through them like coins." He didn't look up. The chisel whispered against the whetstone. "You'll drive yourself mad doing that."

"Better than driving myself mad *not* doing it."

He said nothing to that, because there was nothing to say. Ilo slept on the far bed, curled small under three blankets, frost-scars still silvering his knuckles where the cold had gotten into him before they pulled him from the bell-road. Even asleep, the boy's hands stayed tucked against his chest, fingers curled around nothing, holding something no one could see.

Oscar turned back to the window.

That was when she saw the woman in the street below, standing beneath the lamp the lighters had just lit, looking up at their window.

She wasn't dressed for the cold, though everyone else in Vell bundled even in the bought warmth, old habits dying hard. Her coat was dark wool, unbuttoned, and her hair was white — not aged-white but true white, the white of frost ferns on glass, and it moved slightly in a wind Oscar couldn't feel through the sealed window. She stood perfectly still while the city flowed around her, vendors and cart-drivers parting without seeming to notice they were parting, and she looked up at Oscar with an expression of mild, patient interest, the way one looks at a shop window.

The lamp between them guttered. For half a heartbeat its light went the color of starlight — that dead, hungry color Oscar had seen on the night everything went dark — and then it steadied.

The woman smiled and beckoned.

* * *

"She's Court," Sefa said flatly, ten minutes later, standing at the window himself. The street below was empty. The woman was gone. "Or she's bait. Either way, you don't go."

"She asked me to come. She didn't ask you."

"That's worse."

"How is that worse?"

"Because it means they know exactly who you are, Oscar. Not the exile. Not the beacon-farmer. They know about the storms. They know about Wren." He said his sister's name carefully, the way he handled the chisel — with respect for how much damage it could do. "They've done their reading. Which means whatever happens tonight, it isn't chance. It's design."

Ilo stirred on the bed. "Then don't go alone," he mumbled, half-asleep, and Sefa shot him a look that said *you're supposed to be unconscious.*

Oscar pulled her coat off its hook. "I'm not going to talk to her. I'm going to find out what she wants. There's a difference."

"There really isn't," Sefa said, but he was already reaching for his own coat, and Oscar felt something twist in her chest — gratitude or guilt, she couldn't tell them apart anymore.

* * *

The woman was waiting in the garden behind the inn, where the hedges grew green in midwinter and roses bloomed out of season, fat and scentless, like wax fruit. Bought summer. It hung over the whole garden like a held breath.

Up close, the woman was beautiful the way ice formations are beautiful — precise, symmetrical, and utterly indifferent to anything alive. Her eyes were pale gray, so light they were nearly colorless, and when she looked at Oscar, Oscar felt the storm-sense flare awake in her bones, screaming a warning she couldn't parse: not danger exactly, but *absence*. Standing near this woman was like standing near a place where weather used to be.

"Oscar Galloway," the woman said. Her voice was pleasant, unhurried, the voice of someone reading terms aloud. "Storm-caller. Exile. Sister." She let each word sit a moment. "My name is Maren. I serve the Hollow Court as an agent of negotiation. I'm told you've been walking toward our mountain for some weeks now, and I thought we might spare one another the inconvenience of the rest of the journey."

"You could have just waited for us."

"We prefer to meet people before the mountain changes them." Maren gestured to a stone bench, dry and warm despite the season. "Please. What I have to offer won't take long, and you'll want to be sitting down."

Sefa stepped forward, and Maren's gaze slid to him with something that was almost pity. "Ward-smith Anwar. You may listen. You may not speak. Those are the terms of parley, and you know them — you served us long enough."

"I know the terms," Sefa said quietly. "I also know they mean nothing."

"They mean everything," Maren said. "That was always your mistake, Sefa. You thought the contracts were tricks. They aren't. We keep our word to the letter. It's the only honest institution left in this world." She turned back to Oscar. "Sit. Please."

Oscar sat. Sefa remained standing behind her, and she was aware of him there the way you're aware of a wall at your back.

Maren reached into her coat and produced a single sheet of paper. It looked ordinary — cream-colored, ruled, the kind of thing any clerk in any city might use — except that it did not move in the windless garden, and when Maren set it on the bench between them, the roses nearest it went brown at the edges, very slightly, as if something had been drawn out of them.

"A contract," Maren said. "Simple terms. Read them if you like; I encourage it. But I'll summarize, because the summary is what matters." She folded her hands. "The Hollow Court holds the soul of Wren Galloway. Your sister is aware. Your sister has been aware for eleven years, Oscar, and I want you to understand that clearly, because I think your mother lied to you about it, and lies make poor foundations for decisions."

The words landed like stones dropped into still water. Oscar heard herself say, "Aware of what?"

"Of everything. Of the vault. Of the crown being forged from the stolen stars. Of you, walking toward her through the snow." Maren's voice didn't soften — softness wasn't in its repertoire — but it slowed, the way one slows speech for the grieving. "She knows you're coming. And she knows what it will cost you, because she watched your mother stand in a room very much like this one, eleven years ago, and refuse the same offer I am about to make you."

The garden seemed to tilt. Oscar gripped the edge of the bench. Behind her, Sefa had gone very still.

"The terms," Maren continued, "are these. You surrender your storm-magic — all of it, freely given, freely signed. In exchange, Wren's soul is released from the vault. Returned to the world. Whole, free, hers again." She paused. "We will even see her settled. A body can be found. A life can be arranged. The Court keeps its word to the letter."

"And the rest of the souls?" Oscar heard her own voice come out strange, thin. "The thousands in the vault. The stars. The winter."

"Not part of this contract."

"So she goes free into a frozen world. Alone. While you forge a crown that ends everything."

"Yes," Maren said simply. "That is the shape of the offer. We are not asking you to save the world, Oscar Galloway. We are offering you your sister. The world was never on the table. It never was — not for your mother, either. That was her tragedy. She thought refusing us was protecting you both. All it did was leave Wren in the dark and you in exile, and the Court with two prizes instead of none."

Oscar stared at the paper. The ink on it seemed to shift when she wasn't looking directly at it, letters rearranging themselves into plainer language, language meant for her: *her name*, her sister's name, the word *freely*, the word *forever*.

"She refused," Oscar said slowly. "Our mother stood in a room like this, and they offered her my magic for Wren's freedom — no. No, wait." Something cold moved through her, colder than the winter outside the walls. "They offered her *my exile*. My safety. That was the deal she took. Sell Wren's soul, buy Oscar's banishment to the valley. That's what Chapter Nine of my life was. So what was the offer she *refused*?"

Maren's expression didn't change, but something behind it did — a flicker of genuine interest, the look of a negotiator whose counterpart has finally read the fine print.

"The original offer," Maren said, "was both daughters' freedom for the mother's own soul. Hers, freely given, in perpetuity. She declined. She counter-offered. One daughter's soul for the other daughter's safety." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "She chose you, Oscar. I've always found that the most interesting part of the story. Everyone assumes the mother sold the elder child to save the younger. But the contract makes it plain. Given the choice between sacrificing herself for both of you, or sacrificing Wren for you alone — she chose you."

The words went through Oscar like a spear finding the gap in armor.

She thought of her mother's face, the last time she'd seen it — drawn, gray, already dying of whatever slow price the contract had levied. She'd spent eleven years building a story out of that face: *she sold Wren to save me, she was weak, she was cruel, she chose.* And now the story inverted itself, and the inversion was worse. Her mother hadn't been choosing between her children. She'd been offered a way to save them both, and she'd flinched from the fire, and she'd pushed her eldest daughter into it instead.

And Wren had known. Had watched. Had been *aware* for eleven years in the dark of a glass cell, knowing her mother chose the storm-child over her.

"Don't come."

The words from the gorge, carried down the soul-thread, clear as a bell across ice. Oscar had taken them for fear for her. Now she heard them differently. *Don't come* — not *it's dangerous*. *Don't come* — because Wren knew what Oscar would be offered. Because Wren knew exactly what she would be asked to sign, and knew her sister, and knew that some part of her would want to sign it.

"Oscar." Sefa's voice, low, careful. "Don't."

"She's been awake for eleven years." Oscar's throat was tight. "Eleven years in the dark, and I'm sitting here with a pen in reach, and you're telling me not to."

"I'm telling you it's a lie."

"It's a *contract*," Maren said mildly. "We don't lie. Ask him." A nod toward Sefa. "Ask him what happened when the Court broke faith with him. It didn't. He broke faith with himself, and blamed us, because blame travels lighter than guilt. The terms are real. Sign, and Wren walks free. Refuse, and she stays in the glass until the crown is finished, and after the crown is finished—" Maren spread her white-haired head in a small, elegant shrug. "After, there is no after. Only winter, forever, and the vault sealed until the sun burns out. These are your options. I didn't build them. I'm merely the messenger who reads them aloud."

Oscar looked at the paper.

Here is the truth she would carry for the rest of her life, the truth she would turn over on sleepless nights in a valley rebuilding its wards: in that moment, she wanted to sign. Not a little. Not briefly. She wanted it with her whole chest, with the full weight of eleven years of exile and grief and the gap in her memory where a sister used to live. The world was ending — she believed that now, believed it in her marrow — and the world's salvation was a maybe, a plan, a terrible cost with no guarantee. But Wren was *certain*. Wren was a name on a line, a signature away, a real and finite trade: her storms for her sister. Power she'd hated, power that had made her a monster in her mother's court and an exile in her mother's valley, power that had nearly killed Ilo on the road — traded for the only person who had ever made her feel like she belonged anywhere at all.

What kind of fool wouldn't sign?

"What happens to her," Oscar asked, "if I die before I sign? On the road. In the mountain. If your heralds kill me next week — what happens to Wren?"

Maren considered the question honestly, which was somehow the most frightening thing she'd done all night.

"She remains in the vault," Maren said. "Until the crown completes. Then the vault's contents are rendered into the forging. It is not painful, if that helps. Souls in glass don't suffer. They simply... stop being separate."

Oscar picked up the pen lying beside the contract. It was black lacquer, warm to the touch, warmer than the bought summer of the garden, warm as a living hand.

Behind her, Sefa made a sound — not a word, just a breath shaped like a plea.

*Ilo's village sold its warmth,* she thought. *Vell fed its neighbors to the Court for a decade of roses. Everyone signs. Everyone signs something, eventually, when the alternative is the person they love in the dark. Maybe that's not evil. Maybe that's just arithmetic.*

She set the nib to the paper.

The ink came out silver, like starlight, like the night the sky went empty, and the contract drank the first stroke greedily, the letters of her name beginning to form—

Fire.

Not heat — light. A sheet of flame swept across the bench, and the contract screamed. There was no other word for it: a high, thin, furious shriek as the paper blackened and curled, the silver ink boiling off like steam, and Maren rose from her seat with the first expression she'd shown all night that was genuinely human — shock.

Sefa stood with his ward-chisel in his fist, and the chisel's edge burned white-hot, and his face was gray with effort and terror and something older than either.

"Ward-smith's privilege," he said hoarsely. "Any contract witnessed by a smith of the old guilds can be contested by fire and iron. Article One. You wrote it yourselves, back when you needed us." His hand shook. The burning page crumbled to ash between them. "I built the flaw in the valley wards, Maren. I've got a debt. This one's mine to pay."

For a long moment, nobody moved. The roses had all gone brown now, every bloom in the garden, as if the contract's death had drawn the last of the bought summer out of them.

Maren straightened her coat. When she spoke again, her voice was as pleasant as ever, and that pleasantness was more terrifying than rage.

"Contested and voided," she acknowledged. "Well done, Anwar. Truly. You've cost us a document and gained yourself a place on the list." Her colorless eyes found Oscar's, and held them, and in them Oscar saw something she hadn't expected: not malice. Patience. The patience of winter itself. "But understand what you've chosen, storm-caller. The offer stands until the crown is finished. After that, there are no offers. There is only the mountain, and the mountain does not negotiate." She began to walk toward the gate, her white hair stirring in wind that touched no one else. At the gate she paused, without turning. "Your mother hesitated too, at the end. Ask him why. Ask him what he told her, all those years ago, that made her refuse to save both her daughters."

Then she was gone, and the ordinary cold came flooding back into the garden like water into a broken hull, and the first true frost of the night crept across the dead roses.

* * *

Oscar didn't remember getting to her feet. She was standing, though, and Sefa was standing in front of her, chisel still glowing, and the space between them was full of everything neither of them was saying.

"You burned it," she said.

"You were going to sign."

"You *burned* it." Her voice climbed. "You made the choice for me. Eleven years, Sefa. Eleven years I've been someone things were decided *for* — exiled for, sold for, protected against my will — and the first time in my life I get to choose something, actually choose, you take the pen out of my hand!"

"If you'd signed, you'd be powerless in a week and she'd still be in the vault! You think they'd honor it? You think—"

"You said it yourself. They keep their word to the letter. You *told* me that's what makes them dangerous!" She was shouting now, and somewhere above them a window banged open, and she didn't care. "And what did she say? What did Maren say at the gate? *Ask him what he told your mother.* What did you tell her, Sefa? What did you tell my mother eleven years ago that made her push Wren into the dark instead of going in herself?"

Sefa went white.

The chisel's glow died. He stood in the frost-killed garden with his mouth open and nothing coming out of it, and Oscar watched the truth arrive in his face before any words did — watched him understand that she understood.

"You were there," she whispered. "You were there when she made the deal. You were the smith. You drew up the contract. You—" The pieces slammed together, ugly and complete. "You designed the valley wards. You built the flaw. You drafted my exile. How deep does it go, Sefa? How much of my life did you *build*?"

"Oscar—"

"*How much?*"

Above them, Ilo appeared at the window, small and frightened, calling their names. Somewhere across the city, a clock began striking midnight, each note falling into the frozen air like a stone into a well. And down the street, at the corner where the lamplight ended, Oscar saw figures standing in the snow — three of them, four, tall and still, wearing the shapes of men the way hollow things wear shapes — watching, waiting, patient as winter.

Sefa followed her gaze. His jaw tightened.

"Not here," he said quietly. "They'll talk to us. Both of us. Everything — I swear it, everything, tonight, on the road. But not here, and not with them listening." He held out his hand, palm up, an offering or a plea. "Trust me one more hour. That's all I'm asking. One more hour, and then you can hate me with the whole story in your hands."

Oscar looked at his open hand. She looked at the heralds at the corner of the dark.

She did not take his hand. But she walked beside him out of the garden, and the snow creaked under their boots like old bones, and behind them the dead roses glittered with frost, and ahead the heralds turned in unison and walked away into the dark — not fleeing, she realized.

Leading.

They were being led somewhere. And the only person who might know where — the only person who had ever worked for the thing that led them — was walking at her side, silent, carrying a story eleven years heavy, and the hour he'd begged for was already bleeding away.
