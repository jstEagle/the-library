## 14. What Sefa Buried

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time Sefa finally spoke.

They had made camp in the lee of a fallen watchtower, one of the old border stations from before the Court's reach had grown teeth, and Ilo was asleep in a nest of blankets with frost still clinging to her eyelashes even this close to the flames. Oscar sat across from him, sharpening a knife she did not need to sharpen, and Sefa understood that she was waiting. She had been waiting for three days. He could feel it the way he could feel a ward about to fail — a pressure building behind the ordinary surface of things.

He should have told her at the tollhouse. He should have told her on the road of frozen bells, when she pulled Ilo out of that silent village and carried the child four miles through snow that bit like iron. There had been a dozen moments when the truth sat in his throat like a swallowed coal, and every time he had chosen the coward's arithmetic: *not yet, not while she needs me, not until we're closer to the mountain.*

But they were close now. Close enough that the mountain's shadow fell over them even in the dark — a blackness deeper than the night around it, as though the peak drank the starlight rather than merely blocking it. And close enough that if Oscar learned what he had done after they went underground, she might do something reckless with her storms, something that would kill them all.

Or worse. She might simply look at him the way she looked at the Hollow Court's heralds.

"Oscar." His voice came out rougher than he intended. He cleared his throat. "There's something you need to hear before we go down there. And you're not going to want to hear it."

The knife stopped moving. She didn't look up. "I've known since the tollhouse," she said. "That you're keeping something. You get this line—" she touched the space between her brows— "right here, when you're deciding whether to lie."

He almost smiled. Almost. "You'd have made a fine ward-smith. Half the work is knowing where the flaw hides."

"Sefa."

"Yes. All right." He fed another branch to the fire and watched it catch, buying himself one more breath of ordinary light. Then he made himself meet her eyes.

"I built the valley wards."

* * *

For a long moment she said nothing at all. The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the dark, ice shifted on a dead tree with a sound like a bone breaking.

"You told me," she said slowly, "that you were exiled from the Court for failing them. That your work was considered... insufficient."

"I said they cast me out for my failures. I didn't say which failure." He turned his hands over, palms up, as if the answer were written there. It wasn't. It never was. The hands that had drawn the valley's protection into being looked like any smith's hands — scarred, burn-marked, steady. "Oscar, the Veiled Valley isn't natural. Nothing that old survives by accident. Someone wove those wards, stone by stone, thread by thread, sixty years ago. Someone designed a veil so complete that storm-magic itself couldn't cross it. A place where a child like you could be hidden from everything."

Her face had gone very still. "You hid me?"

"No." The word came out harder than he meant it to. "I never knew you existed. I built the wards for the Court, Oscar. That's where you have to start, if you're going to understand any of this. I was theirs. Their best. They called me their *architect of quiet places*, and I thought it was an honor." He laughed, and hated the sound of it. "I was young, and they flattered me, and I believed that wards were just wards — neutral things, walls without allegiance. Let them keep something in or keep something out; the craft was the same either way."

"And the valley?"

"The valley was a vault." He watched her understand it, watched the color leave her face in the firelight. "Not a refuge. A repository. The Court needed somewhere to store things that couldn't be kept anywhere else — things too dangerous, too loud, too *alive*. Magic that fought back. Souls that hadn't finished breaking. The veil I built doesn't just block power from entering. It presses down on anything inside it. Dampens it. Year by year, decade by decade, it grinds whatever it holds down to nothing." He spread his hands. "You lived your whole life inside my cage, Oscar. You felt it every day and called it peace."

"That's not—" She stopped. Started again. "The valley is full of people. Families. Children born there, generations who never—"

"I know what it's full of. That was the elegance of it, from the Court's perspective. A vault that fills itself. Every exile, every inconvenient child, every soul the court wanted buried alive but not killed — send them to the Veiled Valley, and the wards will do the rest. No guards needed. No expense. The prisoners' own descendants become the prison staff." He heard his own voice flatten into the register he used for technical reports, the cold professional tone he had never managed to burn out of himself, and forced it warmer. "I didn't design it for people. I need you to believe that. I designed it for *containment*, and I was too arrogant to ask what would be contained. By the time I understood—"

"What flaw?" Oscar said.

He blinked. "What?"

"You said the Court exploited a flaw. Chapter and verse, Sefa. You've been circling it for ten minutes and I can feel you circling it. What did you build wrong?"

And there it was — the anger, arriving at last, clean and bright as a struck blade. He had dreaded this moment for weeks, and now that it stood in front of him he found he was almost grateful. Anger was honest. Anger meant she was still treating him as someone whose actions mattered.

"Not wrong," he said quietly. "Deliberate. That's the part you won't forgive, and you shouldn't. When I finally saw what the valley was — really saw it, twenty years ago, when they sent me back to inspect my own work and I found a family who'd been inside three generations and couldn't remember a single song their grandparents sang — I couldn't tear the wards down. Do you understand? I tried. I stood at the border stones with my tools in my hands and I could not do it, because tearing them down all at once would have killed everyone inside. The veil wasn't just pressing down on the magic. It was woven through the valley's water, its soil, its air. Pull it out fast and the whole valley hemorrhages. So instead I did the only thing I could think of."

"You built a way in," Oscar whispered.

"I built a way in." He nodded. "A single seam. One thread of the weave left deliberately loose, hidden in the third border stone on the western arc, keyed to open only under conditions I chose — a convergence of pressures that occurs naturally once in a generation, or artificially, if someone knows how to force it. A pressure valve. If the day ever came when the valley needed to be emptied quickly — truly emptied, safely — the seam would let the veil bleed off gently instead of shattering. My insurance. My penance, half-finished, buried in stone."

The fire had burned low again. Neither of them moved to feed it.

"And the Court found it," Oscar said.

"They didn't find it. They *remembered* it." His mouth twisted. "I forgot one thing about the Hollow Court, in all my years of hating them. They don't forget anything they've bought. And they had bought my work — every sketch, every draft, every version of the weave I ever committed to paper. Including the drafts. Including the first attempt, the one I abandoned because the seam was too visible." He closed his eyes. "They didn't use my insurance, Oscar. They used my mistake. The early version. The crude one. And they forced it from the outside, on the night they stole the stars, because a collapsing veil makes such beautiful chaos to hide behind. The wards didn't fail. They were opened. With my own signature in the stonework, plain as a confession, for anyone with the learning to read it."

"That's why you recognized the sabotage." Her voice was flat. "At the border stones. Before the stars went out. You knew the cut because you'd made it yourself."

"In a different life. Yes."

* * *

She stood up. He had expected that. She walked to the edge of the firelight and stood looking north toward the mountain, and he let her stand there, because he owed her the silence.

When she turned around, her eyes were wet, and that was worse than the anger.

"My whole life," she said. "Every gray morning on the beacon-farm. Every time I reached for the weather and felt nothing come. Every time I thought something was broken in *me* — that I was a storm that had gone out, a fraud, a girl carrying a title for magic she couldn't perform." Her voice cracked and she pushed through it. "It was your wards. It was always your wards. I wasn't broken, I was *contained*, and I spent twenty years being ashamed of a cage I couldn't see."

"Yes."

"Don't *yes* me." She crossed back toward the fire, and the wind rose with her — a small, unconscious stirring, dry leaves lifting, the flames leaning away from her like startled animals. "My mother sold my sister's soul to put me behind your veil. Do you understand what you're telling me? She paid Wren's soul for my safety, and my safety was a lie. It was a slow erasure. Wren's soul for a cell with a garden in it." She laughed, jagged and awful. "And the Court took both halves of the bargain. They took Wren, and they took the valley, and they used your seam to do it, and now they're forging winter crowns beneath a mountain while I—"

She stopped. Breathed. The wind died down as deliberately as it had risen, and Sefa realized she was reining it in by hand, muscle by muscle, the way he had taught her in the gorge. Control. Want, not anger. She was using his lessons to keep herself from striking him, and some distant, exhausted part of him noted that this was the finest compliment his teaching had ever received.

"Why come back?" she asked at last. Quieter now. Worse than shouting. "If you built the cage, if you built the flaw, why spend years crawling back toward the Court that owns your signature? Why follow me? You could have run south with the valley. You could have vanished. Nobody would ever have known."

"Because running is how I got here." He stayed seated. He would not rise to meet her anger; he would sit in it like a man sitting out a storm, because he had earned no better shelter. "Twenty years ago I saw what the valley was and I ran. I told myself I'd fix it later. I told myself the seam was enough, that someday I'd gather the courage to come back and finish the job. And then I told myself the Court had surely forgotten the valley entirely, that it was a backwater, that my guilt was an old man's vanity." He shook his head. "Guilt is patient, Oscar. It waits exactly as long as you need it to wait, and then it presents the bill. The bill came due the night the stars went dark. I stood on that ridge and watched the wards I built come apart like wet paper, and I watched the cold pour into the valley, and I knew — with absolute certainty, the way you know your own handwriting — that the opening was mine. Not my intention. But mine."

"So this is penance."

"This is penance," he agreed. "And I'm not asking you to forgive it. Forgiveness would be a lie we told each other to make the road easier, and the road ahead is hard enough without lies. I'm telling you because you deserve to walk into that mountain knowing exactly whose hands shaped the locks you're about to break. If you want to leave me here, I'll stay. If you want to take the storms and—" he gestured vaguely at the frozen dark— "*express* your feelings, I'll stand still for it. But don't carry me underground wondering. Wondering gets people killed in the deep places."

* * *

Ilo stirred in her blanket nest, murmured something about bells, and slept on. The mountain's shadow lay across the camp like a held breath.

Oscar crouched by the fire. She picked up a stick and drew in the ash — absent, automatic strokes, and Sefa recognized the pattern with a jolt: she was sketching ward-geometry. The valley's weave, from memory, the way a farm girl who'd never been taught would know it. Living inside it her whole life, her bones had learned its shape.

"You said the seam opens under pressure," she said. "Convergence conditions. Once a generation, or forced."

"Yes."

"Can it be closed?"

He stared at her. Of all the responses he had braced for — fury, grief, dismissal, the cold nod of a commander cutting a liability loose — he had not prepared for the craftsman's question.

"Closed," he repeated.

"Sealed. Properly. Not patched — I've seen patched wards, the elder's people patch the border stones every spring and it's like putting wax on a cracked bell. Sealed. Re-woven. Can the flaw you built be un-built, by the person who built it, with the right materials and enough power?"

Slowly, Sefa set down his cup. "Yes," he said. "Given proper anchor-stone and a source of raw power to bind the new weave — yes. It's the work of a lifetime done properly. Done in haste, it's the work of days, and it will hold for days, maybe weeks. Maybe long enough."

"Long enough for what?"

"For whatever we find under that mountain to matter afterward." He heard his voice change, and marveled at it — the flat report-tone burning away, something older and warmer kindling underneath. Hope, maybe. He'd nearly forgotten its weight. "Oscar. Are you saying—"

"I'm saying I'm angry at you." She dropped the stick into the fire and watched it flare. "I'm furious. You built my cage and then you taught me to fight, and I don't know yet which of those things you are. Maybe both. Maybe that's what people are." She looked up at him across the dying fire, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "But I also know that the only person alive who understands that flaw well enough to seal it is sitting across from me offering to stay behind in the snow. So no. You don't get to stay behind. You don't get to die down there and call it penance. Penance is finishing the work. Penance is sealing the seam behind us so that what happened to my valley can never happen to anyone else's."

Sefa found, to his considerable embarrassment, that he could not speak for a moment. He busied himself with the fire.

"There's a condition," Oscar added.

"Of course there is."

"When this is over — if we live — you teach. Everything. The weave, the flaws, the history, all of it, to anyone in the valley who wants to learn. No more smiths hoarding secrets until the secrets rot. No more single points of failure wearing a person's face." She held out her hand across the fire. "Shared burden or none, Sefa. Those are my terms."

He took her hand. Her grip was warm and callused and strong as a border stone, and for the first time in twenty years, the weight on his shoulders shifted into something he could actually carry.

"Agreed," he said.

She nodded once, businesslike, and began banking the fire for the night watches. And it was Ilo — awake, apparently, and pretending magnificently not to be — who broke the silence from inside her blanket nest, in the small clear voice of a child stating the obvious:

"If the mountain drinks the starlight, does that mean the crown is already lit?"

Sefa went cold. He turned north, toward that blackness deeper than black, and saw what he had been refusing to see all evening: a faint pulse in the heart of the shadow. Rhythmic. Patient. Like a forge breathing.

Like a hammer, falling.
