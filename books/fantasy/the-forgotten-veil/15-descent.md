## 15. Descent

The drowned shafts opened in the mountainside like a wound that had never learned to close.

Oscar stood at the lip of the entrance with her lantern raised and felt the dark look back. The mine-mouth was a ragged oval of black stone, half-swallowed by ice that had grown inward from the edges like frost on a windowpane — delicate, branching, wrong. Behind them, the frozen valley of Hollowmere lay silent under its shroud of unnatural snow. Ahead, the mountain breathed out air so cold it tasted of iron and old promises.

"This is it," Ilo said.

The child's voice didn't waver, and that frightened Oscar more than the dark did. Ilo had been steady since the tollhouse, since the memory they'd paid without flinching, and steadiness in someone so young was not courage — it was damage wearing courage's clothes.

"You're certain this leads down?" Sefa asked. He crouched at the threshold, running his scarred fingers along the ice without touching it, reading it the way he read all wards — like scripture he no longer believed in but couldn't stop reciting.

"The Marrow Company cut these shafts two hundred years ago," Ilo said. "Before the Court bought the mountain. My grandmother kept the old contracts. She used to read them to me like stories." A pause. "They weren't stories."

"Contracts rarely are." Sefa straightened. "Lead the way, then. And if you see anything carved into the walls — any sigil, any script, anything at all — say so before you touch it."

"Why?"

"Because down here, everything is a clause."

* * *

They went in single file: Ilo first with the small lamp the child refused to surrender, then Oscar, then Sefa with his pack of stolen tools and stolen guilt. The shaft sloped gently downward, wide enough for three abreast at first, then narrowing as the centuries closed in. Timber supports crossed overhead like ribs, black with water-stain, some collapsed into splinters, others impossibly intact — preserved by cold that had no business being this deep, this old.

The water came at the second turning.

It lay across the floor in a sheet of black glass, ankle-deep, still as held breath. Where it had frozen against the walls it climbed in terraces, and the lantern-light caught things suspended inside it: tools, a boot, the shape of a hand reaching up through the ice from below, fingers spread, patient.

Ilo stepped around it without looking. "Drowned level. The company hit an aquifer in year forty-one. Sealed the lower galleries and walked away."

"They walked away," Oscar repeated. "All of them?"

"The ones who could pay their way out." Ilo's voice was flat as the water. "The rest stayed."

Oscar looked at the hand in the ice again. She made herself look. Somewhere above them, stars were being melted down into a crown; somewhere below them, her sister's soul sat in glass, awake. She owed the dead in this mountain her attention, at least. She gave it, and then she stepped into the water, and the cold climbed her legs like something with intent.

* * *

The memories began at the third gallery.

She felt it before she understood it — a prickle along her skin where her storm-sense used to live, a ghost-itch in bones that had gone quiet when she poured her magic through the veil. No. Not quiet. *Spent.* There was nothing left in her to sense with, and yet the feeling came anyway, faint and familiar as a word on the tip of the tongue.

Then she saw the stone breathing light.

Faint veins of pale gold ran through the black rock of the walls, pulsing slow, like embers under ash. As she drew closer, the light gathered, thickened, and became — not quite image, not quite sound. A smell of rain on hot slate. Her mother's kitchen. Wren laughing with her mouth full of bread.

Oscar stopped walking so fast that Sefa nearly collided with her back.

"What is that?" she whispered.

Sefa lifted his lantern. The gold veins brightened beneath it, and within the glow, shapes moved: a girl of perhaps ten, standing in a doorway, hair plastered to her face by storm-wind. The girl turned toward them, and though her features were blurred as things seen underwater, Oscar knew her. Knew the set of her shoulders. Knew the way she stood braced against weather, because Oscar had spent her whole childhood watching Wren do exactly that.

"That's me," Oscar said. "That's — I remember that day. That was the day the court's men came."

"No." Sefa's voice had gone very careful, the way it did when he was about to confess something. "You don't remember it. Not anymore. Look closer."

And she did look, and the memory unfolded around her like a hand opening: the doorway, the wind, the men in grey with their ledger-books, and her mother's voice — low, furious, terrified — saying words Oscar had never heard, because she had never been there to hear them. Because this wasn't her memory. It was Wren's. It was the memory Wren had carried of the day Oscar was taken away, and it had been sold, traded, paid into the mountain like coin into a merchant's palm, and now it lived in the stone, waiting.

"It's her," Oscar breathed. "These are Wren's memories. They're — the tolls. Everything anyone ever paid—"

"Everything anyone ever paid *here*," Sefa said. "The Court doesn't keep its ledgers in books, Oscar. It keeps them in the rock. Every memory surrendered at every tollhouse feeds back to the mountain. We've been walking through the Court's vault since we crossed the threshold. This whole mine is one long throat, and everything swallowed goes down."

The girl in the wall turned her face away, and the light dimmed, and Oscar found she was crying without having decided to.

"She watched me leave," Oscar said. "She was ten years old and she watched them take me, and I thought—" Her voice cracked. "I thought she forgot me. I built my whole life on the idea that she forgot me, that it was easier for her, that I was the only one who—"

"Memories don't vanish because they're sold," Sefa said quietly. "They just change hands. That's the cruelty of it. Someone else remembers your life better than you do."

Ilo had stopped ahead of them, lamp held high, and said nothing at all. The child knew about ledgers. The child had paid at the tollhouse too, and would never know what piece of themselves was missing, only that the shape of them had a hole in it now, cold-edged, like a tooth lost from the jaw.

"We keep moving," Oscar said, and wiped her face with the back of her glove, and meant it.

* * *

The shaft narrowed. Then it narrowed again. By the fifth gallery they were walking sideways through gaps the mountain seemed to be closing behind them, and the ceiling pressed so low that Sefa had to stoop, and the air grew thick and mineral and warm — which was worse than cold, somehow, because warmth down here meant the mountain was alive, or inhabited, or digesting.

Oscar hated it. She discovered this with each step: she hated the weight of stone overhead, hated the way sound died a foot from its source, hated the dark that the lanterns only dented. Above ground, storms had taught her that violence could be beautiful, that power moved and spent itself and cleared the sky. Down here nothing moved. Nothing spent itself. The pressure just accumulated, patient as debt.

But she kept her feet moving, because stopping meant thinking, and thinking meant the walls of her own skull would start pressing in the same way.

It was Ilo who broke the silence, in the flat reciting voice the child used for contract-law:

"Clause eleven. All passages below the drowned level remain the property of the Marrow Company in perpetuity, including but not limited to the air within them."

"The air," Sefa said.

"And the echoes." Ilo pointed upward, and Oscar saw it: thin channels drilled into the ceiling at regular intervals, running away into the dark like the holes of a flute. "Grandmother said the company sold the echoes separately. Miners whistled while they worked, and the company collected the sound and sold it to the Court. That's why these galleries are so quiet. The mountain ate the noise years ago."

Oscar listened. Ilo was right. Their footsteps made sounds, but the sounds fell dead, absorbed, as though the stone were drinking them. Even their breathing seemed muffled, owned by something else.

"Every comfort has a price tag down here," Sefa murmured. "Warmth. Light. Sound. Memory." He glanced at Oscar, and in the lantern-glow his ruined face — the ward-scars, the eyes of a man who had built beautiful things for terrible people — looked almost gentle. "Including love. Especially love. That's what the soul-trade is, underneath everything. Love, itemized."

"Then we're thieves," Oscar said. "Good. I'd rather be a thief than a customer."

Something flickered across Sefa's face — surprise, then something warmer, quickly banked. He said nothing, but he walked a little taller after that, insofar as a man could walk tall in a tunnel built for kneeling.

* * *

The seventh gallery was flooded to the waist, and there, in the middle of the black water, stood a door.

It made no sense. It was a fine door — oak, iron-banded, with a brass handle gone green — standing upright in the flood with nothing behind it and nothing above it, its frame rooted in the water as if the water were a floor. Ice had grown around its base in a ruff, holding it steady.

"Don't," Sefa said, when Oscar slowed.

"I'm not going to open it."

"You were going to think about opening it. Don't think about it either. Doors like that aren't doors. They're offers."

Oscar looked at the door a moment longer. In its upper panel, fogged with cold, a shape had condensed — the way breath fogs a window. A shape like a handprint. Small. A child's hand.

Wren's hand, she thought, and did not know how she knew, and hated that she knew.

She waded past it. The water dragged at her thighs like hands, and the door watched her go with its fogged eye, and she did not look back, because looking back was also a kind of opening.

* * *

Past the flooded gallery, the memories thickened until walking through them was like walking through weather.

They came off the walls in sheets now — gold light rippling over black stone, and inside the light, lives. A farmer's last summer, sold to buy his village one more winter of grain. A wedding, entire, every vow and every doubt, pawned by a bride who could no longer afford to feel either. A mother's lullaby, sung in a language Oscar didn't know, looping endlessly in a vein of stone no bigger than her arm, and Ilo stopped before it and stood very still, and Oscar saw the child's lips move, shaping words the child's mind no longer owned but the body had never forgotten.

"Ilo," Oscar said softly.

"I know this song." Ilo's voice was very small. "I don't know how I know this song."

"Because part of you is smarter than the part that signed the toll-book." Oscar crouched to the child's height. "Listen to me. Whatever they took from you — it isn't gone. It's here. It's in the walls. And when we break this place open, everything they swallowed comes back out. Every song. Every summer. Every name." She put her hand on Ilo's shoulder, and the contact steadied her own shaking more than it steadied the child's. "We're not just here for Wren anymore. You understand? Nobody gets left in the glass."

Ilo looked up at her, and for a moment the flat contract-voice cracked, and underneath it was just a kid, scared and fierce and nine years old in a drowned mountain. "You promise?"

"I promise," Oscar said, and heard, distantly, the echo of another promise she'd made once — to a valley, to herself, to stay safe behind the veil — and knew this one was the truer of the two.

Behind them, unnoticed, the lullaby in the stone sang on. But as they turned to go, Oscar could have sworn the melody shifted — bent, almost, toward hope. Toward following.

* * *

The ninth gallery was where the mountain showed her her own debts.

She knew it before the light resolved. The air changed — went electric, went *hers*, the way the sky used to taste before a storm broke, and her heart lurched with longing so violent it was physical, grief and hunger in the same breath. The gold in the walls blazed. And there, playing across the stone in fragments, was her life. Not Wren's memories of her. Hers. The ones she'd paid.

At the tollhouse she had given up a memory of her sister and hadn't known which, hadn't been able to tell the gap in herself. Now the mountain showed her the merchandise.

A hundred small scenes, looping: Wren teaching her to whistle against the wind so the sound came back doubled. Wren braiding her hair badly and blaming the braid. Wren, older, thinner, standing at the edge of the Veiled Valley's border stones on the far side of the veil — Oscar hadn't known, hadn't *known*, that Wren had come to look at the prison her sister was sealed inside, had stood there in the snow for hours, close enough to touch the wards, too afraid to try.

"Oh," Oscar said. Just that. Just *oh*, over and over, a small broken syllable, as scene after scene played and she understood the shape of what she'd sold. Not one memory. A constellation of them. The toll-taker had taken everything with Wren's face in it that Oscar could bear to lose, and she had borne losing all of it, and the hole in her had been so large and so perfectly shaped that she'd mistaken it for the world.

Sefa caught her elbow as she swayed. "Breathe."

"She came for me." Oscar's voice came out scraped raw. "She stood outside the valley. She *came for me*, and I paid that away for safe passage through a tollhouse, and I didn't even—"

"You didn't know. That's the design. That's always the design." Sefa's grip tightened. "The Court doesn't want you suffering, Oscar. Suffering makes people hold on. It wants you *comfortable*. It wants the loss to fit so smoothly you can't find its edges. That's how it buys a whole world — not with fire. With arithmetic."

Oscar pressed her palm flat against the stone, against the light, against her sister's face mid-laugh. The gold pulsed under her hand like a heartbeat answering.

"Hold on," she whispered to it. To her. "I'm coming. I'm bringing all of it back."

The light flared — once, bright, unmistakable — and somewhere far below them, deep in the mountain's throat, something answered. Not a sound. A *pull*. The soul-thread, the thread Sefa had taught her to feel, snapped taut as a bowstring, dragging at the center of her chest, downward, urgent.

And with it, faint but clear, threading up through two hundred feet of drowned stone and sold memory, came a voice she knew better than her own.

*"Don't come."*

Oscar went cold from the inside out.

"Wren?" she said aloud, and the dead galleries drank the name.

Down in the dark below, something began, very softly, to sing — and it was singing the lullaby from the seventh gallery, the one Ilo's body remembered, and it was getting closer.
