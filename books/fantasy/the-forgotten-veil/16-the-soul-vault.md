## 16. The Soul Vault

The stone remembered me before I remembered it.

That was the worst part of the descent — the way the mountain's deep galleries had soaked up everything the tollhouse had taken from me, and more besides. Every few dozen paces another memory rose out of the rock like heat off a summer road, and I walked through it whether I wanted to or not.

Here: Wren at seven, teaching me to whistle through a blade of grass until the whole kitchen garden answered. Here: my mother's hands, red to the wrist with dye, pressing a copper coin into my palm and saying *don't spend it all on foolishness* — except she never said that, I'd invented it somewhere along the road, and the stone had kept the invention anyway. The mountain did not care what was true. It only cared what was paid.

"Oscar." Sefa's voice came low from behind me, ward-lantern held high. Its light bent strangely down here, pooling in the wrong places, as if the dark were thick enough to have weight. "You stopped again."

"I'm fine."

"You've said that four times." He didn't add *and you were lying three of them*. He was learning restraint in his old age, or perhaps he simply knew there was no time for the argument.

Ilo squeezed past us both, small feet sure on the wet mine-rails. The child had not spoken since we entered the drowned shafts — since the water had risen black and patient around our knees and then, obedient to some contract older than any of us, parted. But Ilo's hand found mine now without looking, the way you find a banister in the dark, and I understood that silence for what it was: not fear. Navigation. The child was reading the contracts written into the walls, the way other children read street signs.

"This way," Ilo said at last. "The air changes. You'll smell it."

Ilo was right. The air changed two turns later — went sweet, almost, like fruit left too long in a closed room, like sugar rotting into something else. Beneath the sweetness ran a cold that had nothing to do with temperature. I had felt that cold once before, on the night every star went out, when the wards shattered and the Hollow Court's winter poured over the valley like water finding its level.

My storm-sense — the ghost of it, the ache where my magic used to live — turned over in my chest like something sleeping trying to find a comfortable position.

*Close*, it said. *So close.*

We climbed. The mine-shafts gave way to worked stone, then to stone so finely cut it seemed grown rather than built, veined with channels of glass that glowed a pale, starved blue. Starlight, I realized. Or what passed for it here. They had piped the stolen stars through their mountain like water through an aqueduct, and every channel hummed with a frequency I felt in my teeth.

Sefa stopped at the first junction and put his palm flat against the wall, eyes half-closed, reading the wardwork the way a blind man reads a face.

"It's beautiful," he said, and sounded sick saying it. "Whoever laid this — the routing alone would take twenty years to learn. And they've done it with souls as the conductive medium." His hand curled into a fist against the glass vein. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? These aren't wires. These are people."

"I know," I said. Because I did know. Had known since the tollhouse, since the ledger-keeper's ink-stained smile, since the moment I paid a memory of Wren and couldn't remember which one. Everything in this mountain ran on the same currency. We had been spending it our whole lives without ever seeing the mint.

* * *

The guards found us before we found the vault.

They came around a corner in perfect silence, six of them, armored in plates of smoked glass that reflected nothing — not the lantern light, not each other, not me. That was the first wrongness. The second was how they moved: fluid, coordinated, like fingers of one hand. The third wrongness I didn't understand until one of them raised its visor.

There was no face beneath. There was a face-shaped absence, smooth as river stone, and where eyes should have been there were two points of cold light — the same starved blue as the veins in the walls.

"Hollowed," Sefa breathed. He stepped in front of Ilo, arms spread, and I saw his hands shaking. "They hollowed them out and left the bodies walking. Gods. I heard rumors, but I thought—"

One of the guards spoke. Its voice was many voices slightly out of phase with themselves, a choir singing a single note badly.

*"Turn back. The Court is in session. The Court is always in session. Turn back."*

"We're lost pilgrims," Sefa said smoothly, bowing, playing the role he'd rehearsed for a hundred miles. "Seeking audience—"

*"You are the smith."* The guard's head tilted, and the light in its eye-holes flickered, and for one horrible instant I thought I saw something behind the light — something pressed against the inside of the glass, like a face against a window. *"You are Anwar. Your name is still owed here. Your name has been accruing interest for nineteen years."*

Sefa went very still. "Then let me pay it," he said quietly. "Let me through."

The guards looked at one another — or rather, their empty faces oriented toward one another, some consultation passing between them faster than speech. Then the lead guard stepped aside. Just like that. The whole phalanx parted like a door.

"That's a trap," I whispered.

"Obviously," Sefa whispered back. "But it's also an invitation, and I don't think we'll get a second one. Whatever they want from us, they want it in the vault. Which means the vault is where we need to be." He glanced at me, and in the blue light his face looked ten years older than it had at the valley gate. "Stay behind me. If I tell you to run—"

"You won't," I said. "Neither will I."

He almost smiled. Almost. Then he walked through the gap in the guards, and Ilo took my hand again, and we followed.

As I passed the lead guard, it turned its head to track me — only me — and the choir-voice dropped to something almost soft, almost human, a voice worn down to a whisper by decades of use.

*"Storm-child. She watches for you. She has watched for nine years. Do not look away from her. She hates it when people look away."*

My heart stopped, started again, wrong. "She?"

But the guard's face smoothed back into blankness, and it gestured us onward with a courtesy so perfect it was obscene.

* * *

The doors to the vault were made of the same smoked glass as the guards' armor, tall as cathedral gates, and they opened at our approach without a sound. Beyond them, the sweetness in the air became overwhelming — sugar and rot and underneath it something clean, something like the air after lightning.

I had expected darkness. What I got was light beyond counting.

The vault was a cavern the size of the valley's whole sky, ribbed with pillars of living rock, and every surface — every pillar, every wall, the ceiling arching up into darkness — was set with glass.

Thousands upon thousands of vessels, from thimble-small to coffin-long, each one glowing faintly, each one holding a swirl of pale luminescence that moved. Not drifted. *Moved.* With purpose. With restlessness. With the particular quality of motion that can only belong to something aware of its own imprisonment.

Souls. Thousands of souls in glass, arranged in rows that receded farther than the lantern could reach, tiered up the walls like the shelves of some infinite library, and every single one of them awake.

I knew they were awake because they turned to look at us.

Not all at once — that would have been merciful. They turned in a slow wave, nearest first, a ripple of attention rolling outward through the tiers, and the sound they made was not a sound at all but a pressure, a leaning, thousands of imprisoned things straining toward the first living visitors they'd had in who knew how long. The glass fogged faintly where the nearest vessels sat, as if breathed on from inside.

"Oh," Ilo said, very small. "Oh, no."

"Don't touch anything," Sefa said hoarsely. "Don't promise anything. Don't answer if they speak. Some of these contracts—" He swallowed. "Some of these souls are in glass because they *agreed* to be. Speaking to them might count as renegotiation."

I wasn't listening. I was walking.

I don't remember deciding to walk. My feet simply carried me down the central aisle, past vessel after vessel, past a soul the size of a grape that flared hopefully as I passed, past one long as my arm that pulsed slow and sullen, refusing even to look. The soul-thread in my chest — the pull that had dragged me across half a frozen country, through silenced villages and memory-tolling bridges and the drowned dark of the mines — pulled now like a hooked fish-line, straight and taut and singing.

Third tier from the floor. Fourth row from the aisle.

There.

The vessel was no larger than a lantern, ordinary blown glass, unremarkable — and inside it burned a light unlike any of the others. Warmer. Restless. Shaped, when I looked too long, like a girl.

Like Wren.

"Wren," I said, and my voice cracked apart on the second syllable.

The light surged. Pressed against the glass the way the guard's eyes had pressed against theirs — a face at a window, nine years of watching compressed into one desperate flare. And then, though no mouth moved and no air carried it, I heard her. Not in my ears. In the place behind my sternum where the thread lived, in the bone-deep channel where her memories had been leaking into me since the night the stars died.

*Oscar. Oh — oh, you're real. You're really — turn around. Let me see all of you.*

I turned, slowly, arms out, ridiculous, tears already freezing on my cheeks in the vault's deep cold. When I faced her again the light was trembling.

*You got tall. You got — Oscar, why did you come? Why would you come HERE?*

"Because you were taken."

*I was SOLD. There's a difference, and you know it, because you've been carrying my memories and you know exactly what Mother did and you came anyway—*

"Wren—"

*No. Listen to me. Listen.* The light steadied, and the warmth in it banked itself down into something harder, older, a sister's voice becoming a stranger's urgency. *This isn't a prison, Oscar, it's a pantry. And tonight is the harvest. The crown needs one more binding — one soul freely given, freely surrendered, to seal the winter permanent. They brought you here because you're the payment. You've been the payment since the day you were born; Mother only deferred the bill.*

The cold that went through me then had nothing to do with the mountain.

"The guards," I said. "The one at the door. It said someone watches for me. It said—"

*I know what it said.* Wren's light dimmed, guttered, and when it brightened again it was with something I had never once heard from her in life or memory: shame. *It said it because I asked it to. Nine years ago I asked, and it still carries the message, because hollowed things keep their last order forever. Oscar — look at the guards' armor. Look closely.*

I looked. At the smoked-glass plates, at the reflections that weren't there. And I finally understood the third wrongness, the one I hadn't been able to name in the corridor: the armor wasn't reflecting nothing. The armor was *full*. Every plate held a faint, trapped shimmer, a soul pressed flat and thin and shaped into service — the guards weren't wearing soul-glass.

The guards *were* soul-glass. Hundreds of them, hammered into shape, marching on legs of borrowed meat.

"They hollow them out," I whispered, "and wear what's left."

*And the ones who resist,* Wren said softly, *get melted down and poured into the crown. That's what tonight is. That's what you walked into. One girl against a mountain full of the dead who cannot die and the living who cannot leave.* A pause, and then, gentler, breaking my heart with its gentleness: *Go home, little storm. Find a valley and grow old in it. Forget you found me.*

"No," I said.

*Oscar—*

"No." I put my hand flat against the glass, and her light rushed to meet my palm, warm through the cold barrier, and I felt her feel me — felt nine years of loneliness collide with nine years of guilt in the space between two surfaces. "I spent everything to get here. My magic's next, if it comes to that. I am not leaving this mountain without you, Wren Galloway, and if you argue with me I swear I will stand here until the winter comes and freezes us both mid-sentence."

The light shuddered. Something that might have been laughter, or sobbing, or both.

*You always were the stubborn one.*

Behind me, Sefa made a sound — a sharp intake of breath — and when I turned, he was staring up at the vault's far wall, at the great sealed archway there, behind which something vast turned over in its sleep. Blue light bled through the seams. The humming in the glass veins all around us changed pitch, rising, urgent, a thousand souls' worth of stolen starlight being drawn somewhere fast.

"What?" I asked. "What is it?"

Sefa's face had gone grey. "The forge," he said. "They've started the final casting early. The crown is being finished *tonight* — and the last component just arrived." His eyes came down from the archway and fixed on me, and I watched him do the arithmetic I'd been doing since Wren spoke: one soul, freely given, to seal the winter. One storm-child standing in the pantry.

Somewhere above us, deep in the mountain's heart, a hammer the size of a falling star struck the first blow of the long winter — and every soul in the vault screamed without sound, and the glass fogged with their breath, and the doors at our backs began, very quietly, to close.
