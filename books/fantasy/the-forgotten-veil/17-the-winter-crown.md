## 17. The Winter Crown

Sefa Anwar had spent thirty years learning how things broke, and the last three months learning that he had broken most of them himself. So when the floor of the Hollow Court's great gallery began to sing — a thin, crystalline note that climbed through the soles of his boots and settled in his teeth like a splinter of ice — he knew before anyone told him what it meant.

*They're finishing it.*

He stood at the edge of a balcony carved from black basalt, one of dozens ringing the gallery in tiers, hidden among the press of hollowed courtiers who had come to watch. Below him, the throne hall opened like the throat of some enormous beast: vaulted ceilings lost in darkness, pillars wrapped in frost that had never melted, and at the center, suspended above a ring of anvils, the crown.

It did not look finished. That was the horror of it. It looked like a wound mid-healing, or mid-opening — a circlet of black iron the size of a wagon wheel, its band studded with empty sockets, and drifting within those sockets, half-caught, half-free, were stars.

Actual stars. He could see them turning slowly, each one a knot of white fire no larger than an apple, straining against the iron that held them the way a moth strains against a pin. Their light fell on the assembled court and made their faces beautiful and terrible, all those glassy eyes turned upward in rapture.

Sefa counted the sockets without meaning to. It was an old habit, counting flaws, counting load-bearing points, counting everything that could fail. Forty-one sockets. Thirty-six filled.

Five stars still loose in the sky somewhere, or already in transit, being carried down through the mountain's veins by heralds with cold for blood.

"Thirty-six," Oscar breathed beside him.

He hadn't heard her climb onto the balcony. She crouched low behind the balustrade, Ilo pressed small against her other side, and her face was lit from below by the crown's stolen light. Her jaw was set in the way he'd learned to dread — the way it set right before she did something reckless.

"Don't," he said quietly.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to count them out loud."

Oscar's mouth twitched, but the humor died fast. "Thirty-six," she said again, softer. "There were hundreds, Sefa. The night they went out — I felt them go. All of them." Her hand drifted to her sternum, where the soul-thread lived, the thread that had been pulling her toward this mountain since the wards fell. "Where are the rest?"

Below, a bell tolled — deep, slow, wrong, a bell struck underwater. The courtiers sank to their knees in unison, a field of dark shapes folding down like wheat.

And the Hollow Queen came out onto her dais.

Sefa had served the Court once, long enough ago that he'd taught himself to remember it as a fever dream. But the Queen was not a fever dream. She was tall in the way winter is tall — not a height you measure but a depth you fall into — robed in grey that moved like weather, her face a smooth oval of pale light with two darker lights where eyes should be. When she raised her hands, the singing in the floor rose to meet her, and every star in every socket flared.

The courtiers moaned. Ilo grabbed Sefa's sleeve.

"She's feeding them," the child whispered. "Look. Look at the frost."

He looked. The frost on the pillars was *moving*, crawling upward in slow rivers, and where it crawled, the stone greyed and cracked and went dead. The crown was drinking. Not just light — the mountain itself, the deep heat of the world, the slow warm pulse that kept winters ending instead of enduring. The crown drank it all and gave back only cold.

"The forging isn't symbolic," Sefa said, and his voice came out flat because flat was the only way he could say it. "I told you they were making a weapon. I undersold it. They're making a *replacement*. A new engine for the seasons. One setting."

"Permanent winter," Oscar said.

"Within days. Maybe less. Once the last five stars seat themselves, the crown stops drawing on the mountain and starts drawing on everything. Every hearth. Every heartbeat. The Court won't need contracts anymore — they'll own the cold outright, and everyone will pay forever just to stay frozen instead of dying." He watched the Queen's hands turn, slow as clockwork, and the stars turned with them. "The villages we walked through. The city that sold its warmth. They were pilot programs. Down payments."

Ilo's grip tightened on his sleeve. "My cousin signed one of those."

"I know." Sefa didn't look away from the crown. "So did I. Different contract. Same ink."

* * *

They withdrew from the gallery the way they'd come, through service corridors cut into the living rock, passages that Sefa knew because he had helped design their ventilation — another entry in the ledger of his sins, though tonight it finally paid interest in their favor. They walked until the singing faded to a hum, then stopped in a junction where three tunnels met and a shaft of frozen air fell from somewhere far above, carrying the smell of old iron.

Oscar paced. Three steps, turn, three steps. Ilo sat against the wall, knees drawn up, watching her the way children watch storms.

"Say it," Oscar said finally. "Whatever you're not saying. You've been doing your thinking-face since the gallery, and it looks exactly like your guilty-face, which, granted, are very similar, but I've had time to learn the difference."

Sefa leaned against the cold wall. His shoulder ached where the frost-burn from the gorge hadn't fully healed. He found he was stalling, and he hated that he knew why: because the plan forming in his head was the kind of plan that needed someone to pay for it, and he already knew whose coin would be demanded.

"The crown is iron and starlight," he said. "But the iron is just the frame. The actual working — the thing that turns seasons into a single setting — that's the lattice. The pattern the stars sit in. Do you know what a ward is, Oscar? Really?"

"A pattern that holds power in a shape."

"Yes. And do you know what else is a pattern that holds power in a shape?"

She stopped pacing. He watched her get there — watched it land in her eyes, watched her go still the way she went still when the storm-sense used to brush her, back when she had storm-sense to brush.

"The vault," she said.

"The vault. Thousands of souls in glass, each one a sealed vessel of raw power, arranged in rows, in patterns, in—" he exhaled— "in lattices. I built the valley wards out of the same principles. The Court built the vault out of the same principles. And the crown is the same principles again, scaled up past anything either of us ever attempted. Which means the vault and the crown are *resonant structures*. Same song, different instruments."

Ilo frowned. "So if you play one..."

"The other answers." Sefa pushed off the wall and started pacing now, because the idea had fully arrived and it wanted his legs moving. "You can't smash the crown. We saw the anvils — there's enough raw force down here to forge a mountain, and the Queen stands right next to it. You can't steal forty-one stars out from under her nose. But if you take the vault's glass — the vessels themselves, the souls inside them — and you break them *toward* the crown, tuned right, aimed right—"

"The whole lattice shatters," Oscar said. "Both lattices."

"Every soul in that vault goes free. Every star comes loose. The crown doesn't just stop — it *unwinds*, and everything it has drunk gets poured back out. The cold goes home. The warmth goes home." He stopped. Faced her. "The stars relight."

Silence, except for the distant hum and the drip of ancient meltwater somewhere in the dark.

"That sounds," Oscar said carefully, "like the first good news we've had since the gate."

"It isn't good news."

"Sefa."

"There's a reason the Court doesn't use the vault this way. There's a reason nobody does. Breaking a vessel releases the soul, yes — but the release needs a *driver*. Power poured through the glass to crack it along the lattice lines. Not a little. Not a torch applied to a lock. The vessels are warded by the same hands that warded everything else down here, and I know exactly how strong those wards are, because I know exactly how strong *I* am." His voice did something he didn't authorize; it roughened. "You'd need a storm. Not a storm like the ones you've been throwing around since the veil dropped. You'd need everything. All of it, all at once, channeled through the glass — and Oscar, power like that doesn't pass *through*. It spends itself. It spends itself *entirely*."

Oscar looked at him for a long moment. Then she said, "Okay."

"*Okay?*"

"Okay. So I pour my magic into the glass and I come out the other side like the rest of the valley. Magicless. Like I've been for twenty years anyway." She shrugged, and the shrug was a lie so transparent it almost hurt to look at. "I've been practicing."

"No." He stepped toward her. "You don't understand what 'everything' means. You've called lightning down a mountainside. You've thrown thunder into a gorge. That was you *breathing hard*. This is you exhaling your last breath. The magic doesn't come back. It doesn't fade and regrow. It goes into the glass and the glass gives it to the souls and the souls give it to the sky, and you stand in the wreckage afterward with nothing — not tired, not drained, *empty*. Permanently. The way Wren's body is empty. The way—"

"Don't." The word cracked out of her like the first drop before a deluge, and the air in the tunnel went sharp and metallic, and Ilo flinched against the wall. Oscar closed her eyes. Breathed. The pressure eased. "Don't you dare compare it to her. Wren didn't choose anything. I'm choosing."

"That's exactly why I'm comparing it!"

"Because you think choice makes it worse?" She opened her eyes, and they were wet and furious and absolutely steady. "Because you've spent your whole life watching people pay for things they never agreed to — your Court, your contracts, your flaw in my valley's wards — and now here's a payment somebody actually wants to make, and it terrifies you?"

The tunnel hummed. Somewhere above, faint and vast, the crown sang its unfinished song.

"Yes," Sefa said quietly. "It terrifies me. Because I have seen what people look like after the Court takes everything, and I have never once seen what they look like after they give it. I don't know if there's anything left to see."

Oscar crossed the space between them and put her hand flat against his chest, over the old scar the Court's brand had left, the one he never talked about.

"Then watch," she said. "That's your job. Watch, and tell me if there's anything left."

* * *

They planned through the dark hours, because planning was the only prayer Sefa knew how to speak.

The geometry of it spread across the tunnel floor in chalk and charcoal: the vault lay four levels beneath the throne hall, directly under the crown's axis — of course it did; the Court built everything resonant, everything nested, the whole mountain one instrument with the crown as its sounding board. The vault's central aisle ran north-south. The vessels sat in cases of lead and bone. If the vessels were shattered in sequence — outer rings first, spiraling inward, each release feeding the next — the resonance would climb, wave upon wave, until it reached the frequency the crown itself was built on.

"And then the crown breaks," Ilo said, tracing the spiral with one finger.

"And then the crown breaks," Sefa confirmed. "But the sequence matters more than the force. Break them out of order and you get noise, not music — a surge that the Queen can smother. In order, in rhythm, it becomes a chord she can't swallow." He drew the final ring, the innermost, where the oldest vessels sat. "This last one carries the chord. Whoever pours the power into the final vessel decides whether the whole thing resolves or collapses."

"So it has to be me," Oscar said. "On the last one."

"On the last one. After thirty-some vessels' worth of released souls have passed through the room. After the guards — the hollowed ones — realize what's happening. After the Queen feels the first note and starts hunting the source." He met her eyes. "You'll be standing in the middle of a collapsing lattice, holding the only note that matters, while everything we've fought since the gate tries to stop you."

Ilo spoke up, small and fierce. "We'll clear the path. Me and the contracts. Half the guards down there are bound by paper — I've been reading the ledgers since the tollhouse. Paper burns, Sefa. Paper *unravels*."

Sefa looked at the child — the orphan twice over, the village that sold its warmth, the smallest member of their ragged expedition — and felt something in his chest that wasn't quite hope but was at least its stubborn cousin.

"Then we go tomorrow," he said. "During the seating of the fifth-to-last star. The whole Court will be in the gallery, singing the anchor-rites. The vault will be thinnest-manned it will ever be." He began rolling up the chalk-dusted cloth, and his hands, he noticed, were shaking. "One more thing. About the cost."

"We covered the cost."

"Not yours. Mine." He tied the cloth shut. "The vessels are warded with my work. Old work, but mine. I can weaken them from the inside — open the seams so your power can crack them clean instead of fighting through. But to do it, I have to touch each vessel. Skin to glass. And the wards will know me. They'll take their toll going in." He smiled, and it felt like an artifact from another life. "Ward-smith's rule, Oscar: every door you open charges the opener. By the time we reach the innermost ring, I may not be much use for anything except pointing."

Oscar stared at him. "How much?"

"Enough. Not too much. I've paid worse." He stood, joints cracking, and looked up the tunnel toward the distant red glow of the forges. "That's the plan. A child with a ledger, a broken smith opening doors, and a storm standing at the center of the chord."

Above them, muffled through miles of stone, the crown sang on — and in its song, unmistakable now, Sefa could hear the shape of the final star arriving, falling toward the mountain like the last note of a requiem.

Two days, perhaps three, before the winter became forever.

Oscar touched the soul-thread at her sternum and whispered something too quiet to catch. But Sefa had spent a lifetime listening to patterns, and he caught it anyway:

"Hold on, Wren. I'm coming — and I'm bringing the sky."
