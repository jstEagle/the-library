## 18. Storm Against Stone

The mountain breathed.

Oscar felt it through the soles of her boots as they stood at the mouth of the final gallery — a slow, vast rhythm, like lungs the size of a country drawing in cold and exhaling colder. The stone around them was veined with light that wasn't light: star-stuff, molten and wrong, the stolen sky pumped through the mountain's arteries toward whatever heart beat at its center.

Sefa pressed his palm flat against the wall and closed his eyes. When he opened them again, they were wet.

"It's almost done," he said. "The crown. I can feel the forging from here. Days, Oscar. Maybe hours."

"Then we don't have days." She rolled her shoulders, and beneath her skin something answered — a low rolling growl of pressure, like a summer sky deciding whether to break. Since the gorge, since the lessons, she had kept it leashed with Sefa's breathing exercises and her own white-knuckle will. But now, standing in the dark throat of the Hollow Court, she let the leash slip one link at a time. "How far to the throne hall?"

"Past the next junction. The hall sits above the vault — the Queen likes to keep what she owns where she can see it." Sefa turned to Ilo, who had gone very still in the way the child did when the contracts in their head started whispering. "You remember the way down?"

Ilo nodded once. "Left at the drowned stair. Past the tollhouse door. The vault keeps its own weather — cold that doesn't touch you unless it means to." They swallowed. "I signed a paper once. It'll know me."

"It'll know all of us," Sefa said grimly. "That's why we go separately. Oscar—" He caught her arm, and his grip was iron despite everything, despite the tremor in him that never fully stopped. "Listen to me. The storm is not a key. It's a hammer. You can break the doors, break the guards, break the hall itself if you have to. But the Queen will talk. She talked to your mother. She talked to half the world into selling itself, one warm room at a time. Whatever she offers you—"

"I know what she'll offer me."

"You don't. You think you do, because you've imagined it every night since Wren was taken. But imagination is kind. She won't be." His voice dropped. "I built wards for these people, Oscar. I sat at their tables. The Queen doesn't lie. That's the worst thing about her. Everything she says will be true, and it will still be a trap."

Oscar looked at his hand on her arm — the burn-scarred fingers of a smith, the same hands that had shaped the flaw in the valley walls, the hands that had come back across a dying world to undo it. Somewhere between the frozen bells and the buried truths, she had stopped counting his sins against her own.

"Then I'll listen to true things," she said, "and kneel for none of them."

Something moved behind his eyes — fear, or pride, or both wearing each other's faces. "When the glass breaks," he said, "when the souls start coming out — run for the crown. Don't stop for anything. Not for me. Not even—"

"Don't say it."

"Not even for her voice."

Oscar pulled away from him. Above them, deep in the stone, something enormous turned over in its sleep, and the veins of stolen starlight pulsed brighter, once, like a heartbeat skipping.

* * *

She went up alone.

The gallery climbed in switchbacks cut by no human hand, smooth-walled and sweating frost, and with every turn the pressure under her skin rose another degree. By the time the passage opened onto a ledge above the throne hall, her teeth ached from clenching and the air tasted of copper and ozone — her own storm, leaking through the seams of her.

The hall below was a wound in the mountain.

It was vast beyond sense, a cavern hollowed and dressed in black glass until the walls became mirrors and the mirrors became windows, and through every window Oscar could see sky — real sky, the old sky, the one with stars in it, captured and pinned behind the glass like moths. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. The whole murdered firmament, hung as decoration.

And in the center, on a dais of fused bone-white stone, the crown floated above an anvil of dark iron, turning slowly, unfinished. Half its points were forged; half were still falling stars, screaming silently as hammers of shadow beat them into shape. Around the anvil stood figures that had been people once — heralds, courtiers, souls poured into shapes of service, their faces smooth as river stones. And upon a throne grown out of the living rock sat the Hollow Queen.

She was beautiful the way winter is beautiful. Tall, silver-pale, robed in something that moved like snowfall running backward. Her face was kind. That was the horror of it — it was genuinely, warmly kind, the face of someone who had never once doubted that everything she did was a mercy.

Oscar crouched on the ledge and did what Sefa taught her.

Not anger. Want.

She wanted the stars back in the sky. She wanted Wren's soul out of its glass prison. She wanted the long winter broken over her knee like kindling. She wanted it with a want that had been growing in her since the night the valley went dark, since the night before that, since a childhood spent pressing her palms against ward-stones and feeling the storms she could never touch.

The clouds came.

There were no clouds this deep underground — so she made them. Pressure bloomed off her shoulders in visible rings, frost flashing to steam, and the air above the throne hall curdled and thickened and *darkened*, a thunderhead born indoors, pregnant with a sky that hadn't existed a moment before. The smooth-faced guards looked up. Even the Queen's serene gaze lifted, and for the first time Oscar saw something flicker across that kind face.

Recognition.

"Ah," the Queen said, and her voice filled the hall without effort, gentle as snowfall. "The storm-child. Your mother's daughter after all."

Oscar stepped off the ledge and fell into her own lightning.

The strike hit the hall floor like the fist of a god. Black glass shattered outward in a tide of shards; two of the smooth-faced guards simply ceased, unmade where they stood. Oscar landed in the crater of her own making, knees bent, hair lifting around her head in a halo of static, and the storm came down with her — not around her but *through* her, forked lightning walking the walls, thunder rolling off the mirrored ceiling in avalanches of sound.

More guards came. Dozens. They didn't run; they flowed, unhurried, inevitable, and where her lightning took them others simply walked over the ash. She threw a wall of wind that peeled three of them apart. She called hail the size of fists and it fell like siege ammunition. She was screaming — she couldn't hear herself over the thunder, but she knew she was screaming, and some distant part of her noted it with Sefa's clinical calm: *anger is leaking in, want is drowning, hold the line, hold—*

A guard got inside her reach. Its stone-smooth face tilted, considering her, and it raised a hand that had been a woman's hand once, and Oscar saw, just for a heartbeat, a wedding band fused into the gray flesh.

Her lightning faltered.

The blow that followed caught her across the ribs and sent her skidding through glass shards, and the pain was almost a relief — clean, honest, a rope back to herself. She rolled, came up spitting blood, and let the want rise again: *Wren, awake in glass, watching, always watching—*

The storm answered like a living thing.

She drove it outward in a ring, a hurricane given a single spinning instant of existence inside a mountain, and when it died the guards nearest her were scattered wreckage and the ones beyond had stopped, re-forming their lines at a distance, waiting. Smart. Patient. They only needed to delay her, and they knew it.

Because the Queen had risen from her throne.

"No more," the Queen said softly, and the hall obeyed. The surviving guards froze mid-step. Even the storm in Oscar's chest stuttered, cowed by something older than weather. "Come, child. Walk with me. There is no need for any of this to hurt."

Every instinct screamed at Oscar to throw another bolt. Instead — because Sefa and Ilo were even now slipping down the drowned stair toward the vault, because every second the Queen's eyes were on her was a second they weren't on the glass — she straightened, wiped blood from her mouth, and walked.

* * *

Up close, the Queen smelled of cold stone and, absurdly, of bread baking somewhere far away — the smell of every hearth that had ever traded itself away. The unfinished crown turned above the anvil between them, and its half-made points drank the light, and looking at it made Oscar's eyes water like staring at the sun.

"Do you know what I admire about you?" the Queen asked. She circled slowly, robes whispering, and the smooth-faced courtiers parted before her like water. "Your mother came to me with love and shame, and shame makes people small. Their bargains are small. Hide the girl. Muffle the storm. Bury the payment deep enough that no one has to look at it." She smiled, and it was a warm smile, and Oscar hated how much it warmed her. "But you crossed a dying world. You broke my roads and burned my tollhouses and walked into my mountain. That is not shame. That is *appetite*."

"My sister," Oscar said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "Where is she."

"In the vault. Awake. Aware. As she has been for eleven years." The Queen said it gently, the way a physician delivers a diagnosis. "Watching through the glass every time a soul was brought in. Every time a village signed. Every time the lights went out over another name." She tilted her head. "Did you imagine I would lie to you? Lies are for the weak. I deal only in true things."

The words landed exactly where Sefa had said they would. Oscar's hands shook, and she hid it by closing them into fists.

"Then here is mine." The Queen spread her hands, and the air between them filled with images — not illusions, Oscar realized with a lurch, but *true things*, shown as plainly as a window shows a street. Wren, in her glass, whole and luminous, her face unlined by the years that should have marked it. Wren's eyes opening. Wren's lips shaping a word through the glass, and though there was no sound, Oscar knew the shape of her own name anywhere. "Your sister's soul, released. Whole. Returned — yes, even to a body; I am not without mercy, and the Court keeps bodies the way it keeps seasons. She walks out of this mountain alive. She remembers you. She forgives you."

The hall went very quiet. Somewhere above, impossibly, Oscar heard rain beginning against the stone — her own storm, grieving.

"And in exchange?"

"The storm." The Queen's kind eyes held hers. "All of it. Every year of it, every storm you ever called or ever would have called. Pour it into the veil-work, into the crown's foundations — it will make the winter *gentle*, child, I promise you that. No famine. No freezing villages. A soft eternal season, and your sister alive in it, and you beside her, magicless, safe, together." She paused, and her voice dropped to the register of a lullaby. "Or refuse, and take the crown's completion as it comes. The winter will be hard then. Cruel. And Wren stays in her glass until the last star burns out — which, under my crown, will be never."

*Everything she says will be true,* Sefa had said, *and it will still be a trap.*

Oscar looked at the image of her sister floating in the air. Wren's mouth was still moving. Still shaping words. And now, watching closely — watching with eleven years of guilt sharpening her sight — Oscar finally read them.

*Don't.*

The image rippled. For half a heartbeat, the Queen's perfect window flickered, and behind Wren's luminous figure Oscar saw the truth the window was built to hide: the vault stretched away in rows beyond counting, thousands of glass souls, and every one of them would stay in the dark forever while the Queen wore the sky as a hat. Wren free. Everyone else entombed. The bargain wasn't small at all. It was the largest bargain the Queen had ever offered, and its price was the whole world minus one soul.

"You showed my mother this same vision," Oscar said quietly. "Didn't you. And she refused. And everyone has called her a monster for eleven years."

"She refused," the Queen agreed, "and lost everything, and gained nothing. I am offering you a better arithmetic than I offered her."

"No," Oscar said. "You're offering me the same deal with the numbers hidden. My mother wouldn't buy my safety with my sister's soul." She lifted her head, and the storm rose with it, filling her throat, her eyes, her cracking voice. "What makes you think I'd buy hers with everyone else's?"

The Queen sighed — genuinely, softly sad, which was the most frightening thing yet.

"Then we are finished talking," she said, "and the crown must finish too."

Above the anvil, the falling stars began to scream aloud.

Oscar let go of everything Sefa had taught her.

The thunderhead she'd carried since the gorge detonated all at once — not a bolt, not a wall, but the entire storm given one command: *the anvil*. Lightning the width of a river came down through the mirrored ceiling, through the pinned false skies, shattering windows of captured heaven as it fell, and the throne hall filled with white. In the heart of the blaze Oscar heard the Queen cry out — not in pain, in *anger*, the first true emotion she'd shown — and heard the great anvil crack like a bell struck by God.

Through the smoke and raining glass, one thought burned brighter than the storm:

*Sefa. Ilo. The vault. Now — before she seals it.*
