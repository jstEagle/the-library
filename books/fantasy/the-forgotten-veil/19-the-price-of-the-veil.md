## 19. The Price of the Veil

The Hollow Queen's offer hung in the air like smoke that refused to rise.

Kneel. That was all. Kneel, and Wren would walk out of the vault with her soul intact and her body remade from winter itself. Kneel, and Oscar would keep her storms — would keep *everything* — and the world outside the mountain could freeze for all the Court cared.

The throne hall stretched around them, carved from black stone veined with frozen light. The crown hovered above the Queen's dais, half-forged, a ring of stolen stars turning slowly in a lattice of silver wire and older, crueler magic. Each star was a small sun strangled to an ember. Together they burned cold enough to make Oscar's teeth ache. When the forging finished — days, Sefa had said, maybe hours now — the long winter would never end. No spring. No thaw. No morning that was not white and silent and dead.

"Your mother refused this bargain," the Queen said. Her voice came from everywhere at once, from the walls, from the floor, from the glass cases stacked in their thousands along the hall's far side. "She chose exile over power. Look what it bought her. A daughter in a valley that forgets. A daughter here." One pale hand gestured toward the vault, toward the case where Wren's soul burned soft gold behind glass, awake, aware, watching. She had been watching for eleven years. "I am offering you better arithmetic than your mother ever had."

Oscar's knees wanted to bend. That was the horror of it — not the temptation but the *ease*. The stone was right there beneath her boots, cool and patient, and all she had to do was lower herself onto it and the war would be over. Her sister would breathe again. She could carry Wren out of this mountain on her own back, storm-magic blazing, untouchable, and no one — not the Court, not the heralds, not the frozen world — could stop them.

*Don't come,* Wren had told her through the soul-thread, clear as a bell in a frozen gorge. *Whatever they offer you, don't.*

"You're wrong about my mother," Oscar said.

The Queen tilted her head. Beneath the crown of her hood there was no face Oscar could hold onto — only a suggestion of one, shifting, assembled from everyone who had ever knelt.

"She didn't choose between power and exile. She chose between two daughters, and she chose wrong." Oscar's voice cracked and she let it. There was no use being clean about this. "But she paid everything she had to pay, and she never once asked the world to kneel for it. That's the difference between her and you."

"Sentiment," the Queen said, and something in the throne hall shifted — the heralds moving in the shadows, the wire lattice tightening around its stolen stars. "The winter does not care for sentiment. Kneel, storm-caller, or I will take the magic from your corpse and forge the crown anyway."

"Yeah," Oscar said. "I figured."

She reached down into herself, into the place where the storms lived, and found it nearly empty.

That was the truth Sefa had seen in her face when they'd breached the hall — the truth she'd been carrying since the drowned mineshafts, since the tollhouse, since the moment she'd understood what pouring power through broken glass would cost. She had spent herself getting here. Every ward she'd shattered, every herald she'd thrown across the ice, every mile of wild thunder she'd dragged out of a body built too small for it. What remained was a puddle. A last swallow in a dry cup.

It would have to be enough.

"No," she said.

She did not kneel. She opened her hands instead, palms up, the way Sefa had taught her in the gorge — *storms obey want, not anger* — and she wanted. Not destruction. Not victory. She wanted the sky back. She wanted stars over the beacon-farm and frost melting off the border stones and Wren's laugh, Wren's actual laugh, not a memory of it, not a fragment, not a voice in a thread.

The last of her magic rose.

It came up through her like water finding a crack in a dam — thin, trembling, nowhere near enough — and the Queen laughed, a sound like glaciers calving.

"That is all?" the Queen said. "That is what the valley feared? That is what your mother sold a soul to bury?"

"It's not for you," Oscar said.

And she turned — turned her back on the throne, on the Queen, on the offered bargain — and threw everything she had left at the crown.

The bolt struck the silver lattice and the world went white.

* * *

Sound came back first: ringing, enormous, the bell-toll of breaking starlight. Then cold, real cold, the mountain's own breath rushing into the wound she'd made. Oscar was on her knees after all — thrown there by the recoil, blood in her mouth, ears bleeding warmth down her jaw — and above her the crown hung shattered.

Not destroyed. *Shattered.* The lattice had burst apart and the stolen stars hung loose in the air like fruit from a broken branch, dimming, dying, each one guttering toward darkness. And below them, scattered across the black stone floor, glittered the fragments of the vault glass — because the blast had torn through the vault wall too, and hundreds of cases lay cracked open, souls spilling their light across the ground like spilled honey.

Sefa was shouting her name. Ilo was screaming something else. The Queen was rising from her throne with a sound like a mountain deciding to move.

Oscar looked at the broken glass. At the dying stars. At the souls flickering unanchored, beginning to scatter, to fade — freed and lost in the same instant.

*Sefa's voice, from the gorge, from the road, from every night around a frozen fire:* The glass remembers what it held. Glass can be turned. If someone feeds it enough raw power—

There was no one left to feed it. There was only her, and the puddle, and the last swallow already spent.

Except.

Except her magic wasn't only hers. It had never only been hers. It was weather, and weather belonged to the sky, and the sky went on forever past the ceiling of this mountain, past the stone, past the dark — and somewhere above it all, the real stars were still burning, waiting, holding their places like lamps lit for someone expected home.

Oscar pressed both palms flat against the broken glass and stopped holding anything back.

She didn't reach for her own power. There wasn't any. She reached *through* — through the shards, through the memory in the glass, through the thread that had led her across a frozen world to her sister's side — and she pulled the storm down.

It came the way grief comes. All at once. From everywhere.

The mountain shook. Light poured through her — not hers, never hers, borrowed from the sky itself, channeled through the broken glass and the souls it remembered and the girl kneeling in the wreckage of the Court that had stolen them all — and Oscar screamed, because it was too much, because it was exactly enough, because a body was never meant to be a window for the whole sky and she was becoming one anyway.

The dying stars caught.

One by one they flared back to full burning, ripped free of the Court's wire, and shot upward — through stone, through rock, through the mountain's heart as if it were mist — homeward. The whole hall lit like dawn. The glass cases burst open in cascades, and the souls rose, thousands of them, gold and green and blue-white, spiraling up through the shattering ceiling in a river of returning light. Somewhere in the flood Oscar felt her own magic go — felt the last of it pour out of her and not come back, felt the storm-sense that had haunted her whole life go quiet as a held breath released.

The Queen screamed. It was not a triumphant sound.

Then the light took even that away, and Oscar fell forward into the dark, into the warm dark, into silence —

— and heard her name.

"Wren," she whispered.

Her sister stood three feet away.

Not a soul in glass. Not a voice in a thread. *Wren* — sixteen forever, the age she'd been when their mother signed the contract, wearing the dress she'd worn the day the Court's agents came, barefoot on the black stone, glowing faintly at the edges like a lamp seen through a curtain. The freed souls were streaming past her, upward, homeward, and she alone stood still, looking at Oscar with eyes that had watched eleven years pass from inside a bottle.

"You came anyway," Wren said. "I told you not to."

"I've never once done what you told me," Oscar said. Her voice broke on the second word. She tried to crawl toward her and couldn't feel her legs. "Wren — the glass, the bodies — Sefa said —"

"I know what he said." Wren knelt. Her knees made no sound on the stone. She was smiling, and it was the smile from before, from the valley, from the summer that had ended everything, and it was also new — worn smooth by years Oscar couldn't imagine. "My body's been gone a long time, Osc. The soul can't stay without one. The magic gave me back just long enough to—" She stopped. Started again. "Long enough."

"Don't." The word came out small. Eleven years old, exiled, standing at the valley gate while the wagons rolled away. "Don't say goodbye. Don't you dare."

"I'm not going to say goodbye." Wren reached out and touched Oscar's face, and her hand was warm — impossibly, gloriously warm, warmer than anything in this frozen country, warmer than the summer they'd lost. "I'm going to say *thank you*. For coming. For refusing to kneel. For spending every drop of yourself on people you hadn't even met yet." Her thumb traced the scar on Oscar's cheekbone, the one from the night the wards fell. "You were the best part of the life I didn't get to live. Do you understand? Everything good in me went into keeping you safe. It wasn't wasted. Look at you."

Oscar was crying so hard she could barely see her. "It should have been both of us. It should have been — we were supposed to grow old and argue about the beacon harvest and —"

"We will," Wren said. "Different way. You'll talk to me anyway. You always talked to me anyway." She leaned forward and pressed her forehead against Oscar's, and the glow of her bled gently into Oscar's skin, into her chest, settling somewhere behind her ribs like a coal that would not go out. "Every windless night. Listen for me. I was always in the weather, Osc. Even in the valley. Especially in the valley."

"Wren—"

"Let the stars come back," her sister whispered. "Go outside and watch them come back. That's how you say goodbye to me. Not with words. With *morning*."

The light around her thinned. Wren pulled back, and her edges were going soft, dissolving into the river of souls still climbing toward the broken ceiling, and she was still smiling, she was still looking at Oscar, and the last thing she said — the very last thing, in a voice growing quiet as snowfall — was:

"There you are. There's my storm."

And she was gone.

* * *

Oscar woke to Ilo shaking her shoulder and Sefa's coat draped over her and the sound — the impossible, unbearable, beautiful sound — of the mountain opening above them like a flower.

He had blasted a way out. Of course he had; the man who built the flaw could unbuild anything. He carried her up the collapsed stairways and Ilo walked beside them holding a shard of vault glass that glowed like a lantern, and when they came out of the mountainside into the open air, the sky was doing something none of them had ever seen.

The stars were returning.

They came back in rivers, in constellations reassembling themselves like flocks of birds finding their old roosts, and as each one settled into place the frost on the world loosened its grip by a degree, then another. On the horizon, far south, the black of the sky was paling. Not to grey. To the pale gold that comes before dawn.

"The long winter's breaking," Sefa said hoarsely. He sat down heavily on a rock, an old man suddenly, weeping without any shame at all. "Eleven years of forging and it breaks in one night. Because a farm girl from a valley that forgets wouldn't kneel."

Oscar couldn't speak. She lay against Ilo's shoulder and watched the sky fill up with light and felt nothing where her storms used to be — a great quiet emptiness, a room with the windows open — and inside that emptiness, behind her ribs, something small and warm refused to go out.

Far below, the mountain groaned. Deep in its heart, the Hollow Court — throneless, crownless, its vaults emptying into the thawing wind — began to come down.

"We should move," Ilo said, not moving at all, watching the first true sunrise in eleven years bleed up over the edge of the world. "Like... soon. Possibly now."

"In a minute," Oscar whispered.

The sun crested the ridge. Warmth touched her face for the first time since the night the stars went dark, and high overhead, riding the morning wind that was already smelling of meltwater and spring, she heard — or imagined she heard, or would spend the rest of her life believing she heard — a laugh she knew better than her own.

She closed her eyes. Turned her face to the light.

Then the mountain shuddered again, harder, and Sefa was hauling them both upright, and the descent from the dying Court began.
