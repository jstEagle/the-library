## 20. After the Stars Return

The mountain screamed.

Ilo felt it before he heard it — a shudder through the stone floor of the mine-shaft, deep and groaning, like the whole mountain was a bell someone had struck from inside. Dust sifted down from the ceiling in pale ribbons. Somewhere far above them, glass was breaking by the thousands.

Then came the light.

It poured down the drowned shafts like water finding its old channels — silver, then gold, then a color Ilo had no name for, a color that felt like his mother's kitchen before the frost took it, like bread and woodsmoke and the smell of summer grass. It ran along the walls of the tunnel and lit up the paid-away memories sleeping in the stone: faces, voices, small bright moments that people had sold to survive. They rose off the rock like startled birds.

"Oscar," Sefa's voice cracked across the shaft. "Oscar, can you hear me—"

She couldn't have answered if she'd wanted to. Ilo could see her at the edge of the broken vault, kneeling in the wreckage of the winter crown, both hands pressed flat against a shard of star-glass bigger than she was. Her storm-magic was leaving her. He didn't know how he knew that, but he did — the way you know when someone is bleeding out even before you see the wound. The light was pouring *out* of her, through the glass, out into the world, and every bit of it cost her something he couldn't name.

The souls went free all at once.

They rose through the mountain like embers through a chimney — thousands of them, tens of thousands, each one a small burning point of whatever it had been in life. A farmer's laugh. A child's first word. A grandmother's patience, worn smooth as river stone. They streamed past Ilo close enough to touch, and one of them paused beside him — just for a heartbeat — and he felt warmth on his cheek like a hand that remembered him.

"Mama?" he whispered.

But it wasn't her. Her soul had gone home years ago, back to the village that had traded everything for one more winter of survival. This was only a stranger's kindness passing through. Still, he stood there with tears freezing on his face as the souls climbed toward the sky, and he thought: *this is what we were worth. All of us. This much light.*

The Hollow Court died quietly, which surprised him. He had expected thunder. He had expected the Queen's voice, furious and vast, cursing them all. Instead there was only a long sigh through the tunnels — the sound of a great machine running down at last, of contracts expiring, of arithmetic finally coming out wrong. The heralds crumbled where they stood, their hollowed bodies folding like empty coats. The throne hall above them went dark, and stayed dark, and no one mourned it.

The cold broke like ice on a spring river.

Ilo heard it happen — an enormous cracking, rolling away southward over the frozen country, mile after mile after mile, the long winter letting go all at once. Then water. Water everywhere, dripping from ceilings, running down the walls of the mine, pooling around their boots. The drowned shafts were drowning again, but gently now, honestly, the way water was supposed to behave.

"We have to climb," Sefa said. His voice was wrecked. He had Oscar's arm over his shoulders and was half-carrying her toward the ladders, and she was so pale, so *empty*-looking, that Ilo's stomach clenched. "Now, before the melt floods the lower galleries."

"I can walk," Oscar said. It came out as barely a breath.

"You absolutely cannot."

"She can lean on me too," Ilo said, and wedged himself under her other arm before anyone could argue. She weighed almost nothing. That frightened him more than the dark had.

They climbed.

It took hours — up rusted ladders and flooded stairways, through chambers where the freed memories still glowed faintly in the stone, past the tollhouse levels where the memory-ledgers lay blank and useless, their ink run in the wet. At every landing Ilo looked back down into the dark and expected to see something following them. Nothing followed. For the first time in his life, nothing followed.

Near the top, Oscar stirred against his shoulder.

"Ilo," she murmured. "You still there?"

"Where would I go?"

"Smart answer." A ghost of her old crooked smile. "Don't lose it."

Then she was out again, and they climbed on.

* * *

They came out of the mountain through a ventilation shaft near the summit, hauling themselves up over crumbling stone into—

Starlight.

Every star. All of them, back where they belonged, scattered across the sky like salt from a spilled jar, so many and so bright that the snowfields below glowed blue-white under them. Ilo stood at the mouth of the shaft with his head tipped all the way back and forgot how to breathe. He had been nine years old the last time he'd seen stars. He had forgotten there were this many. He had forgotten the way they made you feel small in a good way — small like a seed is small, full of everything you're going to become.

Sefa lowered Oscar onto a flat boulder and wrapped her in both their cloaks. She was awake now, barely, watching the sky too.

"It worked," Ilo said. His voice sounded strange to him, thin and high. "It actually worked. You did it."

"Everyone did," Oscar said. "I just... opened the door."

"You shattered a crown made of stolen stars."

"After Sefa told me how." She reached out without looking and found Sefa's wrist, held on. He let her. "We did it. All three of us. Don't argue, I'm too tired to win properly."

Ilo laughed. It burst out of him sideways, half sob, and once it started he couldn't stop — laughing and crying at the same time, sitting down hard in the snow because his legs had decided they were finished. The two of them let him. That was another thing he was learning about them: they let you fall apart without making it a problem.

Above them, the last of the freed souls were still rising. He watched them go — little lights threading upward between the stars, some pausing as if getting their bearings, some racing straight home. Southward, over the thawing country, he could see them scattering like seeds on wind. Going back to villages and cities and graves and living rooms. Going back to the people who had sold them, maybe, or the people who had missed them, or nobody at all.

"Where do they go," he asked quietly, "when there's no body left to go back to?"

Sefa was quiet a moment. "Wherever they choose," he said. "That was always the difference between them and the Court. Choice."

Ilo nodded and didn't say what he was thinking, which was that he hoped one of them would choose to come find him someday. He wasn't sure he believed in anything enough to say it out loud yet.

* * *

Dawn came slowly, the way dawns do when you've been awake all night and the world has ended and started again.

First the east went grey, then bruise-colored, then a soft burning pink that crept down the mountainside and turned the snowfields to rose quartz. And then — Ilo kept checking, sure he was imagining it — the air began to warm. Not quickly. But steadily, unmistakably, the way warmth used to move before the Court taught the seasons to lie. Frost steamed off the rocks. Meltwater chuckled in the gullies. Somewhere below them, he would swear later, a bird sang one experimental note and then, deciding the coast was clear, sang several more.

"The first warm morning," Sefa said. He stood at the edge of the overlook with his ruined ward-smith's coat flapping in a wind that smelled, impossibly, of green things. "In how long? Two years? Three?"

"Four," Ilo said. "Since my village signed." He counted winters the way other children counted birthdays; it was the only calendar that had mattered lately. "Four years since anyone had to burn furniture to stay alive."

Oscar had fallen asleep again on the boulder, her hand still loosely closed around the shard of star-glass she'd refused to put down. In sleep she looked younger than Ilo had ever seen her — younger than the exile, younger than the storms, just a tired person who had given away almost everything she had and was resting for the first time in longer than she'd admit. The shard in her fist had gone dim and ordinary, just glass now. Whatever lived in it had moved on.

He sat beside her and pulled his knees up and watched the sun come all the way up.

His village was south, two weeks' walk if the roads had thawed. What was left of it. The contract-holders would be dead or scattered; the neighbors who'd fed the Court its payments would be standing in their doorways blinking at the sun, trying to remember how to be people who owned their own warmth. Some of them would pretend it hadn't happened. Some of them would build memorials. One or two, maybe, would actually change. He'd seen enough arithmetic in the last month to know you couldn't sort people into monsters and innocents. Everyone had signed something, somewhere. Everyone had paid someone else's price to keep their own.

But his cousin Brenna had signed *his family's* names. And that, he suspected, was going to take longer than one warm morning to forgive.

"What happens now?"

He didn't realize he'd said it out loud until Oscar's eyes opened.

She blinked at the sky for a while, gathering herself, then pushed up on one elbow. Even exhausted, even hollowed out, she had a way of looking at you like your question deserved a real answer.

"For you?" she said. "Or in general?"

"Either. Both." He shrugged, and it came out smaller than he meant it to. "I don't have anywhere. My village sold itself. My parents are—" He stopped. Started again. "I'm orphaned twice over. Once by the frost and once by the contract. There isn't a word for that. There isn't a place for it either."

Oscar was quiet for a long time. Below them the valley mists were burning off, and Ilo could see for miles — rivers running again, black and bright, forests dripping, the whole frozen world turning soft at the edges.

"When I was six," she said finally, "they exiled me to a valley where my magic couldn't reach. Told me it was for my own safety. And it was safe. It was the safest place in the world." She turned the dead glass shard over in her fingers. "And I spent fifteen years feeling like a coat hung in the wrong closet. Safe, and completely wrong-shaped for the space. Belonging isn't the same thing as safety, Ilo. Nobody told me that until it was too late to matter."

"So what do I do?"

"You find the people who make the wrong shape fit anyway." She smiled, tired and crooked. "Or you build a new shape. Honestly, the second one's more work but better views."

"That's not advice. That's a fortune-teller's riddle."

"I'm powerless and sleep-deprived. Take what you get."

He laughed again — easier this time — and leaned his shoulder against hers, and she let him, and for a while none of them said anything at all. The sun climbed. The meltwater sang. Somewhere down the mountainside, Sefa had started walking the old border-stone lines with a surveyor's eye, already muttering about ward-geometry, already planning, because some men could not look at a broken thing without needing to mend it.

* * *

It was midmorning when Ilo saw the lights coming up the valley.

At first he thought they were more souls — but these moved low, in a loose procession, following the thawed river road toward the mountain. Dozens of them. Then scores. People, he realized. Walking. Carrying lanterns against the bright morning out of habit, because four years of darkness doesn't leave you all at once. Villagers from the frozen bell-road. Survivors from the city that had paid its warmth. Faces he half-knew and faces he didn't, all of them drawn north by the same rumor traveling faster than any of them: *the stars came back. Someone broke the winter.*

"They're coming here," he said.

Oscar shaded her eyes. "Looks like."

"To the mountain. To *you*."

"Not to me." But her voice had gone careful, the way it did when she was hiding something behind dry humor. "To the story. Stories are lighter to carry than gratitude. Give it a week and half of them will swear a knight did it."

"A knight with a temper and no magic left," Ilo said.

"Exactly. Very traditional."

But she was watching the procession come, and Ilo saw her hand drift to her chest — to the place where the storms used to live — and press flat, the way you touch a scar. Powerless. She had bought the whole sky back and paid with the only thing that had ever made her special, and now the world was walking toward her expecting a miracle-worker, and she was going to have to stand there and be a person instead.

He understood that better than she knew. He'd been the boy whose village sold its warmth; he'd been the arithmetic made flesh. People looked at you differently when you were a symbol. It never once helped.

"Sefa," Oscar called down the slope. "Company."

The ward-smith looked up, squinted at the approaching lights, and Ilo watched something complicated cross his face — guilt and hope fighting to a draw. "Word travels fast when the sky turns back on."

"Word travels fast when people are desperate for good news." Oscar got to her feet, swayed, and let Ilo steady her. "What do we tell them?"

"The truth," Sefa said. "For once in my life, the truth. That the Court is dead, the wards need rebuilding, and it will take every pair of hands that shows up." He glanced at her, and then at Ilo, and something almost like a smile touched him. "Starting with ours."

Ilo looked at the crowd coming up the valley road — hundreds now, maybe a thousand, a river of lanterns in broad daylight — and felt the question rise in him again, the one he'd asked on the mountaintop, except this time it had edges and weight:

*What happens now?*

Because the winter was over, and the stories were starting, and somewhere in that crowd were people who had signed contracts and people who had lost everything to them, walking side by side toward the same broken mountain. And standing at the front of it all, hollowed out and human, would be the girl who'd saved them — with no magic, no plan, and no idea what to tell a thousand strangers who needed the world to make sense again.

Oscar took a breath beside him.

"Well," she said. "I suppose we'd better figure out what to say before they get here."

Below them, the first of the lanterns reached the foot of the mountain, and began to climb.
