## 21. Homecoming, Unwritten

The road to the Veiled Valley had never felt long before.

Oscar remembered it as a child — the day the wagons brought her through the pass, the moment the world went quiet around her like a door closing on a noisy room. Back then the silence had been a mercy. Her storms had been breaking windows in the capital, cracking flagstones, making grown courtiers flinch when she sneezed. The valley's dead air had wrapped around her like a blanket soaked in cold water: uncomfortable, but safe. Safe was what her mother had paid for with Wren's soul. Safe was supposed to be enough.

Now she walked that same road with empty hands and an emptier chest, and the silence ahead of her felt less like a blanket and more like a held breath.

She could feel where the veil used to be. That was the cruelest part of losing her magic — not the absence of power, but the absence of *sensing*. For twenty years she had lived inside the valley's hush without knowing she was listening to it, the way you don't hear your own heartbeat until it stutters. Now, cresting the last ridge above the valley, she felt nothing at all where the border wards should have hummed against her skin. Just mountain wind, ordinary and cold, pulling at her patched cloak.

Ilo walked beside her, chattering to fill the quiet the way the child always did — about the thaw, about the first green shoots pushing up through the frost-killed fields below, about whether valley people ate honey or only pretended to. Sefa trailed a few paces back, hands in his pockets, wearing the particular silence of a man returning to the scene of his own crime.

"They'll want to know why," Ilo said. "When they see you. They'll ask what happened up there."

"I'll tell them," Oscar said.

"All of it?"

"All of it." She looked down at the valley — small, gray-roofed, ringed by its broken stones like a hearth ringed by cold ashes. "They sold me nothing and gave me everything, and I left anyway. They deserve the whole truth."

What she didn't say: *and then they will tell me to go, and I will have nowhere to go, and that will be fair.*

* * *

The gate stood open.

That was the first wrongness. In all her years tending the beacon-farm, Oscar had never once seen the valley gate stand open after dusk. Elder Maren had rules the way other people had bones — structural, unarguable. Gate closed at sundown. Strangers questioned thrice. No one crossed the border stones without witness, because the border stones were the only thing between the valley and a world full of people who would trade a child's soul for a season of good weather.

The gate was open, and lamplight spilled through it onto the road, and there were people in the light. Dozens of them. More than Oscar had ever seen gathered in one place outside of the harvest moot.

Her stomach dropped. She had imagined this homecoming a hundred times on the road south from the mountain — rehearsed it like a confession. She had imagined the gate shut, the watch-fires lit, faces at the wall looking down at her with the flat, careful expressions valley folk wore for anything that smelled of magic. She had prepared speeches. *I gave my magic away. I can't call a storm now if I tried. I'm no danger to you.* She had prepared herself for the word *exile* said kindly, which is the worst way to say it.

Instead, an old woman looked up from the gatehouse step, squinted, and said, "Galloway? Stars alive, girl, you look like you've been dragged behind a mule."

"Hello, Tamsin," Oscar managed.

"You have." Tamsin peered past her at Ilo and Sefa. "All three of you. Get in here before the wind does."

And that was it. That was the whole gauntlet. No questions at the threshold, no warding signs, no elder summoned to judge her. Oscar walked through the open gate of the Veiled Valley carrying a dead god's winter in her hair and the ash of a shattered crown still under her fingernails, and nobody so much as paused their work to stare.

It took her a hundred paces to understand why.

The beacon-farm was gone.

Not destroyed — *changed*. Where the rows of dull vigil-stones had stood in their obedient grid, each one a mute sentinel that had never once been allowed to do more than glow faintly at solstice, there was now something alive. Stones half-buried in fresh-turned earth, wired together with copper thread and river-silver, humming — actually humming, a sound Oscar felt in her teeth rather than heard. And everywhere, people. Farmers kneeling in the dirt packing soil around ward-anchors. Children running spools of wire between the rows, shrieking when the current tickled their fingers. Old men who had spent sixty years insisting magic was a debt best unpaid now arguing passionately about grounding-runs.

In the middle of it all, sleeves rolled to the elbow, chalk dust to his shoulders, stood Sefa Anwar — except Sefa was standing right beside her, which meant the man at the center of the beacon-farm was someone else entirely.

"Sefa," Oscar said slowly, "who is that?"

Sefa followed her gaze and made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "That," he said, "is Ward-Mistress Odile Ferran. Who, as of three weeks ago, stopped pretending she'd retired."

Odile Ferran. The name landed in Oscar's memory like a stone in a well. The hermit woman in the far cottage who mended clocks and never came to moot. The one who had once, famously, been asked to leave the capital guilds for reasons no one in the valley would speak aloud.

"She was a ward-smith," Oscar breathed.

"One of the best. Better than me, before the Court got its hooks in me." Sefa rubbed the back of his neck. "When the wards fell and the stars went out, she walked into the moot square, put a piece of the broken border stone on the table, and said, 'Right. Whose fault this is doesn't matter tonight. Fixing it does.' I've never heard anyone end an argument faster."

"But the elder—"

"Maren voted against rebuilding. Said the veil was the valley's protection, that reaching toward magic again was how the Court found us." Sefa's voice went quiet. "The valley voted her down. First time in anyone's memory. Turns out nearly dying of unnatural frost makes people reconsider their relationship with the border stones."

Oscar watched a farmer — Hesta, who had complained every harvest about Oscar's beacon-tending being a waste of good land — carefully lower a new anchor-stone into the earth while a boy held the copper threads taut. The stone caught the lamplight and threw it back in colors Oscar had no names for.

"They're building it different," she said.

"Different and better. The old wards hid you by cutting you off — that was my design, my flaw. A wall that works by making you deaf and blind." Sefa looked at his own hands. "Odile's design listens. The new wards don't cut the valley off from the world. They let the valley *choose*, moment to moment, what comes through. It's harder. It requires tending. It requires—" he hesitated, "—people who can feel weather coming."

Oscar's chest tightened. "There's no one left who can feel weather coming."

"No," Sefa agreed gently. "There isn't."

* * *

They fed her in the moot hall, which had become a mess kitchen, which had become something closer to a festival that refused to admit what it was. Someone pressed a bowl of barley stew into her hands before she'd finished sitting down. Someone else — a girl of maybe ten, born after Oscar's exile began — demanded the story of the mountain, and when Oscar started with *it's not a very nice story*, the whole table leaned in anyway.

So she told it. All of it, the way she'd promised Ilo. The dark stars. The frozen villages. The tollhouse and the memory she'd paid without knowing she'd paid it. The vault full of souls in glass, Wren awake among them. The Hollow Queen's offer — *your sister and your storm, kneel* — and the crown shattering, and the magic pouring out of her like blood from a wound, and the stars coming back one by one like lamps relit in a house everyone had given up on.

She told them about Wren's voice, lasting just long enough to say goodbye.

By the end, the hall had gone quiet in the way halls go quiet when a story has weight enough to hold everyone still. The fire cracked. Somewhere outside, the new wards hummed.

Then Hesta — gruff, practical Hesta, who had never in twenty years said a kind word to Oscar Galloway — reached across the table and gripped her wrist.

"You gave away your storm," Hesta said. Not a question.

"Every last thunderhead."

"And you knew you would. Before you climbed the mountain. You knew, and you went anyway."

Oscar looked down at her bowl. "I didn't know what else the price would be. But yes. I knew it would cost."

Hesta released her wrist and sat back, and said, to the table as much as to Oscar: "My brother lives south, past the frozen villages. Or lived. When the frost came for his town, we sent what we could and prayed the veil would keep us safe, and we told ourselves the veil was wisdom." She snorted. "It was fear with good posture. This girl went out the gate we all stayed behind."

"It wasn't bravery," Oscar said quickly. "It was my sister. Anyone would—"

"No," said old Tam Broadley from the end of the bench, and his voice carried the authority of a man who had buried two wives and most of his friends. "Anyone wouldn't. That's rather the point, girl. Sit with it."

So Oscar sat with it, uncomfortably, while the hall filled again with noise, and Ilo fell asleep face-down in a puddle of honey someone had finally produced, and Sefa talked wire-gauge with Odile Ferran across three tables like a man coming home to a language he'd sworn off.

Later, when the fires burned low, Elder Maren appeared beside her.

Oscar stiffened. Of everyone in the valley, Maren had ruled longest, guarded hardest, and lost the vote most publicly. The old woman lowered herself onto the bench with the care of someone whose joints kept their own ledger, and for a long moment said nothing at all.

"I told them you'd bring ruin back with you," Maren said finally. "When the wards fell. I said, that Galloway girl has storm-blood, and storm-blood draws storms."

"You weren't wrong," Oscar said. "Storms came."

"Mm." Maren studied the fire. "But I notice the storms left. And I notice the stars are back. And I notice that the woman who made both happen is sitting in my hall eating my barley like she belongs at this table." She turned her head, and her eyes were sharp and tired and, unexpectedly, not unkind. "I was wrong about the shape of it, Galloway. I thought safety was a wall. Your mother thought safety was a bargain. You seem to be the first of our line of fools to figure out it's neither."

"My mother wasn't a fool," Oscar said quietly. "She was desperate. There's a difference, and I've spent a whole winter learning it."

Maren was silent a moment. "Yes," she said. "I suppose you have." She rose, joints cracking, and paused. "The moot convenes at dawn. There's a matter on the table that concerns you. Sleep — or don't. You'll be woken either way."

* * *

Dawn came gold and strange.

Oscar had forgotten what dawn looked like with stars fading honestly overhead instead of simply absent. She stood in the moot square among half the valley, Ilo drowsy against her side, Sefa at her shoulder, and listened to Odile Ferran lay out the future in the plain voice of a woman who had retired twice and intended to mean it this time eventually.

"The new wards need three things," Odile said. "Copper, which we have. Time, which we'll make. And tenders — people trained to read the border, feel what presses against it, decide what passes. Not wardens with swords. Tenders. Gardeners, if you like. The valley's safety stops being a wall tonight and starts being a garden, and gardens die untended."

She looked around the square. "We begin teaching at week's end. Any hand willing to learn. And we'll need someone to lead the beacon-farm — someone who knows those stones row by row, who tended them through the years everyone called it wasted land."

Every head in the square turned to Oscar.

Heat crawled up her neck. "I can't," she said, before she'd decided to say anything at all. "You've misunderstood. My magic is gone. All of it. I can't sense the wards any better than—"

"That's not what she asked," said Hesta.

"The beacon-farm was never about your storm," Tam Broadley rumbled. "Twenty years you walked those rows. Twenty years you showed up. We remember who shows up, girl. Magic's a tool. Showing up is a character."

"But I'm—" Oscar's voice caught. She had prepared for exile. She had prepared speeches, defenses, apologies. She had not prepared for *stay*. "I left. I broke the rule. I opened the gate myself and walked out into a dying world, and I'd do it again, and you should want someone steadier than that keeping your border."

"You should want someone *stubborn* enough," said a voice near the front — the ten-year-old from supper, arms crossed, radiating certainty, "because stubborn is what fixed the stars."

Laughter moved through the square, warm and unashamed, and Oscar stood in the middle of it feeling like a door she hadn't known was locked had quietly come open.

Maren stepped forward. "The moot asks, Oscar Galloway: will you stay? Not as protector. Not as sacrifice. Not as the price somebody paid. As neighbor. As teacher, if you'll teach. As the woman who tends the rows." The old woman's mouth twitched. "As the person whose stew bowl we'll keep filling, whether or not she saves the world in any given season."

Oscar looked at Sefa. He was watching her with an expression she'd learned on the road — the careful hope of a man who had built the flaw in the walls and was spending the rest of his life learning to build better ones. He nodded once, small.

She looked at Ilo, who was grinning up at her with honey still somehow in their hair.

She looked at the beacon-farm beyond the square, the living stones humming their new song, the garden already growing.

"Yes," Oscar said. And then, because twenty years of exile had taught her precision: "Yes. I'll stay. As neighbor."

The square cheered, and the sound rolled out over the rebuilt rows and up against the mountains, and the new wards caught it and — Odile swore later — carried it along the whole border like a bell rung once, clean, in welcome.

* * *

Evening found her at the farm alone, walking the rows out of habit older than memory.

The stones hummed under her palms. She couldn't feel the weather coming anymore — that ghost-sense was gone with everything else — but she could feel *this*: the valley's pulse, steady and chosen, a protection that listened instead of hiding. She knelt at the row's end where the ground was still bare and pressed her fingers into the cool soil, thinking about anchor-stones, about what to plant there, about spring.

Behind her, footsteps. Sefa, a lantern in one hand and a coil of copper over his shoulder, stopping a respectful distance away.

"First lesson's in three days," he said. "Odile wants you sitting in. Says tenders learn fastest from someone who loved the stupid stones before they were interesting."

"I'll be there."

He lingered. Lantern-light moved across his face. "Oscar. The letter."

Her heart stumbled. "What letter?"

"The one that came through the new wards this morning. First thing they ever let pass the border." He drew it from his coat — cheap paper, travel-worn, sealed with plain wax. "It's addressed to you. From the southern provinces. From—" he hesitated, "—from someone signing herself *Wren's friend*. Says she has things to return to you. Things Wren left in her keeping, before the end. She says one of them is a letter Wren wrote the year you were exiled. A letter that was never sent."

The wind moved through the beacon-farm, gentle, and the stones sang softly to each other in the dark, and Oscar Galloway — powerless, home, belonging at last — reached out and took her sister's unfinished words into her hands.
