## 22. The Veil Remembers

The border stones had been rebuilt the way old bones knit — crooked in places, stronger at the breaks.

Oscar knelt in the spring mud with her sleeves pushed to her elbows and a beacon-stone cradled against her chest, and she thought: *Wren would have laughed at this.* Her sister had never been able to keep her hands clean. Wren had come home from the orchards streaked green to the shoulder, from the riverbank caked gray to the knee, wearing every season of the valley on her skin like a second name. She would have laughed, and then she would have knelt down beside Oscar without being asked and started digging.

The hole was already deep. Sefa had insisted it be deep — "a beacon-stone is not a fence post, girl, it's an ear" — and Ilo had done most of the digging because Ilo was eleven and made of restless energy and could not bear to watch two adults do slowly what one child could do quickly. The pit smelled of thawed earth and last year's leaves and, faintly, of stone dust where Sefa had chiseled the socket that morning.

"You're holding it wrong," Ilo said, crouching at the edge of the hole, chin on knees.

"I'm holding it like something precious."

"You're strangling it." Ilo reached out, pried one of Oscar's fingers loose with the matter-of-fact authority of someone who had lost everything twice and therefore considered themselves an expert on what mattered. "You hold it like you're afraid it'll leave. It's not going anywhere. That's the whole point of planting things."

Oscar looked at the child — at the sharp face still too thin from the winter, at the frost-scarred fingertips that had never quite regained their color — and did not argue. There was no arguing with Ilo about loss. The boy had paid more of it than anyone should pay before growing their adult teeth, and had come out the other side with a kind of terrible clarity that Oscar both admired and grieved.

"All right," she said. "Show me."

Ilo took the beacon-stone in both hands — it was the size of a heart, which was not an accident; Sefa had carved them all that size and refused to explain why — and lowered it into the socket with the exaggerated care of a child imitating reverence they'd seen but didn't yet feel. The stone settled into the earth with a sound Oscar felt more than heard, a low hum through her knees, through her palms pressed flat against the ground, through the place behind her sternum where her magic used to live.

Nothing happened. That was the design now. Sefa's new wards were quiet ones — no humming borders, no visible shimmer, no storm-light crawling along the valley rim at dusk. He had torn out everything he'd built before, every flaw and every flourish, and started over with a principle he repeated so often the children of the valley had begun chanting it as a skipping rhyme: *A ward is not a wall. A ward is a promise kept quietly.*

This stone would listen for weather. That was all. It would sit in the dark soil at the edge of the Veiled Valley and listen, and if anything unnatural ever came over the mountains again — anything cold and hungry and forged beneath a mountain by hollow hands — it would wake the whole valley gently, the way a mother wakes a child for a festival day rather than a funeral.

Oscar pressed her palms harder against the earth.

She felt it take root. Not with power — she had none, not anymore, not a spark, not a whisper — but with the plain animal sense she'd always had, even as a child in exile, even when she'd hidden it like a shame: the sense of things that moved. Water finding its way down through soil. Roots uncoiling in the dark. The stone drinking the dampness around it, settling, becoming part of the hillside the way a buried seed becomes part of the field.

"There," Ilo said softly. "Now it's staying."

* * *

They planted six stones that day, one for each year of the long winter, though the winter itself had lasted only months. The elders had argued about the number. Elder Maren — who had refused, three years ago now, to walk to the border and look at the scorched wards, and who had spent every day since trying to earn back that refusal — wanted seven, one for each village that had sent survivors to the valley after the thaw. Sefa wanted five, one for each of the original anchor points. In the end they'd compromised on six because six was what the stone-cutters could finish before the ground softened, and everyone pretended this was the reason.

That was how the valley did things now. It argued, and compromised, and pretended the compromise had been the plan all along, and then it built something together. Oscar had spent her childhood believing the valley was a cage dressed up as a sanctuary — quiet, suffocating, a place where safety meant stillness and belonging meant being small enough to fit. She had been wrong, or rather she had been half-right, which was worse. The valley *was* small. But it had turned out that small things, when they held together, could carry enormous weight. A valley had sheltered the survivors of six villages. A valley had taken in a disgraced ward-smith and an orphaned boy and a woman with no magic and no family and no explanation, and had asked nothing of any of them except that they show up to the work.

The work was endless. That was the other thing she'd learned. Belonging wasn't a gate that opened once; it was a roof that needed mending every autumn, a granary that needed turning every month, a border that needed walking every week whether or not anything threatened it. She walked the border now. Everyone did, in rotation, pairs of neighbors with baskets of bread and tools, checking stones, clearing brush, talking about nothing. The first time she'd walked it alone, in the second autumn after the stars returned, she had stopped halfway along the ridge and cried into her sleeve for no reason she could name, and had understood only later that she was grieving the version of herself who had once believed she would die outside these walls, unwanted, unclaimed, a payment's shadow.

She was not unwanted here. She was not unclaimed. She was the woman who had gone into the mountain and come back out, and the valley had decided — without ceremony, without vote, without asking her — that this made her theirs.

* * *

The sixth stone was Wren's.

They saved it for last, and they planted it at the highest point of the border, on the ridge where the veil met the sky, because Sefa said the high stones heard farthest and Ilo said — with the particular stubbornness of a child who had learned grief early — that Wren would want to see everything.

Oscar carried it up the ridge herself. She'd refused help, and no one had offered twice, because the valley had also learned when to let people carry things alone.

The wind was up there. It was almost always up there — the ridge caught every current off the western peaks, and the grass grew bent sideways, combed permanently toward the valley as if the land itself were leaning home. Oscar stood in the bent grass with the stone against her chest and let the wind push at her, and she thought about the night every star had gone dark, and the voice that had called her name in her sister's voice across the broken wards, and how she had spent three years learning the difference between a haunting and a hope.

Wren's soul had come home. That was the truth of it, the plain truth, the truth she built her mornings on: the glass had shattered, the souls had scattered on the thawing wind like seeds, and Wren's soul had flown back to the body that had waited for it in the churchyard clay for twenty years. It hadn't stayed. Souls that spend decades in glass don't fit their bodies anymore; the body forgets the shape of them. Sefa had tried to explain it once, gently, using words like *anchor* and *decay* and *the price of the veil*, and Oscar had stopped him halfway through, because she had been there. She had felt Wren pass through her on the wind like a hand brushing her cheek. She had heard the goodbye.

*"You gave me back the sky,"* Wren had said. *"Don't give them back your grief. Keep it. It's mine. I want you to have something of mine."*

So she kept it. She kept it the way the valley kept its dead — not locked away, not displayed, just carried, folded into the ordinary days, brought out on the ridge tops and the planting days and the nights when the wind died completely.

Sefa climbed up behind her, slower than he used to, his bad leg stiff in the cold. He set down the tools without being asked and began preparing the socket, and neither of them spoke while he worked. They had passed beyond the need for constant speech years ago, somewhere between the frozen gorge where he'd taught her that storms obey want and not anger, and the drowned mineshaft where he'd confessed the flaw he'd built into the first wards, and the long terrible beautiful morning after the stars returned when they'd sat on the mountainside among the freed souls and he had said, "I'm going back to the valley," and she had said, "I know," and that had been the whole conversation, and it had been enough.

He finished the socket. He stepped back.

Oscar knelt and lowered her sister's stone into the earth.

It seated with that same deep hum, and this time — this time, though she knew it was impossible, though she had no magic left to feel it with — the hum went through her like a chord. Like a voice. Like a laugh she'd spent twenty years forgetting and three years relearning, note by note, from fragments of stolen memory and a single goodbye on the wind.

She stayed kneeling long after the others had started down the slope. The sun moved. The shadows of the peaks crept eastward across the valley floor, and the beacon-farms below lit their evening lamps one by one, and somewhere down there Ilo was probably already inventing reasons why the supper needed supervising.

"Wren," she said to the stone, to the ridge, to the open sky. "It's a good view. You were right."

The wind answered, the way wind does, saying nothing and everything, and she got up, and brushed off her knees, and went home.

* * *

The strange nights came in autumn.

Not every autumn. The first time was two years after the planting — a windless October evening, the kind of stillness that settles over the valley like a held breath, when the smoke from the chimneys rises perfectly straight and the beacon-lamps burn without flicker and the whole world seems to be listening for something. Oscar had been sitting on her porch with tea going cold in her hands when she felt it.

Weather coming.

Not nearby. Far — days away, past the western peaks, past the border of maps — a pressure building in the air like a fist closing slowly, a vast slow gathering of wind and water and light. She felt it in her teeth. In her wrists. In the old empty place behind her sternum, the place where her storms used to live, which had been silent since the night she poured them through the shattered glass and relit the sky.

She sat very still and counted her heartbeat and told herself it was memory. Grief does this, Sefa had warned her once, kindly, in the first year: *the body remembers having power the way an amputee remembers having fingers.* Phantom storms. Ghost weather. Nothing more.

But the next morning, a traveler came over the western pass soaked to the bone and swearing that a storm had broken over the far ranges in the night — a storm out of a clear sky, sudden and enormous, the kind the valley hadn't seen since before the veil.

Oscar stood in her doorway with her tea and watched the straight smoke rise from the chimneys, and felt the ghost of thunder rolling somewhere behind her ribs, and smiled.

It happened again the next autumn. And the one after. Always on windless nights, always distant, always arriving a few days before the weather itself — as if some part of her, some splinter of the storm-self she'd surrendered into the veil, still stood at the world's edge keeping watch. As if the price she'd paid had not been a severing but a scattering, and pieces of her were out there in the moving air, doing what she had always done best: feeling the sky turn.

She told Sefa on the third occurrence. He listened with his cup halfway to his mouth, and then he set the cup down very carefully, the way he did when he was about to say something he'd been thinking about for a long time.

"The veil doesn't destroy what's given to it," he said. "I designed it. I should know. It holds. It keeps. Everything you poured into it that night — every storm, every spark — it's still in there. Held. Kept." He looked at her with an expression she'd learned to read as tenderness wearing a scholar's clothes. "Maybe the veil isn't a wall between the valley and the world. Maybe it never was. Maybe it's a membrane. And maybe some things pass through it both ways."

"And my storms are knocking," Oscar said.

"Your storms," Sefa said, "are saying hello."

She thought about that for a long time. She thought about it through that whole autumn and into the winter, on the border walks and the long evenings, and she came to a conclusion that surprised her with its gentleness: she didn't want it back. Whatever lived in the veil now, whatever ghost of her rumbled across the far ranges on still nights — it belonged to the sky. She had given it to save the stars, and the stars burned, and the bargain was complete, and she was not a woman who reneged.

But she listened. Every windless night, she sat on her porch with her tea and she listened, and sometimes — not often, not reliably, but truly — the horizon whispered back.

* * *

On the fifth anniversary of the night the stars returned, the valley held a festival, because the valley had learned that survival deserved marking. There were lanterns on the beacon-farms and music in the square and Ilo — sixteen now, tall and sharp-faced and apprenticed to Sefa, with stone dust permanently under his fingernails — dancing badly with a girl from the eastern farms and not caring who saw.

Oscar danced too, a little. Then she slipped away, as she always did on that night, and walked up to the ridge alone.

The wind was blowing hard, which meant nothing would come to her tonight, and she was glad of it. Some nights were for listening. This one was for speaking.

She sat beside Wren's stone — five years rooted now, moss softening its shoulders, the socket-seal Sefa had carved worn smooth by her hand — and she talked. About the harvest. About Ilo's disastrous dancing. About the new families in the southern villages, rebuilding, naming children after the thaw. About Sefa, who had finally, after years of deflection, allowed the valley to put his name on the rebuilt wards — not as their maker, but as their mender — and who had wept at the ceremony and denied it ever after.

"I miss you," she said, at the end, because some years the ending was simple. "And I'm all right. Both things. You'd understand that better than anyone."

She pressed her palm flat against the warm stone, and stood, and turned toward home — and stopped.

The wind had died.

All at once, between one breath and the next, the ridge went utterly still. The bent grass lifted. The lanterns down in the valley burned steady. The silence was total, enormous, the held-breath hush she knew better than her own name.

And in the empty place behind her sternum, something stirred.

Far away — past the peaks, past the maps, out where the world ended and the sky began — a storm was gathering. She could feel it building, vast and slow and bright, pressure folding over pressure, lightning waiting inside the cloud like a word inside a mouth. Closer than it had ever come before. Days closer. Hours, maybe.

And threaded through the thunder, faint as a pulse, familiar as a laugh —

— not her own.

Oscar stood on the ridge in the windless dark with her hand over her heart, and the storm rolled nearer, and nearer, carrying something in it that knew her name.

Down in the valley, the first lanterns of the festival flickered.

Every star above her burned.
