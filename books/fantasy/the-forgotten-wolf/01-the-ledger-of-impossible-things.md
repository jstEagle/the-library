## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

There are three rules to keeping the ledgers of the Guild of Impossible Things, and I wrote all three myself, which is either a great honor or a warning about what happens when you stay somewhere too long.

Rule one: write everything down. Rule two: trust nothing written down that you don't remember writing. Rule three, the one apprentices always argue with: *the ledger does not record the trade. The ledger records the cost.*

Anyone can record a trade. A man trades his shadow for his brother's name back from a river spirit; fine, ink it in, next customer. But the shadow is not the price. The shadow is only the coin. The price is that he will never again be able to stand in afternoon light without flinching, never remember why, and will spend the rest of his life feeling like half a man without knowing which half he's missing.

That's the cost. That's what goes in my columns.

I was balancing the autumn quarter on the morning everything was still ordinary — the third Tuesday of Frostfall, if we're being precise, and I am always precise, because imprecision is how guilds get burned down. Quill behind my ear, spectacles on my nose, a pot of black tea going cold at my elbow the way tea does when you're the sort of person who finds arithmetic more comforting than company.

The counting room of the Guild of Impossible Things is not impressive. Visitors expect something grander — vaulted ceilings, floating candles, a talking raven at minimum. What they get is a low room above a chandler's shop on Candlewick Lane, smelling of tallow and old paper, with one window that looks out on absolutely nothing interesting and one door at the back that looks out on everything.

But we'll come to the door.

* * *

The first entry of the day belonged to a cooper named Merrit Stave, who arrived at the ninth bell looking like a man who had rehearsed a speech for three days and then forgotten all of it on the stairs.

"I want," he said, "to sell my shadow."

I set down my quill. "Sit down, Master Stave."

He sat. He was a big man, barrel-chested, hands like hams, and they were shaking. "It's my daughter. She's sick. There's a woman up Graywater way — they say she can pull sickness out like drawing a splinter, but she asks a price, and the price is—"

"A memory," I said. "I know her work. Maren of Graywater. She'll take the memory of your daughter's laughter, or your wife's face, or whatever else she judges dearest, and she'll take it clean, and you'll walk home lighter and not know what you're missing."

Merrit stared at me. "Then why would anyone—"

"Because love makes terrible accountants." I turned to a fresh page. "You don't want to sell your shadow. You want me to tell you there's another way."

"Isn't there?"

And here is where I should confess something: I am very good at my job, and part of being good at my job is knowing exactly which lies are mercies. So I said, "There might be," and I spent an hour writing letters, and by dusk I had found a hedge-healer in Lowmarsh who took payment in honest silver and had a survival rate that was merely bad instead of miraculous.

Merrit left with his shadow intact and a letter of introduction, and before he went he said, "Why do you do this? The guild, I mean. You could take the trades. Everyone says the guild takes any trade."

"The guild takes any trade," I agreed. "I just decide which ones it takes today."

He looked at me the way people look at locked doors they're grateful for, and left, and I opened the ledger and recorded the whole thing under *Trades Declined*, because rule one applies even to the trades that don't happen. Especially to those.

* * *

The second entry was a real one, and it's the kind I want you to understand, so pay attention.

A young woman came in at the eleventh bell, hood up, rain still dripping off the hem of her cloak. She didn't give a name. She put a small clay jar on my desk and said, "I want to trade my shadow for a name."

Whose name, I asked. She said she couldn't say it out loud, not anymore, because saying it made her hope, and hoping had become unbearable. Someone she loved had gone into the war in the east and hadn't come back, and there was a necromancer in the fens — there is always a necromancer in the fens — who could speak the names of the dead, once, truly, so the bereaved could hear them say goodbye. But the necromancer's price was a shadow, freely given, and shadows are not nothing. A man without a shadow casts no reflection in water, cannot cross running water uninvited, and is followed, all his life, by dogs.

"You understand the cost?" I said. "Not the shadow. The cost."

She looked at me with the flat calm of someone who has already grieved everything there is to grieve. "I won't remember wanting to hear him. That's the cost, isn't it? Whatever memory the shadow buys with. I've heard how it works."

"It works however the trade works," I said. "Memory is the only currency that matters, because memory is the only thing that's truly yours. Everything else — coin, land, blood — belongs to the world. Memory belongs to you alone. So that's what the world charges."

"And after?"

"After, you'll have sold something you can't inventory. You'll know the shape of the hole. You'll feel it every day, the way an amputee feels weather in a missing limb. And you will not know what used to live there."

She was quiet a long moment. Rain ticked against the window.

"He used to sing," she said finally. "Badly. If the trade takes that — if it takes the singing — I'd rather it did. I'd rather lose the song than spend sixty years wondering."

So I opened the ledger to the current page, and I wrote it out properly, in my best hand, because every trade deserves good penmanship:

*Received: one shadow, whole and willing, from a woman of no given name.*
*Paid: passage to the fen-necromancer, and one true speaking of a dead man's name.*
*Cost, borne by trader: [to be determined by the trade itself].*
*Witnessed: Felix Thackeray, Keeper of Ledgers.*

She signed. Her hand shook only a little. Then she unpinned her shadow from her heels the way you'd unpin a wet stocking — it came away with a sound like a sigh — folded it into the clay jar, and sealed it, and set it on my desk among the other jars, and walked out into the rain lighter than she'd come in, and I sat there feeling the particular grief of my profession, which is that I help people and hate it.

That's the job. That's the whole job. We are the bank where the world stores what it can't bear to keep, and somebody has to count the deposits, and somebody has to guard the vault.

Which brings us to the door.

* * *

At the back of the counting room, past the shelf of jars and the cabinet of confiscated wishes, there is a door.

It doesn't look like much. Grey wood, iron hinges, no handle on this side — you knock, and it decides. It has stood at the back of the guild since before the guild had a name, since the founder — old Mother Corvane, may she rest uneasily — drove its frame into the wall and told us: *This is the Hollow Gate. Everything traded passes through it. Nothing traded comes back through it unchanged.* It opens onto wherever the trade needs to go. Fen-huts. Palaces. Once, allegedly, the inside of a dream. The traders call it the Door, capital D, and they touch the frame for luck, and I let them, because superstition is cheaper than insurance.

My second duty, after the ledgers, is the Gate. I oil the hinges — it drinks lamp oil, refuses tallow — I sweep the threshold, and I keep the tally of what passes through. In and out. Every day. Forty years the guild has stood, and forty years the Gate has kept its accounts as faithfully as I keep mine.

Until this month.

Here is the hairline crack in the porcelain, the thing I have told no one, because telling it aloud would make it real and I am, despite everything, a coward before bedtime:

For eleven nights now, the Hollow Gate has been whispering my name.

Not *Felix Thackeray*. Not the full formal thing, the way the ledgers have it. Just — *Felix*. Softly. The way you'd wake someone you loved. A voice like dry paper turning, like wind through a keyhole, and every night a little clearer, and every night a little more patient, the way a thing sounds when it knows it will be answered eventually.

I have checked the hinges. I have checked for drafts, for pigeons in the walls, for apprentices playing games (we have no apprentices; I fired the last one for licking a wish). I have stood before the Gate with a candle at midnight and demanded, in my best ledger-keeper's voice, that it state its business, and it said nothing, and I felt foolish, and I went to bed.

And last night — this is the part I keep circling — last night it didn't whisper.

Last night it knocked.

Three times, slow, from the other side. And then, in a voice I would swear on every ledger I have ever kept, in a voice I knew, though I could not say from where or when:

*"Felix. Open the door. It's almost time."*

I did not sleep. I sat up with the ledgers and the cold tea and told myself: doors knock. Doors are knocked on. Some traveler lost between trades, some poor soul the Gate swallowed decades ago, coming home at last. There is a rational explanation. There is always a rational explanation; that is what the rational explanations are *for*.

Then, at dawn, with the frost still on the windows, I heard the criers.

They were a long way off, down by the river square, but the wind carried it in fragments through the shutters — the royal cadence, the one they use for wars and taxes:

*By order of the Crown... by order of the Crown...*

I put down my pen. I listened. And the words came through the frost-bright air one at a time, like coins dropped one by one into an empty jar:

*"...a hunt... declared... for all those bearing the gift of unbinding..."*

I looked at the ledgers. Forty years of them. Names, trades, costs — my handwriting on every page, evidence enough to hang a hundred people like me.

I looked at the Gate.

And the Gate, very softly, in the voice I knew, said: *"Now, Felix."*
