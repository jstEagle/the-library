## 2. A Proclamation in Ash

The trouble with running a guild of impossible things, I have found, is that the impossible has no respect for bookkeeping. It arrives when it likes, in whatever condition it likes, and it never, ever includes a receipt.

I was balancing the autumn column when the criers came.

You could hear them before you saw them—three bells, then the flat cry of the crown's voice, rolling down Copperlane like a barrel dropped on stairs. I set down my pen. I have learned to set down my pen when the crown raises its voice; ink and panic do not mix.

Through the ledger room's one window I watched them pass at the corner of Gilders' Row: two criers in ash-grey livery, flanked by soldiers, unrolling a proclamation with the ceremony of men who believe paper is a weapon. The herald's voice carried, pitched for crowds:

*"By order of His Majesty, let it be known: all persons bearing the gift of unbinding shall surrender themselves to the Crown's inquisitors within seven days. Those who conceal such persons are traitors. Those who harbor them are traitors. There is no sanctuary. There is no appeal. The Hunt is opened."*

Unbinding. My pen stayed exactly where I had left it, because my hand had stopped being entirely mine.

Let me explain what the gift is, since the crown was about to pretend it had never heard of it. Every trade through the Hollow Gate costs memory—that is the first law, older than the guild, older than the door itself. But some few of us can work the trade in reverse. We can take a binding off a thing: unhook a name from a shadow, loose a curse from a bloodline, open a memory that someone else has nailed shut. Unbinding. Rare enough that the guild kept perhaps six of us in its rolls, scattered across the country like seeds nobody wanted to plant in the same field.

And rare enough that every king for two hundred years had signed the same quiet compact: *the guild stays neutral, the crown stays blind.* We did not ask what the palace traded away. The palace did not ask what we kept in our ledgers. It was not friendship. It was arithmetic. Both sides understood that some sums are better left undone.

Now the sum was being shouted from every corner in the city.

I watched the soldiers nail the proclamation to the customs post, watched a shopkeeper read it and go carefully, deliberately blank, the way people do when reading might be dangerous. Then I went downstairs, unlocked the strongbox under the floor, and took out the guild's charter—the original, signed by the founder, with the crown's seal countersunk beside her mark. Neutrality, written in wax and vellum.

It looked smaller than it had yesterday. Documents do that, when the world changes around them.

* * *

The courier arrived at dusk, which should have been my first warning. Guild couriers keep daylight hours; night roads are where memories get thin and things follow you home. The second warning was that he came through the front door without knocking, which no courier does, because the front door of the Guild of Impossible Things is warded against exactly that kind of confidence.

He crossed the threshold anyway, trailing smoke.

Not metaphorical smoke. He was burned—hands, forearms, the left side of his face gone to raw red weals, his courier's coat scorched to lace along one sleeve. He was maybe nineteen. He was crying without any expression at all, the way people cry when the part of them that organizes grief has been traded or taken.

"Felix Thackeray," he said. Not a question. "Keeper of ledgers. Warden of the gate." He recited it like a list he'd been made to memorize by someone frightening. "I'm supposed to give this only to you."

"Sit down," I said. "There's a salve—"

"There's no time." He laughed, a small broken sound. "That's the joke. There's never time anymore. They were waiting at the Ashford relay. Inquisitor's men. Grey coats. They knew the route." He held out his right hand, palm up, fingers curled around something. "They knew the route, Mistress Thackeray. Nobody knows the routes except the guild."

I took what he offered.

It was a page of ledger paper, royal stock—heavy, cream-colored, watermarked with the crowned stag that the palace archive uses for nothing less than the king's own accounts. Torn, not cut. Torn in haste, by someone who wanted it out of a binding more than they wanted it whole.

And I knew the hand.

I want to be precise about this moment, because precision is the last honest thing I own. I did not recognize the handwriting immediately. I recognized the *binding*. The page was wrapped in one—a threadwork of pale script stitched through the margins, the kind of seal an apprentice learns first and a master never quite stops using. A memory-binding, holding the words beneath it shut. I have opened hundreds. I know their weight in the hand the way a midwife knows a child's.

This one was mine. Or near enough to mine that the difference was years, not hands.

"Who wrote this?" I asked the courier.

"I don't—" He pressed his burned hand against his eyes. "They took it out of me. What I was carrying. They took the message out of my head so I couldn't be made to say it, and they left the paper, and the paper screams, Mistress, it screams all the way from the capital, I've heard it for three days—"

"It doesn't scream," I said gently. "It's bound. Bound things feel loud to people who've been emptied. You've been *read*, boy. Someone went through you looking for this."

"The grey coats," he whispered. "And after them, others. Worse ones. Things that smelled like cold iron and old meat. I ran. I ran until the road forgot itself." He looked up at me with the terrible clarity of the half-mad. "Is it true? What the paper says?"

I laid the page flat on my desk. I am, before anything else, a keeper of records, and there is a procedure for opening a binding, even one screaming, even one wearing my own younger hand. Salt at the corners. The counter-phrase. Three breaths, no more.

The binding parted like a scab.

The page said:

*Item the eleventh, private account of the Crown. Paid into the royal ledger: one hunger, unnamed, of foreign origin. Accepted by His Majesty in exchange for the war's ending. Terms: the Crown will feed it. Note appended in the archivist's hand: His Majesty no longer recalls consenting. Recommend the item be rebound. Recommend it be rebound soon.*

Below that, torn off mid-sentence, the beginning of a second entry: *The binding holds, but it does not imprison. It keeps it patient. God forgive whoever made it, because it grows fat on—*

I sat down. The chair received me the way furniture receives bad news.

An apprentice's hand. Pale threadwork. The style of the seals we taught twenty years ago, back when I was the youngest binder the guild had taken in a generation, back when I was sent—once, once only—to the capital, to the sickroom of a king who had stopped sleeping, to lay a binding on whatever had come into him with the peace treaty.

I had believed I was healing him. I had been twenty-two years old, and proud of the work, and the archivist had thanked me, and I had ridden home light as a girl with no debts.

*It keeps it patient.*

The courier was watching me. "You know whose hand that is," he said.

"I know whose hand it looks like," I said, which was true, and also the most cowardly sentence I have spoken in ten years.

Outside, the criers had reached the far end of Copperlane, and their bells sounded different now—or I heard them differently, which amounts to the same thing. *All persons bearing the gift.* Six names in the guild's rolls. One of them mine. And somewhere in the capital, a king who no longer recalled consenting to anything, wearing a crown that was eating him course by course, kept patient by a binding I had stitched myself and signed with pride.

The guild's neutrality had lasted two hundred years. It had died tonight on my desk, between a torn page and a burned boy, and I found I could not even say what had killed it: the crown's hunger, or ours, or mine specifically, mine most of all.

"Mistress Thackeray," the courier said. "The people who sent me. They said if the paper reached you, one more thing would follow it. They said to tell you: *he's coming north. He remembers you.*"

I looked up. "Who is?"

The boy's ruined face did something complicated—fear and pity fighting for the same ground.

"The king's brother," he said. "The exiled one. Aldous Thackeray."

The fire in the grate had burned down to coals while I wasn't watching, and the whole room seemed suddenly lit the color of that torn page, and somewhere behind the wall—behind the Hollow Gate, in the dark where the trades go—I heard the door whisper my name.

It had been doing that for weeks. I had told myself it was the wind.

It was not the wind.
