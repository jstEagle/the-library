## 3. The Brother in Exile

The courier's page sat on my desk for two days before I could bring myself to touch it again.

I had done what any sensible keeper would do. I locked it in the iron box beneath my floor, weighted it with three ledgers and a stone that had once been a man's grief, and told myself the matter was settled. The guild did not involve itself with crowns. Crowns involved themselves with everyone; that was the whole trouble with them. Our neutrality was older than the current dynasty and would outlast it, provided nobody did anything foolish.

Then, on the third night, someone knocked on the guild door with the particular rhythm of a man who had been followed for a very long time and had finally stopped caring.

Three knocks. A pause. Two more. Then nothing — not because he'd finished, but because he'd collapsed against the door. I heard the slide of him going down, the soft clatter of something metal.

I opened it with a poker in one hand and a lie ready in the other.

He was soaked through, thinner than I remembered, and wearing the kind of hood that says *I have paid good money to look unremarkable*. But there was no mistaking Aldous Thackeray. Exile had sharpened him the way weather sharpens a coastline — taken away the softness and left the shape. His eyes were the same, though. That was the unfair part. Ten years, and the eyes hadn't changed at all.

"Felix," he said, from the ground, with the ghost of his old crooked smile. "You've kept the step swept."

"You're bleeding on it," I said. "That's hardly keeping it swept."

I got him inside, which was, I want it noted, a decision made entirely out of professional obligation regarding the doorstep.

* * *

Here is what you need to know about Aldous Thackeray, and why I am going to be careful about how I tell it.

Ten years ago he was not an exile. He was the second son, the spare, the one who rode through the lower city without guards because he liked the noise of it. He used to come to the guild as a young man — officially to inspect our charter, unofficially to read by our fire and ask questions no prince should have been asking about memory-trades. What does a forgotten name feel like, he wanted to know. Does the person who gave it up miss it? Can you trade a memory twice?

I answered him the way I answer everyone: precisely, and slightly less honestly than I pretend.

And somewhere in those answers, between the ledger lines, we fell into something neither of us ever wrote down. There was never a declaration. There was a winter of shared suppers, a spring of arguments about whether love could be entered in a ledger as a debt or only as a loss, and one evening in the archive room when he kissed me and I let him, and then — because I was twenty-three and a coward with excellent penmanship — I wrote in the margin of my private book: *noted, but do not act upon it further.*

I acted upon it further for eleven months.

Then the king began to sicken, and Aldous was blamed — whispered about, accused of sorcery, of poisoning, of dealings with the guild that no honest man would have. He fled rather than hang. And I, keeper of the ledgers, keeper of neutrality, said nothing in his defense. I recorded his exile in the guild annals in my own neat hand: *Aldous Thackeray, departed under cloud.* Four words. I have hated every one of them for ten years.

That is the history. Now back to the man dripping on my floor.

* * *

I put him by the fire in the reading room, fed him broth, and pretended not to notice how carefully he watched the door.

"The page," he said finally. He set something on the table between us, wrapped in oilcloth. "You've seen it. The courier came through here?"

"Did you follow the courier?"

"I followed the rumor of the courier." He unwrapped the cloth with hands that weren't quite steady. "Felix. It's torn from the royal ledger. The royal ledger. Which means someone in the palace has been trading memories for the crown — or having them traded. And the hunt Vane is running isn't a purge. It's a search. She's not looking for unbinders to punish her. She's looking for whoever bound the king, because whoever bound him can unbind him."

"And if she finds them first, she'll use them," I said. "Or kill them. With Vane it's usually both, in whichever order is convenient."

"So we find them first."

"*We*?" I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Aldous. You are the most wanted man in the kingdom sitting in the most neutral building in the kingdom. If I so much as pour you a second cup of broth, the guild burns with me in it."

"You already poured the second cup."

"That was hospitality. Hospitality is within charter."

He looked at me across the firelight, and for a moment ten years fell off him like a wet cloak, and he was twenty-six again, arguing that love was a debt, and I was twenty-three again, writing *do not act upon it further* in a margin while my heart acted upon it constantly.

"The page," he said quietly. "There's a notation on it. A binding mark. Old style, apprentice work — but skilled. Whoever made that binding knew exactly what they were doing, and they did it believing they were helping. That's what frightens me. Not malice. Help."

My hands went still around the broth bowl.

Because I knew the mark. Of course I knew the mark. I had spent two days not looking at it, and now I looked, and there it was in the firelight: the interlocked circles, the descending stroke, the little hook at the end like a question that had given up asking.

It was my hand. My nineteen-year-old hand. The binding I had made as an apprentice, the year the king nearly died of a fever that ate his mind — the binding Master Corvus had stood beside me for, guiding my strokes, telling me *this will hold the sickness, child, this will keep him whole.* I had believed I was healing him. I had gone home that night and slept like the innocent.

"Aldous," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "How old do you think this binding is?"

"Old. Ten years, perhaps—"

"It's mine."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs the Hollow Gate whispered my name, soft as a moth against glass, and for once I wished it would whisper louder and swallow me.

"I made it," I said. "The winter the king sickened. I was an apprentice. Corvus brought me to the palace himself, told me the king was dying of a hunger-fever, told me a binding would hold it. I thought—" I stopped. Started again. "I thought I was saving him. I signed the ledger entry myself. *Binding rendered, patient stabilized.* Stabilized. Like he was a leaking roof."

Aldous didn't move. That was the worst of it — he didn't recoil, didn't curse me, just watched me with those unchanged eyes while everything I had built my contentment on quietly collapsed.

"Corvus," he said at last. "The founder."

"He died eight years ago."

"Did he?" Something moved behind his face. "Felix, listen to me. Whatever you bound that winter — it's still there. Bindings don't cure. They cage. And whatever's in my brother has had ten years in a cage to grow patient, and strong, and hungry. You didn't heal him. You bought him time, and the thing you caged has been spending it."

"The proclamation," I said slowly. "The hunt. Vane isn't hunting unbinders to destroy them."

"She's hunting *you*. Or whoever like you. Because a binding that strong needs its maker to undo it — or someone willing to pay what the unbinding costs." He leaned forward. "And Felix — unbindings always cost memory. What do you suppose it costs to unbind a king?"

I didn't answer. I was looking at the torn page, at my own handwriting from another life, and doing arithmetic I didn't want to finish.

Everything traded costs memory. I had built my whole careful life on that rule, balanced it nightly in my ledgers like a woman counting spoons. And now the rule had turned around and was counting me.

"They'll come here," I said. "When Vane works out whose hand that is. She'll come here, and the charter won't save us, and the ledgers—" I stopped, because the next part was unsayable: the ledgers held every trader's name for forty years. Every soul who'd ever sold a shadow, a name, a first kiss, a grief. In Vane's hands, that book wasn't a ledger. It was a list of hostages.

"Then burn them," Aldous said simply.

"I can't—"

"You can. You're the only one who can." He reached across the table, and before I could decide whether to allow it, he covered my hand with his. Warm. Steady. Exactly as I remembered, which was precisely the problem. "You told me once that everything traded costs memory. So tell me what it costs to keep those people safe."

Upstairs, faint and patient, the Hollow Gate whispered my name again.

And for the first time in ten years, I considered answering.
