## 4. What the Crown Remembers

The archive beneath the palace had no door, which was the first thing I liked about it.

Doors can be watched. Doors can be barred, bribed, burned. But the archivists of Vellmarin had built their vault the way a miser builds a fortune—by pretending it did not exist. There was no entrance in any blueprint. You reached it through the well shaft behind the kennels, down a ladder of iron rungs slick with two centuries of condensation, into a corridor that smelled of cold stone and older grief.

Felix went first. Of course she did. She always went first when there was something to be afraid of, and she went last when there was something to be trusted. It was one of the thousand small things I had catalogued about her during our years apart, the way a man starving catalogues recipes.

"Rung," she said, from below.

"I know."

"You're counting them."

"I'm counting everything," I said. "It's what Thackerays do instead of praying."

She laughed, low, and the sound bounced off the wet stone and came back to me changed, warmer, as if the dark had kept it a moment and returned it with interest.

* * *

The corridor ended at a wall of drawers.

Not cabinets—drawers, thousands of them, floor to ceiling, each no bigger than a hymnal, each fitted with a small brass plate bearing a name and a date. The names ran on for what looked like miles. Some of the plates were green with age. Some were bright as new coin. And some—the ones that stopped my breath—were blank, polished smooth, as if whatever they had once held had been eaten out of them.

Felix ran her fingers along a row of plates like a woman reading braille. "These are memories," she said. "Withdrawn ones. Trades."

"The guild's ledger said the crown was its best customer."

"The guild's ledger says a great many things." She pulled a drawer. It slid out silent on oiled runners, and inside, nested in grey felt, lay a small glass vial stoppered with wax. The liquid inside moved. Not sloshed—*moved*, deliberately, the way a sleeping thing turns toward warmth. "Oh," Felix said softly. "Oh, this is wrong. This is so much worse than wrong."

"What is it?"

"This isn't stored memory. Stored memory is still. It's a record; it doesn't want anything." She held the vial up to the little lamp she'd brought, and the liquid pressed itself against the glass toward her hand, hungry, patient. "This is memory that's been *fed on*. Something drank most of it and corked the rest."

I thought of my brother then. Edmund, who had been the easiest boy in the world to love and the hardest to know, who laughed at everything and remembered almost nothing of his childhood—not the fall from the orchard wall, not our mother's face, not the winter the fever took half the household and left him the heir. We had all called it a blessing. Grief forgets itself, we said. The mind protects the heart.

"Edmund," I said.

"Yes," Felix said. "I think so too."

* * *

We found the royal drawer an hour later, and I will tell you honestly: I wished we hadn't.

It sat alone in a alcove at the corridor's end, apart from the common rows, in a case of black iron banded with silver—silver, the old warding metal, the metal the guild used before it learned cheaper ways. The brass plate read EDMUND THACKERAY, and beneath the name, in a clerk's careful hand, a number that meant nothing to either of us until Felix translated it.

"That's a count," she whispered. "That's how many withdrawals. Aldous—" Her voice did something I had never heard it do. It cracked. "There have been four hundred and six withdrawals from this drawer. Four hundred and six times someone has opened my brother's head and taken something out of it."

"Since when?"

She checked the earliest entry scratched inside the case door. "Forty years ago."

Forty years. Edmund was forty-three. They had been feeding on him since he was a child, since before the crown, since before any of us understood what a king was for. I put my hand flat against the iron and felt—this is the part I would swear to on any altar you like—a faint warmth, like a body sleeping under blankets.

Inside the case, beside the drawer, lay the archive's master record for the royal account: a slim book bound in the same black leather as the guild's ledgers, because of course it was. The guild had built this place. The guild had sold the crown its first withdrawal and every one after, and had told itself, all the while, that keeping accounts was the same as staying innocent.

Felix opened the book and read aloud, and her voice got quieter with every line.

"*Subject presents recurring parasitic attachment. Designation: the Hunger. Feeds exclusively on episodic memory. Growth commensurate with intake.*" She turned pages. "*Note, year twelve: subject's childhood fully consumed. Parasite has begun preferring recent memory. Note, year twenty: parasite no longer waits for scheduled withdrawal. Note, year thirty-one:*"—she stopped.

"Read it," I said.

"*Year thirty-one: the Hunger has begun to speak in the subject's voice. Court attributes changes to temperament. Recommend increased intake to maintain dormancy. The fuller it feeds, the gentler it lies.*"

The lamp flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft.

"The fuller it feeds, the gentler it lies," Felix repeated. "Aldous, do you understand? Everyone thinks if we starve it, it weakens. It's the opposite. Every proclamation, every hunt, every burning—it isn't cruelty for its own sake. The Hunger is *hungry*. It's eating the kingdom's memories now because Edmund's are nearly gone."

"And when his are gone entirely?"

She didn't answer. She turned to the last page instead, the freshest ink, dated nine days past, and read the final entry in a voice gone almost to nothing:

"*Withdrawal attempted: subject's own name. FAILED. Subject retains name by unknown means. Parasite agitation extreme. Estimated capacity remaining: one memory. One.*"

One.

The word sat in the cold air between us like a coin on a table.

"One more memory," Felix said, "and it's full. And the last thing left in my brother's head is his own name. When it takes that—" She closed the book. "When a man forgets his name, there's nothing left to be king *of*. The Hunger won't need to wear the crown anymore. It'll just *be* the crown. And it will rule a country that can't remember why it ever loved anything."

* * *

We were three steps from the ladder when the lamps along the corridor began to light themselves.

Not all at once. One, then the next, then the next, a line of small flames running away from us deeper into the dark, deeper than the archive should have gone, lighting a passage I had sworn did not exist on our way in. And from somewhere down that impossible length, unhurried, courteous, in a voice I knew better than my own—

"Aldous?" it called. "Is that you, little brother? You've come such a long way. Come and let me look at you. I've missed your face."

Felix's hand found mine in the dark. Her fingers were ice.

"It's using his voice," she breathed.

"No," I said, because I had finally understood the shape of the thing we were hunting, and understanding is its own kind of horror. "Listen again. That's not it wearing him. That's forty years of practice. It *is* him now, as far as anyone up there knows—and it knows we know the difference."

The lamps burned on, patient as interest accruing.

And far above us, through forty feet of stone, we heard the palace bells begin to ring—not the hour, not the alarm, but the particular pattern every child in Vellmarin knew: a royal summons. The king wanted his brother found.

The Hunger had one memory left to eat.

And it had just decided what it wanted for dessert.
