## 5. The Hunt Finds Her

The ledgers burned beautifully.

That was the first thought Felix Thackeray had, standing in the guild's counting room with a candle in one hand and forty years of impossible accounting going up in smoke around her, and she hated herself for it. But there was no denying it: the pages caught with a soft golden rush, ink flaring silver as the trades recorded on them came loose, and for one absurd moment the whole room smelled like a festival—roasted chestnuts, hot wine, the sugar-dust of fair days that half these entries had been traded away in exchange for.

A shadow, sold by a miller's son who wanted to be brave in the dark. A name, given up by a woman fleeing a marriage. The memory of a brother's face, pawned by a soldier who could not bear to carry it home. All of it curling now into ash and light, every trader's name unmade before Mistress Vane's inquisitors could read a single one.

"Beautiful," Felix said aloud, because no one was left to hear her say anything else. "Absolutely beautiful, and I am going to hang for it."

* * *

It had started, as most catastrophes at the Guild of Impossible Things did, with a knock at a door that should not be knocked upon.

Felix had been in the counting room since dawn, reconciling the week's entries against the Hollow Gate's ledger—the great black book chained to the wall beside the door itself, which wrote its own accounts whether she liked it or not. The gate had been quiet lately. Quieter than usual. After years of whispering her name in the small hours, it had gone almost polite, and Felix distrusted politeness in doors the way she distrusted it in tax collectors.

The knock came at the street door. Three blows, evenly spaced, struck with the confidence of someone who had never once been turned away from anywhere.

Felix set down her pen. She looked at the black ledger. It had written, sometime in the night, a single line across the top of a fresh page:

*She is coming. Burn what loves you.*

"Well," Felix told it. "That's helpful."

She went down through the guild's corridors—past the Hall of Small Trades where apprentices once bartered hiccups for courage, past the vault of confiscated wishes, past the door that only opened for liars—and unbolted the street door herself, because if the world was ending she preferred to watch it arrive.

Mistress Vane stood on the step with six soldiers behind her and the morning sun behind the soldiers, so that they all arrived as silhouettes, which Felix suspected was deliberate. Vane was smaller than the proclamations had made her. A neat woman in grey, with a face like a closed ledger and eyes that did arithmetic on everything they touched.

"Mistress Thackeray," Vane said. "Keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things."

"Felix is fine."

"I have a warrant." Vane produced a scroll sealed in black wax. "By order of the crown, this premises is to be searched for practitioners of the gift of unbinding. All records are to be surrendered. All names are to be given."

"And if I refuse?"

Vane smiled. It was a small smile, economical, like everything else about her. "Then I take the records anyway, and you besides. I would prefer your cooperation, Keeper. Cooperation leaves fewer things broken."

"You've burned three guilds already," Felix said. "Corveth. Ashmoor. The little chapterhouse in Dell. Your cooperation left quite a lot broken there."

"The gift is treason," Vane said, without heat, as though reciting a recipe. "The king has declared it. I don't make the law, Keeper. I merely balance it."

*Balance.* The word went through Felix like a splinter finding its way under skin. She knew balancers. She was one. And she knew what it meant when a woman like Vane used the word—it meant Vane kept her own ledger somewhere, of everyone taken, everyone questioned, everyone whose memories were opened to see what they held. It meant Vane thought of the Hunt as arithmetic too.

"Give me a moment," Felix said. "To fetch my coat."

Vane considered this. Whatever she saw in Felix's face, she apparently found it acceptable, because she inclined her head a fraction. "One moment. Sergeant, with me. The rest of you, the back entrances. Nothing leaves this building."

Which was, of course, exactly wrong. Everything important in the guild had already left it, years ago, in the only way the guild ever let anything leave: traded, paid for, forgotten. The ledgers were just the receipts. But they were receipts with names in them. Hundreds of names. People who had come to the Hollow Gate in their worst hours and paid the only coin the gate accepted, and if Vane read those names, she would not need a gift of unbinding to destroy them. She would simply read them aloud in a square somewhere, and let neighbors do the rest.

Felix climbed the stairs slowly, buying seconds, doing sums. Six soldiers. Two at the back already. Vane and a sergeant at her heels. The ledgers: eleven volumes, plus the black book, which could not burn and would not surrender and had, in any case, already told her what to do.

*Burn what loves you.*

She reached the counting room. She took down the candle.

* * *

The sergeant came through the door first, and took a candlestick to the temple for his trouble—not hard enough to crack anything, Felix was a professional—but hard enough that he sat down on the floor with an expression of profound personal betrayal. Then Felix kicked the door shut, dropped the iron bar across it, and got to work.

Paper burns faster than soldiers can break doors. That is not poetry; it is physics, and Felix had tested it more than once. She fed the ledgers to the hearth two volumes at a time, and the fire took them greedily, hungrily, and the room filled with that impossible festival smell as the trades came loose—a burst of somebody's wedding day here, a whiff of somebody's childhood dog there—and behind her the door shuddered under the sergeant's shoulder.

"Keeper!" Vane's voice, muffled, perfectly calm. "This is destruction of crown evidence. It will go badly for you."

"It's going badly for everybody today," Felix called back. "At least I'm consistent."

Nine volumes. Ten. Eleven. The black book sat on its chain, watching. When she reached for it, the chain slid off the wall hook on its own, coiled itself around her wrist, and tightened—not painfully, but with the unmistakable firmness of a thing making a decision.

"Yes, all right," Felix muttered. "I'm going. Stop being dramatic."

The door broke. Splinters, boots, torchlight. Vane stepped through the smoke with her little economical smile gone at last, replaced by something sharper, and her eyes went from the burning hearth to Felix to the Hollow Gate at the far end of the room—the plain grey door, older than the walls around it, that everyone assumed led to a cellar.

"Stop her," Vane said.

Felix ran. Not toward the windows, not toward the back stair, but straight down the length of the counting room toward the Hollow Gate, and she heard the soldiers' boots thundering after her, and she heard Vane shout something that was probably a warning and possibly a prayer, and she put her hand on the cold iron ring—

—and hesitated, for half a heartbeat, because the gate had never been opened from this side without a trade. That was the rule. Everything costs memory. She had spent her whole life keeping that ledger; she knew the price of passage better than anyone alive.

The whisper came then, the old whisper, the one that had been saying her name at night for years. But it didn't say *Felix* this time.

It said: *Paid in full.*

"What?" she gasped, hauling the door open. "What did I ever pay you?"

And the gate, in the voice it used only in dreams, answered:

*You are still paying.*

Cold poured over her like river water. She stepped through.

* * *

There was no corridor. There was no cellar. There was a moment of falling that felt like being remembered rather than moving, and then grass under her hands, and sunlight, and the smell of cut hay and woodsmoke and a river she did not know.

Felix knelt in a meadow with the black ledger chained to her wrist and her lungs full of air that tasted younger somehow, greener, and she did what any sensible accountant does when reality stops adding up: she checked her figures.

No road. No distant smokestacks. No criers' posts bearing the king's proclamation. Instead: a cart track, a crooked stone bridge, and down the slope, where the guild should have stood—where the guild *did* stand, she'd walked its halls five minutes ago—there was nothing. Empty riverside. A stand of willows. And men raising timber.

Not rebuilding. Raising. First beams, fresh-cut, pale as bone. Scaffolding. A foundation trench. Forty years of weathering absent, forty years of moss and repair and candle-smoke nowhere in evidence.

At the center of the site, directing the work with a rolled plan under one arm, stood a woman Felix knew only from a portrait in the guild's entrance hall: broad-shouldered, grey-eyed, laughing at something a carpenter said. The founder. Magda Corvane, who had died twenty-two years before Felix was apprenticed, and whose portrait Felix had dusted every week for a decade.

The black ledger, against her wrist, wrote a line of its own accord. Felix looked down.

*Welcome to the founding,* it said. *Mind the wet mortar.*

Felix Thackeray, Keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things, destroyer of ledgers, fugitive from the crown, began—very quietly, in the middle of a meadow forty years from home—to laugh. Because terror, she discovered, has a cousin, and its name is awe, and between them they sound remarkably like hysteria.

Magda Corvane turned at the sound. Saw her. Went very still.

"You're late," the founder called across the meadow. "I expected you decades ago."

The black ledger wrote one more line, in ink that gleamed like the gate's own whisper:

*Chapter six begins here.*
