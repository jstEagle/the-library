## 6. The Apprentice I Was

The Hollow Gate spat me out into candlelight.

I knew the room before my eyes finished adjusting. I had spent six years of my life in it—the counting room of the Guild of Impossible Things, back when the guild still kept its house on Candlewick Row, before the fire, before the move, before everything. The long oak table. The brass scales with their worn pans. The smell of tallow and old paper and, underneath it, the faint green tang of the gate itself.

Forty years ago. Give or take a season.

I stood very still, because the first rule of impossible things is that they behave worse when startled.

"You're bleeding on the floor," said a voice from the doorway, "and Master Corvane hates that."

I turned. A girl stood there with a ledger under her arm and ink to her elbows—thin, sharp-eyed, her hair escaping its braid in every direction at once. I knew that face. I shaved in front of it every morning, forty years older and worse for the wear.

She was me.

Not a reflection. Not a ghost. A girl of nineteen, holding a ledger she hadn't yet learned to fear, looking at me the way you look at a stranger who is standing in your kitchen at midnight.

"I'm—" I began, and stopped, because what was I going to say? *Hello. I'm you. You have no idea what's coming.*

"Traveler," she said, deciding for me, the way I always decided things too fast. "Through the Gate. That's forbidden, you know. Nobody comes through. It only takes."

"It only takes," I agreed, and my voice cracked on it.

Her eyes narrowed. She set the ledger down on the table with the deliberate care of someone buying time to think, and I watched myself think, and it was like watching a clock work with the face off.

"You're not a traveler," she said. "You're wearing a keeper's ring. Third finger, left hand. Only keepers wear them there." She stepped closer. "And you've got my chin."

* * *

Master Corvane found us an hour later, sitting across the table from each other like a negotiation between two versions of the same debt.

He was younger than I remembered him being, which is the cruelest trick time plays. In my memory he had been ancient—a mountain of a man gone gray, his hands spotted, his voice like a door in a cold house. This Corvane was perhaps fifty, broad-shouldered, black-bearded, laughing at something before he'd even crossed the threshold. He stopped laughing when he saw me.

"Well," he said. "That explains the whispering."

"The gate has been whispering my name," I said. "For months. At night."

"It does that when it's about to make a withdrawal." He pulled out a chair and sat, heavily, and some of the age I remembered came back into his face as he did it, as if the chair itself were owed years. "Sit down, Keeper. Both of you. And girl—go fetch the black ledger. The one under the floor."

My younger self looked at him. Looked at me. Went.

Corvane waited until the door closed behind her. Then he leaned forward, and his voice dropped into the register I remembered from the worst lessons, the ones that ended careers.

"How much do you know about why this guild exists?"

"We keep the ledgers," I said. "We record trades. Everything traded costs memory. We guard the Hollow Gate so the desperate don't feed themselves to it whole."

"That's what we *do*." His eyes didn't leave mine. "Why we *exist* is a different question. Answer it honestly, Keeper. Why does the world need a guild to stand between hungry people and a hungry door?"

I thought of the ledgers. Thousands of them. Trades of shadows for names, griefs for fortunes, first kisses for second chances. "Because otherwise they'd come anyway," I said slowly, "and the gate would eat them unrecorded. Unrecorded memory doesn't just vanish. It goes somewhere."

"It goes somewhere," Corvane repeated. "Say more."

"It pools." My mouth had gone dry. Every apprentice learned this as a warning, a fireside scare: *misuse the gate and the lost memories pool somewhere dark and grow teeth.* "The teachers say it's a story."

"The teachers say that because the truth is worse than a story." He rubbed his face with both hands. "The truth is that the pool grew a hunger. Long before this guild, long before me. A thing that eats remembering. It lived in the dark under the world and it starved, because the world remembers too much and forgets too little, and it could never reach enough of it. Until someone opened it a door."

The candle flame bent sideways, though there was no draft.

"Who?" I asked, though I already felt the answer coming down on me like weather.

"Me," said Corvane.

* * *

I want to record here, in whatever part of me keeps the true ledger, that I did not scream. I did not even stand up. I sat in my own past, in a room that smelled like my own apprenticeship, and listened to a dead man confess, and the worst part—the part I will carry until something eats it—was that it made sense. It always makes sense, in the moment. That is how every impossible thing gets made.

"I was young," he said. "Younger than you are now, if you can believe it. I had a sister the fever took, and I wanted one hour of her voice back. Just one. I found the door in the dark and I knocked, and I offered it a trade, and it gave me the hour." His hands lay flat on the table, very still, the way a man's hands go still when they're holding down something that wants to move. "It gave me the hour, and it asked so little. One memory. One small forgetting. And I paid, and I came back, and I told myself the price was fair."

"And then?"

"And then I wanted another hour. And then I wanted the guild—you understand? I built the whole edifice around my appetite. Rules, ledgers, scales, all of it, a cathedral built over the thing I'd let in, so I could keep feeding it in an orderly fashion and call it stewardship." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as ash. "Every trade through the gate feeds it a little. Every memory anyone gives up. I told myself we were keeping it fed so it would stay quiet. A leashed dog. A banked fire."

"But it isn't leashed," I said.

"No. It's patient. There's a difference, and I learned it too late." He looked at me then with an expression I recognized, because I'd seen it in mirrors for forty years: the look of a bookkeeper who has found the error and knows it runs all the way back to the first page. "It doesn't want scraps anymore, Keeper. It wants a host. Something that walks and rules and is remembered by everyone, so that it can drink from ten thousand minds instead of one door. And thirty years ago it found the shape it wanted."

The door opened. My younger self came in carrying the black ledger, and paused, sensing the temperature of the room.

"Girl," Corvane said gently, "this next part isn't for you."

"It's about me, though," she said. "Isn't it. Whatever it is." She set the ledger down and didn't leave, because I never left, and I loved her for it and pitied her for it in the same breath.

Corvane sighed. "Then hear it. In three years, girl, a boy will be carried to this guild half-dead, and his father will beg us to save him, and you will be the one who opens the gate. You'll bind something out of him. You'll think you're healing him."

"I know," I said quietly. "I remember doing it."

"You remember *doing* it," Corvane said. "You don't remember what it *did*. Open the black ledger, girl. Last page. Your hand."

She opened it. I watched her read it, and watched her face do what mine must be doing now, which was nothing at all, which was the face we both make when the numbers stop adding up and the only honest entry left is the one we can't write.

"What binding does," Corvane said, "is fold a thing away. Fold it small. Fold it deep. And the Hunger—our Hunger, the one I invited in—it *likes* being folded. Do you understand? All these years we thought the bindings imprisoned it, starved it, kept it caged beneath the palace. But a folded thing is concentrated. A bound hunger is a hunger with its edges tucked in, waiting, saving its appetite. Your binding didn't cage it, child. It seasoned it. It taught the thing patience, and patience is the one ingredient it was missing."

The candle went out. Neither of us moved to relight it. The green light of the gate seeped under the door, faint as pond water.

"So when I unbind him," I heard myself say, "when I finally undo what I did—"

"You won't free a prisoner," Corvane said. "You'll open a package. Everything it has swallowed for thirty years, unfolded all at once, into a king with nothing left inside him to fight with." He spread his hands. "Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless it's given a better meal first. Something it wants more than a kingdom. Something it will trade for." He was looking at me the way I look at a ledger page when I already know what the balance will be and am checking anyway. "The gate whispers your name, Keeper. It has been whispering your name for months. Doors don't whisper names. Debts do. Somewhere ahead of you, there is a trade already written, and you are the currency."

My younger self looked between us, nineteen years old and furious with not-knowing. "What trade? What's coming?"

I stood up. My legs held, which surprised me.

"Don't tell her anything else," I said to Corvane. "Please. Let her have the years."

"She'll need them," he said. "All of them. Even the bad ones." He rose too, and for a moment the mountain of my memory stood whole again in front of me. "One more thing, Keeper, and then you should go, because the gate won't hold this door open long. When you make your trade—and you will make it; I know that look, I wore it once—remember that the Hunger cannot create. It can only take and rearrange. Which means everything it offers you was taken from someone first. Check the provenance. Always check the provenance."

"And if the thing it offers was taken from me?"

Corvane smiled, and it broke my heart, because it was my smile, forty years early, worn by a man who had sold his sister twice over.

"Then ask yourself," he said, "who it took her from."

Behind me, the gate's green light flared, and the whisper came again—my name, my name, patient as interest accruing—and I turned toward the door in the dark, knowing exactly what I would find on the other side: a ruined guild, a hollowed man, and a bill forty years overdue, addressed in my own handwriting.
