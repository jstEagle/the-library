## 7. A Bargain in the Dark

The cell they gave me had a window, which I took as a compliment. Vane wanted me to see the sky. She wanted me to remember what I was giving up.

I sat on the cot with my back to the wall and counted what I knew. Felix was through the Hollow Gate by now—past tense, gone, unfindable—and every hour Vane spent on me was an hour she wasn't spending tearing the guild apart stone by stone. That was the arithmetic. I had learned arithmetic from a bookkeeper who could make a shadow balance against a name, and I trusted her sums more than my own heartbeat.

So I let them take me.

It hadn't been hard. I had walked down the Processional with my hood up and my hands empty, straight past the criers with their ash-and-honey voices proclaiming the Hunt, and stood in front of the palace gates until the guards noticed me. *Aldous Thackeray.* My brother's face did the work for them; even the ones too young to have served him wore it secondhand, that particular tightening around the eyes. They didn't ask why the traitor prince had come home. They were too busy deciding whether to be afraid of me or of whoever had sent me.

Vane came herself. Of course she did. She stood at the gate in her grey coat with the frost of early winter on her shoulders and looked at me the way an auditor looks at a ledger that won't close.

"You're bait," she said.

"I'm a distraction," I said. "There's a difference. Bait doesn't get to choose."

Something moved behind her face—not quite a smile. "You think she's still in the city."

"I think you don't know," I said, "and that's the only reason I'm still standing here instead of in one of your cells." Which was a lie about the cells—I was already in one—but lies are just trades made quickly, and I'd been raised by traders.

She ordered me taken anyway. We both understood the transaction.

* * *

They put me in a room with no clock, because Vane understood that time is a currency and withholding it is a kind of torture. But I had spent six years in exile learning to keep time by other means: the angle of light through the window, the shift of the guard's boots outside my door, the growing hunger in my own body that arrived, punctual as rent, twice a day. I kept count. On the third day—or what I judged was the third day—the door opened and Vane came in carrying a chair.

Not a weapon. A chair. She set it down facing mine and sat, and for a long moment we simply regarded each other like two merchants waiting for the other to name a price.

"The archive sent for you," she said.

I kept my face still. Beneath the palace there was a vault older than the crown, older than the guild's ledgers, where the kingdom stored what it could not afford to remember. I had heard of it the way you hear of the sea—constantly, and never once.

"Did it," I said.

"It has an interest in you. In your bloodline." She folded her hands. "Your brother sits on a throne with something wearing him like a coat, Thackeray, and the archive knows more about that thing than either of us will ever be told. It wants to talk. And it wants you as the interpreter."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I go back to hunting the girl with the gift of unbinding, and I am very good at my work." She said it without heat, which was worse. "You've bought her three days. Buy her thirty."

I looked at the window. The light was going amber, the way it does before snow. Somewhere across the city, in whatever impossible country the gate had carried her to, Felix was walking through forty years ago believing she had left me safe behind her. She would be furious when she found out what I'd done. She'd do the sums herself and find that I had overpaid, and she'd tell me so in that flat bookkeeper's voice, and then she'd forgive me, because that was the order she always did things in.

Unless she couldn't. Unless the price was paid in the one coin she'd never let me spend.

"I'll hear the offer," I said.

* * *

They took me down through the palace's bones—stairs that predated the stairs, corridors where the torchlight behaved badly—and into a round chamber with no doors I could see once we were inside it. The walls were shelved floor to ceiling with boxes of grey wood, each one labeled in a hand so small the labels looked like frost. The archive. The kingdom's attic. The place where memories went when remembering them was more dangerous than losing them.

There was no one there. There didn't need to be. The room itself attended.

When I spoke, I spoke aloud, because that seemed to be the custom. "You asked for me."

The air changed pressure, the way it does before a storm decides. Then, from everywhere and nowhere—the shelves, the floor, the space between my own thoughts—a voice like pages turning:

**ALDOUS THACKERAY. BROTHER TO THE HOST. YOU CARRY HIS FACE AND HALF HIS GRIEF.**

"I carry less than half," I said. "He kept the throne. I got the exile."

**THE HUNGER WEARS HIM. IT HAS EATEN HIS CHILDHOOD, HIS MOTHER'S VOICE, THE TASTE OF THE FOOD HE LOVED. IT IS NEARLY FULL. WHEN IT TAKES HIS NAME, IT WILL NOT NEED HIM ANYMORE.**

"I know. I've read the torn page."

**THEN YOU KNOW WHAT IS REQUIRED. UNBINDING IS A RITE OF THREE PARTS: THE WORD THAT OPENS, THE WORD THAT NAMES, THE WORD THAT RELEASES. YOUR GUILD KEEPS TWO. THE THIRD WAS DEPOSITED HERE NINETY YEARS AGO BY A KEEPER WHO THOUGHT SHE WAS BEING KIND.**

My mouth went dry. "And the price?"

**THE ARCHIVE DOES NOT ACCEPT COIN. IT ACCEPTS MEMORY. YOU WISH THE RITE ENTIRE. THE RITE IS EXPENSIVE.**

"How expensive?"

The pause was precise, surgical, the pause of something that had already done this sum ten thousand times and enjoyed watching me do it slower.

**YOU WILL TRADE EVERY MEMORY IN WHICH THE WOMAN CALLED FELIX THACKERAY APPEARS. ALL OF THEM. FROM THE FIRST TO THE LAST.**

The chamber was cold, but I felt colder than the chamber. I thought of the first time I saw her—she was nineteen, arguing with a ledger, telling a book it was wrong—and how I had stood in the doorway of the guildhall like a fool who'd forgotten what doors were for. I thought of rain on the canal, her hand in mine, the night she bound my brother and wept over the binding and wouldn't tell me why. I thought of her saying my name the way she said nothing else, carefully, like a figure she refused to round off.

Every memory. From the first to the last.

"If I pay," I said, "she'll never know. Not what it cost. Not that I chose it."

**CORRECT. SHE CANNOT REFUSE A SACRIFICE SHE DOES NOT KNOW EXISTS.**

That was the whole cruelty of it, laid out plainly. If I told her, she would stop me—she would tear the rite out of the archive's hands, she would find another way, she would trade herself instead, and I knew her well enough to know she'd already priced that option and found it acceptable. The only way to save her from the bargain was to make it without her. Love, it turns out, is mostly logistics.

"And after?" I asked. "After the rite. After the Hunger is out of my brother. What's left of me?"

**A MAN WHO WILL PASS HER IN A CORRIDOR AND FEEL NOTHING. A MAN WHO WILL SEE HER CRY AND WONDER WHY THE SIGHT IS FAMILIAR.** The voice softened, if a thing like that can soften. **THE ARCHIVE IS NOT CRUEL, ALDOUS THACKERAY. IT IS ONLY EXACT.**

"Exact," I repeated. My hands were steady, which surprised me. I had expected them to shake. "One condition. When it's done—when the king is free—you'll return what's left of the memories you don't use. The scraps. The residue. Whatever doesn't fit the price."

**WHY?**

"Because I'm a trader's man," I said. "You never sell the whole stock. You keep back something to bargain with later."

Another pause. Longer. Somewhere above us, muffled through ninety feet of stone, a bell rang the hour, and I marked it—day four, evening—and thought: *hold on, Felix. Hold on.*

**AGREED,** the archive said at last. **THE RESIDUE WILL BE RETURNED. NOW CHOOSE.**

I stood in the middle of the kingdom's attic, surrounded by everything it had decided to survive forgetting, and I chose.

"Yes," I said. "Take them."

* * *

It did not hurt, which I had not expected. I had braced for pain the way you brace for a blow, and instead it was like snow falling—soft, cumulative, obliterating. Memories left me in no order I could control. Her arguing with the ledger. Her laughing at my brother's terrible jokes out of politeness. Rain. Canal water. The word *carefully*. A green dress I had no idea why I suddenly missed. Each one lifted out clean, like a splinter drawn by a sure hand, and each departure left a small smooth absence that I could feel but not fill.

I held onto the last one as long as I could—the most recent, her face at the gate of the guildhall two nights ago, telling me to stay behind, telling me she'd come back, telling me not to be brave in any interesting ways. I held it the way you hold a door against wind.

Then that went too, and I was standing in a round room full of grey boxes, and I could not have told you why my face was wet.

**THE RITE IS YOURS,** said the archive. **THREE WORDS. KEEP THEM AS I GIVE THEM, FOR THEY WILL NOT SURVIVE BEING WRITTEN DOWN.**

It gave them to me. They settled somewhere below language, in the place where you keep your own name. I turned to leave, and at the door I stopped, because there was a box on the shelf beside me, small, grey, unlabeled, and warm to the touch like a living thing.

**YOUR RESIDUE,** the archive said. **AS AGREED.**

I opened it. Inside was almost nothing: the smell of ink, the sense of a corridor, an emotion with no object attached, vast and patient and entirely unexplained. Grief, I supposed. Or love. With the memories gone, there was no difference I could detect.

I closed the box and carried it up out of the dark, and behind me the shelves whispered shut, and somewhere far away—through stone, through snow, through forty years of impossible distance—a woman I could no longer remember was coming home to a city that wanted her dead, carrying the end of my brother's hunger in her hands.

I had bought her time. I had bought my brother's freedom. I had paid for both with the only currency I'd ever truly owned.

All that remained was to survive long enough to spend it.
