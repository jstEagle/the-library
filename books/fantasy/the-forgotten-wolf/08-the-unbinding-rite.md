## 8. The Unbinding Rite

The gate spat me out into ash.

I had stepped through expecting the guild's back hall, the smell of tallow and old paper, the creak of the third stair that I had never gotten around to fixing. Instead there was smoke, and under the smoke a silence so complete it rang.

The Guild of Impossible Things had been burned from the inside out. The ledgers' alcove was a black socket. The great oak table where I had balanced forty years of trades lay split down its length like something butchered. Vane's work, or her soldiers'. They had taken the ledgers, but they hadn't found what they were looking for, because what they were looking for was standing in the doorway holding a lantern with a shaking hand.

Aldous.

He looked at me the way you look at a stranger who has wandered into your house during a funeral. Polite. Bewildered. A little sorry for you.

"You shouldn't be here," he said. "It isn't safe."

Four days ago—my time—he had kissed me in this hallway and told me he'd come back by morning. Four days ago his voice had cracked on my name like a boy's. Now there was nothing in his face when he saw me. Nothing at all. He was a man looking at weather.

"Aldous," I said. Just to hear it fail.

"I'm sorry," he said gently. "Do we know each other?"

I want to tell you I said something clever. I want to tell you I kept the ledger-keeper's composure, the dry wit I wore like armor for twenty years. What I actually did was make a small sound, the kind of sound a door makes when it hasn't been oiled in decades, and Aldous caught my elbow as I swayed, because he was kind even hollowed, kindness being apparently deeper than memory.

"There were men," he said, when I could stand. "They took a woman. Dark hair, gray at the temples. She wouldn't give them names." His brow furrowed, reaching for something just out of reach, the way a man pats his pockets for keys he no longer owns. "She looked at me like she knew me. It bothered me all night. Why would a stranger look at me like that?"

"Because she did," I said. "Because everyone does. You're very easy to know, Aldous Thackeray."

He smiled at that, puzzled and pleased, and it was the worst thing yet, worse than the ruin, worse than the ash: he smiled at me the way he used to smile at girls in taverns before either of us admitted anything. A stranger's smile. A beginning-of-the-story smile.

"The king," I said. "Where is he?"

* * *

We went up through the burning city like thieves in our own story. The Hunt had emptied the streets; everyone with anything to hide was hiding it, which in Ashfall meant nearly everyone. The palace gates stood open—not stormed, simply abandoned, the guards having decided somewhere between dawn and dusk that a crown that eats its own people's memories was not a crown worth dying on.

The throne room doors were carved with the founding of the realm: the first king kneeling to receive the light. Someone had scratched out the kneeling figure's face. I chose not to wonder who.

Inside, the court had fled or forgotten itself. Candles guttered in their hundreds, and every one of them threw a shadow that leaned the wrong way, toward the throne instead of away from it.

The king sat slumped in the chair of state like a coat hung on a hook. He was still wearing his crown. That was the horror of it—the thing had never needed to take it off. It wore him the way he wore it.

"Felix," the king said, and then, in a different voice, one that came from somewhere behind his teeth, "Felix Thackeray. Keeper of the Hollow Gate. We have been *waiting* for you."

The Hunger did not rise from the throne. It didn't need to. The room bent toward it, candle flames all bowing like courtiers.

"You're nearly full," I said. My voice held. Twenty years of balancing impossible accounts will teach you to keep your voice level while the numbers go mad. "One memory left. His name."

"Clever clerk." The king's mouth smiled without the king in it. "Do you know what happens when a man forgets his own name? He becomes a doorway. And doorways are so much more useful than kings."

"I've come to unbind him."

Something flickered across the stolen face—not fear exactly. Appetite recalibrating. "You cannot. The binding is the only thing keeping him alive. Cut me out and he bleeds out everything he has left. You would trade a puppet for a corpse."

"Then I'll pay the difference."

The candles all leaned closer.

"Ah," the Hunger said softly. "Yes. You have it in you. We smelled it on you the night you bound us, little apprentice, forty years and four days ago. You carry more love than any mind we have ever eaten. It is why your trades cost you so much. It is why you guard a gate instead of walking through it." The king's head tilted. "Here is our offer, then. Clerk to parasite. His life entire—his name, his years, his waking—for yours. Every memory of Aldous Thackeray. Given freely, through the Gate, which makes it binding."

The room went very quiet. Even the fire in the great hearth seemed to hold its breath.

And here is the thing I could not stop thinking, standing there with my heart going like a hammer on a nailed-shut door: I had already done the arithmetic. In the ruins below, a man had caught my elbow and asked if we knew each other, and he had smiled at me like a stranger, and some cold clear part of me had understood that the ledger was already written. Aldous had paid first. He had traded me away to buy the rite, and he had done it without telling me so I couldn't refuse, and now the man I loved was a stranger with kind hands.

What was I keeping? A memory of someone who no longer remembered me? Love with one leg cut off?

But that wasn't true either, and I knew it wasn't, because when I reached for him in my mind—his laugh, the scar on his knuckles, the way he said my name like an answer—I felt the whole shape of him, and it was the largest thing in me. Larger than the guild. Larger than the gate. You do not sell the largest thing in you because it has become inconvenient. You sell it because it is the price of someone breathing.

That's the lie I told myself, anyway. I have had forty years to get the accounting right, and I will tell you now what I knew then: I sold it because I could not watch him die. Everything else was bookkeeping.

"Yes," I said.

The Hunger exhaled through the king's mouth, long and satisfied, and the candles stood upright again, all except one.

"Not here," it said. "At the Gate. All trades pass through the Gate. Bring him. Bring yourself. Bring your love, wrapped and weighed."

* * *

We carried the king down through the ruined city, Aldous and I, the brother who didn't know me bearing his brother who didn't know himself, and there was a bitter symmetry in it that even my grief appreciated. The king weighed nothing. Whatever the Hunger had left of him was mostly breath and bone.

At the guild's threshold, Aldous stopped.

"This place," he said slowly. "I dreamed about it. There was a woman on the stairs. She was laughing at something I said." He looked at me, and for one terrible half-second something moved behind his eyes, a fish turning under ice. "Was that you?"

"No," I said.

It was the first lie I had ever told him. I made myself remember that too, so I could lose it properly.

The Hollow Gate stood untouched in the ashes of the hall, because fire does not burn a door that isn't entirely in this world. It looked as it always had: plain oak, iron-banded, utterly ordinary, humming faintly like a struck bell that never quite stops.

I laid the king down before it. I put my hand flat against the wood.

"Hollow Gate," I said, in the words of the founding rite, the ones I had spoken a thousand times over smaller trades—a shadow for a name, a grief for a song. "I bring a life to ransom. I bring payment freely given."

The door opened onto dark, and the dark opened onto listening.

"What do you offer?" the Gate asked, in my own voice, because it had always spoken in the voice of the trader. That was the cruelty of it. Every bargain I had ever brokered, the traders had heard themselves agreeing.

"My memories of Aldous Thackeray," I said. "All of them. His face. His voice. His name. The stairs where he kissed me. The winter he taught me to skate on the flooded canal and fell through and laughed underwater. The letter he wrote me that I never answered until spring. All of it. To the last and least of it."

"And the price received?"

"His life. Whole. Unbound. Let the thing wearing his crown be cast out and let him wake remembering who he is."

The dark considered. Somewhere in it, the Hunger made a sound like a man settling into a comfortable chair.

"Freely given?" asked the Gate, in my voice.

And here the moment narrowed to a point, the way moments do when everything after them will be different. I thought of Aldous on the stairs, laughing. I thought of him asking *do we know each other*. I thought of the torn page in the royal ledger, my own handwriting, young and certain and wrong: *bound for healing, cost deferred.*

Cost deferred. Forty years of interest, come due.

"Freely given," I said.

The Gate took a breath—it had never breathed before—and the world tilted, and the memories began to leave me.

Not all at once. Like water going out of a lock, level by level. First the small things went: the smell of his cloak, wet wool and woodsmoke. Then the middle things: our arguments, our jokes, the particular way he held a cup. I grabbed at them as they drained and they slid through my fingers like minnows, and I heard myself saying his name over and over, *Aldous, Aldous,* practicing it, trying to press it somewhere the flood couldn't reach, and the Gate said, in my own voice, gentle as a thief:

"That is not how it works, Felix."

Then the big things went, and I will not describe them, because I no longer have them, and describing them would be a second theft.

The last thing to leave was not a memory at all. It was the feeling underneath them, the load-bearing wall, and as it went I understood suddenly why the founder had wept, why every trader I had ever served came back to the guild years later with empty faces and full purses, standing in the doorway unable to say what they had lost. You don't forget the person. You forget that there was a person-shaped hole, and the hole closes, and the floor is smooth, and you walk on it.

The king cried out behind me. The sound of the Hunger leaving him was like a great door slamming in a house with all its windows open.

And then it was quiet, and I was kneeling on the stones of my ruined guildhall before an ordinary oak door, and a man I did not know was helping a stranger in a crown sit up, and both of them were looking at me with concern.

"Are you all right?" asked the man I did not know. "Why are you crying?"

"I don't know," I said, and wiped my face, and found that my cheeks were wet, and could not account for it. "There's something—" I pressed my hand flat against my chest, where an ache sat like a stone in an empty room. "There's something missing. I can't think what."

Behind him, the king—whole, blinking, weeping with the simple animal relief of owning his own name again—was staring past me at the Hollow Gate. At the door, which had closed. At the door, on which, in fresh-cut letters that had not been there a moment before, something was now written.

The man I did not know turned to read it. And I watched his face change.
