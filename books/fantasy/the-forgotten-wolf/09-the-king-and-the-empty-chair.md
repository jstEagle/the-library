## 9. The King and the Empty Chair

The rite ended the way a fever breaks: all at once, and with a sound like a held breath finally let go.

I did not see it happen. I was on my knees on the throne room floor with my hands pressed flat against cold stone, and the last thing I remembered of the working was the Hunger's voice folding itself into my ear like a letter I would never be allowed to read again. *Trade me what you love most,* it had said. *And he lives.*

I said yes. I know I said yes, because the king is alive.

What I traded, I could not tell you. That is rather the point.

* * *

When I came back to myself, dawn was coming through the great windows in bars of grey and gold, and the throne room smelled of scorched silk and old smoke. The court had not yet been admitted. There were only guards along the walls, pale as candle wax, and one figure standing at the foot of the dais where the throne sat empty.

The king stood beside the chair instead of sitting in it. He looked like a man who has just woken from a long illness — thinner than his portraits, greyer, blinking at the light as though it were a language he'd once known and half forgotten. But his eyes were his own. I knew that without knowing how I knew it. Some part of me had spent years — or days, or hours; my sense of time was a torn page — learning to tell the difference between a man and the thing wearing him.

"Keeper," he said. His voice cracked on the word. "Felix Thackeray."

"Your Majesty."

He came down the steps slowly, one hand trailing the wall like a man walking a ship's deck in rough water. When he reached me he stopped, and looked at me for a long moment, and something moved across his face that I could not name — grief, perhaps, or its cousin, guilt.

"You paid," he said. "I felt it leave you. I felt—" He stopped himself. "Whatever it took, I will spend the rest of my reign repaying it."

"It took nothing I'll miss," I said, which was the truth, because you cannot miss what you do not remember owning.

He studied me, and I watched him decide not to argue. Kings learn early when a door is closed.

"There will be changes," he said instead, straightening, and his voice found its old register, the one that carried across courtyards. "Mistress Vane is in custody. The Hunt is ended — today, by proclamation, before noon. Every soul taken under it will be released, every record of it burned. And the Guild of Impossible Things—" he paused, and for a heartbeat he looked very tired and very young, "—the Guild will be left alone. Permanently. That is a royal decree, witnessed by every guard in this room."

Around the walls, the guards bowed. One of them, a big woman with a burn scar down her jaw, was crying quietly and pretending not to.

"Thank you," I said.

"Don't thank me." He glanced back at the empty throne, and his jaw tightened. "It sat in that chair for eleven years, Felix. It wore my face while it ate me. Do you understand what I'm saying? I remember all of it. Every audience. Every signature. Watching myself from somewhere behind my own eyes." He exhaled. "There are men in this court who served it gladly, thinking it was me. And there are men who knew. We will sort out which is which. But that work begins now, and it does not require you."

"No," I agreed. "It doesn't."

He hesitated. "Is there anyone you want sent for? Anyone who should be told you're safe?"

And here is the strange thing, the thing I have turned over a hundred times since: the question opened onto nothing. A room in my mind with no furniture in it. I searched for a name the way you pat your pockets for keys, and came up with empty hands, and felt — not sadness exactly. A shape where sadness should go. Like pressing your tongue to the socket of a pulled tooth.

"No one," I said. "No one at all."

* * *

They gave me a horse and an escort, which I refused, and a carriage, which I also refused, so in the end a guard walked me to the city gate and left me there with a royal seal stamped on a scrap of vellum, in case anyone asked questions. Nobody asked questions. The criers were already shouting the Hunt's end by midmorning, their voices bouncing off the whitewashed houses, and people came out of doorways to listen with the careful blank faces of folk who have learned not to believe good news too quickly.

I walked home.

The guild hall was a ruin, as I knew it would be. Vane's raiders had gone through it like a fire goes through paper: doors off their hinges, shelves split, ash ankle-deep in the reading room where the ledgers used to live. I stood in the doorway for a long time, breathing the smell of burnt binding-glue and charred calfskin, and felt nothing I could put a word to. Grief, probably. It had the right weight. It simply had no face.

I set about salvaging what I could, because that is what keepers do. We balance. We mend. We carry forward what survives.

By lamplight that evening I sorted the wreckage into piles: salvage, scrap, ash. Most of the ledgers were gone — I had burned them myself, or Vane had, the order of events had gone soft in my memory like ink in rain — but a few had survived, scorched at the edges, their spines cracked. I opened the first one expecting the familiar columns. Trades received. Trades given. Names redacted by fire.

Instead, every surviving page bore a single name, written over and over in the margins, in the headers, in the spaces between entries, sometimes so densely the columns underneath were illegible.

*Aldous.*

*Aldous Thackeray.*

*Aldous.*

My own handwriting. My own hand, unmistakably — the narrow letters, the way I cross my t's like little fences. Page after page after page, in ledgers spanning years. As if some past version of me had needed to write the name until her fingers gave out, and then started again.

I sat down on the floor amid the ash with the book open in my lap and read the name until it stopped meaning anything, and then kept reading it anyway.

Aldous. Aldous. Aldous.

I knew the shape of it. I knew it was a man's name, an old name, a name that belonged to someone. I knew — this was the worst part, the part I still cannot explain — that knowing the name was like pressing my tongue to that same empty socket. A tooth gone. A tooth I must have had.

But there was no entry. No trade recorded. No date, no counterweight, no line reading *given:* or *received:*. Whatever transaction had carved this name into every ledger I owned had happened outside the books entirely, in the one place I kept no accounts.

I am the keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things. I know what memory costs. I know the rules better than any living soul: everything traded leaves a hole, and holes can be mapped even when they can't be filled. You find the edges of the absence and you work inward.

So I worked inward.

I went to the Hollow Gate and stood before it with my lamp raised. The door was quiet — quieter than it had ever been in my remembering, which admittedly now covered less ground than it should. No whispering. No whispering my name at night, which apparently it used to do; I found that noted in my own journal, in an entry dated three weeks ago, written in a hand that shook slightly. *The gate spoke my name again. Third night running. Must ask Aldous whether—*

The sentence stopped there. Not crossed out. Just stopped, mid-thought, the way sentences stop when the writer looks up at a sound and never comes back.

Must ask Aldous whether.

Whether what? Who was I supposed to ask?

I laughed. I don't know why. It came up out of me like a bubble from deep water, and it turned into something else halfway, and I stood in front of the Hollow Gate in the ruins of my guild hall and cried without knowing what I was crying for, great ugly sobs that echoed off the stone, while the lamp guttered and the name *Aldous* sat open in the book in my lap, written in my own hand, patient as a debt.

That is the cruelty of our trade that no ledger records: you can lose the thing itself and keep the shape of the loss. The mind forgets; the body keeps the account. My eyes wept for a man whose face I could not summon if you paid me. My hands knew a name my heart had never heard.

* * *

Near midnight, I heard footsteps in the reading room.

I went cold. Vane was in custody; the Hunt was ended; but old habits die hard, and I picked up the poker from the dead hearth before I crept to the doorway.

Someone was kneeling in the ash by the shattered shelf where the ledgers had lived. A man, broad-shouldered, in a travelling cloak still wet with road dust. He had a book in his hands — one of ours, one of the survivors — and he was turning its pages with a care that made my chest ache for reasons I could not source, the way a tune can ache when you've forgotten where you first heard it.

He looked up when I stopped in the doorway. He had a face like weather — handsome once, worn now, with eyes that did something complicated the moment they found mine. Something that looked almost like recognition, and almost like pain, and almost like a man deciding, very deliberately, to smile.

"I'm sorry," he said. "The door was open. I heard the guild had burned, and I came to help rebuild."

"Help?" I kept the poker low. "Who sent you?"

"No one." He rose, closing the book gently, and I saw that he'd been careful to keep it right side up, spine uncracked, the way you handle a thing you know is precious. "I'm Aldous," he said. "Aldous Thackeray."

The name went through me like a bell struck in an empty room. Everywhere at once, and touching nothing.

"That's—" I frowned. "That's a name I know."

Something flickered across his face and was gone, fast, swallowed whole. "Is it?"

"It's written all over my ledgers." I gestured at the ash, the salvage piles, the survivors stacked in their crate. "Hundreds of times. My own handwriting. And I have no idea why."

He was quiet for a moment. When he answered, his voice was steady, but I noticed he didn't look at me while he said it, the way people don't look at you when they're carrying something breakable across ice.

"People write names down for a lot of reasons," he said. "Maybe you owed him money."

"I don't owe anyone money. I keep perfect accounts."

"Do you?" He smiled again, and this time the smile stayed, and it was warm, and it was wrong, it was wrong the way a key is wrong when it fits a lock in a house you've never entered. "Then perhaps," he said, setting the book down on the shelf, aligning its edge with the wood, precise as a ritual, "you'd let me stay and help you rebuild. I'm told I'm useful. And I'm told the guild takes in strays."

I should have said no. I was keeper of the Guild of Impossible Things; I knew better than anyone what walks in through open doors after midnight.

But the gate behind me was silent, and the ledgers were full of his name, and my eyes were still wet, and I could not remember a single reason — not one — to send him away.

"The guild takes in strays," I said. "There's a room at the end of the hall. Mind the third stair; it's loose."

"Third stair," he repeated, softly, like a man memorizing something. "I'll remember."

And that, I thought later — much later, though I couldn't have said why later either — was the strangest trade I ever witnessed: a man paying for a place to stay with a smile, and a woman accepting it without asking what it cost him.

I blew out the lamp and showed him to his room.

Behind us, in the dark of the reading room, the surviving ledgers sat in their crate, every open page bearing the same name in my own hand — and beneath the last entry, in fresher ink, in handwriting I did not recognize and could not place, a single new line:

*If you are reading this, love him anyway.*
