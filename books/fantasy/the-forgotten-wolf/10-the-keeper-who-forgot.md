## 10. The Keeper Who Forgot

**Aldous**

She laughed at his joke the way she had laughed at it eleven years ago, before the exile, before the hunt, before he had traded himself hollow in a cell beneath the archive. The same laugh. That was the cruelty of it and the mercy both: memory had been taken from her, but not the shape of her joy.

"The Guild of Impossible Things," she said, wiping her eyes, "is now a tea shop run by two people who can't agree on how to steep oolong."

"Three people," Aldous said. "The king insists on visiting every Thursday."

"The king," Felix said, "should learn to knock."

He watched her hands as she spoke. She still counted things without noticing—spoons on the table, coins in the till, the seconds between lightning and thunder. A ledger-keeper's habit, worn into the bone. He wondered if she knew that about herself. He wondered if she would ever know it again.

Because here was the truth he carried like a stone in his chest: he remembered everything. Every trade he had made to Mistress Vane's archive. Every hour of her he had spent buying the unbinding rite—the way she hummed when she worked, the ink stain on her left middle finger, the argument they'd had about whether a door could be loyal. He had paid for the rite with those memories, and then the Hunger had taken hers, and the arithmetic of it was obscene: he had bought back a king with the only currency either of them truly owned, and the exchange rate had been love itself.

Except the archive had made an error. Or perhaps the archive had made no error at all, and this was simply what mercy looked like when it came through official channels: they had taken his memories *of* her, but they could not take what his body remembered. His hands still knew the weight of her hand. His mouth still turned toward her name in sleep.

So he had made a choice, alone, in the ash-stink ruins of the guild hall, and he had kept it: he would not tell her. Not because the truth would wound her—though it would—but because telling her would be another trade. It would give her back a grief she had already paid to escape, and take nothing away, and the ledgers of impossible things did not balance that way. He had read enough of them to know.

Instead, he courted her. From the beginning. Badly, at first—he had forgotten how one begins—but she seemed to find his badness charming, which was either luck or the oldest magic in the world.

"You're staring," she said.

"I'm auditing," he said. "You have exactly four cups out for three customers."

"The fourth is yours. You always stay too long." She paused, and something flickered behind her eyes, quick as a fish under ice. "I don't know why I said *always*. I've known you three weeks."

"I have an honest face," Aldous said. "People assume history with it."

* * *

**Felix**

There was a hole in her where a person should be.

That was how she thought of it—not as absence, exactly, but as architecture. A room in her mind with the furniture gone, the walls still bearing the pale rectangles where pictures had hung. She walked past it every day. Some days she stood in its doorway and listened.

The doctors of the new court called it a wound of the rite. The king—who was kind now, who was *himself*, who wept openly at council meetings and had ended the Unbinding Hunt with his first living decree—had offered her anything. A title. A tower. A pension drawn on the royal ledger until the sun burned out. She had asked instead for the guild's charter restored and a kettle.

She rebuilt the guild from the ashes because rebuilding was the only prayer she knew. And because of the pages.

They were everywhere, once she started looking. Scraps of ledger paper, singed at the edges, hidden in the most absurd places—a loose brick by the Hollow Gate, the lining of her own coat, sewn into the spine of a book of children's rhymes. On each page, in her own handwriting, fragments. Names of traders she had saved from Vane's fire. Rules of the gate. And sometimes, always unsigned, always unexplained:

*The gate whispers. Do not answer it in your own voice.*

*A binding is not a cage. Ask what it feeds.*

And once, in letters pressed so hard the pen had torn the paper:

*Do not let him go. Whatever you traded, whatever you forgot—do not let him go.*

Him. She did not know who *him* was. She had asked Aldous once, casually, over the fourth cup, and he had gone very still, the way people go still around open doors, and said, "Perhaps you'll meet him again," and changed the subject so gracefully she almost believed nothing had happened.

Almost. She was a keeper of accounts. She noticed what did not balance.

* * *

**Aldous**

In the third month, he began leaving her things.

Not gifts—she would have asked why, and why was a door he could not yet open. Small debts, instead. He returned a book she'd lent him with a sprig of dried rowan marking a page she loved. He fixed the shop's crooked sign without being asked. He learned her tea order by heart and recited it wrong on purpose, because the first time he'd gotten it right she'd frowned and said, *how did you know that,* and he had nearly told her everything right there between the counter and the window, and only the memory of her face after the rite—empty, serene, a lake with no bottom—had stopped him.

Some nights he sat in his rooms above the shop and wrote letters he never sent. *You loved me first,* one began. *Or I loved you first; the ledgers disagree.* He burned them in the grate, one by one, and told himself this too was a kind of keeping.

His brother visited on Thursdays and drank tea badly and pretended not to see the letters' ash. Once, quietly, while Felix was below arguing with a delivery boy about the price of cinnamon, the king said: "You could tell her. The crown owes you any truth you want."

"She paid for my brother's life," Aldous said. "I won't invoice her for it twice."

"And if she finds out on her own?"

Aldous looked out the window, down into the street, where Felix was laughing at the delivery boy and counting coins into his palm, always counting, and felt the stone in his chest turn over.

"Then," he said, "she'll know I loved her enough to lose her twice."

* * *

**Felix**

In the fifth month, she found the last page.

It was winter again, the anniversary of the fire—or what she supposed was the anniversary; her calendar had a gap in it shaped like a season. She was re-shelving the recovered ledgers, the ones the traders had smuggled out of the flames, when a loose board shifted beneath the Hollow Gate itself, and there it was: a full page, folded eight times, tucked into the wood like a splinter.

Her handwriting. But younger somehow, or written fast, or written crying—she couldn't tell which. The ink had feathered where something wet had fallen on it.

*If you are reading this, love him anyway.*

No name. No explanation. Just that, and beneath it, smaller, almost an afterthought:

*You will want to know what you lost. You will dig for it like a coin in snow. Stop. Some trades are not meant to be unwound. You made this one with your whole heart, and your whole heart agreed, and if you are reading this and you are happy—even a little, even some mornings—then the trade was fair, and the room in your head is not empty. It is full. You just can't see over the walls.*

She sat down on the cold floor in front of the Hollow Gate, which had long since stopped whispering, and read it four times.

*Love him anyway.*

Him. The him of the torn pages, the him of Aldous's careful silences, the him whose shape she had begun to suspect in the doorway of that empty room. She thought of the fourth cup. Of the sprig of rowan. Of the way Aldous looked at her when he thought she wasn't watching—not the way a man looks at someone he's courting, but the way a sailor looks at a lighthouse. Like something he had steered by once, in weather.

She thought: *he knows.*

She thought: *he has known all along, and he stayed anyway, and he never once tried to fill the room in my head with his version of it. He just kept showing up, cup after cup, winter after winter, letting me arrive at him at my own speed.*

She thought: *that is either the cruelest thing a person can do or the kindest, and I am a keeper of impossible things, and I know which one I believe.*

* * *

**Aldous**

He heard the floorboard give and knew, before he reached the stairs, that she had found it. He had re-nailed that board twice. Some doors insist.

When he came down she was standing in front of the Hollow Gate with the page in her hands, and her face—her face was not the empty lake. It was weather. All of it at once.

"Aldous," she said. Her voice was steady, which frightened him more than weeping would have. "What did I trade?"

Everything in him leaned toward the truth. He felt it the way you feel a stair in the dark that isn't where your foot expected.

"You traded the memories of someone you loved," he said carefully, "to save a king."

"Was he worth it?"

"He is my brother," Aldous said. "And yes. And also—" and here his voice failed him for the first time in five months of careful arithmetic, "—also he was worth less than what you paid, and I have hated the mathematics of it every day since."

Felix looked at the page. At him. At the gate, silent and patient, its old hunger long since starved out of it.

"This person I loved," she said. "Tell me one thing about them. Not who. One thing. Something small."

He could have refused. He could have deflected, the way he had deflected for a hundred and fifty days. Instead he heard himself say, "They hummed when they worked. Badly. They counted everything—spoons, coins, thunder. And they kept a ledger of impossible things because they believed the world should have to account for itself." He swallowed. "And they had an ink stain on their left hand, here, just so, and I used to think it was the most honest mark anyone ever carried."

Felix looked down at her left hand. At the stain.

The silence lasted the length of one held breath, one turning year, one door deciding.

Then she closed the book in her hands—gently, the way you close a ledger whose accounts have finally balanced—and crossed the room, and opened the door of the shop onto the winter street, and took his hand, ink stain and all.

"Come in from the cold," she said. "You always stay too long anyway."

"How would you know?" Aldous asked, smiling.

"I don't," said Felix Thackeray, keeper of the guild of impossible things, "but I intend to find out. From the beginning. Write it all down. We keep records here."

And somewhere behind them, very softly, the Hollow Gate sighed shut—like a book, like a lung, like a heart remembering its rhythm—and outside, the snow went on falling on the city, erasing nothing, promising everything, beginning again.
