## 1. The Date That Wasn't Hers

There are three rules to guiding smugglers through Thornfell, and I've broken all of them at least once.

Rule one: never take a contract you can't walk away from. Rule two: never sleep in the same place twice. Rule three — and this is the one that matters — never let the cargo know more than you do about where you're going.

Tonight I was breaking rule three on purpose, because the cargo was nervous, and nervous cargo makes noise, and noise in Thornfell gets you dead faster than bad luck ever could. So I'd told them: shrine at the old salt road fork, half a night's shelter, walls still standing. What I hadn't told them was that I'd never actually been inside it. I'd only seen it from the ridge, three years ago, when I was running a different convoy for a different man who's now feeding worms in the Lowmarsh. Shrines look solid from ridges. Everything does.

"Veyra's shrine," said the boy with the lantern — Tam, they called him, though he wasn't part of the crew proper, just some scribe-turned-runner the merchants had hired to keep their ledgers dry. He squinted up at the ruin through the rain. "Thought they tore those down."

"They tried." I pushed the door. It gave with a groan that sounded almost offended. "Thornfell tears down everything eventually. Doesn't mean it stays down."

The interior was exactly what I'd expected and nothing like what I'd hoped: collapsed roof over the eastern corner, puddles mapping the sky's progress, an altar of grey stone furred with moss. But the western wall held, and there was a hearth, and dry wood enough if we rationed it, which meant heat, which meant the cargo would stop whining. In my trade, that counts as a miracle. Fitting, given the address.

I set the watch, got a fire going small and smokeless, and did what I always do in old shrines — nothing. You don't pray in Veyra's houses anymore. Not because it's forbidden. Because it's embarrassing. A hundred years ago the Silent God answered her faithful with signs and stillness and the particular peace of being listened to. Now she answers with the same thing she's answered everything since the Silence fell: nothing. Praying to Veyra these days is like shouting letters into a well. Some people find that holy. I find it rude.

But I touched the altar stone anyway, the way you'd touch a grave marker of someone you never met but whose name is stitched into your company's banner. Habit. Contract, almost — my order holds its oaths in her name, silent as she is.

That's when I saw the book.

It was tucked behind the altar, wedged into a gap in the masonry like something had been hiding it rather than storing it. Leather gone soft as old skin, corners rounded by decades of damp. My first instinct — professional instinct — was *trap*. Smugglers learn early that anything left conveniently in a ruin is either bait or garbage, and both will cost you.

My second instinct was curiosity, which has cost me considerably more over the years.

I pulled it out. The cover had no title, just a stamped sigil worn nearly smooth: a circle with a line struck through it. Veyra's mark. The god of silence, represented by silence itself — a circle that says nothing, crossed out because even silence, apparently, can be denied.

"Well," I said, to no one, because the crew was sleeping and the boy was out gathering wood. "Let's see what you are."

Forgery, most likely. Thornfell is full of forged prophecy — it's practically the city's second-largest export after salt and before regret. Every con artist with a steady hand and a dying rich man's ear has penned a doom or a deliverance. I've burned a dozen such books in cookfires. This one I opened.

The pages were vellum, real vellum, expensive even rotting. The hand was careful, disciplined, the kind of script you learn copying scripture, not forging it — forgers go ornate, because their buyers want drama. This was plain. Almost administrative. Like a clerk recording facts.

And then I found tonight's date.

Not the date as written — dates shift between calendars, and Thornfell alone uses three — but the shape of it. *The ninth night of the Drowned Moon,* it said, *when the salt road forks and the rain comes east off the marshes.* I looked up. Through the hole in the roof, the moon hung low and swollen, tinged that bruised yellow the marsh-fog gives it. The Drowned Moon. Ninth night. Rain coming east — it had been at my back all evening, pushing me toward this shrine like a hand between my shoulder blades.

Coincidence. Fine. The Drowned Moon comes every year; shrines sit near roads; rain happens. I'm a mercenary, not a milkmaid frightened by weather. I kept reading, telling myself I was assessing the forgery's quality. For craft's sake.

*On that night a woman of the silent order shall shelter where the altar stands forgotten.*

I stopped.

I am a woman of the silent order. Sworn under Veyra's name, marked with her circle-and-line on the inside of my wrist where a brand meets a tattoo in a compromise my order calls tradition and I call indecision. I am sheltering beside a forgotten altar. On the ninth night of the Drowned Moon. In the rain.

*Coincidence,* said the sensible voice in my head, the one that keeps me alive. *You chose this route. You chose this shrine. You're making the prophecy true by walking into it backwards.*

That's the trick of prophecies, of course. They're nets woven wide enough that any fish swimming past can imagine itself caught. Say "a woman of the silent order" and somewhere in the world, guaranteed, a woman of the silent order is doing something on any given night. The book wasn't describing me. It was waiting for anyone to fit.

Except.

Except the next line.

*Her name is written in ash: E—*

The rest was eaten. Water damage, maybe, or deliberate scraping — someone had taken a blade or a thumb to the vellum, worrying away the letters until only a ghost remained. But the first letter was clear as a scar. *E.*

Elodie.

I sat very still and listened to the rain, and told myself that E is also Edith, and Esme, and Estrid, and a hundred other names, and that a century-old book could not possibly know mine, and that the sensible thing — the professional thing — was to put the book back behind the altar, or better, feed it to the fire, and get a few hours' sleep before the dawn push to the river.

Instead I turned the page.

*She shall carry three keys to the crown that eats, and the crown sits upon a king who is not fed but feeding, and the kingdom dreams around him and calls the dream peace.*

I read it twice. Three times. The words didn't improve with familiarity. *The crown that eats.* Everyone knows the Ashen Crown — every child in the Marches knows it, the way children know bogeys and boundary-stones. King Aldric wears it. King Aldric has worn it for longer than any man should wear anything, and the priests say it's blessing and the ballads say it's burden and nobody — nobody — says what this book said. That it *eats.*

"You're good," I murmured to the book. "You're very good. Whoever wrote you knew how to hook a reader."

Because that's what it felt like — being hooked. That little tug below the breastbone, the fisherman's patience on the other end of the line. I knew better. I always know better. Knowing better has never once stopped me.

Footsteps outside. I closed the book and had my knife halfway drawn before the door scraped and Tam came in backwards, dragging an armload of wet wood, rain streaming off his hood. Young. Twenty, maybe. Ink stains on his fingers that no amount of scrubbing would ever fully surrender. He had the look of a boy who'd fled one life and hadn't yet decided what the next one was, which is a look I know intimately, having worn it myself for most of a decade.

"Fire's low," he said. "Found wood. Mostly dry. There's a—" He stopped. He was staring at the book in my hands. At the sigil on the cover. Something moved across his face too fast for me to name it, and I filed that away, because in my trade you file everything, even things that turn out to be nothing. *Especially* things that turn out to be nothing.

"What?" I said.

"Nothing." He knelt and fed the fire. Too quickly. "Just — haven't seen that mark outside a chapel since I was small."

"You're a long way from chapels, ledger-boy."

"Aren't we all." He smiled, and it was a good smile, easy, and I decided — provisionally — that whatever he'd recognized, it wasn't worth waking the crew over. Provisionally. I have been wrong about provisional decisions before. People I've trusted provisionally have sold me to people I now hate. But the rain was cold and the night was long and paranoia is a luxury you rations like the firewood.

He slept. The crew slept. And I sat by the fire with a dead god's book open on my knees, reading by flame-light while the Drowned Moon crawled across the hole in the roof like something keeping an appointment.

* * *

Here is what I found in the rest of it, before the fire burned down to coals:

The book was a chronicle wearing a prophecy's clothes. Half of it was history — the founding of Ashvale, the crowning of Aldric, the fall of the old faiths — written in that same flat clerical hand, dates and names and deaths. The other half was prediction, and the predictions were dated, and here is the thing that took me an hour to understand and another hour to wish I hadn't: the dates were *past.*

Every prophecy in the book had already come true. Or failed to, depending. There was a passage about a flood in the Lowmarsh, forty years back — happened, ask anyone. A passage about a fire in the tanneries — happened. A passage about a queen's miscarriage that unmade a dynasty — happened, quietly, horribly, the way dynasties actually end. Page after page of things the book had gotten right, which is the single most damning feature a forgery can have, because forgers hedge and real prophets don't, and this book hedged *nothing.*

And threaded through it all, recurring like a refrain, the crown. The crown that eats. The king who forgets. Lines like: *the crown feeds at the anniversary rite, and each year the king wakes smaller.* Lines like: *they call it peace, the forgetting; they built a kingdom on it.* Lines like — and this one I read four times, because my eyes kept sliding off it the way eyes slide off a price you can't afford —

*The keys are three: salt, iron, bone. The first lies drowned beneath the mines where men dig light from dark. The second is caged where prophets are kept. The third must be given, never taken, and it beats.*

*It beats.* Keys don't beat. Unless the third key isn't a key the way doors need keys. Unless—

Stop. I made myself stop. I closed the book and pressed the heels of my hands against my eyes and performed the small ritual I perform whenever the world starts tilting: I named five things I could see (fire, coals, rain, moss, knife), four things I could hear (rain, breathing, wind, the wet tick of the boy's drying boots), three I could touch (knife-haft, stone floor, the book's soft leather). Grounding. My order teaches it for panic. It works about half the time, which is a better rate than prayer.

The problem was the last line of the prophecy. The one about me. The one I'd read and reread and read again, the one that had stopped being a fisherman's hook and started feeling like a noose settling comfortably around the neck:

*Nine nights hence, on the Drowned Moon, she comes to the fork and the forgotten altar, and the counting begins.*

Nine nights hence. Written — judging by the ink, the wear, the dust behind the altar — decades ago. Maybe a century. And tonight was the ninth night of the Drowned Moon, and I had come to the fork, and I was sitting at the forgotten altar, and whether the counting had begun or not, something in my chest had started keeping time.

* * *

Dawn came grey and apologetic, the way it does in Thornfell, as if the sun were reporting for a shift it didn't want. I'd slept perhaps two hours. The book was wrapped in oilcloth inside my coat, against my ribs, where I carry the things I can't decide about.

We broke camp fast. The cargo — six wagons, salt-iron and silk, the usual lies about quantities — grumbled awake and rolled out onto the salt road south. I rode point, as always, watching the marsh fog burn off in slow rags.

And that's when the details started.

Small things. Stupid things. The kind of things you'd never notice unless a book had primed you like a pump.

A crow on a boundary post, facing us, silent — and the book had said, in passing, in that flat clerk's voice, *and the birds will not sing on the road south.* I noticed it the way you notice a wrong note. Then two more crows. Then none at all, for a mile, in marsh country, in morning, where the birds should have been deafening. The silence sat on the road like weather.

Then the fork. The book: *at the fork stands a stone with a false name, and she will pass it without reading it.* There was a milestone at the fork — I've passed it a dozen times — carved with a village name that hasn't existed since before my grandmother. I'd never once read it. I realized, with a lurch, that I couldn't have told anyone what it said, and that the book had known that too, had known that I — that *she* — would ride past a lie a hundred times without seeing it.

Coincidence. Coincidence. The word was starting to taste like the mud on the road.

The third detail was worse, because the third detail involved people.

At midday we halted at a waystation to water the horses, and there was a peddler there, a toothless old man selling charms against the marsh-fever, and he took one look at me and went white around the mouth and made a sign — not against evil, not against fever, but the old sign, the *circle-and-line*, Veyra's sign, the way the faithful used to make it a hundred years ago, palm flat then folded shut, *heard and kept silent.* Nobody makes that sign. I've seen it twice in my life, both times in frescoes.

"Where did you learn that?" I asked him.

He wouldn't answer. He packed up his tray of charms with shaking hands and left at a pace just short of running, and I stood there with the horses steaming around me and my heart going like a drum in a procession.

Because here's the thing. Here's the thing I haven't said yet, the thing I've been circling all night like a moth around the flame it intends to die in.

When I touched the altar last night — just touched it, skin to stone, the way I always do, habit, contract — I heard something. Not a voice. Veyra doesn't speak; that's rather her whole identity. But a *pressure*, a momentary weight behind my eyes, like a memory trying to surface in water too deep for it. And in that pressure, for less than a heartbeat, I saw a garden. White walls. Ash falling like snow, gentle, unhurried, and beneath the ash, green things growing anyway. Stubborn. Patient.

I have never seen that garden. I would swear on every oath I hold that I have never seen that garden.

And the book, in its flat clerk's hand, in a margin beside a verse about the crown that eats, had drawn — small, careless, the doodle of a bored scribe — a garden. White walls. Falling ash.

I told the crew we'd make the river crossing by tomorrow dusk. I told them the road was quiet, the tolls paid, the risks ordinary. All true. All beside the point. The point is that I spent the afternoon's ride doing arithmetic I didn't want to do: the age of the vellum, the accuracy of the fulfilled prophecies, the letter *E*, the silent crows, the false-named stone, the peddler's ancient sign, the garden I've never seen growing under ash I've never stood in.

One coincidence is luck. Two is pattern. Three is a net, and I could feel the mesh closing, thread by thread, each detail a knot tied by hands a hundred years dead, tied with terrible patience, tied for me — or for whoever came down this road carrying my name and my order and my habits, which amounts to the same thing, because the net doesn't care which fish it catches so long as the fish fits.

By dusk I'd made my decision, which is to say I'd admitted the decision I'd made the moment I read the letter *E*.

The convoy camps tonight at the ruined tollhouse above the river. Tomorrow I pay them off — full fee, plus a bonus for the inconvenience of losing their guide — and I turn north. Alone. Toward Ashvale. Toward the crown that eats and the king who forgets and three keys, one of which drowns beneath the salt mines, one of which is caged with prophets, and one of which *beats.*

Toward whatever wrote my name in ash a hundred years before my mother met my father.

I told myself I could still walk away. I'll probably tell myself that again tomorrow, and the day after, right up until the moment walking away stops being something a person like me can do. That's the trap inside the trap, I think. Prophecies don't catch you with fate. They catch you with curiosity. They catch you the way any good lure works — by looking exactly like the thing you can't leave alone.

The book shifts against my ribs when I breathe. Behind us, the Drowned Moon is rising over Thornfell, swollen and yellow and silent, and ahead the road runs north through the dark toward a kingdom that dreams around its king and calls the dream peace.

The counting, whatever it counts, has begun.
