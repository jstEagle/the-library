## 2. What the Silence Owes

The shrine of Veyra smelled of pigeon droppings and old wax, which is what most gods smell like eventually if you wait long enough.

I'd found it by accident the night before, or it had found me — hard to say with shrines. I'd been walking off the prophecy book, the way you walk off bad liquor or a knife wound, and my feet had carried me down a crooked lane in Thornfell's oldest quarter until they stopped at a door that shouldn't have been open. Shrines to Veyra were supposed to be locked. Had been locked, for a hundred years, ever since the god went silent and the faithful stopped coming and the city stopped caring. But this door stood ajar like an invitation, and I have never once in my life turned down an invitation that might be a trap. It's a character flaw. My mother said so. Several dead men have said so.

Inside: dust, pigeons, a roof with a hole in it shaped like a country nobody had ever bothered to name. And at the far end, beneath that hole so that one blade of grey morning light fell across it like a stage cue, the altar.

Veyra's altar is not like other altars. Other gods get statues — fat, golden, smiling, holding sheaves of wheat or scales of justice or their own magnificent bellies. Veyra got silence. Her altar was a block of black stone, uncarved, unadorned, worn smooth in one place where generations of foreheads had pressed against it. The Silent Lady. The Listener. The god who never spoke because speaking was beneath her, who heard everything because hearing was all there was.

That was the theology, anyway. The mercenary version was simpler: Veyra kept your secrets, took your confessions, and asked nothing back, because asking was also speech. You could tell her anything. She would never repeat it, never judge it, never use it against you. In a trade built on leverage and betrayal, a god who couldn't gossip was worth ten gods of war.

I knelt on stone that was cold even through leather trousers, and I did what I always did. I talked.

"Good morning," I said. "It's Elodie. Again."

The pigeons shifted on the rafters. Dust drifted through the light. Somewhere outside, Thornfell began its daily argument with itself — cart wheels, fishmongers, a woman two streets over screaming about the price of eels with real conviction.

"You'll notice I'm still alive," I went on. "Which is either your doing or nobody's, and I've stopped being able to tell the difference. That's a joke. Mostly." I rubbed my thumb along the altar's worn hollow. "Here's my confession for the day, since we're keeping the tradition alive: I found a book last night that says tonight is important. Says *I'm* important. Names me, names the date, names a king I've never met and a crown I've only seen in woodcuts. And my first thought wasn't *what a wonder*. My first thought was *who forged this, and how much are they paying*."

I laughed, but it came out wrong, thin as the light.

"That's the part that frightens me, if you want the truth of it. Not the prophecy. The fact that I can't make myself believe it's a lie. A forgery has a purpose, right? Someone wants something. Money, fear, a mercenary company led somewhere convenient for an ambush. But I've read it six times now, and I can't find the angle. There's no profit in it. There's no *anything* in it except —"

Except the details. Except the way the book described the shrine, this shrine, down to the hole in the roof and the pigeon roosts and the crack running through the altar's base like a healed wound. Except the way it described *me*: *the sworn daughter of the silent house, marked by salt and iron, who walks with the dead man's debt.* Salt — the convoy I was guiding ran salt out of the mines. Iron — my sword, plain iron, the only luxury I've never been able to give up. The dead man's debt — Ferro Vance, the fence who owed me his life from the Marrowbridge job, whom the prophecy named before I'd even decided to visit him.

Coincidence has a shape. I've survived twenty-nine years in Thornfell's underworld by knowing its shape — how coincidences cluster around money, around lies, around men who want something. This coincidence had no shape. It just *was*, sitting there in century-old ink, patient as a spider.

"I need a sign," I told the altar. "Anything. A feeling. A draft. Knock something off the rafters. I'll take a pigeon, honestly. Aim one at my head and I'll call it scripture."

Nothing.

There never was anything. Ten years I'd prayed to Veyra, every morning, the way other soldiers sharpened blades — ritual, habit, the thing that kept the hands busy while the heart figured itself out. Ten years of talking into a silence so complete it had texture, weight, a kind of personality of its own. Other people had given up. Whole congregations had given up. The Church of the Listener had sold its bells and dissolved itself into three smaller churches with gods who at least had the decency to perform the occasional miracle, or at least claim one credibly.

But I kept coming back. And I'd never fully examined why, because examining it felt like pulling a thread that held up more than my coat.

"Right," I said, and stood, knees cracking. "Well. If tonight goes badly, I want it noted that I asked."

The pigeons said nothing. The light moved. Outside, the woman had won the argument about eels, or lost it; either way, the screaming stopped.

I left a coin on the altar anyway. Old habit. Payment for services never rendered, rendered anyway.

* * *

The vote happened at noon, in the back room of the Drowned Rat, which was less a tavern than a rumor with a bar attached. My company — the Graywater Company, nine strong when everyone remembered to come home — sat around a table scarred by knives, cards, and one memorable evening involving a live crab, and argued about whether to abandon the richest contract any of us had ever held.

"The contract stands," said Brannoc, our quartermaster, who had a face like a dropped pie and the moral flexibility of good rope. "Salt runs tonight, same as always. We're being paid to guide wagons through the Undervault, and we'll guide wagons through the Undervault. What we're *not* doing is chasing ghosts out of a book."

"The book named her," said Petra. Petra was our youngest, nineteen, all elbows and certainty. She'd read the prophecy pages twice — I'd let her, because someone in the company needed to think I was invincible and she hadn't grown out of it yet. "It named Elodie. Named tonight. You don't find that a little —"

"I find it a lot of things," Brannoc said. "Mostly I find it a reason for someone to be hunting whoever's named in it. Which is her. Which means every fool who reads those pages is a threat, and every clever one is worse. Burn the book, take the salt contract, get paid. That's the whole sermon."

"It's not that simple," Petra said.

"It's *always* that simple. Complication is what people sell you right before they take your purse."

Around the table, heads nodded. Nine of us, and I could count the votes already: Brannoc, Hesk, the twins, old Marenna — not the regent, different Marenna, ours had one eye and a crossbow — Dov, Sella, little Fitch who was technically our cook and technically armed. Eight against one, and the one was me, and I hadn't even opened my mouth yet.

They looked at me anyway. They always looked at me anyway. I was the one who'd signed us to the contract, the one whose name was in the cursed book, the one who'd spent the morning missing while they imagined all the ways a person can go missing in Thornfell.

"Say your piece," Brannoc said, almost gently. For him, that was tenderness.

So I stood, because sitting felt wrong, and I gave them the piece.

"We finish the contract," I said. "All of it. Not just the salt run — the whole shape of it. Because here's what none of you are saying out loud: the prophecy didn't just name me. It named the contract. *The gray water shall carry her to the salt doors*, that's the line. Our company. Our route. Whoever wrote that book knew what we'd be doing tonight, a hundred years before we did it, and I've spent all morning trying to make that mean *fraud* and coming up empty."

"Coming up empty isn't proof," Hesk said. He was our tracker, a quiet man who trusted his nose more than any god. "Empty is just empty."

"Empty is just empty," I agreed. "But consider the alternative. Consider that it's true. Then somewhere in Ashvale there's a king wearing a crown that eats him alive, and a book says I'm the one who stops it, and every hour we spend arguing about it is an hour the crown keeps eating. And consider *this*—" I pulled the book from my coat and set it on the table, and watched eight faces flinch away from it like it was a snake. "Consider that last night, after I found this, someone burned the smuggler's cache it came from. All of it. To the stone. I went back at dawn. Whoever wrote this, or owns it, or fears it, is already cleaning up. We're not choosing whether the story is real. We're choosing whether we're in it standing up or dragged in lying down."

Silence. The kind of silence that in a shrine would have been holy and in the Drowned Rat was just expensive ale going warm.

"Vote," Brannoc said finally. "All in favor of taking the salt run and nothing else, and burning that book before it burns us."

Eight hands. Slowly, unhappily, some of them — Petra raised hers last, and wouldn't look at me while she did it — but eight hands.

"And against?" Brannoc asked, though he already knew.

"One," I said, and raised my own hand, and held it there alone in the smoky air like the last tree on a hillside.

"Then it's settled—"

"No," I said. "It's voted. Those aren't the same thing."

Brannoc sighed the sigh of a man who had buried friends and expected to bury one more. "Elodie. Ten years you've prayed to a god that doesn't answer. I've never once mocked you for it, have I? Never said a word. Because it costs me nothing and it keeps you steady, and a steady Elodie is worth more to this company than a second tracker. But now it's costing us. Now the silence you keep talking to wants *wages*, and the wages are the rest of us. You can't ask nine people to pay your god's debts."

"It's not about the god," I said.

"Then what's it about?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. I opened my mouth to answer it and found the answer wasn't where I'd left it. It wasn't duty — I'd broken contracts before, when the math went bad, and slept fine. It wasn't the money. It wasn't even the book, not really, not anymore.

It was the shrine. It was ten years of kneeling in front of that black stone and telling it everything, and getting nothing back, and coming back anyway. And somewhere in all that one-sided conversation, I had apparently made a promise I didn't remember making. Something older than oaths. Oaths are words exchanged between people who might betray each other, sealed with witnesses who might die. What I felt wasn't that. What I felt was the way a river feels about the sea — not obligated. Just *going*.

"I don't know what it's about," I admitted. "That's the honest answer, and I know how much you all love those. But I know that if I walk away tonight, I'll spend the rest of my life listening for it the way I listen at that altar. Waiting for an answer that already came, and that I refused to hear."

"So that's it," Marenna said, her one eye fixed on me. "You're going regardless."

"I'm going regardless."

"Then the company's split," Brannoc said. "Formally. You take the contract alone, you carry it alone, and whatever finds you tonight finds you without us. No hard feelings." He paused. "Some hard feelings. But you'll live."

"I always do," I said, and hated how much it sounded like a prayer.

* * *

I spent the afternoon doing the arithmetic of going alone, which is a short sum: one sword, one coat, one book of impossible instructions, and one life-debt owed by a fence named Ferro Vance who was going to be very unhappy to see me.

Petra caught up with me at the door of the Drowned Rat, breathless, furious, nineteen.

"This is stupid," she announced.

"Almost certainly."

"You're going to die."

"Statistically, yes, eventually. Tonight is less certain."

She shoved something into my hands — a bundle of oilcloth, tied with the ugly knot she used for everything. Inside: bandages, needle and gut-thread, a flask of the good spirits, and a strip of dried eel, because she'd been listening to the argument too.

"If you're going to be a martyr," she said, "be a *prepared* martyr."

"Petra—"

"Don't." Her chin was trembling and she was holding it the way soldiers hold a wall. "Don't make it a moment. Moments are how you get people killed. Just — come back. Or send word. Or haunt Brannoc specifically, he deserves it."

I laughed, and it hurt, and I hugged her quick and rough the way you hug people when you're both pretending it's nothing.

Then I walked out into the grey afternoon with a dead god's coin in my pocket and a living girl's eel in my pack, and I went to find Ferro Vance, because the prophecy said *salt first*, and the drowned chapel under the salt mines wasn't going to open itself.

Behind me, Thornfell went on arguing about eels. Ahead of me, the first key waited in the dark, guarded by something that had been waiting a hundred years.

And somewhere north, in a palace I'd never seen, a king I'd never met woke up and could not remember his own morning.

I didn't know that yet. But the silence did. I'd swear the silence did.
