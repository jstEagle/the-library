## 3. A King in a Locked Room

I woke with the taste of copper in my mouth and the distinct impression that I had been somewhere. Not dreaming — *somewhere*. There is a difference, and the difference matters. Dreams leave you with fog and fragments. This left me with the feeling of having just returned from a long journey on which I had been an extremely reluctant passenger.

The ceiling above my bed was unfamiliar.

That was the first problem. It was a fine ceiling, vaulted and painted with constellations that had been touched up recently enough to gleam, and it was very clearly the ceiling of a king's bedchamber, because I was lying under it in a king's bed, in a king's nightshirt, in what every rational sign suggested was a king's body. But I could not for the life of me remember falling asleep beneath it, and when I tried to remember the evening before — the supper, the wine, the walk down whatever corridor led here — I found nothing. Not a blur. Nothing. A page torn cleanly out of a book, so neatly you'd almost believe the book had been printed that way.

"Good morning, Your Majesty."

A servant stood at the foot of the bed. Young woman, gray dress, hands folded, eyes fixed on a point approximately six inches to the left of my head. I did not know her name. That was the second problem, because I had the strong, unshakable sense that I *should* know her name — that a king who has slept in a palace longer than a fortnight knows his own chamber staff, knows their names and their children's names and which one waters the ferns too much. She was a stranger wearing the livery of my own household, and she was looking at me the way people look at a dog that has bitten someone.

"Morning," I said. My voice was hoarse, as if I'd been talking all night. "You have the advantage of me."

She blinked. "Sire?"

"Your name," I said, and smiled, because kings are supposed to smile when they ask questions they ought to know the answers to. "Humor me. I'm not fully awake."

A pause. Long enough to be uncomfortable. "Sera, Your Majesty. I've served three years."

Three years. Three years of mornings, presumably, of this exact exchange or some version of it, and my mind offered me nothing. Not even the shape of her. I thanked her, asked for water instead of wine, and watched her leave with the particular quickness of someone escaping a room.

I sat up and took inventory, the way you do after a fever. Body: mine, more or less. A little thinner than I felt I should be. Hands steady. A small scar on my left forearm I didn't recognize — thin, white, old enough to be years old, and yet I had no story for it at all. Everyone has stories for their scars. *Fell out of a tree at nine. Cut myself carving. My brother dared me.* Mine was just there, anonymous as a stranger's.

Memory: patchy. Worse than patchy. When I reached backward from this morning, I found yesterday in pieces — a council meeting, I thought, something about grain tariffs, Maren's voice saying something dry and precise — but the day before that was soft at the edges, and the week before that was like trying to recall a book I'd read drunk. And beyond a certain point — I kept reaching, the way your tongue keeps finding the gap where a tooth was — beyond a certain point there was simply nothing, and worse than nothing: a smoothness. As if the missing years hadn't been lost but *paved over*.

I got dressed. There were clothes laid out that fit perfectly, which somehow made it worse.

* * *

Maren was waiting in the solar, which meant either that I was late or that she had been sitting there since dawn doing whatever it is regents do before breakfast, which in her case I suspect is sharpening things.

My sister is the only person in Ashvale who has never once been impressed by me, and I include in that count our mother, who was impressed by everyone. Maren is four years older, half a head shorter, and entirely composed of edges. When we were children she once convinced me that our father's crown was cursed and that if I wore it during the harvest festival I would grow a tail. I wore it. I spent the whole festival checking behind me. She watched me do it with the serene satisfaction of a scholar whose experiment has confirmed the hypothesis.

"Brother," she said now. "You look tired."

"I slept ten hours."

"Mm." She poured tea without asking how I took it, which she knew, which was reassuring, right up until I remembered that I couldn't actually verify how I took my tea. Milk, apparently. The cup arrived already milky. Fine. Good. One thing confirmed.

"The council meets at noon," she said. "The tariff question. You'll want to review the—"

"I reviewed it last night," I said.

Her teacup paused, very slightly, on its way to her lips. It was such a small pause that anyone else would have missed it. I have known Maren for thirty-odd years; I catch her pauses the way sailors catch weather.

"Did you," she said.

"Didn't I?"

"You were… unwell last night." Her eyes came up and settled on me, and for a moment — just a moment — there was something underneath the composure that I can only call vigilance. "You retired early. You weren't well."

"I don't remember being unwell."

"No," she said softly. "I imagine you don't."

And then it was gone, folded away, and she was discussing grain tariffs as though nothing had happened, and I sat there drinking tea I couldn't confirm liking, next to a sister who was watching me the way you watch a candle guttering in a draft — with concern, yes, but also with the practical question of what you'll do when it goes out.

* * *

The palace was full of strangers.

Not literally — I understood that rationally. These were liveried servants, guardsmen, clerks, the ordinary population of Ashvale's seat, and they bowed when I passed and said "Your Majesty" in the correct tones. But I knew none of them. Not the captain of the door guard, a broad man with a burn-scarred jaw who greeted me by name with obvious relief. Not the steward, not the master of the kitchens, not the young clerk who scurried past me in the east corridor clutching papers and staring at me with an expression I couldn't read until I realized I *could* read it, because I'd seen it before, on the faces of people looking at something dangerous.

Fear. Polished, court-trained, carefully hidden fear — but fear.

I stopped him. "You. Clerk. What's your name?"

He went white. Actually white, blood leaving the face in real time, a physiological event. "O-Ossen, sire."

"And how long have you served the crown, Ossen?"

"Six years, sire."

Six years. Six years of serving a king he looked at like a wolf on a chain. I thanked him kindly and walked on, and behind me I heard him exhale like a man surfacing.

Here is the thing about being king: you assume the fear is about power. People fear the crown, the writ, the headsman. That's healthy; that's architecture. But this was different. This was the fear of the *man*, specifically, personally, this man — the way Sera had flinched at my waking voice, the way the captain of the guard had gripped my shoulder a half-second too long, as if confirming I was solid.

By midday I had catalogued eleven servants whose names I should have known and didn't, and three who looked at me as though counting the days until something.

Counting. The word snagged in my head and wouldn't come loose, like a fishhook in a sleeve.

* * *

I found the scratches that evening.

I had dismissed Sera early — she seemed grateful in a way that stung — and shut the bedchamber door myself, wanting one room in this palace that behaved like it belonged to me. The latch caught. It always catches, I thought, and then stopped, because I had no idea whether it always caught, and the not-knowing had begun to acquire weight, to sit on the chest like wet wool.

The door swung inward and I stood holding the handle, looking at the inside face of it, at eye height, in the wood just beside the hinge-side frame.

Scratches. Tallies. Four vertical strokes crossed by a fifth, then another group, then another — dozens of groups, hundreds of strokes, cut into the varnish and through it into the pale wood beneath. Old ones, darkened with age and grime. And below them, near the bottom edge, fresh ones. Pale. New-cut. The varnish still gouged bright around them.

Someone had been counting days on the inside of my bedroom door.

I put my fingers on the fresh marks. They were clean-edged, done with something sharp, done recently — within days, within hours, possibly. The wood dust was still in the grooves.

Now, a reasonable man looks at tally marks on a prison cell door and thinks: prisoner. A reasonable man looks at them on the inside of his own bedchamber and thinks several other things, most of them involving intruders and madness, and none of them comforting. But what struck me — what struck me with a cold that started in my fingertips and climbed — was not *who* had cut them.

It was the height.

They were at my eye height. All of them, old and new alike, the whole history of tallies, in a hand-sized cluster exactly where I would have knelt or stood to cut them. And the newest stroke — the last mark, the freshest cut of all — was angled slightly leftward, the way I hold a knife, the way I've held a knife since I was a boy whittling bad arrows behind the stables while Maren told me I was doing it wrong.

I knew that handwriting. Every hand has one, even in knife-cuts on wood. It was mine.

I sat down on the floor with my back against the door, among the tallies, like a man sitting in the wreckage of his own signature, and I did the arithmetic I had been avoiding all day.

If the marks were days, and the fresh ones were recent, and the old ones went back and back and back into the darkened wood — then someone had been waking up in this room, morning after morning, year after year, cutting a mark into the door to prove the day had happened. To prove *he* had happened. And that someone used my knife-hand, at my height, in my room, in my body.

And I could not remember a single one of those days.

There is a particular horror in finding evidence of your own captivity when you cannot locate the prison. I turned and looked at the door — really looked — at the lock, at the bolt, at the keyhole with its brass worn bright. Worn bright from use. From the outside, a key turned often. From the inside, a bolt thrown nightly, I'd have wagered anything.

A gilded room, locked from both sides, marked inside with a prisoner's calendar in a king's own hand.

I laughed. I'll admit it — I sat on the floor of the most secure bedchamber in Ashvale and laughed, quietly, the slightly unhinged laugh of a man who has just realized the joke is on him and has not yet located the punchline. Somewhere out in the corridor, a guard shifted his weight. Listening? Protecting? Guarding the room or guarding against what was in it?

Then the laughter stopped, because a thought arrived that was not funny at all.

This morning, I had woken with the taste of copper and the feeling of having been *somewhere*. A passenger. And tonight, when sleep came, it would come again, and tomorrow morning I would wake in this bed, and I would find these marks, and I would — perhaps — begin again. The same discovery. The same cold climb of dread. The same day, discovered fresh, by a man who had discovered it a hundred times before and been unable to keep the knowledge overnight.

Unless.

Unless whoever — whatever — was taking my days had missed something. Had left a thread. People who erase always miss something; I was sure of that the way you're sure of gravity, though I couldn't have told you why I was sure, which was itself becoming a familiar and unwelcome sensation.

I got up off the floor. I took the letter-knife from the writing desk — my writing desk, with its inkstone worn into a hollow that fit my habits if not my memory — and I knelt at the bottom of the door, below the old tallies, below the new ones, in the shadow where a searcher's eye would skim past.

And I wrote, cutting deep, in the plainest letters I knew:

*I am Aldric. If you are reading this, you are Aldric. Do not trust the mornings. Count everything.*

I looked at it for a long time. It seemed pitifully small, five words and a warning, scratched into a door by a king who could not vouch for his own yesterday. Then I added one more line, because some instinct older than reason — the same instinct, maybe, that had once made a boy check behind himself for a tail — made me write:

*Ask Maren about the crown.*

I did not know why. That was the worst part. The words came out of my hand as if the hand remembered something the rest of me had been forbidden to keep.

Outside, the palace bells rang the hour — ten, I think, or eleven — and somewhere below, faint through the stone, I heard the sound of a key turning in a lock, and footsteps approaching along the corridor, unhurried, regular, the footsteps of someone who had made this walk many times before.

I blew out the candle and lay down on top of the covers, fully dressed, with the letter-knife in my fist, and listened to them come.
