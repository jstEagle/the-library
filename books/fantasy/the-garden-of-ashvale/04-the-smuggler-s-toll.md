## 4. The Smuggler's Toll

Ferro Vance kept his shop behind a false wall in a tannery that hadn't tanned anything in twenty years, which told you everything you needed to know about Ferro: he liked his doors the way he liked his debts — hidden, heavy, and smelling faintly of something dead.

I'd been to him twice before. The first time, I'd pulled him out of the Sump Canal with a hook through his shoulder and three of the Brackish Boys' knives already in him, and he'd sworn on his mother's grave — a woman, by all accounts, who had never once been lied to by her son and would have found the experience novel — that he owed me his life until the day he died. The second time, he'd tried to charge me full price for a lockpick set.

Mercenaries learn early that gratitude is a currency with terrible exchange rates. But the prophecy book had given me a name — *the fence with salt on his hands* — and Ferro was the only fence in Thornfell whose hands smelled of the mines, because he laundered his coin through the salt merchants' guild like everyone else too clever to be honest.

So there I was, at the hour when the tannery district's lamps burned low and honest, knocking the rhythm we'd agreed on years ago: two, pause, three, pause, one. Like a heartbeat giving up.

The false wall slid open a hand's width. One eye appeared in the gap, brown and bloodshot and deeply unhappy to see me.

"No," said the eye.

"You don't know what I'm going to ask yet."

"I know you. You've got the look. The look says trouble wearing its Sunday coat." The eye narrowed. "Also, you're Elodie Hartwell, and the last three people who came asking after you left in pieces. Small ones."

That was news. News I didn't love. "Who came asking?"

"Men in grey. No colors, no sigils, paid in old-mint crowns — Ashvale mint, if you can believe it, coins nobody's struck since the king stopped being seen in public." He paused, and I watched him decide whether to lie to me. He decided against it, mostly because lying to me had historically gone poorly for him. "They asked where you sheltered. I told them nothing, Elodie, on my mother's—"

"Open the door, Ferro."

The wall opened all the way, which was as close to an apology as Ferro Vance ever got.

* * *

Inside, his shop was exactly as I remembered: shelves of stolen things arranged with the loving precision of a man who catalogued other people's losses. Candlesticks from houses that had burned. Wedding rings from marriages that hadn't lasted. A child's wooden horse, polished smooth by small hands, sitting on a velvet cushion like it was the most valuable thing in the room. For Ferro, maybe it was. Guilt was the only inventory he never sold.

He poured us both wine without asking. It was good wine, which meant it was stolen from someone important, which meant drinking it was probably a crime with a name.

"Salt," I said.

"Is this about your skin or mine?"

"Mine. Mostly." I set the prophecy book on the table between us, wrapped in oilcloth. His eyes went to it the way a dog's eyes go to a bone. "There's a key. First of three. The book says it sleeps where the chapel drowned, under the salt, guarded by what the miners wouldn't dig past. I need to know where that is, Ferro. Exactly where. And I need to know who else knows."

Ferro looked at the book for a long time. Then he looked at me, and something in his face shifted — the merchant falling away, the man underneath showing through, the one who'd bled into the Sump Canal and meant every word of his mother's-grave oath.

"You know what I do with things people want badly?" he said. "I sell them. That's the trade. But some things—" he tapped the oilcloth once, lightly, like it might bite, "—some things aren't worth selling because the buyers kill the sellers. Those men in grey? They weren't asking after you like you were a mark. They were asking after you like you were a plague. 'The woman who reads forbidden pages.' Their words. There's a price on questions now, Elodie. Not answers. Questions. You ask the wrong thing in the wrong tavern, and by morning there are men measuring you for a shroud."

"The drowned chapel," I said. "Where."

He drank. He drank again. Then he got up, went to the back of the shop, and pried up a floorboard I'd have sworn was just a floorboard, and came back with a map so old the vellum had gone the color of weak tea.

"Old Thornfell," he said, spreading it flat. "Before the city grew up over its own corpse. The salt mines go down six levels, and the sixth level isn't a mine anymore because the miners broke into something they shouldn't have. A chapel. Submerged — the aquifer runs through it. The guild sealed it, called it a collapse, paid the widows, moved on." His finger traced down through the inked levels and stopped at a scrawl in faded red: *Chapel of the Tides, closed by order.* "The key, if it exists, is down there. So is whatever guards it. Three crews went looking forty years ago. One came back. He didn't come back right — sat in the Drowned Rat for the rest of his life staring at his hands and saying, 'She has my face, she has my face,' until his heart gave out."

"She?"

"The book doesn't say?"

The book said plenty, none of it comforting. *The guardian wears what you bring down to her,* one line read. I hadn't shared that part with anyone. I wasn't going to start with Ferro.

"What do you want for it?" I asked. "The map. The location. Everything."

"I want you to burn that book."

"Ferro—"

"I want you to take it out into the street and put it under a lamp and let it go up, and then I want you to get on a caravan heading anywhere, and I want you to be alive somewhere far from here, doing mercenary work, which is a stupid trade but at least it kills you honestly." He said it fast, all in one breath, like a man diving into cold water. "That's my price. That's what I want. Because I owe you my life, and the way you keep it is to stop."

For a moment — a real moment, one I'd remember later, though not for long, though not nearly long enough — I almost considered it.

Then I thought of the page with my name on it. My name, written a hundred years before my mother met my father, in ink that predated the paper it sat on. *Elodie Hartwell, on the night the convoy shelters at the shrine of Saint Verene.* Which had happened. Which had happened exactly, down to the rain and the broken shutter and the owl in the rafters.

You can't walk away from a sentence that finished itself before you were born. Believe me. I'd spent four days trying.

"I can't burn it," I said quietly. "It's the only copy. And it's — Ferro, it's not just a prophecy. It's a debt. Somebody wrote this down so somebody would act on it, and I'm the somebody. I don't get to be comfortable. I get to be useful."

"That's a horrible philosophy."

"It's kept me fed."

He stared at me across the candlelight, and then he laughed — short, bitter, defeated — and pushed the map across the table. "Take it. Take it and may every god who still answers prayers regret helping you." He stood, walked me to the false wall, and stopped with his hand on the mechanism. "One more thing. Free of charge, because apparently I enjoy watching people I care about die."

"Ferro."

"The men in grey. When they left my shop, they didn't threaten me. That's what scared me. They thanked me. Said I'd been 'a faithful servant of the quiet years.'" His hand trembled slightly on the wall. "Elodie, I have never served anyone in my life. Whatever they think serves them — it's bigger than a bounty. It's older. Go carefully. Or better, don't go."

I went.

* * *

The convoy was camped in the quarry hollow outside Thornfell's south gate, thirty wagons of contraband silk and untaxed brandy and one very nervous smuggler-captain named Ossa who paid my company well to keep her cargo breathing. I came back over the ridge an hour past midnight, and I knew before I reached the sentry line that something was wrong, because the camp was too quiet. Not asleep-quiet. Held-breath quiet.

Brannick found me first, materializing out of the dark the way he did, all elbows and worry. "You're back," he whispered. "Good. We need to talk, and we need to whisper while we do it."

"Report."

"Two hours ago, a peddler came through selling charms. Tin and ribbon, worthless stuff. Wren bought one — you know Wren, buys anything shiny. Peddler leaves. Twenty minutes later, Wren's charm starts humming. Low. Like a wasp in a jar." Brannick's jaw worked. "We smashed it. But Elodie — it was singing our positions. I'd swear it on anything you like. It knew where the watch fires were. It knew where *you'd* pitched your tent, and you weren't even here yet."

A scrying charm. Cheap enough to hand to a peddler, precise enough to sing a camp's anatomy to anyone listening. That wasn't banditry. Bandits didn't need to know where you slept; they just needed to know you had things worth taking. This was something else. This was a hunt with paperwork.

"How many watches posted?" I asked.

"All of them. Doubled."

It wasn't enough. I knew it wasn't enough the way you know weather's coming — in the teeth, in the old scar along my ribs that ached before storms and before, twice in my life, before violence. It was aching now.

"Get Ossa," I said. "Wake the whole camp. Quietly. Nobody strikes the tents, nobody shows light — just get blades into hands and eyes open. If anything moves on the ridge, I want to know before it knows we know."

Brannick went. I stood at the edge of the hollow and looked at the ridge line, black against marginally-less-black sky, and let myself feel what I'd been refusing to feel since the shrine: fear. Clean, honest, useful fear. Ferro was right. The cost of knowing too much was that the knowing spread. Every question I asked left footprints, and something out there was reading them like a tracker reads snow.

I touched the oilcloth-wrapped book inside my coat. Warm. It was always warm now. I tried not to think about that.

* * *

They came an hour before dawn, which was flattering — it meant they respected us.

No war cries. No arrows arcing in with whistles attached. Just a soft wrongness at the perimeter, the sound of the night changing its mind, and then the first sentry's lantern guttering out with a crossbow bolt through it, and then the hollow was full of moving shapes and our doubled watches were earning their pay and then some.

I'll give the assassins this: they were professionals. Grey-wrapped, faceless, working in pairs, silent except for the wet sounds of their work. They didn't want the cargo. They ignored the brandy wagons entirely, which insulted Ossa more than the attack did. They wanted one thing, and they cut through my company toward it with the patience of surgeons.

Toward me.

I killed two of them at the tent line — quick, ugly, the way killing should be if you're doing it right, because pretty fights mean someone's enjoying themselves. A third nearly took my arm off at the elbow; I gave him my dagger in the throat instead and felt bad about it for exactly as long as it took the fourth one to come out of the dark at my back.

What saved me wasn't skill. It was the book.

It flared. Inside my coat, against my chest, the prophecy book went hot as a forge-stone, and the assassin behind me hesitated — half a heartbeat, no more, but half a heartbeat is a country in a knife fight — and I turned and put him down. When I fished the book out afterward, expecting scorched cloth and ruined pages, the oilcloth wasn't even warm anymore. The book lay in my hands like any old book, innocent as a hymnal.

Except the open page. The open page had changed.

I memorized it by lantern-light while the last of the attackers fled and Brannick shouted the wounded count — four of ours hurt, none dead, thank every indifferent god — and the new lines sat there in that same impossible century-old ink:

*Three keys for the crown that eats,*
*and hunters for the one who seeks.*
*The first is salt, the second iron, the third is bone —*
*and she will go down alone, and she will not come back the same.*

Below it, fresh as a wound, one final line that had definitely not been there in the shrine:

*They know your name now. Run anyway.*

"Elodie!" Brannick, limping toward me, blood on his sleeve that turned out to be mostly his own. "Elodie, the ones that ran — they left something. Deliberately. Staked it to the ground at the north end like a message."

It was a strip of grey cloth, pinned to the earth with a knife of Ashvale make, and written on the cloth in charcoal, in a neat clerk's hand:

**THE CROWN THANKS YOU FOR YOUR INTEREST.**

I stood there in the wreck of the camp, among groaning men and scattered cargo and the smell of blood soaking into quarry dust, holding a book that wrote itself and a threat that said *please continue*, and I understood — finally, fully, in my bones — what Ferro had been trying to buy with his map.

Knowing too much wasn't the danger.

The danger was that the thing I knew about wanted to be known. It had sent killers who couldn't finish the job because the job wasn't to finish me. The job was to herd me. To make the road to Ashvale feel like the only road left.

And the worst part — the part I carried out of that hollow at dawn, with my company licking wounds and asking questions I couldn't answer — the worst part was that it was working.

The first key slept under the salt, six levels down, guarded by something that wore faces. And I was going to go down alone, exactly as the book demanded, because the book had never once been wrong.

I only wished, packing my kit while the sun came up bloody over Thornfell, that being right counted for more than it cost.
