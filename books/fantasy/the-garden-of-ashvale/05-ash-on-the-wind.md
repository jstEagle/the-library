## 5. Ash on the Wind

There is a particular kind of meeting that begins with servants being dismissed and ends with you owing something you cannot name. I have sat through perhaps forty of them in my life as Regent of Ashvale, and I have learned to count the candles on the way in. Tonight there were seven, which meant someone expected to be here a long while and wanted me to know it.

The Ashen Court keeps its chambers beneath the east wing, past the wine cellars my grandfather dug and deeper than any cellar has a right to go. The guards who let me through do not salute. They simply step aside, the way men step aside for weather.

"Regent." The voice came from the far end of the long table, where the candlelight gave out. "You're late."

"I'm punctual," I said. "You're early. There's a difference, and it flatters me."

A dry sound that might have been laughter. Custodian Vell — I knew him by voice alone; he preferred it that way — gestured to the chair across from his. I sat. Seven candles, one of them guttering, and between us a small lacquered box I had seen twice before in my life and hoped never to see again.

"You've brought the tally," I said.

"We've brought the truth," said another voice, a woman's, from my left. I hadn't heard her enter. That was the thing about custodians: they moved like drafts under doors. "The tally is only how we say it politely."

I looked at the box. Lacquer over iron, iron over whatever was inside. I did not ask what was inside. In twenty years of these meetings I had never asked, because asking makes you a participant, and participation, in the Ashen Court, is a debt with compound interest.

"Then say it impolitely," I told them. "It's late, and I have a kingdom to run in the morning."

Vell opened the box.

Inside, on a bed of grey velvet gone greyer with age, lay a ring of ash. Not ash *in* a ring — ash shaped like one, holding its form the way smoke holds a memory of the fire it came from. It did not glitter. It did not move. And yet every hair on my arms stood up and paid attention, the way dogs do before an earthquake.

"The Crown of Aldric the First," Vell said, "as it was before we bound it to your brother's brow. Do you know what this is, Regent? Truly?"

"A relic," I said. "An heirloom. A story we tell foreign ambassadors so they behave themselves at dinner."

"It's a mouth," the woman said. Her name was Sera, though she had worn three others since I'd known her. Custodians change names the way other people change gloves. "And it has been eating for four hundred years. Kings feed it their memories — the crown takes them slowly, gently, a course at a time, and in exchange it gives what it has always given. The long life. The unaging face. The stability of a realm whose king never grows old enough to grow foolish."

"And my brother," I said, "is running out of courses."

The silence that followed had texture to it. Velvet silence. Ash silence.

"Your brother," Vell said at last, "has been wearing the crown for eleven years. His father wore it for thirty. His grandfather for forty-one. Aldric was crowned young — too young, some of us thought, but the succession does not consult committees — and a young man has fewer memories to give. Twenty-two years of living when the crown closed around his head. He is now thirty-three, and the crown has eaten nearly all of it."

"He forgets things," I said. My voice came out flat, which took work. "He forgets servants' names. He forgot our mother's funeral. Last month he asked me why the garden smells of ash, as if he'd never—"

"As if he'd never stood in it," Sera finished. "No. He wouldn't remember standing in it. He remembers almost nothing of the garden now. Or the nursery. Or you, Regent, beyond what we permit the crown to leave him."

That landed somewhere under my ribs and stayed there. "What do you mean, *permit*?"

"The crown is not indiscriminate," Vell said. "It takes memories the way a careful steward spends coin — the oldest first, the least useful, the ones that won't be missed. But it keeps certain things. Your face, for instance. Your voice. The fact that you are his sister and can be trusted. We curate it. Someone must, or the king would wake each morning a stranger in his own palace, and that way lies panic, and panic in a capital city is a fire no one puts out."

"So you decide what my brother remembers of me."

"We decide what he remembers of everything. Yes."

I looked at the ash-ring in its box and understood, with the slow cold certainty of a woman reading her own sentence, that I had known this for years without letting myself know it. You cannot be Regent of Ashvale and not notice that the king wakes some mornings gentler than he went to sleep, or that his childhood has shrunk to three stories he tells the same way every time, word for word, like a prayer. You notice. You file it away. You tell yourself the crown is a symbol, that kings are strange, that memory is a soft thing and soft things wear.

You tell yourself whatever lets you keep sleeping in the palace.

"How much does he have left?" I asked.

Sera turned the box toward me, and for the first time I saw that the velvet beneath the ash-ring bore faint marks — scratches, tallies, hundreds of them, like the counting-marks a prisoner cuts into a door.

"Months," she said. "Perhaps a season. After that, the crown begins taking things he needs. Language goes first. Then the ability to form new memory at all. Then—" she made a small gesture, delicate and horrible, "—then he becomes a very well-dressed absence, and Ashvale has a problem."

* * *

Here is what they do not put in the histories, because the custodians write the histories and custodians do not write confessions:

Ashvale has always had two thrones. One sits in the great hall, gilded and uncomfortable, where my family has perched for four hundred years and smiled at ambassadors. The other sits beneath it, in rooms without windows, worn by no one and governing everyone. My ancestors made a bargain in the founding years — I have read the fragments Tam-scholars smuggle out of the archive, before the archive burns what it burns — and the bargain was simple. The crown keeps the king ageless and the kingdom stable. The kingdom keeps the crown fed. And when one vessel runs thin, the court finds another.

"When you say *procure*," I said carefully, "what exactly—"

"A new vessel," Vell said. "A new head for the crown. Young. Healthy. Preferably of the royal line, for continuity's sake, though the crown is not proud. It has worn commoners before, in lean centuries. It isn't particular about blood. Only about fullness."

"Fullness."

"A rich mind, Regent. A mind with years in it. A mind that has loved and feared and traveled and grieved. The crown eats best from a full table." He smiled, and I wished he hadn't; custodians should not smile, it is like watching furniture attempt warmth. "Your brother was a thin meal from the start. We warned your mother. She chose hope. Mothers do."

My mother had died believing her son would outlive her forever. I wondered, briefly and uselessly, whether anyone had told her the price, or whether she too had been fed the word *heirloom* and swallowed it whole.

"And if I refuse?" I asked. "If I stand up right now and walk out of this room and tell the court what you are?"

Sera laughed — actually laughed, a short bright sound entirely wrong for the room. "Tell them what? That the beloved king is hollowed by a relic? That the stability they've praised for four centuries is a feeding schedule? Regent, you would burn down the only thing keeping this kingdom from tearing itself apart, and the crown would go hungry, and a hungry crown does not wait politely for a successor. It takes. From whoever is nearest. From whoever has the fullest head in the room."

She let that sit.

"And you," she added softly, "have such a full head, Maren. All those negotiations. All those betrayals you've catalogued and forgiven and catalogued again. All those nights you spent memorizing your brother's face so you'd recognize him after. So much *material*."

I did not answer. There was nothing to answer with. That is the architecture of the Ashen Court — every exit bricked, every window painted shut, and always, always a door marked *cooperation* glowing warmly at the center of the room.

"What do you want from me?" I said.

"Simple logistics," Vell said. "A candidate. Someone the court can take quietly, when the time comes. Someone whose absence will be explained by competent people." He folded his hands. "We leave the selection to you. You know the palace. You know the staff. You know which lives are heavy and which are light."

They were asking me to choose who would be eaten.

I want to write that I refused. I want to record, for whoever reads this — for myself, later, when I need to believe I was ever clean — that I stood up and called them monsters and walked out into the honest dark. But the truth is that I sat there for a long moment doing arithmetic, because I am my father's daughter and arithmetic is the family disease. A stranger chosen deliberately, prepared, eased into it — or the crown starving loose in a year and taking whom it pleased, possibly my brother's last shreds, possibly mine, possibly a kitchen girl with a laugh like bells and a mother waiting tables in the lower town.

That is how they get you. Not with threats. With menus.

"I'll need time," I said.

"You have until the coronation anniversary," Sera said. "The rite. The crown feeds deeply that night — it always has — and we will know then how much of your brother remains. Come to us before the rite with a name, or come to us after with your own."

* * *

I did not go back to my chambers. I walked instead, which is what I do when my thoughts get loud — through the gallery, past the portraits of kings who all share the same suspiciously unwrinkled faces, down the long corridor to the west doors and out into what remains of the garden.

The garden of Ashvale was once famous. Three kingdoms sent gardeners to study it. Now the soil is poisoned with something old — ash, the chroniclers call it, though no fire ever burned here — and nothing grows but pale lichen and one stubborn rowan that my grandmother swore at daily and loved fiercely. The trees my ancestors planted stand black and bare, and have stood black and bare for longer than anyone alive can remember, and every king has promised to restore the garden, and every king has forgotten the promise by spring.

Because the crown ate it. Of course it ate it. The garden was the first thing to go, every time — the place where each boy-king played before the crown came down over his head. The crown takes childhood first. It is the richest meal.

Aldric used to play here. I found a memory of it once, in a box of our mother's letters: a sketch she'd drawn of two children under the rowan, a boy of six holding up a stick like a sword, a girl of nine laughing at him. On the back she'd written, *Aldric says he'll be the greatest knight the garden ever grew. Maren says knights don't grow in gardens. They were both right.*

I stood under the rowan now, in the dark, in the wind that comes off the mountains carrying actual ash sometimes — real ash, from hearths in the lower town, ordinary human fire — and I tried to remember the boy in the sketch.

I could. That was the obscene part. I remembered him perfectly: the gap in his teeth, the way he pronounced *knight* as *nigit*, the summer he buried a dead sparrow under the rowan and held a funeral attended by the entire household staff because he insisted Sparrows Deserve Dignity. I remembered him learning statecraft at fourteen, frowning over trade ledgers with ink on his nose. I remembered him at his coronation, white-faced under the crown's weight, whispering to me as they lowered it: *Maren, it's warm. Why is it warm?*

I remember everything. That is my curse in this family — I am the one who keeps what the crown discards. Every memory my brother loses, I seem to hold a copy of, hoarded without meaning to, a museum no one visits. Sometimes I think that is why the custodians tolerate me. A regent with a perfect memory is useful. A regent with a perfect memory is also, I now understood, a very well-stocked pantry.

The wind moved through the dead branches, and for a moment — I will swear this to no one but this page — the sound it made was the sound of a boy laughing.

"No," I said aloud, to the tree, to the wind, to the seven candles burning in a room beneath my feet. "Not him. Not again. Find another way."

The wind did not answer. Winds don't. Gods don't either, in this kingdom; the silent god Veyra stopped answering a century ago, and the priests say she abandoned us, and the custodians smile when they say it, and I have begun to wonder what, exactly, a god's silence costs, and who collects the payment.

I stayed under the rowan until my fingers went numb. Then I went back inside, past the guards who do not salute, past the cellar stairs that lead down to the seven candles, and up to my chambers, where I sat at my desk and drew out paper and ink and did not write anything for a very long time.

A name. They wanted a name by the rite.

I thought of the kitchen girl with the bell-laugh. I thought of the ambassador's second son, dull and expandable. I thought, with a shame I will not dress up, of the servant who brings Aldric his morning tea and whom Aldric no longer recognizes, and of how easily an accident could be arranged, how cleanly, how *kindly*, compared to the alternative.

And then I thought of a stick held up like a sword. *Nigit.* Sparrows Deserve Dignity.

I blew out the candle. In the dark I made myself a promise, the kind of promise that feels noble at midnight and impossible by morning: I would find another way. Some third door the custodians hadn't bricked. Some way to save my brother without feeding them anyone at all.

I did not know yet that the third door had already opened — that even then, in a smuggler's den across the mountains, a mercenary with a dead god's eyes was reading a prophecy with my brother's name in it, and that assassins were already moving through Thornfell's streets toward her convoy, dispatched by orders signed in a room with seven candles.

I did not know that the choice I was agonizing over had already been made for me.

Or rather — I learned it the next morning, when a courier arrived mud-spattered from the coast with news that a convoy had been hit in the night, that survivors spoke of a woman who fought like a legend, and that among the wreckage the attackers had been searching for, they had found nothing but a burned book and a trail of blood leading north.

Toward Ashvale.

Toward me.

Toward my brother.

I stood at the window a long time, watching ash drift on the wind from the lower town's chimneys, and I understood at last that the menu had been taken out of my hands — and that whatever came through my gates next, crown or prophecy or girl with a dead god behind her eyes, I would have to decide, finally and forever, whose side I was on.

The rite was in nineteen days.
