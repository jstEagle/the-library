## 6. The First Key: Salt

The salt mines of Thornfell smell like a dead god's kitchen — brine and old stone and something underneath both that I can only describe as *waiting*. I'd been descending for three hours by the time the air turned thick enough to chew, following the prophecy's riddle down a shaft the miners had sealed with iron plates and, judging by the scratches on them, genuine religious terror.

*Where the sea forgot to go, the faithful built a door.*

The miners hadn't sealed it to keep people out. They'd sealed it to keep the water *in*.

I stood at the edge of a flooded gallery, black water stretching into darkness, so still it looked like polished obsidian. Somewhere beneath it was a chapel. Somewhere in the chapel was the first key. And somewhere behind me, presumably, were the crown's agents Ferro Vance had warned me about, which meant standing still was its own kind of death sentence.

"Right," I told the water. "Nothing for it."

I'm not afraid of drowning. I want that on the record. I've held my breath through smuggler holds flooding in a storm, through a river crossing in Vellmark where the current tried to keep me, through three separate interrogations involving buckets. Fear of drowning is fear of the water winning. The water and I have an understanding: it takes what it wants, and I take it back.

So I tied my knife-belt tight, filled my lungs, and went under.

The cold hit like a slap from someone who loved me. Down, along a guide-rope some long-dead priest had anchored into the rock, past salt formations that hung like teeth, until the chapel opened below me — a vaulted nave drowned whole, pews furred white with mineral bloom, an altar at the far end glowing faintly with light that had no business existing underwater.

There was air in the chapel. A pocket of it, trapped under the vaulted ceiling, stale as a tomb and twice as quiet. I broke the surface gasping and hauled myself onto the altar steps, and there I stopped, because the altar wasn't empty.

A figure sat on it. Or knelt on it. It was hard to say which, because the thing had been kneeling so long that kneeling had become its shape — a wight crusted in salt like a candle burned down to its intention, hollow-eyed, wearing the rotted remnant of vestments that might once have been white.

It turned its head toward me. Salt sifted off it like snow.

"You come asking," it said. Its voice was the sound of tide going out over gravel. "They always come asking. Fewer come *answering*."

"I'm not big on questions," I said. "Occupational hazard. People who ask too many in my line end up in the harbor."

The wight regarded me. There was nothing in its eye sockets but shadow and, faintly, a light like the last coal in a banked fire.

"The key is not taken," it said. "The key is *given*. And it is only given to those who know whose hands they carry." It raised one salt-crusted finger. "Show me your past, mercenary. All of it. Even the parts you buried. Even—" and here something moved in its voice that might have been pity, or might have been hunger, "—the parts you never lived at all."

"I don't love the sound of that last bit."

"No one does. That is rather the point."

I should have turned around. Swum back up the rope, climbed out of Thornfell, found a contract escorting wool to Vellmark, and let the king keep his cursed hat. That's what any sensible mercenary would have done.

But the prophecy had my name in it. My name, and tonight's date, written down before my grandmother's grandmother learned to walk. And when I'd prayed at Veyra's dead altar — the god my order still serves, the god who hasn't answered in a hundred years — I'd felt something I couldn't explain. Not an answer. More like the memory of being answered. Like walking into a room where someone you loved used to live.

"Fine," I said. "Show me."

The wight smiled. I could tell because its face cracked.

* * *

The visions came like falling.

First, mine — the ones I knew. My mother's kitchen in Harrow's Bend, steam on the window, her hands floured to the wrist. The day she died, and the specific creak of the third stair, the one I still hear in strange houses. My first kill, at seventeen, and how I'd been sick afterward, and how my sergeant had put a hand on my shoulder and said *the sickness means you're still worth saving*, and how I'd believed him for exactly four more years.

The wight walked me through all of it without comment, a patient archivist turning pages. I knelt on the flooded steps and shook and let it happen, because this was the toll and I'd agreed to pay it.

Then the pages started turning wrong.

I saw myself at a hearth I'd never sat at, in a house I'd never entered, and I was older in it — gray at the temples — teaching a child to hold a blade, and the child called me by a name that wasn't mine but fit my mouth perfectly. I saw myself standing on a cliff above a green sea, beside a man whose face kept sliding away like wet paint, and I was grieving him, grieving him with a grief so old and enormous it had furniture, habits, a language of its own. I saw a battlefield from three centuries ago, and I was on the losing side of it, and I knew the battle-cry before it was shouted.

"That's not me," I said. My voice came out thin. "None of that happened to me."

"Didn't it?" the wight asked gently.

And then the last vision came, and it didn't bother dressing itself up as mine at all.

I stood in a temple — *the* temple, Veyra's temple, in the years when it had been full — and I looked down at my own hands, except they weren't my hands, they were broader, scarred differently, a sword-callus in the wrong place. I turned and saw my reflection in the consecrated bronze of the altar doors, and the face looking back was my face, but worn by someone else. Someone who carried it the way you carry a tool you've used for decades. She smiled, and it was my smile, and it was not my smile at all.

She was praying. I was inside her prayer. And the words she spoke were words I have said every night since I took my vows, word for word, the evening litany of the silent god — except she spoke them in the present tense, to a god who answered, and the answer came like sunlight through water, warm and total and *known*.

Veyra. The god wore my face. Or I wore hers. By the end of the vision I genuinely could not have told you which way round it went.

I came back to myself on the altar steps, soaked in sweat despite the cold, salt water lapping at my knees, and the wight watching me with its banked-coal eyes.

"What," I said, "was that."

"A test," it said. "Which you have passed. Though I confess you passed it strangely. Most who see the god's face in their own run screaming. You just look *annoyed*."

"I'm saving the screaming for later. I find it keeps better." I wiped my face with a shaking hand. "That woman. That life. Those aren't my memories."

"No," the wight agreed. "They are older than you. They are what remains when a god is eaten slowly, over a century, and the crumbs fall wherever they fall. Some fell into bloodlines. Some fell into dreams. And some—" it leaned closer, and the light in its sockets flickered, "—some fell into *you*, mercenary of the silent god. You are not haunted. You are *leftover*."

The chapel was very quiet. The water lapped. Somewhere far above, muffled through fathoms of stone, Thornfell went about its rotten business.

"If you're lying—"

"I am a dead thing guarding a key in a drowned church. Lying is a luxury of the living." It extended its hand, palm up, and the salt on it split and fell away like a shell breaking, and there in its palm lay the first key: small, black, made of something that wasn't metal or bone but felt like both, cold enough to burn. "Take it. And take a warning with it, freely given, because I liked your face even if it isn't entirely yours. The crown knows what you are now. It has known since you read your name. It will send everything it has, and what it has is very old and very hungry, and it does not care whom it eats to reach you."

I took the key. It sank into my palm like it belonged there, which frightened me more than anything else in that chapel, including the visions.

"One more question," I said. "The woman. The one with my face. Did she—"

"Ask the blind seer in the prison-fortress at Karroch's Hold," the wight said. "She has waited a long time to tell you. Longer than you've been alive." It began to settle back into its kneel, salt already creeping over it again, a candle re-forming around its flame. "Now go. The water is rising, and I am tired, and you have a war to lose beautifully."

"The water is *what*?"

* * *

I swam out of there with the key against my sternum and the gallery flooding behind me — the wight hadn't been making conversation; whatever bound that place to the tides had broken when the key left it, and the sea was coming home to its forgotten chapel with interest. I came up the guide-rope with my lungs screaming, hauled myself over the iron lip of the shaft, and lay on the mine floor coughing brine while the water rose below with a sound like slow applause.

I should have felt victorious. First key, retrieved, prophecy on schedule. Instead I lay on cold stone and stared at the dark and turned my hands over and over, looking at them, trying to work out whose they were.

Because here's the thing nobody tells you about inherited memory: it doesn't feel foreign. That's the horror of it. My mother's kitchen felt like mine. The cliff and the green sea and the grief with furniture — those felt like mine too, deeper than mine, mine the way a riverbed belongs to the river. For a few horrible minutes on the chapel steps I had remembered a husband I'd never had and mourned him honestly, and some part of me is still standing on that cliff, and I don't know how to get her down.

Veyra went silent a hundred years ago. Every priest says the same thing: the god withdrew, the god's purpose is mystery, the god tests us with silence. We built a whole faith on the silence. Prayers thrown down a well for a century.

Nobody ever asked what made the well so deep.

I got up. I wrung out my coat, checked my knives out of habit, and started the long climb back toward the minehead, where Ferro's warning was waiting for me along with the crown's agents and the rest of my short, eventful future. The key pulsed faintly against my chest with each heartbeat, like a second heart learning the rhythm.

Two keys remained. Iron, somewhere in a prison-fortress full of caged prophets. Bone, in a place the prophecy described in words I hadn't been able to parse yet.

And between me and all of it, apparently, a god's scattered memories wearing my face like a hand-me-down coat.

At the top of the shaft, dawn was coming through the mine entrance, gray and thin. I stepped out into it and stopped, because there were boot prints in the frost outside the minehead. Fresh ones. A lot of them. Heading in.

They'd found the convoy. Which meant they'd found my company. Which meant that whatever the wight had shown me under the water, the crown was already collecting its own memories of me — and it had a head start.

I pulled my hood up and ran.
