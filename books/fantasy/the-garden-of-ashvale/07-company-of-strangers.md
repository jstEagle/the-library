## 7. Company of Strangers

Three days after the ambush, I learned that blood dries faster than grief.

I'd stopped counting the dead by the second night. There had been eleven of us when Ferro Vance gave up the first key's location — a good number, a lucky number, we used to joke, because eleven was one more than ten and ten was what you needed to survive anything in Thornfell. Now there was me, and the joke had curdled into arithmetic I couldn't finish.

The assassins hadn't been sloppy. That was the worst part. Sloppiness you could read, could learn from, could curse. This had been craft: fire first to scatter us, then crossbows from the rooftops in overlapping arcs, then blades for whoever crawled out of the smoke. Someone had paid for precision, and precision in Thornfell cost more than most lords earned in a year.

Which meant the crown's agents weren't a rumor anymore. They were a budget line.

I moved by day and slept badly by night, holed up in drain-culverts and the backs of shuttered dye-houses, keeping the salt key wrapped in oilcloth against my ribs like a second heart. Twice I spotted watchers on my trail — gray-cloaked, patient, the kind who didn't hurry because they knew something I didn't. I lost them both, once through the tannery runoffs and once by walking into a funeral procession and becoming, briefly, a mourner with no name and no face. The dead of Thornfell are generous that way. Nobody looks hard at grief.

By the third evening I was hungry enough to make mistakes, which is how I came to be crouched behind a stack of pickle barrels in the back room of a chandlery, watching a boy copy a book by candlelight.

He couldn't have been more than nineteen. Thin as a stylus, ink-stained to the elbow, hair cut short in the way of someone who'd recently decided his old life was over. He sat at a plank table with a sheaf of cheap paper before him and was transcribing — carefully, lovingly, with the particular hunched devotion of a man copying scripture — a page he'd torn from somewhere else.

I recognized it before I recognized him.

The margins. The little hooked serifs on the capital letters. The way the scribe dotted his *i*s slightly left of center, a habit so specific it might as well have been a signature.

It was the same hand as the prophecy book sitting in my pack. The same hand that had written *Elodie Hartwell* beside tonight's date a hundred years ago.

"Put the pen down," I said, "slowly."

He did not put the pen down slowly. He knocked the inkpot over, grabbed a knife off the table — a scribe's knife, for trimming quills, about as threatening as a spoon — and held it out in front of him with both hands, which is the posture of a man who has never held a weapon and knows it.

"I don't have any money," he said.

"I'm not robbing you." I stepped into the candlelight, hands open. "I want to know where you copied that page."

His eyes went to my face, then to the sword at my hip, then to the bloodstiff bandage peeking above my collar where a bolt had grazed me three days ago. Something in his expression shifted from fear to recognition, though not of me — of the *kind* of thing I was. Hunted people know each other the way dogs know thunder.

"You're her," he said. "You're the one from the book."

And there it was. My whole life, I had been nobody in particular — a mercenary with a silent god and a decent reputation for keeping convoys alive. Now every stranger who looked at me twice seemed to already know my name, and none of them had learned it from me.

"Where did you copy it," I said again, not making it a question this time.

"The royal archive. In Ashvale." He set the knife down. "My name's Tam."

* * *

We talked until the candle burned down to a puddle, and here is what I learned about Tam, arranged in the order I believed it, which was not the order he told it:

One: he had been a junior archivist in the royal archive at Ashvale, third generation of scribes, raised on vellum and lamp-oil fumes.

Two: four months ago, cataloguing a sealed folio that had no business being unsealed, he found a prophecy in an antique hand — and made an unauthorized copy, because that is what scribes do; a text that isn't copied is a text waiting to die.

Three: someone noticed. He never learned who. His quarters were searched, his master was questioned, and Tam did what clever cowards do: he ran, south, all the way to Thornfell, carrying nothing but his knife, his ink, and one loose page of a hundred-year-old lie he'd thought was a curiosity.

Four: three days ago, the same night my company died, men in gray cloaks had come asking after a runaway scribe in the riverside wards.

"So they're not hunting you," he said. "They're hunting *us*. Or hunting the book. Which means—" He stopped, because he'd arrived where I'd arrived hours earlier.

"Which means the ambush wasn't about the key," I said. "Or not only. They're burning everything that points at the crown. Vance. The archive. You. Me." I tapped the prophecy book between us. "This is the last copy of anything, isn't it?"

Tam looked at the book the way you look at a wounded animal you've agreed to care for. "As far as I know. The original was in the sealed folio. If they've burned the archive—"

"They've burned the archive."

"How do you know?"

"Because I'm still alive," I said, "and they're thorough about everything else."

He absorbed that. Then, in the voice of a man negotiating with his own better judgment: "If you're going to Ashvale, you'll need someone who knows the archive. Who knows the city. Who knows which gates have honest guards and which ones sell their honesty by the pound."

"I'm going to Ashvale," I confirmed. "But I haven't asked you to come."

"No," he said. "You haven't."

* * *

Here is the truth I didn't tell him, mostly because I was still refusing to say it to myself: I didn't trust him, and I took him along anyway.

Not because I needed a guide — I'd walked into stranger cities with less. Not because he charmed me; he had the conversational warmth of a ledger. I took him because of the hand. Because a hundred years ago, some scribe with the same left-leaning dots and hooked serifs had written my name next to a date, and the universe had gone to great expense to prove the page true. And now here was another hand just like it, attached to a living boy, offering to walk me toward the thing the page described.

Coincidence had stopped being coincidence three days ago. I'd stopped pretending otherwise around dawn.

Still, I watched him. On the road north out of Thornfell, disguised as a wool merchant's clerk and myself as the merchant — I make a passable merchant if the merchant has recently suffered losses and doesn't want to talk about it — I noted things. He flinched at loud voices but not at weapons. He counted everything under his breath: steps, coins, fence posts, the syllables in other travelers' sentences. When we stopped at a wayside shrine — Veyra's, cracked-lipped and cold like all of them now — he didn't pray, but he stood in front of the altar a long moment with his head bowed, like a man observing a silence out of respect rather than faith.

"You're not one of hers," I said, when we were back on the road.

"No. Archive staff kept to the Ninefold rite. Officially." A pause. "Unofficially, I always thought it was strange. A whole kingdom's patron god, and not one answered prayer in living memory. You'd think someone would have mentioned it. You'd think it would bother people."

"It bothers me," I said.

"Why do you keep praying, then? To a god who doesn't answer?"

I considered lying to him. It would have been easy; he wanted to believe the best of people and would have accepted any smooth answer. But there was something about a boy who copies texts without permission to keep them alive that makes lying feel like vandalism.

"Because silence is still a relationship," I said finally. "Veyra swore an oath to hear her people. She hasn't released me from mine. Maybe that's stubbornness. Maybe it's faith. From the outside they look identical."

"And from the inside?"

"From the inside they look identical too. That's the trick of it."

He nodded slowly, filing it away somewhere. I could see him doing it — sorting my words into whatever internal archive he carried, cross-referencing against the prophecy, against the page in his pocket. It should have made me nervous. Instead it made me feel, for the first time since the ambush, slightly less alone, which is its own kind of danger and I knew it.

* * *

The trouble came on the fourth night, at a coaching inn called the Drowned Bell, two days north of Thornfell's walls.

I'd chosen the inn because it was the kind of place where questions die of neglect, and I'd chosen our cover story with care. But I'd underestimated two things: the reach of the gray cloaks, and Tam's face. Specifically, Tam's habit, when nervous, of reciting things under his breath — and what he chose to recite, when frightened, was the prophecy. Our prophecy. My name, my date, the three keys, murmured into his soup like a prayer to a god even quieter than mine.

A carter at the next table heard enough. By midnight there was a man in a gray cloak at the end of the common room, drinking nothing, watching everything.

I got Tam upstairs through the kitchen. We went out the hayloft window into the yard, and we might have made it clean if the yard hadn't contained a dog. The dog did what dogs do. The gray cloak did what gray cloaks do.

We ran. Through the paddock, over a wall slick with frost, down into the drainage ditch that ran alongside the north road — and it was there, flat on our bellies in freezing runoff while boots crunched overhead, that Tam made his contribution to our survival, and it was not courage. It was memory.

"There's a culvert," he whispered. "Under the road, forty paces back. Old survey maps put it at six feet high, dry season flow only. It comes out in the alder stand on the far side."

"You memorized drainage surveys?"

"I memorized *everything*," he hissed, with the first real heat I'd heard from him. "That was the job. You think archives run on paper? Paper burns. Paper gets stolen, misfiled, eaten by damp. The real archive—" he tapped his own temple, "—is up here, and it doesn't burn, and right now it's the only version of half those documents left in the world, so yes, Elodie, I memorized the drainage surveys."

Boots passed close enough to hear buckles. We lay in the ditch, shoulder to shoulder, breathing through our mouths, and I understood that I had just heard a scribe describe himself as a fortress, and that he was right.

When the searchers moved on, we went back forty paces, found the culvert exactly where his head said it would be, and crawled out the far side into the alders, filthy, soaked, and — I noticed with some surprise — laughing. Quietly. The unhinged, oxygen-drunk laughter of two people who have recently expected to die and instead got wet.

"That," Tam gasped, "was the least dignified escape in the history of escape."

"I've done worse. I once got out of a Saltmarsh debtors' pit through a latrine shaft."

"I don't want to know."

"You absolutely don't."

We sat among the alders catching our breath, and something settled between us in the dark — not trust, not yet, but the scaffolding of it. Trust, I've found, is rarely built from grand gestures. It's built from culverts. From the accumulated evidence that another person's knowledge holds, that their fear is manageable, that when the boots come crunching they whisper useful things instead of screaming.

"All right," I said. "Guide me to Ashvale."

He was quiet a moment. "You should know what you're asking. The custodians — the gray cloaks, whatever they are — they'll expect you to come by the river road or the coast path. Those are watched. But there's a third way. Old pilgrim route, through the Ashvale foothills. It's slow, it's cold, and half the maps have it wrong." He smiled thinly. "Fortunately, I have the correct versions memorized."

"Of course you do."

"Elodie." He said my name carefully, like handling a document too fragile to touch. "The book says you break the crown. It says a lot of other things too. Things about keys, and about—" he hesitated, "—about price. Before we get there, I need to know you've actually read the whole thing. All of it. Including the parts you probably skipped."

I had skipped them. I told him so.

"Then we'll read it together on the road," he said. "Slowly. The way it deserves." He paused. "There's a line near the end I copied four times trying to decide if it was hope or a warning. I still don't know."

"What line?"

He wouldn't say. He pulled his blanket around his shoulders, settled against an alder trunk, and within minutes was asleep — genuinely asleep, deeply, the sleep of a man who has decided, on insufficient evidence and against all sense, that the armed mercenary watching over him will keep watch. 

I sat up the rest of the night with the prophecy book open on my knees, reading by moonlight, looking for the line he meant.

I found it near the end, in that careful century-dead hand, and read it three times, and felt the temperature of the world drop around me:

*She will give the crown what it hungered for all along, and it will take until nothing remains but the shape of her going.*

Somewhere north, past the foothills and the pilgrim road, a king I'd never met was losing himself one dawn at a time. And the price of saving him, written a hundred years before either of us drew breath, was everything I was.

I closed the book. I let Tam sleep. And I started composing, in the privacy of my own skull, the speech I would give him at sunrise — the one about turning back, about finding a safer life, about how a guide was entitled to know what cargo he was carrying.

I rehearsed it for two hours. I never delivered it. Because at dawn, when I woke him, he looked at me with those ink-stained, clear-sighted eyes and said, before I could speak:

"You read it, then. The ending."

"Yes."

"And you're still going."

It wasn't a question. That was the moment I trusted him, I think — not for his culvert, not for his memory-palace of a mind, but because he'd known, somehow, that I would go anyway, and hadn't wasted the night pretending otherwise.

"Ashvale," I said. "The pilgrim road. Show me the way."

He shouldered his pack, checked his ink — of course he checked his ink — and led me north, toward the foothills, toward the locked room where a king was counting days he couldn't remember, toward whatever the crown was and whatever it wanted from me.

Behind us, faint on the wind, I could have sworn I heard bells. Ashvale's coronation-anniversary rites, Tam said, weeks early this year.

Weeks early. As if the city itself were hurrying to meet me.

As if something in the vault already knew I was coming — and was setting a place at its table.
