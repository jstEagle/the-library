## 8. The Archive Burns

I want it on the record — my record, whatever survives of me — that I did not want to come to Ashvale.

Elodie asked. Elodie insisted, in that particular way she has of insisting without raising her voice, which is somehow worse than shouting because you can't accuse her of anything afterward. She just looks at you with those grey eyes and waits, and somewhere in the waiting you discover you've already agreed.

"The archive," she said, three days out of Thornfell, chewing on dried mutton that could have sole a boot. "You copied the prophecy there. You said so."

"I said I copied *a* prophecy there. There were hundreds. The archive is the largest collection of paper in the kingdom. It's a city of paper. It has streets, Elodie. Catalogues within catalogues. I spent four years there and I still got lost in the west annex."

"So you can find things in it."

"I can find things in it when it isn't trying to kill me, yes."

She smiled at that. It was a small smile, tired at the edges, but it did the thing her smiles do, which is make you feel like you've been let in on something. I've decided this is how she recruits people. Not oaths, not coin. Just that smile, and then you're carrying her pack up a mountain.

Anyway. That's how a runaway scribe with a price on his head and no weapon more dangerous than a stylus came to be crouched on a rooftop in the capital of Ashvale, watching the greatest library in the known world burn.

* * *

We'd reached the city at dusk, through the Weeping Gate, because Elodie refused the smugglers' tunnel on principle. "If we're going to do this," she said, "we do it walking in like people, not crawling in like rats." I pointed out that rats have considerably better survival rates. She was unmoved. She is frequently unmoved. It's her defining feature, after stubbornness and mercy and that thing where she touches altars like they might bite.

The capital in the evening was all gold and shadow, lamplighters working their way down the Processional, the palace on its hill wearing the sunset like a crown of its own. Somewhere up there was King Aldric, the man the prophecy wanted unbound from his own head. Somewhere up there, too, were the custodians — though back then I thought custodians were janitors. Oh, the innocence. I miss it.

"First light tomorrow," Elodie said, studying the archive district from the mouth of an alley. The Grand Archive sat in its walled compound like a fat grey hen, dome catching the last light. "Tonight we sleep. Tomorrow we—"

The bell started.

Not the hour bell. Not the alarm for fire in the tanneries or flood by the river, both of which I knew from my years in the city. This was the deep bell, the one in the Archive's own tower, rung — I had been told, once, by a master who thought it a funny story — only twice in the archive's history. Once for the earthquake. Once for the plague year.

It was not being rung for an earthquake now. You could smell why. Smoke, but wrong smoke — not woodsmoke, not lamp oil. Paper. Tons and tons of paper, burning sweet and acrid, the smell of every argument ever written down going up at once.

Elodie was moving before I finished understanding what I was smelling. I followed, because that's apparently my whole personality now.

* * *

Here is what you need to know about scribes, since one is narrating: we are not brave. Bravery requires something to defend and confidence in the defense. A scribe's entire profession is the admission that memory fails, that everything burns eventually, that the best we can do is copy things forward fast enough to outrun the loss. We are couriers of other people's certainties. We are not certain ourselves.

But I stood in that crowd on Scribe's Row, watching sparks climb off the Archive's roof into the dark like a reverse snowfall, and I felt something crack open in my chest that I don't have a better word for than rage.

Because it wasn't an accident. Accidents start small and grow. This fire had started everywhere at once. I could see it in the way the flames moved — too orderly, venting through the tower windows in columns, as if the building itself had been designed to burn clean. And I could see it in the guards. They weren't fighting the fire. They were holding a perimeter. Keeping us back. Keeping us *watching*.

"They're erasing it," I heard myself say.

Elodie looked at me. Around us, the crowd murmured — grief, mostly, and confusion. Old Master Corvin from the scriptorium was weeping openly. A woman clutched her son and turned him away from the light, as if the sight of burning knowledge could injure a child.

"Erasing what?" Elodie asked.

"Everything." My voice came out strange, flat, like someone else's. "The prophecies. The histories. The genealogies. Four thousand years of 'this happened' and 'then this happened,' and someone has decided it shouldn't have." I grabbed her arm. "The prophecy book. Your book. If there are other copies — if there's anything about the crown, about Veyra, about what the custodians actually *are* —"

"It's in there."

"It's in there."

She didn't say anything for a moment. Then she said, "Can you get in?"

And here's the thing about being asked a question like that by Elodie Hartwell: the honest answer is no. The honest answer is that I am a slight young man whose greatest physical achievement is carrying a ladder. The honest answer is that the perimeter guard would fold me in half and use me to beat out sparks.

"No," I said. "But I know how."

* * *

The Archive has a coal chute.

This is not a secret. It's just that nobody thinks about coal chutes, which is better than a secret. Every winter, carts delivered coal to the boiler house in the sub-levels, and the chute ran up from the delivery yard behind the east wall, and I knew it intimately because for two years it was my job to haul the coal sacks, having lost a bet to a senior archivist involving a mislabeled folio and a bottle of plum brandy that I maintain to this day was watered.

The delivery yard was empty except for two guards warming themselves at a brazier, their backs to the wall, faces lit orange by a fire they weren't fighting. Elodie watched them for exactly as long as it took to learn their rotation, which was not long, and then she did something with a coil of rope and a drainpipe that I will describe only as "efficient" and "deeply unfair to drainpipes," and the guards were asleep — actually asleep, she'd hit them precisely, mercifully, at the base of the skull, because even unconsciousness is a mercy when she administers it — and we were in the yard.

"You've done this before," I whispered.

"Once or twice."

"With guards?"

"With worse than guards."

I chose not to ask. This is another survival skill I've developed recently.

The chute was hot. Of course it was hot; the building above it was on fire. But heat rises, and the chute was angled, and the fire hadn't reached the sub-levels yet — I could see that from the yard, could see the lower windows still dark. Whoever set this had started at the top. Methodical. Top-down, like reading a page.

"We go up until it gets bad, then sideways," I said. "The catalogue rooms are stone. Fire eats the wooden galleries first, the shelving, the roof. The vaults under the west wing hold the restricted materials — the oldest stuff. If anything survives, it'll be there, at least long enough for us to—"

"To steal from a burning library," Elodie said. There was no judgment in it. There was almost admiration. "Your god of paper must be weeping."

"Scribes don't have gods. We have deadlines." I swallowed. "Come on."

* * *

Inside was hell with a floor plan.

Smoke layered the stairwells in grey rivers. Embers drifted past the shattered clerestory windows, riding the updraft, and everywhere — everywhere — pages burned. Loose pages, lifted by the convection, whirling through the dark like dying birds. I caught one out of the air without thinking, a reflex older than sense, and it was half a tax ledger from some dead duke's estate, and I held it while it charred between my fingers because letting go felt like a second murder.

Tam, I told myself. Focus. You are here for one book's worth of truth, not for the whole world's memory. Though God forgive me, I wanted the whole world's memory. I wanted to stand in the flame and catch it all, every last leaf of it, and carry it out under my coat like a madman.

Elodie moved through the smoke like she'd been born in it, hand on my shoulder, steering me by the sound of my coughing. "Which way?"

"West. Down one level. The restricted vaults."

We went. The heat grew teeth. Somewhere above us a shelf collapsed with a sound like a giant clearing its throat, and a blizzard of burning fragments sifted down through the levels, and I thought: four thousand years. Four thousand years of somebody's grandmother teaching somebody's daughter to write, of treaties and recipes and love letters and laws, and it's all becoming weather.

That's when the rage stopped being a feeling and became a plan.

* * *

The restricted vault door was bronze, and cool, and locked — but locks are arguments, and I had spent four years learning how they argue. The archivists' keyhole is a warded lock, elegant, meant to keep out thieves, not former coal-haulers who'd watched the under-clerk open it nine hundred times. Two minutes. Three. My hands shook — from fear, I told myself, though the smoke probably deserved some credit.

It opened onto cold air and darkness, and I nearly wept at the temperature change alone.

Lamps. There were emergency lamps, oil-sealed, struck against the stone. Light bloomed over shelves of lead-sealed cylinders — the restricted collection, the things the archivists kept from casual eyes. Dynastic records. Sealed testimonies. Things kings had ordered hidden and archivists had obeyed, because archivists obey, that's our tragedy, we file away our own chains.

"Search fast," Elodie said, already pulling cylinders, cracking seals, scanning spines. "The fire will reach the foundations."

"How do you know?"

"Because whoever set it is thorough." She tossed me a cylinder. "What are we looking for?"

"The crown. Ashvale. Anything older than the kingdom." I cracked the seal on mine and unrolled brittle vellum. Land grants. Useless. Another. A heresy trial, some poor woman in the reign of Aldric's great-grandfather who'd claimed to hear a silent god. My blood went cold. They'd burned her for saying what your prophecy says, Elodie, I thought. They've been burning this truth for a hundred years.

Then Elodie made a sound. Small. I've learned her sounds. That one was recognition.

She held a folio — loose leaves, not sealed, shoved carelessly into a cylinder as if someone had hidden it in haste, or shame. The vellum was old, older than old, the skin thin as onion and stained brown. The script was pre-dynastic. I knew it from paleography lectures I'd slept through and regretted now with my whole soul.

"Read it," she said. Her voice had gone quiet in the wrong way.

I read. My lips moved. The smoke above us groaned.

"...the Crown was not made. The Crown was *found*, in the ash-fields before the first king, before Ashvale was Ashvale, when the valley belonged to no one and everyone..." I stopped. Started again. "...it wore the god before it wore the king. It drank the god dry. What sits on the throne sits inside a thing older than the throne, and the throne exists to feed it..."

I looked up. Elodie had gone very still, the way she does, the stillness of a drawn bow.

"It predates the kingdom," she said.

"It predates the *idea* of the kingdom." My voice cracked. "Elodie — the custodians aren't protecting the crown for the monarchy. The monarchy is a *farm*. Four thousand years of kings, grown and crowned and harvested, and someone built an entire country around keeping one relic fed—"

The ceiling shuddered. Dust rained. From far above, through stone, came voices — shouts, orders, boots. Not firefighters. Firefighters shout about water.

"They're coming down to check the vaults," I breathed. "To make sure nothing survives."

"Then nothing will." Elodie took the folio from my hands, wrapped it in the oilcloth from her pack with quick, precise movements, and pushed it inside her shirt, against her heart, which is where she keeps everything that matters. "Except what we carry."

"There could be more—"

"There's no time."

There wasn't. The heat had found the stairwell; I could hear the fire arriving like a tide. And beneath the roar, closer now, boots on stone, and a voice calling orders in that flat administrative tone that frightened me more than any scream: *"Check the seals. Nothing leaves. Nothing leaves."*

We ran.

* * *

I will pass quickly over the exit, partly because it was chaos and partly because my dignity was among the casualties. There was a service corridor, a grate Elodie bent like it owed her money, a drop into the coal yard that I did not land gracefully, and a sprint through backstreets while the sky behind us turned the color of an open wound. We came out by the tanners' canal, filthy and gasping, and stood watching the glow over the rooftops while the city's fire-bells finally, belatedly, began to ring.

And that's when it happened. The thing I should have seen coming, because the prophecy warned us and because my luck has always been the kind that arrives dressed as catastrophe.

A patrol came around the corner ahead of us — six guards, lanterns high, sweeping the district for looters. Routine. We pressed into a doorway, heads down, and would have passed fine, except one of them stopped.

Stopped, and turned, and raised his lantern right at Elodie's face.

"Hartwell," he said.

I didn't know the name meant anything yet. But I saw her face change — saw the flicker of calculation, the instant assessment, the way her weight shifted to her back foot. And I saw the guard's face change too, saw recognition curdle into something like fear, and he shouted it again, louder, to his fellows and to the night:

"It's her! The mercenary from Thornfell — Hartwell! She's here!"

They recognized her. In the capital, fifty leagues from anywhere she'd worked, they knew her face. Which meant descriptions had gone out. Which meant the custodians' reach went further than fences and assassins — it went into the city watch, into the patrols, into the ordinary machinery of law, and every gate and bridge and checkpoint between us and freedom now had her name attached to it.

"Run," Elodie said, and shoved me, and we ran.

Behind us: boots, whistles, the lantern-light leaping along the walls. Ahead: the canal, black and stinking, and beyond it the maze of the low quarter where even the watch gets lost. Elodie ran like water finding a downhill, pulling me by the wrist, and I ran like a man being chased by everything he'd ever failed to write down.

At the canal's edge she paused one heartbeat — one — looking back at the burning glow above the rooftops, at the archive dying, at the sky full of somebody else's memory turning to ash.

"They didn't just destroy evidence," she said. Low. Almost to herself. "They destroyed the *witnesses*. Every record of every person who ever tried to say this out loud." Her jaw tightened. "They've been doing this for centuries. Burning the same truth over and over, generation after generation, because it keeps growing back."

"And now?" I panted.

"Now we're the only copy left." She looked at me, and her eyes reflected the fire, and I understood that I had signed on for something much larger and much worse than a smuggling contract. "You and me and a scorched folio, Tam. We're the archive now."

Somewhere behind us, a whistle shrilled — closer, splitting off, cutting toward the canal path. They were boxing us in, patient and professional, and Elodie pulled me down into the dark of the tanners' walk as the lanterns swept past overhead.

"We need to reach the palace," she whispered into the dark. "Before they finish burning every road that leads there."

I closed my eyes. I, Tam of the Archive, keeper of nothing, courier of one burnt page, began composing — in inkless memory, the only library left — the opening line of the truest account anyone would ever write:

*The kingdom was founded on a lie, and the lie was hungry.*

Then the whistles sounded again, on both sides now, and we ran.
