## 9. What Wears the Crown

I have been king of Ashvale for eleven years, and I have discovered that the office consists mostly of being lied to by people who love you.

This morning's lie arrived on a silver tray with my breakfast: a report from the western provinces, written in a clerk's careful hand, describing harvests I apparently inspected last month. I remembered no such inspection. I remembered no clerk. I remembered, if I was honest, very little of last month at all — a smear of banquets and signatures and my sister's face saying *you were there, Aldric, you approved it*, and me nodding because what else does a king do when his own past is vouched for by everyone but himself?

I had taken to keeping a list. Not in writing — writing could be found, read, corrected by helpful hands. In my head, which I was beginning to suspect was not the secure vault I'd once assumed.

*The inspection I don't remember. The ambassador from Vell who wept at my farewell and whose name I cannot summon. The dog. There was supposed to be a dog.*

That one bothered me most. A servant had mentioned it weeks ago — *your majesty remembers old Bramble, surely* — with that particular softness people use when they think they're comforting you. I had said *of course*, and the servant had relaxed, and I had spent the rest of the evening trying to remember a dog and finding only a shape where a dog should be, like a hole cut in paper.

Eleven years of rule, and my kingdom was held together by other people's assurances.

Maren was waiting in my study when I came down. She did that more and more lately — arrived before me, arranged herself by the window where the light was kind, and smiled the smile she'd been perfecting since we were children: the one that meant *everything is fine and I will fight anyone who says otherwise.*

"Brother." She poured the wine herself, which she only did when she wanted my guard out of the room. "You look tired."

"I look tired," I agreed, "because I am tired. I am also confused, Maren, and I have decided this morning that I'm done pretending otherwise."

Her hands paused over the decanter. Just a moment. Just long enough.

"Confused how?" she asked, too lightly.

"Let's see." I sat across from her and counted on my fingers, because there is a certain comedy in it and I needed the comedy. "There's a harvest festival I supposedly opened. There's a treaty I supposedly signed. There's a dog named Bramble who everyone in this palace remembers better than I do. And last night I found scratches inside my bedchamber door."

The light through the window was doing its kind work on her face, but kindness can't hold everything. Something moved behind her eyes — something old and exhausted.

"Scratches," she repeated.

"Tally marks. Fresh ones. Counting days." I leaned forward. "Maren. Whose hand is that?"

She set down the decanter with exaggerated care, the way one sets down a thing that might otherwise be thrown. "Yours," she said finally. "It's your hand, Aldric."

"And why would I be counting days on my own door like a prisoner?"

"You sleepwalk," she said. "You always have. Since we were children. You used to scratch at things in the night — Mother had the furniture padded—"

"I don't remember that either."

"No," she said quietly. "You wouldn't."

And there it was. The sentence underneath every sentence she'd spoken to me for months, maybe years, laid bare between us like a bone on a table. *You wouldn't remember.* She hadn't even tried to dress it up.

I stood. My chair scraped back and in the silence after it, I heard how quiet the palace was — how carefully, deliberately quiet, the way a house is quiet when the servants have all been told to listen.

"Tell me the truth," I said. "All of it. Or so help me, Maren, I will walk into the great hall and ask the whole court why their king cannot remember his own dog, and we will see what falls out of this family's cupboards then."

"That would destroy you," she said. "They'd call you mad. They'd call for a regency — they've *wanted* a regency for years—"

"Then tell me something worse than madness. Because whatever it is you're protecting me from, sister, I promise you the not-knowing is already killing me. I wake up every morning as a stranger wearing my life, and everyone around me smiles and hands me the day like it's mine." My voice cracked on the last word, which was undignified, and I found I didn't care. "Please. Maren. Please."

She looked at me for a long time. I watched her do arithmetic behind her eyes — I had seen her do it since we were children, weighing outcomes, counting costs — and for once, mercifully for once, the sum came out wrong for her plans and right for me.

"The crown," she said.

"What about it?"

"It isn't a symbol, Aldric. It never was." Her voice had gone flat, the voice she used for funeral arrangements. "It's alive. Or something like alive. It eats memories. It has eaten yours — every night, for years. And every dawn, the custodians come and put the day back in you like a servant restocking a larder. Whatever you're missing, it's missing because something wore it away."

I sat back down. Not gracefully. My knees made the decision for me.

"How long," I managed.

"Since the coronation."

"Eleven years."

"Ten and a half," she said, as though precision were mercy. "It started slowly. You'd lose an afternoon. Then a week. The custodians said it was normal, that the crown *settles*. By the third year you were losing months, and they were patching you each dawn, and I—" She stopped. Pressed her knuckles to her mouth. When she continued, her voice was smaller than I had ever heard it. "I let them. Gods forgive me, I let them, because the alternative was watching Ashvale tear itself apart over a broken king, and because they promised — they *promised* — that the important things would stay. Your name. Your family. Me."

I thought about the dog. About the ambassador. About the growing country of forgotten things inside my own skull, and how someone had stood at its border all these years, checking passports, deciding what I got to keep.

"The important things," I repeated.

"You still know me," she whispered. "Every morning, you still know me. That's not nothing, Aldric. That's the only thing I bargained for, and I have paid for it every single day since."

We looked at each other across the study, two orphans of the same slow crime, and I felt the strangest thing: not rage, or not only rage. Grief. Grief for her, carrying this alone; grief for the boy I had been, scratching tallies into a door in the dark because some part of him knew, knew and could do nothing but count.

"Take me to it," I said.

"Aldric—"

"*Take me to it.* If a thing has been eating me for ten years while I slept, I want to look it in the face. That is not a request, Maren. That is the order of the King of Ashvale, and you will obey it, or you will explain to the custodians why you didn't."

She closed her eyes. "You'll wish you hadn't asked."

"I already wish I hadn't asked everything else," I said. "At least this wish will be informed."

* * *

The vault beneath the palace was colder than any place I had ever been, and I say that as a man who once wintered at the northern garrisons. This was not weather-cold. It was cold with intention, cold like a held breath, and the torches Maren carried burned low and blue-ish, as though the flame itself were reluctant.

The stairs went down farther than the palace had foundations. I counted the steps — forty, sixty, ninety — and noticed I was doing it with my hand trailing the wall, and understood with a lurch that I was leaving tally marks of my own now. Old habits live deeper than memory, apparently. Some part of me had been counting against the dark for years.

"There are wards," Maren said, her voice bouncing strangely off stone. "Custodian work. They won't stop you — you're the vessel — but they'll... announce you. Be ready for that."

"Announce me to whom?"

"To it."

The stair ended in a chamber that was round when every other room in the palace was square, as though the builders had wanted even the architecture to feel wrong. In the center stood a pedestal of black glass, and on the pedestal, alone, unguarded, almost modest —

the Ashen Crown.

I had worn it at every ceremony of my reign. I had felt its weight on my brow a hundred times, that faint pressure courtiers called *dignity* and poets called *the burden of rule*. Looking at it now, sitting cold on its pedestal with no head under it, I felt a fool of historic proportions.

It was beautiful. That was the trap of it, I realized — it was *beautiful*, ash-grey metal worked into branches and thorns, delicate as frost, and it seemed to drink the torchlight rather than reflect it. It did not gleam. It *took*.

"Well," I said, because my mouth has always run ahead of my sense. "It's smaller than I imagined."

"Don't touch it," Maren said sharply.

"I wasn't going to—"

The whisper began.

I want to describe it properly, because for the rest of my life — however much of it remains mine — I will be trying to describe it to myself. It was not sound. Sound arrives at the ears. This arrived already inside, the way a memory arrives, the way grief arrives, intimate and uninvited. And it was warm. That was the horror of it. After all that cold, the whisper was warm as a hearth, warm as a blanket pulled over a sleeping child, and it spoke in—

in my mother's voice.

*Aldric. Do you remember the garden?*

I did. Gods help me, I did — suddenly, completely, a summer afternoon decades gone: the palace garden before the blight, before the ash killed everything green, my mother kneeling in the dirt with her sleeves rolled up, showing me how to press a seed into the earth. *Not too deep, little king. Things drown if you bury them too deep.* I could smell the turned soil. I could feel the sun. I had not thought of that afternoon in twenty years, and here it was, whole and glowing, handed to me like a gift.

"Yes," I breathed.

*And the dog,* the warmth murmured, sly now, tender now. *Bramble. He slept at the foot of your bed for nine years. When the thunder came, he pressed against your back, and you pretended to be brave but you were glad of him. Shall I give him back? I kept him safe. I keep everything safe. Everything you've lost is here, Aldric. Nothing is truly gone. Come and take it.*

My hand was moving. I want that recorded honestly, wherever this account ends up: my hand was rising toward the crown of its own accord, drawn like a moth, like a drowning man toward air, and every step of its journey felt like *homecoming*.

"Aldric!" Maren caught my wrist. Her grip was iron; she'd always had the stronger grip, even as children, even when I sulked about it. "Look at it. Look at what it's doing and *look at it*."

I looked. And beneath the beauty, now that I knew to see, I saw the rest: the way the thorn-branches flexed, very slightly, like fingers opening. The hairline veins of grey light pulsing beneath the surface in a rhythm — I pressed my free hand to my own chest, sick with recognition — in *my* rhythm. My heartbeat. It beat with my stolen pulse, fed on my stolen years, and it was calling me home the way a spider calls a fly: gently, gently, with silk spun from the fly's own childhood.

"It's bait," I said hoarsely. "It's fishing with my own life."

"Every dawn," Maren said. "Every dawn it offers you back everything, and every dawn the custodians pull you away before you reach for it. They told me it was mercy — that remembering would break you. But I've watched your face just now, brother, and I don't think it wants to break you." Her voice shook. "I think it wants you to *choose* it. I think it's lonely down here, and hungry, and patient, and it would rather be invited than imposed."

The whisper swelled, wounded now, loving now, and my mother's voice cracked with something that sounded almost human:

*You would leave your mother in the dark? You would leave little Bramble? I am all that's left of them, Aldric. I am the garden. I am the summer. Without me, you are a man who owns nothing, and with me—*

"With me," I said aloud, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect, "I am a larder. A well-stocked cupboard for a thing that wears thorns."

I stepped back. My hand ached where Maren held it, and I was grateful for the pain; pain is honest, pain belongs to the present, and I clung to it like a rope thrown to a man going under.

"But hear me," I said to the crown, to the dark, to whatever ancient hunger coiled in that grey metal. "Hear me, thief. You took ten years of my life and you paraded my dead mother's voice to keep me sweet about it. So know this: I am going to end you. Not tomorrow, not when it's convenient. I will find whatever key unlocks your cage, and I will throw it open, and I will drag you into the light where every soul you've robbed can see what you are."

The whisper stopped.

The silence that followed was worse than the whisper — vast, offended, *attentive*. On its pedestal, the crown did not move, and yet I felt it move, the way you feel a stranger's gaze settle on the back of your neck. It had stopped seducing me.

It had started studying me.

Maren pulled me toward the stairs. I went, but at the bottom step I turned and looked back once, and in the blue torchlight the crown sat gleaming on its black glass, patient as a hook, and I swear on my mother's buried garden that the thorn-branches had shifted — just slightly — to point, all of them together, toward the door.

Toward the world above. Toward the city. Toward whatever was coming for it.

"Maren," I said as we climbed, my heart hammering out a rhythm the thing below knew by heart. "Those custodians. How often do they come?"

"Each dawn. Why?"

"Because tonight," I said, "we're going to find out what happens when they don't."
