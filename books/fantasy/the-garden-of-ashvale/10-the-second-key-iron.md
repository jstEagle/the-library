## 10. The Second Key: Iron

The prison-fortress of Ketterhale was built the way grief builds a wall around a heart: too high, too thick, and with no thought given to ever getting back out.

Tam and I lay on a ridge above it for most of an afternoon, watching carts go in empty and come out emptier. The fortress sat in a bowl of grey hills like a fist closed around something it didn't want to look at. Iron-banded gates. No banners. No smoke from the kitchens, which told me either the garrison ate cold or there wasn't much of a garrison — just keepers, the kind the custodians hired because they could watch a person starve slowly without once wondering why.

"That's where they put the unbound," Tam said. He'd said it three times already, each time quieter, as if repetition might make it less true. "Prophets who won't bind themselves to the crown's service. Seers. Anyone who hears too much."

"And the second key's inside."

"According to the seer's letters." He had them wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, the way other people carried infants or knives. "The letters say *iron remembers what flesh forgives*. Which is either a location or a very grim proverb."

"It's both," I said. "Everything about this crown is both."

Here is what I knew by then: three keys, three substances, one relic that ate memories for breakfast. Salt I'd taken from a drowned chapel at the price of learning my own face belonged to a dead god. Bone was still a mystery even to the prophecy, though the seer's letters hinted at it in a way I didn't like. And iron was here, in Ketterhale, behind walls designed by people who understood that hope is just escape with better posture.

"We could go around," Tam offered, without conviction. We both knew the key wasn't negotiable. We also both knew what going around meant: another week of running, another ambush, another set of scars, and the key exactly where we'd left it.

I studied the carts. Empty in, empty out. Except — I squinted — not entirely. Twice that afternoon a cart had gone in carrying crates and come out with one crate fewer, and the drivers hadn't stopped at the gate on the way back. Deliveries that never left. Supplies for prisoners nobody fed publicly, because officially, Ketterhale held no one at all. A prison that doesn't exist can't be inspected. That's the whole genius of it, and the whole rot.

"I'm going in," I said.

Tam looked at me the way you look at someone who has just suggested swimming across a frozen river because the bridge has a toll. "As a prisoner."

"As a prisoner. They don't let visitors past the second gate, but they take captives all the way down. It's the only door that opens inward."

"You'd be surrendering your weapons. Your freedom. Possibly your memory, if the stories are true and they have ways of asking questions."

"They do," I said. "The salt guardian showed me some of mine. I know what their questions feel like." I didn't tell him the rest — that some of what the wight had shown me weren't my memories at all, that a woman with my face had stood in a burning temple a century ago and made choices I was apparently fated to repeat. He'd figured out enough of it on his own. Tam was a scribe; he read between lines for a living.

"They'll search you," he said. "If they find the salt key—"

"They won't find it, because you'll have it." I turned to face him. "That's the plan, such as it is. You stay outside. You keep the first key, the letters, and the book. If I don't come out in four days, you take everything to Ashvale and you find the third key without me."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I'll knock myself out and let them drag me in, and you'll be standing on a ridge holding an unconscious woman and a bag of evidence when the patrol comes. Your choice."

He muttered something in archive-slang that I suspect translates roughly to *mercenaries*, and then he did the thing that decided, more than anything else that season, that I trusted him: he took off his boot, pried open the heel, and showed me the hollow where he kept a lockpick, a stub of charcoal, and a folded page he wouldn't let me see.

"Scribes survive prisons," he said. "It's practically our native habitat. Get yourself caught convincingly. Don't fight unless fighting is cheaper than not fighting. And Elodie—" he hesitated, then didn't finish whatever it was, which was its own kind of kindness.

* * *

Getting captured convincingly is harder than it sounds, mostly because professional soldiers are suspicious of anyone who surrenders well. I let them catch me at the treeline near the cart road, alone, armed with a knife I'd bought specifically to lose, and I made sure to struggle just enough to bruise my pride but not my skull. The keepers who took me were grey men in grey coats with the particular blankness of people paid not to wonder. One of them went through my pack with the thoroughness of a man looking for reasons rather than things.

"No papers," he said.

"Lost them."

"No name?"

"Elodie." No point lying about that; the custodians already knew it. That was rather the point.

Something flickered behind his eyes — recognition, or the memory of orders. "Hartwell?" he asked, testing it.

"Just Elodie."

They bound my wrists anyway, which stung, and hooded me, which stung worse, because the last time I'd been hooded it had been by choice, in a smuggler's tunnel, and the world had felt full of possibility instead of iron. Then came the long descent: wheels on stone, gates opening and closing in sequence, air going from cold to damp to a kind of mineral stillness, as if the mountain itself were holding its breath.

When they took the hood off, I was in a cell. Of course I was. But it wasn't the cell I expected.

It was large. There were eleven other women in it, sitting or lying on pallets along the walls, and every one of them turned to look at me with the unhurried attention of people who had nothing left to hurry for. The ceiling was high enough that voices would carry. There was a grate in the floor through which warm air rose — the mines below, maybe, or the furnaces. And in the far corner, cross-legged on the stone with her hands open on her knees like a woman waiting for tea, sat a blind seer.

I knew she was blind before I saw her eyes, which were the pale, unfocused grey of river ice. She was perhaps sixty, perhaps ninety; her face had the settled quality of someone who had stopped negotiating with age years ago.

"The last page," she said.

The room went quiet in the way rooms do when everyone is pretending not to listen.

"I think you've got the wrong prisoner," I said.

"I have never once gotten the wrong prisoner." Her head tilted toward me, and I had the uncomfortable sense of being read — not my face, not my posture, but something underneath, the way the salt wight had read me. "You smell like a library that burned. You smell like a god's unfinished sentence. Sit down, girl. The floor is honest, at least."

I sat. Around us, the other women resumed their lives, which consisted mostly of enduring. An older woman with cropped hair was braiding straw into rings. Two younger ones played a game with pebbles and a scratched grid, moving pieces in silence with the fluency of ten thousand repetitions. Nobody wept. Nobody raged. That frightened me more than screaming would have. Screaming means you still believe someone might hear.

"What is this place?" I asked quietly.

"A shelf," the seer said. "We are books they haven't burned yet. Unbound prophets, they call us. We heard the future and refused to hand it over neatly, so they shelved us until we mold." She smiled, and it was a terrible, wonderful smile. "My name was Sera Vane, when names mattered. I prophesied the drought of the borderlands, thirty years early, free of charge. For that crime I have been in this room since before you were weaned."

"How do you know what I am?"

"I don't need eyes for the things that matter. I felt you come down the mountain like a struck bell." She reached out and, without asking, took my bound wrists in her hands, turning them over as if checking a horse's legs. Her fingers were cool and precise. "Salt key. Yes. I can taste it on you — old brine, drowned bells. You gave something for it."

"Some of my memories. Or some of hers."

"Mm. And how much of you is left to give, I wonder? That's the question the crown is asking too, in its way." She released my hands. "You're here for the iron."

"You know where it is."

"Child, I *am* where it is. Or near enough. Iron remembers what flesh forgives — you've heard the line. Do you know what it means? It means the key isn't an object you steal. It's a thing this place is made of. Every bar, every chain, every shackle in Ketterhale was poured from one forging, and the forging holds the key the way a body holds a name. You cannot pick it up. You have to wake it."

"Wake it how?"

"With consent," she said, "which is the one currency they never tax, because they assume no one down here has any left." She laughed, low and dry. "They're usually right. But you — you walked in through the front gate. On purpose. That's either consent or lunacy, and in my experience the two are indistinguishable at this altitude."

* * *

Four days. That was the window I'd given Tam, and I spent the first two of them doing the least heroic work of my life: listening.

Prisoners know everything. It's the one advantage of having nothing — every scrap of information becomes treasure, hoarded and traded. By the second evening I knew the guard rotations (thin, bored, changed at odd hours precisely so no pattern could be learned — except that boredom always makes patterns), the layout (three levels down, the lowest flooded and abandoned, which everyone insisted was haunted and which I immediately marked as an exit), and the schedule of the Questioning.

The Questioning happened twice a week. They took one prisoner up to a room with no windows and brought her back the next morning, walking, breathing, and missing something. Not speech — they all spoke fine. Missing *certainty*. The woman who'd been braiding straw, Maret, came back from her second Questioning unable to remember why she'd started the braid, or what she'd intended the rings for, and she sat turning one in her fingers for hours with an expression I recognized from mirrors: the look of a person checking their own pockets and finding them lighter than expected.

"They're feeding it," Sera Vane told me that night, in the dark, in a voice pitched under the sleep-breath of the others. "The crown eats memory. Aldric's run thin — a king's life is only so long, even eaten slowly. So the custodians harvest. We are the larder."

"And the ones they question — does it kill them, eventually?"

"Eventually it empties them. Then they walk them out the cart road and a different prison gets a new inmate who doesn't know her own name. The system is very tidy. Tidiness is the custodians' one true faith." Her hand found my arm in the dark, grip surprisingly strong. "Which is why you must not be tidy, last page. When you move, move like fire. Fire is the one thing tidiness cannot file."

I asked her about the visions. About the woman with my face. She was quiet a long moment, and when she spoke again her voice had changed — softer, and older somehow, as if she'd stepped briefly into a room she'd been avoiding.

"I knew her," she said. "Before. Before the silence. I was young and my sight was raw and I went to the temple at Veyra's Ford to be tested, and the priestess who tested me wore your face. She told me my gift was real and my duty was heavy and that someday a girl would come to me smelling of burnt libraries, and that I should give her everything I had, because she would be carrying everything anyone had." Her grip tightened. "A hundred years I kept that appointment. Don't make me miss it now."

* * *

On the third day, the keepers came for me.

I'd half expected it — Hartwell was a name the custodians wanted, and I'd handed myself over gift-wrapped. Two guards, the grey coats, the blank faces. They walked me up through the levels to a room with no windows, and there was a chair, and there was a man in a mask.

Not a dramatic mask. That's what I want recorded somewhere, if anywhere keeps records anymore: the custodian's mask was plain, pale, featureless, like a face that had been sanded down for safety. He sat across from me with a ledger and asked questions in a mild voice, and between the questions he touched small iron instruments laid out on a cloth, and each touch pulled.

I will not catalogue what they took. Some of it was mine and some of it was hers and all of it hurt in the specific way of losing something you only identify after it's gone — a name I couldn't recall wanting, a face I couldn't place grieving. But I'd prepared. The salt wight had taught me the shape of the pulling, and I'd spent two days building a false inventory in my head, a decoy self, small memories arranged like shops in a burning street. Let him loot the storefronts. The vault was elsewhere.

Near the end, the masked man paused. Tilted his head. The featureless face managed, somehow, to convey suspicion.

"You are hiding something," he said mildly.

"I'm hiding everything," I said. "You're just not good enough to find it."

He considered that. Then, instead of anger, he did something worse: he wrote a note in his ledger, stood, and said, "Keep her comfortable. She may yet be useful to the Court intact." And I understood, with a cold clarity that no amount of mercenary experience quite prepares you for, that I was no longer a prisoner to them. I was inventory. Reserved stock. Somewhere in Ashvale, a starving crown had a claim on me, and these grey men were merely its pantry.

They returned me to the cell at dawn. Sera Vane met me before I'd crossed the threshold, her hands finding my shoulders, reading me like a page.

"They took from you," she said.

"Scraps. I fed them scraps."

"And they marked you as theirs." She clicked her tongue. "Good. Hated things get watched. Watched things get schedules. And scheduled things—" her mouth curved, "—can be predicted. Tomorrow night the furnace-tenders change shift at the moon's setting, and the outer gate stands open for the coal cart for the length of two hundred heartbeats. You will want to be fire, remember? Fire moves at moonset."

"And the key?"

"Tonight," she said. "Tonight we wake the iron. All of us. Every soul on this shelf." She raised her voice, not loudly, but with a resonance that filled the high ceiling like water filling a vessel, and around the cell, one by one, the women looked up from their straw and their pebbles and their long endurance. "Sisters. The last page has come. What we swore at our taking — do you remember it? Do you remember what we promised we'd do if ever she arrived?"

Maret, the braider, looked down at the ring in her fingers. And then, slowly, she smiled — the first expression I'd seen on her that wasn't endurance — and said, "We promised we'd stop being patient."

* * *

What they'd sworn, it turned out, was simple: that if the one from the prophecy ever came within these walls, they would give her the prison itself. Their testimony, their knowledge, their bodies if it came to that. A hundred years of unbound prophets, shelved and harvested, and every single one of them had passed the promise down like a flame cupped between generations — not hoping, exactly. Hoping hurts too much in a place like this. Just *keeping*. The way you keep a seed you don't expect to plant.

At moonset, we moved.

I wish I could tell you it was clean. It wasn't. Fire rarely is. Maret had spent thirty years loosening the hinge-pins of the cell door with a straw-braiding needle, an act of patience so vast it deserved its own chapter in somebody's holy book. The door opened without a sound, which felt almost obscene after a century of captivity. Sera Vane led — of course she led, blind, barefoot, walking the corridors of her prison by some sense deeper than sight, counting turns in a whisper. Twelve women moved through the sleeping fortress like a rumor.

The forge level was above us, not below — the furnace that heated the cells also served the smithy where Ketterhale made its own chains, because a prison that outsources its shackles is a prison with a weakness. The iron of Ketterhale had been forged here, quenched here, worn here against the skin of the condemned for longer than the kingdom had existed. Iron remembers what flesh forgives.

Sera Vane stood before the quenching trough, where the first bars had cooled a century and more ago, and she spoke to it. Not a spell — I know spells, I've bought enough of them — but something older and stranger. Testimony.

"Iron," she said, "we were honest with you. Every hour you held us, we did not lie to ourselves, and we did not lie to you. Remember us."

And the twelve of them, ragged and half-starved and magnificent, each pressed a palm to the wet stone of the trough and spoke a piece of themselves into it — a name, a prophecy, a loss, a love. Small coins, all of them, paid out of empty purses. The air over the water shimmered. The temperature dropped. And up from the trough, out of the standing water, rose a shape like a bar of night — dense, dark, humming faintly, the way a struck bell hums after the sound should have ended.

The second key. It weighed nothing in my hand and everything in my arm, the way truth weighs.

The alarm went up before we cleared the yard. Torches bloomed along the walls; the grey coats spilled from barracks, calling numbers at each other in confusion, because no one had ever rehearsed an uprising here — why would you, in a prison that officially held no one?

At the coal-cart gate, two hundred heartbeats from closing, we came out of the dark in a knot of women, and I did the arithmetic I always hate: fight or scatter. The keepers had crossbows. My sisters-in-captivity had needles and fury and one stolen key.

So I gave them the only order I had. "Scatter. Every direction. You were never a unit — you're twelve separate miracles, and miracles don't get recaptured in groups." I pushed the salt key's hiding-place knowledge into Sera Vane's hands — the ridge, the tree split by lightning, the boy with the boot-hollows waiting with a bag. "Tell him Elodie says the plan worked, and that he's insufferable, and that I'll meet him at the Weeping Gate in six days."

"And you?" Sera Vane's blind eyes found me with unnerving accuracy. "Where does the last page go?"

I looked back at Ketterhale, at the torches, at the masked man I knew was already writing in his ledger — *asset escaped; inform the Court* — and I felt the two keys against my skin, salt and iron, and thought of a third substance the letters had only whispered about.

"Bone," I said. "The third key is bone. And there's only one place in this country with bones that matter." I started down the dark road toward Ashvale, toward the capital, toward whatever waited for me there — and behind me, faint on the wind, I could swear I heard the prison-fortress groan, deep in its iron bones, like a bell that had finally been allowed to finish ringing.

Six days. One city full of enemies. And somewhere inside the palace, a king who didn't know he was waiting for me.
