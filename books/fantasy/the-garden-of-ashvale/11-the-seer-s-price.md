## 11. The Seer's Price

The blind seer's cell smelled of lye and old stone, and she was waiting for us before we finished climbing the last stair.

"You took your time," she said. Her voice was dry as a page left too long in the sun. She sat on a pallet against the far wall, hands folded in her lap like a woman at prayer, though her face was turned toward the door — toward us — with an accuracy that made my skin crawl. No milky film over her eyes, no bandage. Just eyes that worked fine and looked at nothing, or perhaps at everything, in a way that suggested both were equally beside the point.

"You knew we were coming," Tam said behind me. He was clutching his satchel of rescued folios like a shield, which, given what we'd crawled out of at the archive, wasn't entirely unreasonable.

"I've known since before you were born, boy." She patted the pallet beside her. "Sit. Both of you. The floor in here is older than the fortress and it holds grudges."

I didn't sit. Old habit — never give a stranger the high ground, even when the high ground is a lice-ridden mattress. "You called me something," I said. "In the yard. When they brought me in. You said *the last page*."

"I did."

"What does that mean?"

The seer smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of someone who has read to the end of a very sad book and is now watching you start chapter one. "It means you're the last page," she said. "Of a very long book. One that was begun by a god and is being finished by a mercenary with mud on her boots. The gods have always had a taste for irony. Veyra especially."

My hand went to the salt key at my throat before I could stop it. "Don't say that name."

"Why not? It's yours."

"It isn't mine. It's the silent god's name, and the silent god doesn't answer, and I don't make a habit of invoking dead things I work for."

"Dead." She tasted the word like a cook testing spoiled broth. "Is that what they teach you in the shrines now? That Veyra *died*?" She shook her head slowly. "No, child. Dead things are quiet because they are finished. Veyra is quiet because she is *elsewhere*. There is a difference, and the difference has teeth."

Tam had sat down at some point. I noticed he'd gone pale. "The crown," he said quietly. "The Ashen Crown. In the archive — the folio I saved — it predates the kingdom. It predates—"

"Everything," the seer agreed. "It predates the kingdom, the dynasty, the walls around you. It predates the silence. Do you know why the god went silent, Elodie Hartwell?"

"Nobody knows why the god went silent. That's rather the definition of it."

"Nobody knows because nobody remembers. And nobody remembers because there is nothing left to remember *with*." She leaned forward, and even blind, even ancient, she managed to loom. "A century ago, Veyra did not abandon her faithful. A century ago, Veyra went to war. Against the thing in the crown. And the thing in the crown won. It ate her, child. Devoured her whole — every memory, every prayer she ever answered, every name she ever loved. It swallowed a god and it was still hungry. That is why your altar gives you nothing back. Not silence. *Indigestion.*"

The cell was very cold all of a sudden, or maybe that was just me.

"That's a lie," I said, and my voice came out flat and hard, which is how my voice goes when I'm trying to hold a roof up during a collapse. "That's heresy. That's—"

"The truth usually is, the first time you hear it." She tilted her head. "You want proof? Fine. Proof. What did you see in the drowned chapel, under the salt mines, when the wight tested you?"

I opened my mouth to say *nothing*, which was a lie, and then closed it again, because apparently I'd decided to be honest today, which was unlike me and getting worse by the hour.

"Visions," I said. "Of my past."

"All of them yours?"

"...Most of them."

"And the others?" She spread her hands. "The ones that weren't yours. The woman standing in a garden that no longer exists, planting something in ash. The voice singing a hymn in a language that died before your grandmother's grandmother was born. The feeling of being *enormous*, Elodie — of being prayed to by ten thousand throats at once. Was that your past?"

"How do you—"

"Because I saw them too," she said simply. "Forty years ago, when I still had eyes. I saw a girl in a smuggler's camp holding a prophecy book she hadn't read yet, and I saw Veyra's face looking out from behind hers like a lamp behind a curtain. And I have spent forty years in this cell waiting for the curtain to tear." She reached out and caught my wrist — quick, strong, unerring. Her fingers found my pulse as if checking it against some inner ledger. "You carry her, child. Scattered through you like shrapnel. Every fragment of the god the crown couldn't digest, it vomited back into the world, and the world put her where she'd be safest: inside a mercenary who prays to her and gets nothing. The joke writes itself."

I pulled my arm free. Or maybe I didn't; I honestly couldn't say afterward whether I moved or whether she let go. My head was full of the chapel visions — the garden, the hymn, the vastness — and they rearranged themselves as I stood there, the way a pile of kindling rearranges itself when someone finally says the word *fire*.

"If that's true," I said slowly, "then when I pray—"

"She hears every word. She just can't answer. You can't speak when you're scattered across someone else's soul, any more than a shattered mirror can show you your face." The seer settled back against the wall. "She's been listening to you your whole life, Elodie Hartwell. Every prayer. Every curse. Every time you talked to her in the dark because talking to her was easier than being alone. She heard all of it, and she couldn't so much as whisper back. If you want to know what faith costs, imagine loving someone across a distance you cannot cross, for a hundred years, unable to say so much as *I'm here*."

I thought about all those nights at dead altars. All those one-sided conversations. All that silence I'd taken personally, because what else do you do with silence but take it personally?

I sat down on the pallet after all. My legs had made the decision without consulting me.

* * *

Tam asked the question I was too proud to ask.

"So the second key," he said. "Iron. Where is it? How do we get it out?"

The seer laughed. It was a terrible sound, like a hinge that had been oiled with regret. "Get it out? Boy, the second key isn't *in* anything. The second key is a price." She turned her empty gaze on me. "You've been thinking of these keys as objects. Little metal things to collect, like a child collecting pretty stones. Salt. Iron. Bone. Three keys, three locks, open the binding. But the binding of the crown was never a lock, child. It was a *wound*. And wounds don't open with keys. They open with blood."

"Then what does the iron key actually do?" Tam asked.

"It burns," she said. "When the three keys converge on the binding circle, each one takes its toll. Salt washes away what was false. Bone demands consent from the crowned. And iron—" she looked at me, and for the first time since I'd met her, the blind seer looked almost gentle, "—iron burns away what is borrowed. What is carried. What was never yours to begin with."

The cold came back. Colder.

"The fragments," I said.

"Every one," she said. "Veyra's memories live in you like embers in wool. The moment the iron key touches the binding, it will burn them out of you — all of them, the garden, the hymn, the vastness, everything of the god that you carry. And here is the part you will hate: you will feel them go. You will feel a hundred years of a goddess's life gutter out of you like candles in a draft, and you will know, in that last moment, exactly what you are losing, and you will lose it anyway, because the alternative is letting the crown finish its meal."

The cell was quiet. Somewhere above us, boots crossed stone — guards on rotation, the fortress breathing its slow mechanical breath.

"And if I refuse?" I asked.

"Then you walk away. Truly. The prophecy names you, but prophecies are not chains — they are invitations. Someone else might manage it, in fifty years, after the crown has eaten another king down to the marrow and spat out the husk. After it has eaten more of the world. After Maren's masters decide the vessel is running thin and go shopping for a new one." She shrugged. "Or you do it now, and you pay now, and the payment is *her*. The god you've been praying to since you were small enough to believe prayers were letters and gods read them."

I looked at my hands. Mercenary's hands. Scarred knuckles, broken nails, a burn scar on the left palm from a job gone wrong in Callowmere. My hands. Except apparently they weren't only my hands, and hadn't been for a while, and the woman whose hands they also were had been screaming silently inside me for a hundred years.

"When I was eight," I said, without meaning to, "I got lost in a snowstorm outside Thornfell. I shouldn't have survived it. I remember — I remember deciding to keep walking, and something deciding with me. Something that felt like being carried." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Was that her?"

"Yes."

"And the time I took an arrow at Marrow Ford and should have bled out, and woke up three days later with the wound half-closed?"

"Yes."

"And every time I've prayed and gotten nothing—"

"*Especially* then," the seer said. "Especially then. Because she was right there, child. Right there inside your own chest, hearing herself prayed to, unable to answer. Do you understand now? You were never praying to a distant god. You were praying *at yourself*. The loneliest prayer in the world is the one that gets answered by your own heartbeat."

I put my head in my hands. I'm not ashamed of it. Well. I'm somewhat ashamed of it. But there are moments when the body insists on honesty regardless of the professional reputation of the head attached to it.

Tam touched my shoulder. "Elodie."

"I'm fine."

"You're crying."

"I'm *fine*."

"You're crying and lying about it, which is worse."

"Thank you, Tam. Your gift for consolation remains unmatched."

He squeezed my shoulder and withdrew, which is the correct amount of consolation for a man who met you four days ago.

* * *

I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough that the light through the high slit window changed from grey to gold to grey again. The seer waited with the patience of someone who has waited forty years and can evidently wait forty more.

Finally I said, "Why me? Why her — why Veyra — in *me*? Out of everyone. I'm nobody. I guide smugglers through swamps and stab people who deserve it and can't hold onto a contract longer than a season. Why would the world hide a god in *that*?"

"Because you were safe," the seer said. "Because the custodians hunt prophets and priests and kings, and no one hunts mercenaries. Because you swore yourself to her service at fourteen, broke and starving, and meant it — and meaning it matters. Because you have spent your whole life doing her work without knowing it: protecting the small, punishing the cruel, walking the dark places alone so others don't have to." She smiled again, that sad-end-of-the-book smile. "And because, child, when the moment comes — and it is coming soon, sooner than you'd like — you will be the kind of person who says yes. I've watched you for forty years. I know how you end."

"That's ominous."

"That's a compliment. Learn the difference."

I stood. My knees cracked. Outside the cell, the fortress went on being a fortress, indifferent and enormous, and somewhere beyond it was a palace, and in the palace was a vault, and in the vault was a crown that had eaten a god and was still hungry.

"The third key," I said. "Bone. A king's willing bone. That's in the prophecy. That means Aldric has to choose. We can't take it from him."

"No," the seer agreed. "You can't. Which is why the hardest part of your journey isn't the keys, child. It's convincing a man who has been eaten alive for years that he is worth saving — and that saving him requires him to hand over the only thing he has left: himself." She tilted her head. "But that is tomorrow's grief. Tonight, you have a simpler choice."

"The iron key."

"The iron key."

I thought about the snowstorm. About the arrow at Marrow Ford. About a hundred small salvations I'd chalked up to luck, to stubbornness, to a mercenary's reflexes, and understood that I'd been carrying a passenger the entire time — a drowned woman clinging to driftwood, and the driftwood was me, and the sea was a crown.

If I burned her out of me, she'd be free. Free to return to whatever gods return to. Free to answer prayers again. Free to be *Veyra* instead of a voiceless weight in a mercenary's chest.

And I'd lose the storm. The arrow. The sense of being carried. I'd lose the only evidence I'd ever had that I was more than luck and scar tissue.

"Will it hurt?" I asked. Stupid question. I knew the answer before I asked it.

"Like nothing you have survived yet," the seer said. "And you have survived a great deal."

I nodded. Then, because I am who I am — whoever that turns out to be — I said, "One condition."

The seer raised an eyebrow, which is impressive for a blind woman. "Name it."

"When it burns out — when she goes — tell her—" I stopped. Started again. "Tell her I said thank you. For the storm. For the arrow. For listening to all of it and never once making me feel stupid for talking to an empty room. Tell her the silence was never her fault. Tell her—" My voice did something embarrassing. I pushed through it. "Tell her I'd have carried her longer if she'd needed it."

The seer was quiet for a long moment. Then she reached up and touched my cheek, gently, the way you touch something you're memorizing.

"She knows," she said. "Child, she has known since the day you swore yourself to her at fourteen. She heard every word of that oath. She has been trying to say the same thing back to you for a hundred years." Her hand fell away. "Now go. Before the custodians remember you exist. The iron key is already burning, Elodie Hartwell. It has been burning since the day you were born. All you have to do is arrive."

We climbed the stair. At the top, Tam paused and looked back down at the little cell, at the blind woman sitting in it like a candle in a window.

"What happens to you?" he asked.

"I wait," she said. "It's what I'm for."

* * *

We moved through the fortress corridors in the grey hour before dawn, when even jailers sleep badly, and the freed prisoners flowed around us like water finding cracks. The breakout had cost us — two dead, one missing, and a hole in the wall that wouldn't stay secret past sunrise — but we were out, and out was a direction.

Tam walked beside me, hugging his folios, glancing at me sideways every few steps. He'd been doing that since the cell. Finally he said, "You're quieter than usual."

"I'm processing."

"Processing what?"

"The fact that I'm a reliquary."

"Ah." He considered this. "For what it's worth, you don't seem different."

"I don't *feel* different. That's the problem. Apparently I've been two people my whole life and neither of them told me."

"Well." He adjusted his satchel strap. "For what it's worth — the second person seems nice."

I stopped walking. He stopped too, wary, the way you get wary when a mercenary stops walking for no reason.

"Say that again," I said.

"The second person seems—"

"Not that. Before. You said 'for what it's worth' twice in a row. You do that when you're nervous. You did it in the archive, right before the ceiling came down." I stepped closer. "What aren't you telling me?"

He winced. "It's nothing. It's probably nothing."

"Tam."

"There's a passage. In the folio I saved from the fire. I translated part of it last night while you were sleeping." He dug the scorched folio out of his satchel with the reluctance of a man producing a death warrant. "It's about the iron key. The binding. And it says—" he swallowed, "—it says the toll isn't fixed. The iron burns away what is *borrowed*. But if the bearer offers *more* than what's borrowed — if she offers willingly, wholly, past the point of the toll — the crown will take it. All of it. It can't refuse a willing mind. It's built to consume, and a willing offering overloads it."

I read the passage twice. The words didn't change. Neither did the arithmetic underneath them, which was simple and terrible: the keys alone would free Aldric and cost me Veyra. But the keys plus *everything* — every memory I owned, every scar, every name, every night at every dead altar —

"—it would break the crown," Tam finished quietly. "Permanently. But you wouldn't survive it intact. You wouldn't survive it *at all*, Elodie. Not your memories. Not—" he gestured vaguely at all of me, "—you."

The corridor was very quiet. Somewhere below, water dripped in the dark, keeping time like a patient clock.

I handed the folio back to him. My hands were steady, which surprised me. I'd expected shaking. Instead there was just a strange, ringing calm, the kind that comes after a decision has already been made somewhere deeper than the part of you that votes.

"Then we'd better make sure it works," I said.

"Elodie—"

"Tam." I looked at him — really looked, the way you look at someone when you're adding them to the short list of people you'd trust with your life, which in this case was going to turn out to be literal. "I've spent my whole career taking contracts I didn't write, for employers I'll never meet, dying for causes other people chose. This is the first job I've ever accepted where *I* set the terms. So here are the terms: we get the third key. We get Aldric's consent. We break the crown. And when it breaks—" I smiled, and it felt strange on my face, like wearing someone else's expression, "—someone remembers me. Properly. Not the legend. The person. The stubborn parts. The merciful parts. The parts that were afraid of drowning. Can you do that?"

He stared at me. His eyes were wet. He nodded.

"Good," I said. "Then let's go steal a king."

Above us, dawn was coming up over the prison-fortress like a bruise going yellow, and somewhere far to the east, in a palace I had never seen, a man with holes in his memory was waking to scratches on his door and a sister who watched him too closely — and a crown that was, at this very moment, beginning to starve.

It didn't know yet that its meal was coming to it.
