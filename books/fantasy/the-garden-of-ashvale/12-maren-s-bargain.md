## 12. Maren's Bargain

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that only the desperate practice. It is not the arithmetic of merchants or tax collectors — those are honest trades, dealing in things that exist. No, this is the arithmetic of the cornered: you take one sin in your left hand and one hope in your right, and you weigh them until they feel like the same weight, and then you call it balance.

I had been doing that arithmetic for eleven years, ever since the night our father died and I found my brother on the throne with ash on his lips and no memory of how he'd gotten there. Eleven years of small trades. A servant dismissed here, a physician silenced there, a chamber of the palace sealed and its key swallowed. Each trade had seemed reasonable at the time. That is the trick of it. Reasonable, reasonable, reasonable — and then you look up one morning and you are standing in a room with three faceless custodians, negotiating the fate of a stranger, and you cannot remember where the path was that brought you to the door.

The meeting was called, as always, without being called. I simply found them in my study when I returned from the evening rite, arranged around my fire like crows who had decided the hearth was theirs by right of occupation. The tall one sat in my chair. They always took my chair. It was a courtesy they extended to no one else in the palace, and I had long ago stopped pretending it wasn't deliberate.

"Regent Maren." The tall one's voice came from somewhere behind the porcelain smoothness of his mask. "You've heard the news from Thornfell."

"I hear most news from Thornfell," I said, pouring myself wine because my hands needed employment and they knew better than to stop me. "It arrives with the salt wagons."

"The prophecy book has surfaced. A mercenary carries it. She has taken the Salt Key from the drowned chapel." He said it the way a physician reads a symptom list. "She will come for the Iron Key next. Then the third."

"Then she'll be disappointed," I said, "because there is no third key in existence, as we have all agreed to pretend for a hundred years."

"Regent." The short one, the one whose mask had a hairline crack running through the left cheek like a tear frozen mid-fall. "You know what happens if the binding opens."

I knew. I had been told when I was nineteen and still capable of being frightened into obedience by men in masks. If the crown were unbound improperly, everything it held would spill out at once — every memory it had eaten, a century of devoured minds, dumped into the world like a dam breaking. And King Aldric, hollowed to a rind by then, would simply stop. Not die. Stop. A king-shaped absence in a chair.

What they did not know — what no one in that room knew — was that I had seen the crown feed. Once, early, before I learned to look away. I had been twenty-two, sneaking down to the vault against every instruction, because I was twenty-two and my brother had just called me by our nurse's name and I needed to understand why. I had watched through the vault glass while the crown settled onto his sleeping head like a spider settling onto a fly, and I had watched him smile in his sleep. That was the part that haunted me. Not the feeding. The *smile*. Whatever the crown gave back in those moments, whatever counterfeit sweetness it dripped into the hole it was carving, he loved it. He reached for it.

That image had done more to keep me complicit than any threat the custodians ever made. You can defy men in masks. It is much harder to defy the thing your brother smiles at.

"The girl," the tall one said, "must not reach Ashvale."

"Naturally," I said. "Shall I have the guards write her a stern letter?"

"Maren." My name in that voice, flat and patient, the way one speaks to a child who has forgotten which adults hold the leash. "We are not asking you to be clever. We are asking you to be useful. Your guard intercepts her at the gates. She is taken quietly. She is given to us. In exchange—"

"In exchange you continue to permit my brother to wake up each morning believing he is a king," I finished. "Yes. I know the terms. I wrote half of them."

"You wrote all of them," the cracked-mask one said softly. "Eleven years ago. When you begged us to let him live."

And there it was, laid out on my own rug between us like a corpse. Because it was true. I had begged. I had knelt on this same floor, nineteen years old and drowning in grief, and I had promised them anything, *anything*, if they could keep Aldric alive after the crown killed our father. They had kept their side of it with perfect, monstrous fidelity. My brother woke every morning. He simply woke as a slightly smaller man each time, a candle burning down, and I had signed the wax.

"I want something new in the terms," I said.

The masks turned toward me. Three blank faces, attentive as owls.

"When it's done — when she's given to you and the crown is fed and the crisis passes — I want the feeding reduced. Aldric's memories run thin. Even your own physicians say so. Take less. Let him keep something. Let him keep *himself* a little longer."

The tall one was quiet for a long moment. When custodians are quiet, it is never because they are considering. It is because they are deciding how much truth the moment can afford.

"The crown does not take what we choose," he said finally. "It takes what it needs. But—" and here he leaned forward, and I felt the old vertigo, the sense of standing at the edge of a well, "—a vessel that arrives *willingly* changes the arithmetic. A mind offered freely feeds deeper than a mind taken. One willing soul could sustain the crown for years. Decades, perhaps. Long enough that your brother might grow old as himself. More himself than he has ever been."

*More himself than he has ever been.* I have thought about that sentence many times since. At the time, it went into me like a hook baited with exactly the fish I wanted to catch. They had studied me for eleven years. Of course they knew which lie would work.

"And the alternative?" I asked, though I already knew. There is always an alternative, and it is always worse, and they always make you say it aloud so that it becomes yours.

"The alternative is that she reaches the vault with her keys, and the crown unbinds, and everything it holds comes out at once, and your brother ends in front of the whole court. Or—" a pause, delicate as a scalpel, "—we procure a vessel ourselves. There are always candidates. A servant. A prisoner. Someone forgettable." The cracked mask tilted. "Though I confess, Regent, a stranger's memories would feed poorly. The crown has grown accustomed to royal vintage. It may grow... restless. Restless crowns make demands. Demands require vessels. Vessels require procurement. You see how the arithmetic compounds."

I saw. Oh, I saw. They were telling me, in their bloodless way, that if I did not hand them the mercenary, they would begin harvesting the palace — and that every harvest after the first would land on my desk for signature, because I was the regent, and regents sign things.

Here is what I told myself, that night, standing at my window with the wine going warm in my hand. I told myself: *Aldric first. Aldric always first. This girl chose her road when she picked up the prophecy book; my brother never chose his. If I give them the stranger, I buy him years. Real years. Years in which perhaps I find another way — a physician, a scholar, some thread I haven't pulled yet. This isn't betrayal. This is triage.*

I believed it the way you believe a rope bridge holds: not because you've tested it, but because the alternative is looking down.

* * *

The orders went out before dawn. I wrote them myself, in my own hand, because a regent's hand on a warrant makes it law and also because I am enough of a coward to want the thing done quickly, before the arithmetic could finish itself. Captain Orell received them in the map room, lamplight catching the grey coming into his beard. He had served our father. He had taught Aldric to ride. He read the warrant twice.

"A smuggler and a scribe, my lady?" he said. "Taken alive at the Weeping Gate?"

"Alive. Unharmed, if it can be managed. She is to be treated gently. She is..." I searched for a word that wasn't *sacrifice* and didn't find one. "...ill. Confused. She believes herself part of an old story. The court physicians wish to examine her."

Orell looked at me for a long moment over the top of the warrant. He is not a clever man, but he is an honest one, and honest men have a way of seeing straight through furniture to the room behind it.

"And if she resists?"

"She won't," I said, and hated how certain I sounded, and hated more that I was right. "She has a reputation for mercy. Use it against her."

He saluted and went, boots ringing on stone, and I stood alone in the map room surrounded by the painted borders of a kingdom I had been holding together with wire and lies, and I did the arithmetic again, because apparently I couldn't stop. One stranger, unknown, unloved, against one brother, known since the womb, loved past all reason. Any court in the world would call that a fair trade. Any mother. Any sister.

So why did my hands shake?

Because — and here is the confession I have only ever made to this page — because I had read the intercepted reports from Thornfell. I knew about the drowned chapel. I knew she had faced the wight's visions and walked out carrying a key, and I knew what the surviving witnesses said she did afterward: she sat with a dying miner for two hours because no one else would, though every minute cost her pursuit-time she could not spare. I knew about the prison-fortress, and the breakout, and the blind seer. I knew she had traded herself into chains to reach a key she could have bought with blood, and I knew that when the prisoners asked her why, she had said — and this line I carried around for days like a stone in my boot — *"Because someone should have done it for you a long time ago."*

That is not a woman you feed to a crown. That is a woman you follow.

But love is not a court of ethics. Love is a siege, and it takes the walls it can take, and mine had been under assault for eleven years. I poured the wine I wasn't drinking into the fire and watched it hiss, and I told myself the last lie in the set: *either way, I save him. Either way.*

* * *

They brought me word at dusk. The ambush at the Weeping Gate had gone wrong — wrong in the specific way that honest soldiers executing a dishonorable order tend to make things go wrong. The mercenary had surrendered rather than fight. Had *laid down her sword*, in front of thirty drawn bows, rather than spill conscripted blood. Tam, the scribe boy, was in custody. The woman had gone over the wall wounded, with help from inside the guard — Orell suspected the gate-sergeant, and I suspected Orell suspected correctly and was choosing, for reasons of his own, to lose the trail.

"So we have half of it," the tall custodian said that night, in my study again, in my chair again. "The scribe. And the report says the woman is wounded and alone, hunted by our agents on both banks of the river. She will be taken within days."

"Days," I repeated.

"You sound doubtful, Regent."

"I sound tired. There's a difference." I set down the decanter. "The scribe knows nothing. He copied a book once. Give him to the archive clerks and let them bore him to death honestly."

"The scribe memorized patrol rotations in his cell within six hours of arriving. A scribe's rebellion, the jailer calls it. Inkless memory." The cracked mask clicked softly — laughter, or the memory of it. "They are all more than they appear. That is rather the problem with vessels."

When they had gone, I sat a long while in the dark. Then I did the thing I had been not-doing for eleven years: I went down to the east corridor, past the sealed door, to the little gallery where the old portraits hang, and I stood in front of the painting of Aldric at nine years old, on his first pony, grinning like the sun had personally chosen him. Our father had commissioned it. Our mother had laughed until she cried when Aldric insisted the pony be painted too, and larger than scale, because "Bramble is the hero, Mama, everyone knows it."

The crown had eaten that morning. I had checked, years ago, in the ledger the custodians keep — the inventory of my brother, itemized by decade. Age nine, first ride, Bramble the pony: *consumed, year four of the reign.* He doesn't remember Bramble. He doesn't remember the grin. Somewhere in a vault beneath my feet, a parasite wears my brother's happiest day as a lining.

I pressed my palm flat against the cold frame, and I made myself say it, silently, to the painted boy: *I am doing this for you. Whatever it costs anyone else, I am doing this for you.* And the painted boy grinned on, nine forever, and did not answer, and I understood suddenly that he hadn't answered in eleven years either — that I had been praying to a portrait the way Elodie Hartwell prayed to a silent god, and getting exactly what she got.

Except.

Except the reports said the god had answered her. That was the whisper moving through the lower city now, in the markets and the mines: the silent god spoke to the mercenary, the seer called her the last page, the dead chapel gave up its key to her hand. Coincidence upon coincidence upon coincidence, stacking into something that was no longer coincidence, and every rational bone in my body said *superstition*, and every bone that had ever loved my brother said *what if*.

What if the story was real. What if the crown could be broken cleanly, without the flood, without the ending. What if there was a third way that I had never looked for because looking for it would have meant admitting the first two ways were murder.

I went back to my study and pulled the guard rotations toward me, and I did not alter them. I want that recorded, wherever such things are recorded: I did not alter them. I sat with the power to open a gate, lose a file, delay a patrol — the small treasons that would have cost me nothing — and I did nothing, because the custodians' agents were watching my desk, my doors, my ink, and because I was still telling myself the rope bridge held.

But I did leave one thing off the morning brief. A report had come in overnight, from a river pilot, of a wounded woman buying passage at a fishing village downstream — paying with a ring, traveling toward the capital, asking questions about the coronation-anniversary rites. Standard procedure was to forward such reports to the custodians within the hour. I folded it instead into the drawer where I keep our mother's letters, among the things I cannot bear to read and cannot bear to burn.

It was not mercy. Do not mistake it for mercy. It was the arithmetic turning over one final time, the last bead clicking along the abacus frame: because if the story was real, then the woman walking toward my city was not a stranger to be traded but the answer to a prayer I had stopped believing in — and if it was not real, then my silence would cost a few days, and the custodians would take her anyway, and the outcome would be the one I had already sold my soul to purchase.

Either way, I told myself, I save him.

The drawer closed. Outside, the bells began the midnight count, and somewhere across the river a wounded woman was moving toward the Weeping Gate with a god's memories rattling in her skull like coins in a jar, and I sat in the dark doing sums that would not come out even, no matter how many times I ran them.

Three days remained until the anniversary rite. The crown would feed. The vault would open. Everything I had spent eleven years balancing would tip, one way or the other, and I was beginning to suspect — with the cold clarity that arrives only after the last lie is told — that I had never once been the one holding the scales.
