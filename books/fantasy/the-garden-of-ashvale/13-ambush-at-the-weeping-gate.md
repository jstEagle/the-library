## 13. Ambush at the Weeping Gate

The Weeping Gate earned its name honestly. Water seeped through the mortar of its ancient arch in a hundred thin threads, so that even on the driest summer day the stones glistened and dripped, and the gutter-mouths carved into the archivolt — faces, old ones, worn to suggestion by centuries of rain — seemed to be crying for reasons no one remembered anymore. I'd passed under it a dozen times in my years running Thornfell's underworld, usually at night, usually with someone else's goods strapped to my back.

Tonight I was carrying something worse than stolen goods. I was carrying a key made of iron that hummed against my ribs like a caged wasp, and a boy who wouldn't stop talking about how we were going to die.

"We should have gone through the sewers," Tam said for the fourth time since the salt mines. He had his satchel clutched to his chest like a shield, which was exactly wrong; if it came to blades, that satchel would get him killed. "I mapped them once. There's an outflow channel behind the tanneries that—"

"The sewers flood with the tide," I said. "And the custodians know every inch of them. They built half this city's plumbing, Tam. You think they don't know where the pipes go?"

"They built the *archive* too, and you were going to walk right into it."

"That was different."

"How?"

"It burned down before we got there. That makes it a completely different plan."

He gave me the look he gave me when he thought I was being deliberately stupid, which was often, because I usually was being deliberately stupid. It's a useful skill, deliberate stupidity. It keeps people from asking the questions you can't answer. Like why a blind seer in a prison-fortress had called me *the last page*, or why the iron key warmed when I prayed, or why I kept dreaming of a woman's face wearing mine like a mask.

We came up out of the cooper's lane into the square before the gate, and I knew before I saw them.

You learn things in twenty years of mercenary work. You learn how a crowd sounds when it's nervous — a particular hush, like the moment before a wave breaks. You learn how lamplight falls differently when there are more bodies than lamps can account for. And you learn the specific quality of silence that means men are standing very still in doorways, waiting for someone to give them permission.

The square was empty of everyone but us and the shadows, and the shadows were full of soldiers.

"Elodie," Tam whispered.

"I see them."

"Doorways. The well. Behind the—"

"Behind the fountain, yes. Left side, six. Right side, four. At least two on the wall above the gate itself." I kept walking. Stopping would tell them we'd seen them, and seeing them meant deciding, and deciding meant one side or the other got to stop waiting. "Keep walking. Casual. We're two travelers going out the gate at midnight like normal people do."

"Nobody goes out the Weeping Gate at midnight."

"People with bad plans do."

"*Our* plan is bad?"

"Our plan is terrible," I said. "That's what makes it ours."

The gate guards stepped out first, as they always do. Two of them, halberds crossed, and behind them the rest came out of the dark like water finding its level: twelve, fourteen, maybe more, chain mail over palace livery, spears leveled but not thrusting. Conscripts, most of them. You could tell by the way they held their weapons — too tight, knuckles white, the grip of men who hoped the other fellow would run so nobody would have to do anything. Veterans hold their spears loose. Conscripts strangle theirs.

A sergeant pushed through. Big man, gray at the temples, the kind of face that had been kind once and had been paid not to be anymore. He had a folded paper in his hand.

"Elodie Hartwell." He said it like a man reading a death sentence he didn't write. "By order of the Regent Maren, you're to surrender yourself and all items in your possession. Come quiet, and no one needs to bleed tonight."

"And if I come loud?"

"Then a lot of boys who joined the guard to feed their mothers are going home in boxes," he said. "And their mothers will know your name."

Honest, at least. I liked him immediately, in the way you like a man who's going to try to kill you but won't pretend otherwise.

Tam shifted beside me, and I felt him reaching for the satchel — the scorched folio, the prophecy book, everything we'd bled for — and I put my hand flat on his arm without looking away from the sergeant.

"Here's my problem, Sergeant," I said. "Everything in my possession includes a thing that's keeping a king alive. Hand it over, and I've handed a parasite its dinner. Don't hand it over, and I've got to cut through fourteen boys who'd rather be anywhere else. Neither option's great."

"The king is dying anyway," the sergeant said. "Everyone says so. Everyone's always said so."

"Everyone's been saying it for a hundred years," I said. "That's rather the point."

Something moved above us. On the wall, crossbows. Two of them, cranked and loaded, tracking me with the lazy patience of men who'd done this before. Not conscripts, those two. Professionals. The custodians' insurance policy, tucked up where the moonlight couldn't find their faces.

So. Fourteen conscripts I might talk my way past, and two killers I couldn't.

I did the arithmetic I hate doing. It's the arithmetic every mercenary does before every fight, the cold little ledger: can I win, what will it cost, who pays. Fourteen conscripts, tired, scared, badly led. I could take them. Maybe not clean — I was already carrying a knife wound from the prison-fortress that hadn't stopped weeping — but I could take them. Tam couldn't. Tam would die in the first ten seconds holding his satchel like a shield, and the folio would burn with him, and the custodians would win everything they wanted while I lay bleeding in a gutter having won nothing but a body count.

Or.

Or there was a third column in the ledger, the one mercenaries aren't supposed to have. The one Veyra's silent altar had carved into me somewhere along the way, back when I still prayed to her and hated her for not answering. *Some debts aren't paid in blood.* She'd never said that. No one had ever said that. But I'd carried it so long it felt like scripture, and scripture doesn't need an author.

"Sergeant," I said. "What's your name?"

He blinked. "Bren. Bren Colm."

"Bren Colm. How many of these boys have wives? Mothers? Somebody waiting up?"

"That's not—"

"Because here's what happens next," I said. "Option one: I fight. I'm good, Sergeant. Ask anyone in Thornfell's underworld, ask the wight in the salt mines, ask the prophets in Ironhold. I'm very good. Some of your boys die. Probably most of them. I probably die too, eventually, but I'll be expensive. And then the custodians send someone *worse* than me after the key, and those same boys' families lose them twice — once to the grave, once to whatever hollow thing comes back wearing the crown's leash. Option two: I tell you where I found the second key, you let the boy go, and everyone here goes home to breakfast."

"You'd just give it up?" One of the crossbowmen laughed from the wall. "Just like that?"

"Just like that," I said, and I smiled at the wall, at the darkness where the professionals stood, because I wanted them to hear the smile in my voice. "It's only iron, friend. Keys are cheap. What they open isn't. And you'll find the key's location is the least interesting thing about tonight."

It wasn't entirely a lie. The key itself hung warm against my ribs, and I had no intention of giving it up — but the location, the drowned cell in Ironhold where I'd pried it from its socket, that I could surrender like a coin tossed to beggars. Let them dig through a flooded prison cell while I walked out a gate. It bought time. Time was the only currency I had left.

Sergeant Bren looked at me for a long moment. Then at Tam. Then at the paper in his hand, which named two fugitives and offered silver for both.

"The boy stays," he said finally. "Regent's orders. Both of you."

"The boy is a scribe," I said. "He copies things. That's his whole crime — copying things. You want to hang a man for handwriting?"

"He copied *that*." Bren nodded at the satchel. "The prophecy. The custodians want the copyist, not the copy."

Of course they did. Of course. Erase the evidence, then erase the man who could reproduce it. I'd watched the archive burn three nights ago and told myself the rage would keep me warm. It hadn't. It had just kept me awake.

"Then here's my counteroffer," I said, and I heard my own voice go flat and cold, the voice I used for contracts. "The boy walks out that gate with me, or I start spending your conscripts like coin. Your choice, Sergeant. But choose fast, because the professionals on your wall work for someone else, and they don't care whose mothers wait up."

Bren's jaw worked. Above us, the crossbows tracked. Below us, fourteen young men strangled their spears and waited for someone to make the world simple again.

And then Tam did the bravest stupidest thing I have ever seen anyone do.

He stepped forward. Away from me. Into the torchlight, alone, holding his satchel out in front of him like an offering.

"She's telling the truth," he said, and his voice cracked in the middle and he kept going anyway, which took more spine than any sword-arm I've ever hired. "She's trying to save the king. All of this — the keys, the prophecy, everything — it's to save King Aldric. Check the folio. Read it. The crown is eating him alive and she's the only one trying to stop it, and if you kill her, you're killing the only person in Ashvale who—" His voice broke again. "Please. Just her. Take me. Let her go."

"Tam," I said.

"Somebody has to carry the folio," he said, not looking at me. "If they take me, they'll search me, they'll find it, they'll read it. That's better than burning. A read truth survives. A burned one doesn't. Elodie, *please*. This is the part I'm good for."

Gods below. Twenty years a mercenary and I'd never trained a recruit half that brave.

Bren looked at the boy. Then at me. Then, briefly, up at the wall, and something passed across his face — some old soldier's arithmetic of his own, some tally of orders given versus orders that deserved following.

"Take the boy," he said quietly. "Let the woman go. That's the trade. Crossbows stand down."

"We don't take orders from—" one of the wall-men started.

"From me, tonight, you do," Bren said, without raising his voice, and something in the flatness of it made the crossbows lower by a degree. "Regent wants the woman alive. Nobody said alive *and* whole. Nobody said she can't be bleeding when she goes. Nobody said anything about the boy except bring him in. So: boy comes in walking, woman goes out walking, and if either crossbow fires before she's through the arch, I'll personally introduce you to the discipline schedule."

For a heartbeat, the square held its breath.

Then Tam turned to me, and pressed the satchel into my hands — the folio, the prophecy book, all of it — and grinned at me with tears cutting tracks through the road-dust on his face.

"A read truth survives," he said. "Don't you dare burn it."

"I don't burn books," I said. "I burn people who deserve it."

"That's the spirit."

They took his arms. He went without struggling, which was somehow worse, and at the mouth of the gatehouse tunnel he turned back one last time.

"Elodie. The third key. The seer's letters — page nine, the marginalia. It's not what you think it is. Read the marginalia."

And then the dark of the tunnel swallowed him, and he was gone.

* * *

I walked out through the Weeping Gate alone, and the stone faces wept on me as I passed, and I let them, because somebody in this city ought to cry about it.

Four streets beyond the walls, in the shadow of a dyer's shed, I let myself lean against the bricks and shake. Not fear. Fear's a tool; you spend it and move on. This was the other thing, the thing with no name, the cost-of-doing-business feeling that arrives after the ledger closes and shows you the final sum. I'd traded a boy for a head start. A boy who'd known me eleven days. A boy who'd copied a dead god's prophecy in a royal archive and followed a stranger into fire because she promised him a story worth telling.

I pressed my palm against the knife wound in my side. It had torn open somewhere during the parley — adrenaline's little joke — and my hand came away red.

"Well," I said to the empty street. "That went well."

The iron key pulsed against my ribs, warm as a sleeping bird, and somewhere beneath the city, beneath the palace, beneath the vault where a king slept dreamless and devoured, I felt — or imagined, or remembered, though I'd never lived it — a woman standing at a dead altar, praying into silence, refusing to leave.

*Page nine,* Tam had said. *Read the marginalia.*

I opened the folio with bloody fingers, right there in the dyer's shadow, and read.

And what the marginalia said changed everything — because the third key wasn't hidden in some tomb or temple. It was walking around wearing a crown, and it had been waiting for me to figure that out all along.

The wound in my side chose that moment to remind me it existed, and the street tilted, and the last thing I saw before the dark took me was the ink of the marginalia swimming like fish:

*the third key is a king's willing bone — and bones remember what minds forget.*

Then nothing.
