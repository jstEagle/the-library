## 15. Cells Below the Palace

The cell was six feet by eight, which by Thornfell standards would have been a generous studio apartment. By Ashvale palace standards, it was apparently a place to keep people who had read the wrong books.

I'd been in it for three days when I decided to start counting things. It's a scribe's habit — when you can't write, you count. Fourteen steps of corridor between my door and the turning. Three meals a day, delivered through a slot at knee height, because the custodians evidently believed I might weaponize eye contact. One bucket. One blanket that smelled of mildew and someone else's despair. And one jailer, who came twice a shift and never once looked at me.

His name, I learned from the guards who talked over my head as though I were furniture, was Doss. He had served in the cells below the palace for nineteen years. He had a bad knee, a worse marriage, and a face like a man who had long ago stopped expecting anything from the universe except its next disappointment.

In other words: he was perfect.

You have to understand something about me. I am not brave. I want that on the record, in a fair hand, with margins. I copied documents in the royal archive for six years and my greatest act of rebellion before all this was undercharging for private commissions. When the custodians' men dragged me out of the Weeping Gate ambush — Elodie bleeding in an alley somewhere behind us, gone, escaped, alive, *alive* — I did not spit defiance. I cried. Quietly, into my sleeve, like a professional.

But I have one skill, and it turns out it works even without ink.

A scribe's memory is a strange organ. You spend years copying other people's words until your hands know letters the way a sailor's legs know a deck. And when they take away your paper — when they strip you, search you, find nothing, and lock you in a stone box — the memory doesn't stop. It just goes underground. Inkless. Waiting.

So on the third day, I began my rebellion.

* * *

It started with the patrols.

They came every four hours, or near enough. Two guards, boots on stone, past my door, then a longer gap, then the changing of the watch with its ritual exchange of countersigns mumbled too low for me to catch — at first. But sound carries strangely below the palace. The cells are old, older than the kingdom above them if the archive folios I'd memorized were right, and the corridors funnel noise like a throat.

Day four: patrol at what I judged to be dawn, midday, dusk, and midnight. Day five: I noticed the midnight pair always lingered at the corner nearest my cell, because there was a draft there and they huddled against it to smoke something illicit. Day six: I learned their names. Brannick and Ollis. Brannick was the talker. Ollis was the one who laughed.

I lay on my blanket in the dark and built it all into a grid, the way I'd once laid out a tax ledger. Times. Names. Directions. The creak of the stair at the far end, third step from the top, every ascent, no exceptions. The jangle of keys — Doss carried his on a ring of nine, and I could tell them apart by pitch. The small iron one that opened the food slot. The heavy one that opened my door. The one he never used, which rattled differently, duller, and which I eventually deduced opened the cells deeper down, where the custodians kept the people they didn't want anyone, including themselves, to think about.

A scribe's rebellion in inkless memory. Every rotation, every counter-sign fragment, every irregularity, filed away in the vault of my skull where no search could reach it. They had taken my satchel, my pens, the little knife I used to cut quills. They had not taken the six years of copying other people's secrets, which had taught me that information is the only possession that grows heavier the more you carry it and lighter the more you give away.

Now I just needed someone to give it to.

* * *

Doss came on the morning of the eighth day, and for the first time, he looked at me.

Not through me — at me. A long, flat, assessing look, the kind a man gives a horse he's been told to sell but suspects is lame. Then he slid the tray through the slot and said, "You're the scribe."

"I'm a scribe," I corrected, because pride is the last luxury they can't confiscate. "There's a difference. There are thousands of *the* scribes."

"Brannick says you haven't slept."

"Brannick should try listening to himself smoke at midnight. It's very restful, actually. Like a dying bellows."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of one, exhumed after a long burial. "You've been counting," he said.

Ah. So he'd heard me too. Of course he had; I'd been muttering the tallies under my breath some nights, unable to help it, numbers spilling out of me like water from a cracked jug. "Counting what?"

"Everything." He crouched by the slot, and up close I could see the nineteen years written on him in fine lines — around the eyes, across the knuckles, in the particular gray of a man who has spent his working life underground. "Patrols. Meals. My keys, I'd wager. You've got the look. Had a prisoner once, years back, an accountant out of Vell. Counted everything till the day they took him upstairs. Never found out what happened to him."

"What happened to the accountant is the least of my concerns," I said. "What happens to me is somewhat higher on the list."

"And what's that?"

"Well." I sat up, folded my blanket with unnecessary precision — a prop, a bit of business, anything to make me look like a man worth talking to rather than a man worth forgetting. "Either they question me properly, in which case I'll be interrogated, or they don't, in which case I'll be left here until I stop being interesting, and I've read enough archive requisitions to know what 'disposal of materials' means in this building."

Doss was quiet for a while. Somewhere above us, the palace went about its morning — bells, boots, the distant thunder of the coronation-anniversary preparations, because the whole city was being scrubbed and garlanded for a hundred years of a king who couldn't remember the last ten.

"You ask a lot of questions for a man in a hole," he said finally.

"I'm told it's the questions that put me here."

Another silence. Then, so quietly I almost missed it: "The woman. The one they took you with. Gate guard says she went over the wall wounded. Half the watch thinks she's dead."

My heart did something complicated and painful. I kept my face still, because I'd learned that much from watching Elodie — that hope is a thing you hide the way you hide coin from thieves. "Is she?"

"That's not what I asked."

"No," I said. "It isn't."

He stood, knees cracking like green kindling, and gathered his tray. At the end of the corridor he paused, and without turning around, said: "Ollis's sister works in the laundry up top. She's got opinions about the crown. Loud ones, sometimes, after her second cup." And then he was gone, and I lay back on my blanket and let myself shake, just a little, just for a minute, because she was alive, she was alive, and the machine above me had a loose thread in it named Ollis.

* * *

The thread unraveled faster than I expected.

Ollis came down two nights later with Brannick, and while Brannick smoked his contraband at the drafty corner, Ollis drifted toward my door — casual, unhurried, a man taking a stroll he had no reason to take. He didn't speak. He just stood there, close enough that I could hear him breathing, and waited.

I gave him what he'd come for. "She made it over the wall," I said softly. "Wounded, but moving. If she died, they'd have paraded the body. They haven't. Which means either she's alive, or she's alive somewhere they can't admit."

"They say she took the second key into the city."

"They say a lot of things."

"The seer got out too. Whole block of them, gone like smoke. Warden's furious. Custodians are—" He stopped himself, glanced back at the corner. Brannick was exhaling blue clouds and pretending, badly, not to listen. "There's talk upstairs. Talk I don't like. About the rite. About needing another vessel if the king's memories run out before the anniversary feeding."

Vessel. The word landed in my stomach like a dropped stone. I thought of Elodie's face in the salt chapel, lit by wight-light, wearing someone else's expression. I thought of the scorched folio hidden somewhere in the city — *the crown predates the kingdom* — and of Maren Ashvale, regent, sister, whatever she was, sending soldiers to the gate.

"How many guards on the deep cells?" I asked. "The ones Doss's dull key opens."

Ollis went very still. "Why?"

"Because if they're going to feed someone to that thing during the rite, I'd like to know how many doors stand between the prisoners and daylight. Call it professional interest. I catalog things. It's a sickness."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then, in a voice barely above the drip of the walls, he gave me a number, and a shift change, and the fact that the deep-cell door stuck in winter and had to be shouldered. And Brannick, from the corner, without turning around, added: "Stair's unwatched between the second and fourth bells. Has been for a year. Nobody checks because nobody's ever come up it."

I lay in the dark afterward and built it all into the ledger. Names. Numbers. Bells. The sticking door. The unwatched stair. A map of the machine, drawn nowhere, held everywhere, in the one place the custodians could never burn.

* * *

On the eleventh day, Doss brought me bread instead of gruel, and with it, folded inside the cloth, a stub of charcoal no bigger than my thumb.

I stared at it for a long time. Nineteen years, I reminded myself. Nineteen years of keeping his head down and his knee quiet and his opinions locked in the same vault where I kept my ledgers. And now this — a stick of burnt wood, passed through a slot at knee height, worth a flogging if anyone saw.

"Doss," I said carefully. "This is—"

"Don't write anything," he said. "That's not what it's for."

"Then what—"

"It's for thinking with. In my experience, a man thinks better holding something." He shrugged, embarrassed, already turning away. "My daughter used to say that. Before. Anyway. Eat your bread."

Before. The word hung in the corridor after him like smoke. Everyone down here had a *before*. The custodians had built their whole empire on taking people's befores away, and here was a jailer smuggling comfort to a prisoner in the shape of a charcoal stub, because his daughter — wherever, whoever — had taught him that hands need something to hold.

I held the charcoal and I thought.

And what I thought was this: the rite was coming. Five days, maybe six, by my count of meals. The whole palace would turn itself inside out for the anniversary — every guard pulled to the halls above, every eye on the throne, every door below left to men like Doss and Ollis and Brannick, men with sisters in the laundry and daughters in the *before* and opinions they'd been swallowing for nineteen years.

And somewhere out in the ash-grey city, a mercenary with a hole in her side and a prophecy in her pocket was gathering keys.

I turned the charcoal over in my fingers. I couldn't write to her. I couldn't send word beyond these walls except through Ollis's sister and the laundry line and whatever fragile chain of cups-of-tea opinions connected the cells below the palace to the world above. But chains are how revolutions work. Not swords — chains. Link by link by link, each one small, each one breakable alone, none of them breakable together.

On the twelfth day, Ollis leaned against my door and told me the laundry had sent a bundle down with the prison linens, and in the bundle, stitched inside a pillowcase in thread the color of the fabric, were three words.

*Garden. Third bell.*

I closed my eyes and pressed the pillowcase to my chest like a fool, like a boy, like a scribe who has just received the first letter addressed to him in months and cannot even answer it properly.

Then I got to work. There was a map in my head, and it was accurate to the step, and in five days the machine above me was going to discover exactly how dangerous it is to lock up a man whose entire life is remembering things.

* * *

The night before the rite, Doss came down the corridor with his nine keys singing their nine notes, and he stopped at my door, and he looked at me with nineteen years of careful nothing on his face.

"Brannick's requested the deep-cell watch tomorrow," he said. "Ollis swapped onto the garden gate. Funny thing, both of them volunteering for the worst posts on the busiest night of the century." He jingled the ring, once, absently. "Almost like somebody organized it."

"Somebody with a very good memory," I agreed.

"Mm." He crouched, and through the slot his eyes were tired and bright and terrified all at once, and I understood that whatever happened tomorrow, this man had already spent his courage, quietly, in advance, on a charcoal stub and a piece of bread. "If it goes wrong," he said, "they'll come down here first. That's how it always goes. The ones below pay for the ones above."

"Then I suppose," I said, "we'd better make sure it doesn't go wrong."

He almost smiled. The ghost again, climbing out of its grave, blinking in the light. "Third bell," he said. "The stair's unwatched. The deep door sticks — shoulder it low, on the left hinge. And scribe—"

"Yes?"

"When you get out of here. Whatever you write about all this." He stood, knees cracking, keys singing, a small brave man in a large machine. "Spell my name right."

And he walked away down the corridor, and I sat in the dark with my charcoal and my pillowcase and my ledger of stolen hours, and above me the palace began to dress itself for a hundred years of forgetting, and below me, in cells the custodians thought were silent, something vast and patient and entirely made of memory began, link by link, to move.
