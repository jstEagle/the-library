## 16. The Custodian's Mask

I had been Regent of Ashvale for eleven years, and in that time I had learned that power is mostly a matter of doors — which ones you may open, which ones open for you, and which ones you are never, ever permitted to see behind.

The custodians had always kept one door closed to me. Tonight they opened it, and I would have given anything to have remained ignorant for the rest of my natural life.

It began, as these things do, with a summons written in a hand too elegant for its content. *The Lady Regent will attend the Custody at the ninth bell. Come alone. Come unaccompanied by sentiment.* That last phrase was not a figure of speech. I checked. With the custodians it never was.

* * *

The Custody sat beneath the eastern wing of the palace, in rooms that appeared on no architect's plan and appeared on no servant's tongue either. I had walked past the blank stretch of corridor where its entrance hid perhaps a thousand times, and the door had only ever resolved itself out of the stonework when it wished to be found. Tonight it wished. The stones simply decided, between one step and the next, that there had always been an archway there, and I stepped through into air that tasted of candle smoke and old coin and something else — something sweetish and wrong, like fruit beginning to rot in a locked room.

Nine of them waited in a chamber lit by a single brazier. Nine robed figures, nine masks of pale ceramic, featureless except for the suggestion of a face — the ghost of a nose, the ghost of a mouth, as if someone had pressed cloth over wet clay and peeled it away before the features could finish forming. I had negotiated with these masks for a decade. I had lied to them, flattered them, feared them in the disciplined way one fears weather.

"Lady Regent," said the central figure, and its voice was a dry rustle, paper turning. "You have asked, on four occasions this year, what becomes of a king who serves the Crown fully."

"I've asked what is expected of my brother," I corrected. "There's a difference."

"There is not," said the mask, "but we admire the distinction. You have earned the truth tonight, because the truth has become operationally relevant." A pause, and I swear the thing managed to make a pause feel like a hand on the back of my neck. "Your brother's memories thin faster than anticipated. The Crown requires a successor vessel within the year. We are instructing you in the succession so that you may assist."

"Successor vessel," I repeated. The words felt like handling a dead bird — light, wrong, feathers coming off on your fingers. "You mean a person."

"We mean a mind," said the mask, with the mild pedantry of a grammar tutor. "A body is incidental. A mind, whole and rich and willing or at least capturable, is the requirement. Your brother was such a mind once. He is becoming less so. This is not cruelty, Lady Regent. It is arithmetic."

And then — because I suppose they believed that showing me would settle me, would make me theirs entirely, the way showing a child the sausage-making is supposed to cure her appetite — the central custodian reached up and removed its mask.

I want to tell you I screamed. I didn't. I am a Vane of Ashvale; we are raised to keep our faces still while our minds run screaming down corridors. But some part of me did scream, and it has not stopped since.

Under the mask was a face, and under the face was almost nothing. Skin stretched over a skull like parchment over a drum, yellowed, translucent at the cheekbones so that when the brazier flickered I could see the shadow of the bone beneath. The eyes were the worst. They were open, and aware, and ancient — eyes that had watched centuries go by from inside a body that should have crumbled before my grandmother's grandmother drew breath. And around those eyes, faint as watermarks, I could read the residue of expression. Not the current face's expression. Older ones. Layered. A smile worn into the skin by ten thousand repetitions, now frozen. A frown beneath it. Beneath that, something that might have been weeping, pressed so deep and so long it had become geology.

"Do you recognize me?" the custodian asked, and the voice was the same dry rustle, but now it came from lips, and that made it infinitely worse.

"No," I said honestly.

"No," it agreed, and there was — God help me — there was relief in it. "Nor does anyone. I was Aethelric the Second. Before your kingdom had that name. I wore the Crown for forty years, and when my memories ran thin, they did not let me die. Dying is wasteful. They kept me. The Crown's leavings sustain us — the dregs of memory it cannot digest, the residue of selves it has finished eating. It is not life. It is preservation. Meat kept from spoiling." The hollowed king tilted its head, and the layered expressions shifted against each other like plates in an earthquake. "Your brother will be this. Unless his mind lasts longer than mine did, which yours will not, if you continue to love him. Love burns memory like a furnace. We have measured."

"You're telling me," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect, "that if I stop loving my brother, he suffers less."

"I am telling you that everything suffers less when it is empty," said Aethelric the Second, who had once, somewhere under all those fossilized expressions, presumably been a man who laughed at something. "Emptiness is the great mercy. You will learn this."

They dismissed me after that. There were logistics — there are always logistics — schedules for the feeding rites, instructions regarding a mercenary woman currently making inconvenient inquiries in Thornfell, a list of names whose archives were to be quietly thinned. I took the papers. I bowed. I walked out through the archway that stopped existing behind me, and I made it all the way to my chambers before my legs gave out and I sat down on the floor beside my own bed like a scullery maid having a fit of the vapors.

Eleven years. Eleven years I had told myself I was protecting Aldric. Managing the custodians. Keeping the machine fed so it wouldn't devour him whole — a farmer's logic, feed the wolf so it doesn't eat the flock. And the whole time the wolf had been wearing my brother's crown and eating him bite by bite, and the flock was me, and the shepherd was a dead king pickled in other people's memories, and I had been bringing hay to the trough with my own two hands.

I thought about the boy Aldric had been. This is dangerous territory for me; I could live there. He was seven when Father died and he cried at the funeral not because he was sad but because he thought he wasn't sad enough and it frightened him. He used to hide candied plums in the lining of his sleeves during council meetings and eat them when the ambassadors got tedious, and once he leaned over to me, aged twelve, deadly serious, and whispered, *Maren, if I'm ever king, promise you'll tell me when I start talking nonsense.* 

He'd started talking nonsense years ago. I'd noticed. I'd noticed the way he forgot servants' names he'd known a decade, the way he sometimes looked at his own hands as if they'd been dealt to him in a card game he didn't remember joining. And I had explained it away, because explaining things away was my function, my office, my sacred duty as Regent: grief, fatigue, the weight of rule, poor sleep.

Poor sleep. My brother was being eaten alive at the rate of one dawn per dawn, and I had prescribed him rest.

I sat on the floor for a long time. Then I got up, washed my face, and began committing treason.

* * *

Treason, it turns out, looks remarkably like paperwork. That is the first thing I would tell anyone contemplating it. You do not need daggers in the dark. You need a steady hand, a good memory, and the ability to copy out a patrol rotation without your ink trembling.

I knew things. Eleven years of managing the machine means eleven years of knowing where its gears are. I knew the vault beneath the coronation hall had three entrances, not the two the custodians believed I knew about — the third being the old cistern passage, sealed officially since the flood of my grandfather's reign, unofficially since a mason named Corvin took bribes from my father and never quite finished bricking it up. I knew the rite schedule: the crown fed at the anniversary vigil, when the palace emptied toward the hall and the guard rotated thin along the undercrofts. I knew the counterseal on the binding circle required three keys, because the custodians had discussed it in front of me for years in their dry rustling voices, assuming that a woman who understood nothing would understand nothing.

That last assumption had been the most insulting and the most useful thing they ever did.

The problem was delivery. I could not be seen passing intelligence; the moment I turned openly, Aldric's value as a vessel would end and they would simply take him somewhere I couldn't follow, somewhere with worse arithmetic. No — if I moved, I had to move invisibly, through channels that pointed anywhere but at me.

Which meant the scribe boy. Tam. The guards had taken him at the Weeping Gate, and the custodians had ordered him held rather than killed, which told me they wanted something from his head — a scribe who'd copied prophecy has a head worth picking. He was in the cells below the palace, and cells below the palace have jailers, and jailers have vices, and vices have prices.

I went down myself, veiled, carrying a basket of "prisoner comforts" as ladies of a certain reforming temperament occasionally do. The jailer, a gray man named Hobb with the eyes of someone who has seen every kind of prisoner and been impressed by none of them, let me stand outside the bars of the boy's cell.

Tam looked terrible. Scribes generally do, but captivity had added a fine patina of misery. He looked up at me with the wariness of someone who has learned that noblewomen bearing baskets are rarely bearing baskets.

"The woman you traveled with," I said quietly. "Elodie. She lives."

Something moved in his face — hope trying to get past suspicion at a narrow doorway. "Prove it."

"She refuses to kill soldiers defending their own homes," I said. "She gave up ground rather than blood at the gate. If she were dead, you'd know it, because half the city would be burning out of respect." I set the basket down. Under the bread and the small beer, wrapped in linen, lay a folded paper. "Eat well. Sleep when you can. And when the vigil comes — be ready to move. Someone will open your door."

"Who?" he whispered.

"A friend of your friend," I said, which was a lie, and a treason, and the truest sentence I'd spoken in eleven years.

Hobb watched me leave with an expression I chose to read as professional indifference. Whether it was, I'd learn soon enough. Treason is a ladder you climb in the dark, trusting each rung.

Back in my chambers, I burned my drafts and kept only the final copies: the cistern route, the vigil schedule, the guard rotations, the location of the binding circle's counterseal. Three copies, three couriers, none of them knowing what they carried or for whom. One to a wine merchant in the lower city who owed my late husband debts of a non-monetary nature. One tucked into the prison basket's false bottom, for the boy to pass along his own mysterious channels — scribes always have channels; it's the whole profession. And one I would deliver myself, tomorrow, to a wounded mercenary reported convalescing in a dyer's loft near the tanneries, guarded by ex-prisoners with grudges and knives.

The last courier was the most dangerous errand, because it put my face in front of the one person in Ashvale with every reason to hate it. Elodie Hartwell had lost her companion to my guards. Whatever she'd surrendered at the Weeping Gate, she'd surrendered it to men wearing my livery. When she saw me she would reach for a blade, and she would be right to.

I would let her. I'd even hold still for a moment. Some debts should be paid in the currency of fear; it's the only coin the guilty can mint honestly.

Tonight, though, I stood at my window looking down at the darkened palace, at the wing above the Custody, and I thought about Aethelric the Second's face — those layered expressions, that smile worn into stone by repetition until it became a scar. Somewhere under all of it had been a man. Someone had loved him once, probably. Someone had probably promised to tell him when he started talking nonsense.

The vigil was nine days away. The crown would feed. The keys would converge, if my letters traveled true and my luck held, and luck, I have found, holds exactly as long as you refuse to relax.

"Hold on, Aldric," I said to the window, to the city, to nobody. "Your sister is finally doing something stupid enough to work."

Below, in the dark under the eastern wing, something ancient turned over in its long preserved sleep, and did not dream, because dreaming costs memory, and memory was the only food left in the world it cared about.

Nine days.
