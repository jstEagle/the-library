## 17. Breaking the Vault

There is a particular kind of silence that comes before a thing breaks. I'd heard it in sieges, in the held breath before a wall comes down, in the moment before a blade leaves the sheath. Tonight the whole palace wore that silence like a shroud.

I crouched in the shadow of the aqueduct with Tam's shoulder pressed against mine and counted my army: eleven freed prisoners, one blind seer who kept smiling at things that hadn't happened yet, one regent's stolen floor plans inked on a scribe's forearm, and me — a mercenary who was, according to a book older than the kingdom, supposed to do something impossible before dawn.

"Remind me," I whispered to Tam, "why we didn't just knock politely."

"Because last time you knocked politely at the Weeping Gate, you got shot."

"That was surrendering, actually. There's a difference."

"You were bleeding from two places."

"Three," I said. "You weren't looking at my back."

He didn't laugh. Neither did I, really, but it was the kind of joke you make when your hands won't stop shaking and you need them to. The aqueduct ran above us, old Ashvale stone sweating in the night cold, carrying water down toward the palace gardens where nothing had grown in living memory. Above the gardens rose the vault.

Maren's intelligence was good. Of course it was good — she'd bought our lives with treason, and treason makes for meticulous work. Three keys converge during the rite, her note had said. The crown feeds on the anniversary of its own coronation; the custodians will be gathered around it like priests around an altar, and their attention will be inward. That is the hour. There will not be another.

She'd signed it with no name at all, just a pressed ashleaf, which was either poetry or a threat. With regents, I'd learned, it was usually both.

* * *

We came into the palace through the cisterns, because every fortress has a door its architects are ashamed of, and Maren had found this one. The water came up to my ribs and tasted of stone and old iron. Behind me, the prisoners moved in single file, breathing shallow, hands on each other's shoulders in the dark. Half of them had spent years in cells below the prison-fortress. Sera, the woman who'd once picked a lock with her own tooth, went first without being asked. The blind seer — they called her Mother Vess, though she insisted she'd never been anybody's mother — walked the flooded passage as if it were a garden path.

"You're smiling again," I told her.

"I'm listening." She tilted her head. "The palace hums tonight. Do you hear it? Like a struck bell that won't finish ringing."

"The rite," Tam said. "It's started."

He was right. Even down here, under half the mountain, I could feel it — a pressure in the ears, a wrongness in the water, as if the whole palace were leaning toward some center of gravity that shouldn't exist. The crown feeding. Somewhere above us, King Aldric sat in a gilded chair while a relic drank his life out through his skull, and everyone in the room called it tradition.

I thought about him the way you think about a person you've never met but have already bled for. The seer had shown me his face in visions — or Veyra's memories had shown me, I still wasn't sure whose recollections those were. A boy with ink-stained fingers. A young king laughing at his sister's jokes. And lately, a hollowed man staring at scratches on his own door, counting days he couldn't remember.

"Hartwell." Sera's voice, low, from ahead. "Grate."

The cistern ladder rose out of black water into a maintenance shaft, and at its top, an iron grate that should have been locked. It wasn't. Maren's doing, or the jailer Tam had turned, or simple luck — and I'd stopped believing in simple luck somewhere around the drowned chapel.

Above the grate, the palace opened up like a throat.

* * *

We moved through servant corridors that smelled of tallow and yesterday's bread. The palace was emptier than it had any right to be — the rite pulled everyone, guards included, toward the great hall and the vault beneath it. We passed two maids who took one look at eleven gaunt prisoners and a bleeding mercenary and decided, wisely, to be elsewhere.

Tam navigated by Maren's plans transcribed onto his skin, sleeves rolled, forearms bare, reading his own body like a map. It should have looked ridiculous. It looked like devotion.

"Left here," he murmured. "Then the gallery, then the stair behind the tapestry of the Hunt. Maren marked it 'poorly guarded' with an underline."

"How many underlines?"

"Two."

"Then it's a trap."

"It's a *regent*," Tam said. "Underlines mean she's sorry about it."

The tapestry of the Hunt showed Ashvale kings riding down a white stag through a forest of grey trees. Someone had stitched the stag's eye red, decades ago, in what I chose to read as protest. Behind it, the stair went down.

And waiting at the bottom of the stair, exactly as predicted, stood six of the palace guard in ceremonial black, halberds crossed.

I put up a hand and my people stopped behind me. The corridor was narrow. Bad ground for us, worse for them if it came to blood — but it would come to blood, and these were conscripts, farm boys and second sons given black livery and orders, the same kind of soldiers I'd refused to kill at the Weeping Gate. My shoulder still ached from that mercy.

One of them stepped forward. Young. A scar through his eyebrow, fresh enough to shine.

"You're her," he said. Not hostile. Almost reverent, which was worse. "The one from the prophecy. Hartwell."

"I'm a mercenary with a bad reading habit," I said. "But yes."

"The regent said—" He faltered. Looked back at his fellows. "The regent said you'd come this way. She said to let you pass."

Silence. Eleven ex-prisoners holding their breath. One seer smiling at nothing.

"She said that," I repeated.

"She said—" The guard swallowed. "She said to tell you the vault opens from the east, and the custodians will be nine, and that she is sorry about the Weeping Gate. Those were her words. She made us memorize them."

I felt something crack a little in my chest, some wall I'd built against the arithmetic of Maren's betrayal. She'd sold us once, or tried to. Now she was spending her treason like a woman emptying her purse all at once, because there would be no tomorrow in which she'd need coin.

"Tell her," I said finally, "that mercy keeps better books than vengeance. And get out of this corridor, because in about four minutes it's going to be very unhealthy."

They went. All six, at a pace just short of running.

Mother Vess touched my arm as they passed. "That one," she said softly, meaning the scarred boy, "plants an orchard someday." Then she walked on into the dark, leaving me to wonder, as always, whether her prophecies were comfort or cruelty.

* * *

The east door of the vault was bronze, older than the kingdom, worked with figures I now recognized: a crowned figure seated, and around its head, small devouring mouths rendered in relief so fine you only saw them when the torchlight moved. The crown predates the kingdom itself. Tam's scorched folio had said it, and here it was, carved in metal someone had wanted forgotten.

Sera knelt at the lock and had it open in less time than it takes to tell. She stood, flexed her fingers, and gave me a look I'd learned to read in the prison-fortress: *whatever's behind this door, I've survived worse.* She probably had.

"No plan survives contact," I told them, because it was true and because saying it steadied my own voice. "Keys to me. Everyone else holds the stair. If we fail, burn the archive folios, scatter, live. That's an order."

"You're not our commander," said a prisoner named Doss, who'd been in a cell eleven years and had opinions about authority.

"No," I agreed. "I'm worse. I'm the one the prophecy's about. Move."

The door swung inward on hinges that made no sound at all, which frightened me more than any scream would have.

* * *

The vault was round, and vast, and wrong.

Not vast in size — it was perhaps forty feet across — but vast in the way a well is vast when you're falling down it. The ceiling curved away into darkness overhead. The walls were lined with niches, and in the niches sat thrones, dozens of them, each one occupied.

I'd seen a custodian unmasked once — Maren's letters had described it, and the seer had shown me worse. But seeing one described and seeing thirty of them seated in state are different griefs. They didn't move. They didn't breathe, or breathed so slowly it amounted to the same thing. Old monarchs, hollowed out, kept animate by the crown's leavings, wearing robes that had rotted onto their bodies. Their eyes were open. All of them. Every throne faced the center of the room.

At the center, on a dais of black stone, burned the binding circle — and above it, suspended in nothing, hung the Ashen Crown.

It was smaller than I'd imagined. That was the horror of it. Something that had eaten a god and was eating a king should have been enormous, a cathedral of iron and ash. Instead it was delicate, almost beautiful, a circlet of dark metal filigreed with patterns like frost or veins, turning slowly, turning always, and drinking.

Below it, in a chair of state, sat Aldric.

He was awake. That was the first thing I understood, and the worst. His eyes were open and fixed on the crown above him, and tears ran down his face without his expression changing at all, the way a roof leaks without the house admitting anything's wrong. Around him stood nine figures in grey — the custodians of the present tense, the ones who still walked — robed, masked, arranged in a ring, chanting in a language that predated the kingdom and possibly the language itself.

The rite. The feeding. We'd come at the exact hour, and the exact hour was a table set for dinner, and the king was the meal.

"Keys," I breathed.

Tam was already beside me, unwrapping the bundle we'd carried from the prison-fortress. Salt. Iron. Bone. Three keys that had cost a drowned chapel, a captivity, a betrayal, and more of my memory than I liked to count — I knew that last part only by its absence, holes in my past shaped like people I must have loved. The three pieces of iron-grey metal lay in my palms, warm as living things, and they *pulled*, gently, toward the circle, like compass needles finding north.

"Now or never," I said, and stepped off the edge of everything.

* * *

Here is what the histories will not record, because I will not remember to tell them: crossing that vault was the longest walk of my life, and I have walked through burning cities.

The chanting changed the moment my boot touched the dais. The nine custodians turned as one, masks catching light, and the sound they made was not surprise. Surprise requires a self to be surprised. What they made was a sound like a door closing.

"The vessel," one said. Its voice was dust and honey. "Come to feed herself at last."

"I'm nobody's vessel," I said, which was a lie, and the crown knew it, and laughed — a sound inside my skull rather than my ears, familiar and awful, because I'd heard it before in visions, wearing my own face. *Veyra,* the laughter said. *Little page. Little last leaf.*

The keys burned in my hands. I dropped to my knees at the circle's edge and set them down — salt at the north point, iron at the west, bone at the south, exactly as the seer had taught me in a cell that smelled of rot and hope. For a heartbeat, nothing.

Then the circle woke.

Light ran the grooves of the black stone like fire down a fuse, and the vault filled with a sound I can only call a chord — three notes struck together, salt-iron-bone, the binding opening after a century of silence. The thrones along the wall shuddered. The dead custodians leaned forward in their seats, all of them, all at once, like puppets feeling their strings remembered.

And the crown screamed.

Not aloud. Inside. Inside everyone. I felt Tam stagger behind me, felt the prisoners at the stair cry out, felt the whole palace flinch like a struck animal. The crown had been fed gently for a hundred years, sip by sip, memory by memory, and now the binding circle had opened beneath it and it understood, in whatever way such things understand, that the long dinner was ending.

"Custodians!" I shouted. "Hold the stair! Nobody lets them reach the circle!"

They came anyway. Of course they came. Nine grey figures moving faster than anything that old had a right to move, and my people met them at the mouth of the vault with prison-honed desperation — Sera's knife, Doss's chains still wrapped around one fist, Mother Vess standing untouched in the middle of it, smiling, *listening*, calling out directions to fighters who couldn't see what she saw.

I turned back to the circle. To the king.

Aldric was looking at me.

His eyes had cleared — not fully, not sanely, but enough. Enough to see a stranger kneeling at the binding circle with blood on her hands and three keys laid out like an accusation. Enough to speak.

"You're her," he rasped. "From the book. You came."

"I came," I said. "Your Majesty, I need you to stand up."

"I don't—" His face crumpled, and I watched a man try to find himself in his own head and find mostly vacancies. "I don't know what's left of me to stand with."

"Then borrow mine," I said, and reached across the circle's light for his hand.

Behind me, steel rang on steel. The custodians broke through. And the crown, hanging above its king, began to pour everything it had ever eaten into him at once — a flood, a drowning, a century of stolen lives crashing into one breaking mind — because if it could not keep him, it would spend him.

Aldric screamed, and gripped my hand, and the world went white.

* * *

I write none of what came next, because next is the offering, and the offering is the part of me that's already gone.
