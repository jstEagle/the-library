## 19. The Offering

The binding circle opened like a wound.

I don't mean that poetically. I mean the floor of the vault split along lines that had been carved a thousand years ago and sealed with something that wasn't quite stone and wasn't quite resin, and what came out of the seams was light — pale, hungry, patient light — and it made my teeth ache in a way I'd only ever felt once before, standing at the edge of a frozen lake as the ice began to talk.

Three keys. Salt, iron, bone. They turned in their locks — three locks set into three pillars around the dais where King Aldric of Ashvale knelt with the Ashen Crown burning on his brow — and the circle sang a note so low I felt it in my sternum rather than heard it.

"Is it working?" Tam shouted from behind me. He was crouched over the scorched folio, cross-referencing symbols against the binding rite with the frantic energy of a man who had spent his whole life believing that reading could save him, and who was discovering, right now, at the worst possible moment, that sometimes it couldn't.

"It's doing *something*," I said.

"That's not the same as working!"

"No," I agreed. "It really isn't."

The crown had noticed us. That was the thing nobody warned you about, the thing the prophecy danced around in its maddening, century-old hand: the crown was not an object. It had never been an object. It was a hunger wearing the shape of one, and hungers notice when the food starts moving.

Aldric's head came up. His eyes were wrong — not cruel, not mad, just *full*, stuffed to bursting with other people's lives, other people's mornings, other people's deaths, packed in so tight there was no room left for the man himself. When he looked at me, I felt seen by a library.

"You brought me back," he said, and his voice was a chorus of everyone the crown had ever eaten. "You brought all of them back."

"I'm told that's the idea."

"They're so *loud*." He laughed, and the laugh broke in the middle. "Elodie Hartwell. Veyra's daughter. Do you know how long I've been waiting for you? A hundred years. The crown has been whispering your name to me every night like a prayer said backwards."

Behind him, the custodians came through the vault doors.

There were seven of them left, out of however many there had once been. They moved like men underwater, robes trailing ash, faces hidden behind masks of beaten gold that showed nothing but a smooth, featureless oval. Maren's intelligence had gotten us past the outer defenses; nothing she knew could have prepared anyone for the sound they made when they walked, which was the sound of a page turning very slowly, over and over, forever.

"Hold the circle!" I shouted, and the freed prisoners held it — the blind seer at their center, her ruined eyes aimed at the crown with an expression I can only describe as *recognition*. She had called me the last page. She had known, better than any of us, what book we were trapped inside.

The custodians did not run. They didn't need to. The crown simply *reached*, and the light pouring from the seams in the floor bent toward Aldric like water finding a drain, and the note the circle was singing began to slide, to sour, to become something else entirely.

"The keys aren't enough," the seer said. Her voice was calm in the way of people who have already grieved. "It was never going to be enough. Salt, iron, bone — those open the lock. But the thing inside doesn't live in the lock, girl. It lives in the *mind*. It has to be fed a mind whole enough to hold it. That is the price that was always coming."

I looked at her. She looked back, sightless and certain.

"And whose mind would that be?" I asked, though I already knew. I think I'd known since the drowned chapel, since the wight showed me Veyra's face wearing mine. Since the prison, when using the second key burned away my sister's wedding day and I hadn't even flinched, because some part of me understood I was spending coins I'd promised to spend.

The seer smiled. It was a terrible kindness.

"Whose else?"

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about offering your entire self to a parasitic relic: there is a moment, right at the beginning, when you get to see the menu.

The crown took my offer the way a drowning man takes a rope — instantly, greedily, without grace. And then it *read* me. All of me. Not the way the wight had shown me visions, curated and distant, but everything, all at once, laid out on its tongue like a feast it intended to take its time with.

I felt it taste my mother's kitchen. The smell of bread and tallow smoke. My first kill, a man named Oskar with a wife in Brindle and a debt he couldn't pay, and the way I'd thrown up afterward and sworn never again, and the way I'd done it again anyway, because mercenary work doesn't care about your feelings. Tam's face the night he told me about copying the prophecy, ink-stained and terrified and braver than he knew. The seer calling me the last page. Aldric kneeling in the dark of his own skull for thirty years, scratching days into his door.

And beneath all of it, threaded through everything like gold wire through cheap cloth: Veyra.

Her memories rose in me as the crown pulled, and for the first time I saw them whole instead of in fragments. The silent god had not been silent at all. She had been *screaming* — for a hundred years, inside the relic, inside every vessel it wore, scattered into pieces small enough to hide in mortal blood until one line of them pooled, generation after generation, into me. I felt her ordination. Her doubt. The day she first touched the crown, believing herself strong enough to study it, and the day she realized her mistake, and the hundred years of watching through borrowed eyes while her own name was forgotten by everyone who had ever prayed to her.

She had been alone longer than most kingdoms last.

*I'm sorry,* I thought at her, or at the memory of her, or at whatever remained. *I'm sorry it took this long.*

*Don't be sorry,* something answered, and it might have been memory, might have been her, might have been me. *Be full.*

Because that was the trick. That was the thing the seer had understood and the prophecy had only hinted at, the arithmetic at the bottom of everything: the crown devoured minds because it was starving, but it had never met a mind that came *willingly*. Every king it had eaten had clung, had fought, had hoarded themselves down to scraps — and scraps were exactly what the crown wanted, easy meals, thin gruel. But a mind offered whole, without resistance, without rationing? A mind carrying a god's worth of memories stacked on top of a mercenary's hard, dense, unregretted life?

That wasn't a meal.

That was a flood.

* * *

I want to be honest about the pain, because I think honesty is the last thing I'll ever own, and I'd rather spend it well.

It hurt more than anything. More than the arrow at the Weeping Gate. More than the second key burning my sister's face out of my head. It hurt like being unwritten — like every word I had ever been was being read aloud by something with too many mouths, faster and faster, and each word vanished the moment it was spoken. I felt myself losing things in real time. The smell of bread. Oskar's face. The weight of my sword, which I realized, distantly, I was no longer holding, because my hands had gone somewhere without me.

But here is the other honest thing, the thing I would want someone to tell me if I could still be told anything:

I was not afraid.

Somewhere in the flood, in the terrible abundance of it, the crown began to choke. I felt its hunger hit the limit of itself — the way a fire meets a forest too wet to burn, the way a throat meets more than it can swallow. It had spent a century learning to sip. I gave it an ocean. Veyra's hundred years poured into it alongside mine, and her memories were not passive; they were *hers*, they were divine, they carried the weight of every prayer ever whispered at her altars, and the crown had never evolved to digest a god.

It tried to stop. Relics can't stop. That's the flaw in every hunger: it cannot choose to be less hungry.

Through the roaring, through the unraveling, I heard it crack.

Not metaphorically. There was a sound like glacier ice giving way, high and vast and final, and the light pouring from the floor seams reversed itself, sucked backward, and the crown on Aldric's brow split from rim to peak like an egg, and everything it had stolen for a thousand years came out at once.

I remember — I will always half-remember, the way you half-remember a dream that changed you — the moment of release. Every stolen life flying home to wherever stolen lives go. Veyra rising out of the ruin of the relic like heat off summer stone, vast and grateful and *free*, and the last thing she gave me, deliberately, a parting gift pressed into my emptying hands, was not a memory.

It was a direction. Like a compass needle settling. Like a hand on the small of your back, turning you gently toward home.

Then the crown finished cracking, and Aldric screamed — a human scream, a single man's scream, wonderfully ordinary after all that chorus — and collapsed, and the vault went dark except for the ordinary torches, which suddenly seemed like the most beautiful things I had ever seen.

And I stood there, in the ruin, amid the ash of seven custodians who had crumbled the instant their master died, and I waited to feel like Elodie Hartwell.

Nothing came.

* * *

"Elodie?" Tam's voice, very close, very careful. "Elodie, can you hear me?"

I could hear him. I could hear everything — the prisoners weeping, the seer singing something low and old, Aldric breathing in great ragged pulls like a man surfacing from deep water. What I couldn't do was answer to the name. It sat in the air between us like a coin on a table, and I had no idea whose it was.

"Tam," I said, because the seer had said it, and the guards had said it, and words are things you can borrow even when you can't own them. "What happened?"

He made a sound that was half laugh, half sob. "You won. You absolute — you *won*, do you understand? The crown's dead. Ash. He's free." He was crying. I noted it the way you note weather. "Are you — " He stopped. Started again, gentler. "Do you know who I am?"

I looked at him. Young, ink-stained, tear-tracked, alive. Someone worth knowing, probably. Someone who clearly knew me.

"No," I said.

The word fell into the vault and kept falling. The seer stopped singing. Even Aldric, struggling upright against the broken dais, went still.

"But," I added, because the compass was still there, faint and steady under everything, pointing at this boy, at these people, at whatever came next, "I think you're going to tell me."

Tam wiped his face with the heel of his hand, leaving a fresh smear of soot across the old ink, and knelt in the ash beside me like a man preparing to write on the only surface left in the world.

"Well," he said. "It's a long story. And you're going to need all of it."
