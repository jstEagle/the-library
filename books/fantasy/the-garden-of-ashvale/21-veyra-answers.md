## 21. Veyra Answers

The shrine smelled of lye and fresh-cut pine.

That was the first thing I noticed — the smell, not the god. Three months of scrubbing and carpentry had done what a century of neglect couldn't: the little chapel above the salt mines no longer stank of rot and pigeon droppings. It smelled like work. Honest work, someone else's, which was about all I could claim familiarity with these days.

I stood in the doorway with my boots half in shadow and thought, *I have been here before.* Not remembered it — I want to be precise about that, because precision is the only thing I own outright anymore. I didn't remember. I simply knew it the way you know your own hand is at the end of your arm: without evidence, without gratitude, just fact. My feet had crossed this threshold once. Some other woman's feet. Mine, allegedly.

"Elodie." Tam's voice behind me, careful, the way people spoke to me now — like I was a kettle that might whistle or might crack. "You don't have to do this."

"I know."

"You could wait. The masons aren't finished with the—"

"Tam." I turned. He was holding his satchel against his chest like a shield, ink-stained fingers white on the strap. He'd grown a beard since the prison. It made him look older and more frightened simultaneously, which I hadn't known was possible. "If I wait for everything to be finished, I'll wait forever. That's sort of my whole situation."

He winced. I'd learned to stop apologizing for jokes like that. They were how I held the shape of myself together, and if they cut him a little, well — he'd read me the ledger of who I'd been. He could stand to bleed a drop or two for the privilege.

*Who I'd been.* There's the joke again, wearing its little mask.

I walked down the aisle. The floorboards were new but laid over old stone, and through the gaps between them I could see the original tiles, salt-eaten, still bearing the pattern of waves. Veyra's symbol wasn't a wave, exactly. It was a line drawn under water — the mark of something moving beneath a surface, felt rather than seen. The silent god. The drowned prayer. The one who never answered.

Except that wasn't true either, was it? She hadn't been silent. She'd been *eaten*. And the woman who freed her — the woman whose body I apparently inhabited like a squatter in a fine house — had paid for it with every memory she owned, including the ones that weren't originally hers to pay with.

Tam had explained it eleven times. I kept asking, because each explanation landed on me fresh, like weather. The crown devoured Veyra a century ago. Her divinity scattered. Some of it lodged in bloodlines, in heirlooms, in — this was Tam's favorite part, delivered with scholarly relish — "a heritable mnemonic residue," by which he meant that a mercenary from Thornfell had been walking around with a goddess's leftover thoughts in her skull without ever noticing, the way you don't notice a stone in your boot until you take the boot off.

Then she took the boot off. Willingly. Fed herself to the crown until the crown burst like a glutton at a feast.

And now here I was. Same face, same hands, same stubborn set of the jaw — everyone assured me of the jaw — and nothing behind the eyes but three months of other people telling me stories.

The altar was new pine too, pale as bone. Someone had carved the under-water line into its face, shallow and true. Before it sat a single bowl of salt water, because the old rites called for it and the old rites were all we had. I knelt. My knees found the position without instruction, which unsettled me more than anything else in the room. Muscle memory is a cruel kind of inheritance. It remembers what the mind cannot.

I folded my hands. I stared at the water.

And I realized I had no idea what to say.

* * *

Here is what nobody warns you about praying to a god who owes you everything: the etiquette is impossible.

If I prayed as a stranger — *great Veyra, hear me* — it was a lie, because I was supposedly the closest thing to kin she had left. If I prayed as kin — *it's me, you know me* — that was a worse lie, because I didn't. If I thanked her, I'd be thanking her for a sacrifice I couldn't remember making, which felt like thanking a stranger for a gift addressed to someone else. If I asked for anything, I'd be a beggar at the table of a woman who'd already given the whole pantry.

So I said the only honest thing available to me.

"I don't know why I'm here."

My voice sounded small in the new-timbered room. Salt water shivered in the bowl. Somewhere behind me, Tam pretended very hard to examine a wall.

"I mean, I know why. Everyone tells me why. You were in me, and then I — she — gave you back." I swallowed. "But knowing a story isn't the same as living it. I've heard it so many times I can recite it. I dream about it sometimes, except they're not dreams, they're just Tam's voice reading the prophecy, which is honestly a bit insulting, dreaming about being lectured."

The water didn't move. Gods, in my limited experience, which was entirely secondhand, were not known for their conversational skills.

"But I keep coming back to shrines." I pressed on, because stopping would have been worse. "Three months. Every ruined altar between Ashvale and the coast. I tell myself it's habit, that the body remembers, but I don't think that's it. I think—" My throat tightened, which surprised me; I hadn't authorized that. "I think some part of me is waiting. Not for an answer. Just for proof that it wasn't wasted. That whatever she gave up bought something real. Because from where I'm sitting, the purchase looks like this: a woman with no name she recognizes, kneeling on fresh pine, talking to a bowl."

The candle flames leaned, all together, though there was no draft.

I stopped breathing.

It wasn't much. I want to be clear about that, because later — when the ballads got hold of it, and they did, gods help us all — later it became trumpets and light and the heavens splitting. What actually happened was that six candles tilted the same direction, and the surface of the salt water went perfectly, impossibly still, stiller than water has any right to be, and then—

Warmth. Behind my sternum. Like swallowing sunlight, like standing near a hearth after years in the cold, like — and this comparison arrived whole, unbidden, in a voice that was not mine and yet somehow had always been mine — *like coming up for air.*

"Oh," I said.

*I am here.*

Not words. I need you to understand it wasn't words. It was meaning arriving directly, the way you know a room's mood when you walk into it, the way you know someone is crying in the next room by the quality of the silence. But if I had to translate it, if I had to do what Tam does and pin living things to paper, it would come out: *I am here. I am small. I am trying.*

"You're—" My hands were shaking. I gripped them together. "You're really—"

*Grateful,* came the warmth, and it was so enormous and so gentle that my eyes flooded. *Grateful. Grateful. Grateful.* A god repeating herself like a person fumbling for the right coin. A century of silence, and the first word out of her was thank-you.

"Don't," I managed. "Don't thank me. I didn't do anything. She did. The other one. I'm just—" I laughed, wet and ugly. "I'm the receipt."

The warmth shifted. Became something almost like amusement, almost like sorrow, the two braided tight. And then it did the thing I will spend the rest of my life failing to describe properly.

It looked at me. All of me. The hollowed-out interior, the rooms with no furniture, the halls where my memories should have hung and didn't. And instead of pity — I braced for pity, I'd had three months of pity and I could have built a fort with it — instead there was recognition. The way you recognize a house you grew up in, even gutted. Even burned.

*You are not empty,* she said, or meant, or was. *You are cleared ground.*

"That's a very pretty way of saying I've got nothing upstairs."

*Cleared ground grows things.*

The warmth deepened. And then, slowly, carefully, with the delicacy of someone handling a wounded bird, she began to give.

Not memories. I want that on the record, chiseled in stone, because Tam wrote it down wrong the first time and I made him strike it. She did not give me back a single moment of my past. Not the convoy, not Ferro Vance's crooked smile, not the drowned chapel, not the prison, not the vault, not the crown cracking open like an egg. None of it. Those belonged to the dead woman, and dead women keep their wages.

What she gave was underneath memory. Deeper. The layer that memory is written on.

She gave me the knowledge that I flinch left, always left, when something comes at my face — and that this was earned, not taught. She gave me the certainty that I hate drowning, fear it down to the marrow, and that this fear had once been a compass pointing toward a chapel under a lake, and that following it had saved a king. She gave me the shape of my own mercy — that I will not kill conscripts defending their homes, that I will trade blood for surrender every time, that this is not weakness, it never was, it is the load-bearing wall of whoever I am. She gave me stubbornness like bedrock. She gave me the specific texture of loving people quickly and completely and being embarrassed about it afterward.

None of it came with dates. None of it came with faces. It was a compass, not a map. It pointed home without telling me where home was.

But oh, it pointed.

* * *

When I came back to myself, I was sitting on the floor beside the altar, and Tam was crouched next to me with his satchel open and a blank page ready, because of course he was. The man documents grief the way other men breathe.

"The candles," he said. His voice cracked on the second word. "Elodie. The candles stayed lit for — I counted — and the water, the water went still, and then there was light under the door, there was light under the *ground*, I saw it through the floor seams—"

"Did you write it down?"

"What?"

"The light under the ground. Did you write it down?"

He blinked at me. Then he laughed — a startled, broken sound — and pulled me into a hug that smelled of ink and woodsmoke and three months of shared exile. "You're making jokes. You're sitting in the presence of a returned god and you're making jokes."

"She started it." I patted his back. "She's very warm. For a drowned deity."

We stayed like that for a while. The candles burned straight. The salt water in the bowl had gone clear as glass, and when I finally looked into it, I saw my own face — and for the first time since waking in a healer's cot with a head full of fog, the face didn't look like a stranger's. It looked like mine. Whatever that meant. Whatever it was starting to mean.

"Can I ask what she said?" Tam had his pencil poised. "For the record. For the archive. People will want—"

"People will want trumpets and light."

"There *was* light under the ground!"

"People will get the light." I stood, knees cracking, thirty-some years old in a body that felt newly issued. "They don't get the rest. The rest is mine."

He lowered the pencil. Something moved across his face — hurt, briefly, then understanding, then something gentler than both. "Okay," he said. "Off the record."

"She said I'm not empty." I looked at the altar, at the under-water line carved in the pine, at the bowl of clear water holding a small bright reflection of the ceiling beams. "She said I'm cleared ground. And she gave me—" I searched for it, and found it, and it was there, solid as a keel. "She gave me my bearings. Not my past. My bearings. Apparently those are different things."

"Are they?"

"I used to know," I said. "Ask the other me."

* * *

We walked out of the shrine into late afternoon, the kind of gold light that makes even salt mines look forgiving. The path down from the chapel wound through gorse and old slag heaps, and below us Thornfell sprawled in its usual state of organized chaos — carts, arguments, the smell of frying onions, a city rebuilding itself one grudge at a time now that the custodians' long shadow had lifted.

Tam talked the whole way down. He was good at that, filling silence, and I'd learned to let him, because his chatter was a rope I could hold while I climbed back up into myself after moments like the one in the shrine. He was describing — I caught maybe a third of it — the plans for the garden at Ashvale. The palace garden, poisoned by ash for as long as anyone could remember, that Aldric had ordered cleared and replanted the week after the crown broke. Maren was overseeing it. There was to be a ceremony. There was, Tam said with the delicacy of a man defusing something, to be a tree planted.

"For her," he said. "For the woman you were. If you wanted. Nobody expects—"

"I'll come."

He stopped walking. Gorse scratched at our ankles. Somewhere downhill, a cart driver swore magnificently at a mule.

"You'll—really? You said last month you'd rather be shot."

"Last month I didn't have my bearings." I kept walking, and after a moment he caught up. "Also, I want to see it. The garden. Everyone says it's blooming. Everyone says ash doesn't bloom, and it's blooming anyway, and I keep thinking—" I paused at the bend in the path, where the whole valley opened up, sea-light and smoke and spring. "I keep thinking that sounds familiar."

Tam looked at me for a long moment. Then he smiled, small and real, and dug into his satchel, and produced — because he was Tam, and he always, always had it — the scorched folio from the burning archive, its edges black as burnt toast, the prophecy's final lines still legible along the margin.

"I haven't read you the end yet," he said. "You always stop me."

"I know. I'm not going to stop you this time."

He unfolded it carefully, the way you handle a thing that has already survived one fire and shouldn't have to survive another. The wind picked up. Below us, Thornfell clattered and smoked and lived. Above us, in a small pine-smelling shrine, six candles burned straight in a room where a god had finally, after a hundred years, said thank you.

"'And she will not remember,'" Tam read, "'and it will be enough.'"

The wind carried the words out over the valley. And somewhere in my chest, in the cleared ground where a goddess had told me to grow, something took root at the sound of them — not a memory, not quite, but the sure and sudden knowledge that the woman who fed herself to a crown had heard this line too, a century before I was born, and had walked toward it anyway.

"It will be enough," I repeated.

And for the first time, I believed it.
