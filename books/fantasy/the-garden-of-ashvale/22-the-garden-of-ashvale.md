## 22. The Garden of Ashvale

### Aldric

There is a particular kind of terror in waking up and remembering everything.

I had spent so long bracing for the gaps — the missing hours, the missing years, the missing faces — that when they did not come, I lay in bed like a man waiting for the floor to open. Dawn came. The light came through the east windows and fell across the floorboards, and I remembered falling asleep, and before that I remembered supper, and before that the council meeting where Lord Hesse had droned on about grain tariffs until even Maren's legendary patience began to audibly creak.

Nothing was missing. Nothing at all.

I got up and walked to the mirror and looked at myself the way you look at a stranger who has been following you through a market. There were new lines around my eyes. There was grey coming in at my temples that I had apparently earned while I wasn't watching. Time had been happening to me all along; it simply hadn't been reporting to management.

The crown sat in its case in the vault below, cracked down the middle like an eggshell from which something had finally hatched or died — the scholars still argue about which. I go down and look at it sometimes. Not often. But sometimes, because I think a king should be required to look at the thing that ate him, the way a man who has survived drowning should occasionally stand at the edge of the water and say: *I know what you are.*

It doesn't whisper anymore. Whatever lived in it is gone — spent, broken, or perhaps, if Elodie's theory holds, *full* for the first time in a thousand years, gorged past hunger into something like peace. I choose to believe that last one. It makes the vault easier to visit.

Maren found me there once, early on, standing over the shattered relic with my arms crossed like a disappointed schoolmaster.

"You could have it melted down," she said. "The smiths would do it tomorrow."

"And lose the only honest lesson this palace owns?" I said. "No. Let it sit there. Let every custodian-in-training walk past it and see what devotion to a hungry thing looks like when it finishes eating."

She had laughed — a short, startled sound, the laugh of someone remembering how. My sister has been learning a great many things again lately. How to sleep without one ear open. How to want things out loud. How to be loved by her brother without first checking whether he remembers loving her.

Because here is the truth I carry now, heavier than any crown: I remember almost nothing of the years the thing took from me. They are gone the way a river is gone once it reaches the sea — not destroyed, exactly, just no longer anywhere you can point to. Maren remembers them for me. She tells me stories of the boy I was, and I listen the way you listen to tales of a brave cousin who died young, grateful and grieving and unable to claim the grief as fully my own.

But the mornings since — those are mine. Every tedious tariff meeting, every bad harvest report, every evening Maren beats me at cards and gloats about it with unregal delight. All mine. Nobody eats them in the night. Nobody resets me at dawn like a clock that keeps lying about the hour.

This morning I did not go to the vault. I went to the garden.

You should understand what the garden was. For six generations it had been the pride of Ashvale and a lie told in flowers — beautiful above, poisoned beneath, the soil so saturated with ash-rot from the old forges that nothing grown there could be eaten and little could even bloom without sorcery propping it up. The custodians had liked it that way. A garden that only lived because they permitted it. A metaphor they tended personally.

Elodie broke whatever was feeding the rot. Nobody can explain precisely how — the scholars write very long papers about resonance and relic-death and the dispersal of consumed essence back into the land, and I nod along, and privately I suspect the answer is simpler: some things fester in the dark until someone walks in carrying enough light, and then they stop.

The garden stopped festering. And then, this spring, it did something no gardener alive had seen it do.

It bloomed.

Not the pale, sickly, sorcery-sustained blooms of my father's reign. Real flowers. Reckless ones. Roses climbing the old trellises as if making up for lost centuries, lavender running riot along the paths, fruit trees — actual fruit trees — blossoming white and pink against the wall where the ash-orchards used to stand. The gardeners wept. I am told the head gardener, a flint-hearted woman named Bess who has not cried since the war, sat down in the dirt among the new crocuses and sobbed like a child.

I walked the paths this morning while the palace slept, and the bees were already at work, and the air smelled sweet in a way this hill has not smelled in living memory, and I thought: *this is what I rule now.* Not the kingdom of ash and careful forgetting. This. Something that grows because it wants to.

Maren joined me at the far gate, wrapped in a robe, holding two cups of tea, looking rumpled and human and entirely un-regal.

"The council will be scandalized," she said, handing me a cup. "A king walking in his own garden before breakfast. Unheard of."

"Let them be scandalized. It builds character."

"Yours or theirs?"

"Both, ideally." I drank the tea. It was good tea. I would remember it tomorrow. That, too, felt like a small miracle, and I intended to keep collecting them.

We stood together in the blossoming ruin of our ancestors' vanity, and after a while Maren said, quietly, "She'll be here today. To plant it."

"I know."

"Are you nervous?"

"Aldric the king isn't," I said. "Aldric the man who owes her everything he can't remember losing is terrified."

My sister bumped her shoulder against mine, the way she used to when we were children, before either of us knew what the palace kept in its basement. "Good," she said. "Terrified is honest. We've had enough of smooth."

### Maren

I betrayed my brother. I want that written somewhere true, not smoothed away by the historians who are already at work sanding me into marble.

I handed a stranger to monsters because the arithmetic said it might save him. I told myself a story about how I could still pull everyone out of the fire, and the story was half true, which is the most dangerous fraction there is. If Elodie Hartwell had died at the Weeping Gate instead of escaping wounded — if Tam had rotted in those cells below the palace — I would be a regent of a hollow kingdom right now, smiling at a brother who didn't know his own name, and I would have deserved it.

Instead I got forgiveness, which is much harder to live with.

Aldric forgave me in eleven words, the morning after it ended. He was bruised, half-drowned in borrowed memories, sitting on the vault steps with his head in his hands, and I knelt in front of him ready to confess everything, rehearsing it, and he looked at me and said: "You came back. That's the part I'm keeping."

Eleven words. He doesn't remember saying them — that hour is one of the fogged ones, the price the crown had already collected before it broke. So I keep the sentence for both of us. I have written it down. I keep it in a drawer with the other things I've decided never to let this family forget again.

The custodians are scattered. Some knelt the moment their masters' masks came off and the rot underneath was visible; some ran; a few fought and were put down. The ones who surrendered told us things in exchange for leniency, and the things they told us turned my stomach — centuries of managed forgetting, kings raised like livestock, whole dynasties pruned and replanted to keep the relic fed. We published all of it. Every page. Tam insisted, and Tam was right: the cure for deliberate forgetting is deliberate, stubborn, public remembering. Let the archives choke on it.

Some days I miss the old efficiency. That is the shameful confession of this chapter of my life: there were mornings, in the first weeks, when the kingdom seemed to be held together with string and apology, and I thought, *the machine worked, at least.* Then I would walk through the garden, or watch Aldric laugh at something small, or see Elodie standing blank-eyed in a courtyard trying to learn her own face, and the shame would arrive on schedule and do its work.

The machine ate people. String and apology feed them. I know which I'd rather govern.

Today the garden is full of people — courtiers pretending not to stare, gardeners trying to herd the ceremony into some kind of order, children who have been let out of the nursery specifically to witness history and are therefore climbing the trellises. In the center of it all, a hole has been dug in the richest soil any of us have ever touched, and beside the hole sits a sapling in a clay pot: a rowan, red-berried, stubborn, the kind of tree that grows out of stone walls if you let it.

Bess the head gardener chose it. "For a mercenary," she said, "you don't want anything delicate. You want something that survives being cut back and comes up meaner and prettier every time."

Elodie arrives mid-morning, walking beside Tam, and the crowd does the thing crowds always do around her now — parts, hushes, stares, whispers the word *saint* in tones of varying sincerity. She hates it. I can tell she hates it because her jaw sets in a way I have learned to read, though she herself cannot yet read it, since the woman who set that jaw is someone she knows only by reputation.

That is the strangest arithmetic of all, the one I still haven't solved: she gave away every memory of the journey, and the rest of us kept ours. She built the peace we're standing in and cannot remember building it. She saved my brother and meets him each time as a stranger. And she carries it — the blankness, the borrowed testimony, the legend growing around her like ivy — with more grace than I ever managed carrying far smaller burdens.

When she reaches the hole in the earth, Aldric steps forward, and the king bows to the mercenary, deeply, publicly, in front of every gossip in Ashvale, and says something I don't catch, and she laughs — startled, rusty, real.

Then they plant the tree together. The woman she was goes into the ground as a rowan sapling, and the woman she is becoming pats the soil down around it with dirt-blackened hands, and the crowd cheers, and I stand at the edge of it all doing the slow work of letting myself deserve to witness it.

### Tam

Here's what nobody tells you about saving the world: afterward, everybody wants a piece of the story, and nobody wants to hear the boring true version, which is mostly walking, worrying, and copying things down.

I was a runaway scribe. I am now — and I quote the Chancellor of the restored archive — "an asset of considerable historical significance," which is archive-speak for *we will pay you a salary you cannot refuse to organize the mess you helped make.* I have an office. It has a window. The window looks out on the garden. I suspect this is not an accident; I suspect the Chancellor noticed how often I drift toward it.

My job, formally, is the Custodian Records: forty-one crates of confiscated documents, three centuries of managed forgetting, and the job of turning horror into footnotes. My job, informally, is Elodie.

Not — let me be clear, because the ballad-writers have gotten entirely out of hand — in the way the ballads imply. The ballads have me as her devoted chronicler-companion, steadfast through fire and prison, and yes, fine, the broad strokes are accurate, but the ballads also have her slaying a guardian wight with a song, which did not happen, and give me a tragic backstory involving a dead twin, which I resent both for being false and for being dramatically superior to my actual backstory, which involves a disapproving father and an unpaid apprenticeship.

No — my job with Elodie is testimony. She asked me for it, months ago, in the smallest voice I'd ever heard from her. We were sitting on the wall by the training yard, and she said, "Everyone keeps telling me what I did. Nobody tells me who I was." And I said, "Those aren't different questions," and she looked at me with those flat grey eyes that hold absolutely nothing of the woman I marched beside, and I understood I had just been given the most important commission of my life.

So I keep the record. Not the legend — the person.

*Elodie Hartwell, entry 114: afraid of deep water. Would not admit it. Once took a ferry detour of four hours rather than cross a swollen ford, and blamed the horse.*

*Entry 115: merciful to a fault that was nearly fatal, repeatedly. Refused to kill conscripts at the Weeping Gate when killing them would have been easy and safe. Called it strategy. It was mercy wearing strategy's coat.*

*Entry 116: stubbornness as a primary element, like fire or water. Argued with a blind seer. Argued with a fence. Argued with me. Won approximately sixty percent of arguments, which she counted, because she counted everything.*

*Entry 117: laughed at her own jokes before finishing them, which ruined roughly half of them.*

I read her entries when she asks. She takes notes. She has a notebook now — a real scribe's notebook, good paper, and she writes in a careful, unpracticed hand, building herself entry by entry, testimony by testimony, a woman constructing her own foundation and inspecting every stone.

Today, at the planting, she finds me by the wall where I always stand at gatherings — scribes gravitate to walls; walls have good sightlines and low social obligations — and she says, "Read me the last line. Of the prophecy. I keep almost remembering it, which I know is impossible, but it sits on the tip of my mind like a word in another language."

So I take out the folio — the scorched one, the one I pulled from the burning archive with my eyebrows briefly on fire, the one that started as evidence and became scripture — and I find the final lines, and I read them to her under the new rowan tree, in front of the king and the regent and the whole assembled curiosity of Ashvale:

"*And she will give what no thief can take, and break what no hammer can break, and she will not remember, and it will be enough.*"

Silence. Bees. Somewhere a child falls off a trellis and is caught by a gardener and immediately tries again.

"It will be enough," Elodie repeats. She turns the phrase over like a coin she's checking for clipping. "Was it?"

And I — who have spent my life believing that words are the only durable thing, that ink outlasts flesh, that memory is the whole treasure — I look at this woman who traded every memory she had and stands here anyway, upright, curious, kind, *choosing* herself anew from other people's accounts, and I say the truest thing I have ever written or spoken:

"No," I say. "It was more than enough. Enough would have been surviving. You got everyone else through too. That's abundance. That's a surplus." I tap the scorched folio. "The prophecy undersold you. Prophecies usually do. They're written by people who've never met the subject."

She laughs — entry 117, ruining the joke before she's made it, except this time it isn't a joke, it's just joy arriving ahead of schedule — and she says, "Write that down, scribe," and I do, right there, kneeling in the dirt with the folio on my knee, adding a new entry to the record of who Elodie Hartwell is:

*Entry 118: asks better questions than the prophecy did.*

### Elodie

They tell me I planted a tree today.

I know because my hands are dirty and there is a rowan sapling behind me and a hundred witnesses, and because Tam wrote it down, and Tam's word is currently load-bearing in the architecture of my self. That's the arrangement. That's the deal I struck with the world: I gave it everything, and it gives me back myself in installments, secondhand, annotated.

People think that must be agony. It was, at first — the first weeks were a howling dark, waking in a strange bed in a strange city with everyone calling me *savior* and me not knowing my own surname until a nursemaid told me. But agony needs something to press against, and I had nothing. What I had instead was emptiness, and emptiness, it turns out, is just a room before the furniture arrives.

So I furnished it. Slowly. Deliberately. From testimony.

I am told I was stubborn. Good — I use that daily.

I am told I was merciful. Harder to verify, but I find that when strangers ask me for help, something in me moves before I've decided anything, and I've decided to trust that reflex. It seems to come from somewhere reliable.

I am told I was afraid of drowning, and I am, still, viscerally, though I've never drowned in any life I can recall — which means the fear itself outlived the memories that fed it, and I find that oddly comforting. Some things survive the crossing. Maybe I'm one of them.

And I am told — by the seer's letters, by Tam, by the priests who rebuilt the altar — that I carried a god inside me. Fragments of Veyra, scattered through my blood like seeds in winter soil. I don't remember her. But some nights I dream of a garden I've never walked, silver-leaved, silent, and in the dream I am not a visitor there. I am the ground it grows from.

I went to the altar last month without knowing why. My feet decided. I knelt in the rebuilt shrine where the silence had lived for a century, and I prayed the way you hum a song you can't name, and for the first time in a hundred years, something answered.

Not words. Veyra doesn't waste words. What came back was warmth, and a sense of being *known* — known all the way down, by someone who had been all the way down with me — and one impression, clear as a bell struck once:

*Thank you. Now grow.*

So that's the project. Growing.

The rowan is in the ground now, and the crowd has dispersed, and the king and his sister have gone back to the business of ruling a kingdom that is learning, slowly, painfully, to stop lying to itself. Tam is packing up his folio, muttering about crate nineteen and the misfiled custodian ledgers. The bees are closing up shop for the evening. The garden smells of lavender and turned earth and the green, insistent smell of things that intend to keep going.

I stay behind a while, alone with the tree, the way you'd sit with a grave, except this grave is planted upside-down and everything buried in it is going to come up flowering.

"Hello," I say to it. To her. To me. "I'm told you were worth remembering."

The wind moves through the new leaves, and the leaves make the sound leaves make, which is not an answer, and also, somehow, is one.

I don't remember the woman who bought this peace with everything she had. But I get to decide what to do with it. And I have decided — entry by entry, testimony by testimony, stone by inspected stone — that whoever I build out of her sacrifice will be someone she'd have approved of.

Stubborn. Merciful. Afraid of deep water, and crossing it anyway.

The sun goes down over Ashvale, over the palace and the garden and the vault where a broken crown sits quiet at last, and I walk back up the path through the blossoms, and I do not look back, because everything behind me is safely recorded, and everything ahead of me is mine.
