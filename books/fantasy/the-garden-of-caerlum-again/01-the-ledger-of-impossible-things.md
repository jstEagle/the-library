## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The Vellum Guild kept its ledgers in three copies: one for the guildmaster, one for the fireproof cabinet in the counting room, and one for Orsen Halloran's own steady hand, because the first two had a regrettable tendency to disagree with each other.

Orsen sat at his desk in the under-vault with a pot of ink the color of dried plums, a fresh quill he had already sharpened twice out of nervousness rather than need, and the great open ledger titled *Inventory, Impossible* — which was not its official title but was what every keeper for two hundred years had written on the spine anyway, because accuracy mattered more than dignity.

He was halfway through the autumn audit, and the autumn audit was going wrong.

Not badly. Not in any way a magistrate or a tax assessor would notice. But wrong in the way that made the hairs on his forearms stand up and take an interest in proceedings.

"Item four hundred eleven," he read aloud, because saying things aloud made them realer, and some of the things in this vault needed all the reality they could get. "One memory, bottled, contents unknown. Donor deceased. Buyer pending. Condition: *restless.*"

Restless. He looked up at the shelf where item 411 sat — a squat green vial stoppered with wax, wedged between a jar of someone's childhood summer (contents: lake water, cicadas, a grandmother's laugh) and a long black phial labeled only *the night I decided to leave her*, which no buyer had ever touched, for obvious reasons.

The green vial was rattling. Gently. Politely, even. Like a guest tapping a spoon against a teacup.

Memories did not rattle. That was rather the point of bottling them. A properly extracted memory sat still as pond water until it was poured into a willing skull, whereupon it became the owner's problem. In nine years as keeper of ledgers, Orsen had catalogued griefs, joys, one entire wedding day sold by a groom who'd decided marriage was easier without the memory of promising anything, and a forty-year feud between two brothers sold separately by each brother so that neither would have to remember why they weren't speaking. None of them had ever so much as sloshed.

Orsen dipped his quill, hesitated, and wrote in the margin, in his small precise hand: *Vial agitated. Cause unknown. Recommend observation.*

Then, because he was honest even when honesty was inconvenient, he added: *Recommend observation soon.*

* * *

The under-vault of the Vellum Guild occupied three floors beneath a respectable stationer's shop on Quillmonger's Lane, and it smelled the way all the guild's holdings smelled: candlewax, old paper, and something faintly sweet that newcomers always mistook for honey and veterans knew was the smell of memories aging in glass. Old memories mellowed. Bitter ones turned to vinegar. There was a whole sub-cellar of vinegar memories that apprentices were forbidden to enter alone, on the grounds that a broken jar of concentrated resentment could sour the temper of everyone on the floor for a week.

Orsen walked the aisles with his ledger under his arm, ticking items against their entries, and tried very hard to be only a bookkeeper.

That was the trick of it. Nine years of practice. You did not think about what you were cataloguing. You thought about *quantities*. Vials, stoppers, wax seals intact or compromised. You thought about provenance and pricing and the guild's commission. You did not think about the fact that jar 88 held the last living memory anyone had of a village called Fenmarch, which had burned with all its people twenty years ago, and that somewhere in the world there might be a person who would give everything they owned to smell woodsmoke and hear their mother call them in to supper one more time.

You especially did not think about the small lead-lined box on the third shelf of aisle nine, third row from the left, between a bottle of a soldier's courage (sold; the man had become a pacifist afterward, to everyone's surprise) and a delicate crystal flask of a woman's first love (unsold; she'd asked too high a price, and who could blame her).

Orsen stopped in front of aisle nine anyway. He always did. It was the closest thing he had to a ritual, and Orsen Halloran was a man who understood rituals better than most, being in the business of selling them.

The box was plain. Unlabeled. In the ledger it appeared as item 3,000 and something-or-other — he'd chosen the number deliberately, buried in the middle of a run of miscellaneous wares: *assorted curiosities, value negligible.* Inside the box, wrapped in wool and packed in ash to keep it cold and quiet, lay a single vial of grey glass with no shine to it at all.

His own memory. Stolen from himself, eleven years ago, by a thief who had been very good at his trade and had wept the entire time he worked.

He didn't know what was in it. That was the arrangement. He knew only what he'd told the extraction priest at the time — *take it, all of it, whatever it costs* — and he knew the shape of the hole it had left, the way you know the shape of a missing tooth. Certain rooms in his mind were unfurnished. Certain years were simply gone, like pages razored out of a ledger, and he had learned to walk around the gaps without looking down.

He had never once, in eleven years, opened the box.

He stood in front of it now for exactly as long as he always did — long enough to confirm it was there, not so long that he started wondering — and then he squared his shoulders, adjusted his cuffs, and continued the audit like a sensible man.

Which was when he heard the singing.

* * *

It came from the reliquary hall, the oldest part of the under-vault, the part the guild pretended was a storage annex for "ceremonial stock." Orsen followed the sound with his ledger clutched to his chest like a shield, past the vinegar cellar, past the cold room where the guild kept memories of deaths (there was a market for those; Orsen tried not to think about which end of the transaction he found worse), down the worn steps into a low stone chamber lit by a hundred niches cut into the walls.

Each niche held a prayer candle.

This was the guild's founding shame and its founding fortune, depending on which history you read. Before the Vellum Guild traded in memories, it had served them — had been, in the old days, the keeping-house of the gods themselves. When mortals prayed, the old gods took the prayers and stored them here, in wax and flame, and the priests of the guild tended the candles and carried the prayers up the mountain. Then, some three centuries ago, the gods had gone silent — every one of them, all across the realm, on the same night, for reasons no scholar had ever satisfactorily explained — and the guild's priests had done what institutions do.

They'd started selling the inventory.

A stored prayer, it turned out, could be unbraided into its components: hope, desperation, longing, faith. Each component had its uses. Hope went into tonics. Longing went into love-charms, which were illegal in four provinces and the guild's most reliable revenue stream. And pure faith — distilled, concentrated, undiluted faith — was worth more per ounce than gold, because a single drop of it could convince a dying man he'd be remembered fondly, and dying men paid anything for that.

The candles had burned down over the centuries, one by one, as the guild harvested them. Most niches were empty now. A few held stubs, preserved behind glass like museum pieces. The guildmaster liked to bring wealthy clients down here and let them believe the candles were still holy, which was technically fraud and practically excellent business.

Every candle in the hall had been dark for three hundred years.

Except one.

Orsen stood at the mouth of the hall and stared at the far wall, at niche sixty-one, where a thin white candle burned with a small, steady, utterly impossible flame.

He checked the ledger first. It was reflex; it was also cowardice, and he knew it. *Niche 61,* said the oldest volume, in handwriting two centuries dead. *Prayer of Maren of the Ash Orchards, offered for the safe return of her son from the war of the Broken Crown. Status: UNANSWERED. God: unnamed. Note:* the note was cramped, urgent, pressed hard enough into the page to tear it, *note: the god has not spoken in six years. The candle gutters. We do not know who keeps it lit.*

Nobody kept it lit. Orsen had personally audited this hall every season for nine years. Niche 61 had been cold wax and darkness every single time, and he had recorded it as such, in triplicate, because recording it otherwise would have meant believing something.

And now it burned. Small and white and patient, like a lamp left in a window for someone expected home.

And it was singing. Not aloud — the sound came less through his ears than through his teeth, through the floor, through the ink in his pot, which had begun to tremble in fine concentric rings. A wordless hum, low and sweet and terribly sad, the sound a mother makes over a cradle when she thinks no one is listening.

Orsen did the only thing his profession had trained him to do. He opened the ledger, uncapped his ink, and wrote:

*Niche 61: candle alight. Duration of ignition unknown. No staff assigned. No wick replacement on record. Flame does not consume wax — see attached sketch.*

He sketched it. His hand shook only slightly, and he chose to be proud of that.

Then he sat down on the cold step and watched the candle burn, because it seemed rude to do otherwise, and because his legs had informed him they were not currently accepting instructions.

Three hundred years, he thought. Three hundred years the gods had been silent, and the guild had grown fat in the silence, carving up the prayers of the faithful like a feast left unattended. And now a candle that should have been dust was burning in the dark, humming a lullaby for a boy who had died in a war before Orsen's grandfather was born.

Either the candle was lying, or everything else was.

* * *

"You're sitting in the dark talking to inventory again."

Orsen startled badly enough that his quill left a plum-colored streak across his cuff. He looked up to find Tam Broadstairs, the junior keeper, descending the steps with a lantern and the particular expression of a young man who has rehearsed a joke and intends to deliver it regardless of atmosphere.

"The candle in sixty-one is lit," Orsen said.

"Yes." Tam set the lantern down. "I saw. I've seen it for three days."

Orsen went very still. "Three days."

"I mentioned it to Master Corvane. He said—" Tam shifted his weight. "He said to leave it be. Said old candles sometimes catch, drafts and such, nothing to trouble the audit over."

"Drafts," Orsen repeated. He gestured at the hall, at the sealed niches, at the air so still it felt embarrassed to be moving at all. "In here."

"He's the guildmaster," Tam said, with the tone of a man concluding an argument he'd already lost with himself. "Anyway. Message for you. There's a client come up top. Big one. Veiled, private parlor, offering stupid money." He paused, savoring it. "For a royal memory."

Orsen closed the ledger slowly. Somewhere behind his ribs, in one of the unfurnished rooms, something turned over in its sleep.

"A royal memory," he said. "We have perhaps six royal items. All restricted. All requiring the guildmaster's seal and a writ from—"

"That's the strange bit." Tam lowered his voice, though there was no one to hear; the candle's lullaby seemed to swallow other sounds. "Corvane's already agreed. Didn't check the writ, didn't check the seals. Just pulled the lot number himself and told me to fetch it." He grimaced. "Lot 77. The king's."

The king's. Aldric Caerlum's. The memory the crown itself had sold — or so the ledger claimed — decades ago, entered in a hand Orsen had always privately admired for its elegance, back when he'd first taken the post and studied his predecessors' penmanship the way other men studied swordwork.

He had never quite managed to make his own writing look like theirs. He'd told himself it didn't matter.

Behind him, in niche sixty-one, the little flame leaned — just slightly, just once — as if turning to listen.

Orsen stood, brushed off his coat, and picked up the ledger.

"Well," he said, in the calm professional voice he used for clients, for catastrophes, and for the box in aisle nine. "Let's go meet our buyer."

The candle sang him up the stairs.
