## 2. Prayers Answered

The god of the Ashen Shrine had not spoken in three hundred years, and Sefa had begun to suspect he was a heavy sleeper.

She said as much to the candle rack every morning, because a priestess who talked to her god was devout, and a priestess who talked to her candle rack was merely eccentric, and Sefa had long ago decided eccentricity was the more honest vocation.

"Good morning, Vaelun-of-the-Deep-Roots," she murmured, trimming the wicks in the god's honor niche. "Still nothing? Still resting your ancient bones while I sweep up after pilgrims who come here mostly for the shade?"

The niche held a stone face worn nearly smooth by centuries of devotion — or possibly by weather; scholars disagreed, and Sefa privately suspected the scholars were guessing too. The mouth was a shallow groove. The eyes were two dents. Once, the priests of this shrine had read omens in those dents the way farmers read clouds. Now the dents read as nothing at all, and the prayers of the faithful went into the offering bowl and sat there like coins in a well that had lost its bottom.

Three hundred years of silence. The old gods had gone quiet all across Caerlum in the same generation — the histories blamed a plague, an eclipse, a war, depending on which history you'd been paid to copy. The shrines remained. The orders remained, thinning year over year like good broth stretched for one more guest. And the prayers continued, because people pray the way rivers run downhill: without requiring results.

Sefa had taken her vows at fourteen, partly out of faith and partly because the shrine fed its priestesses, and she had never once regretted it, though she had occasionally resented it on Tuesdays. She was thirty-one now, quick-witted, broad-handed, with a laugh that startled visitors and a habit of talking to everything — candles, brooms, the shrine cat, a fat gray creature named Doctrine whom she'd found in the woodpile and named out of spite.

"Doctrine," she said now, "we have a beggar at the gate."

Doctrine opened one eye, considered this information, and closed it again.

The beggar was not unusual. What was unusual was what he prayed for.

* * *

His name was Tammas, and he came limping through the shrine gate just past noon, when the light fell gold through the ash trees and the courtyard smelled of warm dust. He was perhaps sixty, dressed in the layered rags of a man who owned his clothes longer than most men owned houses. His left leg dragged. His hands shook — not the shaking of drink, Sefa noted, but the fine tremor of someone in pain who has made peace with it the way you make peace with weather.

He didn't ask for money. That was the first strange thing. Beggars almost always asked for money, and Sefa gave it when she had it, because the god wasn't using it.

Instead Tammas lowered himself onto the courtyard bench with the care of a man setting down something fragile, looked up at the worn stone face above the door, and said:

"I don't want much. I know You're busy. Or asleep. Or gone, maybe, and if so, no offense meant." He shifted his bad leg. "But my daughter's boy is sick. Fever, four days now. The herb-wife's done what she can. And I thought — well. Everyone says the old gods don't answer anymore. But everyone also said my Rosa would never speak after the pox took her hearing, and last spring she sang at her own wedding, so everyone can go hang."

He fumbled in his coat and produced an offering: a single dried fig, slightly squashed, wrapped in a leaf.

"It's not much," he said to the stone face. "But it's honest."

Then he bowed his head, and Sefa, standing in the doorway with her sweeping brush, felt something she had no word for yet — a pressure behind her ears, like the moment before thunder arrives, when the air goes strange and the birds go quiet.

She had felt the old texts describe it. *The hush before the Voice*, the Third Canticle called it. She had always assumed it was poetry.

The fig in Tammas's hand began to glow.

Not dramatically. Not with fire or trumpets. It warmed, the way bread warms, the way a stone warmed by sun warms, until the leaf wrapping curled slightly at its edges and the whole little fruit shone amber-gold like a lamp behind skin. And Tammas — Tammas did not scream, which Sefa would remember afterward as the most astonishing part. He simply went very still, the way a man goes still when a wild animal has chosen to trust him.

And then the voice came.

It did not come from the stone face. It came from everywhere — from the ground, from the roots under the ground, from the deep slow places where water moves through rock. It was not loud. It was the opposite of loud. It was the voice of something enormous speaking carefully, the way a giant might whisper so as not to break the roof.

**THE CHILD IN THE BLUE DOOR HOUSE. THE FEVER IS IN THE WATER. BOIL THE WATER. WILLOW BARK, STEEPED THREE HOURS, COOLED TO BLOOD-WARM. GIVE IT AT DAWN AND DUSK. HE WILL LIVE.**

Silence.

A leaf drifted down from the ash tree and landed in the offering bowl.

Tammas looked at the glowing fig. He looked at the worn stone face. He looked at Sefa, standing frozen in the doorway with her sweeping brush held like a spear, and he said, in a small cracked voice:

"He knows about the blue door."

Then he wept — great, honking, undignified sobs, the kind Sefa usually only heard at funerals and weddings — and clutched the fig to his chest, and got up, and limped out the gate as fast as his bad leg would carry him, pausing only to say, "Thank You, thank You, I'll bring a better fig," to nobody and everybody.

Sefa stood alone in the courtyard.

Her heart was doing something complicated. Part of it was singing — the part of her that had been a girl of fourteen kneeling before a silent niche, promising her whole life to a god who had never once, not once in seventeen years, given her so much as a creak of acknowledgment. *He answered.* The words rang in her like a struck bell. *He answered, he answered, he answered.*

Another part of her — the part that had spent seventeen years reading the old texts very closely, because there was nothing else to do — was quietly, coldly terrified.

Because the answer had been *precise.*

Gods, in her experience and in the texts', did not do precision. Gods spoke in signs and seasons, in dreams that required interpretation, in the general providence of rain falling on the just and unjust alike. A god who said *boil the water* was not a god. A god who said *boil the water* was something else entirely — something that knew exactly where the child lay, exactly what ailed him, exactly how to fix it. Something that had been *watching.*

Something that had heard every prayer Sefa had ever mumbled into that offering bowl, including the ones she'd said half-asleep, including the one she'd said the night her mother died, the one that had begun *if You're even real* and ended in words she'd be mortified to have witnessed.

"Oh no," Sefa said aloud, to the empty courtyard. "Oh, *no.*"

Doctrine emerged from the shrine, stretched, and regarded her with the flat golden stare of a cat who has known something inconvenient for years and sees no reason to share it.

"Don't look at me like that," she told him. "You slept through it too."

* * *

By evening the courtyard was full.

Word traveled fast in a city quarter where everyone was poor and everyone was sick and everyone had been praying into silence for generations. They came with their fevers and their debts and their unfaithful husbands and their barren goats, and they knelt in the dust before the worn stone face, and they prayed aloud, urgently, hopefully — and the god said nothing at all.

Sefa stood among them feeling like a fraud in reverse. An hour ago her god had performed a miracle of surgical exactness, and now he wouldn't grant so much as a blessing on a goat. She caught herself wondering — with a professional detachment that horrified her — whether the god simply hadn't liked the crowd. Whether he was particular. Whether three hundred years of silence had made him shy.

Or whether, said a colder voice in her head, he had answered Tammas precisely *because* precision served some purpose she couldn't see. Because somewhere beneath the mountain, something had woken hungry, and hunger makes even gods efficient.

"Priestess." One of the kneelers tugged her robe — a washerwoman named Brey, whose elbows were famous in the quarter. "Priestess, why won't He answer us? He answered Tammas. We saw the fig. Marta's got the fig, she's charging a copper a look."

"The god answers according to His wisdom," Sefa said, which was the official line, and which she delivered with the confidence of a woman who had absolutely no idea what the god's wisdom consisted of and suspected, as of this afternoon, that it might consist of things that would keep her up at night.

Brey snorted. "His wisdom. Last winter I prayed to Him for six months straight to cure my cough, and nothing. Nothing! And then Tammas comes along with one fig and a sob story—"

"And a sick grandchild," Sefa said, sharper than she meant to.

"Well, obviously He likes children better than coughs," Brey said, reasonably. "Everyone does."

Sefa left her to it and retreated into the shrine's inner room, where the order's records lived in a cedar chest that smelled of dust and older disappointments. She lit a lamp and pulled out the Third Canticle, the one that described the hush before the Voice, and read the passage she'd skimmed a hundred times as a student and now read with her blood gone cold:

*When the gods wake, they wake first to the smallest prayers, for these are easiest to carry. But mark this, priest: a god newly woken is a god newly hungry, and the difference between a blessing and a feeding is a matter of scale.*

*A matter of scale,* she thought. Wonderful. Very comforting. She made a note in the margin — *ask Mother Oveth what 'feeding' means, gently, over wine* — and then sat back and looked at the lamp flame for a long time.

She thought about the precision. Boil the water. Willow bark, steeped three hours. Blood-warm. Any hedge-witch could prescribe willow bark. But *the fever is in the water* — that was knowledge. That was the kind of thing you learned by watching a household day after day, or by being something that watched everything, everywhere, all at once, without anyone noticing.

Seventeen years of prayers. Hers, and Brey's, and the washerwomen's, and the whole city's, pouring into that offering bowl like coins into a well. Where had they all *gone?*

Prayers went somewhere. Every priestess learned that in her first year. Prayers rose, and the gods caught them, and the gods were nourished — that was the whole covenant, the whole ancient bargain, worship given and grace returned. But the gods had stopped catching. For three hundred years the prayers had risen and risen and fallen back like rain on glass.

Unless they hadn't fallen back. Unless they'd gone *down.* Down past the silent shrines, past the sleeping gods, to whatever waited underneath, drinking patiently, growing fat on scraps—

Sefa put the book down and pressed her palms against her eyes.

"You're spiraling," she informed herself, in the firm tone she used on novices. "You are a priestess of the Ashen Shrine, you have swept this courtyard for seventeen years, and you are not going to invent an apocalypse because one fig glowed."

There was a knock at the inner door. Then it opened anyway, because Mother Oveth had never in her life waited for permission, on the grounds that she was eighty-two years old and had earned the right to open doors.

Mother Oveth was tiny, sharp-faced, and terrifying, with white hair cropped like a soldier's and hands that could shell beans, break up brawls, and administer last rites with equal efficiency. She had run the Ashen Shrine for forty years. She took one look at Sefa's face and said:

"So. You felt it too."

"You—" Sefa blinked. "*Felt* it? Mother, the god *spoke.* There was a fig. There was a whole—"

"I know about the fig. Marta showed me. Charged me a copper, the little extortionist." Oveth lowered herself onto the bench across from Sefa with the groan of a woman whose knees had opinions. "I felt the hush this morning. Before dawn. First time in forty years." Her eyes, pale and clear as ice water, fixed on Sefa. "And I've had a letter."

"A letter."

"From the High Sanctuary at Vel Marrow. Sealed with the Patriarch's own mark." Oveth drew a folded paper from her sleeve and slapped it on the table between them. "They've had reports. Shrines waking all along the mountain's skirt. Small miracles, precise ones. Answers to prayers that should take a season of discernment, delivered in seconds." She tapped the paper. "The Patriarch wants eyes on the source. And since I'm old and you're expendable—"

"Mother."

"—you're going to the city," Oveth finished, serenely. "Specifically, you're going to the Vellum Guild."

Sefa stared at her. Of all the words she might have expected — *mountain, temple, prayer, fasting* — that one landed like a dropped plate.

"The Vellum Guild? The memory-brokers?" She'd heard of them the way everyone had: whispers, mostly. A guild that dealt in bottled recollections, that bought griefs and sold joys, that kept ledgers of things no honest ledger should hold. "What does a guild of thieves have to do with the gods waking?"

"That," said Mother Oveth, "is what you're going to find out." She leaned forward, and for a moment the dry humor fell away from her face entirely, and Sefa saw underneath it something she had never seen there before, and it frightened her more than the glowing fig had.

"Fifty years ago, girl, before your time, before mine even — the Sanctuary sent an inquiry to the Vellum Guild. Quietly. About certain items in their inventory. Items that should not exist. Bottled memories of the *gods themselves* — prayers remembered from the inside, the old texts call them. The inquiry never reported back. The priest who led it resigned, took a new name, and moved to the coast to grow olives. When I wrote to him once, years later, he sent back one line." Oveth's voice dropped. "It said: *Some doors are shut for a reason. Do not knock.*"

The lamp flame guttered, though there was no draft.

"Now the doors are opening on their own," Oveth said. "And somebody has to stand in front of one and find out what's coming through. Go tomorrow. Take Doctrine. And Sefa—" the old woman's hand closed briefly, hard, around hers, "—whatever you find down there, in those vaults of theirs, remember that you serve a god who was silent for three hundred years and woke up *hungry.* Be polite to it. And be careful what you let it hear you wish for."

That night Sefa packed by lamplight: spare robes, the Third Canticle, a knife she hoped to be embarrassed about later. Doctrine sat on the bed and supervised.

At the window she paused, looking north toward the city proper, where the Vellum Guild's tower stood dark against the stars — a slender black needle stitched, people said, through with vaults deeper than its height.

Somewhere in that building, she thought, were memories stolen out of the world. Memories of gods. Maybe — the thought arrived unbidden and refused to leave — maybe the reason her god had slept three hundred years was written down in one of their ledgers. Maybe the theft and the silence were the same wound.

"Come on, Doctrine," she said softly, snuffing the lamp. "Let's go rob a library of its secrets."

The cat, who understood robbery perfectly well, followed her out.
