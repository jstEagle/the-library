## 3. The Client with No Face

The Vellum Guild kept its appointment book the way a spider keeps a web: patiently, in the dark, and with every expectation that something would eventually blunder into it.

Orsen Halloran had been Keeper of Ledgers for eleven years, which meant he had spent eleven years learning that the most dangerous thing in the vaults was not the bottled scream of a drowned sailor or the jar of somebody's first love (which hummed faintly when you walked past it, like a wasp in a jam pot). The most dangerous thing was an entry that didn't add up.

He found this one on a Tuesday, which he would later consider unfair. Tuesdays were for inventory. Tuesdays were safe.

The morning had begun pleasantly enough. He had his tea — black, bitter, brewed strong enough to strip varnish — and his slate of tasks, and the small private pleasure of a man who knows exactly where everything is. The vault beneath the Guildhall held four thousand two hundred and six catalogued memories, give or take the ones that wandered. Memories did wander, occasionally. Grief especially. You'd shelve it properly under *Personal Loss, Familial, Moderate to Severe*, and three weeks later you'd find it sulking in the *Childhood Joys* aisle, leaking.

"Good morning, Master Halloran," said Pim, the junior clerk, who was seventeen and had the unshakable cheer of someone who had never yet had to catalogue a war crime.

"Is it?" Orsen asked, because ritual mattered.

"It's raining sideways and Mrs. Duvvel from the third floor says her memory of her wedding is humming again."

"Tell her that's normal. Tell her if it starts singing, that's also normal, but she should come in so we can look at it." He sipped his tea. "And Pim? If anyone asks for me before noon, I am auditing."

"You're always auditing."

"Yes," said Orsen, "and it's always true. That's what makes it such a good lie."

* * *

The client arrived at half past ten, which meant Orsen's lie lasted ninety minutes, which he considered respectable.

She came through the front door of the Guildhall on Candlemaker's Row the way fog comes through a window left open — no announcement, no footman, no sound of the street following her in. She wore grey silk from throat to wrist, gloves to the elbow, and a veil of fine silver mesh that turned her face into a suggestion rather than a fact. He could see, or thought he could see, the shape of a mouth. It did not smile. Most clients smiled. Smiling was how people apologized for buying something that wasn't theirs.

"Master Halloran," she said. Her voice was low and even, like water over smooth stones. "I wish to make a purchase."

"The Guild serves," Orsen said, and gestured toward the consultation room, because that was the script, because the script was load-bearing.

The consultation room was designed to put buyers at ease: warm lamplight, a fire already lit, chairs deep enough to lose small children in. The veiled woman sat on the very edge of hers as though the cushions might be a trap. Orsen took the ledger stand across from her and opened the great book to the current day.

"What sort of memory were you hoping to acquire?"

"One that is not mine," she said.

"That describes rather a lot of our inventory." He turned a page. "We organize by provenance, by emotional weight, and by age. If you can tell me anything at all about what you're looking for — a feeling, a face, a season — I can narrow the search considerably."

"A season," she said, after a pause long enough that the fire popped twice. "Spring. And a garden. And a king."

Orsen's pen stopped moving.

It was a small thing, the stopping. A professional keeps his hands still; that is the first lesson of the ledgers. But the client's veil tilted, very slightly, and he understood that she had seen it anyway.

"There are," he said carefully, "a number of royal memories in our holdings. Most are minor. A coronation headache. The tedium of state dinners. One remarkably vivid recollection of stepping in something in the royal mews."

"I want the one from the garden," she said. "Caerlum. The summer palace. Spring, thirty-one years ago. Catalogued under—" and here she recited, without hesitation, without drawing breath, "—*Vellum Registry, Royal Acquisitions, Shelf Nine, Vial Forty-Four.*"

There is a particular silence that falls when someone quotes your own inventory back to you better than you know it. Orsen lived in that silence for three full seconds.

"That is a restricted holding," he said. "Royal acquisitions require—"

"I have brought the fee." From somewhere within the grey silk she produced a purse and set it on the table between them. It made no sound when it landed, which should have been impossible, and Orsen noted that the way he noted all impossible things: quietly, in the back room of his mind, where he kept them filed under *Later*.

He opened the purse. Inside lay coins of old gold, minted in the reign of Aldric's grandfather, each one bright as the day it was struck. Old gold, unfaded. Another item for the *Later* file, which was beginning to feel crowded.

"Shelf Nine, Vial Forty-Four," Orsen repeated, mostly to buy time, and pulled the working ledger toward him to log the transaction. This was procedure: every sale entered by hand, dated, signed by the keeper on duty, countersigned by the client or their mark.

He wrote the date. He wrote the vial number. Then, per procedure, he checked the acquisition record — because royal memories required chain-of-custody confirmation, and because Orsen Halloran had never once in eleven years skipped a step of procedure, a fact his colleagues mocked and his sleep schedule depended upon.

The acquisition record for Vial Forty-Four was thirty-one years old. The ink had gone brown at the edges. The entry read:

*Acquired from source unnamed. Seller's fee waived. Condition: pristine. Emotional weight: extreme. Note — seller requested anonymity; payment refused; memory surrendered freely.*

And beneath it, the keeper's signature of the era.

Orsen stared at it.

He knew that handwriting.

Of course he knew it. Every keeper learned the hands of those who came before; the Guild's records were a continuous line, keeper to keeper, forty years of Orsen's own predecessors preserved in ink. He had studied them the way a priest studies scripture. There was Master Ellery-Vane's cramped, furious script. Old Mother Cass's looping, patient letters. And there was—

There was his own.

Not similar. Not reminiscent. Identical. The same forward lean. The same way the crossbar of the *t* slashed slightly upward, like a man in a hurry to be done. The same little hook he added to the terminal *s* because a tutor had beaten it into him at age nine and he had never managed to beat it out again.

Orsen looked at the date. Thirty-one years ago. He had been nine years old thirty-one years ago, being taught that hook by a tired tutor in a house he did not remember well, and he had certainly never set foot in the Vellum Guildhall, and he had absolutely, definitely, under no circumstances whatsoever sold a king's memory of a spring garden.

"Master Halloran?" said the veiled woman. "Is there a difficulty?"

"No difficulty," said Orsen, whose voice came out one note too high, and he coughed and brought it down. "An administrative curiosity. Nothing that concerns you."

"Everything in this building concerns me," she said mildly. "That is why I am here."

He fetched the vial. What else could he do? Procedure said fetch the vial. Procedure was the handrail over the abyss, and Orsen gripped it. Shelf Nine stood in the restricted gallery, behind the second iron door, and Vial Forty-Four sat where it had sat for three decades: a slender phial of smoked glass, stoppered with wax the color of dried blood, holding a curl of pale light that moved like wind through wheat.

Even through the glass, even through the wax, he felt it look at him.

Kings' memories were like that. They had weight. This one had more than weight — it had *attention*. He carried it back to the consultation room balanced on both palms like a live coal, and the whole way down the corridor the hairs on his arms stood up and informed him, unanimously, that this was a mistake.

The client received it without touching it. She leaned close instead, veil whispering against the glass, and for a moment — just a moment — Orsen saw the shape behind the mesh go still in a way faces do not go still. The way *rooms* go still.

"Yes," she breathed. "This is the one. He was happy here. Before." She straightened. "You will witness the transfer?"

"Standard practice," Orsen said, and performed it: the sigil chalked on the table, the vial uncorked, the memory poured into the client's cupped hands like liquid moonlight, the incantation of severance that unmade the Guild's claim and made the thing wholly hers. The pale light sank into her gloved palms and vanished. Somewhere in the vault, distantly, Orsen felt four thousand two hundred and five catalogued memories shiver, like a flock of birds hearing a door slam.

The client rose. "Your fee is generous," she said, "because I may return. When I do, I will ask for you specifically, Master Halloran. Keep well. Keep *careful*." A pause. "You have lovely handwriting."

Then she was gone through the front door, into the sideways rain, and the street noise resumed as though it had merely stepped out for a moment, and Orsen Halloran sat alone in the warm lamplight with a purse of impossibly bright gold and the growing, cold certainty that he had just handed a king's happiness to something that intended to do *something* with it, and that the something was not going to be gardening.

* * *

He told himself he would let it go.

He told himself this while locking the consultation room, while logging the sale, while eating lunch he did not taste. He told himself it while climbing the stairs to the archive stacks, telling himself he was only going up to reshelve a stray grief from the *Childhood Joys* aisle, and then he told himself nothing at all, because he had pulled the acquisition ledgers for the years 1189 through 1194 and opened them on a reading table under a lamp, and the telling-himself portion of the day was over.

Thirty-one years ago. Who had been keeper then?

The registry of officers gave him a name: **Master Corvus Hale, Keeper of Ledgers, 1171–1196**. Orsen had heard the name, of course. Everyone had. Corvus Hale was the guildmaster's old teacher, a legend of the craft, the man who had supposedly perfected the sealing techniques still used today. He had retired abruptly — the histories said *to pursue private study* — and died, the histories said *peacefully*, some years after. The histories, Orsen was beginning to suspect, had been written by people who had not checked the handwriting.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing that had hooked him under the breastbone and would not let go:

It wasn't only Vial Forty-Four.

He went through the royal acquisitions page by page, year by year, and began to see it everywhere once he knew to look. Entries in that same forward-leaning hand. The same slashing *t*. The same hooked *s*. Dozens of them. And they weren't random acquisitions — they clustered. Memories of gardens. Memories of coronation days. Memories of *prayers answered*, which was a category so thin in the Guild's holdings that Orsen had always assumed nobody bothered selling such things, because for three hundred years the gods had not been answering prayers, and you cannot bottle a miracle that doesn't happen.

But thirty-one years ago, someone with Orsen's handwriting had catalogued eleven of them.

*Prayer answered: rain, ending a drought, witnessed at [illegible].*
*Prayer answered: fever broken, child recovered, source weeping with gratitude.*
*Prayer answered: [entry struck through in later ink].*

Struck through. By whom? In what ink? He held the page to the lamp and tilted it, and beneath the strikethrough, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper from the pen above, he could just make out the original words:

*Prayer answered: the god beneath Caerlum mountain speaks, and the speaker is—*

The rest was lost. Someone had wanted it lost. Someone had taken a knife or a blotting page to the most interesting sentence Orsen had ever read, and had done it decades ago, and had used — he checked, heart hammering, comparing stroke by stroke against the margin notes — the *same hand* to do it.

His hand. Which was not his hand. Which belonged to a dead man, or to a man who had become dead on purpose, or —

Or to whatever had taught Orsen to write, all those years ago, in a house he did not remember well.

He sat back. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, Pim laughed at something, bright and oblivious, and the ordinary sounds of the Guildhall went on around Orsen like weather around a man standing very still in the middle of it.

There was a rational explanation. There was always a rational explanation; that was the religion of ledger-keepers. Apprentices copied their masters' hands — it was traditional, it was training, it was practically liturgy. Some apprentice with a gift for imitation had worked under Corvus Hale. Fine. Plausible. Comforting.

Except that apprentices' copies drifted. Hands wander over years; that was how forgers were caught, in the drift. These entries spanned twenty-five years and did not drift *at all*. Not a hair. As if the writer had decided, once, precisely how to write, and then simply stopped being a person about it.

And there was one more thing. The worst thing. The thing he kept turning over like a coin he suspected was cursed.

Corvus Hale had sealed memories. Perfected the art, the histories said. And Orsen — Orsen had one sealed memory of his own, bought and paid for in blood he couldn't recall spilling, locked away in the deepest drawer of the deepest shelf of his own mind, behind work he did not remember commissioning but recognized, now, with a sick lurch, the way you recognize your own handwriting.

The technique used on him had been flawless. He'd always assumed a Guild master had done it, some anonymous kindness, some colleague taking pity on a young man who came to them hollow-eyed and bleeding from a past he refused to discuss.

Flawless. Undrifted. Twenty-five years of perfect strokes.

"Oh," Orsen said aloud, to the empty archive, and his breath fogged in the lamplight though the room was warm. "Oh, that's not funny at all."

* * *

He worked until the candles burned down to puddles. He pulled every ledger Corvus Hale had touched, and then every ledger he might have touched, and then the private daybooks keepers were supposed to surrender on retirement and almost never did. Three of Hale's daybooks were listed in the registry as *retained for study*. Their shelf location was given as the sub-cellar. Orsen went down to the sub-cellar with a lantern and found the shelf, and found the dust on the shelf undisturbed in a neat rectangle where three books had sat for a very long time, and found that the rectangle was empty.

Someone had taken them. Recently — the dust edges were crisp. Within the month. Possibly within the week.

Orsen stood in the cold dark of the sub-cellar, listening to the building settle around him, and made a list. Lists calmed him. Lists were how he prayed, if he was honest, which he tried not to be.

One: a veiled client had purchased a king's memory using knowledge of the inventory no outsider should possess, and had complimented his handwriting, which no one had ever done, including his mother, presumably because he didn't remember her, which was a thought he shelved immediately and firmly.

Two: entries in his own hand existed from before he could write.

Three: Corvus Hale, the greatest keeper who ever lived, had catalogued answered prayers thirty-one years ago — right around, he realized now, checking the accession dates with fingers that trembled slightly, right around the time a nine-year-old boy somewhere in the city had been taught a peculiar little hook on the letter *s*.

Four: the daybooks were gone, and whoever took them had keys, and the number of people with keys to the sub-cellar was four, and Orsen was one of them, and he did not think he had done it, and the fact that he had to specify *I do not think* was doing terrible things to his evening.

Five: the god beneath Caerlum mountain *speaks*, and the speaker is—

He needed the rest of that sentence. He needed it the way a drowning man needs the surface, with a violence that surprised him. Orsen Halloran was not a violent man. He was a careful man, a tidy man, a man whose passions were expressed exclusively through marginalia. But he stood there in the dark with his lantern and understood that he would tear the Guild down to its foundations, shelf by shelf, ledger by ledger, before he would let that sentence stay unfinished.

Because there was a sixth item on the list, the one he hadn't written down, the one sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone:

Six: whatever had sealed his memory — whatever had reached into his mind with that perfect, undrifting hand — had been *Corvus Hale's hand*. And Corvus Hale had spent his career handling the Guild's oldest stock. And the Guild's oldest stock, everyone knew, everyone *knew*, was the tithe-taken memories from the founding days, when the Guild had served purposes no one discussed, for patrons no one named.

Patrons who, according to every text Orsen had ever dared skim, had once included gods.

The lantern guttered. Above him, faintly, through floors of stone and timber, Orsen heard the evening bell of Sefa's shrine district — the priests ringing the hour of listening, as they had rung it every night for three hundred years, to a god who had never once, in living memory, listened back.

Except perhaps he had. Perhaps he had listened the whole time. Perhaps he had simply been waiting for someone to finish a sentence.

Orsen climbed the stairs, locked the sub-cellar behind him, and went to find out who else in this building had keys — and whether the guildmaster, when asked directly about Corvus Hale, would lie to him badly or lie to him well.

He was braced for either.

He was not braced for the guildmaster to already be waiting at the top of the stairs, lantern in hand, wearing an expression Orsen had never seen on that placid old face before, saying, in a voice gone quiet as a closed book:

"Master Halloran. We need to talk about what you've been reading."
