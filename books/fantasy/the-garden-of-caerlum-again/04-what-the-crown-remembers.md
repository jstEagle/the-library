## 4. What the Crown Remembers

The summons arrived on a Tuesday, which Sefa took as a personal insult.

Tuesdays were her day for the beggars' hour — the one service the shrine of Vethis still performed without complaint from anyone, because no one important attended it. She had a system. Old Marn got his knee blessed first because he complained loudest; the twins who sold eels got their nets blessed second because they tipped in actual eels; and everyone else was seen in order of how long they'd been waiting, which was the only fair method Sefa had ever discovered, and she'd defended it before two high priests and one very confused magistrate.

Instead of all that, she stood in a silk traveling robe that didn't fit her shoulders, holding a summons sealed with the royal sunburst, while a courier in palace livery looked at her shrine's leaking roof with an expression usually reserved for diseased livestock.

"His Majesty requests the presence of the priestess Sefa of the Third Shrine," the courier recited, "to offer blessing at the autumn court."

"His Majesty," Sefa said, "has not requested anything from this shrine in eleven years. I counted. It was a slow decade."

The courier blinked at her. "I only carry the letter, madam."

"Yes, well. So does the wind, and it has better manners." But she took the letter, because you did not refuse a king, even a king who had forgotten your god existed. Especially then. Neglect was a wound, and wounds were Vethis's particular specialty.

She read the summons three times on the road east. The handwriting changed halfway through. That was the first wrong thing, though she didn't yet know to call it wrong. The opening lines were written in a firm, elegant hand — *the crown gives thanks for faithful service* — and then, somewhere around the formal enumeration of her duties, the letters grew slightly too round, slightly too careful, like a child imitating its mother's script. As if whoever wrote the second half had learned Aldric Caerlum's hand recently, and from a distance.

The second wrong thing was the date. The court wanted her on the eve of the harvest moon, which any priestess worth her incense knew was the old holy night, the night the mountain god had once been fed. Nobody at court had observed it in centuries. The calendar had been scrubbed of it so thoroughly that Sefa herself had only ever heard of it from a drunken archivist who wept into his wine about "what they burned."

Someone at court remembered.

*Or something at court remembers,* said a small voice in her head, and she told it firmly to hush, because she was a priestess of the god of small mercies and did not traffic in ominous voices, however correct they turned out to be.

* * *

Caerlum's summer palace was less a building than a boast made of white stone. It sat above the river terraces with the smugness of a cat that has never once been wrong about a closed door, and its gardens — she'd heard stories of the gardens all her life — were, in late autumn, a wilderness of brown stalks and stubborn roses that refused to accept the season's decision.

They dressed her in a side chamber for an hour. Two attendants brushed her hair until it crackled. A third brought jewelry, and when Sefa declined, looked personally wounded, as if Sefa had insulted her grandmother.

"You'll want the blessing oils," the eldest attendant said. "His Majesty is very particular about the rite."

"Is he," Sefa said. "Particular how?"

"He likes it done properly." The attendant smiled. It was a lovely smile. It reached every part of her face except her eyes, which stayed behind like servants who hadn't been paid. "He likes everything done properly now."

*Now.* Sefa filed the word away. Words were her trade; she noticed when one arrived wearing new boots.

They processed her into the great hall at dusk, when the light through the western windows turned everything the color of honey and old blood. Courtiers lined the walls in ranks, and here was the third wrong thing, and this one she felt in her stomach before her mind caught up: they were all smiling. Not the strained smiles of people enduring a long ceremony — those she knew intimately, having caused thousands of them. These were easy smiles. Identical easy smiles. Forty courtiers, forty faces, and every smile curved to precisely the same depth, like they'd all been measured against the same ruler.

A young lord near the door caught her looking and smiled wider. His eyes were pleasant and empty as two cups after washing.

"Well," Sefa murmured to herself, "either the wine is excellent or I'm about to be sacrificed."

Then trumpets, and the king came in, and she forgot to be clever.

* * *

She had seen Aldric Caerlum once, years ago, at a distance, reviewing the harvest festival. He'd been a big man then, broad through the shoulder, with a laugh that carried across a square and a way of listening to farmers as if farming were a fascinating religion he'd simply never gotten around to joining. The people had loved him the way you love good weather: casually, constantly, without thinking about it.

The man who sat the throne now wore that same face the way you wear a coat cut for someone slightly larger. It fit. Nothing hung wrong. And yet.

He was thinner. Not ill-thin — *edited*-thin, as if someone had taken the king and trimmed away whatever seemed unnecessary. He sat very still. Kings could be still; stillness read as power. But this was the stillness of furniture. His hands lay on the arms of the throne, and Sefa, who had spent twenty years watching hands because hands told the truth mouths wouldn't, saw that they never shifted. Never flexed. In the whole time she watched, they moved exactly twice, both times to perform a gesture — a wave, a beckon — and both times the gesture stopped at exactly the right angle, like a drawing of a wave rather than a wave.

And his eyes. She searched them for the weather-love, the farmer-listening, the man. What she found instead was attention without warmth: the gaze of something reading him.

*Reading,* she thought. *Not being.*

"Approach," said a herald, and Sefa approached, robes whispering, heart doing something undignified against her ribs.

"Your Majesty." She knelt, as the protocol scroll had insisted, with a flourish she'd practiced on the road and abandoned somewhere outside the city gates. "You honor my poor shrine."

"The crown honors all faithful service," the king said.

His voice was right. That was the worst discovery of the evening. It was warm, measured, faintly amused — a voice you could listen to for hours, a voice built by committee from every compliment ever paid to the real one. If she hadn't seen the eyes, she might have believed.

"I am come to offer the blessing of Vethis," she said, "god of small mercies, keeper of griefs, the Quiet Neighbor." The old titles came out of her mouth and fell into the hall like coins down a well. No echo. Not one courtier shifted. Somewhere, a fly landed on a pillar and seemed embarrassed.

"We welcome the blessing," the king said.

She opened her kit. The oils, the salt, the little bell — all of it suddenly feeling like toys she'd brought to a funeral. But ritual was ritual, and ritual was a door, and doors worked whether or not the house behind them was occupied. That was the whole theology of her calling, and she clung to it now like a railing over a drop.

She dipped her thumb in the oil. "By the Quiet Neighbor's leave," she said, and pressed it to the king's brow, and spoke the blessing.

It was a good blessing. She'd improved it over the years, tightened the meter, cut the flab. It asked Vethis to look upon this servant, to know his griefs, to sit with him in the small hours, to return to him what sorrow had cost and mercy could mend. It was, in essence, a prayer that the person before her be *known* — thoroughly, kindly, all the way down.

The blessing slid off him like rain off glass.

Sefa had felt blessings fail before. Failure had textures. There was the failure of a distracted heart — hers, usually — which felt like speaking into wind. There was the failure of an unwilling subject, which felt like knocking on a door with someone standing behind it, refusing to answer. There was even, once, the failure of a dying man, which had felt like blessing an empty room where a room should have been.

This was none of these. This was smoothness. Her words touched the king's brow and ran off it, all the way down, pooling nowhere, soaking into nothing. It was as if the surface of him declined, politely and completely, to exist in the same world as her god.

Her mouth went dry. She finished the rite anyway — the salt, the bell, the closing words — because stopping would have meant admitting what she already knew, and some part of her understood that the next few minutes depended entirely on nothing in this hall learning what she knew.

The bell rang. Its note fell flat in the huge room, and died.

Silence. Forty identical smiles. One king, sitting like a portrait of a king, oil gleaming on a brow that had accepted nothing.

*Vethis,* she prayed silently, desperately, *if you're awake the way the beggar's bread was awake, if you're answering prayers again, answer mine. Tell me what I'm kneeling in front of.*

Nothing answered. Which was, in its way, an answer. Even the god of small mercies, newly woken and apparently in a mood to show off, had nothing to say about the thing on the throne. You did not knock on a door when the house was already occupied by something that ate visitors.

"Stand, priestess," the king said.

She stood. Her knees reported their complaints and she ignored them.

"You serve the Third Shrine," the king said. "In the city. Near the river."

"Eleven years, Majesty."

"There was a guild there. Once. Of vellum." He said the word carefully, tasting it. "Keepers of records. They are still there?"

"They are, Majesty." Her voice held. She was almost proud of it. "They keep ledgers."

Something moved behind the king's eyes. Not a flicker of memory — memory has warmth, even bitter memory, even grief. This was colder. This was the look of a man remembering a *debt*, and Sefa realized with a lurch that she was watching something do arithmetic.

"Ledgers," the king repeated softly. "Yes. Everything must be tallied. Everything comes due." He smiled then, and the smile was the courtiers' smile, the measured one, the ruler-checked curve, and for one vertiginous moment Sefa understood that she was looking at the source. The smiles hadn't spread through the court like fashion or flattery. They had spread like weather off this thing, this front of pleasant emptiness, and everyone who stood too long in it came away smiling the same smile, thinking the same nothing, loving the same hollow king.

"Thank you for your blessing," the king said. "It was... properly done."

Behind her, she heard the courtiers exhale — a soft, collective sound, like a tide going out. The audience was over. She bowed, backed away the prescribed seven steps, and let an attendant steer her toward the doors, and the whole time her mind was screaming in a register only she could hear:

*It knows about the guild. It knows about the ledgers. It used the word tally.*

* * *

They gave her a guest room with a view of the ruined garden and a supper she couldn't eat. She sat on the bed in her undertunic with the door barred — a courtesy the attendants had found charming rather than suspicious, which itself was suspicious — and made herself think in lists, because lists were how she kept the fear organized and the fear, unorganized, was enormous.

List one: the king was not the king. Certainty arrived without argument, the way certainty about rain does. Whatever wore Aldric Caerlum had put him on like a coat and learned his voice from letters, and it was asking after the Vellum Guild with a creditor's patience.

List two: the court. Those smiles. She thought of the young lord by the door, the washed-cup eyes, and wondered how much of each of them was still home. Had they given it up gladly? Did it feel like peace, being smoothed? She suspected it did. That was the horror of it — not teeth, not shadow, but *contentment*, distributed evenly, like butter.

List three: the harvest moon. The summons had placed her here deliberately, on the old feeding night, and the thing on the throne had questioned her about the guild within minutes of meeting her. Coincidence was a luxury she'd stopped affording herself around the time the blessing slid off.

She went to the window. Below, the dead garden sprawled in the starlight, and among the brown stalks she saw movement — gardeners, working late, a dozen of them, moving between the beds with lamps. Planting, she thought at first. Then she saw that they weren't planting. They were digging. Long, patient trenches, row after row, in soil that no autumn planting would explain.

One of them straightened, stretched his back, and smiled up at the stars. From thirty yards and through glass, Sefa could see that it was the smile. The measured one. He held it a long time, aimed at nothing, and then bent back to his digging.

She thought of the archivist weeping into his wine. *What they burned.* The rites, the names, the old feeding nights — the church had spent three hundred years burying the gods' ledger, and now something in this palace was digging it all back up, trench by careful trench, and it had sent for a priestess to stand witness.

There was a scratch at her door. She froze. Then a folded slip of paper slid underneath, and footsteps hurried away down the corridor.

She picked it up. The handwriting was cramped and hurried, nothing like a courtier's trained flourishes:

*You blessed him. Did it take? If it did not take — if it slid — come to the shrine of the low stair, midnight. Come alone. I keep accounts too. — O.H.*

Sefa sat down slowly on the edge of the bed, the paper trembling very slightly in her hand, and laughed — one short, unsteady bark of a laugh, at the absurdity of receiving secret notes in a haunted palace, at the signature, at the phrase *did it slide*, which meant a stranger across the city had felt the same rain-off-glass wrongness and named it exactly as she had.

*I keep accounts too.*

Somewhere below, the gardeners dug on. Above the mountains, faint and early, the harvest moon was already showing its rim, pale as a coin someone had bitten and found wanting.

Midnight, then. She had until midnight to decide whether the author of a smuggled note was a friend, a trap, or — and this possibility she liked least — another debt collector.
