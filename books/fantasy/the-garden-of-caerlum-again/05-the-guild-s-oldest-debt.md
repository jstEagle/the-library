## 5. The Guild's Oldest Debt

The summons came at dawn, delivered by a boy who smelled of tallow and terror.

"Master Halloran," the boy recited, as though he'd rehearsed it on the stairs and was afraid he'd forget a word before reaching the bottom, "the Guildmaster requests your presence in the Understudy. Immediately. She said — she said to bring nothing."

Orsen looked down at the ledger under his arm.

The boy's eyes followed his gaze and went slightly gray around the edges. "She said *nothing*, Master Halloran."

"Of course she did." Orsen set the ledger on his desk with the care of a man laying down a sleeping cat. "Tell her I'm coming."

He did not tell her he was coming. He told her he was coming after he had done three things, because thirty years in the Vellum Guild had taught him that "bring nothing" always meant *bring something we can't be seen knowing you brought*.

First, he locked the day's audit in his desk — the inventory of waking wares, which had grown by four items overnight. Four memories stirring in their vials like fish rising to feed. He wrote the count twice: once in ink, once in the private shorthand only he could read, pressed so lightly into the margin it might have been paper remembering an old bruise.

Second, he checked on the candle.

It still burned in its niche behind the shelving of the Long Vault, this third morning running. Three hundred years the guild had kept prayer candles as curiosities — dead wax, dead wicks, bought from ruined temples by weight. And now this one burned with a small, patient flame that cast no heat and left no smoke, and every time Orsen passed it, he felt the peculiar sensation of being *read*. He had considered dousing it. He had decided, in the end, that one does not blow out a candle that has waited three centuries for a match. One simply notes it in the ledger and hopes the fire has better manners than to spread.

*Item 4,411: Candle, votive, provenance unknown. Status: lit. Recommendation:* — and here he had paused, quill hovering, before writing — *none.*

Third, he took the key.

There were forty-one keys to the guild's inner vaults, and Orsen knew them all, wore them all, jingled them all like a miser's conscience. But there was a forty-second thing that was not quite a key — a sliver of black glass on a cord, found in his predecessor's effects, that opened no lock Orsen had ever found. He had carried it for eleven years without telling anyone, the way one carries a splinter too deep to dig out and too small to matter.

Today it felt less like a splinter and more like a seed.

Then he went down to the Understudy.

* * *

The Understudy was the room beneath the guildhall where new keepers learned the trade, and it had not been used for teaching in forty years. Dust lay on the practice desks in soft gray drifts, undisturbed except for one clean circle where the Guildmaster stood, hands folded, facing the far wall.

Maren Voss had been Guildmaster since before Orsen had handwriting worth forging. She was a small woman built like a knot in oak — compact, hard to cut, impossible to pull apart — and in thirty years he had never once seen her uncertain. This morning she was facing away from him, and her shoulders were doing something he had no ledger category for.

"You brought something," she said.

"I brought myself. That's usually sufficient."

"It hasn't been sufficient for anyone in a very long time." She turned. Her face was composed, but composed the way a battlefield is composed after the fighting stops — technically quiet, thoroughly wrong. "Sit, Orsen."

"I'd rather stand. Standing keeps the mind honest."

"The mind is precisely what we need to discuss." She gestured anyway, and he sat, because there are chairs and then there are chairs, and the Guildmaster's chair in the Understudy was the kind you sat in when the conversation was going to be long enough to need support. "How long have you kept our ledgers?"

"Eleven years as keeper. Six as apprentice before that."

"And in seventeen years, have you ever known me to lie to you?"

"No." He let that sit a moment. "You've declined to tell me things. You've told me things that were true in ways designed to mislead. But I don't believe you've ever lied."

Something moved through her face — relief, or grief wearing relief's coat. "Good. Then hear this plainly, because plainness is all I have left." She drew a breath. "The gods are awake. You've seen the inventory stir. The candle. The vials warming in their racks like eggs under a hen. It isn't leakage, Orsen. It isn't decay. It's *attention*."

"We trade in memory, Guildmaster. Memory doesn't wake."

"These do. Because these were never meant to be ours." She crossed to the far wall — the one she'd been facing when he came in — and did something with her hand against the stone that he would spend years trying to replicate and never manage. A seam opened. Behind it, stairs went down into dark that was older than the guildhall above it.

"The Vellum Guild was founded nine hundred years ago," she said, starting down. "Not as merchants. As *keepers*. Every temple in the old days had its reliquary, and every reliquary had its tithe — prayers too heavy for the god to hold, griefs too large for mortal minds to carry whole. The gods stored their overflow with us. We were the cellar of heaven, Orsen. We held what the divine could not."

The stairs ended in a chamber that stopped Orsen's breath in his chest.

It was a vault, but not like the vaults above. Those were tidy — racked vials, labeled shelves, the loving bureaucracy of theft. This place was vast and rough-hewn and *full*. Shelves rose into darkness beyond the reach of any lamp, and on them sat vessels of every age: clay jars sealed with wax and prayer, lead boxes, glass phials, things that might have been grown rather than made. And everything — everything — was humming. Not sound, exactly. A vibration in the teeth, in the sternum, like standing near a cathedral bell that hadn't been struck yet but remembered how.

"What is this?" His voice came out smaller than he intended.

"The original collection." Maren's lamp threw her shadow huge against the shelves. "What the guild was founded to guard. And what it began selling, six hundred years ago, when the gods went silent and the keeping turned to hoarding and the hoarding turned to trade." She said it flatly, without excuse. "We robbed a sleeping house, Orsen. Methodically. For generations. We told ourselves the owners were dead. We wrote provenances. We invented sellers. We became very, very good at paperwork."

Orsen thought of his ledgers. Of the elegant columns, the immaculate chain of custody, the beautiful lie of order that he had spent eleven years maintaining and secretly, shamefully, adoring. "The client records," he said slowly. "The gaps. The entries in handwriting that matches mine though I never wrote them."

"Now you understand why I never let you audit the deep registry."

"I assumed corruption."

"I know. It was easier for both of us." She set her lamp on a shelf bracket, and the light caught the nearest vessels, and Orsen saw — really saw — that some of the jars were *warm*. A faint amber glow pulsed inside them, slow as breathing. "The gods are awake, and they are coming home, and they are counting. Do you know what a god does when it finds its cellar emptied?"

"I imagine it sends a bill."

"A bill." Maren laughed, and it was the worst sound Orsen had heard all year. "The god beneath the mountain — the one my own order starved with neglect until it learned to eat anything offered — has already sent its collector. It came three nights ago. Through the front door. Politely."

Orsen went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the vault's chill. "I've seen no one."

"You wouldn't have. Collectors don't look like anything. They look like whatever makes you want to open the door." She turned to face him fully, and now he saw what her shoulders had been doing all along: bracing. "It gave us terms. The debt is total — every vessel, every stolen ware, returned to the gods' keeping within two turnings of the moon. And collateral, to guarantee good faith. One item, named specifically, to be surrendered now."

"The candle."

"No." Maren's eyes were very steady and very sorry. "A sealed memory. Held in the personal collection of the keeper of ledgers. Taken — the collector was quite precise — *by the debtor himself, from himself.*"

The vault hummed around them. Somewhere in the dark, a jar glowed a little brighter, like a dog hearing its name.

"That's impossible," Orsen said. "My personal holdings are—"

"Don't." Gentle, almost kind. "Don't inventory at me, Orsen. I've known about your sealed ware for eleven years. It's why I made you keeper. The guild needed someone with skin in the game when this day came, and I am a coward, and I chose you the way one chooses a man to stand between the village and the wolf — hoping the wolf eats him first."

The words landed one at a time, each with its own small weight. Eleven years. The promotions. The trust. The way she had always, always made sure *he* handled the strange entries, the impossible clients, the veiled buyer with no face who purchased royal memory with coins minted in a kingdom that no longer existed.

"You used me."

"I protected you," she said, "badly, for eleven years, and it has run out, and I am telling you the truth now because it is the last thing of value I have left to give." She reached into her coat and drew out a single sheet of vellum, sealed with the guild's oldest stamp — the one Orsen had always assumed was decorative, some founder's vanity. A hand holding a lamp over an open book. "The terms of surrender. Your sealed memory, named as collateral, due at moonrise tomorrow. Refuse, and the collector takes the whole vault — including, I suspect, the keeper of ledgers, who has after all handled stolen goods for a decade and knows exactly where everything sleeps."

Orsen took the vellum. His hands were steady, which surprised him; inside, something was moving furniture.

"Why tell me tonight? Why not simply take it from my desk while I slept?"

"Because—" and here, at last, Maren Voss's composure cracked, just at one corner, like ice at a river's edge, "because I read the terms closely, Orsen, and there is a clause. Collateral surrendered *knowingly* discharges the debtor's portion of the debt entire. Surrendered unknowingly, it buys nothing. They don't want your memory, child. They want your *consent*. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole of the law, and I think — I think it may be the whole of everything."

He looked down at the vellum. The clause was there, written in a script that hurt to read — letters that seemed to rearrange themselves politely whenever he nearly grasped them, like a dream resisting retelling. But the shape of it was clear enough.

And beneath the legal language, one line in plain trade-tongue, almost gentle:

*That which was taken to survive may be returned to live.*

"Guildmaster." He heard his own voice go quiet and level, the voice he used for closing accounts. "When the gods went silent — when the guild started selling — did anyone ever ask *why* they slept?"

Maren stared at him. "What?"

"Nine hundred years as heaven's cellar. Six hundred years of robbery. And in all your archives, all your founding charters and deep registries — did anyone record what the gods were *doing*? Why they stopped answering?" He tapped the vellum. "Because I've spent three days watching a candle burn that hasn't burned in three centuries, and I've watched memories wake in their vials like men snoring in a burning house, and I keep thinking: they're not gathering their property. They're gathering *provisions*. And I would very much like to know, before I hand over the only piece of myself I cannot replace, whether I'm paying a debt or stocking a larder."

For a long moment the only sound was the hum of the vessels, rising now, faintly, all together — hundreds of stored griefs and joys and sins stirring toward the surface of their jars, like sleepers turning toward a window before the thunder arrives.

Maren picked up her lamp. "Come upstairs," she said. "There's a priestess waiting in my office who arrived an hour before you did, wearing the colors of the mountain god, asking questions I don't have answers to. She says her god sent her to find the guild's keeper of ledgers."

"She'll be disappointed. I mostly keep them closed."

"She also said," Maren went on, watching him carefully, "to tell you that the candle in your Long Vault is *hers* — that she lit it three hundred years ago, and she would very much like to know why it's burning again. And Orsen—" the Guildmaster's voice dropped, and for the first time since he'd known her, she sounded less like an institution and more like a tired woman at the end of a very long shift, "she knew your name before I gave it to her."

Orsen stood in the humming dark, holding the terms of his own surrender, and understood that the ledger had finally turned: he was no longer the one keeping accounts.

Somewhere below the mountain, something ancient had opened his books.

And it had found his page.
