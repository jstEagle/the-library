## 6. A Thief and a Priestess

Orsen had robbed forty-one shrines in his life, and he had never once felt good about it.

He stood now in the doorway of the smallest one — a chapel of the Quiet God wedged between a cooper's yard and a tenement that leaned on it like a tired drunk — and told himself this was not robbery. He was only looking. The Vellum Guild kept its ledgers balanced by knowing what everyone else kept hidden, and the priestess Sefa had been hiding something for six days. He intended to know what.

The shrine smelled of tallow and old stone and, faintly, of rain that hadn't fallen yet. Candles guttered in their niches. A beggar slept under the altar, which Orsen approved of; a god who let the poor sleep indoors was at least a god with his priorities straight.

And there she was.

Sefa knelt before the altar with her back to him, her grey robes pooled around her like spilled milk, and she was talking. Not praying — talking, the way you talk to a stubborn relative. Her voice was low and worn smooth, a river-stone voice.

"You can't just *do* that," she was saying. "You were quiet for three hundred years. We built an entire liturgy around your silence. There are hymns about how you don't answer. I have *memorized* them."

Orsen eased the door shut behind him. It clicked, and she went still.

"I know someone's there," she said without turning. "The candles leaned toward you. They do that when someone who's stolen something comes in. It's very rude of them."

"The candles lean?"

"Everything leans, lately." She rose and turned, and up close her face was younger than her voice — mid-thirties, sharp-boned, with the particular tiredness of someone who has been awake with a secret for too long. Her eyes went over him the way a customs officer goes over a merchant's cart. "You're the guild's man. Halloran. The ledger-keeper."

"You know me?"

"I know your handwriting. You catalogued the votive offerings we sold to the guild in the spring. Your figures are beautiful. It's unsettling." She folded her hands. "So. Either you've come to rob me, or you've come because you're frightened. Which is it?"

Orsen considered lying. He was very good at lying; it was practically a guild trade. But there was something about a woman who talked to her god like a wayward uncle that made lying feel like bringing a knife to a conversation that hadn't asked for one.

"Frightened," he said.

"Good. Honest men frighten more usefully." She gestured to the bench beside the altar. "Sit. Would you like wine? It's sacramental. Technically stealing it is blasphemy, so I'd be doing you a favor if I gave it freely."

* * *

Sefa watched the ledger-keeper drink her god's wine and tried to decide whether he was a danger or a fellow drowning man.

He sat like a man trying to take up less space than he occupied — shoulders drawn in, hands wrapped around the cup as though it might be repossessed. His eyes kept going to the altar, then away, then back, the way a man looks at a debt he can't pay.

"You've been watching me," he said.

"You've been watching me first. Four days. You stand across the street from the shrine in the mornings wearing a coat that wants people to think you're poor, which is a lie, because your boots cost more than my roof." She refilled his cup. "I watch everyone. It's the job. Most people come in, ask their god for things, leave. You came in and *audited*."

"I did no such thing."

"You counted the candle stubs. You read the donation board twice. You looked at my reliquary the way a man looks at a strongbox." She smiled, and made sure it reached her eyes, because disarming people was also the job. "I'm not angry. I'm curious. Why does the Vellum Guild care what happens in a poor shrine nobody attends?"

The question landed exactly where she'd aimed it. She saw it land — a small flinch behind his calm, a ledger closing somewhere behind his face.

Because she knew what the guild cared about. Six nights ago her god — her silent, ornamental, three-hundred-years-silent god — had answered a beggar's prayer with a precision that had left the man weeping on the flagstones and Sefa questioning every hour of her vocation. And the prayer the beggar had prayed was simple: *let me remember my mother's face.* And the god had reached into the air and pulled down something that shimmered, and pressed it into the man's chest, and the man had sobbed with recognition.

A memory. Given back.

Which meant somewhere, a memory had been taken. And there was exactly one institution in Caerlum that traded in taken things.

"Your guild," she said quietly, "sells memories."

Orsen set down the cup with great care, as though it might go off.

"That," he said, "is a slanderous oversimplification."

"Then simplify it correctly."

He looked at her for a long moment. And here was the thing she would remember afterward: he decided to tell the truth, and it visibly cost him, like a man paying out coin he'd sworn never to spend.

"We don't sell memories," he said. "We keep them. People bring us what they can't carry — griefs, shames, whole years they'd rather not own — and we seal them in vellum and glass and store them. Some clients later sell their stored memories to others. That's the trade. We're not thieves, priestess. We're a bank for pain."

"And where do the deposits come from? Does everyone walk in willingly?"

His silence answered her.

"That's what I thought," she said. "My god woke up six days ago after three centuries of silence. Do you know what he said to me? Not in words. In — pressure. Like a hand on the back of my neck. He said: *someone is holding what is mine.*" She leaned forward. "I don't think your bank is full of pain, Master Halloran. I think it's full of stolen goods. And I think the owners are starting to wake up and want them back."

For the first time since he'd entered, Orsen Halloran looked genuinely afraid. Not of her. Of the sentence itself, as though she'd read out a figure from a ledger he'd hoped was lost.

"You felt that," he said. "The reaching."

"Every night. Stronger."

"So do I." He laughed, but there was nothing in it. "Priestess, I'm going to tell you something I haven't told anyone, and I need you to understand that I despise telling it. Something in the guild's vault is waking up. Something old. And I believe — professionally, as a keeper of accounts — that when it finishes waking, it will want everything in our inventory returned. Every memory. Every sealed year. Including—"

He stopped.

"Including yours," Sefa said softly.

His head came up sharply. "How could you possibly—"

"Because you flinch when I say the word *inventory*. Because you count things when you're anxious — your fingers move, right now, counting something. And because you came to a shrine at midnight to sit with a stranger rather than sleep, and men only do that when they're carrying something they can't put down." She gentled her voice. "What did you take, Orsen?"

"Not from anyone else." His voice had gone rough. "From myself. Whatever it was — I paid to forget it. And lately, at night, I can feel it down in the vault like a coal under ash. Warm. Getting warmer. As if it knows it's wanted."

The candles leaned again — all of them, together, toward Orsen, like flowers toward sun. Sefa felt her god attend, vast and patient, and her skin prickled.

"He's listening," she whispered. "He never listens. Three hundred years, and now he hangs on every word like a starving man at a bakery window."

"Is that bad?"

"I don't know yet. That's what frightens me." She looked at him across the little shrine, at this dry, careful, frightened man clutching a cup of communion wine, and felt something in her chest crack slightly along a line she hadn't known was there. "You came here to investigate me. I investigated you right back. And underneath all of it, we're two people sitting up at night because we're afraid of the same dark. Isn't that ridiculous?"

"A little," Orsen admitted. "Though in my experience most alliances start as mutual surveillance."

"Does it? What's your experience of alliances?"

"Short."

She laughed — really laughed, for the first time in six days, and saw him almost smile in response, the expression arriving on his face like a man testing ice. Then his face sobered.

"Sefa. If your god is collecting debts — and mine is a debtor — then we're on opposite sides of the same ledger. Why help me?"

"Because I've been praying to a silent god my whole life, and now that he's awake, all he talks about is *taking*." She turned her cup slowly on her knee. "I loved him silent. I don't yet know if I love him awake. And I'd like to find out whether the god I serve is the thing demanding payment — or whether something is *making* him demand it. There's a difference. There has to be."

"And if there isn't?"

"Then I'll need a thief," she said. "Someone who knows how stolen things are moved, and stored, and sold. Someone whose handwriting is beautiful." She extended her hand. "Wary alliance. Mutual surveillance. Terrible wine."

He took it. His grip was cool and precise, and he held on perhaps half a second longer than the transaction required.

"One condition," Orsen said. "Whatever you learn about me — whatever your god shows you — you tell me first. Before your order. Before anyone."

"Why?"

"Because I paid good money not to know some things about myself. I'd like the courtesy of a warning before the bill arrives."

Something in his face made her heart ache, and she hated it, and agreed anyway.

"Wary alliance," she said. "Now finish the wine. It's blasphemous to waste it, and after tonight, we're both probably damned regardless."

* * *

They talked until the candles burned to pools.

It started as strategy — vault layouts, liturgical calendars, which guild clerks could be bribed and which merely blackmailed — but somewhere past midnight it drifted, the way conversations do when two exhausted people stop guarding the doors.

He learned that she had entered the priesthood at nine because her father died and the god's silence had seemed, to a grieving child, like a kind of company: a grief so large even heaven didn't interrupt it. She learned that he kept a cat he described as "a colleague" and fed it better than himself. She learned that he could not abide the sound of a knife being sharpened, and went pale when she asked why, and she did not press, because she was beginning to understand that Orsen Halloran was a locked room, and that the lock was bought and paid for.

"What was your brother like?" she asked at one point — she couldn't have said why; the question simply arrived, fully formed, the way prayers do.

Orsen went very still.

"I don't have a brother," he said.

The candles leaned toward him. All of them. Together.

Neither of them spoke for a moment. Outside, the tenement creaked, and somewhere a dog barked at nothing, and Sefa felt her god lean in through the walls of his own shrine, attentive as a held breath.

"No," Orsen said again, quieter, to the candles rather than to her. "No, I don't. Forget I said anything. There's nothing to forget."

"Yes," Sefa said gently. "I can see that."

He looked at her then with something raw moving behind his professional calm — terror and gratitude fighting for the same ground — and she understood that she had just seen the shape of the wound without seeing the wound: a brother-shaped absence, a knife-shaped silence, a memory sealed so deep that even its owner's mouth denied it while his god's candles mourned it.

She said nothing. She poured him more wine instead.

* * *

He left before dawn, as thieves do.

At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and did not turn around. "Priestess. If your god demands payment of me — when he does — send word before you send anyone."

"You think I'd deliver you to him?"

"I think you serve him. And I think love of a god and love of a person can end up pointing different directions, and I'd rather know which way you're pointed before it matters." He glanced back, just enough that she caught the edge of his profile against the candlelight. "I've spent my life watching people trade what they love for what they owe. I'd hate to add you to the inventory."

Then he was gone into the blue dark, and Sefa stood alone in her shrine among the leaning candles, holding two cups and an alliance she wasn't sure either of them could honor.

Her god pressed against the world like a tide against a door.

*Give him to me,* the pressure said, gentle as snowfall. *Give me what he buried, and I will forgive the guild everything.*

Sefa closed her eyes.

"Not tonight," she said aloud, to the god she had loved silent and feared awake. "Tonight he drank your wine as a guest. Let that count for something."

The pressure receded — slowly, reluctantly, like a tide that had decided to be patient.

And far across the sleeping city, in a palace above the harbor, something wearing a king's face lit the first candle of an old rite, and smiled with borrowed lips at the taste of approaching harvest.
