## 7. The Memory That Bites

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in the Vellum Guild's deep vaults at three in the morning, and I have come to think of it as a colleague. It does not gossip. It does not ask questions. It simply stands beside you while you work, patient as dust, and it was the only colleague I had ever fully trusted.

Which is why it felt like a betrayal when, on the night I finally decided to open my own sealed memory, the silence seemed to lean away from me.

I told myself it was draft. Vaults are drafty. Vaults are supposed to be drafty; a certain coolness preserves the wares. A memory kept too warm goes soft, the way butter does, and begins to spread into things it ought not touch. So: draft. Certainly not the vault itself deciding, in its ancient and nonverbal way, that what I was about to do was unwise.

I set my lamp on the reading table — the good table, the one with the iron rings bolted into its corners for exactly this sort of work — and I laid out my instruments the way I had done ten thousand times for other people's memories. Silver tongs. The cradle of black glass. A saucer of well-water, because even a thief deserves a courtesy. And last, from the inner pocket of my coat, where it had lived against my heart for nineteen years, a vial no bigger than my thumb.

It looked so ordinary. That was always the joke of the trade, if you could call it a joke: the most devastating thing a person owns fits in a thimble. I have held bottled griefs that could level a city's worth of weeping, and they sat in their shelves looking like plum wine.

This one had no label. Guild law forbade labeling one's own work — an odd modesty, considering we labeled everything else down to the year and weather — but I had never needed a label. I knew it by weight. Nineteen years of carrying a thing teaches you its weight the way a mother knows her child's cry in a crowded market.

"Right," I said aloud, because talking to oneself in a vault is not madness, it is procedure. "Orsen Halloran, keeper of ledgers, age forty-one, in sound mind." I paused. "In *mostly* sound mind."

The silence did not laugh. It never does. But I imagined, just then, that it wanted to.

Here is what I had told myself for nineteen years, in the small hours, when the vault was quiet and the ledger was balanced and there was nothing left between me and my own thoughts: that I had taken the memory out of myself the way a man draws a splinter. Cleanly. Necessarily. Because the alternative was dying, and a man who dies cannot keep excellent records.

And here is what I had begun to suspect, ever since the prayer candle in Vault Nine lit itself after three hundred years of dark, ever since the client with no face bought a king's memory with my own handwriting on the bill of sale: that I had not drawn a splinter. I had buried a body. And bodies, in this new world where old gods were waking up and remembering every debt ever owed them, have a way of knocking.

The guildmaster's words were still in my ears. *Your sealed memory is collateral, Halloran. It has been collateral since the day you made it. The gods are collecting.* He had said it with the weary gentleness of a man delivering a tax notice, which in a sense he was.

So. If the gods intended to open my memory whether I consented or not, then I would open it first, on my own terms, at my own table, with my own tongs. There is a dignity in auditing your own accounts before the auditor arrives. My father taught me that, back before — well. Back before whatever it was I had paid so much to forget.

I fitted the vial into the black glass cradle. It settled with a small click, snug as an egg in a nest, and I confess my hands were not steady. I am not ashamed of that. Any appraiser whose hands are steady when handling an unknown quantity is either lying or dead inside, and I have met both kinds, and neither retires well.

"One look," I promised myself. "One look, and then we decide what to do about it."

I turned the key.

* * *

You should understand how opening a memory works, because otherwise what happened next will sound like mere horror rather than the precise, professional catastrophe it was.

A stored memory is a room. The keeper builds a door into it — a keyed door, a warded door, a door with our guild sigil worked into the hinges — and when the client wishes to remember, the door opens and the room breathes out, and for a few minutes the client stands inside their own past again, warm and whole. Then the door closes. Simple. Elegant. We are very proud of it.

But a memory that has been *sealed against its owner* — that is different work. That is not a door. That is a wall, mortared with will and guilt and whatever else the thief had in abundance on the worst night of his life. To open it, you must find the seam. And the seam, in my considerable experience, is always wherever the memory hurts most.

I found the seam in four minutes. I know because I count everything; it is both my gift and my affliction.

Four minutes of careful probing along the vial's surface, feeling for the thin place, the way you'd run your thumb along a jar lid looking for the tab. And I found it — a hairline weakness near the base, cold to the touch, colder than glass has any right to be — and I thought, with the fatuous relief of a man who believes his troubles are nearly over:

*There you are.*

Gentle reader — no. There is no gentle reader in a vault at three in the morning. Let me say it plainly instead: I was a fool, and the memory knew it before I did.

The moment my awareness touched the seam, something on the other side *noticed* me.

Not stirred. Not shifted. *Noticed* — the way a spider notices the fly that has landed politely on the outermost ring of its web and is apologizing for the intrusion. I felt the attention of it turn toward me through three layers of glass and nineteen years of stone-cold denial, and I heard — not with my ears, ears are useless for this — I heard a voice that was mine and yet arranged all wrong, pitched high and young and absolutely furious, say:

*No.*

"I'm sorry," I said, which is a stupid thing to say to yourself, and also the truest thing I have ever said. "I only want to look."

*NO,* said the thing in the vial, and the wall came apart all at once, not opening but *shattering*, and the room that breathed out was not a room at all.

It was a country.

I have stood inside ten thousand memories. I know their architecture. A memory of a wedding smells of lilies and nerves; a memory of a battlefield tastes of copper and wet wool. They have edges. They have exits. Even the worst of them, the drowned children and the burning houses, even those have edges, because human memory is mercifully finite and the mind builds walls around its ruins.

This had no edges. This went on. Hills I did not know, under a sky the color of a bruise healing, and a road, and orchards — ash orchards, white-barked, though why that word rose in me I couldn't say — and somewhere ahead of me on that road, someone was waiting, someone whose name lived in my mouth like a stone I'd swallowed so long ago I'd mistaken it for part of my body—

And the screaming started.

It came from everywhere and nowhere, from behind the hills and beneath the road, and it took me three full seconds to understand the worst part, the part I still cannot write without my hand shaking: the screaming was not one voice. It was two. Two voices, screaming the same scream, in unison, like a round, like a song children sing—

One of them was mine.

I reached for the key. I want that recorded in whatever ledger keeps the accounts of fools: I reached for the key immediately, professionally, with admirable presence of mind, and the key was gone. Not lost — *gone*, as in the concept of keys had been revoked, as in the memory had eaten the exit along with the edges, and I understood with a clarity that felt almost like humor that this was the defense. Not a lock. A country. You cannot leave a country by finding its door, because countries do not have doors. Countries have borders, and borders are made of everything you're willing to lose.

The two voices screamed. The bruise-sky pressed down. And far off on the white road, the someone who was waiting began, very slowly, to turn around.

"No," I said, and this time it came out as a whisper, because some part of me — the part that had built this wall, the part that had been willing to lose *everything*, that was the mortar, don't you see, the mortar was willingness — some part of me knew exactly who was turning around, and knew that if I saw the face, the wall would never hold again, sealed or unsealed, because some knowledge cannot be re-bottled. "Don't. Please. I take it back. I'll balance the ledgers, I'll pay the tithe, I'll—"

The figure raised one hand.

And that was when the nosebleed started, hot and sudden, dripping onto the reading table, onto the guild sigil, onto nineteen years of careful bookkeeping, and I thought distantly, *that will stain, that will absolutely stain, Master Corvane will have my hide for staining the good table* — because the mind is a coward and grasps at any small ledger when the great one opens.

The figure on the road took a step toward me.

I did the only thing left to a thief caught in his own theft.

I let go.

* * *

Letting go of a memory you are inside of is like letting go of a rope over a ravine. Everything in you screams to hold on, because holding on is identity, holding on is *here*, and letting go is falling. But sometimes the rope is the thing pulling you down.

I let go of the country. I let go of the road and the orchards and the two-voiced scream. I let go of the name-shaped stone in my mouth. I fell — not downward, but *inward*, back through the shatter-point, back through the broken seal, and I hit the reading table of the Vellum Guild's deep vault like a man hitting water after a long fall, all breath and flailing and cold.

Then there was lamplight. Real lamplight, yellow and ordinary and smelling faintly of tallow. Then there was the ceiling, which I had memorized years ago (four hundred and eleven stones, three cracks, one suspicious stain shaped like a fish) and which had never looked so beautiful. Then there was blood in my mouth and blood on the table and, distantly, the sound of the vault door opening, because I had locked it, hadn't I? Hadn't I locked it?

Apparently not well enough.

"Orsen?"

Sefa's voice. Of course it was Sefa's voice. The gods wake up after three centuries and the first thing they do is send the one person in the world I cannot bear to be seen like this.

"I'm fine," I said, into the table.

"You're bleeding on the inventory."

"It's a small donation. The guild accepts donations."

She crossed the room — I heard her boots, and something else, the soft whisper of her vestments — and then her hands were on my face, tilting it up toward the light, and her breath caught in a way I felt in my chest like a hooked fish.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, Orsen. Your eyes."

"What about them?" My voice sounded like gravel being polite.

"They're—" She stopped. Started again. Priestesses are trained in composure; watching her lose hers twice in one sentence was somehow more frightening than the screaming country. "They're full of somewhere else. There's — there's sky in them. Hills. Like a window painted on the inside of you." Her thumb brushed my cheekbone, came away red. "What did you open?"

"Nothing," I said. "Nothing opened. The seal held."

We both looked at the vial in its cradle. The black glass was intact. The silver tongs lay neatly where I'd left them. The saucer of well-water had a single drop of my blood in it, blooming pink, like the world's smallest and saddest flower.

Everything was exactly as it should be, except for the blood, and the shaking, and the fact that I now knew — the way you know a word is on the tip of your tongue — that somewhere inside me was a country with ash orchards and a road, and someone standing on that road with their hand raised, waiting for me to remember their face.

And except, perhaps, for the other thing. The small thing. The thing I almost didn't catch, because terror drowns out subtlety.

When the seal shattered — for half a heartbeat, less — I had seen the vault. Not this vault. *A* vault. Older. Deeper. With shelves carved directly out of living rock, and upon those shelves, thousands upon thousands of vials, each glowing faintly, each pulsing in slow unison like the heart of some enormous sleeping animal.

Like prayers. Thousands of prayers, stored. Hoarded.

Fed upon.

"Orsen." Sefa's voice pulled me back. She had fetched the well-water herself and was pressing a cool cloth to my nose with the brisk tenderness of a woman who has bandaged worse. "Whatever you saw — and you saw *something*, don't insult us both — you can tell me tomorrow. Tonight you need sleep, and I need to check the shrine records, because I dreamed of a mountain last night, a mountain with a red light under it, and my god has been *humming* since sundown, and I don't think those things are unrelated to whatever you've done to your face."

"The mountain," I said. My tongue felt thick. "Which mountain?"

She paused, cloth in hand, and looked at me with an expression I would learn to dread in the weeks to come — the expression of a priestess doing arithmetic she wished she didn't know how to do.

"The one under Caerlum," she said. "Why? What did you see?"

I opened my mouth to say *nothing*, because *nothing* had served me faithfully for nineteen years and I saw no reason to dismiss a loyal servant.

But the vault of living rock rose behind my eyes again, all those vials pulsing in the dark, and for the first time since I was a boy I heard myself say the truth instead:

"Sefa," I said, "I think I saw where the prayers go."
