## 8. The Rite Beneath the Mountain

The archive beneath the Shrine of the Listening Ear had not been opened in Sefa's lifetime, which was why it smelled like a tomb that had been pretending to be a library for four hundred years.

She had told the archivist, old Brother Tammas, that she needed to consult the foundational texts for a sermon on gratitude. This was a lie, and lying sat poorly on her the way borrowed shoes sit poorly on a pilgrim — functional, but you kept noticing them. Brother Tammas had looked at her over his spectacles for a long moment, and then he had handed her the key without a word, because Brother Tammas had been a priest long enough to know that when a woman's god has started answering prayers again after three centuries of silence, she is going to go looking for answers whether or not anyone gives her a key.

"Mind the third shelf," he'd said. "It bites."

"The shelf bites."

"It did last time I dusted it. There's a preservation ward on it that's gotten cranky with age." He'd paused. "Like me."

So now Sefa stood alone in the round stone chamber with its shelves spiraling up into darkness, holding a lamp, wearing borrowed shoes of a different kind entirely: the calm face of a priestess doing routine research, while underneath it her heart hammered out the rhythm of *the gods are awake, the gods are awake, and one of them knows my name.*

The third shelf did, in fact, bite. She yanked her hand back and sucked at the sting, glaring at the row of leather spines as if they were misbehaving children. "I am trying to save the world," she informed them. "You could be gracious."

The shelf, being a shelf, declined to respond.

* * *

She found what she was looking for by accident, which Sefa had come to believe was how all important things were found. She had been hunting for the *Codex of Petitions*, the great catalog of every prayer the old gods had ever answered — a book so vast it supposedly required its own cart — but the Codex was missing from its cradle, and in its place someone had wedged a much smaller volume, bound in grey goatskin, with no title on the spine.

Inside, in handwriting that started crisp and grew shakier page by page, was an account.

*Being a true record of the Rite of Unbinding, as performed beneath Mount Caerlum in the Year of the Long Hunger, set down by my own hand so that no one may ever again claim ignorance of what we did.*

Sefa sat down on the floor. It seemed wise.

The account was written by a priestess named Ilvaen, who had served a god called the Keeper Below — though even in the first pages, Ilvaen admitted this name was a comfort rather than a truth. The thing beneath the mountain had no name the priests could pronounce. It was old, older than the pantheon above; it was the god of *what is given*, and it was starving.

Not hungry. Starving. There was a distinction Ilvaen took three pages to make, and Sefa read those pages twice, feeling the temperature of the room drop around her.

A hungry god wants. A starving god *takes*. And the difference between the two, written in ink that browned toward rust as the account went on, was the difference between a beggar at your door and a fire in your rafters.

The old gods of the pantheon — the Listener, the Weaver, the Mother-of-Rows, all the gentle named ones whose shrines dotted the realm like buttons on a coat — had not always been the only gods. Before them there had been the First Faith, and the First Faith had understood something the newer religions had preferred to forget: that prayer is not free. Every prayer spoken is a small coin of the self, and coins must go somewhere. The new gods had built their power on collecting those coins and never saying where the vault was.

The vault was under the mountain. It had always been under the mountain. The starving god below was not a rival to the pantheon. It was their *bank*, their reservoir, the deep cistern into which every answered prayer drained — and in exchange for its silence and its service, it had been promised a tithe. A portion. A taste.

And then, four hundred years ago, the guilds of men had learned to bottle memory, and the tithes had stopped.

Sefa turned pages with hands that were not quite steady. Ilvaen described the negotiations, the bribes, the clever merchants of the Vellum Guild who had convinced the kings of men that memories could be sold, stored, traded — that grief could be excised like a rotten tooth and forgotten before the wound closed. And every memory bottled was a memory that would never be prayed over, never grieved aloud, never given. The flow of offerings to the thing below had slowed to a trickle, then to nothing.

"We have dammed a river," Ilvaen wrote, "and congratulated ourselves on the dry land."

The rest of the account was short. In the Year of the Long Hunger, a faction of priests — Ilvaen among them, and she did not flinch from writing her own guilt — had gone beneath the mountain to perform the Rite of Unbinding: to open the reservoir and pour everything out at once. Every stored prayer, every hoarded offering, all of it delivered in a single flood to the god that had waited so patiently to be paid.

It should have ended the drought of miracles. Instead —

Here the handwriting deteriorated into scratches, and then recovered, barely:

*We were wrong about what unbinding means. The rite does not release the waters. It releases the DAM. And a dam does not hold back a river. It holds back a sea. We felt the first inch of it and eleven of us died of awe before the senior among us had the wit to seal the mountain again. What remains below is not a god who can be paid. It is a debt that has been compounding for four hundred years, and if it is ever collected in full, there will be nothing left of the realm above but the shape of having been here.*

*Seal the mountain. Seal the mountain. Seal the*

The sentence never finished. The remaining pages were blank except for one line, pressed hard enough to tear through three sheets beneath it:

*If any find this: watch for the harvest moon. The rite can only be sung when the moon is full and fat and low, close enough to touch. That is when the door between what is owed and what is owned grows thin.*

Sefa closed the book and discovered she had been holding her breath long enough that her vision had gone sparkly at the edges. She breathed. The lamp flame leaned and steadied.

Then she did the arithmetic she had been refusing to do since she'd opened the cover, counting backward from the almanac hanging in the shrine kitchen, the one with the little drawings of saints beside each month.

The harvest moon was nineteen days away.

* * *

Orsen Halloran arrived at the shrine two days later looking like a man who had lost an argument with his own conscience and was hoping nobody could tell.

He came in through the side door, the one used by tradesmen and penitents who didn't want to be seen, and he stood dripping rainwater in the narthex with his hat in his hands until Sefa finished lighting the evening candles and came to stand in front of him.

"You look terrible," she said warmly.

"Thank you. I've been working on it." He glanced past her at the sanctuary, at the candle flames standing perfectly straight in air that had no business being still. "Is it true? What they're saying in the city. That the Listener answered a fishwife's prayer last week. Gave her son back from the fever."

"Healed him. Not gave him back — he hadn't died yet." Sefa folded her hands. "Though I take your point. Yes. It's true. And it wasn't just the Listener. The Mother-of-Rows untangled a weaver's debts in Corren's Ford. The whole pantheon is awake and answering, and everyone in the realm is so delighted that nobody is asking the obvious question."

"Which is?"

"*Why now.*" She let that hang in the incense-smoke between them. "Four hundred years of silence, Orsen. Gods don't wake up out of nowhere. They wake up because something changed. And I think I know what."

His eyes — tired, sharp, the eyes of a man who audited other people's souls for a living — narrowed slightly. "You've been reading."

"I've been reading." She tilted her head toward the archive stairs. "Come down. Bring your ledger-brain. You're going to want it."

* * *

He read Ilvaen's account standing up, which Sefa thought was either very disciplined or very frightened, and she suspected both. He read it twice. Then he stood for a long time with one finger resting on the torn final page, and when he spoke, his voice had the careful flatness of a man reading figures aloud.

"The guild's founding charter dates from roughly this period. I always assumed it was coincidence — that memory-trading arose independently of the religious upheavals." His finger tapped the page once. "It didn't, did it. The guild didn't just profit from the damming. The guild *built* the dam. We're the reason the tithe stopped."

"You're the reason," Sefa said gently. "You personally are approximately forty years old and cannot be blamed for a four-hundred-year-old crime."

"I'm a keeper of ledgers, Sefa. Blame is a matter of accounting. Somebody has to carry it or it goes nowhere." But he said it absently, already turning pages again, and she watched him arrive — as she had arrived — at the passage about the harvest moon.

"Nineteen days," he said quietly.

"Seventeen, now."

"And the king—" He stopped. Started again. "The thing wearing Aldric Caerlum's crown. You've seen it. You've prayed in front of it."

"I have." She remembered the blessing sliding off him like rain off glass, the smile that arrived a half-second too late, the voice that answered her prayer with words that were correct in every particular and alive in none. "And I've been thinking about what Ilvaen wrote. About the rite. Someone has to *perform* it, Orsen. Someone has to open the door and sing. And who in this realm has the authority to gather every soul in the kingdom into one place, at one hour, all praying at once?"

The lamplight caught the hollow places under his eyes. "A king," he said.

"A king. Or something that looks like one." She pulled the almanac page from her sleeve — she had copied it out, because some facts deserved to be carried against the skin — and smoothed it on the shelf. "Here. The harvest moon rises over Mount Caerlum itself. Full, fat, low. Close enough to touch. The impostor isn't preparing a coronation feast, Orsen. He's preparing a collection. Every answered prayer since the gods woke — every miracle the realm has been celebrating — is sitting in the reservoir right now, waiting to be poured. And if the rite completes—"

"—then everything owed gets collected in full." Orsen closed the book with a soft, terrible finality. "Ilvaen says eleven priests died of awe at the first inch of it."

"At the first inch. And that was four hundred years ago, when the reservoir held only a century of hoarded prayers." Sefa heard her own voice go thin and made herself steady it. "Since then? Four hundred years of every grief, every joy, every whispered plea that never got an answer and rotted into something darker? The guild has been bottling memories by the thousand, Orsen. Where do you think the *contents* of those bottles drain to?"

He didn't answer. He had gone very pale, in the way of a man watching columns of numbers resolve into a sum he doesn't want to see.

"There's more," she said, hating herself a little for it. "Ilvaen mentions the rite requires a *key*. Something that belongs to both sides of the door — something stolen and something royal, held together. I don't know what that means yet. But I know the impostor has spent decades buying royal memories through the guild. Your client with no face. The vial with Aldric's childhood in it. If I'm right—"

"Then the theft isn't random. It's *components*." Orsen laughed once, without any humor in it at all. "Of course it is. Everything is inventory. Even apocalypse." He rubbed his eyes. "Seventeen days. To get to the summer palace, get past a court of smiling hollow faces, steal back a king's memory, and stop a rite that four hundred years of clever men built toward without ever knowing it."

"When you say it like that, it sounds difficult."

"When I say it like that, it sounds *impossible*, and I keep the books on impossible things, priestess, and I'm telling you the numbers don't work."

Sefa looked at him — this dry, careful, secretly terrified man who had walked into her shrine out of the rain carrying nothing but a hat and a guilty conscience — and she felt the thing she had been refusing to feel since the night they'd shared stolen wine in the dark, the thing that made lying to him about what she'd seen in his sealed memory feel like swallowing a stone every morning.

"Orsen," she said. "My god spoke to me yesterday. Directly. For the first time since I took my vows."

That got his attention fully. "What did it say?"

"It said: *seventeen days.*" She smiled, and the smile hurt. "Apparently the gods keep almanacs too. Which means this isn't just possible, Orsen. It's *scheduled*. And in my experience, when the divine puts something on a calendar, it also puts someone in the room to do the work. I don't think it's a coincidence that a keeper of ledgers and a priestess of the Listener met over a bottle of bad wine."

"No," Orsen said slowly. "I don't suppose it is." He picked up the grey goatskin book, weighed it in his hands like something that might still bite. "Seventeen days. Then we'd better start walking tonight. The ash orchard road takes six days to the palace if the weather holds, and—" He paused, head tilting, listening to something beyond the walls of the archive.

Beyond the walls, faintly, the shrine bell was ringing. Not the evening bell. Not any scheduled bell. The alarm bell, rung only when—

They climbed the stairs together two at a time and came out into the narthex, where young Brother Pell stood white-faced in the doorway, rain streaming off him, a horse blown and lathered behind him in the yard.

"Beg pardon — sister — sir—" He gulped air. "Word from the capital. The palace criers. The king has—" Pell swallowed. "The king has declared a Great Thanksgiving. Every soul in the realm commanded to make pilgrimage to Mount Caerlum. To give thanks to the waking gods. All together. On the harvest moon."

The rain filled the silence that followed, drumming on the shrine roof like impatient fingers.

Orsen looked at Sefa. "Sixteen days," he said. "He's moved it up. Or—" and his voice dropped, "—or it was never the harvest moon he was waiting for. It was the *crowd*."

Sefa took her travel cloak off its peg.

Outside, far to the north, past the rain and the rooftops, the sky over Mount Caerlum had begun — very faintly, very wrongly — to bruise violet at the edges, like a fruit ripening toward rot.
