## 9. Road Through the Ash Orchards

The ash orchards began three miles out of Millbrook and did not end so much as surrender to the horizon.

Orsen had always assumed the name was poetic — some farmer's fancy, a hundred years stale. It was not. The trees were true ashes, tall and silver-barked, but they grew in rows too straight for any forester's hand, and their leaves turned early each autumn to a color that was not quite gold and not quite grey, as if the trees themselves could not decide whether they were dying or being paid.

"They were planted by the first guild," Sefa said, riding half a length ahead of him on a mule she had named Bishop. "Before the Vellum Guild, before ledgers. Memory-keepers walked this road with the harvest on their backs."

"You know an alarming amount about my profession's embarrassing ancestors."

"I know an alarming amount about everything. It's the god's fault." She said it lightly, but her hand went to the little iron bell at her throat — the one that had not rung since the gods woke, except once, in a beggar's alley, when it had rung like a struck anvil and Orsen had felt it in his teeth.

They had been on the road four days. Long enough for Orsen to learn that Sefa sang badly and knew it; long enough for her to learn that he counted things when he was nervous — fence posts, crows, the times she looked back at him when she thought he wasn't watching. He was a ledger-keeper. He noticed what was written down, even when nothing was.

The villages were the trouble.

* * *

The first one was called Harrow's End, and it should have been called Harrow's Beginning, because everyone in it seemed to have started over from scratch that morning, giddy as children.

A woman stood in her doorway handing out bread to strangers. Not alms-giving — Orsen knew alms, had audited them — but giving, reckless and delighted, as though bread were a thing she'd only just discovered she could make. A boy chased a dog through the square, both of them shrieking with joy. In the shrine at the square's center — every village had one now, shrines dusted off and re-lit like houses after a long tenancy — a man knelt with his hands raised, laughing and weeping in equal measure.

"He prayed for rain last week," the innkeeper told them, setting down stew neither of them had ordered. "Three hundred years the old god under the mountain never answered nobody. Then my wife prays for rain, and it rains. Not just rains — rains *proper*. Soft, all night, exactly where the barley wanted it. And Tam there—" he nodded at the laughing man— "prayed his dead mother's ring would turn up, and it did. Inside a fish. You can't make that up."

"No," Orsen agreed. "You'd need a ledger."

He meant it as a joke. The innkeeper took it as reverence and pressed a second bowl on him.

It was the precision that troubled him. Orsen had spent twenty years cataloguing answered prayers the way other men catalogued debts — as rumors, exaggerations, the wishful arithmetic of desperate people. Real answers were rare as honest tax collectors. But these were exact. Rain where the barley wanted it. A ring inside a fish. And in the next village, a lame girl whose leg was whole, and in the one after that, a widow who had prayed to see her husband's face again and now swore the man who came to mend her roof had his smile, his hands, his way of humming — though he was thirty years too young and insisted he'd always lived there.

"Careless," Sefa murmured that night, watching the village dance around a bonfire. They danced badly and often, these villagers, as though joy were a muscle gone stiff from disuse. "That's what frightens me. They're praying for anything. Everything. Prayers are supposed to cost something. That's the whole architecture of devotion — you ask because you lack, and the lack teaches you something. This—" she gestured at the bonfire, at a man dancing with a wheelbarrow— "this is a child left alone in a sweetshop."

"And the shopkeeper," Orsen said quietly, "is under the mountain. Starving for three centuries. And now the door's open."

She looked at him. Firelight did kind things to her face, and unkind things to his certainty. "You've been listening to me preach."

"I've been auditing you. It's different." He passed her the stolen wine — well, purchased wine, purchased from a man who had forgotten he owned a cellar, which was nearly the same thing. "Sefa. If every prayer in the realm gets poured into that thing at the harvest moon—"

"Then it eats well," she said. "And then it wants more. Gods are like that. So are guildmasters." She drank. "So stop making that face and dance with me, Orsen Halloran. We may have until the harvest moon, and I intend to spend some of it badly."

He danced. He was terrible at it. She laughed at him with her whole self, and he filed the sound away somewhere no ledger would ever reach.

* * *

The trouble in Ashford-on-Wold — the fifth village, the one where everything changed — began with a bell.

Not Sefa's bell. A chapel bell, cracked and green with age, ringing noon though the sun sat low and red in the west. The whole village stopped mid-stride, mid-sentence, mid-chew, and turned toward the sound with expressions of such naked hunger that Orsen's hand found his knife without asking permission.

"It's a calling," Sefa breathed. "I haven't heard a true calling since—" Her bell at her throat shivered, hummed, went silent. "Since before. Something is answering here. Now. In person."

The villagers moved toward the chapel like water finding a drain. Orsen and Sefa were carried along in the current, which was how he came to be standing in a cramped, candle-thick chapel watching a figure on the altar steps that wore a crown of woven ash branches and a face like a thumbprint in wet clay — features almost there, almost human, smudged at the edges as if someone had begun sculpting a man and been called away.

"Hollow," Sefa whispered. "One of the impostor's. A herald."

The hollow thing spoke, and its voice was many voices slightly out of step, a choir rehearsing in separate rooms. *"The King beneath gives freely. The King above asks only small things. Bring your sorrows. Bring your memories of sorrow. Lay them on the stone, and be light."*

And the villagers *went*. One by one they climbed the steps and knelt, and the thing in the ash crown laid its fingers on each brow, and Orsen watched — with the professional horror of a man watching inventory walk out the door unpaid — as something left them. He could see it. Twenty years among bottled memories had taught him to see memory moving: a faint silver shimmer, like breath on cold glass, drawn up out of a skull and swallowed.

The baker who had given them bread gave up the memory of his daughter's wedding day and came down the steps smiling, lighter, empty-eyed in a way that would take weeks to notice and years to mourn, if he ever mourned at all.

"They don't understand," Sefa said. Her voice shook. "They think it's tithing. They think grief is a debt."

"Grief *is* a debt," Orsen said. "That's rather the point of it. You're supposed to carry it, not refinance it to a thing made of wet clay." He was already moving, low, along the chapel wall, because he had seen what she hadn't: the herald's attention sliding past the kneeling villagers, past the candles, and fixing — with the slow, blind patience of a fish — on Sefa.

On the priestess. On the woman wearing an iron bell dedicated to the very god the impostor served, standing in the crowd like a forged coin in a counting-house.

*"You,"* the choir-voice said. The clay face turned. Every villager turned with it, in perfect unison, and Orsen's skin crawled. *"You smell of the robbed one. You carry his silence in your pocket."*

Sefa's hand closed around her bell. "I serve the god beneath the mountain," she said, steady as a litany. "As do you, apparently, though I'd say your tailoring is better than your theology."

*"Give us what he keeps,"* the thing said, drifting down the steps. Villagers parted before it like wheat. *"The sealed one. The thief's own theft. Open your mind, little bell-ringer, and let us look—"*

What happened next, Orsen would later record in his private ledger — the one he kept in his head, the one with no columns for guilt — happened faster than thought, which was fortunate, because thought would have objected.

He stepped between them. He put his hand flat against the herald's clay cheek, the way a guild-keeper takes hold of wares to assess them, and he *took*.

It was absurdly easy. That was the part he hated. The herald was mostly hollow — a vessel, a glove — but something animated it, some scrap of borrowed selfhood, a sliver of memory the impostor had used to shape its servant. Orsen gripped that sliver and pulled it free the way he'd pulled a splinter from Sefa's palm two nights ago, and the thing staggered, its choir-voice collapsing into a single voice, then into nothing, then into a wet clay sound like a pot cracking in a kiln. The ash crown crumbled. The villagers blinked, woke, looked around their chapel as if arriving late to their own lives.

Orsen stood holding a memory that was not his, in a hand that had not trembled, and hated himself with a thoroughness that would have impressed any auditor.

*Easy,* he thought. *Twenty years of restraint and it's still easy. Like riding a bicycle. Like lying.*

"Orsen." Sefa's hand on his arm. "Orsen, look at me. You saved my life."

"I stole," he said. His voice sounded far away. "There's a difference. There has to be a difference or I'm just—" He couldn't finish. *Just what the guild made me.* Just a man who solved problems by taking pieces of people.

The stolen memory sat behind his eyes like a coal. He could feel it wanting out — wanting a home, a skull, anyone's. That was the dirty secret of his trade that no ledger recorded: a taken memory doesn't like being held. It presses. It whispers. *Put me somewhere. Put me in you. You'd like to remember being loved like that, wouldn't you? You'd like to have been that happy once.*

He sealed it in a spare vial with hands that, this time, were not quite steady, and corked it, and labeled it in his neat accountant's script: *Herald, fragment. Provenance: theft. Disposition: pending.*

"Pending," Sefa read over his shoulder. "That's a new word for you."

"There's a column in every ledger called pending. It's where guilty men live."

She studied him for a long moment. Outside, the villagers were emerging into the red evening, confused, already beginning to reach for the holes in themselves and finding edges, like tongues finding a missing tooth. Somewhere a woman started crying — good crying, Orsen hoped, the kind that means something lost has been noticed.

"You could have let it take me," Sefa said quietly. "One priestess against the impostor's plans. Your god — the guild's creditor — would probably have thanked you. It would have simplified everything."

"Yes."

"But you didn't."

"No."

"Why?"

He looked at the vial in his hand. At her. At the terrible arithmetic of himself: a man built out of other people's missing pieces, who had just added one more, and whose only defense was that the piece had been hers to lose.

"Because the ledger has to balance eventually," he said, "and I'd rather it balanced in your favor."

It was not the whole truth. The whole truth was simpler and more frightening: *because I love you, and love, it turns out, is the one entry I can't fudge.* But he was a keeper of ledgers, not a keeper of confessions, and the harvest moon was nine days out, and the summer palace lay two days ahead through the orchards, and some entries, he told himself, could wait.

He should have known better. He was, professionally speaking, an expert in deferred payments — and in what they cost.

* * *

They camped that night in the deep orchard, where the ash trees grew so old and close that the stars came down in pieces through the canopy. Sefa slept first — she slept easily, trustingly, a fact that undid him more than any argument — and Orsen sat awake with the vial, turning it in the moonlight.

The fragment inside stirred. He could feel it pressing at the glass, at the seal, at him. And beneath its whisper he heard another whisper, older, familiar, coming not from the vial but from the sealed place in his own mind where his stolen past sat locked and patient:

*You opened yourself today. Just a crack. Just to pull the splinter out. I felt the draft.*

He held the vial tighter.

Across the dead fire, Sefa murmured in her sleep and turned, and the moonlight caught her face, and Orsen thought — with the calm of a man writing figures he knows will not sum — that she had been looking at him strangely since the chapel. Since he'd touched the herald. As if she'd seen something in the taking that she recognized.

As if, somewhere between the wine and the firelight, she had already read a page of him he'd never shown anyone.

The ash leaves rustled overhead, gold-grey, undecided. Nine days to the harvest moon. Two to the palace. And in the dark of the orchard, Orsen Halloran sat guard over two sealed memories — one in glass, one in his skull — and understood, at last, that the road ahead ran through more than orchards.

It ran through the brother he didn't remember, and Sefa, he suspected, had already met him.
