## 10. The Brother I Don't Remember

Sefa had always believed that the gods spoke in thunder.

Her teachers at the shrine of Veyl had been very clear on this point. The old gods were vast, and vastness required volume. When the mountain god stirred, the faithful would know it: bells cracking, rivers reversing, the sky itself opening like a mouth. Anything less was indigestion, or wind, or a novice's overwrought imagination.

What she had not been told — what no one thought to tell her, perhaps because no one alive remembered — was that when an old god woke from three centuries of hunger and turned his attention upon the world again, it felt less like thunder and more like someone reading over your shoulder.

That was how it began, on the fourth morning out of Caerlum, in the ash orchards where the trees grew pale as bone and dropped their silver leaves without any wind at all.

She was washing her face at a stream, Orsen some twenty paces off fussing with the mule, when the presence settled against her mind like a cat arranging itself on a favored cushion. Warm. Heavy. Utterly unbothered by her opinion of the matter.

*You carry something interesting,* said Veyl-Beneath-the-Mountain, whose proper name was eleven syllables long and tasted of iron and honey, though he permitted her to think of him as *the god*, because eleven syllables before breakfast was asking a great deal of anyone.

"I carry a water skin," Sefa said into the stream. "And a headache."

*The other thing.*

She went still. Water dripped from her chin. Twenty paces away, Orsen was arguing with the mule about a thistle, and the ordinary sound of it — his dry, put-upon voice, the mule's profound indifference — suddenly seemed terribly fragile, like porcelain set out on a ledge.

*I don't know what you mean,* she thought, which was a lie, and both of them knew it, which was the trouble with praying to a god for thirty years. He knew the shape of her silences better than her speech.

*The ledger-keeper,* the god said, with the mild patience of someone who has all the time that ever was. *He carries a sealed memory. Sealed by his own hand, sealed well — the guild taught its thieves properly, once. But seals are only doors, little priestess, and I am very good at doors.*

"Don't," she said aloud, and heard how thin her voice came out.

Orsen glanced up from the thistle. "Talking to yourself?"

"Praying."

"Any answer?"

"No," she lied, and hated it, and washed her face anyway.

*You would keep it from me,* the god observed. It was not quite an accusation. It was worse than that; it was interest. *How loyal. How human. Very well. But understand me, Sefa of the shrine: the debt stands. The guild stole from my storehouses, and the ledger-keeper's hidden thing is named collateral whether you look at it or not. Better you should see it first. Better you should know what you are defending.*

"Why?" she asked. "Why show me?"

There was a pause, and in it she felt something she had never felt from a god in all her years of service: hesitation.

*Because you love him,* Veyl said simply. *And love deserves to be informed.*

Then he withdrew, the way a tide withdraws, leaving her kneeling in cold water with her heart going like a rabbit's.

* * *

She did not look. That was the first day.

She told herself it was principle. A priestess did not pry into souls; confession was offered, never taken. She recited the Litany of Listening while they walked, all nine hundred lines of it, and when Orsen asked why she was muttering she said it was a counting exercise, and he accepted this with the weary courtesy of a man who had learned that priestesses contained depths he did not wish to plumb.

But the god's attention did not leave her. It sat behind her eyes like a lamp left burning in an unused room, and every time she closed her own eyes she saw the door.

Not a real door. Not exactly. The sealed memory showed itself to her sight as a shape — a small black door, iron-banded, set into nothing, standing alone in the gray country behind Orsen's thoughts. It was beautifully made. Whoever had sealed it had done so with love, or terror, or both; the hinges were oiled with care, the lock fitted with a precision that made her professional respect ache. It was the finest piece of memory-work she had ever seen, and it had been built by a frightened person to hold back a flood.

On the second night, camped beneath an orchard tree whose silver leaves ticked softly against each other like patient clocks, she dreamed of the door.

In the dream she stood before it, and the door stood before her, and neither of them moved for a long time.

"You're not real," she told it. "You're a metaphor. Gods don't get to have metaphors about people's private griefs."

The door said nothing, being a door.

"He sealed it for a reason." Her voice cracked in the dream the way voices do, honestly, without dignity. "Whatever's behind you hurt him enough that he chose to cut it out rather than carry it. That's not a crime. That's survival."

Still nothing. But the door had a keyhole, and through the keyhole came light — not the gray light of the dream-country but a warm gold light, kitchen-light, hearth-light, the light of somewhere safe — and from somewhere beyond it, faintly, came laughter. A boy's laughter. High and bright and utterly unguarded, the laugh of someone who had never yet needed to seal anything away.

Sefa woke with tears on her face and no idea whose they were.

Orsen was awake across the dead fire, sitting up with his ledger on his knees even though there was nothing to record, because she had learned that he touched the book the way other men touched talismans. In the dark he looked younger than his years and older than the world, both at once, which she suspected was simply what Orsen looked like when he forgot to arrange his face.

"You dream loudly," he said.

"I'm a priestess. We're trained to project."

"Mm." He turned a page he hadn't read. "Was it a good dream?"

She thought of the boy's laughter, and the door, and the terrible craftsmanship of the lock.

"It was somebody else's," she said.

* * *

On the third day, she looked.

She would like to report that she resisted heroically until some dramatic necessity forced her hand. She cannot. What happened was simpler and more shameful: they stopped at midday in a hollow of the orchards, Orsen went to see to the mule's shoe, and the god's attention leaned against her like a friend leaning close to share a secret, and she was tired, and lonely, and in love with a man who kept a locked room at the center of himself, and she looked.

It was not like reading a book. It was not even like watching. Her god's sight took the seal apart the way rain takes apart a sandcastle — gently, inevitably, with no malice whatsoever — and the memory opened in her like a wound opening, and Sefa knelt in the ash leaves and lived forty years of another person's life in the space between two heartbeats.

A village she did not know, poor and green, terraced up a hillside. A mother with flour on her hands. A father who sang badly and often.

Two boys.

They came to her whole and immediate, the way true memories do, none of the guild's bottled flatness about them. Two boys on a hillside in high summer, and the elder one — dark-haired, solemn, already keeping accounts of everything in a little book he'd made from birchbark — was Orsen, unmistakably, though his face had not yet learned to be careful. And the younger one—

The younger one was laughing. It was the laugh from the keyhole. His name was Tam, and he was eight, and he could whistle through a blade of grass better than anyone in the parish, and he followed his big brother everywhere, and he believed — this was the thing that broke her, this was the thing she knew she would carry to her grave — he believed absolutely, completely, with his entire small blazing heart, that his brother Orsen could do anything.

Anything at all. Climb the church roof. Outwit the tithe-man. Keep them both safe forever.

The guild's tithe-takers came in autumn, when Sefa was seeing through Orsen's twelve-year-old eyes. They came the way famine comes, the way winter comes, inevitable and impersonal, and they took the mother's memories first because hers were richest — twenty years of love, decanted into vials while she smiled vacantly at the wall — and then they came for the children, because children's memories were tender and fetched a fine price among certain clients, and there was a quota, and quotas did not care about anything.

The boys ran. Of course they ran. Everyone ran; running was the whole of the strategy available to the poor.

And then — the orchard, the same ash orchards, though younger then, though the trees were smaller — and the tithe-taker's hand closing on Tam's collar, and Tam screaming his brother's name with total faith, total certainty, *Orsen, help, Orsen—*

And a knife.

Where a twelve-year-old got a knife, the memory did not say. Perhaps it had been in the boy's pack since spring, some farmer's tool, some half-forgotten theft. What the memory said, what it said with a clarity that seared, was this: Orsen Halloran, aged twelve, drove a knife into a man's throat in an ash orchard to save his little brother, and the man died, and it bought them an hour, and in that hour the guild's hounds came anyway, and in the end the hour bought nothing, because Tam fell — a root, a slope, a stone, the memory refused to focus on how, as if even the memory itself flinched — and Tam lay at the bottom of the orchard row with his grass-whistle still in his pocket, and he was not asleep, and he was not going to wake up, and it was Orsen's fault, all of it, the knife and the fall and the empty house afterward, and Orsen was twelve years old and alone in the world and it was his fault.

And so, that winter, in a guild cellar that smelled of wax and vinegar, a boy with careful hands performed the finest work of sealing the craft had ever seen. He took the whole of it — the hillside, the summer, the laugh, the knife, the fall, the fault — and he locked it behind iron bands, and he threw away the key, and he walked out into the rest of his life carrying a hole shaped exactly like his brother.

He had not thrown away the key, Sefa realized, lying in the leaves with her cheeks wet. You couldn't throw away a key you'd made yourself. He had simply hidden it so deep inside the lock that finding it and using it were the same act of courage, and he had spent thirty years hoping never to be brave in that particular direction.

The vision closed. The door reassembled itself, seamless, perfect.

Sefa knelt in the ash orchard and understood, finally and completely, the man she loved.

*Now you know,* said the god, gently, for a god.

"Yes," she whispered.

*Will you tell him?*

She was quiet a long time. Somewhere above, the silver leaves ticked. Twenty paces off, Orsen was saying something to the mule about the inferiority of mules, his voice dry and easy and built, brick by careful brick, over the ruins of a boy who whistled through grass blades and believed in his brother.

"If I tell him," she said, "I take from him the only choice he ever made for himself. He didn't choose to forget, not truly — he was twelve, and drowning. But he can choose to remember. That has to be his. If I hand him his grief like a summons—" Her throat closed. "He'd never forgive me. And worse, he'd be right not to."

*A merciful lie, then.*

"Not a lie." She wiped her face. "A held tongue. There's a difference. We of the Listening orders used to know it, before everyone started talking."

The god considered this. She felt his attention shift, weigh, settle — the vast slow consideration of a being who had watched empires misplace their gods' names and had opinions about all of it.

*Very well,* Veyl said at last. *Keep your silence, little priestess. But mark me: silence is also a debt. And I collect all debts in the end.*

"I know," Sefa said. "Everyone collects from us, apparently. It's the family business."

For the first time in three hundred years, she was almost certain, the god beneath the mountain laughed.

* * *

She said nothing. That was the hardest sentence of her life, and she lived it daily, hourly, through four more days of orchards and bad road and Orsen's increasingly creative complaints about the mule.

It changed things, is the terrible part. Knowing. She caught herself watching him differently — watching the way he counted things without noticing he did it, coins, fenceposts, stars; watching how he always walked on the outside of the path, nearest the road, nearest whatever might come along it; watching how he never, ever let anyone walk behind him. Thirty years of habits built by a boy in an orchard, and he didn't know their source, and now she did, and the knowledge sat in her chest like a swallowed coal.

Once, at dusk, he caught her looking.

"What?" he said, wary. Priestesses looking at you fondly and sadly was, in Orsen's experience, a prelude to either conversion attempts or funerals.

"Nothing," she said. "You just — you'd have made a good older brother."

The words were out before she could stop them, and she watched them land, watched him go still in that particular way of his, the careful face flickering like a candle in a draft.

"I wouldn't know," he said at last, lightly, too lightly. "No siblings. Only child. Guild-raised, mostly." He busied himself with the fire. "Why? Do I seem brotherly?"

"Very," Sefa said. "Protective. Keeps accounts. Terrible singer, probably."

"I'm an excellent singer."

"You hum. Off-key. With conviction."

"That's called style."

And he smiled — really smiled, brief and crooked, the smile of the boy on the hillside surfacing through thirty years of careful masonry — and Sefa laughed with him and grieved behind it, and understood at last what her order had meant, all those years ago, when they taught that mercy was the heaviest burden a soul could carry. Not because mercy was hard to give. Because it was heavy to hold, alone, in the dark, season after season, while the person it was for walked beside you humming off-key, believing himself unburdened.

That night she prayed longer than usual, and her prayer was not a request. It was a promise, spoken into the listening dark of the ash orchards, under the ticking silver leaves:

*When he opens that door — and he will, someday, because doors like that don't stay shut forever — I will be there. Whatever comes out of it, I will be there. That is my offering. Take it or leave it.*

The god said nothing. But she felt, distinctly, the sensation of a bargain being noted down.

It was near midnight, the fire burned low, and Sefa was drifting toward a sleep she hoped would be empty, when Orsen sat bolt upright beside the ashes and said, in a voice stripped of all its careful architecture:

"Sefa. The ledger."

She was awake instantly. "What about it?"

He was holding the book open, staring at a page as though it had bitten him. In the firelight his face had gone the color of the ash leaves.

"The entry for Aldric's memory," he said slowly. "The original sale. I've read it a hundred times. Tonight I read it again, and Sefa — the handwriting." He looked up at her, and his eyes were wide and young and terrified, a twelve-year-old's eyes wearing a grown man's face. "It's mine. It's been mine all along. I sold the king's memory myself, decades ago, and I sealed the memory of selling it — and if I sealed *that*—"

He stopped. His gaze drifted, unwilling, toward the center of his own chest, toward the place where a small black door stood in the gray country behind his thoughts.

"Sefa," he whispered. "What did I do?"

She held his eyes across the dead fire, and kept her promise, and kept her silence, and said the only true thing she was free to say:

"Whatever it was," she said, "you're not that boy anymore. But I think we need to find out who he was — tonight."
