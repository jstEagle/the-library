## 11. Court of Hollow Faces

There were three ways into the palace of Caerlum, according to the guild's oldest records. The front gates, which required an invitation and a spine. The servants' stair, which required a bribe and a better memory for faces than most guards possessed. And the laundry chute.

Orsen Halloran had spent twenty years keeping ledgers, and twenty years had taught him one iron law of accounting: *the entry no one checks is where the theft lives.* Nobody audited laundry. Laundry was beneath audit. Laundry was, in every sense, beneath.

So it was that the keeper of the Vellum Guild's impossible inventory slid down a stone chute smelling of lye and other people's mistakes, landed in a basket of table linens with a gracelessness he would never record anywhere, and lay there for a full ten seconds listening to his own heartbeat argue with him.

"You're forty-one years old," he told himself quietly. "You catalogued a memory of a whale's wedding last spring. You can manage a palace."

The whale's wedding had been easier. Whales, at least, did not post guards.

* * *

The trouble with infiltrating a court was that courts were designed by people who understood infiltration. Every corridor in Caerlum's summer palace was built to be seen down, every gallery arranged so that a stranger stood out like a smudge on glass. Orsen had prepared for this the only way a ledger-keeper knew how: paperwork. He had forged himself a livery badge — under-steward, third rank, wine service — and armed himself with the great universal truth of palaces everywhere:

No one looks at a servant carrying something.

He carried a decanter. He carried it with purpose. He walked the eastern gallery at a brisk, apologetic clip, and the courtiers parted around him like water around a very dull stone.

That was how he first saw the smiles.

He noticed it the way he noticed everything — as an error in a column that should have balanced. A young lordling by the window smiled at a joke. Fine. Courtiers smiled at jokes; it was practically their trade. But then the lordling turned away from his companion, and the smile *stayed*. Not frozen, exactly. It stayed the way frost stays on a pane — present, decorative, unconnected to anything happening behind it.

Orsen slowed. He pretended to fuss with the decanter.

Across the gallery, a lady in green laughed at something her husband said, and her laugh was warm and real, but when she turned to accept a cup from a page, her mouth was already curved in the same smile. The same smile. The same *width*, the same tilt at the left corner, the same faint dimple on the right cheek.

He counted. It was a compulsion; Sefa would have understood, or she would have looked at him the way she looked at him when he alphabetized the spice rack at the shrine, which was fondly and with mild concern. Forty-one courtiers in the gallery and its adjoining hall. Thirty-three of them wore the smile. Not identical faces — identical *smiles*, grafted onto different mouths like cuttings from one plant.

"More wine?" he asked a nearby gentleman, mostly to have said something.

"Oh," said the gentleman, "yes." The smile widened precisely one degree. "Everything is *very good* here."

It was the tone of a man reading a line someone else had written for him. Orsen poured the wine with steady hands and thought about the guild's inventory. There was a category in the vaults they called *shavings* — memories skimmed rather than taken whole, a thin paring off the top of a person's joy, their grief, their surprise. Shavings were cheap. Shavings were what you fed to things that needed to eat constantly but didn't care what.

Something in this palace was eating, and the courtiers were the pantry.

And yet — here was the thing that made Orsen's professional soul itch — the shavings hadn't left them hollow the way stolen memories usually did. Usually a man shaved of his joys went grey inside, flat as unsalted broth. These people still laughed, still danced, still gossiped viciously about the Duchess of Marrow's hat. They had simply been given a replacement surface. A smile that belonged to the crown, worn over whatever face remained underneath.

It was worse, somehow. Theft that left the victim *grateful*.

"The king approaches," someone murmured, and the gallery rearranged itself with the smooth urgency of water finding a drain.

* * *

Orsen had planned not to look at the king. Looking at kings was how people got remembered, and being remembered was fatal to men carrying decanters who shouldn't be there. But Aldric Caerlum — or the thing wearing Aldric Caerlum — came down the gallery toward the dais, and the crowd bowed in a wave, and Orsen bowed too, head lowered, eyes angled up through his lashes because he was a thief before he was a coward and thieves always looked.

King Aldric was beautiful. That was the first obscenity of it. Whatever sat behind that face kept it polished like a relic — the gold-brown hair, the strong jaw, the famous Caerlum gray eyes. But the beauty had the quality of a well-tended grave.

And the king was smiling.

Of course he was smiling. Everyone was smiling. But Orsen had spent two decades studying the fine grain of human expression the way jewelers study flaws, and he knew what he was seeing: the king's smile was the *source*. The thirty-three courtiers wore copies. This was the original — wide, warm, tilted at the left corner, dimpled on the right.

The court wasn't imitating fashion. They were imitating *him*, without knowing it, the way ivy climbs a wall without deciding to.

Aldric paused mid-gallery beside a small girl — some baron's daughter, seven or eight, clutching a posy she'd clearly been coached to offer. She held it up. The king bent to take it, and for one heartbeat his smile flickered — not away, but *inward*, like a lamp guttering — and Orsen saw beneath it, just for an instant, something that was not a face at all but a hunger wearing the idea of one.

Then the girl giggled, and the king straightened, and the smile returned to its full wattage, and he said, in a voice like honey poured over stone:

"What a lovely day. Everything is very good today."

The court sighed, all together, softly, like a house settling.

Orsen's hands had gone cold around the decanter. He backed out of the gallery with his bow held low and his heart hammering out a rhythm he'd have described in his ledger as *irregular, cause: see attached*.

* * *

The treasury of prayers was in the old chapel wing, three floors up and one conspiracy of luck away, and Orsen reached it near midnight because palaces, whatever else could be said of them, slept late after a feast day.

Getting in required the bribe after all. The guild's records had named a gate captain named Doss, whose loyalty was documented across four decades of discreet payments, and Orsen found him exactly where the ledgers promised: drunk on duty, sober enough to count.

Doss examined the purse, then Orsen, then the purse again. "You're not the usual one."

"The usual one retired."

"Hm." Doss weighed the coins. "Retired rich, I hope."

"Comfortably. He sends regards."

"He never sent regards before."

"People change," Orsen said, and Doss, who had been paid to not be curious for forty years, shrugged with the ease of long practice and unlocked the door.

The treasury of prayers was not what the name suggested. Newcomers always imagined candles, altars, little slips of paper. What it actually held was the harvest of three centuries of royal devotion: shelves upon shelves of sealed vessels, each one holding a prayer that had been *collected* rather than answered. The old kings of Caerlum had practiced a particular piety — they had believed prayers were too precious to simply release into the air, and so they'd commissioned the Vellum Guild, generations ago, to bottle them. To keep them safe. To keep them *owned*.

Orsen stood in the doorway and felt sick in a way that was half horror and half recognition. He knew these shelves. He had inventoried their contents. Somewhere in the guild's deep vaults were the twin ledgers to what surrounded him now, columns of entries in his own careful hand: *One prayer for rain, vintage 212. One prayer for a dead child's return, do not open, do not open, do not open.*

Every vessel here was a hope somebody had once been desperate enough to speak aloud, harvested before heaven ever got the chance to answer it.

And now the gods were awake. And now the thing wearing the king's face was preparing a rite to pour all of it — every bottled prayer in this room, every shaven joy in that gallery, every stored hope in the guild's vaults — into the starving mouth beneath the mountain.

Orsen lit no lamp. He didn't need one. Guild-trained eyes learned to read labels in the dark, and besides, the treasury had begun to glow.

Faintly. Here and there. Vessels that had sat dark for centuries were waking with the rest of the world's impossible inventory — a blue shimmer in a jar on the third shelf, a warm amber pulse in a clay pot, a hairline of light moving inside a stoppered flask like a firefly remembering its job. Prayers waking up. Prayers *wanting out*.

He moved along the shelves, checking dates, checking seals. The royal collection was organized by reign, because of course it was; even hoarders loved taxonomy. Aldric's own section was modest — he had been king only eleven years before the change — and Orsen worked through it vessel by vessel, matching each against the description Sefa had pulled from her order's oldest texts.

*Aldric's true memory, taken whole on the night of his crowning. Held in glass. Marked with the seal of the ninth vault.*

Third shelf. Second row. A vial no longer than his thumb, its glass clouded with age — and glowing. Not faintly, like the others. It burned a soft, steady silver, the way a coal burns after the fire has gone down, patient and deep. Inside, something shifted. Not liquid. Something with weight and weather to it. Eleven years of a living mind, folded small.

Orsen lifted it with both hands, the way you lift a sleeping child, and the moment his skin touched the glass the glow flared — and he *felt* it. A brush of something vast and gentle against the edge of his mind: snow on a mountain road, a woman laughing in a garden, the crushing bright terror of a crown being lowered onto your head while everyone watches and no one asks if you're afraid—

He set it carefully inside his coat, against his chest, where it pulsed like a second heart.

"There you are," he whispered. "Eleven years in a bottle. Let's get you home."

Which was when he heard the footsteps in the corridor outside, and the low voices, and — worse, so much worse — the sound of the door's lock beginning to turn from the *other* side.

Doss, Orsen thought with the clarity of the truly doomed, is going to need a much larger bribe.

He looked around the treasury of prayers — hundreds of waking lights, hundreds of bottled hopes, all of them now watching him with the blind attention of things that had waited centuries to matter — and did the arithmetic he always did. One exit. Two doors. No windows. A stolen king's memory warming his ribs.

The lock clicked.

Orsen blew out his breath, put his back against the shelf, and reached for the only weapon a ledger-keeper truly trusted: the truth, deployed at speed.

"Gentlemen," he called out pleasantly, as the door swung open onto torchlight and steel, "I can explain the books."
