## 12. The God Who Was Robbed

Sefa had always imagined that if her god ever spoke to her directly, it would be during prayer — that she would be kneeling, forehead to cold stone, hands open, heart scrubbed clean of want. That was how the old texts described it. *The god comes to the empty vessel.*

Instead, he came while she was mending a sock.

She was sitting in the shrine's back room on a stool too short for her, needle in hand, swearing softly at a hole near the heel that had clearly been gnawed by something with opinions. The candle she'd lit for Orsen — because she was worried about him, and because worry needed somewhere to go or it curdled — had been burning low and blue for an hour, which should have been her first warning. Candles did not burn blue for ordinary reasons.

The second warning was the silence.

Not quiet. Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses against the ears like water. The rain outside the shuttered window stopped mid-fall, as though someone upstairs had put a hand over the sky's mouth.

Then the room filled.

There was no door opening. There was no light show, no thunder, none of the theater the new temples liked so much. There was simply *more* of the room than there had been a moment before, and all of it was him. He stood — if *stood* was the word — by the little altar, tall as grief and shaped like a man who had once been very beautiful and had then waited too long. His robe was the grey of ash under snow. His face kept trying to settle into features and failing, the way a name does when you've known someone too long to need it.

*Sefa,* he said, and his voice was not sound but remembering — the smell of incense from her childhood, her mother's humming, the first hymn she'd ever fumbled through. Every holy thing she'd ever felt, played back at once.

She dropped the sock. She would be embarrassed about that later, but later was a country very far away just then.

"Lord," she whispered, and got off the stool, and knelt properly after all, knees cracking, because some habits are load-bearing.

*You prayed to me,* he said. *Three nights past. You asked me what I wanted.*

"I did."

*Nobody asks that anymore.* Something moved across his unfinished face that might have been a smile, if smiles could be sad in that particular ancient way. *They ask for rain. For sons. For their neighbor's cow to die. You asked what I wanted. So I will tell you.*

The candle flame stood perfectly still, like a soldier at attention.

*I want what was taken from me.*

Sefa's mouth went dry. "Lord, I don't understand—"

*You do.* The voice was gentle, which made it worse. Gentle gods are the ones you have to watch. *You found the ledger. You read the oldest pages. You know what the Vellum Guild was, before it learned to sell.*

She did know. She'd spent three nights in the shrine's archive with a lamp and a growing horror, reading the guild's founding charter, which had not been a merchant's document at all. It had been a covenant. Once, the old gods had kept no memories of their own — they were vast and slow and they forgot, the way mountains forget weather, and so they had entrusted their rememberings to mortal keepers. Every answered prayer, every covenant, every name spoken at every altar: written down, bottled, kept safe. The Vellum Guild had begun as librarians of the divine.

And then some clever keeper, four hundred years ago, had realized that a memory of a god is worth more than a memory of a man, and had sold one. And then another. And the guild had grown rich and respectable and had moved its vaults underground and stopped answering letters from heaven.

"They stole from you," Sefa said quietly. "All of it. Your prayers. Your covenants. Your—" She hesitated. "Your names?"

*Everything,* the god said, without bitterness, which was somehow more terrible than bitterness. *A god who cannot remember his worshippers cannot hear them. Do you know why we went silent, little priestess? Not because we died. Because we starved. You cannot answer a prayer you cannot find.*

The rain resumed outside, all at once, as though released.

"And now you're awake," she said. "Because someone opened the vaults."

*Someone woke the inventory.* His attention settled on her like snowfall. *And among the things in those vaults, priestess, is mine. A debt four centuries old. I have come to collect.*

Sefa stayed kneeling. Her knees hurt. She let them.

"Then take it," she said. "Take it back. Open your own vaults. I'll stand at the door and hold it open myself."

*It is not so simple.* For the first time, the god's form flickered — frustration, or hunger, hard to tell. *What was stolen from me was broken into pieces and sold. Scattered. Some pieces burned. Some pieces were eaten by men who did not know what they swallowed. But one piece remains whole. One piece sits sealed in wax, hidden among the guild's wares, and it is the keystone. With it, I can call the rest home.*

Hope lifted in her chest, foolish and bright. "Then tell me where it is. Tell me what it looks like. I'll get it."

*You cannot. It is guarded by a thief.*

The hope turned over, like a fish showing its belly.

"The guildmaster," she said slowly. "He told Orsen the sealed memory was collateral. He said—"

*He lied about whose collateral it is.* The god moved closer, and the candlelight bent toward him, eager. *That vial is not the guild's pledge. It is my property, stolen twice over. And the man who keeps it hidden — the man who hides his own theft inside my theft — is the man you love.*

The word landed like a dropped stone. Sefa didn't flinch, mostly because flinching required muscles and hers had all resigned.

"Orsen," she said.

*Orsen Halloran.* The god said the name the way a creditor says a debtor's. *Keeper of ledgers. Keeper of secrets. He carries a memory he took from himself, priestess, and he sealed it in guild-wax and hid it in plain sight, and inside that seal — folded into it, knotted into it like a hair in bread — is a thread of me. A fragment of my own stolen self, wound around his sin. He does not know he carries it. He thinks the vial holds only his shame.*

Sefa thought of Orsen bleeding from the nose in chapter seven of her own recent nightmares, eyes full of a country he didn't remember, the seal biting him for daring to look. *Something in it screams*, she had written in her report to no one. She had assumed the scream was his.

It had been her god's.

"What do you want me to do?" she asked. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it.

*Bring me the vial. Unbroken. Unopened.* The god's unfinished face leaned down toward her, and up close she could see that his eyes were made of small patient fires. *Do this, and I will forgive everything. The guild's four-hundred-year theft. The debts of every keeper since. The covenant broken, the silence, the starving — all of it absolved, all of it paid, in one act. My church restored. My name remembered. And you, Sefa, daughter of a woman who hummed off-key — you will be the first high priestess of the returning age. Every prayer in the realm will pass through your hands.*

The offer hung in the air, enormous and gleaming, like a chandelier in an empty house.

"And if I refuse?" she asked.

The fires in his eyes did not dim. They never dimmed; that was the thing about fires.

*Then the debt stands,* he said simply. *And debts of that age are collected with interest. When the impostor completes his rite beneath the mountain — when every answered prayer in this realm is poured down that hungry throat — I will not stop it. I will let him feed. And everything your Orsen loves, everything you love, will be poured in after it. The rite does not distinguish, priestess. It takes prayers, and prayers are only memories of wanting. It will take his wanting. It will take yours.*

Silence again, but a different kind now — the held breath of a woman doing arithmetic she hated.

"You're asking me to choose," she said. "Between him and everyone else."

*I am asking you to be honest,* said the god. *I am the god of kept things, Sefa. I know what people hide. You have loved him since before you admitted it. You will love him after. This is not a flaw in you. But love is not a ledger entry. Love does not balance the books. Someone must pay, and he has been hiding behind a seal for twenty years, letting others carry what was his.*

"That's not fair," she said, and heard how young she sounded, and hated it.

*No,* the god agreed, with terrible gentleness. *It rarely is. Fairness is a human invention. I deal in accounts.*

He began to thin then, the way fog thins when the sun gets serious, and Sefa scrambled forward on her knees.

"Wait — Lord — how do I even open the vault? How do I find the vial? He keeps it—"

*You already know where he keeps it,* the god said, and there was something almost amused in the fading voice, the way there is almost mercy in a knife. *He showed you himself, the night you shared wine in the shrine. Men in love confess constantly, priestess. They simply think they're making conversation.*

And he was gone. The room was a room again — small, shabby, smelling of tallow and wet wool. The rain came down honest and ordinary. The candle had burned itself out entirely, and the sock lay on the floor where she'd dropped it, heel-up, hole exposed, waiting.

Sefa sat on the floor beside the dead altar for a long time.

* * *

She found Orsen at the guild's side entrance at dusk, because of course she did; she had known his habits for two years and had catalogued them with the same shameful precision he brought to his ledgers. He emerged carrying a satchel and the expression of a man who has spent all day being lied to by furniture.

"You look terrible," she said.

"Thank you. I've been working on it." He squinted at her. "You look worse. Which is impressive, given the competition."

"We need to talk. Not here."

They walked. Caerlum's streets at dusk were still drunk on answered prayers — a baker handing out loaves because his dead wife's recipe had come back to him whole; children chasing a dog that had, three days ago, begun speaking in rhyming couplets, to the general delight of everyone except the dog. Sefa watched Orsen watch them, and saw the thing she always saw: tenderness and revulsion sharing a chair.

"There's a shrine two streets east," she said. "Abandoned. The god there won't mind. He's deaf."

"All the good ones are, lately." But he followed her.

The abandoned shrine smelled of pigeons and old wax. Sefa lit no candle. She wasn't sure she trusted candles anymore.

"Orsen." She turned to face him. "Your sealed memory. The vial. Where do you keep it?"

He went very still — the stillness of a man checking all his exits and finding them staffed by enemies. "Why do you ask?"

"Because my god came to me today," she said. "In person. In my *mending room*. While I was darning a sock. He stood next to the altar and told me he wants his property back, and that his property is knotted into your seal, and that if I bring him the vial unbroken, he'll forgive the guild's entire four-century theft and make me high priestess of the returning age."

Orsen stared at her. Then he laughed — one short, disbelieving bark. "Your god interrupted your *sewing*?"

"He's been asleep for three hundred years. He's behind on appointments."

"Sefa." All the humor drained out of his face, and what remained underneath was worse: fear, and under the fear, guilt, arranged in layers like sediment. "If what he says is true — if there's a fragment of him inside my seal — then when I tried to open it, in the archive, I wasn't fighting my own defenses. I was fighting *him*. And he let me live."

"Yes."

"Why? Why warn me off instead of just—" He drew a line across his throat, delicately, the way accountants do.

"Because he wants the vial intact. And because—" She stopped. The lie she'd been carrying since the orchard road, since the night she'd read his memory through her god's sight and seen a dead boy and a knife, rose in her throat like floodwater. *Later*, she told it. *Not now. Not with this also on the table.* "Because he believes I can persuade you."

"Can you?"

"He offered me everything, Orsen. Everything I've ever prayed for. And all it costs is the one thing you've built your whole life around not looking at." She wrapped her arms around herself. "I haven't said yes."

"But you haven't said no."

"No," she admitted. "I haven't said no."

He nodded slowly, and she braced for anger, and it didn't come — which was somehow harder. Instead he looked at her with an awful gentleness, as if she were one more impossible item to be catalogued.

"Do you know what's strange?" he said. "Part of me wants to give it to him. Hand it over, walk away, be done. Whatever's in that vial has been charging me rent for twenty years." He smiled, and the smile didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "But I can't. Because I looked at the harvest moon charts yesterday, and Sefa — the moon turns full in six days. Six. And the impostor's rite needs a full moon, and my god — *your* god — wants my vial before then. Everyone's schedule is converging on the same week, and I don't believe in coincidence. I'm a ledger-keeper. Coincidence is just an error you haven't found yet."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning either your god is racing the impostor," Orsen said quietly, "or they're working together. And I genuinely don't know which possibility frightens me more."

Outside the shrine, far off across the rooftops, the evening bells began to ring — and then stopped, mid-peal, all of them at once, the way rain had stopped mid-fall that morning.

Orsen's head came up sharply. "That's not the hour bell."

"No," Sefa said. Her skin had gone cold. "It isn't."

Down in the streets, people were coming out of their houses, tilting their faces upward. The sky over the eastern mountains — over the mountain — had begun, six days early, to bruise violet.
