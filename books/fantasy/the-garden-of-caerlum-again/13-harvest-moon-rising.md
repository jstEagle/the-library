## 13. Harvest Moon Rising

**Orsen**

The trouble with theft, Orsen had learned over a lifetime of ledgers, was that it never stayed where you put it.

He had the vial. That much had gone according to plan, and plans that went according to plan were so rare in his experience that he should have been celebrating. The true king's memory sat against his chest, wrapped in oilcloth and tucked into a false pocket of his coat, warm as a sleeping bird. Aldric Caerlum's thirty years — coronations and famines, a wife's laughter, a son's first steps — all of it no bigger than a plum.

The plan had ended there. Everything after *acquire the vial* had been a fog with the word *run* written somewhere inside it.

Now the fog was resolving, and it was worse than he'd feared.

"The sky," Sefa said beside him.

"I see it."

"Do you? Because I don't think you do."

They stood in the shadow of the treasury colonnade, pressed between a statue of some forgotten duke and a stack of crates marked *CANDLE WAX — DO NOT PRAY NEAR*. Above them, through the colonnade's ribs, the evening sky was bruising. Not sunset — sunset was gold going to rose going to honest dark. This was violet, deepening to the color of a wound gone bad, spreading outward from the mountain in the east like ink dropped in water. The harvest moon was still two days off. Whatever light bled down through that violet ceiling came from nowhere at all.

"It's early," Sefa said. "The rite is early."

"Rites can't be early. They're rites."

"This one has decided otherwise." She said it flatly, but her hand found his sleeve, and her fingers were cold. "My god is singing, Orsen. Do you understand? He hasn't sung since before my grandmother's grandmother. And what he's singing is *hungry*."

Somewhere across the palace grounds, a bell began to ring. Then another. Then all of them, every bell in Caerlum's summer palace at once, which was not how bells announced anything good. Bells rang in sequence for weddings, for deaths, for the changing of the watch. All bells together meant only one thing in any kingdom's history: *something is loose, or something is coming, or both.*

"That'll be the court noticing their king is glowing," Orsen said. "Or the impostor starting without us. Either way—"

Either way, they ran.

They went the way Orsen had mapped it in the long hours before dawn, when he'd crouched over stolen floor-plans by candlelight while Sefa slept sitting up like a soldier: out through the treasury undercroft, along the old wine passage, up through the gardener's gate, and away south through the orchards toward the ford. It was a good plan. It had been a good plan right up until they reached the wine passage and found it full of men.

Not guards. Guards had faces that did things — frowned, sweated, glanced nervously at the violet sky. These men stood in the torchlight of the passage in perfect stillness, six of them, and when Orsen looked at their faces his professional eye — an eye trained to read people the way it read ledgers — found nothing to read.

No memories. That was what Sefa had called them, back in the shrine, describing the impostor's hunters. Men with no memories at all. Orsen had imagined something dramatic: hollow-eyed wraiths, faces like smudged charcoal. The reality was worse. They looked *ordinary*. Pleasant, even. A man you'd ask for directions. And behind their eyes, nothing. No past, no fear, no want. Just the present moment, held the way a hand holds a stone.

"Halt, please," said the nearest one, politely.

"*Please,*" Orsen muttered. "They've kept the manners and thrown away everything else."

"Orsen." Sefa's voice was very quiet. "Their hands."

He looked. Each hunter carried a long hook of black iron, and from each hook hung a small glass flask, empty, stoppered, waiting. Guild flasks. Guild make. His guild's work, turned inside out.

"They're not going to kill us," Sefa breathed. "They're going to *harvest* us."

The polite man stepped forward, and Orsen did the only thing in his repertoire that had never once failed him in thirty years among thieves and accountants: he threw money.

Specifically, he threw the entire contents of his coin purse — a genuinely impressive sum, gathered for bribes — high into the air above the hunters' heads. Gold caught torchlight and scattered like startled fish. Six memoryless men, who had no past to tell them that accountants do not carry that much coin and no imagination to wonder why, looked up as one.

Orsen grabbed Sefa's wrist and ran.

Behind them, coins rang on stone. Nobody chased. For three full seconds nobody chased, because three full seconds was as long as a man with no memories could hold a single thought, and the thought they were holding was *shiny.*

Then the bells stopped ringing all at once, and the silence that followed was somehow louder, and boots began to move behind them with the terrible even rhythm of things that do not tire, do not doubt, and do not get distracted twice.

* * *

**Sefa**

Sefa had spent her whole life praying in buildings, and she had never once considered how strange that was until she found herself praying while sprinting through a hedge maze with a thief's hand locked around hers and the sky the color of a dying bruise.

*Keep us,* she prayed, to the god beneath the mountain, the hungry one, the one whose priestess she was and whose appetite she had sworn to feed. *Keep us, keep us, keep us.*

And her god — awake now, ravenous now, three hundred years of unanswered prayers backed up behind his teeth — answered.

Not with words. With *weather.* A wind came up through the maze, smelling of deep stone and old iron, and it slid under their feet like a hand under a falling child, and suddenly Sefa and Orsen were running faster than human legs run, carried on a gust that bent the hedges aside for them and snapped them closed behind. She heard the hunters hit the hedge wall. She heard thorns. She did not hear screaming, because things with no memories also had no anticipation, and therefore could not dread anything, including thorns.

"He's helping us," Orsen panted, scandalized. "Your god is *helping* us?"

"My god is investing," Sefa said. "There's a difference. Don't thank him."

The wind set them down at the gardener's gate like a mother setting down a child it intended to eat later, and then it was gone, and Sefa felt its absence like a pulled tooth. She staggered against the gatepost. Orsen caught her elbow.

"You're white."

"I'm always white. I'm a priestess; we're issued pale." She straightened. Her heart was hammering in a way that had nothing to do with running. "He touched me, Orsen. Through the wind. And do you know what I felt?"

"Terror?"

"Gratitude." She said it like a confession, which it was. "Three hundred years my order starved him and told ourselves we were being careful. One answered prayer and I nearly wept with thanks. That's how he gets fed. That's how he's *always* gotten fed. Not by force. By making the starving feel lucky for scraps." She looked east, where the mountain wore the violet sky like a crown of its own. "That's what the rite is. Every prayer in the realm, answered at once, and every answerer grateful. A kingdom teaching itself to be eaten."

"Cheerful," Orsen said. "Any chance of a less theological escape route?"

The orchards lay ahead of them, the famous ash orchards of Caerlum, ten thousand trees silvered now under that wrong light, and beyond the orchards, the river, and beyond the river, the ford, and beyond the ford — Sefa refused to think past the ford. Thinking past the ford was how you died in stories.

They ran into the trees.

For a while there was only running, and the slap of branches, and Orsen's breath sawing beside her, and the moon — the real moon, the harvest moon come two days early because apparently even astronomy had joined the impostor's side — rising fat and violet-tinged over the mountain. The trees threw shadows too long and too many, each tree casting three shadows instead of one, and Sefa decided firmly not to think about that either.

Then Orsen stopped so abruptly she collided with his back.

Ahead, between the trunks, men stood among the trees. A dozen this time. Hooks and empty flasks. And they weren't searching. They were standing in a perfect ring around something, and Sefa followed their blank gazes to the center of the ring, where a farmer knelt in the dirt with his hands raised and tears streaming down his face, praying aloud, praying *hard*, to anyone listening.

"—my boy, please, he's got the fever, I'll give anything, *anything*—"

And the sky answered. A thread of violet light came down through the branches like a finger pointing, and where it touched the farmer's hands, Sefa felt it even from thirty feet away: the shape of a bargain being struck. A prayer answered. A price deferred. A debt entered into a ledger older than her order.

"No," she whispered.

"What?" Orsen's grip tightened on her arm. "What is it? Is he being harvested?"

"Worse. He's being *enrolled*." Her stomach turned over. "Every prayer the rite feeds to the god below has to be given freely. That's the old law. You can't steal worship. But you can *buy* it with desperation, and that's what the answering is. He's not collecting prayers, Orsen. He's collecting *debtors.*"

The farmer rose, glowing faintly, smiling through his tears, and walked away into the trees toward the mountain, and the ring of hollow men parted for him like water and closed again.

And then, as one, twelve blank faces turned toward Sefa and Orsen.

"There's no cover," Orsen said. "There's no route. There's no clever ledger trick for this one."

"Yes, there is," Sefa said, and hated herself, and reached for her god anyway. "*Please.*"

The ground shook. Not the wind this time — the earth itself, rolling under the orchard like a wave, and the twelve hunters swayed, and their flasks swung on their hooks, and one flask slipped free and shattered on a root, and in the second that all twelve blank faces looked down at the broken glass, Orsen hauled her sideways off the path and down into the dry irrigation channel that ran beneath the orchard rows, and they ran doubled-over in the dark with roots clawing at their hair while the shaking slowly stilled behind them.

* * *

**Orsen**

They came out of the channel at the riverbank, filthy, bleeding in small unimportant places, and Orsen allowed himself exactly four seconds of leaning against a willow and breathing like a bellows.

"We lost them," Sefa said.

"We didn't lose them. Things without memories can't lose track of us. We just have a head start." He fished the vial out of his coat to check it — reflex, ritual, the accountant confirming the sum — and froze.

The vial was glowing.

Faintly, softly, the color of candlelight through honey, King Aldric's thirty stolen years were shining through the oilcloth like a lamp under a blanket. Orsen unwrapped it with numb fingers. Inside the glass, the memory moved — actually moved, swirling like smoke in a draft, pressing against the glass from within.

"It knows," Sefa said quietly. "It's waking up, same as everything else the guild ever stole. It knows it's going home."

"Then it needs to know *quieter.*" Orsen rewrapped it, but the glow leaked through, a soft gold stain on the violet night, visible, he estimated, from roughly the distance of *too far.* "We're carrying a beacon. Wonderful. Any other miracles available, or is your god saving those for the invoice?"

"Orsen." Sefa had gone still, looking past him, downstream. "How fast can you run after already running?"

He followed her gaze. Along the far bank, maybe half a mile off, moving toward the ford with that same terrible even rhythm — a line of them. Twenty. Thirty. More, strung out along the water like beads on a wire, all converging on the one crossing point for fifty miles.

"They're not hunting us," Orsen said slowly. "They're herding us. Away from the ford."

"Then we go into the river here."

"Here?" The river at their feet was black, fast, and loud, swollen with autumn rain, full of rocks with opinions. "Sefa, I'm a *ledger-keeper.* My people historically drowned rather than take boats."

"And I'm a priestess. We walk on water or we sink; there's no middle path." But she was pale, and her hand, when she took his, was shaking, and Orsen understood that the bravado was a coat she'd put on over the same fear he wore. So he did what he'd found himself doing, lately, with increasing frequency and no small amount of personal alarm: he told her the truth.

"I'm frightened," he said. "Thoroughly, professionally frightened. But I've spent my whole life keeping other people's memories safe, and I am not going to drown holding the truest thing I've ever carried. So. River."

She smiled at him — tired, terrified, real — and it did something to his chest that he made a note to examine later, if there was a later.

They went in.

The cold hit like a confession. The current took them instantly, spun them, slammed them against a rock that Orsen would remember fondly compared to what came next, and then it was all noise and black water and Sefa's hand crushing his and the vial — he could feel it through the oilcloth, pressed between their bodies — *burning* now, blazing like a coal, as though the king's memory knew its home lay somewhere upstream and objected to every yard of it.

Halfway across, Orsen went under.

The world became roar. He tasted the river, felt it pull him downstream like a ledger page in wind, and thought — absurdly, calmly, in the manner of a man whose mind files even catastrophe under proper headings — *this will be difficult to explain to the guildmaster.* Then something seized his collar. Sefa, small and strong and furious, hauling him up with a strength that had to be borrowed, because he could feel the wind in it again, the deep-stone smell of the god beneath the mountain lending his priestess his arms.

*Investing,* Orsen thought, and broke the surface coughing.

They dragged out on the south bank like drowned cats, collapsed in the mud, and lay there gasping while the harvest moon climbed over the mountain and the violet sky pulsed, slow, patient, like something breathing.

Behind them, across the water, the line of hollow men had stopped at the bank. They stood in their dozens along the northern shore, hooks hanging idle, and did not cross. Orsen watched them, wheezing, and understood.

"They can't swim," he rasped. "Can they? Tell me it's that simple."

"They can't *remember* how," Sefa said. "Swimming's a memory. Everything is." She rolled onto her back in the mud and laughed — a wild, cracked, entirely justified laugh at the sky. "Everything is memory. Even the river remembers flowing downhill. That's why it carries us."

"Poetic. Save it for the shrine." Orsen sat up, wrung out his coat, and checked the vial one more time. Still glowing. Still warm. Still pressing toward home.

South of them, the land fell away toward the low country, toward villages where prayers were being answered tonight in a thousand kitchens and a thousand desperate hearts were signing themselves over without knowing it. Ahead of them, days away, the mountain waited with its rite begun early and its clock running short.

And behind them — Orsen turned, and his stomach dropped — behind them, far up the north bank, the hollow men had begun to move. Not toward the ford. Along the river. Westward, in perfect silence, in perfect step, dozens upon dozens of them, marching with the unhurried certainty of things that had just received new orders.

Toward the one bridge that crossed the river anywhere near.

"They've remembered something," Sefa said softly.

"What?"

"How to wait." She got to her feet, mud-caked, moonlit, magnificent, and held out her hand. "Come on, keeper of ledgers. We're not done running."

Orsen took her hand, tucked the true king against his heart, and ran.
