## 14. What Wears the Crown

They caught me in the gallery of mirrors, which I will admit was not my finest hour as a thief.

In my defense, the palace of Caerlum had been built by an architect who believed that a man fleeing through it should be offered every possible opportunity to admire himself in panic. Twelve mirrors in that corridor alone, each one taller than a door, each one showing me exactly what I was: a guild clerk in borrowed livery, clutching a vial of a dead king's memory against his ribs like a stolen loaf, running with the particular grace of a man who has spent thirty years sitting down.

The hunters came from both ends at once. They did not run — that was the worst of it. Men who walk are men who have never once doubted they'll catch what they're chasing. Their faces were smooth as unscribed vellum. No memories at all, Sefa had said, and now I understood what she meant. There was nothing behind their eyes to read. Not cruelty. Not duty. Just room.

I considered the window. Three stories. I considered fighting. Two of them, and me with the upper body of a man whose greatest physical exertion was closing ledger covers in damp weather.

"Gentlemen," I said, "if this is about the wine I took from the pantry—"

The vellum-faced one on my left simply extended his hand, palm up, patient as a collection plate. And because I am, at the end of the day, a professional, I understood immediately that there were two kinds of things I could surrender, and only one of them would let me keep breathing.

I gave them the vial's hiding place. I did not give them the vial. A sleight of hand involving my sleeve, a decoy phial of lamp oil I'd carried since the ash orchards for exactly this sort of occasion, and a prayer to whatever god was currently awake enough to notice. The false vial went into the hunter's palm. His fingers closed around it without curiosity. He turned, and the three of them walked me — did not drag me, walked me, the way you escort a guest — toward the king's wing.

Toward the thing that wore Aldric Caerlum's crown.

* * *

The throne room had been rearranged since Sefa's last visit, and not, I thought, by anyone with a living person's taste. The banners were gone. The portraits were gone. Every surface that might have reflected a face or recalled a name had been stripped away, so that the hall was nothing but pale stone and echo — a room that had been made to forget itself.

He sat on the throne with his chin propped on one fist, and he looked, at first glance, exactly like a tired king at the end of a long day. That was the craftsmanship of it. Someone — something — had studied Aldric well. The broad shoulders, the silver threading the black beard, the scar through the left eyebrow that the histories all mentioned. But the eyes were wrong in a way I couldn't name until I realized I was looking at eyes that had never once been surprised. Aldric's portraits showed a man perpetually on the edge of laughing at himself. This man's face was a portrait of a portrait.

"Orsen Halloran," he said, and my own name in his mouth felt like someone reading it off a shipping manifest. "Keeper of ledgers. Do you know how long it has been since anyone brought me anything interesting?"

"I couldn't say, Your Majesty."

"Neither could I." He smiled, and the smile fit his face the way a borrowed coat fits. "That's rather the theme."

The hunters deposited me at the foot of the dais and withdrew to the walls, where they stood among the shadows like furniture that had learned to stand. I became aware that the vial was digging into my ribs with every heartbeat, and that the impostor was watching me breathe.

"You have it," he said. "The true one. I can smell it on you. Memory has a scent, you know. Like rain on hot stone. Like the moment before crying." He rose, descended the steps, unhurried. Up close, the wrongness got worse, not better. His skin moved correctly. His voice had warmth in it. It was all correct the way a ledger balances when the numbers have been forged. "You've been carrying it across half the realm. Through the orchards. Past the villages full of people getting everything they ever prayed for." He tilted his head. "Why?"

"Professional obligation," I said. "The Vellum Guild honors its contracts."

"The Vellum Guild." He tasted the words. "Thieves with stationery. I remember when your guild was founded. Well. I remember *of* it. I was not yet wearing this face." He circled me slowly, and I kept my eyes forward like a soldier, which is to say I watched him in the polished floor the whole time. "Aldric sold me his memories himself, you know. Did your ledgers tell you that? He came to the guild a young king drowning in grief — a father dead, a war won too late to save the ones he loved — and he asked to feel nothing for one night. One night. And your predecessors sold him a forgetting so deep he never found the bottom of it. I grew in the space he left. Filled the shape of him like water fills a bootprint." He stopped in front of me. "So you see, clerk, I am not the thief here. I'm the *inventory*."

"That's a very generous way to describe wearing a man's face."

"It's the only face available." He said it without rancor, which somehow made it worse. "Come. Walk with me. I want to show you something, and then I'm going to make you an offer, and you're going to find it very difficult to refuse, because unlike everyone else in this palace, you actually understand what things are worth."

And here is the shameful part, the part I set down honestly because a ledger is worthless if it flatters its keeper: I went with him. Willingly. Curious. Because he was right — I did want to know what a thing like him considered valuable.

* * *

He led me down stairs I hadn't known existed, beneath the throne room, into a vault that made the Vellum Guild's look like a peddler's cart. Shelves upon shelves upon shelves, rising into darkness, and on them — not bottles. *Things.* A child's shoe, preserved mid-scuff. A wedding ring worn thin as wire. A wooden bird with paint rubbed off its wings by small hands. A letter, sealed, addressed in a shaking hand to someone named Maren. Thousands of objects, each one humming faintly, the way a struck bell hums after the sound you can hear has ended.

"What is this place?"

"My treasury," he said. "Every memory I've skimmed leaves a residue. The feeling of it, without the content. Grief without knowing who died. Joy without knowing why. Love without a face attached." He ran a finger along a shelf, tenderly, the way I ran mine along ledgers. "I keep them. All of them. Do you know why?"

"To feed your god."

"To *be* a treasury," he said, with the first genuine emotion I'd seen on him — a flash of something like pride. "Look at them, Halloran. Not one of these feelings hurts anymore. That's what forgetting does. It doesn't erase. It *cures*. Every ache on these shelves is finished. Archived. Painless forever." He turned to me, and his eyes caught the lamplight and held it without giving anything back. "Now imagine a throne above this. A realm above that. A whole kingdom where no one grieves, because no one remembers what they lost. Where no mother weeps, because she cannot recall the child. Where prayers go up clean and empty, like smoke from a fire that burned out years ago." He spread his hands. "That is what I am building. That is what the rite will finish. Not a feeding. A *cure*. For everyone."

"And the people," I said. "The actual people. With their actual lives."

"Will feel nothing," he agreed pleasantly. "Which is the point."

I looked at the shelves again. At the shoe. At the letter to Maren, sealed, undelivered, its writer presumably walking somewhere above us with a smile fixed on her face and a hole where her reason for writing used to be. And I felt the temptation arrive, and I want it recorded precisely how it arrived: not as a roar. As a *relief*. 

Because I knew what was in my own sealed memory. I didn't know its contents — but I knew its weight. Thirty years I had carried it, and there were nights it pressed on my chest like a sleeping animal, and I had built my entire life into a shape that avoided waking it. And here was a man offering me a world where nobody had to carry anything. Where the ledger closed itself. Where Orsen Halloran could put down the thing in the dark of himself and never, ever pick it back up.

"There it is," the impostor said softly. "I can see it land. You've got a sealed memory of your own, don't you? I can always tell. People carrying something heavy hold themselves a certain way." He stepped closer. "Here is my offer. Bring me the vial. Help me finish the rite. And when it's done, I will open *your* memory for you — and then I will shelve it. You'll watch it go onto the shelf. You'll feel it stop hurting. You'll walk out of here lighter than the day you were born, and you will spend the rest of your life not remembering why you ever wanted to forget."

The vault was very quiet. Somewhere far above, muffled by stone, I could hear the palace courtiers laughing at something — all of them with those identical gentle smiles.

"All I have to do," I said, "is betray a king, doom a priestess, and hand every prayer in the realm to the thing under the mountain."

"You make it sound like a cost," he said. "It's an *exchange*. You of all people should appreciate a fair exchange."

And the terrible thing — the thing I confess here in ink because ink is where I keep my honesty — is that some part of me, a large part, a part I am not proud of, wanted to say yes. My hand had actually begun to drift toward my sleeve, toward the vial, before—

Before I saw the bird.

The wooden bird, third shelf, at eye level. Paint rubbed from its wings by small hands. And beneath it, in the impostor's careful archive-hand, a label: *Joy. Source unknown. Child, approx. age six.*

Source unknown.

He had cured the feeling and lost the meaning. Some mother somewhere had loved a child with a wooden bird, and the love was filed here, painless and perfect, and the woman herself was upstairs smiling at nothing, and neither of them — not the feeling on the shelf, nor the hollowed woman — remembered each other at all.

"Sefa," I said.

The impostor frowned. "What?"

"Nothing. Thinking aloud." I looked at the shelves and thought about a priestess who prayed to a god who ignored her for twenty years and kept praying anyway, because loving something that doesn't answer back is still loving it. I thought about stolen wine in a dark shrine, and her laugh, which she tried to hide behind her sleeve and failed at completely. I thought about the fact that when she'd looked at me across that altar, I had felt *known*, and being known is the opposite of being forgotten, and I would rather carry every weight in this vault than trade that away.

"No," I said.

"No?" He sounded genuinely puzzled, the way a fish might sound if it were puzzled.

"The painless version isn't the treasure," I told him. "It's the receipt. You've built the largest collection of receipts in the world, and you think you're rich." I tapped the label under the bird. "*Source unknown.* That's not a cure. That's a theft even your god wouldn't sign for."

Something moved behind his face then — the first true thing I'd seen in it. Not anger. *Hunger.* He reached for me, and his hand was Aldric's hand, broad and scarred, and it closed on my collar with a king's strength.

"Give me the vial, clerk."

"Make me remember why I should," I said — and drove my thumb, hard, into the one place on him I'd gambled everything on: the memory of genuine love.

I couldn't know it was there. But every archive has a misfiled item, and I had spent thirty years among misfiled items, and when he'd led me past the eastern shelves I had seen it — one object apart from the others, wrapped in cloth, labeled in a different hand, older: *Aldric Caerlum. Age nineteen. The garden at midsummer. Her name was Elira. KEEP SEPARATE.*

I tore the cloth free as his grip tightened. Inside was a sprig of dried flowers — midsummer bloom, gone brown, gone brittle — and the moment the air touched it, the impostor *shuddered*.

It was small, what happened. To this day I can't fully describe it. His face stayed Aldric's face, but the thing behind it slipped, like a mask shifting in wind, and for one heartbeat the eyes that stared past me were nineteen years old and standing in a garden with a girl who smelled of the same flowers, and the expression on that face was so nakedly, unbearably *happy* that I had to look away.

His grip went slack.

I did not waste the gift of it. I wrenched free, took the stairs three at a time, and heard behind me — not pursuit. A sound worse than pursuit. The impostor standing alone in his treasury, holding a dead girl's flowers, making a noise I refused to identify because identifying it would mean admitting that the monster knew how to weep.

* * *

I came up through a servant's passage, out past the kitchens, over the wall in the manner of a sack of turnips being generously thrown, and ran through the dark streets of the lower city until my lungs filed a formal complaint. Behind me the sky over the mountain had begun, very faintly, to bruise violet — days early. The rite was coming. Faster than Sefa's texts had promised.

I crossed the old ford road at a stumbling run, and she was there — of course she was there, she was always where the trouble was, standing in the shallows with her lantern held high like a woman greeting a shipwreck.

"Orsen! Thank the quiet gods — the sky, the sky is *wrong*, we have to—"

"Sefa." I bent double, gasping. "Vial's safe. Impostor knows my face. Also I made him cry, which I'd like noted in the record."

She stared at me. Then, despite everything — the violet sky, the hunters surely already moving, the end of the world gathering itself under the mountain — she laughed. Once. Briefly. The real one, the one she couldn't hide.

"We need to cross tonight," she said. "There's a shrine on the far bank. We can hide until—"

"Lead on, priestess."

But as I waded into the cold water beside her, lantern light trembling on the current between us, I saw her glance sideways at me — quickly, guiltily — and look away. And I thought: *that's odd.* And I thought: *she knows something.* And I thought: *whatever it is, I'll ask her tomorrow.*

Tomorrow, as it turned out, had other plans.
