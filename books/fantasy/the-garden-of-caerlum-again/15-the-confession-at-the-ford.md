## 15. The Confession at the Ford

The river had been singing to itself all morning, indifferent and silver, and Sefa thought it was the only honest thing left in the world.

They had been running for two days. Two days of hedgerows and hollowed-out villages and the bruise-violet sky pressing down like a thumb on an eyelid. The harvest moon would rise tomorrow night, full and fat and hungry, and somewhere behind them the men with no memories were still walking — not running, never running, just walking with the terrible patience of things that did not need to eat or sleep or doubt.

The ford at Marrow's Crossing was the last place the road touched water before the mountain country began. Beyond it: stone, pine, and the rite.

"We should cross now," Orsen said. "While the light's good."

"The light is *grey*," Sefa said. "It has been grey for two days. I'm beginning to forget what other colors do."

"Grey is a color. It does things. It makes everything look like a ledger."

"You would say that."

He almost smiled. That was the trouble with him — he almost smiled the way other men actually smiled, and she had spent three hundred miles learning to read the difference, and now she could not stop reading anything about him at all.

They watered the horses. Orsen crouched at the bank, cupping handfuls over his face, and Sefa watched the water run off his knuckles and thought, *tell him, tell him, tell him*, and thought it the way you think *don't fall* when standing on a roof edge — uselessly, repeatedly, while your body does exactly what it wants.

Because her god had seen her hesitation. Her god saw everything now that it was awake, the way a debtor sees every coin in every purse on the street. And in the shrine at Hollowmere, three nights ago, it had spoken through the mouth of its own statue — a voice like dry pages turning — and made her an offer she had not yet refused and could not yet accept:

*Bring me the keeper's sealed memory, priestess, and all debts are forgiven. Yours. His. The guild's.*

She had said nothing to Orsen. She had been saying nothing to Orsen for three nights, which was a kind of lying, which was a kind of theft, and she was a priestess of Vethis who kept accounts of the soul, and she knew precisely what kind of theft this was.

It was the kind that got worse the longer you carried it.

"Orsen," she said.

"Hm." He was checking the girth strap, frowning at it as though it might be hiding a ledger entry.

"I have to tell you something, and you're going to want to drown me in this river, so I'd like you to finish watering the horse first."

His hands went still. "That's a remarkable sentence."

"I know."

"Sefa—"

"Finish watering the horse," she said, "and then I'll tell you why we're being followed by something worse than the crown's hunters."

* * *

She told him sitting down, because her legs had opinions about standing.

She told him about Hollowmere, about the statue's voice, about the offer. She watched his face go through the stages — confusion, then the cold arithmetic he did when frightened, then something worse than either, because the offer itself wasn't the wound. The wound came next, and she made herself deliver it, because a confession delivered halfway was just a lie with better posture.

"And there's more," she said. "Before that. Before any of it. On the road, in the ash orchards, when you slept — I read your sealed memory."

The river sang. A heron lifted off the far bank, unhurried, unbothered by the end of the world.

"How," Orsen said. His voice was very quiet, which she had learned was the loudest thing about him.

"Through Vethis's sight. It's a discipline of my order — reading the shape of a soul's burdens without opening them. I've done it a hundred times to grieving families. To tell them what they lost, so they can mourn it properly." She swallowed. "I told myself it was that. Diagnosis. Due diligence. We were carrying your vial across half the realm; I told myself I needed to know what defenses it had."

"And?"

"And it wasn't due diligence." Her hands were shaking. She let them. "I looked because I love you, and loving someone who has locked a room inside himself is its own kind of torture, and I am not the saint my order thinks I am, Orsen. I looked. And I saw."

The silence stretched so long she counted the river's syllables to fill it.

"What did you see?" he asked finally, and his voice had changed — gone flat and careful, the voice of a man balancing something breakable on a narrow ledge.

"A boy," she said. "A dead boy. And a knife."

* * *

He stood up. Not fast — that was the frightening part. He stood up the way a door closes, complete and final, and walked three steps toward the horses as though the conversation were luggage he'd decided to leave on the bank.

"Orsen—"

"No." He turned around, and his face was wrong. Not angry-wrong. *Empty*-wrong, the way the courtiers' faces had been empty, and she understood with a lurch that this was what he did instead of screaming — he filed himself away. "You read it. You read the one thing I have ever taken for myself, the one thing in all your god's impossible inventory that belongs to me, and you *looked inside it*, and you didn't tell me. You let me walk beside you for a hundred miles. You let me—" He stopped. Recalculated. "What else did you see? Tell me exactly what you saw, since you've already spent it."

"Orsen, that isn't—"

"*What did you see.*"

"A boy of maybe ten," she said, and her voice cracked and she hated it and kept going anyway, because he deserved the whole ugly account. "Dark hair. Your chin. He was laughing in the first image — no, not laughing, *mid*-laughing, caught between one breath and the next. And there was a knife. Guild steel, the short kind the tithe-takers carry. And there was blood, and there was you, older, kneeling, and there was—" she pressed her palm flat against her sternum, against the ache, "—there was a word written under all of it, the way memories are labeled in your vaults. One word. *Brother.*"

The heron came back down. The river kept singing its one stupid song.

"That's all?" he said. "That's all you took?"

"I didn't take anything. I *saw*. There's a difference, and I know it doesn't feel like a difference right now, but I swear it on Vethis's name there is."

"There isn't." He laughed, and it was the worst sound she'd heard in two days of violet skies and memoryless hunters. "Do you know what I did, Sefa? Do you know why a man seals his own memory? Everyone else in that vault sold theirs — grief, shame, first loves, dead mothers. They traded them for coin or safety or a night's sleep. I stole mine. From myself. Because if I had carried it one more day I was going to walk into the sea, and I was twenty-two years old, and some animal part of me chose to live even if living meant living as a stranger to my own worst hour." His hands were fists now. "And you opened it. With your god's eyes. While I slept. Like a client. Like a *buyer*."

"Yes," she said.

"Just — yes?"

"Yes. Because if I dressed it up in excuses you'd see through them, and I'd deserve that too. Yes, Orsen. I did the thing. I am the thing."

* * *

He sat back down eventually. Not near her — a body-length away, on a flat stone, elbows on knees, staring at the water like it owed him money. But he sat, and she had learned enough about Orsen Halloran to know that sitting was surrender's cousin, and she would take the cousin.

"You should have told me," he said at last. "At Hollowmere. Before the god made its offer. If you'd told me then, I could have—"

"Could have what? Given it to him?" She turned to face him fully. "That's what he wants, Orsen. That's the price on the table. Your sealed memory, and every debt forgiven — yours, mine, the guild's ancient theft, all of it, wiped clean. All I have to do is hand over the one thing you ever kept."

He looked at her then, really looked, and she saw the exact moment the arithmetic started again — the cold ledger-mind, weighing whether she'd already decided. Whether the woman beside him was still an ally or had become a very patient trap.

"I haven't decided," she said, answering the look. "I want you to know that honestly. I haven't decided, and the fact that I *haven't* decided should frighten you, and I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. My god offered me absolution for everything I've ever done wrong, and I stood in front of his statue and I *thought about it*. Three days. I thought about it for three days."

"Three days," he repeated.

"While we ran. While you stole a memory out of a bandit's head to save my life and then hated yourself for how easy it was. While you shared your bread with me and lied to me with your kindness, because kindness from someone keeping a secret is a lie with butter on it." She laughed despite herself, wetly. "Vethis forgive me, I've been composing confessions in my head for three days and none of them survived contact with your actual face."

"What stopped you?" he asked. "The offer. What stopped you from taking it?"

And here was the thing she had turned over all night, every night, like a stone with something alive underneath.

"You did," she said. "Not you-as-you-are. You-as-you-were. That boy in the memory, mid-laughing. Orsen, I saw what's under the seal, and I saw what the seal cost you, and I understood something that my god — with all his sight — apparently cannot understand." She moved closer, one stone at a time, until she could have touched his knee, and did. "Survival bought with forgetting isn't survival. It's just a slower way of dying. You didn't escape that memory. You hired a stranger to serve your sentence, and the stranger is you, and you've been doing time for twenty years without knowing the crime."

"Don't," he whispered.

"Somebody has to say it. Your guildmaster won't. Your ledgers won't. Your god certainly won't — Vethis deals in *accounts*, Orsen, and an account with a page torn out isn't settled, it's just *unbalanced*, and unbalanced accounts are how starving gods get born in the first place." Her voice was shaking and she let it shake. "You want to know why the old gods woke up angry? Look at us. Look at this whole realm, drunk on answered prayers, trading away every hard thing we own until there's nothing left but the soft parts. We taught them theft. We were their memory-keepers and we taught them *theft*, and now the debt is coming due, and you — you of all people, keeper of ledgers, thief of your own past — you are going to stand at that mountain and pay it honestly, or all of this was for nothing."

He was crying. She hadn't seen it start; there was no dramatic moment, no sob. Just tears arriving on his face like rain on glass, sliding off, unnoticed by him, and that was somehow the most devastating thing — that he had practiced not noticing his own grief for so long it couldn't even get his attention anymore.

"If I open it," he said, barely audible over the water, "I don't come back from it, Sefa. Whatever I was before the seal — whatever I did — that man comes back. And I don't know if he's someone who can still—" He gestured helplessly between the two of them, at the ford, at the road, at the whole doomed tender project of themselves.

"He can," she said.

"You can't know that."

"I know what I saw under the seal. I saw a boy laughing. I saw a knife. And underneath both, holding them together like roots around a stone, I saw—" she searched for it, the truest thing in her three days of cowardice, "—I saw that you sealed it *because you loved him*. Not to escape punishment. Any coward can run from punishment. You sealed it because carrying it whole would have killed you, and some part of you knew that the world still needed you alive — needed the man who would one day steal a king's memory back from a hollow thing and give it away for free. Grief isn't the wound, Orsen. Grief is the *scar*. It means the wound closed. What you've been carrying isn't a scar. It's an open thing, wrapped in wax, and it's been bleeding into everything you've built for twenty years."

The horses shifted. Downstream, the heron stabbed at the water and came up with nothing, and tried again, undiscouraged, and Sefa thought there was a sermon in that if she ever got home to preach it.

"And if you're wrong?" he said. "If I open it and I'm not the man you—"

"Then I'll know the man you actually are," she said, "instead of the man I've been inventing. Which, frankly, I've done long enough. It's how I ended up reading your seal in the first place — I fell in love with a story I was telling myself, and stories demand endings, and I went looking for yours without permission." She took his hand. It was cold. River-cold, fear-cold. "So here is my penance, freely chosen: I'll tell you everything I saw, whenever you ask, as many times as you ask, until it stops being mine to keep secret and becomes yours to remember. And when we reach the mountain, and my god demands payment — I will stand beside you when you answer him yourself. Not your sealed memory. *You*. The whole, reopened, unforgivable, honest you."

For a long time he didn't speak. The grey light thickened toward evening. Somewhere behind them, patient as arithmetic, the memoryless men kept walking.

"You realize," Orsen said finally, and his voice was wrecked and trying to be dry, "that this is the second worst confession I've received this year. The first was the guildmaster telling me our entire institution was founded on betraying the gods."

"I strive for excellence."

"You're a very gifted woman, Sefa. Terrible judgment, but gifted." He turned his hand over and held hers properly now, and his grip was the grip of a man grabbing a rope over a drop. "If I do this — if I open it — I'll need you there. Not your god. *You*. Because if what comes back is the man who did what that label says he did, I don't trust him to stay. Do you understand? I don't trust him not to finish what the sea started."

"I'll be there," she said. "I'll hold the rope."

"The ford's too shallow to drown in, you know. Knee-deep at best. Your threat earlier was structurally unsound."

"Noted for future confessions."

He laughed — a real one, cracked down the middle, and she counted it as the first honest sound either of them had made in three days.

Then he went quiet, looking east, where the pines climbed toward the mountain and the sky above them had begun, very faintly, to bruise. Not sunset. Sunset was hours away. This was something else — a violet gathering at the horizon like a storm deciding, like a page being marked.

"It's early," Sefa breathed. "The moon isn't full yet. He's not supposed to—"

"He's moving up the date." Orsen was already on his feet, hauling the girth tight, all the grief in him suddenly converted to motion. "Of course he is. Why wait for the harvest moon when the harvest is already ripe?" He swung up into the saddle and looked down at her, and his eyes were red-rimmed and terrified and more awake than she had ever seen them.

"Cross with me," he said. "Confess the rest on the far side. Because I'm going to open that memory tonight, Sefa — tonight, before the mountain — and I need to know what I'm paying with before I stand in front of a god and offer it."
