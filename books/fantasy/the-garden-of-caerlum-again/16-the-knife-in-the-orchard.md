## 16. The Knife in the Orchard

The vial was small enough to lose in a coat pocket. That had always been the horror of it, Orsen thought — that the worst thing he had ever done could be held between two fingers and rattled like a bead of amber.

He sat on the floor of Sefa's shrine with his back against the altar steps, because it seemed wrong to do this standing, and wronger still to do it sitting comfortably. The candle she'd left burning for him had guttered to a stub. Outside, the harvest moon was three nights from full, and the sky over Caerlum had the bruised look of a thing holding its breath.

Sefa was asleep in the back room. He could hear her breathing through the curtain — steady, unhurried, the breathing of a woman who had confessed an unforgivable thing at knifepoint's distance and then made him tea anyway. *I read your memory,* she had said at the ford, water to her knees, her voice shaking and her eyes not. *There's a boy in it. And a knife. I'm sorry.* He had raged. He had said things about gods and thieves and priests who loved men they didn't understand. She had stood there and taken all of it, and when he finally ran out of fury she had said, quietly: *Survival bought with forgetting isn't survival, Orsen. You've been dead for thirty years and paying rent on a grave.*

He hadn't forgiven her. But he had come back to the shrine, which was perhaps its own kind of answer.

Now he turned the vial in his fingers. The wax seal bore the mark of the Vellum Guild — a quill crossed with a key — pressed by his own hand, twenty-nine years ago, by lamplight, by a boy of seventeen who had just sold the guild the only thing he owned that anyone wanted.

"You're supposed to say something first," he told the empty shrine. "That's what the rites say. Confession before unbinding. Name the sin."

The candle flame leaned as if listening.

"My name is Orsen Halloran," he said. "Keeper of ledgers. I have stolen four hundred and eleven memories, given or take the ones nobody counted. And one of them is mine." His voice did something strange on the last word, cracked down the middle like old binding glue. "I don't know what's in it. That's the whole point of it. That was always the whole point of it."

He thought about putting it down. He thought about walking out into the ash orchards and walking until the walking became a life. He thought about Sefa asleep behind the curtain, and how she had carried this knowledge for weeks without letting it change the way she looked at him — or rather, he realized now, letting it change everything about the way she looked at him, tenderness layered over grief for a boy she'd met secondhand.

"Damn you," he said softly, to no god in particular and possibly all of them.

He broke the seal.

* * *

Memory does not return the way water returns to a dried well. It returns the way a flood returns to a valley.

He was eight years old and it was autumn and the orchard behind the tithe-house was heavy with apples nobody would pick, because the orchard belonged to the guild, and everything belonged to the guild, and the guild took its tenth of everything — grain, wool, coin, and from the children of debtors, memories. One per year, once you turned seven. They called it the tithe of the mind, and they called it fair, because the alternative was your father's hands, and everyone knew what happened to debtors' hands.

His brother Tam was ten. Tam was the kind of boy who whittled flutes out of reeds and gave them away, who could name every bird in the hedgerow, who had already lost two tithes — his fear of the dark at seven, his grandmother's face at eight — and had come out of each extraction pale and smiling, saying *it's fine, Orse, it's fine, I don't even miss it,* which was a lie, because after the second one he stopped humming.

They were hiding in the orchard because the tithe-takers had come early. Off-schedule, off-ledger, which meant someone had sold them — someone hungry, someone frightened, someone whose name Orsen would never learn. The tithes were due in spring. It was October. The takers moved through the rows with their lanterns and their little glass vials and their soft voices, and Tam had grabbed his hand and pulled him over the wall into the wet grass, and they had lain there among the fallen apples breathing hard and trying not to laugh, because they were children, and being hunted still felt like a game.

"We'll run," Tam whispered. "Tonight. Over the mountains. There's a road — Da showed me once. We'll go before they come back."

"They'll catch us."

"They won't. I'm fast. You're fast too."

"I'm not fast."

"You're fast when it matters." Tam grinned at him in the dark, gap-toothed, an apple in each hand, one for each of them. He always saved one for Orsen. Always. Even when he was hungry himself, especially then. "Here. Eat. We'll need our legs."

And that was when the lantern light swung into their row.

Orsen remembered — now, wholly, terribly, he remembered — the arithmetic of that moment. Two boys, one row, nowhere to go. If both were found, both were tithed early, and early tithes took more than scheduled ones; the takers were paid by weight, and a child's terror made the memories rich. Tam would lose something vast. Orsen, at eight, barely anything yet — but the taking itself, the needle-cold hook of it, the way it left you hollowed and docile. Tam had seen it happen to other children. Tam had gone gray watching it.

Or there was another arithmetic. A smaller one. One boy found, one boy hidden under the apple windfall, unseen.

Tam saw it too. Orsen knew that now, knew it the way you can only know a thing thirty years late: Tam understood the mathematics instantly, completely, and made his choice in less than a heartbeat. He stood up. He stepped out of cover. He held both apples, and he said, loudly, in the bright carrying voice he used for singing:

"He ran the other way! Toward the mill! I'll show you where if you let me keep—"

The knife was not the takers'. That was the detail that broke him open, the detail he had buried deepest. The takers didn't carry knives; they didn't need them. The knife came later, in the scuffle, in the dark, when Tam twisted free and ran and a man who was afraid of losing his bonus caught the boy by the collar and the boy fought, and there was a stone, and there was a fall, and there was —

No. Not a stone. Not a fall.

*You picked up the knife.*

Orsen was on the floor of the shrine now, actually on the floor, cheek against cold stone, and the memory poured through him without mercy. He had picked up the knife. It had fallen from the taker's belt in the struggle, and Tam was down, and the man was reaching for Tam's temple with the extraction hook, and Orsen — eight years old, fast when it mattered — had driven the blade into the back of the man's knee, and the man had screamed and fallen, and in the falling his own hand had —

No. Say it true. Say it true or the whole confession is worthless.

*Tam hit his head on the orchard wall. On the low stone. The sound it made — the sound it made was nothing. That was the unbearable part. It sounded like an apple dropping.*

And then the man was limping away into the dark, cursing, and Orsen was kneeling beside his brother in the wet grass, and Tam was looking at him with eyes that were already going somewhere else, and Tam smiled — gap-toothed, even now, even dying — and pushed one of the apples toward him with the last strength in his arm.

"Eat," Tam said. "You'll need your legs."

And Orsen ate the apple. He ate it while his brother died beside him in the orchard, because he was eight years old and starving and his body did what bodies do, and he tasted nothing, and he never forgot the taste of nothing for the rest of his life.

Then the takers' lanterns came back, drawn by the screaming man, and Orsen did the second thing. The ledger-thing. The thief-thing. He crawled to where the extraction kit had dropped in the struggle, and he found a blank vial, and he did what he had watched them do to Tam's grandmother, clumsy, sobbing, hands shaking so badly he nearly shattered the glass — he hooked his own temple and he took it. All of it. The orchard, the knife, the sound like an apple dropping, the taste of the apple he ate. Everything from the moment the lantern light swung into their row.

He left himself the running. He left himself the hiding. He left himself a brother who had simply gone away one October, cause unknown, grief unspecified. A wound without a weapon. He sold the sealed vial to the guild the next spring for bread and a bed, and the guild filed it, and the guild forgot it, and Orsen Halloran grew up quick and clever and hollow in one specific shape, and became the finest keeper of ledgers the Vellum Guild had ever employed, because a boy who has cut a hole in his own mind learns to keep very careful track of everything else.

* * *

He came back to himself by degrees, the way dawn comes — not all at once but in grays.

Sefa was holding him. He didn't know how long she'd been there. Her arms were around his shoulders and his face was wet and his nose had bled again, a thin dark line down his lip, and she was saying his name the way you say a word that has to mean something: *Orsen. Orsen. Orsen.*

"He gave me the apple," Orsen said. His voice was wrecked, sanded raw. "He was dying and he gave me the apple. He always — he always kept one for me. Even when—"

"I know," Sefa said.

"You read it. You read it and you — you watched me eat it. You watched—" A sound came out of him that he was ashamed of, and she gathered him closer and let him make it. "Why didn't you tell me?"

"Because it wasn't mine to give back." Her voice was steady but her heart was hammering; he could feel it against his shoulder blade. "Because a memory returned by someone else is still a theft, just wearing kinder clothes. Because I prayed for weeks about whether to tell you, and my god went quiet, and I think — I think even he was waiting to see what you'd choose."

"What I'd choose," Orsen repeated. He laughed, and it hurt. "Thirty years I built a man out of the pieces. Do you understand? I chose the ledger because ledgers balance. I chose the guild because the guild says everything has a price, and if everything has a price then what I did has a price, and if it has a price then it's paid, and if it's paid then I can stop—" He stopped. Breathed. "It doesn't work. I've been doing the sums for thirty years and it doesn't balance. Nothing balances. He was ten years old."

Sefa smoothed his hair back from his forehead, gentle as handling a wounded bird. "What was his name?"

"Tam." Saying it aloud felt like setting down a weight he'd forgotten he was carrying, and immediately missing it. "Tamas. He whittled flutes. He couldn't carry a tune but he whittled beautiful flutes. He wanted to see the sea." Orsen's breath caught. "I've never seen the sea. I always thought I just — never got around to it. I think I couldn't. I think some part of me knew the sea was his."

"That's why you're going to see it," Sefa said. "After. When this is done. We're going to walk to the coast and you're going to stand in the water and grieve until your knees buckle, and I'm going to sit on the sand and watch, and then we're going to eat terrible fish."

Despite everything — despite the flood still receding through him, despite the taste of nothing ghosting his tongue — Orsen laughed. A broken, wet, astonished laugh. "Terrible fish."

"The worst fish. Grief and bad fish. It's the oldest cure there is."

He sat up slowly. The shrine was dim; the candle had gone out entirely, and the only light was the bruised-violet glow leaking through the shutters, the harvest moon rising fat and wrong over the mountain. Three days. Maybe less. Somewhere above them, in a palace full of smiling courtiers, a hollow king was counting down to the Unbinding, and below them, beneath the mountain, a starving god waited with its mouth open.

But here, in this small room, something else had happened. Something Orsen had no ledger-entry for.

"It doesn't hurt less," he said wonderingly. "Everyone — the guild teaches that pain is inventory. That you move it, store it, sell it. That forgetting is mercy." He flexed his hands, feeling the ache in them, real and specific. "It hurts more than anything. And I feel — *whole*. Is that mad? I killed my brother's killer and my brother died anyway and I ate the apple, and I am the worst person I have ever catalogued, and I feel *whole*."

"You're not the worst person you've ever catalogued," Sefa said. "I've seen your ledgers. You once recorded a man who sold his daughter's wedding day for a horse."

"A genuinely terrible man," Orsen agreed. His voice wobbled. "Tam would have hated what I became. He'd have hated the guild, the ledgers, the — the whole careful gray life. He'd have hated that I forgot him most of all." He looked down at his hands. "But he'd have understood the apple. That's the awful thing. He'd have understood."

Sefa took his face in her hands and made him look at her. Her eyes were shining, and behind the shine was the priestess, the woman who served the god he had helped rob, and the love and the duty sat together in her face without canceling each other out, which he suspected was the entire secret of being human.

"Tomorrow we go up the mountain," she said. "Tomorrow we pour a king's memory back into a counterfeit crown and pray it wakes the true man inside. Tonight—" her thumb traced the dried blood on his lip, tender and unflinching, "—tonight you are a boy in an orchard, and you get to be sad about it. Both things are true. Carry them both."

"Carry them," Orsen said. "Not trade them away."

"Not trade them away."

He closed his fist around the empty vial. Then, outside, a bell began to ring — not the shrine's small bell, but the great cracked bell of Caerlum's citadel, ringing the alarm that hadn't sounded in a hundred years, and beneath it, rolling down from the mountain like a landslide given voice, came a sound neither of them had words for: the god beneath the earth, waking early, and hungry.

Sefa went white. "The rite," she breathed. "Orsen — it's starting. It's starting *now*."

He was already on his feet, pocketing the vial, reaching for her hand — the same hand, he thought wildly, that had once held an apple out to a dying boy, the same blood, the same stubborn fastness when it mattered.

"Then we run tonight," he said. "For Tam. For Aldric. For every prayer in the realm."

The bell rang on, and the mountain answered, and they ran.
