## 17. Ledger Closed

The mountain did not want me to come quietly. That should have been my first warning.

The road up from the ford was the kind of road that exists only in old debts — no cart ruts, no milestones, just a scar of black stone winding through ash orchards gone feral since the guild stopped sending tithe-takers this way. Sefa walked beside me with her god's silence wrapped around her like a second cloak, and every hundred paces or so she would glance at me sideways, the way you check on a man carrying something breakable.

Which I was. Though not in a way she could see.

"You're doing the thing," she said finally.

"What thing?"

"The ledger thing. Where your lips move and you're adding up numbers nobody else can see."

I hadn't realized my lips were moving. "Habit," I said. "Forty years of habit. You spend enough time balancing accounts and the arithmetic starts living in your mouth."

"And what are we counting?"

"Everything I own." I patted the satchel at my hip, where a single vial rode against my ribs like a second heart. Inside it: a boy in an orchard, a knife, his brother's face going slack. My memory, reopened two nights ago at the cost of a nosebleed that took an hour to stop and a grief that had not yet agreed to stop at all. "Because tomorrow I intend to hand all of it to a god, and I'd like to know what I'm spending."

Sefa said nothing for a while. The orchard rustled around us — real wind, ordinary wind, though after everything we'd seen I no longer trusted anything to be ordinary.

"You know he'll take it," she said at last. "That's what frightens you."

"That is *not* what frightens me."

"No?"

"I'm a keeper of ledgers, Sefa. I have spent my whole life making sure that when a debt comes due, someone pays it. Usually someone who deserves it less than most." I turned the vial over in my fingers. The glass was warm. Memory always runs warm; the guild taught us that in our first week, along with how to hold it without letting it hold you back. "If he takes it, the account balances. That's the whole point of an honest accounting. What frightens me is that part of me wants him to. Part of me has wanted, for forty years, to put this down and never pick it up again. And I don't trust any offering made by a man who half hopes to be robbed."

She stopped walking. She had a way of stopping walking that made the whole landscape feel obliged to stop too.

"Orsen Halloran," she said, "you are the only thief I have ever met who audits his own motives before committing the crime."

"Somebody has to. The guild certainly never did."

* * *

We reached the shrine at dusk, if you could call it a shrine. Sefa had described her god's high places as humble, but humility undersold it. It was a cave mouth in the mountain's flank, hung with prayer-ribbons gone grey as cobwebs, and inside it there was nothing at all except a floor of worn stone and a smell like rain about to happen. No idol. No altar. Three hundred years of neglect will do that to a god — or perhaps the god had never wanted furniture. Gods of memory, Sefa had told me once, keep their houses empty so there's room for yours.

I set out the things I'd brought the way I'd set out documents for an audit: precisely, and with more ceremony than strictly necessary. The vial. A candle. A blank page from one of my own ledgers, because if I was going to make an offering to a god of memory, I was damned well going to do it in writing.

"You're going to *invoice* him," Sefa said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I'm going to present a statement of account. There's a difference. An invoice implies he owes me something."

"He does owe you something. He owes everyone something. That's rather the crisis we're all having."

"Sefa." I looked up at her, kneeling there among my neat little arrangements, and felt the strange tenderness that had been growing in me since the night we'd shared stolen wine in her shrine and discovered we were both lonely in exactly compatible shapes. "Tomorrow the impostor finishes the rite unless Aldric wakes. Tonight is the only night I get to decide who I am when I walk into that mountain. Let me be a bookkeeper about it. Bookkeeping is the only prayer I know how to say properly."

Something moved in her face — that flicker she got when her god brushed past behind her eyes. Then she sat down across from me, cross-legged, priestess-fashion, and folded her hands.

"Then say it," she said. "I'll witness."

* * *

Here is what I wrote, by candlelight, on a page torn from the Vellum Guild's finest stock, which felt appropriate in the way that paying a debt with stolen coin feels appropriate:

*Statement of Account, rendered by Orsen Halloran, sometime Keeper of Ledgers, to the God Beneath the Mountain, called by his priestess the Listener.*

*Item the first: One memory, sealed forty years, reopened three nights past. Contents: the death of Tam Halloran, aged eleven, by the hand of his brother Orsen, aged twelve, in the ash orchard at Marrow's Edge, during the guild's autumn tithe. Purpose of the original theft: survival. Purpose of the present offering: payment in full of the debt incurred by the Vellum Guild against the gods it served and betrayed.*

*I am aware that this memory is not mine alone. I am aware that the guild took ten thousand memories like it from ten thousand grieving people, and that my crime is smaller than the institution's and older than my employment. I am aware that a single vial cannot balance a ledger written over centuries.*

*I offer it anyway. Not because it is sufficient. Because it is true, and because it is mine, and because I have learned — late, and badly, and at cost — that a debt you carry yourself is worth more than a debt you trade away.*

*Respectfully submitted, with full knowledge that the creditor may decline these terms,*

*O.H.*

I read it aloud. My voice held until the last line, mostly. Sefa lit the candle, and the flame stood perfectly straight in air that had no business being still, and then the dark behind the cave mouth became occupied.

I want to be careful here, because people who write about gods tend to reach for either terror or beauty, and the truth of that moment was neither. The Listener arrived the way understanding arrives — all at once, everywhere, with the particular weight of something that has been listening to you your entire life and has simply decided, now, to let you notice. The candle flame bent toward the darkness like a bowing servant. The prayer-ribbons stirred. And a voice that was not a voice said, in the tone of a very patient clerk:

*You have kept accounts for the thieves.*

"Yes," I said. My knees were on the stone. I didn't remember putting them there. "For thirty years. I told myself I was only recording. I've learned better recently. Recording is a kind of consent, if you never object."

*And now you object.*

"Now I settle."

The presence considered me. Being considered by a god is an experience I recommend to no one; it is like being read aloud, slowly, by someone who pauses at the embarrassing passages. I felt him turn the pages of me — the forged entries, the quiet thefts, the years I'd spent hiding one small vial among ten thousand large ones, the girl in the market whose memory I'd lifted to save Sefa's life and hated myself for enjoying. He read all of it. He read the orchard last, and when he read the orchard, the cave went cold enough that our breath showed, and somewhere far below us something vast shifted its weight.

*Tam Halloran,* the god said, and my brother's name in that voice was a bell tolling underwater. *You killed him to live. You cut the memory out so that living would be possible. And now you bring him back to me as currency.*

"Yes."

*You understand that this is what the guild did. This is the exact shape of the original sin. A grief taken so that life could continue. You are offering me the guild's crime in miniature, performed by your own hand, and asking me to call it payment.*

"I know," I said. "Believe me, I have audited this from every angle. Here is the difference, and it is the only difference, and I will understand if you find it insufficient." I lifted the vial. "They took other people's grief so they wouldn't have to feel it. I am giving you mine so that I *will*. Every day, for whatever days remain. I'm not trading it away. I'm handing you the receipt and keeping the loss. That's what ownership means. That's the whole of what it means."

Silence. The long kind, the geological kind. Sefa's hand found mine without looking, and I gripped it hard enough that we'd both have bruises, and neither of us let go.

*Forty years,* the god said at last, *you carried a sealed wound and called it survival. Tell me, keeper of ledgers. When you opened it — when the knife came back to you — what did you feel?*

"Grief," I said. "All of it, at once, whole. And underneath it—" I stopped. I had not said this to anyone, not even Sefa. Especially not Sefa. But a god of memory does not accept partial disclosure; I could feel him waiting at the edge of the sentence like a reader at a page-turn. "Underneath it, relief. Terrible relief. Because it was *mine*, even the worst of it. Because for forty years some stranger had been wearing my life and calling it me, and I hadn't known why I always felt like a forgery."

*Yes,* said the Listener, and the word went through me like a plumb line finding bottom. *That is what my brother-gods were robbed of. Not worship. Worship is noise. We were robbed of being *known* — truly entered, truly carried, grief and all. Your guild sold us off in thimblefuls until the world forgot how to hold a god, and a god unheld grows hungry, and a hungry god beneath a mountain becomes a thing that kings build rites to feed.* The darkness gathered itself, and I felt the attention of something enormous narrow down to the size of one kneeling man holding one small bottle. *So. Terms.*

"Terms," I managed.

*Your guild's debt is measured in stolen prayers and stolen memories beyond counting. One vial, however honestly offered, pays none of it. By strict accounting, I should refuse you entirely.* A pause, and — I swear this — something almost dry entered the not-voice, the tone of an auditor who has found an error he secretly admires. *But I have read your ledgers, Orsen Halloran. All of them. Thirty years of entries, and in thirty years you falsified nothing. Do you know how rare that is? Thieves surrounded you. Liars employed you. And you kept clean books in a dirty house, because some stubborn corner of you believed the numbers owed each other the truth even when no one else did.*

"I didn't do it for the gods."

*No. You did it because it was right, which is better.* The candle flared. *Here are my terms, then. I will not take the memory. You will keep it — carry it, grieve it, speak your brother's name until it wears smooth as a river stone. That is the payment I actually want, and it is larger than anything in your little bottle. In exchange, I require one service, and one only, and it must be rendered tomorrow.*

My stomach dropped. "Name it."

*When you pour King Aldric's memory into the thing that wears his crown — and you will, tomorrow, at the mountain's heart, or everything ends — you will do it as my hand. Not my servant. My hand. There is a difference, and the difference is that a servant obeys and a hand is trusted. For the length of the pouring, I will move through you, and you will feel everything the true king feels as he wakes: forty years lost, a kingdom hollowed, a face stolen. You will carry his grief into the world alongside your own, and you will not set either of them down afterward. That is the lesser price. It will not feel lesser.*

I knelt there a long moment, doing the arithmetic in my mouth, the old habit, the only prayer I knew. Two griefs instead of one. A god's trust instead of a god's mercy. Tomorrow, the mountain's heart, and a counterfeit king waiting with a rite that would drink every prayer in the realm.

It was, by any honest audit, a terrible bargain.

"It's accepted," I said.

*Of course it is,* said the Listener, and for the first time the not-voice carried something unmistakably like warmth. *You kept clean books, Orsen Halloran. Clean books always balance in the end. They simply insist on choosing their own hour.*

The presence withdrew the way a tide withdraws — reluctantly, leaving the stones wet. The candle straightened. The cave smelled of rain that had finally happened. And in my hand, the vial with my brother's death in it sat cool and ordinary and utterly unspent, and I found, to my considerable surprise, that I was glad.

Sefa let out a breath she'd been holding for approximately a century. "He refused you," she said. "He refused the offering, and somehow that's the most generous thing I've ever—"

"He took a bigger one," I said. I was still looking at the vial. "He took the carrying."

"Orsen." Her hand tightened on mine. "What did you agree to?"

I told her. All of it. And I watched the color leave her face one shade at a time, because Sefa knew — as I was only beginning to — what it meant for a god to call a mortal his *hand*, and what the true king's waking would feel like poured through a man who had only just learned to survive his own grief.

"You won't be able to set it down," she whispered. "His forty years. They'll live in you beside Tam. Forever."

"I know."

"And you said yes anyway."

"I said yes anyway." I closed my fist around the vial and stood, knees cracking, forty years old and suddenly feeling most of them. "Because tomorrow either Aldric wakes or the world goes into the god's mouth, and I would rather walk into that mountain carrying everything I owe than one thing less."

Outside the cave mouth, the sky had begun to bruise violet at the eastern edge — hours too early, days too early, the harvest moon climbing fat and wrong above the peaks. The rite was starting. The impostor had not waited for the appointed hour.

Sefa saw it at the same moment I did. Her hand went to her god's symbol, and her face went white as ledger paper.

"Orsen," she said. "We're out of time."
