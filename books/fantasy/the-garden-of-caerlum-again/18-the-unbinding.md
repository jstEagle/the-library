## 18. The Unbinding

**Orsen**

The mountain had a throat, and they were walking down it.

That was the only way Orsen could describe the passage — a great descending tunnel of black stone, smooth as poured glass, wide enough that ten men might have marched abreast, and warm in a way that made his skin crawl. The air tasted of copper and old incense. Somewhere far below, something was breathing. Not wind. Breathing.

"Ledgers," he muttered, mostly to keep himself from screaming, "are supposed to balance."

Sefa glanced at him. In the violet light bleeding up from below, her face looked carved from dusk itself. "What?"

"When I was a boy," Orsen said, "the first thing they teach you at the Vellum Guild is that every entry has two sides. What was taken, and what was given. Debit and credit. If the columns don't match, someone has stolen something." He flexed his fingers around the strap of the satchel at his hip, where Aldric's memory rode against his ribs like a second heart. "Right now the columns don't match. A whole kingdom's prayers are about to be poured into one very hungry mouth, and nobody has entered the payment on the other side of the page."

"And you intend to correct the entry," Sefa said.

"I intend to *audit*," Orsen said. "The correction comes after. Assuming we survive the audit."

She almost smiled. It was a small thing, that almost-smile, but he had learned to treasure them the way a miser treasures coins of doubtful minting. They had come too far together for anything so honest as an actual smile.

The passage opened without warning.

One moment there was stone; the next, a cavern so vast that Orsen's eyes refused to accept it. The ceiling was lost in darkness overhead. The floor was a plain of black glass, and across it, in rings upon rings upon rings, stood thousands of people — no, not people. *Shapes* of people. Men and women in courtly dress, in soldiers' mail, in farmers' rough wool, all of them standing perfectly still, all of them wearing the same faint, pleasant smile.

The hollow ones. The crown-thing's hunters, and more than its hunters — its congregation. Every face turned toward the center of the cavern, where a dais of white stone rose from the glass like a tooth from a gum.

Upon the dais stood King Aldric Caerlum.

Except it wasn't. Orsen had studied the true king's memory vial for weeks now; he knew the man inside it the way he knew his own handwriting. This thing wore Aldric's face the way a glove wears a hand — correctly, smoothly, and with nothing inside. The crown on its brow was not gold. Up close — and the light made it horribly clear — it was *vellum*. Tanned skin, scraped thin, inscribed with prayers in an alphabet older than any kingdom. The god beneath the mountain had been written on once, long ago, and this crown was the receipt.

Before the impostor, suspended in the air by means Orsen's ledgers offered no category for, hung a vessel of light. It was shaped like a chalice the size of a cathedral bell, and it was filling. Threads of radiance drifted down into it from everywhere and nowhere — from the hollow congregation, from the mountain's walls, from the sky itself beyond the stone. Every answered prayer in the realm, being gathered. Every whispered *please* and *thank you* and *why have you forsaken me*, decanted into one cup.

"He's early," Sefa breathed. "The harvest moon isn't full for two more nights. He's *early*."

"Rites don't keep appointments?" Orsen asked.

"Rites are contracts," she said. "Contracts have terms. But if the cup fills before the moon—" She didn't finish. Her hands had gone to the amulet at her throat, the worn iron symbol of her neglected god, and Orsen saw her knuckles whiten.

Below them, on a ledge of rock overlooking the cavern floor, they crouched in the shadow of a fallen pillar the size of a ship. Close enough to see everything. Far enough, Orsen hoped, to be mistaken for rubble.

"The cup fills," said a voice, "when the last prayer arrives."

Orsen's blood went cold. The impostor hadn't moved. Hadn't turned. And yet the voice rolled up the cavern walls with perfect clarity, mild and pleasant and utterly empty, the voice of a man reading a menu.

"The priestess of the Quiet God," the crown-thing continued, conversationally. "And the keeper of ledgers. You are both expected. You have *always* been expected. That is what expectation *is* — a debt scheduled in advance."

Sefa rose.

Orsen grabbed for her arm and missed. She stepped out onto the black glass as though stepping into her own garden, chin lifted, and her voice when it came was steady, though he could hear the tremor underneath it, the way one hears the river under ice.

"You know my god's name," she called down.

"I know *all* names," said the thing wearing the king. "Names are entries. I keep the great ledger now. Soon I will keep the only ledger. Every prayer ever prayed, filed and fed. No more unanswered longing in the world. No more waiting. Doesn't that sound like mercy?"

"It sounds like theft," Sefa said. "You're stealing their *waiting* from them. The waiting is where faith lives. The waiting is where *people* live."

The impostor tilted its head, and the smile deepened by a fraction, and Orsen felt his stomach drop, because he recognized that expression. He had seen it in the guild's oldest clients. The look of something that genuinely believed it was helping.

"Come down," the crown-thing said gently. "Both of you. The rite has room. There is always room for one more forgotten thing."

* * *

**Sefa**

Her god had been silent for three hundred years, and then, in a shrine that smelled of tallow and mice, he had answered a beggar's prayer with terrifying precision — and Sefa had understood, in that moment, that silence had never been absence. Silence had been *fasting*.

Now, walking out onto the black glass toward a thing wearing her king's face, she prayed the way she had never dared pray before: not with words, but with *opening*. She unlocked every door in herself and stood aside.

*Take the house,* she thought. *I don't live in it anymore anyway.*

The cold came first. Then the vastness — a pressure behind her eyes like the whole night sky leaning close to look through one small window. Her knees buckled. She locked them. A priestess does not kneel unless she chooses to kneel, and she was not choosing it now.

When she spoke again, her mouth shaped words, but the voice that came out was not hers alone. It was hers layered over something older, deeper, a voice like a bell heard through fathoms of water.

"*Thief of faces.*"

The impostor went still. Even the chalice of light above it seemed to pause mid-pour, threads of radiance trembling.

"*You wear a crown of skin,*" Sefa's god said through her, "*inscribed with My own name, torn from My own mountain. You pour stolen longing into a cup and call it mercy. Hear Me: the account is called due.*"

"Great one," the crown-thing said, and its pleasant voice developed, for the first time, a hairline crack of something that might have been fear or might have been hunger — with such things, Sefa thought, they were the same emotion. "I feed you. Three hundred years I have gathered while your other servants slept. Every prayer in this realm flows to your table. Am I not your most faithful—"

"*You are a funnel,*" the god said, "*that has begun to believe it is a well.*"

The chalice flared. The impostor raised one regal hand, and the light bent toward it, and Sefa felt her god strain against her like a tide against a seawall — because the rite was already running, the contract half-executed, and even gods must honor the shape of old agreements. He could accuse. He could demand. He could not simply *stop* the pouring. Only the crown could do that. Only the crown could be unmade.

Only the crown could be *fed the truth*.

"*Keeper of ledgers!*" the voice boomed out of her, out of the mountain, shaking dust from the unseen ceiling. "*Now. While the funnel is full of Me and cannot look away.*"

Orsen came off the ledge at a dead run.

* * *

**Orsen**

Later — if there was a later — Orsen would remember the crossing of the black glass as the longest forty seconds of his life. Four thousand smiling faces turning to track him in eerie unison, like a field of sunflowers following a cloud. None of them moving to stop him. The crown-thing wanted him close. Wanted him delivered. Wanted, perhaps, exactly what he carried.

He reached the base of the dais. Above him, the impostor stood with its hand raised, drinking the light, its borrowed face serene. The chalice churned overhead, brimming now, overflowing in bright runnels that fell and vanished before touching the stone.

"Aldric Caerlum," Orsen shouted up, over the roar of gathering prayer. "By right of inventory! By right of *receipt!*"

The king's stolen face looked down at him. "Little clerk. Do you truly think a vial can undo me? I have worn this man for thirty years. I know his laugh. I know his griefs. I have *improved* him — trimmed the doubts, skimmed the sorrows. He was heavy. I have made him efficient."

"You've made him *hollow*," Orsen said, and pulled the stopper.

The memory of a king came out like dawn.

It rose from the vial in a column of gold and green — summer light on orchards, a woman laughing in a garden, a boy on a pony, a coronation oath spoken with a shaking voice, decades of days, ordinary and enormous, all of it at once, all of it *alive*. The nearest hollow shapes flinched back from it. One of them, a woman in courtly dress, made a sound — a small, cracked sound, like a door opening in a house long shut — and for just a moment her fixed smile wavered into something rawer. Something human.

The impostor recoiled. "No. *No.* That is paid for. That is *filed*—"

"Accounts get audited," Orsen said, and climbed.

The dais steps were white stone, and each one he mounted, the memory pressed harder against him, trying to pour itself into *him*, offering him its warmth, its gardens, its terrible beautiful weight. He gritted his teeth and held it the way he had learned to hold everything: at arm's length, catalogued, *owned*. This was not his grief. His job was delivery, not consumption.

He reached the top. The crown-thing loomed over him, and up close Orsen could see that its eyes were wrong — not empty, worse than empty. *Full*. Full of everyone else's light, and none of its own.

"This is mercy," the impostor said, and now the crack ran through the whole voice. "I can give you a throne of forgotten things. Painless. Empty. I showed you. You *saw* it. Why do you climb toward pain?"

"Because," Orsen said, "pain is mine. It's the only property I hold free and clear." And he seized the thing's jaw with both hands — it was strong, it fought him, its grip closed on his wrists hard enough to grind bone — and he poured the king home.

* * *

**Sefa**

From the cavern floor, Sefa watched the gold and green light go into the counterfeit king's mouth like a river into a drain, and she felt her god surge within her, and she felt — unmistakably — her god *laugh*.

Not cruelty. Recognition. The laughter of a creditor watching a debtor finally, *finally* open the books.

The crown-thing screamed. The scream was two voices braided together — the hollow thing shrieking in rage, and beneath it, cracking through, a third voice no one had heard in thirty years: rough, disused, *human*, saying words that were almost lost under the noise.

"—my garden — where is my garden — who has my *garden*—"

The vellum crown began to burn.

Not with fire. With *ink*. The ancient inscriptions glowed, then lifted off the skin of the crown like smoke rising off water, letter by letter, prayer by prayer, returning to the air they'd been stolen from. The god beneath the mountain drew breath — Sefa felt it in her teeth, in the glass under her feet, in the marrow of the world — one vast, slow inhalation, three hundred years of held breath released at last.

The chalice of light shattered.

Every thread of stolen prayer snapped loose at once — and instead of falling into the dark, they *rose*, streaming upward through the mountain's throat, out into the night sky, homeward, each spark of answered longing finding its way back to the chest that had sent it out. Somewhere in the realm, Sefa knew, a beggar was feeling an inexplicable warmth. A widow was remembering why she'd prayed. A farmer would wake tomorrow with his hope returned, unskimmed, entire.

On the dais, the counterfeit collapsed inward like a robe slipping off a hook.

And the man who remained caught himself on his hands and knees, gasping, gray-haired, crowned in nothing, wearing thirty years he had never lived.

Orsen knelt beside him. Sefa climbed the steps as fast as her legs allowed, her god receding from her like a tide going out, leaving her wrung-out and gloriously, ordinarily *herself*.

King Aldric Caerlum looked up at them with eyes that were wet and furious and achingly awake.

"How long," the true king rasped, "have I been gone?"

"Thirty years, Your Majesty," Orsen said. His voice shook. He didn't try to hide it. "Give or take an audit."

Aldric stared at him. Then, slowly, horribly, the king began to laugh — a broken, wheezing, entirely human laugh — and Sefa found she was laughing too, and crying, and holding on to Orsen's arm to stay upright, and somewhere below them four thousand hollow faces were blinking in the shattered light, smiles melting, memories seeping back into their owners like rain into drought-cracked earth.

The mountain exhaled one final time and went still.

Then, from the throat of the passage behind them, came footsteps. Many. Measured. Unhurried.

Orsen went pale. "Tell me those are friends."

Aldric got to his feet, swaying, empty-handed, uncrowned, and squared his shoulders with the stubborn grace of a man who had, at least, survived his own garden. "In my experience," the king said dryly, "they never are."

Out of the darkness marched the high priests of Sefa's order, and behind them, in robes of state, the Lord Chancellor — the impostor's oldest servant — with a warrant in one hand and chains in the other.

"Sefa of the Quiet Shrine," intoned the high priest. "For consorting with a thief of the Guild, for trafficking in stolen memory, and for raising her voice against the crown—" his eyes flicked to Aldric, and narrowed, "—or *what wears it* — you are commanded to return with us. And the thief," he added, "comes bound."

Sefa felt Orsen tense beside her. She felt her own heart, bruised and battered and still beating, make its decision before her mind caught up.

She stepped forward — not away from Orsen, but *in front* of him.

"No," she said.
