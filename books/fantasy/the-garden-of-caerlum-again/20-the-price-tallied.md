## 20. The Price, Tallied

The ledger room of the Vellum Guild had not changed in the three days since a king woke inside a counterfeit and a god went back to sleep beneath the mountain. The same green lamps. The same smell of vellum and lamp-oil and cold stone. The same ledgers, ranked on their shelves like patient soldiers waiting for orders.

Only Orsen Halloran had changed, and he felt it most sharply here, in the room where he had spent eleven years being very good at not looking at what he was looking at.

He set his resignation letter on the guildmaster's desk — the new guildmaster, Master Ellery Voss, who had been deputy for nine years and had the harried look of a man who had always known this day would come and hoped it would come to someone else.

"You're certain," Voss said. It was not really a question. It was the sound a man makes when he is buying time.

"I am." Orsen stood with his hands folded behind his back, the posture of a clerk delivering an audit. "The charter's dissolved. The king's decree came down yesterday morning. We have until the first frost to wind up our affairs."

"The king's decree," Voss repeated, tasting it. "You'd think after everything, Aldric might have let us keep *something*. We kept his memory safe for thirty years."

"We sold his memory," Orsen said mildly. "We also kept it safe. Both things are true. Kings get to weigh them however they like." He allowed himself a small smile. "Besides, he didn't dissolve us out of malice. He dissolved us because we were an abomination that happened to be well-organized. That's nearly a compliment."

Voss laughed despite himself, then pressed his palms flat against the desk as if steadying a table in rough weather. "And what will you do? A man of your talents. There's work in Caerlum for a careful mind. Banks. Archives. I could write you a letter—"

"No letters." Orsen shook his head. "I'm staying through the dissolution. All of it. Every vial, every bottle, every sealed locket in the vaults gets opened and returned."

The silence that followed had weight to it. Somewhere above them, rain had begun against the high windows, soft as fingers drumming.

"Do you know what you're proposing?" Voss asked quietly. "Some of those memories belong to dead men. Some belong to people who sold them gladly and built whole lives on the hole where they used to be. You'll be handing a farmer back the face of the child he paid us to make him forget. Is that mercy?"

"It's arithmetic," Orsen said. "I've been doing the other kind for eleven years. Grief deferred accrues interest, Master Voss. We've been charging it to people who never signed the loan." He looked toward the vault door, iron-banded and patient. "It comes due now. And someone should be standing there when it does. Someone who knows exactly how much was taken, and from whom, and why. That someone is me."

Voss studied him for a long moment. "You could just burn them unopened," he offered, and there was something almost kind in it. "Spare yourself the witnessing."

"That's what got us here," Orsen said. "Everyone sparing themselves."

* * *

They began the burning on a grey Tuesday, in the guild's inner courtyard, under a sky the color of old pewter.

Orsen had designed the procedure himself, because of course he had; if a thing must be done, it must be done properly, and if it must be done terribly, properly was the only mercy left. A brazier of black iron, tall as a man. A table beside it. A scribe — young Pell, who had joined the guild two years ago and still believed it was a respectable institution — to read each entry aloud from the master ledger while Orsen matched vial to entry, confirmed the owner or the owner's heir, and only then broke the seal.

The first hundred were easy. Memories of debts forgiven, of faces the owners wished never to see again, of griefs so heavy the owners had wept with gratitude when the guild took them. For these, Orsen wrote letters. *Your memory has been destroyed unopened. Whatever it held is yours to remember or not, as you choose. No one will ever sell it, buy it, or look upon it but you.* He signed each one. Eleven years of immaculate penmanship, spent at last on apologies.

The trouble began with the ones whose owners still lived, and wanted them back.

The first was a woman named Maren Ostwick, a dyer from the Tanners' Quarter, who arrived clutching her summons letter like a weapon she wasn't sure how to swing. She was perhaps fifty, with dye-stains permanent in the creases of her knuckles, and she stood in the courtyard doorway glaring at the brazier as though it owed her money.

"Entry four thousand one hundred twelve," Pell read, his voice cracking only slightly. "Sold by the bearer, twenty-two years past. Contents: one afternoon, spring. Description withheld at seller's request."

"That's mine," the woman said. "I know it's mine. I don't want it."

"Madam," Orsen began, "the decree—"

"I know what the decree says." Her jaw worked. "I sold it because my daughter died. That afternoon was the last — the last ordinary one, before the fever came. I couldn't bear it. Twenty-two years I've been fine. I've been *fine*." Her voice did something complicated on the word. "And now some boy-king says I have to feel it again?"

Orsen came around the table. He did not offer her the vial immediately; he had learned, in three days, that the offering itself was a kind of violence if done carelessly.

"No one can make you take it," he said. "The decree returns what was taken. It doesn't compel acceptance. If you refuse, I'll open it over the fire myself, and it will be gone, and no one will ever hold it over you or sell it to a stranger. That is also within your rights."

She stared at him. "That's it? You're not going to lecture me about healing?"

"I'm a ledger-keeper, madam, not a priest. My job is to tell you what you own. What you do with it is between you and your daughter."

Maren Ostwick stood in the courtyard for a long time, rain beginning again, fine as sifted flour. Then she held out her hand, palm up, like a beggar or a queen.

When Orsen broke the seal, the memory left the vial as a breath of violet light and sank into her through her open palm. She made no sound. But her knees went, and Orsen caught her elbow, and she leaned on him — a stranger, a thief, a clerk — and wept into his shoulder for the better part of an hour while Pell stood frozen by the ledger and the rain came down.

"There was a dog," she said at last, wiping her face with the heel of her hand, laughing and furious about it. "I'd forgotten the dog. Stupid animal stole the whole cake off the table. We laughed until we couldn't breathe." She looked up at Orsen, eyes red-rimmed and somehow younger than they'd been. "Twenty-two years I thought I was carrying nothing. Turns out I was carrying the shape of it. The hole. That's heavier than the thing itself, do you know that?"

"I know it exactly," Orsen said.

* * *

Not all of them went gently.

On the fourth day a man came for a memory he had not sold — one the guild had *taken*, in the bad decades before Orsen's time, from a debtor who could not pay in coin. His name was Tam Broadhurst, and he was sixty-one years old, and when the memory entered him he screamed.

It was a small memory, by the ledger's measure. One evening. A kitchen. His mother, alive, singing badly. The guild had valued it at almost nothing.

But Tam Broadhurst had spent forty years with a wound in him shaped like a song, and when the song poured back in he went down on the wet cobbles and howled like something hunted, and Orsen knelt beside him in the rain and held his shoulders and did not look away, because looking away was the whole of the guild's original sin.

"You knew," Tam gasped at him afterward, hunched and shaking. "You people *knew* what you were doing. Forty years. I thought I was mad. I thought I'd dreamed her. There was a — a shape in my head, a mother-shaped nothing, and everyone told me I'd never had—" He stopped. Started again, quieter, which was worse. "Why am I thanking you? Why do I want to thank you?"

"Because grief is the receipt," Orsen said. "It means the love was real and the theft is over. Both at once." He helped the old man to his feet. "Come inside. There's tea. It's terrible tea, but there's a great deal of it."

Tam Broadhurst came back the next day. And the day after. Not for anything — just to sit in the courtyard and watch the fires and drink terrible tea, as though keeping the guild company while it died. On the fifth day he brought honey cakes, which improved matters considerably.

"He's not the only one who screams," Pell said that night, as they banked the braziers. The boy had lost the last of his illusions somewhere around entry four thousand six hundred, and had replaced them, apparently, with a stubborn competence that reminded Orsen uncomfortably of his younger self. "Master Halloran — Orsen. Why do you stay for it? You could witness from a distance. Letters. A representative."

"Because I signed the entries," Orsen said. "Most of them, anyway. In the last eleven years, every memory that passed through this guild passed under my pen. I priced them. I catalogued them. I once described a man's wedding day as 'one lot, minor sentimental value.'" He fed another page to the coals and watched the ink curl and go gold. "A debt isn't paid when the coin changes hands. It's paid when the person you wronged looks you in the eye and you don't flinch. I intend to be looked at a great deal, Pell. It's the least flinching I can manage."

Pell considered this. "That's a horrible way to live."

"Yes," Orsen agreed cheerfully. "It's called a conscience. You'll want one too, eventually. They're dreadful, but they keep excellent records."

* * *

There were harder nights. There was the widow who took back a memory of her husband's betrayal and thanked Orsen with such quiet dignity that he lay awake until dawn wishing she had screamed instead. There was the merchant who refused his returned memory, then changed his mind at the door, then changed it back, then sat down on the courtyard steps and put his head in his hands while Orsen sat beside him and said nothing at all, which turned out to be the correct service. There was the vial marked with no name at all, contents unknown even to the ledger, which Orsen opened himself over the fire rather than let it pass to strangers — and caught, for half a heartbeat, an edge of something: snow, a bell, a voice calling a name that was almost his — before it burned away forever, and he wept without knowing for whom, which he suspected was the point.

And there was Sefa, who came on the ninth evening, hood dripping, smelling of woodsmoke and shrine-incense, and stood watching the fires with her arms crossed and her expression carefully neutral in the way that meant it was not neutral at all.

"Your order is asking questions about me," Orsen said, by way of greeting. He'd heard it from Voss, who heard it from a customs clerk, who heard it from a novitiate with loose lips. "Whether the former keeper of ledgers should be permitted to walk free, given what he knows. Given what he is."

"They are," Sefa said. "There's a faction. They call themselves the Unspotted Hand. They believe the guild's corruption lived in its people, not its charter, and that dissolving the charter just scattered the corruption into honest homes." She glanced at him sideways. "You feature prominently in their pamphlets. You're drawn with too many fingers."

"Naturally." He handed her a cup of the terrible tea. She drank it without complaint, which was one of the ten thousand reasons he loved her, and which he had not yet said aloud, because some entries a man keeps off the ledger until he's certain of the valuation. "And what does the rest of the order say?"

"The rest of the order remembers that my god slept peacefully for the first time in centuries after a thief gave back what he stole and chose to carry his guilt instead of trading it away." She said it lightly, but her hand found his in the dark between them, and gripped. "The rest of the order is mostly embarrassed by the Unspotted Hand. Embarrassment, however, moves slower than fear."

"How much slower?"

"Enough," she said. "For now." She watched a page of the great ledger catch and flare, thirty years of inventory going up in a column of gold sparks. "How many remain?"

"Nine hundred and change. Another month, perhaps less. The living claimants come first; the unclaimed burn at the end." He hesitated. "There's one I've been saving. Entry one. The oldest in the catalogue. Sold ninety-eight years ago by a seller recorded only as 'a servant of the crown.' I've written to every archive in Caerlum trying to trace an heir."

"And?"

"And yesterday a reply came from the palace." He drew the letter from his coat — palace cream, royal seal, and beneath the seal a line in a strong, grieving hand. "Aldric is coming himself. Tomorrow. He says—" Orsen stopped, and read it again silently, though he knew it by heart. "*Whatever my servant sold, it was likely the only piece of me the guild never touched. I would see it home.*"

Sefa was quiet a moment. "A king in your courtyard," she said. "Drinking your terrible tea."

"He's bringing his own honey cakes, according to the second paragraph. He's very insistent about it. Apparently grief has made him an enthusiast." Orsen smiled, and then the smile settled into something more careful. "After that, the last of the unclaimed. And after that—" He looked at the fires, at the guild dying honorably around him, at the woman beside him whose god had opinions and whose order had factions. "After that I'll need to decide where a man goes when the only trade he ever learned was stealing, and he's sworn off stealing."

Sefa squeezed his hand. "I may know a shrine," she said, "with a leaky roof, an empty donation box, and a priestess who's recently developed a taste for stolen wine and terrible tea."

"The wine was legally purchased."

"The *first* bottle was."

Above them the rain eased, and the fires burned on, patient as penance, consuming the guild's long inventory one impossible thing at a time — and tomorrow, a king would walk through that gate to collect the last piece of himself, and Orsen Halloran, keeper of ledgers no longer, would be there to witness it, flinching at nothing.
