## 21. Where Prayers Go Now

The silence came first to the small prayers.

Sefa noticed it the way you notice a clock stopping — not all at once, but in the accumulation of small absences. The widow Marta's prayer for rain on her cabbages went unanswered for a week, then two, and when she came to the shrine to complain about it, Sefa found herself saying, "Perhaps He thinks your cabbages have earned some independence," and the old woman laughed, and something in Sefa's chest unclenched for the first time since the mountain.

The great prayers had gone quiet too, but those silences were easier to bear. Nobody missed the thunder of a god answering a king. It was the small ones that left holes — the child asking for her fevered brother, the fisherman asking for one more season, the lonely asking, always, simply, *not this, please, anything but this*.

Her god still answered. Rarely. Carefully. The way a man who has nearly drowned wades into water.

She felt Him most clearly on mornings like this one, when the mist off the Caerlum lowlands pooled in the shrine courtyard and the candle flames stood perfectly straight, as if listening. She would kneel at the altar stone — cracked now down the middle where the god's presence had split it during the Unbinding, a fissure she had decided not to repair because it looked, frankly, dramatic — and she would feel something vast turn its attention toward her like an old dog hearing its name.

Not *speak*, the attention said. Not *ask*. Just: *I am here. Go on.*

So she talked. About the weather. About Orsen burning his breakfast again because he insisted ledgers were the only thing he'd ever been good at tending and he refused to learn cookery out of principle. About the way the ash orchards were blossoming out of season, white flowers on black branches, as if the land itself had decided grief and beauty could share a root system.

And the god listened. And did not fix anything. And somehow that was the miracle.

* * *

"You're doing it wrong," said Mother Ivenna.

Sefa set down the broom. She had been sweeping the courtyard, which was a thing she did now, daily, though the courtyard was never dirty — a discipline her hands had learned from Orsen, who swept his counting room every evening even when there was nothing to count, because, he said, *the ledger doesn't care how you feel, and that's why I love it.*

"Doing what wrong?"

"All of it." Mother Ivenna stood in the shrine gate in her traveling grays, dust on her hem from the long road down from the high temple, her face arranged in the expression Sefa privately called *the mercy of the disappointed*. Behind her stood four acolytes, young, solemn, clutching satchels full of doctrine. "We received your reports. The god answers what — one prayer in fifty? One in a hundred? The faithful travel days to reach this shrine and leave with nothing."

"They leave with being heard," Sefa said. "It turns out that's most of what they wanted."

"That is not what they wanted. That is what they *settled* for, because the gods were sleeping." Ivenna stepped into the courtyard, her sandals clicking on the stones. "Now the gods are waking. The Unbinding was stopped, yes, praise be, but the hunger below the mountain is real, and our order exists to manage that hunger through proper devotion. Proper rites. Proper *asking*. Instead we find you holding — what is that, a tea service? You've given the god a tea service?"

"He likes the blue cup."

Ivenna closed her eyes briefly, the way one does before a long fall. "Sefa. You are the finest priestess of your generation. You stopped the rite. You stood before a starving god and lived. The Synod wants you elevated. Third Voice of the High Temple. They want your counsel on how to rebuild the old covenant."

"And if I decline?"

"Then they want to know what to do about the other matter." Ivenna's voice gentled, which was worse. "The keeper of ledgers. Halloran. The Synod has reviewed the guild records you sent. A man who spent years trafficking in stolen memory. Who stole from a king. Who stole from *himself*, Sefa, which the theologians can't even agree is possible—"

"It's possible," Sefa said quietly. "I've seen it."

"—and who, by his own confession, took memories from living people for coin." Ivenna spread her hands. "The doctrine is clear. A soul that deals in stolen remembrance cannot stand within consecrated ground. Exile from the temples. All of them. He may live, he may be forgiven by whatever private god forgives bookkeepers, but he cannot stand where you stand. And you, if you remain a priestess, cannot stand where he stands."

The mist was burning off. Somewhere beyond the wall, Orsen was arguing with the neighbor's goat, which had developed a firm conviction that ledger paper was edible, and losing the argument with the particular patience of a man who had once catalogued ten thousand impossible things and considered a goat a manageable entry.

"The doctrine was written," Sefa said, "when the gods slept. When we thought prayer was a transaction. Ask, pay, receive. We built a whole faith on the shape of a vending machine, Ivenna, and then the machine woke up and asked us for *tea*."

"That is not—"

"I sat with Him." She hadn't told anyone this. Not the Synod, not her letters, no one. But Ivenna had walked nine days through the ash orchards to deliver this ultimatum, and she deserved truth rather than tactics. "After the Unbinding. He came to the shrine — not in fire, not in voice, just *present*, the way grief is present — and do you know what He asked me? Not for sacrifice. Not for the tithe of remembrance the guild cheated Him of. He asked me whether anyone had ever simply *stayed* with Him while He remembered what He'd lost. Three thousand years of being prayed at, and nobody had ever stayed."

Ivenna's jaw tightened. "You cannot build doctrine on an afternoon."

"No," Sefa agreed. "But you can notice when the doctrine has been building itself on an absence."

* * *

That evening she found Orsen on the shrine steps with the goat's teeth marks in his sleeve and a look on his face she had learned to read the way she once read omens.

"You've had a letter," he said.

"How could you possibly know that?"

"Because you have the face you get when you're composing a reply in your head and rejecting every draft. Also there's ash on your fingers from the incense you burn when you're anxious, and you only burn that particular one when someone from the high temple writes, because you say it smells like authority." He shifted over on the step to make room for her. "Third Voice?"

"Exile," she said. "For you. From every consecrated ground in the realm. And for me, elevation, if I accept it."

He was quiet for a moment. Below them, the valley lights were coming on one by one, little answers people made to the dark without needing a god to countersign them.

"Well," he said finally. "It's a reasonable position. Doctrinally sound. I did spend eleven years selling pieces of people's souls to lonely aristocrats, and I kept immaculate books about it, which I suspect bothers them more than the thefts." He turned the blue teacup — he'd taken to carrying it, God help them both — in his hands. "You should take the elevation."

"Orsen."

"You should. It's what you've earned. Twenty years of service, and you stopped the end of the world, and you make a passable cup of tea even with the inferior cups." He said it lightly, the way he said everything that mattered, and she had learned that too — that his humor was a door held open, not a door shut. "I can live anywhere. I'm very good at living nowhere. It's practically my only credential besides the ledgers."

"Where would you go?"

"There's work in the free cities. Someone has to audit the memory trade now that it's been driven underground, and I am, regrettably, the foremost expert in the realm on how thieves think." He smiled at the valley. "I'd write. You'd get letters. Long ones. You complain about my margins and I'd ignore you, and it would be a fine arrangement."

"Orsen Halloran." She took the teacup out of his hands and set it on the step between them, deliberately, like a piece on a board. "Look at me."

He did. His eyes were the same eyes that had bled on the floor of the vault when his own memory screamed at him; the same eyes that had poured a king back into his crown; the same eyes that had wept in an orchard over a brother whose name he'd carried for thirty years without knowing he carried it. She had watched him choose, over and over, the harder thing — the remembering thing — until choosing it had worn a groove in him, and the groove had become a man.

"I spent my whole life," she said, "being taught that love was a debt the gods collected. That devotion meant surrendering what you wanted. And then I met a god whose entire request was that somebody stay, and I met a thief whose entire redemption was that he stopped trading things away, and I am *not*, Ivenna's doctrine or any other, going to stand in a temple built on transactions and call that holiness while the actual holy thing sits on a step arguing with a goat."

The goat, hearing itself mentioned, looked up with an expression of profound innocence.

"So," Sefa said. "Here is what I wrote to the Synod tonight, in my head, drafts one through forty. I resign my office. Not defrocked — resigned, which sounds better and lets me keep the gray shawl. I keep the shrine, because the shrine belongs to the god and the god has not objected, and I invite Him to object any time He likes, loudly, so we can settle this properly. And I rebuild it around listening. No tithe. No transaction. People come, they speak, they are heard, and the god answers when answering won't break anyone. Which will be rare. Which will be *precious*. Scarcity, Orsen, is the only honest theology left."

"And the exile?" he asked softly. "They'll hold you to it. If I set foot on consecrated ground—"

"Then I'll reconsecrate it differently." She picked up the teacup again and pressed it back into his hands. "This is mine. This shrine is mine. I've decided the god and I are in agreement, and if the Synod disagrees, they're welcome to walk nine days through the ash orchards and argue with both of us."

Orsen looked at the cup. Then at her. Something moved through his face that she suspected he would have bottled and sold, years ago, to a client who'd lost the ability to feel it — and instead he just let it sit there, unledgered, unpriced, entirely his.

"You understand," he said, "that I will now be underfoot constantly. There is no guild left to flee to. I'll reorganize your candle inventory. I have *opinions* about your candle inventory."

"Dire ones, I hope."

"Sefa, you store the votives next to the tallow and it keeps me awake at night."

"Then it's settled," she said. "You'll suffer productively."

* * *

The god answered three days later.

It was dawn, and she was alone in the courtyard — Orsen having gone to town to purchase candles against her explicit instructions regarding quantity — when the mist gathered itself into something like attention, and the cracked altar stone hummed under her palms, and she knew Him the way you know footsteps on your own stair.

*You gave up a throne,* the presence said. Not in words, exactly. In the way a warmth fills a room. *They offered you the third-highest seat in the faith, and you chose a leaky shrine and a bookkeeper.*

"Not a throne," she said aloud, because talking to gods out loud was another thing she'd decided to stop being embarrassed about. "A counter. A very ornate counter. And I didn't give it up. I traded it."

A pause. The vast, dry amusement of something ancient settling closer.

*Traded.*

"For something better. Yes. I know what that makes me. I know exactly what that makes me." She smiled at the mist. "It makes me yours, actually. You taught me that. Everything worth having is a trade, and the only question is whether you're honest about what you paid."

The silence stretched — but it was the good silence now, the listening kind, and she had learned to tell the difference the way farmers learn clouds.

*The Synod will come,* the god said at last. *In a year, perhaps two. With doctrines and decrees and young acolytes with serious satchels. They will declare this shrine irregular. Unsanctioned. Possibly heretical.*

"I know."

*What will you do?*

"What I've been doing. Sweep the courtyard. Pour the tea. Listen." She rested her hand flat on the cracked stone, on the fissure that had become, over the months, less a wound than a feature — proof that something enormous had passed through and chosen, in the end, to stay gentle. "Let them call it what they like. The people who need it will find it. They always do. That's the one doctrine I never needed teaching."

The warmth withdrew slowly, reluctantly, the way morning leaves a bed. And in its wake, on the altar stone, in the crack, a single white flower had opened — ash-orchard blossom, impossibly out of season, impossibly far from any orchard.

Sefa looked at it for a long moment.

Then she went inside, found paper and ink, and began the letter she'd been drafting in her head for weeks — not to the Synod, but to King Aldric, who had written twice already asking, with the careful formality of a man learning to feel things again, whether the royal garden at Caerlum might be restored, and whether the two of them might come and see it done.

*There is a tree I mean to plant there,* Orsen had said, months ago, in the orchard, with his brother's name finally in his mouth. *When there's time. When it's over.*

It was over. Spring was coming early to the ash orchards, and the king was asking, and the god had left a flower on the altar like a signature on a contract.

Sefa dipped her pen, and wrote: *Your Majesty — we will come before the blossom falls.*
