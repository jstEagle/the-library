## 22. The Garden of Caerlum Again

The garden had been dead for thirty years, and now it refused to stay that way.

Orsen noticed it first in the roses — or what had been roses once, before the frost-wards failed and the impostor's reign let everything go to seed. They had come back wrong and wonderful, climbing over the broken trellises in colors no royal gardener would have permitted: bruise-purple beside fire-orange, white bleeding into pink on the same stem. Weeds had moved into the gravel paths and made themselves at home. A fig tree had split the wall of the old orangery and was bearing fruit with the shameless abundance of something that had been told, for three decades, that it was not allowed.

"It's a mess," Sefa said, standing beside him at the gate with her hands folded in her sleeves, in the manner of a priestess assessing a shrine.

"It's a garden," Orsen said. "It's just stopped pretending."

She smiled. He was still, after all these months, not entirely used to the fact that she smiled *at him*, and that it meant what it meant.

King Aldric had given them leave to do whatever they liked with the place. "Burn it," he'd said, when Orsen asked, with the tired honesty of a man who had lost thirty years and gained a kingdom full of strangers who remembered him better than he remembered himself. "Give it away. Let goats live in it. I can't look at another ordered hedge for a while." Then he had paused, and looked out through the window at the wild tangle below, and added quietly: "Although. It does seem *happier* this way. Doesn't it?"

It did. That was the strange thing. Everything the guild had ever touched had been about keeping things exactly as they were — bottled, sealed, catalogued, safe. The garden had done the opposite. It had let everything rot and fall and reseed itself, and what came up was more beautiful than anything the original design had intended.

Orsen had a spade over his shoulder and a sapling wrapped in sacking under his arm, and his heart was doing something complicated about both.

"Where do you want it?" Sefa asked.

He had thought about this for weeks. There was a corner near the old reflecting pool where the light came down gold through a gap in the ruined pergola, and the soil was dark and soft from years of fallen leaves nobody raked. But that wasn't the reason. The reason was that when he finally opened the memory — the knife, the orchard, Tam's face going still beneath his hands — the last thing in it, the very last thing, was sunlight coming through leaves exactly like that. His brother had died looking up into green and gold. It seemed right that something should grow there now, in the shape of that light.

"There," he said.

They walked down together. The path crunched with weeds going to seed. Somewhere overhead a thrush was singing with the total self-absorption of a bird that has never once considered its own mortality, and Orsen found himself envying it and then, surprisingly, not envying it at all.

* * *

The digging took the better part of an hour, because the ground had not been turned in thirty years and had opinions about it. Sefa knelt and pulled stones out of the way while he worked the spade, and at some point she got mud on her sleeve — real mud, shrine-mud, the kind her order spent half its liturgy symbolically washing away — and laughed at herself for it, a small startled sound like she'd surprised even herself.

"You're going to tell me," she said, as he lowered the sapling into the hole, "that this is the part where you say the whole thing aloud. You promised."

"I promised."

"And you're stalling."

"I'm *tamping the soil*," Orsen said with dignity, pressing the earth down around the roots with the flat of the spade. "There is a difference. Tamping is a sacred horticultural duty."

"Uh-huh."

But she settled onto the low wall by the reflecting pool and waited, and he knew she understood why it mattered. She had read the memory through her god's sight months ago — the dead boy, the knife — and had carried that knowledge alone, saying nothing, because some mercies are lies and some lies are the only shelter a person can stand in. Then at the ford she had told him the truth instead, and he had nearly destroyed them both with his rage, and then he had gone into the orchard behind the ash farms and opened the seal himself, and learned what he had paid three hundred crowns' worth of stolen years to avoid knowing:

That his brother's name was Tam. That Tam was nine. That the tithe-takers came every autumn, and one autumn they came early, and there were two children hiding in a root cellar with one way out, and Orsen — eleven years old, clever, quick, already half a guildsman in his instincts — had done the arithmetic that no child should ever be asked to do. One could run. Two could not. And he had chosen himself, and lived, and then stolen the memory of choosing so thoroughly that he had built an entire life on top of the absence, the way you build a house over a well.

He watered the sapling from the pool with a dented cup. Its leaves were small and new and slightly crooked, and it looked, frankly, terrible — a stick with ambitions. He loved it immediately.

"All right," he said, and sat down next to Sefa on the wall. "Start to finish. As promised."

* * *

He began, as all good ledgers begin, with the inventory.

"I was born in Marrowgate, in the tanners' quarter, which means my first memory is the smell. People romanticize poverty, but nobody romanticizes tanneries." Sefa made a face of profound agreement; her own childhood parish had been downwind of the dye-works. "My mother died of the lung-rot when I was seven. My father lasted two more years and then the guild's tithe-takers came for us, because our parents had borrowed against their memories during the famine year — sold little pieces of themselves to buy grain, the way everyone did. The debt passes to the children. Did you know that? I didn't, until the night I learned it."

"The guild taught you that debt is inherited," Sefa said softly.

"The guild taught me that *everything* is inherited. Grief, guilt, memory. We just pretended we were only moving paper." He turned the dented cup in his hands. "So. Root cellar. Autumn. Tam and me. And the choice." He said it plainly, without flinching, because he had decided that if he was going to carry it, he would carry it in the open where it could be seen, the way you carry a scar rather than hiding it under a sleeve. "I ran. He didn't follow. Not because he couldn't. Because I told him to wait, and he trusted me, and trusting me was the last thing he ever did."

Sefa's hand found his. She didn't squeeze it or make it better. She just held it, the way you hold a thing that is true.

"Afterward I did the second-worst thing I've ever done. I went to the guild and bought a forgetting. I was eleven and I had nothing to pay with except the memory itself — they took it as the fee. Neat accounting. The crime pays for its own erasure. I've audited enough ledgers since to know how elegant that looks on paper and how monstrous it looks everywhere else." He laughed, briefly, without much humor in it. "You know the worst part? For thirty years I felt *guilty* and didn't know why. Some nights I'd wake up certain I'd killed someone. I'd apologize to people for no reason. I once wept at a stranger's funeral for a man I'd never met. The grief stayed. Only the reason left. That's the great fraud of stolen memories, Sefa — you don't remove the pain. You just blindfold it."

"I know," she said. "I've prayed with a hundred people like that. Hurting without knowing what hurt them. I used to think it was a sickness of the age." Her mouth twisted. "It's a sickness of the *industry*."

"So. The guild raised me. Made me a ledger-keeper, because I was good with numbers and better with silences. And for twenty years I kept the books of impossible things — bottled first kisses, bottled betrayals, a memory of the sea owned by a woman terrified of water, forty years of a marriage sold off in installments by a husband who wanted to be free of it but not *lonely*. And in among all of it, in vault nine, shelf fourteen, filed under H —" his voice caught, steadied — "my brother. Catalogued. Sealed. Mine, and not mine. I checked on him every audit day. I told myself it was inventory control."

"Was it?"

"No. It was a grave visit. I just didn't know whose."

A bee was investigating the sapling's crooked leaves, finding nothing, refusing to accept this, trying again. Orsen watched it with something close to affection.

"Then the candles started burning again. Three hundred years of silence, and suddenly the gods were answering prayers, and everything in the vaults began to wake up. Including King Aldric's memory, which someone had sold decades ago in handwriting I recognized as my own predecessor's — and including, eventually, me. The guildmaster told me my sealed memory was collateral against the guild's oldest debt. The gods wanted payment for every theft. And the crown-thing — the hollow king — wanted to feed every prayer in the realm to the starving god under the mountain, and end hunger forever by ending *wanting* forever." He shook his head. "It offered me that, you know. When it caught me. A throne of forgotten things. No grief, no guilt, no well under the house. Just smooth empty peace. And for one whole moment — I want you to know this, because you deserve the whole ledger — for one moment I wanted it."

"I know," Sefa said. "I saw your face when you told the king. Anyone who says they wouldn't have wanted it for a moment is lying or hollow already."

"What saved me was Tam. The crown-thing reached for my memories to unmake me, and it hit the reopened one — the orchard, the knife, the *love*, because that's what it was underneath, Sefa, the whole horror of it was love, loving him so much that living without him was unthinkable — and it faltered. A thing with no memories of its own, choking on mine." He looked out at the wild roses. "Grief is proof of love. That's the whole secret. The hollow thing couldn't digest it because grief is the one food that's also a root. It grows into you."

* * *

He told the middle parts too, the parts she hadn't seen: the road through the ash orchards where the villages were drunk on answered prayers, asking the gods for rain and revenge and bigger cabbages with equal abandon; the night he stole a slaver's memory of mercy to save her life and hated how easily his hands remembered the old craft; the treasury of prayers under the palace, shelves of bottled devotion stacked like wine; the moment he poured Aldric's thirty years back into the counterfeit king's mouth and watched a true man wake inside the mask, screaming, weeping, alive.

And then he told the ending, which was the part he had rehearsed least and needed most to say.

"When the god came to collect — the honest god, the hungry one — I offered it the only thing I had left that was truly mine. Not the sealed memory. The *open* one. All of it. Tam, the knife, the guilt, the grief, kept whole, carried daily. I told it: this is what I owe, and I will pay it by remembering, every day, for the rest of my life. No trades. No bottles. An honest entry in an honest ledger." He swallowed. "And it accepted a lesser price instead. Do you understand what that means? Even a starving god wouldn't take grief freely given. It only ever grew fat on what was *stolen*. Mourning, honestly owned, fed it better than any theft — fed it enough to sleep. That's what the king figured out too, afterward. He mourned his lost thirty years out loud, in front of the whole court, and the mountain went quiet."

Sefa was quiet for a long moment. The thrush had stopped singing; the garden held its breath the way gardens do, which is to say not at all, everything rustling and dripping and growing regardless of human drama.

"Do you know what my order demanded, after?" she said at last. "Your exile. The man who robbed a god's own vault, they said, cannot be suffered to live within sight of any shrine. Doctrine. Very tidy. Very *catalogued*." She smiled, and this time there was iron in it alongside the warmth. "I told them the god had already ruled on the matter, and that if they wished to overrule him they were welcome to file the petition themselves. In person. Under the mountain."

"They were horrified."

"They were *horrified*. Half of them resigned in protest. The other half secretly asked me how I got the god to answer questions so reliably." She laughed, and he joined her, and the laugh did what laughter does in a garden — scattered, took root somewhere, came back doubled.

* * *

"So," Sefa said, when the sun had moved a hand's width across the sky and the sapling stood crooked and stubborn in its turned earth. "That's the whole ledger. Start to finish."

"Every entry. Audited and true."

"Then here's mine." She turned to face him fully, knees drawn up on the wall, mud still on her sleeve, and her eyes were bright and absolutely steady. "I was a girl who prayed to a silent god and decided silence was holiness, because that was easier than feeling abandoned. I served an institution that mistook inventory for faith. And then I met a man who kept ledgers of stolen souls and hid his own wound in the catalogue, and I fell in love with him precisely there — in the hiding, in the vault, in the dark. I lied to him to protect him. I told him the truth when the lie started killing him. And I choose him now with my eyes open, knowing every entry, including the ugly ones." She took both his hands. "*Especially* the ugly ones. That's the covenant, Orsen. Not forgetting. Keeping."

"Memory kept, not traded," he said. It had become their phrase, over the months — half vow, half joke, entirely serious.

"Memory kept, not traded."

He leaned his forehead against hers. Around them the garden went on being gloriously, disobediently alive — the fig tree shouldering its wall apart, the roses rioting over their trellises, the weeds making themselves emperors of the gravel. Nothing here was preserved. Everything here was *kept*.

"I'm going to burn the last of the inventory next week," he said quietly. "Every bottle. Every victim gets their own self back, even the ones who'll scream, even the ones who'll hate us. I'll witness each one. That's the penance, and I choose it." He paused. "And then I'm going to plant an orchard. Here. Apples, maybe. The kind Tam would have climbed."

"He sounds like he would have climbed anything."

"He climbed everything. Fell out of everything. Laughed the entire time." Orsen's voice cracked on the laugh and the grief together, and he let it, because that was the covenant too — letting them arrive together, unmixed, untraded, whole. "This tree's for him. And the orchard's for whoever comes next. Kids from Marrowgate, maybe. Kids the guild never gets its hands on, because there isn't going to be a guild."

Sefa kissed his muddy cheek. "The guild's charter is ash, the ledgers are burning, the god is asleep, and the king is napping off thirty years of catching up," she recited, counting blessings on her fingers like a village grandmother. "We are, by any professional measure, unemployed."

"Unemployed," Orsen agreed solemnly. "In a haunted garden. With one crooked tree."

"And each other."

"That too," he said. "Though technically that's not employment. That's—" he searched for the word, the precise entry, the honest line item— "*everything else*."

The thrush started up again. The sapling's leaves trembled in a wind that smelled of figs and turned earth and the far-off sea, and if you squinted — if you wanted to, if you chose to keep it — the light through those small crooked leaves fell in exactly the shape of a country two brothers once shared, and would share again, every day, for the rest of his life.

Down in the city, the evening bells rang for no god in particular, and everyone who heard them heard something different, and that was the whole miracle.
