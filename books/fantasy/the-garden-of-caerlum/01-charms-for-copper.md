## 1. Charms for Copper

The lamps of the Gullwing Market were lit by hand, one by one, and Quentin Joubert had learned to read the city by which ones came up late.

The tallow-man on the corner of Salt Row was always first — he had no gift for anything except waking early. The fishwife's lanterns came next, hissing as they caught. And last, always, the great iron lamp above the well at the market's heart, because the man paid to light it had stopped caring sometime around the second winter of his employment, and lit it only when the dark became impossible to argue with.

Tonight it was already burning when Quentin arrived, which meant it was later than they'd hoped.

They set out their tray on the third step of the fountain that hadn't run water in forty years: luck-knots braided from river-grass and red thread, fever-charms sewn into scraps of felt, a small dish of copper pennies to make change and, more importantly, to make noise. People trusted a seller who made noise. People did not look too closely at a seller who was busy counting coins.

That was the whole art of it, really. Marisol had taught them that before anything else — before knots, before words, before the breathing exercises that were supposed to teach control. *The best hiding place is a busy pair of hands.* Quentin had been seven years old, standing in her kitchen smelling of woodsmoke and rosemary, and she had put a coin purse in their small hands and said, *Count these. Out loud. Every time you feel it rise.*

Twelve years later, they still counted.

"Charms," they called now, voice pitched low enough to seem embarrassed, high enough to carry. "Luck-knots, two coppers. Fever-strings, three. Charms for copper, honest work for honest coin."

*Honest* was doing a great deal of labor in that sentence, and Quentin felt every ounce of it settle into their shoulders like wet wool.

The market filled around them the way it always did — not quickly, but the way water fills a cellar, seeping in through cracks you didn't know you had. Night workers coming off the dye-works with stained hands. Dockmen spending their wages before their wives could find them. A woman in a widow's black who bought a luck-knot every single week and never seemed to have any luck, and never stopped buying either, because hope was cheaper than bread and lasted longer.

"Evening, Quill." Odile appeared at their elbow, as she always did, smelling of beeswax and something faintly burnt. She was sixty if she was a day, with forearms like dock rope and a laugh that could be heard over the fish auction. Her stall was four steps down, hung with dried herbs and little clay bottles. "You look like something the tide refused."

"I feel like it."

"That's the weather." Odile squinted up at the sky, where snowclouds had been sitting on Caerlum like a debt collector for six days straight. "Long winter's coming early this year. Mark me. You'll sell every fever-string you've got."

"I only have honest ones."

Odile snorted. "And I only sell honest saffron, which is why my saffron is turmeric." She patted Quentin's cheek with a hand rough as bark. "Keep your head down tonight, love. There's a graycloak been walking the rows since dusk. Not doing anything. Just walking."

Quentin's hands went still over the coin dish. Just for a breath. Then they counted the pennies again, aloud, under their breath, and the stillness passed through them and out the other side.

"Tax collectors walk the rows," they said.

"Tax collectors count things." Odile's eyes were sharp and brown and missed nothing. "This one just looks."

She went back to her stall, and Quentin arranged their charms, and told themselves the cold in their stomach was only the wind off the harbor.

* * *

Here is what the city of Caerlum knew about magic, officially:

There wasn't any.

Here is what the city of Caerlum knew about magic, unofficially, in kitchens and cellars and behind the locked doors of certain shops after midnight:

There was a little. There had always been a little. A grandmother who could coax a sourdough starter back from death with a song. A midwife whose hands found pain and pressed it flat. Boys who could whistle birds down off roofs, girls who knew which knot would hold and which would slip before they tied it. Small things. Quiet things. Things that lived below the threshold where the throne bothered to look.

Because the throne had looked, once. Everyone knew the shape of that history even if no one said it plainly: the Mage War, three hundred years gone, when the gifted had burned half the map and the mortals had burned the rest, and what ended it was an artifact so terrible that both sides agreed to pretend it had never existed. After that came the Edicts. Magic was outlawed. Gifted blood was forfeit. And the word *witch-hunter*, which had once meant a profession, became a word mothers used to frighten children into silence.

So the small magics went underground, and stayed there, and learned to be small.

Quentin's magic had never once managed to be small. That was the problem. That had always been the problem.

Marisol used to say Quentin's gift was like a voice in a house built for whispering. It didn't matter how quietly Quentin tried to speak; the walls carried it. A fever-charm stitched by any hedge-witch in the city might shave a night off a child's illness. Stitched by Quentin — even stitched blind, even stitched while counting pennies, even stitched with deliberate mistakes worked into the pattern to bleed the power off — it ended fevers outright. Luck-knots didn't tilt fortune slightly. They bent it double.

Which was why Quentin sold them as trinkets. Cheap thread, cheap grass, cheap little lies. And why, every time one worked, Quentin lay awake half the night listening for boots on stairs.

* * *

The child came at the hour when the market was thickest, when there were too many bodies and too much noise for anyone to track any single thing.

He was maybe six, wrapped in a coat that had belonged to someone larger, and he stood at the edge of Quentin's tray with the particular gravity of a child who has been sent on a mission. Behind him, at a respectful distance, hovered a young woman — his mother, by the set of her jaw — holding three coppers so tightly her knuckles had gone white.

"Fever-string?" the boy recited, solemn as a priest.

"For your sister?" Quentin asked, because in the Gullwing Market it was always for someone else. Nobody bought charms for themselves. Hope spent on yourself felt like vanity.

The boy nodded. "She's hot all over. Ma says the physicker wants nine silver just to look at her."

Nine silver. Quentin looked at the woman, who lifted her chin in the way poor people do when they're bracing to be refused. Three coppers against nine silver. There was no arithmetic in the world that made that work, which was why the arithmetic of charms existed at all.

"Three coppers," Quentin said, and took them, and let the boy choose his own string from the tray — children always chose better than adults, unburdened by knowing what they wanted — and then, because it was habit older than memory, began to count the change aloud.

One penny. Two—

The gift rose.

It always rose at moments like this, when Quentin's attention split between the counting and the wanting. Because here was the truth Quentin hid even from themselves on good days: they *wanted* it to work. Twelve years of discipline, and underneath the discipline sat a child's simple wish — *let the sister be well, let the mother stop being afraid, let me be worth the risk Marisol took* — and the gift heard the wish the way dry ground hears rain.

Quinntin counted faster. *Two pennies, three—*

Too slow. The warmth went down their arm, through their fingertips, into the string the boy was already clutching, and Quentin felt it settle into the braid like water into roots. Felt the deliberate flaws they'd stitched into the pattern — the crossed thread, the uneven knot — seal themselves closed, smooth and perfect, the gift tidying up after itself like a maid in a hurry.

The string glowed. Very softly. Green as new moss, green as the first leaf of spring, visible for perhaps the length of one heartbeat in the shadow of the dead fountain.

Then it was gone, and the boy was looking at his palm, and the mother had gone very still, and somewhere in the crowd a graycloak turned his head.

"Strong string," the boy said happily, entirely unaware. "Ma, look how tight the knots are."

"Yes," his mother said. Her eyes came up to Quentin's, and Quentin saw the whole war happen in them — the fear, the gratitude, the arithmetic of a woman deciding whether the person who had just healed her daughter was a miracle or a noose. "Thank you," she said carefully. "For the... string."

"It's good grass this year," Quentin said. "River-grown."

She took the boy's hand and drew him away, fast, not running, but with the urgency of someone leaving a building they've decided is on fire. Quentin watched them go and counted the remaining pennies twice, though there was nothing left to hide behind.

Across the market, the graycloak had stopped walking. He stood near the fish stalls with his hands folded, and he was not looking at Quentin. He was looking at the space where the woman and boy had been, the way a man looks at a door that has just closed on a sound.

Odile appeared at Quentin's elbow again. She didn't say anything. She simply began, loudly, to complain about the price of wax, and stood there blocking the graycloak's line of sight until he moved on, and then she said, very quietly:

"How bad?"

"He'll forget," Quentin said. "Children forget. Mothers forget, mostly."

"And the cloak?"

"The cloak wasn't looking yet." Quentin gathered their tray with hands that wanted to shake and refused to. "But he'll start."

* * *

The market wound down the way it always did, lamp by lamp, the crowd thinning from a crowd to a scattering to a few last figures hurrying home through streets that got colder and meaner the darker they grew. Quentin packed the unsold charms into a canvas bag, wrapped the coin dish in cloth, and climbed the hill toward the boarding house on Tanner's Lane with their collar up and their breath smoking.

They took the long way. They always took the long way after a night like this, past the shuttered chandlery and under the aqueduct's stone legs, because the long way passed the one place in Caerlum where Quentin could stand still and not feel watched: a scrap of old wall where Marisol's name was scratched into the mortar, so faint now that Quentin had to touch it to read it.

*M. SOLAINE. HERBS AND REMEDIES.*

Twelve years gone, and the shop was a cobbler's now, and the woman who had kept Quentin alive was a rumor even among the people who'd known her, and Quentin stood in the cold touching letters scratched in plaster and thought: *I am so tired.*

Not tired the way sleep fixes. Tired the way a held breath fixes nothing. Twelve years of counting pennies. Twelve years of stitching flaws into their own work on purpose, crippling their own gift by hand, hating the gift and depending on it in the same motion. Twelve years of watching neighbors buy charms and denounce magic in the same afternoon, of loving people who would report them by morning if the price or the fear was right — and not blaming them, that was the worst part, not even blaming them, because fear was reasonable, fear was sensible, fear was what kept everyone alive including Quentin.

Every friendship was a performance. Every kindness was a debt that could be called in as testimony. Every single day, Quentin woke up and put on a person the way other people put on coats, and the person fit worse every year, and there was no one — no one in the whole gray sprawl of Caerlum — to whom they could say *this is what I am* and keep living afterward.

Marisol had promised, at the end, that there was a reason. That she'd left something. That when Quentin was ready —

But Quentin had never been ready. The box she'd left sat where it had sat for twelve years, under the floorboard of Quentin's room, and Quentin had never opened it, because opening it meant the hiding was for something, and if the hiding was for something then it wasn't just cowardice, and Quentin had built twelve years of architecture on the foundation that it was.

They climbed the stairs to their room. They lit the candle. They ate bread and hard cheese standing up, the meal of a person who doesn't want to give the evening the dignity of sitting down.

Below the floorboard, under a layer of dust and a decade of deliberate not-thinking, the box waited.

Quinn blew out the candle and lay in the dark and listened to the city breathe, and told themselves, as they did every night, that tomorrow would be the same as today, and today had been survivable, and survivable was enough.

They almost believed it.

Then, far off across the sleeping rooftops, a bell began to ring — not the fire bell, not the tide bell, but the deep bronze voice of the cathedral, rung out of hours, rung twice, paused, rung twice again. A pattern Quentin had never heard in twenty-two years of Caerlum nights.

In the room next door, the landlady's floorboards creaked. In the street below, shutters opened. Somewhere a dog started up, and then another, and the sound spread through the dark city like a crack spreading through ice.

Quentin sat up in the cold and listened, and understood, with the animal certainty that had kept them alive this long, that tomorrow was not going to be the same as today.

Under the floorboard, in its box, something waited — and for the first time in twelve years, Quentin thought it sounded almost like knocking.
