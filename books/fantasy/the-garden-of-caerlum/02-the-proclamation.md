## 2. The Proclamation

The criers came at dawn, and by noon the whole of Caerlum had learned to say *Gift-Hunt* without flinching.

Quentin heard it first from the window of their rented room on Candlemaker's Row — a bell struck three times, then a voice pitched to carry over rooftops, flat and official as a ledger line:

*"By decree of His Majesty Aldric the Third: every soul within the walls of Caerlum shall present themselves at their parish well before month's end for the testing of blood. Those found bearing the Gift shall be registered. Those concealing it shall be judged as traitors. So says the Crown."*

*Registered*, Quentin thought, watching frost melt off the glass under their breath. As if the Gift were a dog to be licensed. As if a fever-charm were a cart requiring a tax stamp.

They had known this was coming. Everyone who lived the way Quentin lived had felt it gathering all winter, like weather in a bad knee. The trials in the capital. The new office of hunters with grey cloaks and silver pins. The way old Mother Hesk stopped selling birth-charms openly and started wrapping them inside loaves of bread. Fear travels faster than any crier.

But knowing a thing is coming is not the same as standing still while it arrives.

* * *

The parish well on Candlemaker's Row was a squat stone thing older than the city charter, ringed by a low wall where generations of neighbors had leaned to gossip while buckets clattered down into the dark. By midmorning a queue had formed along that wall, and above it hung a new banner: white cloth, a red drop embroidered at its center, so clean and simple it looked almost gentle. That was the trick of it, Quentin thought. Cruelty works best when it's tailored well.

They joined the queue because not joining the queue was itself an answer, and answers got people visited.

The line moved slowly. At the head of it stood two figures in grey — one holding a leather case of glass vials, the other holding a book, and between them a small brass instrument Quentin didn't recognize, something like a lancet crossed with a tuning fork. A woman ahead of them gave her name, held out her hand, winced at the prick, watched her blood bead into glass. The man with the fork touched it to the vial. It stayed silent. He made a mark in his book. Next.

It took perhaps ten seconds per person. Ten seconds to end a life, or spare it. Quentin counted the seconds the way they'd once counted coins, back when coins were scarce.

"Name?"

"Quentin Joubert."

"Trade?"

"Lamplighter's assistant." It wasn't even a lie, exactly. They did assist the lamplighter, on the nights when luck-knot sales were thin. A life built out of true lies, each one load-bearing.

The needle bit the pad of their finger. Quentin kept their breathing even and thought about nothing at all — a discipline Marisol had drilled into them until it was bone. *Your heart is a door, little wren. Learn to close it.* Their blood welled up ordinary and red. The fork touched the vial.

Silence.

The hunter glanced up — just a flicker, just long enough to look at Quentin's face — and made his mark. "Next."

Quentin walked away with their pulse hammering in their ears and their gift pressed down flat beneath their sternum like a bird held too long in two hands. It hadn't stirred. It never stirred anymore; twelve years of practice had taught it to sleep through anything. But sleeping things could be woken, and somewhere behind them in that leather case were vials that would be carried to some cold room and read by someone whose whole trade was waking sleeping things.

Ten seconds. They'd been spared in ten seconds, and it had cost them nothing but a drop of blood and a year shaved off their life in quiet terror.

Then why did it feel like losing?

* * *

The market that evening was wrong.

Quentin saw it from the far end of Tanner's Lane before they understood what they were seeing: the stalls open, the lanterns lit, the usual crowd — but the crowd stood in knots instead of drifting, and the knots were talking, and the talk had a shape to it. A hard shape. An arrow shape.

"—heard they took a woman in Saltmarket yesterday. Right off her own doorstep—"

"—my cousin says the testing isn't even real, that they keep the blood, that's the point of it, they're building a list—"

"—good, I say. Good. My brother's roof fell in and no charm caught it, did it? All these years paying witch-women for *luck*, and where was our luck? Let them test everyone. Let them find them all."

That last voice belonged to Gaspard Fenn, the cooper, a broad cheerful man who had bought a luck-knot from Quentin not four months ago — had haggled over it, cheerfully, and paid with a smile and a story about his daughter's wedding. Now he stood with his arms folded and his chin up, denouncing magic to a nodding circle of neighbors, and Quentin stood six feet away in the shadow of a shuttered awning with the memory of his coin warm in a pocket that suddenly felt like evidence.

He doesn't know, Quentin told themselves. He never knew. He bought a knot from a girl with quick hands and assumed the luck came from the knotting.

But that wasn't quite it either, and Quentin knew it. Because Gaspard hadn't said *the charms don't work*. He'd said *let them find them all*. He knew someone had sold him that knot. He'd simply never cared who, until caring became dangerous — and now the safest move, the smartest move, the move every sensible person in Caerlum was making, was to want the hunt harder than anyone else. To be loudest. Loudness was armor now. You denounced magic the way you once bought charms against it: not because you believed, but because everyone else was watching to see whether you did.

Odile found Quentin at the edge of the square, where the light didn't reach.

The old charm-seller looked smaller than she had a week ago. Her stall was half-stripped already — the love-tokens gone, the birthing charms gone, only dried herbs and honest teas left, the inventory of an innocent. She'd wrapped her knuckles in wool against the cold, and her hands shook a little, though her voice didn't.

"You went to the well today," she said. Not a question. Everyone had gone to the well today.

"I went to the well today."

"And?"

"Red blood, silent fork, next." Quentin tried a smile. "You?"

"Tomorrow morning." She looked out at the crowd, at Gaspard Fenn holding court. "I've sold charms on this corner for thirty-one years, wren. Half these people have my knots in their pockets right now. Their mothers' knots. Their children's fever-strings." Her jaw worked. "And there he stands, telling anyone who'll listen that we ought to be swept out like rats."

"He's frightened."

"He's *convenient*," Odile said. "There's a difference, and the difference is that frightened men can be forgiven, and convenient ones will hold the door open for the hunters and call it civic duty." She turned and gripped Quentin's wrist, sudden and hard, her wool-wrapped fingers surprisingly strong. "Listen to me. When they come to my stall tomorrow — and they may, the testing isn't the only testing going on, there are informers, there are always informers — you don't know me. Do you hear? You're a lamplighter's boy who buys tea. Nothing else."

"Odile—"

"*Nothing else.*" Her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely clear. "I'm sixty-three years old, child. I've had my life. You haven't had yours yet, and I'll be damned if you spend it paying for mine."

She squeezed once more and let go, and shuffled back toward her half-empty stall, and Quentin stood in the dark of Tanner's Lane understanding, with terrible clarity, that this was how it happened. Not with fire and masks and mobs. With queues at wells. With neighbors choosing loudness. With old women pre-forgiving the people who would betray them, so the betrayal would sit easier on everyone's conscience after.

The city wasn't turning on its strangers. It was turning on its own history — thirty-one years of knots and fevers and births, all of it suddenly rewritten as crime — and it was doing it politely, in order, ten seconds at a time.

* * *

Three nights later, they took Odile anyway.

Not at the stall. At her rooms above the chandler's, in the blue hour before dawn, when the streets belonged to the Watch and no witnesses had any excuse to be awake. Quentin learned this the way the whole Row learned it: by sound. Boots on the stair. A knock that wasn't a neighbor's knock. Then voices, low and procedural, and then Odile's voice — steady, almost bored, saying words Quentin couldn't make out but whose tone needed no translation. *I know my rights. I know how this goes.*

They took her out the front and down the street in a closed cart with no markings, and by the time the Row woke up, her shutters were closed and her stall was just a stall and the chandler swore he'd hardly known her, hardly at all, barely a tenant, kept to herself.

By evening, Gaspard Fenn was telling anyone who'd listen that he'd always thought there was something off about the woman. Always. Something in the eyes.

Quentin sat in their cold room with the lamp unlit, because lighting it meant seeing the room, and seeing the room meant seeing the box.

It sat under the floorboard by the bed where it had sat for twelve years — Marisol's parting gift, left the last night they ever saw her, wrapped in oilcloth and sealed with wax she'd pressed her thumb into and said, *not until you must, wren. Not one day sooner.* Quentin had obeyed her for twelve years the way they'd done everything she asked: completely, without understanding, because understanding had seemed like a luxury they couldn't afford alongside survival.

*Not until you must.*

Odile's face in the cart-light. Gaspard's voice, *let them find them all*. The white banner over the well, the red drop stitched neat as a signature. Ten seconds per soul. Month's end coming on like winter.

Quentin knelt, worked up the floorboard, and lifted the box into their lap. The wax seal caught the streetlight from below the shutter — Marisol's thumbprint, still perfect, still hers.

Their hands were shaking.

"Not one day sooner," Quentin whispered to the empty room. "I think it's sooner now."

Outside, a crier's bell rang once, distant, counting down days.
