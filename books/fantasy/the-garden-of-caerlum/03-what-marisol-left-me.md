## 3. What Marisol Left Me

The box had been under the floorboard for twelve years, and Quentin had walked over it every day of those twelve years like a man walking over a grave he refused to admit was there.

It lived beneath the bed, which lived in the corner of the room Marisol had rented above the dyer's shop on Tanner's Row, back when Quentin was nine years old and the world still had a shape they could trust. Marisol had pried up that board herself, on an evening Quentin remembered with terrible clarity: the smell of tallow, the rain starting against the shutters, Marisol's hands—knuckled, ink-stained, always faintly smelling of rue—lifting the board and setting the box inside and pressing the wood back down as if she were closing a wound.

"When I'm gone," she'd said, "you open it."

Quentin had been nine. Nine-year-olds did not think about *when*. They thought about supper.

Then she was gone—not dead, not at first, just gone, taken one gray morning by men in the king's livery who didn't knock because knocking was a courtesy owed to people who existed—and Quentin had inherited the room, the floorboard, the box, and a silence so complete it had its own weather. Twelve years. Quentin had paid the rent on time, learned to sell luck-knots without selling magic, learned to be nobody so well that nobody looked twice. And every night, lying on the bed, they had felt the box through the boards like a second heartbeat that wasn't theirs.

Tonight, after the criers, after the proclamation, after old Fenwick spitting into the gutter and calling for blood tests like a man calling for bread—tonight, Quentin pulled up the board.

* * *

The room was dark except for one candle, shuttered behind tin so no light leaked to the street. Quentin knelt on cold planks and lifted the box out with both hands, and it was heavier than memory said, heavier than a box that size had any right to be, as though twelve years of not being opened had packed it with something dense and patient.

It was made of blackthorn wood, iron-banded, unmarked except on the underside, where Quentin's thumb found letters carved shallow and worn: not words but a sigil—a circle crossed by a living branch. Quentin knew that mark. Every hedge-witch's apprentice knew it, or used to, before knowing it became dangerous. It was the sign of the Greenwise, the old country cunning-folk who predated the guilds, the crown, the laws. Marisol had worn it scratched into the heel of her left boot where only another witch would ever kneel close enough to see.

There was no lock. There was a seam, and along the seam a hairline of something green.

Quentin sat back on their heels. The candle hissed. Somewhere below, the dyer's dog turned over in its sleep, and the city went on being the city—carts, arguments, a drunk singing three streets over—all of it oblivious, all of it about to line up at parish wells and bleed into vials for the honor of the throne.

"Twelve years," Quentin told the box. Their voice sounded young in the dark, and angry, and afraid, which was accurate. "You could have burned, you know. If the house caught fire. You could have been found. Anyone could have—" They stopped, because that wasn't true, and they knew it wasn't true, and the knowing settled over them like frost. No one found things Marisol hid. No one saw what Marisol warded. The whole architecture of Quentin's invisible life—the sleight of hand, the copper-charms that worked through misdirection alone, the careful nothing of their public self—had been built by hands that were gone, and built around this, whatever this was, the way a wall is built around a keystone.

Their invisibility had never been theirs. That was the thought that finally made them reach for the lid. It had been designed. They had lived inside someone else's design for twelve years, grateful for it, and never once asked who drew the plans or why.

The seam parted without resistance. Of course it did. It had been waiting for them specifically; Quentin understood that the moment the lid came free and the smell rose out of the box—loam and rain and crushed mint, spring itself, bottled against twelve Caerlum winters.

Inside, on a bed of dried moss gone silver with age, lay a seed.

Not a seed like an apple seed. A seed like a coal, if coals burned green instead of orange—something the size of a plum stone, veined with light, pulsing. Slowly. Rhythmically. Quentin stared at it and their mind, trained by years of market arithmetic, tried to make it small: phosphorescent moss, alchemist's trick, marsh-gas in a jar. But the pulse wasn't flickering. It was beating. And it was beating—

Quentin pressed a hand flat against their own chest.

—in time.

They snatched their hand back as if the seed had bitten it. The light stuttered when they moved, quickened, then steadied again into that slow green rhythm, and Quentin sat very still on the cold floor and watched it beat and felt their own heart obey it, or be obeyed, and could not tell which, and that not-knowing was worse than anything the criers had shouted.

"Don't," they whispered to it. "Don't do that."

It pulsed. Patient. Warm. It did not feel like an object. It felt like attention.

* * *

Marisol had taught Quentin letters with charcoal on slate, sums with beans, and caution with stories. She'd had a story for everything: why you never sold a charm that worked too well, why you never healed a stranger past a bruise, why the Gift-Hunters' white cloaks meant ash and you crossed the street when you saw them coming. Quentin had thought, for years, that the stories were Marisol's fears. Now, holding the box, they began to suspect the stories were instructions, and that they had failed to read them correctly for twelve years.

Because there was one story she'd told differently from all the others. Once. Only once, on a night Quentin had since buried so deep they'd nearly lost it, and it surfaced now whole, the way drowned things surface when you disturb the water.

She'd been mending a net by the fire—it might have been a net, it might have been a shawl, the object didn't matter, what mattered was her voice going flat and quiet in a way it never went, the voice she used for true things:

"There was a war once," she'd said, "before your grandmother's grandmother, between the mages and everyone else. Not a war like wars now. A war where the sky burned for a season. And it ended—not because anyone won. Because something was taken out of the world and hidden, and both sides were too frightened of losing it to keep fighting over it." Her needle had paused. "Remember that, little Q. The most dangerous thing in any war is the thing both sides are afraid of. Not the sword. The reason for the sword."

Quentin had asked what the thing was. Marisol had looked at them for a long moment—too long, a look Quentin had spent years forgetting—and said, "A garden," and laughed, and changed the subject, and Quentin had filed it away as one more of her riddles.

A garden.

Quentin looked at the seed pulsing in its silvered moss, answering their heartbeat, and felt the riddle come apart in their hands.

* * *

They should have burned it. The thought arrived calmly, reasonably, wearing the sensible clothes of survival. The Hunt was coming. Blood-testing at the wells by month's end. Whatever this thing was, it was the reason Marisol had built a fortress of ordinariness around a child; it was the reason men in royal livery had taken her; it was, quite possibly, the reason Quentin had been born at all—or at least the reason Quentin had survived being born, somewhere, to someone, in circumstances Marisol had never once spoken of and Quentin had never dared ask about, because asking would have meant admitting they didn't know who they were.

They didn't know who they were. That was the truth under everything. Nine-year-olds don't ask where they came from when the person who took them in is the whole world, and by the time Quentin was old enough to ask, the world was gone, and the question had hardened into a stone they carried instead of a door they opened.

The seed pulsed. Quentin's heart kept its time.

"No," they said aloud, to the seed, to Marisol, to the nine-year-old still kneeling somewhere inside them. "I'm not burning it. It's hers. It's—" They stopped. It wasn't hers either. Nothing here was hers; she'd been a keeper too. The whole chain of them, keepers all the way down, each one hiding the thing from the thing that wanted it, each one dying or disappearing with the secret intact and the next keeper unprepared.

Anger came then, sudden and scalding, and Quentin let it, because anger was easier than dread. *You could have told me.* The words hammered in their skull with the force of prayer. *You could have said, here is what you are, here is what you carry, here is why we hide—you could have given me one true sentence instead of twenty years of riddles—*

But even as the anger burned, Quentin heard her answer, the way you hear a dead person's answer, in their own voice wearing hers: *And what would you have done with the truth, little Q? At nine? At fourteen? Truth is a load-bearing wall. You don't hand it to someone before they've built the house.*

The candle guttered. Outside, the drunk had stopped singing. Quentin knelt in the dark with a box full of springtime and understood, finally, the shape of the trap they'd been living in: the hiding hadn't protected them from the Crown—the hiding *was* the Crown's protection, designed by Marisol, maintained by Quentin's own fear, and now the design was failing because the world outside had stopped believing in quiet things.

The proclamation had changed the arithmetic. In two weeks, every citizen of Caerlum would bleed into a vial at the parish wells. Quentin's blood would go into a vial. And Quentin did not know—could not know—what a Gift-Hunter's instruments would make of blood that had answered a goddess-seed's rhythm for twenty-odd years.

They closed the box. The green light dimmed obediently, like an eye closing, and Quentin flinched at how obedient it was, how *aware*.

* * *

Sleep didn't come. Around the third bell, Quentin gave up, relit the candle, and did the only useful thing left: they searched the box properly, the way Marisol had taught them to search a market stall for false bottoms—slowly, by touch, trusting the fingers more than the eyes.

The moss bed lifted out. Beneath it, sewn into the lining of the lid, was paper.

One page, folded small, soft as cloth from handling. Marisol's handwriting, cramped and slanting, written fast—which was strange, because Marisol wrote slowly, deliberately, a woman who treated ink like money. This was a letter written in fear, or in haste, or in the particular clarity of someone who knows exactly how much time remains.

Quentin read it by candlelight, twice, lips moving on the second pass.

*Little Q —*

*If you are reading this, I am gone and you are older than I hoped you'd have to be. Forgive me the riddles. Fear makes cowards of teachers, and I was afraid of the wrong things for too long, and then afraid of the right ones.*

*What sleeps in this box is called the Verdant Crown. Do not speak that name aloud. Not to friends. Not to walls. Names are handles, and there are hands everywhere.*

*It was cut from a dying goddess at the end of the Mage War so that neither side could hold her power entire. It has slept in my keeping, and my mother's, and hers, for six generations. It sleeps in you too—I bound it to your blood the hour you were born, because a thing in a box can be stolen, but a thing in a person must be survived. I am sorry. It was the only shelter I could build, and I built it out of you.*

*Three roads lie ahead of you, and I cannot choose for you, though God knows I have wanted to. The Crown can be destroyed, but not while it lives in you; the destruction will take its vessel. It can be wielded, and every soul who has ever wielded it has rotted from the inside out, however good their reasons. Or it can be returned—given back to the land it was cut from—and the land, if it consents, will spread it thin enough that no hand can hold it. There are people north of the city who remember the Returning. Bramwen of Thornhollow, if she yet lives. Trust the old rites and no one in fine clothes.*

*Do not go to the crown. Do not go to the guilds. And if a man named Cassel ever speaks to you kindly, run.*

*I loved you before I met you. That is not a riddle. It is the truest thing I know.*

*M.*

The candle had burned down to a stub. Quentin sat on the cold floor with the letter in their hands and did not cry, though something in their chest cracked along a fault line that had been forming for twelve years, quietly, the way ice cracks on a pond before it gives.

*Bramwen of Thornhollow, if she yet lives.*

Below, the dyer's dog barked once—at nothing, at the wind, at the turning of some vast slow wheel—and Quentin blew out the candle and began, in the dark, to pack.
