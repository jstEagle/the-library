## 4. The Hunter at the Door

The knock came at dawn, and Quentin had been awake for it since midnight.

They heard the boots first—two pairs, heavy, unhurried, the walk of people paid by the hour rather than the result. Then the voice on the stair, low and official, and Madame Corvel's answering murmur, pitched in the key she saved for authority: deference with a spine of resentment. Quentin lay in their narrow bed under the eaves and did arithmetic.

One vial of blood per adult resident. That was the proclamation's wording. Drawn at the parish well or, for the infirm and the bedbound, drawn at home. Results posted within three days. Failure to present oneself: presumption of guilt.

Presumption of guilt. Quentin turned the phrase over the way they'd once turned a copper to check for shaving. It was such clean work, such confident work. The throne had learned that you didn't need to catch a mage if you could make every non-mage prove, over and over, that they were one. Fear did the sifting for you. Fear was cheaper than witch-hunters.

The knock, when it finally reached their door, was almost gentle.

"Quentin Joubert?" A woman's voice. Not Madame Corvel's.

"Yes."

"Gift-Hunter Sera Vane. I'm required to draw your sample. May I enter?"

*Required.* Not *ordered*, not *commanded*. Required, as if the vial were a tax and she a collector who regretted the rate. Quentin sat up, pulled on their shirt, and opened the door before the hesitation could look like anything.

She was younger than Quentin expected. That was the first wrong thing. Everyone in Caerlum knew the name Vane—it traveled through the night market the way news of weather travels through sailors—and the stories had built her into something tall and gray and pitiless, a woman carved from the same stone as the Assize Hall. But the person standing in the corridor with a leather case in one hand was perhaps thirty, dark-haired, plain-dressed, with tired eyes that took in Quentin's face, the room behind them, the single window, the cold stove, and catalogued all of it without appearing to.

"You're the one who does mending," she said. "For Corvel."

"And luck-knots. Before the proclamation." Quentin kept their voice level, bored, slightly put-upon—the voice of someone with nothing to hide and somewhere else to be. "I've stopped. In case that matters."

"It doesn't." She stepped inside uninvited, because the law said she could, and set her case on the table. "Sit where there's light."

Quentin sat. Through the floorboards came the sounds of the house waking: Corvel's cough, the creak of the widow Anselme's chair, a child upstairs—little Tomas, whose fever-charm had worked too well two winters ago, whose mother still didn't know why he'd lived. Every footstep above and below was a life that could be ended by a smear of red on glass.

Vane unwrapped her kit with the economy of long practice. Lancet, glass vials, a stoppered bottle of the testing reagent—a thick fluid the color of old honey that the criers called *truth-wine* because no one would say its real name aloud. Quentin knew the real name. Marisol had taught it to them once, in another life: *argent vitriol*, distilled from grave-silver and something worse, and it burned green when it touched mage-blood. Green like—

Don't think about green. Thinking about green is how you die.

"Hand," Vane said.

Quentin gave it to her. Her fingers were cold and dry and very steady. She wiped the pad of their middle finger with spirits, positioned the lancet, and paused.

"Your hands are scarred," she observed.

"I do needlework."

"Mm." She glanced up. For a moment her eyes were not tired at all; they were quick and sharp and reading something, though what Quentin couldn't say. "Needlework doesn't usually scar the left thumb this way. That's a burn from silver wire. Charm-work wire."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below, Corvel coughed again, and the sound seemed to come from a great distance.

"Or candlework," Quentin said. "I mend by candlelight. Wax burns."

"Wax blisters. Silver scars." But she said it without heat, almost absently, and then she pressed the lancet in before Quentin could answer, a bright sting, and blood welled dark and ordinary into the groove of the vial. Three drops. Four. She stoppered it, pressed a cloth to Quentin's finger, and held the vial up to the window light.

And hesitated.

It was a small thing. A half-second, maybe less—the kind of pause a person makes when they've miscounted change, when they've forgotten a name. But Quentin had spent twenty-three years learning to read pauses the way other people read letters, and this one had a shape. Vane looked at the vial, and something moved behind her face—not doubt exactly, not mercy exactly, but the particular stillness of a person standing at a door they have opened before and not enjoyed what was behind it.

Then it closed. She set the vial in a padded rack with six others—Corvel's, Anselme's, the boy Tomas's, three more from the upper floors—and began packing her kit.

"You'll want to know," she said, not looking up, "that the results go to the parish registrar, and the registrar reports to the Assize, and the Assize doesn't read names. It reads colors. Green is all they see." She snapped the case shut. "Most people find that comforting. The ones who shouldn't, never do."

"Is that a warning?"

"It's an observation." She stood, shouldered the case, and paused at the door with her hand on the frame. She didn't turn around. "The silver wire. If I were you, I'd take up darning. It's slower, but it doesn't leave marks."

Then she was gone, boots on the stairs, the front door, the street. Quentin sat with the cloth still pressed to their finger and their heart going like a trapped bird, and understood two things at once, with terrible clarity: that Sera Vane had seen something in their blood or their face or their silence, and that she had chosen—chosen, deliberately, with open eyes—to let it pass.

Which meant the most dangerous woman in the parish was either their salvation or their executioner, and Quentin wouldn't know which until it was far too late to matter.

* * *

Sera Vane had falsified forty-one results in nineteen months, and she remembered every one of them.

Not the names—names were dangerous, names were things the Assize could pull out of you later—but the faces. The old chandler with liver-spotted hands who reminded her of her father. The twin girls off Tanner's Row, eleven years old, whose blood had flared the faintest green, barely a breath of color, the kind of trace that came from a grandmother's gift two generations back, harmless, dormant, nothing. The Assize burned people for nothing. Sera had swapped their vials with blanks drawn from her own arm, marked the register *null*, and walked out of their house with her face arranged into bureaucratic blankness while their mother wept gratitude at a lie she'd never know existed.

Forty-one. She kept the count the way other people kept prayers.

Her sister Liss had been number zero.

Liss, who could coax a wilted lettuce upright with a touch. Liss, whose entire criminal gift, whose whole arsenal of arcane terror, amounted to making vegetables perky and finding lost buttons. Liss, who had been reported by a neighbor, tested by a hunter whose name Sera had spent seven years trying to forget, and burned on a Tuesday in Wexley Square while Sera stood in the crowd in her brand-new hunter's uniform and did not scream, because screaming would have gotten her killed too, and dead women couldn't keep count.

That was the arithmetic underneath everything: she had joined the Hunt so that she would be in the room. So that when the lancet came out, her hand would be the hand holding it. Forty-one times, her hand had been the hand, and forty-one people were alive who should have been ash, and if the cost was that she had to stand in burning squares and watch, then that was the price and she paid it monthly, like rent.

But Quentin Joubert was different, and Sera knew it, and the knowing sat in her chest like swallowed ice.

She'd known within ten seconds of entering the room. Not from the wire scar—though the scar was confirmation, the way a signature confirms a letter. From before. From the way the boarder had opened the door: the exact calibrated boredom, the performance of ordinariness worn so smoothly it became its own tell. Innocent people were clumsy with their innocence. They fidgeted, they overspoke, they offered tea. Quentin Joubert had done none of those things. Quentin Joubert had done precisely what a guilty person does when they're very good at surviving: nothing.

And then the blood.

She'd drawn four drops into the vial, and she'd watched it, and here was the thing she would never write in any report: the blood had *shimmered*. Not green—nothing so crude, nothing the reagent would ever catch. Just a depth to it, a darkness with movement under it, like looking down into well water and seeing, for half a heartbeat, that the well went further than wells go. She had tested eleven hundred people in her career. She knew what ordinary blood looked like in morning light. This wasn't ordinary. This was a locked door wearing a coat of paint.

So she had hesitated. One half-second, the length of a miscounted coin. And in that half-second she had made the decision she'd been dreading for nineteen months, the one that forty-one smaller decisions had been rehearsing her for:

She let it go. Unmarked. Unremarked. She even gave the fool advice about darning, which was sentimental and stupid and could get her hanged, and she'd walked down the stairs with her heart hammering and her face perfectly still, and now she was three streets away, sitting on a wall in the gray dawn with her kit on her knees, doing the new arithmetic.

Because here was what frightened her. Not the shimmer—she could have talked herself past the shimmer, filed it under imagination, exhaustion, the trick of low light. What frightened her was that when she'd hesitated, Quentin Joubert had *seen* it. She'd felt it happen, felt the moment the boarder read her pause the way she read other people's. Two readers in one small room, each pretending to be illiterate.

Two liars recognizing each other across a table, and neither able to say so, because saying so was death for both of them, and because trust, in Caerlum, in this winter, was a currency you didn't dare spend even when you found someone who might honor it.

A courier in throne-blue livery clattered past on the frozen cobbles, and Sera watched him go and thought about the week ahead. Seven houses left on her route. Then the registrar. Then the Assize's clerks, who were not clerks at all but Cassel's people—everyone knew it, nobody said it—cross-checking every register against every informant's list, hunting discrepancies the way ferrets hunt rats. Forty-one falsifications was a pattern. Patterns got people noticed. And Lord Cassel, they said, noticed everything.

She should swap the vial today. That was procedure, her own invented procedure: dilute, substitute, mark null, move on. She reached for the rack.

Her fingers stopped on Quentin Joubert's vial and did not move.

Because a plan arrived, fully formed, the way plans do when they've been waiting a long time in the dark: *don't swap it. Report it true. Watch what the Assize does with it. Find out whether the shimmer means what you think it means—whether there's something in this city worth more than forty-one small mercies, something that could buy them all.*

No. That was madness. That was Cassel's own game, using people as instruments.

But Liss's face floated up, unbidden—young, freckled, holding up a lettuce like a trophy—and beneath it, her sister's last words from the scaffold, shouted over the drums because Liss had never once in her life been quiet when it mattered: *"Somebody has to stop being afraid, Seri!"*

Sera Vane sat on the wall in the freezing dawn, holding a vial of impossible blood, and understood that she had just crossed a line she could never uncross. Whatever happened next—whatever the shimmer meant, whatever Quentin Joubert was—she was no longer only a counter of mercies. She was a player.

She stoppered the vial, marked the register *pending retest, vial compromised*, bought herself three days with a forgery, and went to find out what she had just decided to protect.

Behind her, in the east, the sun came up the color of old honey, and somewhere across the sleeping city, in a narrow room under the eaves, Quentin Joubert was staring at a drop of their own blood on a cloth and wondering why it had gleamed, just for a moment, faintly green.
