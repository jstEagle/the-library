## 5. Ash Wednesday Market

The criers had been at it for nine days, and the city had learned to hum the proclamation under its breath like a hymn nobody wanted to sing aloud.

*By order of the Crown, all persons of age shall present themselves at their parish well for the testing of blood. Refusal shall be construed as confession.*

Quentin heard it in their sleep now. They heard it in the scrape of the kettle, in the creak of the third stair, in the wet slap of laundry against stone. Nine days of a sentence repeated until the words wore smooth as river stones and meant nothing at all, which was, Quentin suspected, exactly the point.

Tonight they went down to the market anyway.

That was the thing about fear — it didn't stop your hands from needing work. Rent came due whether or not the throne had decided you were a weapon. And the Ash Wednesday market — so called because it gathered in the ash-yard behind the tanneries, where the fires burned off the week's leavings — was the only place left where a person could sell a luck-knot without a license, a conscience, or a name.

Quentin dressed for it carefully. Plain wool, mended twice. No green anywhere on them; they'd stopped wearing green years ago, some instinct older than understanding. Their satchel held nothing but thread, copper wire, dried feverfew, and forty small knots of braided horsehair that were, officially, decorative.

Unofficially, each one held a whisper of warmth. A nudge of fortune. The smallest possible lie a body could tell against the dark.

*Just enough,* Marisol had taught them, back when teaching had still been allowed to look like teaching. *Enough to help, never enough to be seen. A candle behind a shutter, child. Never a bonfire.*

Marisol had been dead twelve years, and Quentin still checked every knot three times before it left their hands.

* * *

The ash-yard smelled the way it always did — smoke and tallow and the sweet rot of the tannery pits — but tonight it smelled wrong underneath. Tonight there was a new note in it, sharp and animal, and it took Quentin a moment to name it.

Fear-sweat. Forty sellers and two hundred buyers, and every one of them wearing the same cologne.

The crowd was bigger than usual, which made no sense until Quentin understood: people weren't coming to buy. They were coming to look at each other. To memorize faces. The Gift-Hunt had turned the whole city into a parish of informants-in-waiting, and here they all were, doing their reconnaissance by candlelight, pretending to haggle over eels.

"Quentin." Odile's voice cut through the murmur, warm as ever, and Quentin felt the day's first honest breath leave their chest.

Odile ran her stall the way she ran everything — loudly, generously, and four steps ahead of the law. She was sixty if she was a day, built like a rain barrel, with silver rings on every finger and a laugh that could be heard from the aqueduct. Her table was spread with charms that were, mostly, just charms: knotted red string, saint-cards, bottles of colored water sold as courage. But tucked among them, for customers who knew how to ask, were the real ones. Odile had been selling real ones since before the outlawry. She liked to say she'd outlasted three kings and intended to outlast this one too.

"You look terrible," she announced, pressing a cup of mulled wine into Quentin's hands before any greeting could occur. "Sit. Eat. You've got the face of someone who hasn't slept since the criers started."

"I've slept."

"Liar." She said it fondly, the way she said everything, and Quentin loved her for it and hated how much they loved her for it, because love was a liability now, wasn't it? Everyone you cared about was a door into you. Everyone who knew your habits could describe them to a man with a ledger.

"Have you signed up yet?" Odile asked, too casually, sorting rings.

"The testing?" Quentin kept their voice flat. "I'll go when my parish calls my street."

"Mm." She sorted rings. "And if they call it tomorrow?"

"Then I go tomorrow." A pause. "My blood will be boring, Odile."

Something crossed her face then — quick, gone, like a fish under ice. Not doubt. Something worse than doubt. Pity.

"Child," she said quietly, "I've known you since you were fourteen and stealing my plums. I've watched you sell luck-knots that actually worked for six years and never once seen your hands shake. Whatever boring is in your blood—" she tapped Quentin's sternum with one ringed finger, "—it isn't boring. It's discipline. There's a difference, and I'm old enough to know the difference is what gets people hanged."

"Odile—"

"Hush. I'm not asking. I'm telling you what an old woman sees." She resumed her sorting, loud again in an instant. "Now eat your wine and watch the crowd. Half these buyers are counting stalls. I counted eleven men tonight who looked at merchandise the way my grandmother looked at meat she couldn't afford."

Quentin ate their wine and watched the crowd, and saw it too. Men who stood too long at the edges. Men whose eyes moved in rows, like fingers down a ledger.

"We should close early," Quentin said.

"We should have closed early last week," Odile said. "But rent doesn't care about prudence, and neither do I. One more hour. Then we fold, and you come to my rooms, and I feed you something with butter in it, because you're skin and secrets and it worries me."

It was such an ordinary kindness. Quentin would spend a great deal of time afterward remembering that it was ordinary. That the world ended on a night that began with butter.

* * *

The riders came at the turn of the hour, and they did not come quietly.

The first sign was the dogs — the tannery dogs, half-feral, who feared no man, going silent all at once. Then the candles nearest the yard's mouth guttered in a wind that hadn't been blowing. Then the sound: hooves on frozen mud, many of them, unhurried. Men who did not need to hurry.

"Pack," Odile said. Not loudly. Just a change in her voice, from market-warm to something flat and old, a voice from before the outlawry. "Pack now, smile, walk toward the tannery gate like you're leaving because you're tired."

Quentin's hands were already moving. Thread away. Wire away. The knots swept into the satchel in one practiced motion that Marisol had drilled into them across a hundred imagined raids that had never come, until tonight, when they came.

They got ten steps.

Lantern light bloomed at the yard's mouth, a wall of it, and silhouettes inside the light — mounted men in the grey-and-gold of the Hunt, and behind them on foot, the testers, carrying their lacquered boxes and their long needles and their little glass vials that caught the firelight like teeth.

"BY ORDER OF THE CROWN," a sergeant bellowed, in the same voice as the criers, the same worn-smooth words, "THIS MARKET IS CLOSED FOR THE TESTING OF BLOOD. NO ONE LEAVES. FORM LINES BY STALL."

Nobody formed lines. Everybody froze. Two hundred people discovering simultaneously that freezing was also a choice, and the wrong one.

Then a woman near the front screamed, and the freeze broke into motion, and the motion broke into panic, and the panic broke like a wave against the riders' shields.

What happened next happened in pieces, and Quentin would spend months reassembling it, and never get the order quite right.

A cart overturned. Eels spilled across the mud, silver and thrashing, and a man slipped in them and went down hard. Somewhere a candle stall caught — just a little, just its own awning, a bright flag of flame that made everything worse because fire meant stampede. The riders' horses screamed. The sergeant screamed louder. And through the middle of it all, the testers kept walking, calm as priests, opening their boxes, calling out: *Line forms here. Line forms here. Your name, citizen?*

Quentin lost Odile in the crush for exactly as long as it took to be terrified, and found her again because Odile was impossible to lose — she'd planted herself in front of her stall with her arms spread wide, shouting at a young tester, of all things, shouting him down like he was a boy caught in her plum tree.

"You'll not stick that needle in anyone's arm in my yard, lad, I knew your mother's mother, I sold her wedding charm, you look me in the eye and tell me—"

"Madam, step aside—"

"I will not step aside, this is my stall and these are my neighbors and I have buried better men than your sergeant—"

Two hunters took her arms. Gently, at first. That was the horror of it, Quentin thought later — how gently it began. Odile shook them off, which was a mistake, because gentleness has a budget, and hers ran out.

They put her face-down in the mud. Sixty years old, rain-barrel-built, rings scattering like startled fish, and they put her down and kneeled on her back and one of them said, almost apologetically, "Vial," and another said, "She's flagged, check the list — Vane's list has her marked, second column," and Odile was saying words into the mud that Quentin couldn't hear and didn't need to, because no words mattered, because they were tying her wrists.

And Quentin — Quentin did nothing.

That was the first fact. Hold onto it. Quentin stood in the smoke with a satchel full of illegal warmth against their hip and did nothing, because doing something meant dying, and dying meant the seed in them falling into someone else's hands, and Marisol hadn't died — hadn't *been taken*, the word Quentin used, the word that let them sleep — for nothing.

Doing nothing was the discipline. Doing nothing was the whole architecture of their life.

Then the boy went under the cart wheel.

He was small — eight, nine — some seller's son helping haul stock out of the crush, and the panicked horses dragged the cart sideways and the rear wheel found him and rolled across his chest, and Quentin heard it, the specific wrong sound of small ribs failing, and the mother's scream came a half-second later, and the crowd recoiled around the boy like water around a dropped stone, leaving a circle of mud and lantern light and a child not breathing.

And Quentin moved.

Not a decision. That was the second fact, and the more terrible one. There was no decision. There was a gap where the discipline should have been, and through the gap came everything Marisol had ever shutters-and-candles away, everything Quentin had starved and rationed and hidden for twenty-six years, and it came out through their hands like water finding a crack in a dam.

They were kneeling. They didn't remember kneeling. Their palms were on the boy's ruined chest, and the heat came up through them from somewhere below the soles of their feet, from below the ground itself, a vast green pressure like spring deciding to happen all at once, and the boy's ribs moved under their hands — knit, reset, rose — and his back arched and he breathed, one huge tearing gasp, and opened his eyes, and they were brown and astonished and alive.

The whole yard saw.

Two hundred people. Eleven watchers. Fourteen hunters. One sergeant with a list. Every candle in the ash-yard chose that moment to burn steady and tall, though there was no wind, and the frost on the ground around Quentin's knees had melted into a ring of bare wet earth, and tiny green things — moss, impossibly, new moss — were creeping up through the mud toward their hands like plants toward a window.

Silence. Total, ringing silence, except the boy's breathing and the fire eating the awning.

Quentin lifted their hands. Looked at them. Green light still clung to the creases of their palms, fading slowly, like glow after touching a lamp moth.

*No,* they thought, with a clarity that felt like falling. *No, no, no.*

The mother snatched her son and ran — gratitude and terror in equal measure, and Quentin understood both. The crowd drew back, a tide going out, leaving Quentin alone in their circle of melted frost. Faces everywhere, and every face was the same face: the face of people memorizing someone. Some of those faces were afraid *for* Quentin. Most were afraid *of*. It didn't matter. Both kinds would talk.

"Name," said the sergeant. He'd recovered faster than anyone. His hand was on his sword, but his eyes were on his list, already writing. "Your name, citizen."

Quentin's mind, trained for six years in the art of the small lie, offered up nothing. Every alias had evaporated. Every cover story had burned with the awning. There was no knot for this, no sleight of hand, no shutter big enough for a bonfire.

Odile, face-down in the mud, wrists bound, laughed.

It was a terrible sound and a magnificent one — wet, ragged, defiant, the laugh of a woman who had outlasted three kings and intended, even now, to make this one work for it.

"Run, you great fool!" she shouted at Quentin, and then, to the hunters, with her face in the dirt: "Well? Are you going to sit there all night? *Get them!*"

Quentin ran.

Later — much later, in a boathouse under the aqueduct, shaking so hard they couldn't hold a cup — Quentin would take the memory apart and understand what they were running from, and it wasn't the swords. It was the moment after the boy breathed, the half-second when the green light drained out of their hands and they looked up and saw Odile watching them from the mud, and she wasn't surprised.

She'd known. All those years of plums and wine and butter, she had known, and she had spent them anyway, knowing what it cost, standing in front of a stall with her arms spread wide because that was what you did for people.

They had taken her for it. They were taking her right now, loading her into a wagon between two other flagged names, and Quentin was running the other way through the tannery gate with their heart trying to climb out of their throat.

Behind them, the ash-yard burned orange against the winter sky, and the criers' words finally finished their work:

*Refusal shall be construed as confession.*

There was no refusing anything anymore. The shutters were gone. The candle was a bonfire, and the whole city had seen it, and by morning every hunter in Caerlum would know that the vessel they were looking for had green light in its hands and had last been seen running east along the tannery wall.

Quentin ran east along the tannery wall.

At the corner, where the wall met the frozen channel, a figure detached itself from the shadow of the arch — tall, cloaked in hunter's grey, and Quentin's legs nearly gave out entirely before the figure spoke, low and urgent, in a voice they recognized from the boarding house, from a vial held up to lamplight and a hesitation that had lasted one heartbeat too long to be honest.

"Don't run from me," Sera Vane said. "Every other hunter in this city is about to be looking for you. I'm the only one looking *with* you. Choose fast."
