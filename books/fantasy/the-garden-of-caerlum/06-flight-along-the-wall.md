## 6. Flight Along the Wall

The knock came at the third hour past midnight, and Quentin knew before opening it that it was her.

Not because of any gift. Because of the silence. The boarding house on Vetch Lane had a rhythm at night—Marta's snoring through the floor, the pipes ticking as they froze, the drunk in 3C singing half a hymn to himself until he forgot the words. Tonight all of it was muffled under something heavier: the particular quiet of a person standing very still outside your door.

Quentin pressed an eye to the crack and saw the grey hood first, then the badge beneath it—the iron circle of the Gift-Hunt, dulled with age, worn like a scar rather than an ornament.

Sera Vane.

"You're awake," she said. Not a question. Her voice was low, roughened, the voice of someone who had learned to speak without carrying.

"I couldn't sleep." It wasn't even a lie. "Is this about my blood vial? I gave it Tuesday. At the well, like the proclamation said."

"It's about what was in it."

The hallway was cold enough that Quentin could see their own breath slide out between them. Downstairs, Marta coughed in her sleep. Quentin thought of Odile in a wagon cage somewhere, of the trampled boy's blood on the cobbles, of the green light that had poured out of their hands in front of forty witnesses and could not be unsaid.

"They'll come for me," Quentin said quietly. "The others. When they read the results."

"Your results are already read." Sera glanced down the stairwell, then back. "I wrote them myself. *No trace of the Gift detected.* Clean as spring water."

"Why?"

"Because I'm going to ask you a question, and depending on how you answer it, you're either going to walk out of this city tonight or you're going to be in a cell by dawn. And I'd rather not write another report." Her eyes were pale, almost colorless in the lamplight, and they did not soften when she spoke. "The seed. Where is it?"

Quentin's hand moved before they could stop it—toward the breast pocket where nothing had been since they'd buried the box deeper, three nights ago, after the market, after the boy.

Sera saw. Of course she saw. She'd been trained to see.

"That's answer enough for now," she said. "Get your coat. Bring nothing that rattles."

* * *

They went up instead of out.

Quentin had expected alleys, drains, the smugglers' doors every child in Caerlum grew up knowing. Instead Sera led them to the roof of the tannery next door, across a gap Quentin's stomach refused at first, and from there into the spine of the city itself—the old wall, the one from before the Mage War, the one the histories said no army had ever taken because no army had ever been stupid enough to try climbing it.

"The aqueduct runs along the top for two miles," Sera said, crouched behind a chimney stack, watching the street below where torchlight moved in pairs. Hunter patrols. Four of them, working outward from Ash Market in a pattern Quentin recognized with a sick lurch—it was the pattern Odile used to lay out her stall. A closing net. "They think you'll run for the river gates. Everyone runs for the river gates."

"And if we fall off the wall?"

"Then you won't have to worry about the Hunt." She said it flatly, without humor, and started climbing.

The frozen aqueduct was a black ribbon against the stars, its arches marching away northward into the dark, ice sheathing the stone rails in long glassy fangs. The wind up there came off the fens with nothing to stop it, and it went through Quentin's coat like the coat was a rumor. They walked single file along the service ledge, boots finding the frost-slick stones by feel, the city dropping away on either side—rooftops below to the south, black fields to the north, and between them the drop, sixty feet of empty air that the wind kept reminding them about.

Halfway across the first span, Sera stopped and held up a fist.

Below, at the foot of an arch, torches. A patrol had stopped. Voices carried up thin and distorted, and then—one voice cutting clean through the wind, a voice used to being obeyed.

"—the vial was falsified. I don't care who signed it. Pull the ledger. Every hunter who tested on Vetch Lane reports to me personally by dawn."

Lord Cassel's spymaster's voice, Quentin thought, though they'd never heard it before. Some voices you simply know.

Sera didn't move for a long moment. Then she turned, and in the starlight her face had gone very still, the way water goes still before it freezes.

"He'll have my signature by morning," she said. "Which means by morning I'm either a traitor or dead, and honestly the order of those doesn't matter much." She looked at Quentin—really looked, the assessment running over them like a hand checking a horse's legs. "So here's the new arrangement. You tell me what you're carrying. All of it. And I get you out of Caerlum alive. That's the trade. Take it or stay and negotiate with him."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I finish my rounds, file my report, and let them find you. I've done it before." A pause. The wind filled it. "I've never liked doing it."

Quentin leaned against the frozen rail and felt, faintly, through layers of wool and leather and skin, the seed answer. Not words. Just warmth—a pulse of green attention, like a cat waking in a dark room. Marisol's voice surfaced unbidden, from twelve years and a lifetime ago: *Trust is a coin you spend once, little sprout. Spend it slow.*

But Marisol was ash in Cassel's custody, or worse. And the net below was tightening, and the only door out of the city ran along the top of a frozen river in the sky, guarded by a woman whose badge meant death and whose hands had just written a lie to save a stranger.

"What do you know about the Verdant Crown?" Quentin asked.

Something flickered across Sera's face—not surprise exactly. Confirmation.

"I know it ended the Mage War," she said. "I know the throne has spent twenty years and more gold than the navy gets trying to find it. And I know that whatever's in your pocket answered you just now, because I watched your shadow lean toward you." Her jaw tightened. "My sister had a gift. Small things. She could make cut flowers last a week. They burned her for it when I was fifteen. So understand me—I'm not helping you because I believe in destiny. I'm helping you because I am *tired*, and because whatever that thing is, I'd rather it belong to a charm-seller who hesitates than to the man whose voice you just heard."

Quentin took out the seed.

It sat in their palm like a held breath—a knot of green light no bigger than a plum, pulsing softly, throwing living color onto both their faces. Up close, Sera's composure cracked, just at the edges. Her breath caught. For one unguarded second she looked fifteen years old again, standing in a parish square watching smoke.

"It's warm," she whispered.

"It talks. Sort of. Not in words." Quentin closed their fingers around it, and the light dimmed obediently, and Sera flinched as though doused with cold water. "That's everything I know too. Marisol bound it into me at birth. I found out four days ago. I've been hiding magic my whole life and apparently the hiding was the plan."

"The plan of a dead woman who got herself caught," Sera said, but there was no cruelty in it—just the blunt arithmetic of grief. She turned north. "Come on. The aqueduct ends at the old cistern house past the wall's edge. There's a horse. After that it's fen country, and the Hunt doesn't ride the fens in winter."

They walked. The wind screamed. Below, torches multiplied, spreading along the streets like spilled fire, and somewhere behind them a bell began to ring—not the alarm bell, but the deep slow tolling of the cathedral, marking some hour that no longer mattered.

At the third arch, the ice gave way.

It happened fast—Sera went first, testing each stone, and the rail she trusted sheathed in ice sheared off whole and dropped silently into the dark, taking her balance with it. Quentin lunged, caught her wrist, and for one hanging moment they were both of them held by nothing but grip and gravity's patience—and the seed *flared*, green light flooding the ledge, and the frost beneath their feet bloomed suddenly thick with new ice, gripping their boots like a hand.

Sera stared at the ice. Then at Quentin. Then at the light still leaking between their fingers.

"You could have dropped me," she said. "One less witness."

"You'd have pulled me down with you. I did the arithmetic."

For the first time, Sera Vane laughed—a short, rusty sound, like a gate opening after years shut. She hauled herself back onto the ledge, and they walked on, and neither of them mentioned it again, but something had shifted in the space between them, the way a loan shifts when the first payment comes early.

The cistern house rose out of the dark at the aqueduct's end, a squat ruin crusted with icicles, and tied inside it, breathing steam, a dun mare with a hunter's saddle.

"She's yours?" Quentin asked.

"She was the throne's. Consider it reparations." Sera swung up, then reached down. "There's a village north of the fens. Thornhollow. People there remember things the capital wants forgotten. If anyone can tell us what you're actually carrying, it's them."

"*Us,*" Quentin repeated, taking her hand.

"Don't make it sentimental. You're cargo with good balance." But her grip was firm, and she pulled them up behind the saddle, and the mare plunged out into the white dark of the fen road.

Behind them, Caerlum burned with torchlight, hunting. Ahead, the fens stretched black and frozen to the horizon, and somewhere out there, a village that remembered.

And beneath Quentin's ribs, patient as winter, the Crown waited to learn what they would become.
