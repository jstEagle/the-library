## 7. The Spymaster's Garden

The summons came at dawn, folded inside a hunter's pay-pouch, which was how Lord Cassel liked to do things: hidden in the ordinary, delivered by hands that didn't know what they carried.

*Vane. The Glasshouse. Before the frost burns off.*

Sera read it twice, then fed it to her stove and watched the ink curl and blacken. Around her, the boarding house went on with its morning noises—boots on stairs, a kettle, someone coughing through the damp cold that had settled over Caerlum like a second roof. She dressed in her hunter's grey, checked that her test-kit was stocked, its vials clean, its lancets sharp enough to draw blood from a liar without leaving a mark.

She had falsified eleven results in nine days. She knew the number because she made herself count them each night, lying awake in the dark, tallying them like sins or coins. Eleven people walking free who should have been in a wagon bound for the Citadel. Eleven small treasons. And one of them still burning in her pocket like a coal: a blood vial from a nervous apprentice named Quentin Joubert, whose pulse had jumped so hard against her fingers that she'd felt it through two layers of wool.

*You could have been anyone,* she'd wanted to say. *You could have been my sister.*

Instead she had written *null* on the slip and moved on to the next door, and the next, and the next, because that was the job now. That was what Caerlum had become: a city of doors, and behind every one of them, someone deciding whether to be brave or safe.

The Glasshouse stood at the edge of the Citadel grounds, past the barracks and the kennels, a long hall of iron and glass built by some dead king who had wanted oranges in winter. Cassel had claimed it twenty years ago and filled it not with fruit but with plants that had no business surviving this far north—black orchids, pale ferns, a trellis of roses that bloomed out of season and smelled, if you got close enough, faintly of copper.

Everyone in the Hunt knew you didn't enter the Glasshouse unless summoned. Everyone knew you left it changed.

A gardener let her in without a word—an old man with soil-blackened nails and the flat eyes of someone paid not to see—and the warmth closed over her like water. Sera hadn't realized how cold she'd been until she wasn't. That was the trick of the place, she thought. It made you grateful just for feeling something.

Cassel was at the far end, among the roses, wearing the brown of a country gentleman rather than the black of his office. He looked up when her boots crunched the gravel path, and smiled the way he always did: as if she were an expected guest and not a summons.

"Sera Vane." He said her name like he was tasting it. "You've been busy."

"My lord."

"Eleven parishes in nine days. Four hundred and six tests administered." He turned back to the roses, pruning shears in hand, and snipped a dead bloom with a soft click. "And eleven null results flagged by my clerks as... statistically improbable. Your district has the cleanest blood in Caerlum, Vane. Either your neighbors are remarkably dull, or you are remarkably kind."

The words dropped into her stomach like stones into a well. She kept her face still. Twenty years in the Hunt taught you that. "My lord, I administer the tests as prescribed. If the clerks doubt my work, they're welcome to retest my districts themselves."

"They will be." Click. Another bloom fell. "But I didn't summon you here to discipline you. If I wanted that, we'd be in my office, and there would be forms." He gestured with the shears toward a wrought-iron bench beneath the rose trellis. "Sit. Please."

She sat. He remained standing, moving down the row, and she understood that too—the geometry of it. He would loom without looming. He would make her crane her neck slightly upward for the whole conversation, and by the end she'd feel smaller without ever being able to say why.

"Do you know what this is?" He touched one of the out-of-season roses, cupping a bloom the color of old blood.

"A rose, my lord."

"It's a *Vane* rose." He glanced back at her, and his eyes were mild and terrible. "Named for a woman hanged in Godsforsaken Square eleven years ago. Lysbet Vane. Convicted of hedge-craft under the old statutes. Her name was struck from the parish rolls, her house burned, her family barred from holding office or commission in perpetuity. A very thorough erasure." He let the bloom go. "I grew these from cuttings taken the day before the execution. I find it useful to keep living things that other people have tried to kill."

Sera's hands were very still in her lap. Somewhere above them, ice ticked against the glass roof.

"You know the story," Cassel said. It wasn't a question.

"Everyone knows the story."

"Everyone knows the official story. I'm asking if you know the true one." He set the shears down on the bench beside her—a casual gesture, intimate, the way you'd hand a tool to a friend—and walked slowly along the trellis. "Lysbet Vane was reported by a neighbor. Tested positive for gift-blood at her parish well. Denied counsel, denied appeal, processed in eleven days. There was no investigation. There was no mercy. There was a clerk with a quota and a magistrate who wanted to be seen as vigorous." He stopped. "Her younger sister was seventeen years old. She was made to watch."

"I said everyone knows the story, my lord."

"Yes." He turned fully now, and the mildness fell away from his face like a mask slipping, and underneath it was something older and hungrier. "And the younger sister joined the Gift-Hunters within the year. Rose quickly. Developed a reputation for mercy—always the last to condemn, always finding null where others found fire. And every year on the anniversary, she visits a certain grave in the pauper's field beyond the Northgate, and stands there in the rain, and says nothing to anyone." He crouched, suddenly, so they were nearly level, and his voice dropped to almost a whisper. "I have watched you mourn your sister for eleven years, Sera. Do you imagine I don't know why you falsify your results? Do you imagine I mind?"

The garden was very quiet. Sera became aware of her own heartbeat, slow and enormous, filling her ears.

"What do you want," she said.

"Ah. Good. Direct." He straightened, pleased, and retrieved his shears. "What I want is what I have wanted for twenty years, since before you were a hunter, since before your sister died, since before the Proclamation. I want the Verdant Crown."

The name landed in the warm air between them and seemed to change it. Sera had heard the word in old files, in whispered briefings—the Crown, the artifact, the thing that ended the Mage War—but only as history. Only as a story told to children about why magic was forbidden.

"That's a fable," she said.

"It's a filing error." Cassel smiled thinly. "The Crown was real. It ended the war—not by being destroyed, as the histories claim, but by being *hidden*. Bound into a vessel. A living one. And the line of vessels has been thinned and scattered and hunted for two centuries, until—" he held up one finger— "one remains. One seed of green light, carried unknowing in the body of a runaway apprentice currently sleeping in a rented room in Saltcote Parish, three streets from your own boarding house."

The world tilted, very slightly, and righted itself. Sera thought of Quentin Joubert's pulse hammering under her fingertips. Thought of the vial in her kit marked *null*. Thought of eleven nights of counting, and how she'd told herself it was for Lysbet, and understood suddenly that she had never once asked herself what she would do if the kindness cost more than the lie.

"You've known," she said. "How long?"

"Since before the Proclamation. The Proclamation exists *because* I know. Four hundred thousand citizens, tested one by one—it's not a hunt, Vane, it's a sieve. And the sieve works. We have narrowed the vessel to a handful of candidates, and your null results—" he tapped her test-kit, still slung at her hip, gently, like a man touching a lover's shoulder— "have been most instructive. You see, I let you run ahead of the other hunters precisely because you are merciful. Mercy leaves patterns. Kindness is a signature. Every person you've saved has drawn a map for me, and the map ends at a boarding house door in Saltcote."

Sera stood up. She didn't remember deciding to. Her legs simply did it, and Cassel watched her rise with an expression of patient amusement, a man watching weather.

"You'll report this," she said. "You'll send riders tonight."

"No." He said it simply, and the simplicity was worse than any threat. "Because I'm not going to take the Crown. I'm going to be *given* it. There's a difference, and the difference is everything. An artifact seized by force can be contested. An artifact surrendered freely, by its vessel, to the lawful authority of the realm—that is legitimacy. That is a foundation to build on." He began to walk again, slowly, and she fell into step beside him without meaning to, which frightened her more than anything else he'd said. "Which brings me to my offer."

"I haven't agreed to anything."

"You will listen anyway. People always do." He stopped at the end of the trellis, where the roses grew thickest, and plucked a single bloom. He held it out to her, and after a moment she took it, because refusing seemed somehow more dangerous. "When the vessel is delivered to me—alive, willing, unharmed—I will restore Lysbet Vane's name. Not posthumous pardon. *Restoration.* Her name returned to the parish rolls. Her record sealed and burned. Your family's bar lifted. You will be able to speak your sister's name in public, in church, in front of the magistrate who signed her death warrant, and there will be nothing—*nothing*—anyone can do about it. She will have existed. Officially. Forever."

The rose was warm in Sera's hand. Out of season. Alive when everything else was dying. She stared at it and thought of a grave in the pauper's field, unmarked except in her memory, and eleven years of standing in the rain saying nothing to anyone.

"And if I refuse?" she said.

"Then I send other hunters to Saltcote. Less careful ones. The vessel dies resisting arrest, the Crown is recovered from the body, and your eleven null results become eleven counts of treason, and you hang beside your sister's ghost in Godsforsaken Square." He said it without heat, the way a man describes tomorrow's weather. "But I don't think you'll refuse. I think you've been waiting eleven years for someone to offer you justice, and you've simply forgotten what it looks like, so you mistook it for a trap."

"It is a trap."

"Everything is a trap. The question is whether it catches anything worth having." He took her hand—the one holding the rose—and closed her fingers around it, briefly, formally, like sealing a contract. "Three days, Vane. Bring me the vessel, and your sister comes back from the dead. Fail, and you both die twice."

She walked out of the Glasshouse into the grey morning, and the cold hit her like a slap, and she stood on the gravel path breathing hard, the rose crushed forgotten in her fist.

Behind her, through the glass, she could see Cassel among his roses, pruning, patient, a man tending a garden of things other people had tried to kill.

She thought of Quentin Joubert's face when the lancet came out—scared, trying not to show it, failing. She thought of Lysbet at seventeen, laughing, teaching her to whistle through a blade of grass, three weeks before the neighbor's report. She thought of eleven names on eleven slips of paper, all marked *null*, all lies, all maps leading to one door.

Three days.

She started walking toward Saltcote, and hated herself for how fast she went.

* * *

The boarding house door was unlocked. That was the first wrong thing.

Sera stood in the narrow street outside, morning fog curling around her boots, and looked at the gap where the door should have met its frame—ajar by a hand's width, dark behind it. Quentin Joubert was careful. Quentin Joubert hid luck-knots inside coat linings and sold charms through intermediaries and flinched at uniformed men the way rabbits flinch at shadows. People like that locked their doors.

She pushed it open and stepped inside, hand on her knife out of habit more than fear.

The room was stripped. Not ransacked—*stripped*, deliberate, a craftsman's work. The mattress stood on end. Drawers pulled and emptied. Floorboards pried at the corners, two of them missing entirely, showing raw earth beneath. On the table, arranged with neat precision, sat a single object: a sprig of dried vervain, tied with green thread.

Hedge-witch's warning. *Run. They know.*

But no note. No sign of struggle. No Quentin.

Sera stood in the ruined room and did the arithmetic she'd spent eleven years avoiding. If Quentin had run, then the vessel was loose in a city crawling with hunters, bounty men, and Cassel's informants—and every hour of delay brought riders closer, and every rider meant blood, and blood meant the Crown passing to Cassel over a corpse instead of into her hands alive.

If Quentin had been taken—

She couldn't finish the thought. She found she was gripping the vervain hard enough to bite into her palm.

There was one place a hunted hedge-witch's apprentice might run. One name Marisol's old circle had whispered, back when Sera had been young enough to listen at doors: a village north of the wall, past the frozen aqueduct, where the old rites had never quite died. Thornhollow. If Quentin had any sense at all, they'd already be on the road.

Which meant Sera had a choice, and the choice had a shape she recognized: report the empty room and let the machine of the Hunt grind northward, or ride out alone, fast, and reach the vessel first.

Reach Quentin first. Reach the person whose pulse had hammered under her fingers like a trapped bird, and put a hand on their shoulder, and say: *come with me, I can keep you safe*—and mean it, and also mean the other thing, the thing she'd sworn in a garden full of impossible roses.

Both things true at once. Both things true in her mouth at the same time. That was what Cassel had really bought, she understood now. Not her betrayal. Her capacity to hold two truths and serve whichever one arrived first.

She looked down at the vervain in her hand, at the green thread gone brown with age, and thought: *Marisol warned you. Someone warned you. Someone loved you enough to try.*

Outside, the fog was lifting. Somewhere north, past the wall, past the aqueduct, a figure was moving through the frozen morning with a seed of a dead goddess buried in their chest, running from a hunt Sera had helped build.

She went to find her horse.
