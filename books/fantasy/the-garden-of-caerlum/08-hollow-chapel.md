## 8. Hollow Chapel

The chapel found them before they found it.

They had been walking since the aqueduct, following a deer track that Sera swore existed and Quentin half believed her about, through pine country where the snow lay blue in the shadows and squeaked underfoot like new leather. By the time the light went the color of old pewter, Quentin's feet had stopped reporting pain and started reporting something worse: nothing at all. They were thinking about that, about how you could lose parts of yourself by degrees and only notice when they were already gone, when Sera stopped so abruptly that Quentin walked into her back.

"There," she said.

It stood in a clearing like a thing that had knelt down to pray and never gotten up. A chapel, or what remained of one — roof gone to lichen-eaten beams, walls of grey fieldstone shouldering out of drifts, one arched window still holding its tracery like a rib cage around a heart of empty air. Ivy had taken the south wall entirely. The door was a black rectangle with no door in it.

"Consecrated ground," Sera said. "Was. Hunters don't bother with ruins unless there's a bounty on them, and there's no bounty on this." She said it too smoothly, the way she said everything she wanted Quentin to believe without argument.

"You've been here before."

Sera's jaw worked. "Once. Years ago. It doesn't matter." She ducked through the doorway, and after a moment Quentin followed her into the dark.

Inside smelled of stone and snow and something older underneath, something green, like the inside of a fallen log. Quentin's breath made ghosts. Their pulse did something strange — a quickening, then a slow heavy roll, like a tide answering a moon they couldn't see. The seed. It had been quiet since the aqueduct, curled behind their sternum like a sleeping animal, but here it stirred, turned over, pressed warm against the inside of their ribs.

*Here,* it seemed to say. *I know this place.*

"Marisol trained here." Quentin didn't know how they knew it until they said it, and then they did know it, the way you know your own name. "She never said where. She just used to go away for weeks, twice a year, spring and autumn. She'd come back smelling of woodsmoke and juniper and she'd never say."

"Then she kept more than one secret from you." Sera was moving along the wall, running gloved fingers over the stones, checking sightlines to the door with a hunter's habits even now, even disgraced, even freezing. "Get a fire going. Small. There's deadfall in the corner there. We'll risk an hour."

Quentin gathered wood with hands they couldn't feel and built a fire the way Marisol had taught them — not stacked, *woven*, so it breathed instead of burning fast. The first flame caught green at its edges for half a heartbeat before settling into honest orange. Sera saw it. She said nothing, which was somehow louder than saying something.

While the fire took, Quentin walked the chapel.

The nave was choked with rubble and bird droppings gone to chalk. An altar stone lay face-down near the crossing, and someone long ago had pried up its silver inlay, leaving scars. But it was the pillar beside the north wall that stopped them — because the pillar was covered in writing.

Carved, not painted. Rows and rows of it, small letters cut deep enough to survive the weather, spiraling up the stone from knee-height to well above Quentin's head. Some of it was Latin, some of it was a script Quentin didn't recognize at all, angular and rooted-looking, like words grown rather than written.

And some of it was in Marisol's hand.

Quentin knew that hand the way they knew their own heartbeat. Twelve years of shopping lists and lesson notes and the single letter she'd left behind, tucked into a box Quentin still hadn't opened — twelve years of that slanted, impatient script, the crossbars always slightly too long, as if every word she wrote was reaching for the next one. Here it climbed the pillar in columns, cut with a knife or a chisel, dozens of visits' worth, the letters changing over years the way handwriting does — younger and sharper at the bottom, slower and deeper at the top.

"Sera," Quentin said. Their voice came out wrong.

Sera came over. She looked at the pillar for a long moment, and Quentin watched her decide not to say whatever she was going to say. "I'll keep watch," she said finally, and went back to the doorway, and gave Quentin the pillar and the dark and the firelight and their own shaking hands.

They read.

---

The oldest carving, lowest on the stone, was a warning:

*If you have come here to learn power, sit down first and learn what power costs. This chapel was raised by people who thought they were building a house for a god. They were building a cage. Ask what happened to the god.*

Below that, in the unfamiliar rooted script, someone else had written something Quentin couldn't read — but Marisol had carved a translation beside it, smaller, in her own letters:

*"We took Her apart while She was still willing. Nine blades, nine vessels, nine oaths. Do not let them be joined. Do not let them be worn. What was mercy in dying is tyranny in living."*

Nine vessels. Quentin's mouth went dry. They looked down at their own chest, at the place where the seed sat warm and patient behind the bone.

*Keep reading,* said the warmth. Or maybe that was just their own mind, cracking open. They kept reading.

Marisol's entries began above the translation, dated in a shorthand Quentin slowly learned to parse — not years but seasons, *the year the elms died*, *the winter the river forgot to freeze*, the way country people marked time. The earliest ones were lessons. Wards. The theory of concealment charms, worked out in knife-cut diagrams. Quentin recognized spells they'd been taught at eleven, twelve, thirteen, rendered here in their original form, and understood suddenly that everything Marisol had ever taught them had been invented or adapted *here*, in this room, for this purpose.

Then the entries changed.

*The ninth vessel is not lost. It is hidden. I have found the thread of it and I will follow it to the end.*

*I was wrong to think it an object. It was never a crown you could set on a table. It passed into blood, as She intended — scattered into the lineages of those who carried Her out of the war. Most lines have thinned to nothing. One has not.*

*I have found her. A girl in the fen country who lights candles by looking at them. She does not know what she is. She is afraid all the time and thinks it is a flaw in her character. I remember being afraid all the time and thinking it was a flaw in mine.*

Quentin's throat closed. They read it three times. *A girl.* Not them. Someone before them.

*I offered her the choice the old texts demand: burn it out, bear it, or pass it on. She chose to pass it on. She said she did not want to die carrying a war. She said — I write it exactly — "give it to someone who gets to choose."*

There was a gap then, a long one; the next entry was cut much higher, and deeper, and the letters had changed, grown heavier.

*She bore a child in the spring. The vessel passes whole to the firstborn, as it has for nine hundred years, thinning and waiting, thinning and waiting. I was present at the birth. I bound the waking deep — not sealed, sealed would kill the child, but folded small, folded asleep. Let it sleep until the world is ready or until the bearer chooses. Those are the only two doors. I built them both myself.*

*The mother asked me to take the child somewhere the name would never be spoken. I have done it. I have done worse things with better reasons, but not many.*

Quentin sat down on the cold floor. The fire crackled. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the pines with a sound like the sea heard from very far inland.

They had known, in the way you know a thing you refuse to look at directly, that Marisol had chosen them — that a hedge-witch in Caerlum's poorest quarter taking in an orphan apprentice was not luck. But knowing it as fact and reading it cut into stone by her own hand were different animals. Here was the moment, preserved in granite, where Quentin's entire life had been decided by a woman kneeling in this ruin with a chisel, deciding that hiding a child was kinder than unmaking one.

*Not sealed, sealed would kill the child.*

They thought of every lesson that had really been a leash. Every charm taught backwards, every talent scolded flat, every year of *smaller, Quentin, smaller*. They had spent twelve years believing Marisol had shrunk them out of fear — hers, or caution, or some cold arithmetic of survival. And here it was in her own letters: she hadn't been hiding Quentin from the world.

She'd been hiding the world from what slept in Quentin. And hiding Quentin from the fate of everyone who had ever woken it.

Because the last entries said that too.

*Read the histories if you doubt me. Every bearer who woke it woke hungry. Not evil — hungering. It does not corrupt. That word lets people off. It offers. It offers you exactly what you most believe the world denied you, and it pays, oh, it pays in full, and the price is only everything you were before you needed it that badly.*

*The goddess let herself be cut apart because She saw what She was becoming. Dying gods are merciful. Living crowns are not.*

*I am old now. My hands shake. If you are reading this, child, then either I failed to reach you in time or I am dead and you came looking anyway, which would be like you. So hear the last of it:*

*I was not ashamed of you. I was not hiding you because you were a burden. You were the bravest thing I ever did and the most frightening. Every day I chose your small life over the world's large safety and every day I told myself the world could wait a little longer.*

*I hope I was right. I hope you never have to find out.*

*Burn bright and stay small. Forgive me both.*

The last line was cut deepest of all, and below it, nearly invisible unless the firelight raked across at the right angle, a single word in the rooted script. Quentin didn't need the translation. They knew it the way they knew the seed's warmth. It meant *garden*. It meant, in the old tongue, the place where things are planted so they can grow into what they were always going to be.

Quentin put their forehead against the stone. It was freezing and they didn't care. Grief came up out of them sideways, the way water finds a drain — not the grief they'd carried for twelve years, the dull resentful grief of an abandoned apprentice, but something larger and stranger: grief for a woman who had spent her whole life holding a door shut with her body, and grief for the girl in the fen country, and grief for themselves, twelve years old and certain they'd been thrown away.

She wasn't hiding me. She was standing between me and the world, and between the world and me, and she died doing it.

"I'm sorry," Quentin whispered to the pillar. "I thought you left me because I was—" They stopped. Started again. "You didn't leave me at all. You posted me. Like a sentry."

The seed turned over behind their sternum, warm as a held hand.

---

An hour later, or three — time had gone soft — Sera crouched across the fire from them, feeding it sticks one at a time, economical as a woman counting coins.

"You read all of it?" she asked.

"All of it."

"And?"

Quentin looked at their hands. "She gave me a choice. That was the whole point. Everyone else who ever carried this thing inherited it and got devoured by it, because nobody ever asked them. Marisol built the asking into it. Two doors: wake it or destroy it. My choice." They laughed, and it came out cracked. "Except destroying it kills me, apparently, and waking it makes me the thing from the histories. So it's a choice the way drowning is a choice."

Sera was quiet for a while. When she spoke, she didn't look at Quentin; she looked at the fire, and her voice had none of its usual hunter's flatness.

"My sister had a gift. Small. She could make bread rise by humming to it. That was all. Bread." A stick snapped in her fingers. "The parish priest reported her. Standard procedure. I was already in the Hunt by then — I'd told myself the law was the law, and most of the gifted were dangerous, and my oath mattered more than one baker's daughter." She fed the pieces of the stick to the flames. "They hanged her on a market day. I attended. In uniform. Because attendance was mandatory for hunters with family under question, and it proved I was loyal."

"Sera—"

"The point is." She cleared her throat. "The point is I've spent ten years telling myself the law was the law, and tonight I watched you talk to a dead woman's ghost in a rock, and every word of it was truer than anything the Hunt ever taught me. So. Whatever your choice turns out to be — wake it, burn it, feed it to the river — I'm past pretending I'm here for the answers anymore."

Quentin didn't know what to say to that, so they said the truest thing. "She wrote that the Crown offers you what you most believe the world denied you."

"What did it offer you?"

"Belonging." It came out before Quentin could stop it. "It hasn't offered yet. But I already know. It'll offer me a world where I don't have to hide. Where nobody tests anybody's blood at the wells. Where I can just *be* what I am, out loud." They shook their head. "That's how it gets you. It doesn't offer power. It offers an end to the loneliness."

Sera looked at them across the fire for a long moment, and something passed between them that neither of them named — two liars, two orphans of one kind or another, warming their hands over a stolen flame in a dead god's house.

"We move at first light," Sera said. "East. There's a place I've heard of, a village that remembers the old rites. If anywhere knows a third option besides die or devour, it's there."

"Thornhollow?"

Her head came up sharply. "How do you know that name?"

"I don't." Quentin frowned, pressing a hand to their sternum, where the seed had begun to pulse — not warm now but urgent, rhythmic, insistent, like a second heartbeat trying to sync with the first. Like a compass needle swinging home. "It's the seed. It's been pulling east since the aqueduct, and it just — said the name. Thornhollow. Marisol must have—"

The seed flared.

Not gently. It blazed behind Quentin's ribs like a struck match, and every candle-stub of light in the ruined chapel leaned toward them at once, and the frost on the north wall bloomed — actually *bloomed*, ferns of ice unfurling into shapes that were not ice at all but tiny green leaves that opened, shivered, and died in the space of a breath.

Outside, far off but not far enough, a dog began to bark. Then another. Then horns — two notes, repeated, the hunting call Quentin had heard once before, on the night the market burned.

Sera was on her feet with her blade drawn before the echo faded. "Riders," she breathed. "On the road. More than one party." She looked at Quentin, at the fading green leaves on the frozen wall, and her face in the firelight was grim as the gravestones in the yard outside.

"They're not following us," Quentin said, and hated how steady their voice came out. "They're following *this*. Whatever I just did — they felt it. Cassel felt it."

"Then we run east," Sera said, kicking snow over the fire, "and we pray Thornhollow is real, and we get there before whatever you're carrying decides to announce us again."

Above the altar-less altar, in the empty window's tracery, the last of the false leaves curled brown and fell — and Quentin, snatching up their pack, could have sworn the seed pulsed once more, soft and sorrowful, like a word of warning spoken too late.
