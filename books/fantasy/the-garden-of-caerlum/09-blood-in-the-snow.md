## 9. Blood in the Snow

The pass out of Caerlum was supposed to be empty.

That was what Sera had promised, anyway, three days ago on the frozen aqueduct, her breath steaming as she traced their route on a scrap of hide: north along the old wall road, through the shepherd's cut, then west into the hills where the roads stopped being roads. *No one travels in deep winter,* she'd said. *Only fools and hunters.*

Quentin was beginning to understand that she hadn't meant it as reassurance.

They made camp at dusk in the lee of a fallen watchtower, its stones furred with frost, and Quentin spent an hour doing what they did every night now: sitting with their hands pressed flat against the frozen ground, listening to the thing inside them breathe.

It wasn't breathing, exactly. But there was a rhythm to it, slow and green and patient, like sap rising under bark. The seed Marisol had bound into them before they could walk—the Verdant Crown, though Quentin still couldn't make themselves say the words aloud—had been waking since the chapel. Since they'd read her carved warnings with numb fingers and understood, at last, that the woman who raised them hadn't been hiding a child from the world.

She'd been hiding the world from what slept in the child.

*You're not a person,* Quentin told it silently, the way you'd scold a dog begging at the table. *You're a burden I'm carrying to a hole in the ground where Bramwen says we can bury you.* The warmth under their sternum pulsed once, unbothered, the way a mountain is unbothered by weather.

"Stop talking to it."

Sera sat across the dead fire, sharpening a knife that didn't need sharpening. She'd been doing that a lot—keeping her hands busy so she didn't have to look at Quentin's face for too long. Ever since the chapel, something had shifted between them. Not trust. Something more careful than trust, the way you handle a lamp after you've smelled smoke.

"I wasn't talking," Quentin said.

"You were doing the thing with your jaw." She tested the edge against her thumb. "You do it when you're arguing with it. With the seed."

"With the *Crown*." Saying it felt like swearing in church. "You can say it too, you know. It's not going to jump out of my chest at the word."

"It jumped out of your chest at a trampled boy in a market square." Her voice stayed level, but her knife hand didn't. "Healed him in front of forty people. So forgive me if I treat it like something with ears."

Quentin looked away, out past the broken tower wall, to where the hills went blue-black against a sky the color of dirty pewter. Snow had started again—fine, dry flakes that hissed when they touched the coals. Three days of walking, and every mile north the world got quieter and colder and more honest. Out here there were no criers, no parish wells, no neighbors suddenly remembering how much they'd always hated magic. Just cold, and distance, and Sera's knife whispering over the whetstone.

"You could still turn back," Quentin said. It had become a ritual between them, this offer. Sera always refused it. Quentin always offered anyway, because offering it was the only way Quentin knew how to say *I know what following me might cost you, and I don't want to be another person who decides your life without asking.*

"You could still dig it out of yourself with a spoon," Sera said. "We've both got our rituals."

The knife whispered. The snow hissed. Somewhere far off, a wolf—or something that wanted to sound like one—gave a long, thin cry.

Quentin was almost asleep when Sera went silent.

Not quiet. Silent. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses down on the air. Quentin opened their eyes and found her standing at the gap in the tower wall, head tilted, one hand raised for stillness.

"What?"

"Horses." Barely a breath. "Two. Maybe three. Coming up the cut behind us."

"The cut's a day back."

"Not anymore."

Quentin rolled to their feet, every joint announcing itself, and joined her at the wall. For a long moment they saw nothing but snow and dark. Then, down in the fold of the hills where the old road ran, a lantern swung—and another—and the sound reached them at last: hooves on packed snow, unhurried, deliberate. Men who knew exactly where they were going.

"Bounty men?" Quentin whispered.

"Or Cassel's riders." Sera was already moving, kicking snow over the coals, sweeping their few belongings into her pack. "Doesn't matter. Same difference now. Down the north side—there's a stream bed, it'll break our scent and our tracks. Move."

They moved.

The stream bed was a wound of black ice winding down through the hills, and they went along it fast and low, Sera ahead, Quentin behind, boots cracking through crust into water that soaked them to the ankle and froze there within minutes. The lanterns above kept pace along the ridge, patient as moons. Whoever rode them wasn't chasing. They were herding.

"They're not trying to catch us in the open," Quentin panted. "Why not?"

"Because catching you in the open means fighting me in the open." Sera didn't slow. "Bounty men prefer beds. Doorways. Places where a Gift-Hunter can't use half her training. They'll wait until we camp. Until we sleep."

"So we don't camp."

"We have to camp eventually. You're swaying on your feet, Joubert. I can hear it."

It was true, and they both knew it. Three days of flight, of half-rations, of the seed pulling warmth out of Quentin's body every time they used it or lost their temper or dreamed too hard. Quentin was running on something that wasn't quite stamina anymore, and the seed was happy to lend the difference—at interest.

They found shelter an hour later: a shepherd's hut, stone-walled, half-swallowed by a drift, with a roof that had mostly decided to stay. Sera checked it top to bottom, then built a fire small enough to hide and set Quentin against the wall nearest it.

"Sleep," she said. "I'll take first watch."

"Sera—"

"*Sleep.* If they come, you'll want your hands steady. Whatever it is you're going to do with them."

Quentin closed their eyes. Behind their lids, the seed glowed like a coal raked over, warm and attentive, and for once Quentin didn't argue with it. For once, falling asleep felt less like rest and more like letting something else stand guard.

They came at moonrise.

Quentin woke to Sera's boot in their ribs—not hard, just urgent—and the sound of snow crunching outside the hut, many feet, spreading out in a line. No voices. Professionals.

"Four," Sera breathed. She had her crossbow up, loaded, aimed at the door. "Maybe five. One circled around back."

"How do you—"

"Because I trained most of them." There was something bitter and tired in it. "Vosk runs the northern crews. Ex-Hunter, expelled for taking payment from both sides of a feud. He won't parley. He'll just—"

The door burst inward.

It happened fast after that, and it happened slow, both at once, the way terrible things do. Later, Quentin would be able to replay every piece of it separately, like beads off a snapped string, and never manage to string them together again.

A man filled the doorway—big, bearded, crossbow leveled. Sera's bolt took him high in the shoulder and spun him sideways, and he screamed, and two more came through the gap behind him with drawn steel. From the back of the hut, a crash: the fifth man coming through the collapsed section of roof-wall, snow and slate tumbling, a net in his hands.

And Quentin—

Quentin didn't decide anything. That was the part that would haunt them longest, in all the nights that followed. There was no choice, no moment of *shall I*. There was only the seed, which had been waiting three days and twelve years and perhaps a century, and which felt, through Quentin's own hammering heart, the shape of the threat: *steel, closing, mine.*

The fire died.

Every flame in the hut bent flat, as if a giant had blown across the world, and the cold that rushed in wasn't winter-cold. It was older than winter. Quentin felt the ground beneath the hut wake—all the ground, the whole sleeping body of the land under the snow—and rise up through the floor, through their boots, through their bones, and pour out of their hands.

Green light filled the room.

Not gentle green, not the soft glow of the seed in its box. This was the green of things growing too fast, of ivy swallowing a house in a single night, of roots splitting stone. It came out of Quentin's palms in ropes and tendrils, and where it touched the men, it *took hold*.

The man with the net screamed as brambles erupted from the frozen ground and wrapped him, thorned and black-stemmed, hauling him down. The second man through the door turned to run and the doorway itself grew shut—branches knitting across it like a basket lid, thickening even as he clawed at them. And the third man, the big bearded one with the bolt in his shoulder, had gotten halfway to his feet—

—and the growth took him differently.

Later, Quentin would understand why. He was closest. He was bleeding, and blood was an invitation, and the Crown had been starved for a very long time. The green light went into him the way water goes into cracked earth, and he arched, and for one horrible instant flowers opened across his chest—small white five-petaled things, blooming out of his skin, out of his beard, out of the corners of his screaming mouth—and then the instant ended, and he ended with it, folding down into the brambles like a coat dropped off a hook.

The hut went quiet.

The fire relit itself, politely, as if nothing had happened.

Quentin stood in the middle of the room with their hands still raised, still pouring light, and it took Sera's voice—said twice, said sharply—to make them stop. The green withdrew slowly, reluctantly, sucking back into their palms like tide off mudflats, and Quentin felt it go the way you feel a tooth pulled: hollowed, relieved, already aching for the next pull.

"No," Quentin said. Their voice came out wrong, thin and high. "No, no, no—"

They crossed the room and knelt by the man. The bearded one. Vosk, maybe; it didn't matter; it mattered enormously. His eyes were open. Flowers still clung to him, wilting now, browning, and there was green in the veins of his neck like frost patterns on glass, fading even as Quentin watched. He wasn't breathing. Where the brambles had held him, the thorns had gone down into him, and Quentin understood—with the sick clarity of someone reading their own confession—that the Crown hadn't killed him the way a blade kills. It had grown *through* him. Used him as soil.

"I didn't—" Quentin's hands hovered over the body, not touching. "I didn't choose. Sera, I didn't *choose*, it just—it heard me being afraid and it answered, and it answered *wrong*, it answered like—"

"Like a crown," Sera said quietly.

Quentin looked up at her. She stood near the ruined door, crossbow lowered, watching the body with an expression Quentin couldn't read. Beyond her, the man in the netted brambles moaned faintly—he was alive, mercifully, unconscious—and the other two had fled, crashing away through the snow, their courage gone with their comrades' screams.

"That's what it does," Sera said. "That's what it *is*. You read the chapel walls same as I did. The goddess who was carved apart to end the war—she didn't die of wounds, Joubert. Everything that came near her either worshipped her or fed her, and she couldn't tell the difference anymore. Neither can it. Neither can *you*." She crouched, slowly, at the edge of arm's reach, the way you'd approach a spooked animal. "You were afraid. It defended you. That's all."

"That's *all*?" Quentin heard their own voice crack. "There's a man—a man is *dead*, Sera. Dead because I was scared in my sleep. I've sold fever-charms for six years. Six years I've been careful, I've hidden, I've done everything right, and the first time it comes out of me it—" They couldn't finish. Their hands were shaking so hard they had to press them against their thighs. Under the shaking, worse, was the other thing. The thing they couldn't say.

But Sera saw it anyway. She always saw.

"Say it," she said.

"He liked it." The words fell out of Quentin like teeth. "When it went through me. When it—when he—I felt it *feed*, and some part of me, some part that is supposedly me, felt—" They swallowed. "*Full.*"

The silence stretched. Snow drifted through the broken roof. Somewhere outside, the horses of the fleeing men faded into the dark.

Then Sera did the last thing Quentin expected. She sat down. Fully sat, cross-legged, in the snow-dusted wreckage of the hut, ten feet from a corpse, and looked Quentin dead in the eye.

"When I was nineteen," she said, "my sister Liss was taken by the Hunt. And I testified at her trial. I stood in front of the Magistrate and I said the words they'd taught me, and I watched them put the grey hood on her, and afterward they gave me a hunter's commission and told me I'd saved the city." Her voice didn't waver. That was somehow the worst part. "You want to talk about feeling full? For eight years I felt *clean*. That's what they give you instead of absolution. A job. A purpose. A way to never sit still long enough to feel what you actually did." She nodded at Quentin's trembling hands. "Whatever's in you, it made you kill a man tonight. What's in me made me kill my sister slower. We're both carrying things that answer when we're afraid, Joubert. The only question—the only one—is whether we spend the rest of our lives pretending they aren't ours."

Quentin stared at her. "I killed him."

"Yes."

"And you're staying."

Sera glanced at the body, at the wilted flowers, at the bramble-shrouded man breathing shallowly in the corner. When she looked back, her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady.

"I'm staying," she said, "because if I run from you now, I'm running from the same thing I've been running from since Liss died. And because somewhere north of here there's a village with an old woman who knows how to put this thing in the ground properly, and you're not going to survive the trip without someone watching your back." She stood, and picked up her crossbow, and began, methodically, to reload. "Also because those two who ran are going to report to whoever sent them, and by tomorrow every bounty crew in the north will know exactly what you are. So we walk tonight, and we don't stop, and you don't sleep near me until Bramwen teaches you how to leash that thing. Can you walk?"

Quentin looked down at their hands. The shaking was slowing. Underneath it, the seed pulsed—warm, satisfied, horribly patient—and for the first time since Marisol's box, Quentin felt afraid of it in a way that had nothing to do with witch-hunters or thrones.

It hadn't felt like a weapon going off. It had felt like a hunger, finally fed.

"Yeah," Quentin said. "I can walk."

They buried the man as best they could—stones and snow, a cairn neither of them spoke over—and left the living one trussed where his own crew would find him. Then they went north, out of the hills, into the deepening cold, two figures and a secret moving through a landscape that seemed, to Quentin's newly opened senses, to lean toward them as they passed. Listening. Waiting.

By dawn, the snow had stopped, and the sky over the western hills had cleared to a hard, brilliant blue—and far off, at the edge of seeing, woodsmoke rose from a valley where, Sera said, the trees grew in rows no forester had planted.

"Thornhollow," she said.

Quentin's chest pulsed, once, like a knock at a door.

It knew the way home.
