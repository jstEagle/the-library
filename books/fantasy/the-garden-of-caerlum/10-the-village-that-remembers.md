## 10. The Village That Remembers

They came down out of the hills on the ninth day, when Quentin's boots had worn through to the shape of their own feet and Sera had stopped pretending she wasn't limping.

Thornhollow sat in a fold of land like a coin dropped in a purse—low stone houses with turf roofs, smoke rising thin and straight from chimneys that should have been cold this deep in winter. And between the houses, impossibly, green. Rows of winter cabbages under glass cloches frosted at the edges but thriving within. A herb bed where rosemary stood upright and unbothered by snow. An apple tree, bare-branched and ancient, but with buds along its twigs like a rumor of April.

Quentin stopped on the frozen track and stared.

"That's not possible," they said. "Nothing grows in the Long Winter. Not without—" They didn't finish. Didn't have to. *Not without magic.* The word still tasted like a confession even out here, three days beyond Caerlum's walls.

"Keep walking," Sera said quietly. "And stop looking like you've seen a ghost. Look bored. Bored people don't get questions."

"You're the one who told me hunters don't come this far north."

"Hunters don't. But villages talk, and talk travels faster than horses." She adjusted her cloak so her hunter's badge—the one she'd been carrying folded in her pocket rather than wearing—stayed hidden. "Follow my lead. We're a wool factor's clerk and his wife, heading to family in Vaelmoor."

"I'm your *wife*?"

"You'd rather be my prisoner? Walk."

The village noticed them anyway. Quentin felt it the way they'd once felt eyes in the night market—not hostile, exactly, but weighing. A woman pulling water from the well paused with her bucket half-raised. Two children stacking firewood went silent mid-game. An old man mending a net looked up, looked at Quentin's hands specifically, and nodded once, as if confirming something he'd already guessed.

They were directed—to Quentin's surprise, since Sera had asked for nothing but directions to the road—to a long low house at the village's heart, its door painted a faded green that had once been bright. The woman who answered was old in the way of trees: bent, weathered, and clearly in no hurry to fall over. Her hair was white as the fields, braided with sprigs of something dried and fragrant. Her eyes, when they found Quentin's, did not weigh them. They *recognized* them.

"Well," she said. "You'd better come in before the whole village sees what you're carrying. It's rude to make a guest stand in the cold, and ruder to make a miracle do it."

Sera's hand moved toward her knife. "You know what they are?"

"I know what they *have*, girl, which is different, and I'll thank you not to draw steel in my doorway." The old woman stepped aside. "I'm Bramwen. Come in, both of you, and mind the cat, she bites strangers and I encourage her."

* * *

Inside, the house smelled of bread and yarrow and woodsmoke, and it was warm in a way that had nothing to do with the fire. Quentin felt it against their skin like standing near an open window in spring—a faint, living warmth that came up through the floor stones. Through the kitchen's back window they could see the greenhouse again, glass glowing faintly gold in the gray afternoon, and inside it, shapes of growing things that had no business existing in the Month of Ice.

Bramwen set down bread, hard cheese, and tea made from something piney and sharp, then sat across from them and simply looked at Quentin for a long time.

"Show me," she said finally.

"Show you what?"

"Don't insult us both, child. I've been waiting thirty years for someone to walk up that road with that look on their face. The seed. Marisol's work, if I'm any judge—and I am, because I taught her half of what she knew."

The name landed like a stone in still water. Quentin set down their cup very carefully. "You knew Marisol?"

"I knew a frightened girl who came through here twice, asking questions about the old rites, about what the Crown was and who'd held it. The second time she was pregnant." Bramwen's gaze didn't waver. "Or carrying something that needed a womb to grow in. She never said which, and I never asked. Some questions are kinder unanswered." She leaned forward. "Now. Show me, or tell me to mind my own business, and I'll feed you and send you on your way either way. But I think you've been carrying this alone long enough, and I think it's killing you by inches. Am I wrong?"

Quentin looked at Sera. Sera, to their surprise, gave a small nod—*your call*—and Quentin realized with a jolt that in nine days on the road, this was the first decision anyone had offered them instead of taken from them.

So they opened the collar of their shirt and let the light show.

It came willingly now, eager even—the green glow beneath the skin of their chest, pulsing gently in time with their heartbeat, brighter in the warm room than it had ever dared be in Caerlum. Bramwen watched it without fear, without greed, without the hungry calculation Quentin had learned to dread in every face that saw too much. She watched it the way a farmer watches rain.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, you poor thing. It's *awake*."

"It talks to me," Quentin said, and heard how mad it sounded, and didn't care. "Not words. More like... weather. Moods. Since we crossed into the hills it's been getting louder. Like it knows where it is."

"It does." Bramwen rose, joints cracking, and pulled back the curtain over the back window fully. Beyond the glass, the greenhouse glowed, and beyond *that*, past the village's edge, Quentin could see the orchard, the fields under snow, the dark line of the forest—and beneath all of it, faint as veins under skin, a green shimmer in the ground itself. Faint. Sleeping. But there.

"What is that?" Quentin whispered.

"The land remembers," Bramwen said. "That's what nobody in Caerlum ever understood, and what the mages forgot, and what the throne has spent two hundred years trying to burn out of us. Magic was never meant to live in people. People *borrowed* it, the way you borrow water from a river. Then the war came, and the mages dammed the river for themselves, and the goddess who kept the flow open died stopping them—and the last thing she did was pull all that power out of every grasping hand and bind it into one thing, so no one could hold it again." She turned. "The Verdant Crown. You've heard the name?"

"I've *been* the name," Quentin said, "for about a month now. Since I opened her box."

"Then you know more than most kings." Bramwen sat back down. "Marisol bound it into you at birth because a vessel that doesn't know it's a vessel can't be forced to give it up. Clever. Cruel. Loving, in the way that costs everything." Her voice gentled. "She came here after, you know. After she left you the box. Asked me to help her prepare a rite. I refused—I wasn't ready, and neither was she, and the price was more than either of us could pay alone. She went back to Caerlum to buy you time instead. Twelve years of time." She looked at Quentin with terrible kindness. "It seems the time is spent."

* * *

Sera spoke for the first time in an hour. "What are the options?"

Bramwen raised an eyebrow.

"You said *the price*. There's always a price, and there's always a choice, or people wouldn't need elders—they'd need priests. So lay it out. What can be done with the thing in their chest?"

The old woman studied her for a moment. "You ask like a hunter."

"I *was* a hunter. Now I'm asking for a friend."

Something passed over Bramwen's face—approval, maybe, or its cousin. She held up three fingers, gnarled and steady.

"Three paths. Only ever three, for a power like this.

"The first: destroy it. Break the binding, scatter the power so fine it dissipates into nothing. It can be done—it's what the mages feared most, why they never tried to kill the Crown's bearers outright; destruction risks taking half the country's fertility with it. Done right, done carefully, the child lives. Probably. The land stays as it is—dead, quiet, magic staying outlawed and hunted until the last hedge-charmer is hanged and the world forgets it ever whispered. Safe. Small. A candle blown out so the room stays dark and predictable."

"And the second?" Sera asked, though her voice had gone flat, careful.

"The second is what every hand that ever touched it chose." Bramwen's voice hardened. "Wield it. Wake it fully, take it into yourself, become the vessel in truth. You could heal the blight, end the Winter, feed the starving—no lie, it could be done, and done *well*, maybe, for a while. And then you would find, as every bearer found, that the power doesn't sit in you. You sit in *it*. You'd become the thing the throne fears, and rightly, and the war would start again with better weapons. Every bearer thought they'd be the exception. The goddess herself split it out of existence rather than hold it, and she was a *goddess*."

Her eyes came back to Quentin.

"The third path is the one nobody's walked. Return it. Give the power back to the land—not scattered dead, but given, freely, the way it was before the damming. It would mean opening yourself completely and pouring everything through. It would mean—" she paused, and Quentin watched her choose honesty over comfort, and loved her for it, and hated her for it, "—it would mean dying. Or something very close to dying. The rite requires a willing death, child. That's the toll. The goddess paid it. Marisol meant to pay it. And no one has ever succeeded, because the willing part is harder than it sounds when the moment comes."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the greenhouse glass ticked in the cold. Quentin became aware that they were crying, quietly, without drama, the way you cry when a wound you'd numbed is finally touched.

"There's a fourth option," they said. "I keep doing what I've always done. Hide. Run. Stay small."

"For how long?" Bramwen asked, not unkindly. "Cassel's riders are already asking after travelers on the south roads. The Hunt tests blood at every well from here to the coast. You can stay small for a year, maybe five. And every year, the thing in your chest gets louder, and hungrier to be *used*—you've felt it, haven't you? When the boy was trampled at the market. When the bounty hunter's blade came down. It doesn't want to hide, child. It wants to *grow*. Staying small isn't a path. It's just the first path, taken slowly, with extra suffering."

* * *

They slept that night in a real bed for the first time in nine days, under a roof where the walls hummed faintly with living warmth, and Quentin lay awake listening to the seed pulse against their ribs like a second heart.

*Belonging,* they thought. *This is what it feels like.*

Because that was the thing no one warned you about—that hiding wasn't just exhausting, it was *lonely*, bone-lonely, a loneliness so total you stopped noticing it the way fish don't notice water. And tonight, in this strange warm house in a village that grew apples in winter, an old woman had looked at the impossible thing inside them and said *you poor thing* instead of *what are you worth*. Neighbors had nodded at them in the lane. Children had waved. Nobody had weighed them, priced them, or reached for iron.

And yet.

In the morning, the whole village came—or it seemed like the whole village. Not to gawk. To *sit*. Bramwen convened them in the longhouse, farmers and weavers and the blacksmith and the net-mender, forty-odd faces, and Quentin stood at the front feeling like a specimen until Bramwen said, "Tell them. All of it. They've earned the truth and so have you."

So Quentin told them. The charms, the hiding, the box, the flight, the man in the snow whose life they'd ended without meaning to. They expected horror. They got silence, and then the net-mender—the old man from the lane—said, "My grandmother could coax beans up in a fortnight. They hanged her when I was six." And a young mother said, "My son's fever broke last spring and I never told a soul how fast." Story after story, small confessions, a village quietly admitting it had been keeping faith with forbidden things for generations, alone together, each family thinking itself the only one.

"We can't choose for you," Bramwen said at last, when the stories had run dry. "But hear this: whatever you choose, you won't choose it alone anymore. Destroy it, and we'll help you survive the breaking. Wield it, and we'll spend our last breaths trying to keep you human. Return it—" her voice caught, just slightly, "—and we will hold the circle, and sing you through, and remember your name in the rites as long as Thornhollow stands. That's all an elder can promise. But it's not nothing."

Quentin looked out at those forty faces—weathered, ordinary, kind—and felt the weight of the choice settle onto their shoulders not as a private curse but as a shared burden, which somehow made it heavier and lighter at once.

They didn't answer. Not yet. Bramwen hadn't asked them to.

But that evening, as the villagers drifted home and the snow began again, soft and thick, Sera appeared in the doorway of the room they'd been given, arms crossed, expression unreadable in the lamplight.

"Quentin." Her voice was low. "There's something you need to know before you decide anything. About me. About why Cassel really owns me." She stepped inside and closed the door. "And you're going to want to sit down."
