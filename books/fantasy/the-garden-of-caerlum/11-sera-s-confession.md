## 11. Sera's Confession

The fire had burned down to a red eye by the time Sera finally spoke.

They had reached the hollow below Thornhollow two days past, and Bramwen had given them a room in the undercroft of her longhouse—a low stone chamber that smelled of turnips and tallow, with a straw pallet wide enough for two if neither of them minded elbows. Quentin slept on the far side, curled small, breathing in the slow rhythm of someone who had learned to make sleep happen on command. A survivor's trick. Sera knew it well.

She should have been sleeping too. Tomorrow Bramwen would take them up to the standing stones and explain what she called *the three roads*, and whatever came after that, Sera suspected there would be no more nights of borrowed quiet. But sleep wouldn't come, and the confession sat in her chest like a swallowed stone, and she had learned—her sister had taught her, in the way of older sisters, by dying—that stones only got heavier the longer you carried them.

"Sera." Quentin's voice, thick with half-sleep. "You're staring at the fire like it owes you money."

"It does."

A pause. The straw rustled. "Then ask it for something else. You've been working up to a speech for two days. I can hear you rehearsing."

Sera almost laughed. Almost. Instead she fed another split log to the fire and watched the sparks climb toward the smoke hole, and said, "I reported my sister."

The words came out flat, stripped of everything, the way you'd read a line from an inventory. She kept her eyes on the flames because she could not have said them to a face.

"Her name was Liss. Lissandra Vane. She was four years older than me, and when I was fourteen she started leaving the house at night, and I followed her once, and I saw her stand in a flooded field with her hands out, and the water rose up around her arms like it loved her. Like she'd asked it to. And she was laughing." Sera's voice caught on the laugh, on the memory of the laugh. "I'd never seen her laugh like that. Not at home. Home was our father counting coins and our mother praying against the Gift like it was a disease in the walls. But out there, in the dark, with the water—she was *happy*, Quentin. That's what I turned in. Not a crime. A girl being happy where no one could see her."

Quentin had gone very still on the pallet. Sera could feel them listening the way you feel a change in weather.

"I told the parish reeve," she went on, because now that the dam had cracked she couldn't stop, hadn't been able to stop for eleven years, telling this story to no one, over and over, in the dark of barracks rooms while other hunters snored. "I was fifteen. I thought—I don't even know what I thought. That it would save her. That the Church courts were gentler than the throne's. That if she confessed and did penance they'd let her keep her hands." Her mouth twisted. "The Hunt took her within the week. They didn't do penance in those years. They did pyres."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, the longhouse creaked under wind.

"And here's the part that matters," Sera said. "Here's the part you need to hear before you trust me with whatever Bramwen is about to hand us. They didn't just take Liss. They took my statement, and my father's grief, and my mother's silence, and they built a little machine out of all of it. Cassel wasn't spymaster then—he was a rising man in the Office of Inquiry, and he interviewed me personally. Twice. He was kind, Quentin. That's the thing nobody tells you about him. He sat across from a fifteen-year-old girl who had just burned her sister alive with her own mouth, and he was *kind*. He said the words *you did your duty* until I believed them, and then, three years later, when I'd earned my hunter's badge and my clean record, he came back and showed me the original statement in his own handwriting, and he said: *This is yours forever. I keep it safe for you.*"

She heard Quentin sit up. Didn't look.

"That's how he keeps people. Not with threats, at first. With custody. He holds the worst thing you ever did, and he keeps it polished, and every few years he takes it out and shows it to you and says, *remember whose this is*. I've spent eleven years hunting Gifts for the throne, and every time I filed a report, I knew a copy went to Cassel's desk, and every time I showed mercy—every vial I swapped, every name I marked *null*—I knew he might already know, might be letting me hang myself with kindness until the rope was long enough to use." She finally turned. Quentin was sitting up in the straw, hair loose, face half-lit and unreadable. "You asked me once why I falsified results. For people who reminded me of her. Now you know what it costs. It isn't courage. It's a debt payment, and the creditor decides when the account closes, and the account never closes."

The silence stretched. Sera made herself endure it. This was the price of the speech: you gave someone the truth and then you had to sit in the room with their face while they decided what it weighed.

"You're waiting for me to say something," Quentin said at last.

"I'm waiting for you to decide something. There's a difference."

"What do you want me to decide?"

Sera exhaled slowly. "Whether I'm a person or a weapon he aimed at you. Because I genuinely don't know anymore, and I've had eleven years to work it out. Maybe everything I am is just his design wearing my face. Maybe mercy was never mine—maybe he *let* me have it because a hunter known for mercy gets taken along on the sensitive jobs, gets trusted near the valuable ones." She gestured, bitter, at Quentin, at the green seed sleeping beneath their skin, at the whole impossible cargo of them. "Near you. Maybe I'm not your rescuer, Quentin. Maybe I'm the leash. He lets me run with you because a running quarry leads somewhere, and here we are, exactly where the old woman with the answers lives. Convenient, isn't it?"

"It isn't," Quentin said quietly.

"How would you know?"

"Because I've spent my whole life asking whether I'm a person or a vessel somebody designed." Their voice didn't rise; it did something worse, it went level, the levelness of someone stating a fact about themselves they'd had years to get used to. "Marisol hid me before I could walk. Every choice I ever made about my own life, I've had to check against the possibility that it was her choice first. So I know exactly what you're describing. And I'll tell you what I've learned, which is not much, but it's this: you can't think your way out of that question. There's no proof available. There's no test that separates the leash from the person holding it. All you can do is choose, right now, in the dark, without evidence—and then choose again tomorrow, and again after that, and let the choosing be the proof."

Sera stared at them. "That's a terrible answer."

"It's the only one I've got."

"No, I mean—" Sera dragged a hand down her face. "It's a terrible answer because it means there's no door out. No verdict coming. Just the choosing, forever."

"Yes," Quentin said. "Welcome to it. I've been resident for twenty-two years."

Something in Sera's chest loosened—not comfort, nothing so gentle, more like a joint setting back into place after a long dislocation. She looked at the fire, at the young person wrapped in a blanket across from her who had killed a man in the snow nine days ago and still flinched at loud noises, and felt the full obscene weight of what she was about to ask.

"There's one more thing," she said. "And then you can hate me properly and we can get on with it."

"Go on."

"The night the Hunt tested my boarding house—your blood vial. I hesitated over it. You remember."

"I remember."

"You assumed I was deciding whether to save you. I wasn't. Or—I was, but not the way you think." Sera made herself hold Quentin's eyes. "Cassel sent me to that district specifically. He'd had whispers about a charm-seller in the night market whose luck-knots worked too well. He didn't send me to find you. He sent me to *collect* you, and he chose me because I have a reputation for bringing people in alive, and because—" her throat worked, "—because he wanted to see if I'd do it. If I'd hand over someone who reminded me of Liss. It was a test inside the assignment. Pass either way, he wins: I deliver the Crown, or I fail to deliver it and he learns I'm soft, and soft hunters get leashes shortened. So when I falsified your result, I wasn't defying him. I was spending the last coin of my deniability. He knows, Quentin. He has to know. When we ran, I didn't escape his design. I walked off the board, and men like Cassel don't lose pieces—they burn the square the piece stood on."

Quentin was quiet for a long moment. The fire had sunk to coals; the light on their face had gone the color of old bronze.

"So he'll come for both of us," they said.

"He was always coming for both of us. Now he comes *angry*, which is faster but stupider. It's the only advantage we have left." Sera leaned forward, elbows on knees. "But none of that matters tonight. Tonight the question is simpler. I've told you what I did to Liss. I've told you what Cassel owns. So: knowing all of it—do you want me beside you when Bramwen shows us those three roads tomorrow? Because if the answer's no, I'll walk out into the snow tonight, and I won't make it hard, and I'll draw the riders east so they never find this village. That's not a bribe. It's just the last useful thing I can do either way."

"That's a horrible offer."

"It's the only honest one I've got."

Quentin looked at her for a long time. Sera had faced down armed mages and burning buildings and Cassel's smile, and none of it had made her want to fidget like this—the still attention of someone deciding, with full information, whether you were worth the risk of their neck.

Then Quentin said, "If I forgive you, I need you to understand what I mean by it. Because I don't forgive lightly and I don't forgive completely, and anyone who promises otherwise is selling something."

"All right."

"When you told the reeve about Liss—you were fifteen, and afraid, and you believed a lie the whole world was built on. I can't put myself in that exactly, but I can put myself next to it. I sold charms to people and let them believe the magic was sleight of hand, because the truth would have gotten me killed, and every day I did it I hated it a little more, and hating it didn't stop me. Fear makes liars of everyone. So yes—I forgive the fifteen-year-old. That part's easy, honestly. That part I'd forgive anyone."

"But?"

"But the rest of it. Eleven years of reports. Eleven years of hunting people like Liss, like me, like Odile, who is probably in a cell right now because of the machine you fed." Quentin's voice stayed level, but Sera saw their hands close in the blanket. "I can't forgive that tonight. Maybe I can forgive it someday, if you spend the rest of your life unbuilding the thing you helped build. Not because you owe me. Because you owe *them*. Forgiveness isn't a feeling, Sera. I've figured that much out. It's a decision you make and then pay for, in installments, with work. So here's my decision: I choose to trust you. Starting now. Not because the feeling arrived—it hasn't—but because I've watched you choose, over and over, at cost to yourself, and the choosing is the only proof there is. You taught me that without meaning to. Every falsified vial was a payment. I'm deciding the account's worth continuing."

Sera found, to her horror, that her eyes were burning. She blinked it back down with the brutal efficiency of eleven years' practice. "You sound like a ledger."

"I sell charms. Everything sounds like a ledger."

A breath escaped Sera that was almost a laugh. "Liss would have liked you. She liked people who were angry and kind at the same time. Said it was the only honest combination."

"High praise from a drowned field."

"She'd have said it better." Sera looked into the coals. "Her name was Lissandra Vane. I haven't said it out loud since the pyre. It feels—"

"Loud," Quentin supplied.

"Yeah. Loud." Saying it had changed the shape of the room somehow, as though a window had been opened onto a night eleven years cold. "Cassel promised me he'd restore her name. Clear the record. Wipe *traitor's daughter* off the family line. That was his offer, through the years—dangle it, reel me in. And I used to want it so badly I couldn't breathe." She shook her head slowly. "But names aren't restored by the people who blackened them. That's not repair, that's a ransom paid to the kidnapper. If her name comes clean, it'll be because the whole rotten system that burned her comes down, and I tear it down with my own hands, and I don't need Cassel's permission to start."

"No," Quentin agreed softly. "You don't."

Outside, the wind shifted, and beneath it—faint, so faint Sera might have imagined it—came a sound like a bell struck underwater, rolling down from the ridge where the standing stones waited. Quentin's head lifted. In the dark, briefly, the veins of their wrists glowed a pale green, like sap catching light, and faded.

"They're awake up there," Quentin murmured. "Bramwen's people. I can feel them now. Have been able to since we crossed the wall. The land here is—" they searched for the word, gave up, "—*listening*."

Sera felt the cold walk up her spine. "Then listen back. What's it saying?"

Quentin's face went strange—afraid and lit at once, the look of someone hearing their own name called from a great distance.

"It's saying the riders are already on the pass."

* * *

They were moving before the bell-sound finished fading—Sera pulling her boots on with the practiced speed of a woman who had dressed for alarms her whole adult life, Quentin stumbling upright with the seed-light already pulsing faintly under their skin like a second heartbeat.

"How many?" Sera demanded, throwing open the undercroft door. Cold hit them like a wall. Above the longhouse, the winter sky was clear and vicious with stars, and the ridge stood black against it, and somewhere beyond the ridge, down the frozen pass, torches.

"I don't know. The land doesn't count. It just—knows weight. A lot of weight, moving fast." Quentin pressed a hand against the doorframe, steadying themselves, and Sera saw the frost on the wood retreat from their fingers in a slow bloom, like spring rehearsing. "Sera. It knows *horses*. It knows iron. It knows—"

"Bramwen." Sera was already climbing the stairs. "Wake Bramwen. Get the village moving. If Cassel's riders reach the valley before dawn—"

She stopped at the top of the stairs.

Below them, down in the white valley, at the edge of the frozen river, a single rider had crested the drifts ahead of the others—alone, unhurried, carrying no torch. Even at this distance, in the starlight, Sera knew the silhouette: the long coat, the still seat, the posture of a man who had never once needed to hurry because the world always came to him.

Lord Cassel had not sent his riders ahead.

He had come to watch them land.
