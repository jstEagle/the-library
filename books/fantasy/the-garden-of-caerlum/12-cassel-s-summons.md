## 12. Cassel's Summons

The dogs started barking an hour before dawn, and Bramwen was in the lane before Quentin had finished pulling on their boots.

"Riders," she said. Not a question. She had lived long enough in Thornhollow to know the difference between a wolf at the sheep pens and something worse.

Quentin followed her up the hill behind the meetinghouse, where the village kept its watch-fire burning through the winter dark, and looked down at the road that wound out of the pass. Torches. Dozens of them, strung along the frozen road like a rosary of orange beads, and above them the black shapes of horsemen moving at an unhurried walk. They weren't rushing. That was the thing Quentin noticed first, and it made their stomach drop. Men who rush are afraid. Men who walk like this own the ground they're standing on.

"How many?" Sera was already there, breath steaming, her old hunter's cloak thrown over her nightshirt. She'd slept in her boots since they'd arrived. Some habits, she said, were load-bearing.

"Fifty," Bramwen said. "Maybe sixty."

"They can't all be Cassel's."

"They're all someone's." The old woman's face was unreadable in the firelight. "The question is whose orders they carry, and whether those orders say *burn* or *talk*."

Sera shaded her eyes against the wind-driven snow, studying the column's banners. Quentin watched her go still — that particular stillness she got when she recognized something she'd been hoping not to.

"The grey stag," she said quietly. "That's his personal guard. Not hunters. Not soldiers on rotation. His household troops." She turned to Quentin. "He came himself."

"Cassel?"

"He wouldn't send fifty men for a conversation he could have by letter." Her jaw tightened. "But he also wouldn't come for a fight he wasn't sure of winning. Which means whatever he wants from you, he wants it badly enough to stand in the cold and ask."

Below them, the riders had reached the outskirts of Thornhollow and stopped. They didn't enter. They formed a ring around the village instead — a slow, deliberate circle of torchlight closing to a perimeter, the way you'd pen livestock, except there was no urgency in it. A fence, not a net. The distinction was meant to be understood.

Bramwen watched it happen with an expression Quentin couldn't name. "Well," she said at last. "I suppose we'd best go see what the wolf has to say."

* * *

They met him in the square by the well, because Bramwen refused to receive him anywhere else. "This is our ground," she said. "If he wants to talk, he talks where we draw our water."

Lord Aldous Cassel dismounted himself, which surprised Quentin. They had built him in their head out of Marisol's silences and Sera's hatred: a tall figure, cold, remote, a man who sent others to do his looking. The man who climbed down from the grey courser was shorter than expected, broad through the shoulders, with the weathered face of someone who had spent real years outdoors. He wore no furs despite the cold. His hair was iron-grey and cut short, practical as a soldier's. He might have been any provincial magistrate come to settle a tax dispute, if not for the eyes.

The eyes were patient. That was the worst part. Quentin had spent their whole life around fear — their own, their neighbors', Odile's quick darting glances, the market crowds flinching from shadows. Fear was loud and fast and stupid. This man's gaze moved over Thornhollow's huddled, torch-lit faces the way a surveyor moves over land he intends to buy.

"Lady Bramwen." He inclined his head with genuine courtesy. "You've kept the old rites alive. I confess I find that less alarming than your predecessors did."

"You've come a long way to be polite," Bramwen said. "Say what you want and go."

"What I want is standing next to you." And then he looked at Quentin, and Quentin felt it like a hand laid flat against their sternum — not magic, nothing so dramatic, just attention, total and weighing. "Quentin Joubert. Apprentice charm-seller. Runaway. Vessel." He said the last word without flourish, the way you'd say *farmer* or *smith*. "I've been looking for you for longer than you've been alive. May I call you Quentin?"

"No," Quentin said.

Something flickered across his face — amusement, maybe, or approval. "Good. I'd have been disappointed if you'd said yes."

Sera stepped forward, and two of the household guards' hands went to their swords before Cassel lifted one finger and they stopped. "Captain Vane," he said warmly. "Or should I say former Captain? You've cost me three good hunters and a season's work. And yet here you are, standing in the snow, protecting the very thing you were trained to find." He tilted his head. "I wonder if you've told them yet. About the report you filed. About your sister."

"Sera," Quentin said, without turning. "Don't."

"I know," Sera said. Her voice was flat and steady. "I told them myself. Weeks ago. You're late, my lord."

For the first time, something moved behind Cassel's patience — a small recalculation, visible only to someone watching for it. Quentin watched for it. *He didn't expect that.* It was a small thing, but Quentin held onto it. Even wolves could be surprised.

Cassel turned back to them and spread his hands, empty, palms out. A gesture learned from someone who knew exactly how it played.

"Then let me speak plainly, since pleasantries are wasted here. In four days, every citizen of Caerlum will have bled into a parish vial. In six, the Gift-Hunt will have its full rolls. In a month, the pyres start — and they will not stop at charm-sellers this time, because for the first time in twenty years, the throne believes magic is not merely possible but *present*, and fear makes poor surgeons. Villages will burn on rumor. Children will denounce parents. I have seen it before; I have the ledgers." He paused. "You can prevent all of it."

"By handing myself over," Quentin said.

"By coming with me. Alive. Willingly." He said it simply, without theatrical gravity, and somehow that was more frightening than if he'd thundered. "Understand what I'm offering, because I suspect the witch who raised you filled your head with nightmares. I am not offering you a cell. I am offering you the finest laboratories in the kingdom, physicians, scholars, protection. Study. Time. Safety for this village, immunity for every name in Bramwen's parish books, an end to the Hunt — not suspended, *ended*, signed under seal. All I require is the Crown. Extracted, contained, destroyed if it can be. You would live. Free. As yourself, whatever that turns out to mean."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I take it anyway, eventually, and everything between now and then is ash." He didn't threaten it. He stated it, like weather. "I'd rather not. Contrary to my reputation, I dislike waste."

"Why?" The word came out of Quentin harder than they intended. "Why does the spymaster want a dead goddess's crown? Why does anyone?"

And Cassel smiled — a thin, real smile, the first honest thing about him. "May I show you something instead of answering?"

He nodded to one of his guards, who brought forward a leather satchel. From it, Cassel drew a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, unwrapped it with careful hands, and held it out to Bramwen first, as if asking her permission. Then, when she didn't move, he held it toward Quentin.

"Twelve years ago," he said, "a hedge-witch named Marisol Duarte was taken in Caerlum's Lower Ward. You may remember her. Everyone assumed she died in the Hunt's holding cells, as most did. She did not. She was brought to me."

The bundle was clothing. A coat, patched at the elbows, smelling faintly of dried rosemary even after twelve years. Quentin knew it before their mind finished assembling the knowledge. Their hands knew it. Their hands had mended those elbow patches with thread bought from Odile's stall, had felt the weave go soft under a thumb pressed absently against it a thousand times while Marisol talked and talked and never said anything that mattered.

"There's more," Cassel said quietly.

Inside the coat's lining, slit open and resewn clumsily, was a packet of papers. Letters. Quentin's own name on the topmost one, in Marisol's cramped leftward-slanting hand. *To whoever holds these when I am gone.*

"She bargained with me," Cassel said. "Her life for yours. She gave me twenty years of intelligence on the old networks — names, safehouses, rites — and in exchange I let a certain child disappear from my records and agreed to wait. She told me what you carried. She told me what it would cost anyone who tried to take it by force. And she made me promise—" his voice caught, just barely, a hairline crack in masonry, "—she made me promise that when I found you, I would give you the choice she never got to give you herself. She said you'd know whether to believe me. She said you'd inherited her eye for liars."

Quentin read the letter standing in the snow, with torchlight shaking around them and sixty armed men forming a ring and Sera's breath going shallow beside them, and the world narrowed to Marisol's handwriting, which slanted worse when she was tired, and this was very tired, this was the tiredness of a woman writing her last leverage into a dead man's keeping:

*Little Q. If you're reading this, he found you, and I'm dust, and I'm sorry for all of it — for the binding, for the silence, for making you small enough to survive. Listen to me. Cassel is a monster, but he is a monster who keeps his word, and that is rarer than honesty and nearly as useful. He wanted the Crown for the throne. I made him want it for himself instead. Ambition can be bargained with; duty cannot. Whatever he offers you, he'll honor the terms. Whether the terms are a trap is a different question, and that one only you can answer. Don't let them take it out of you asleep. Don't let them take it out of you afraid. And Q — if it comes to it — don't let them take it out of you at all.*

*You were never small. I just needed you hidden.*

*— M.*

Quentin's eyes burned. The cold made it worse, tears freezing at the corners. They folded the letter along its worn creases — Marisol had folded and unfolded it many times before sealing it away — and looked up at Lord Aldous Cassel, and felt the rage arrive.

It came quiet, which was unlike them. Not the hot flare of the market, of the ambush in the snow. This was deep and slow and green, a taproot rage, and it spoke in a voice that was partly their own and partly something much older that lived underneath their ribs.

*You kept her coat,* that voice said. *You kept her letters. You sat on her death for twelve years and used her corpse as a door key.*

"You could have sent this years ago," Quentin said. Their voice sounded strange to them. Steady. "You could have given me her letter without the army."

"Yes," Cassel said. "And you'd have run. Or Bramwen would have burned the letter unread. I needed you cornered gently." He paused. "I'm sorry. That was her word for it too. *Cornered gently.* She hated it."

"What did she die of?"

"The extraction attempt." He said it without flinching, and Quentin realized that was its own kind of confession — he wasn't going to lie about this part, not after showing the coat. "Twenty years ago I believed power could be taken. I was younger and stupider. The Crown killed two of my scholars and burned itself out of her reach rather than yield. She spent her last week telling me why. Then she spent her last day negotiating your safety." His jaw worked. "She was the bravest person I ever interrogated. I use the past tense deliberately. There was never a moment she was not free."

The rage crested — and broke — and left behind it something colder and more dangerous: understanding. That was the trap of the man, Quentin saw suddenly. He didn't hide the horror. He showed it to you, priced honestly, and let you decide. Marisol had trusted him with her death. That fact sat in Quentin's chest like a swallowed stone.

"So take the offer," Cassel said. "Sanctuary. Research. Control — yours, not mine. You would direct the study of your own burden. No chains, no cells. Scholars who answer to you. And when it's done — if it can be done — you walk away, and the Hunt dies with it, and no one else burns." He stepped closer, boots crunching frost, close enough that Quentin could see the lines around his eyes, the genuine exhaustion of a man who had carried one obsession for two decades. "Every person who has ever held that Crown used it as a weapon, and every one of them rotted. I am offering you the chance to be the first to hold it as a *problem*. To end it carefully, with witnesses, with method — instead of some desperate rite in a ruined chapel that kills you and maybe half this valley."

He had heard about the rite. Of course he had. Quentin glanced at Bramwen; the old woman's face was carved from oak.

"Think of it," Cassel said, softer now, and here at last was the seduction, unveiled, patient, aimed with twenty years of practice. "Not surrender. Stewardship. You've spent your whole life hiding what you are, apologizing for it, bleeding to keep it small. I'm offering the opposite: a room where your gift is the entire purpose. Where being what you are is not a crime but a *discipline*. Power, used well, this time — by someone who knows its price. By you. Who else? Who else alive has earned the right?"

And the terrible thing — the thing Quentin would lie awake with for nights afterward — was how much of it rang true. The greenhouses. The boy healed in the market square. The hunter dead in the snow. Every mercy they'd paid for with a piece of themselves, alone, untrained, terrified. A room full of scholars. Rest. Help. *Control.*

The seed stirred beneath their breastbone, warm as a banked fire, and whispered — not in words, never in words — something that felt like *yes, and also like beware.*

Quentin looked down at Marisol's letter one more time. *Whether the terms are a trap is a different question.*

"My lord," Bramwen said, into the silence, with the crispness of a woman who had buried three kings' tax collectors and one bishop, "you've made your speech. The village has chores."

Cassel almost laughed. "Lady Bramwen, you are everything the stories promised." He straightened, and the warmth banked itself, professional again. "I'll not force this today. Force kills the vessel — your witch proved that with her life, and I don't intend to repeat her lesson at this scale. So: three days. Think. Consult your elders, your ghosts, your conscience. My men will remain at the tree line. No one enters, no one leaves — I regret the inconvenience, but I regret corpses more." He turned to mount, then paused, one hand on the saddle, and looked back at Quentin.

"For what it's worth — and I know what it's worth, which is little — she loved you very much. She talked about you the last three days the way other people talk about religion."

Then he rode, and the ring of torches withdrew to the tree line with the same terrible unhurried discipline, and Thornhollow stood in its square in the freezing dark, breathing steam, saying nothing.

* * *

They gathered in Bramwen's kitchen afterward, the ones who mattered: Bramwen, Sera, Quentin, and old Hollis the smith who rarely spoke and therefore was listened to when he did. The fire was small. Fuel was counted now, counted in a way it hadn't been a month ago.

"He'll wait three days and then he won't," Sera said. "Fifty men eat. Snow seals the passes soon — he knows it better than we do. He's not besieging us, he's *scheduling* us."

"He showed the coat," Hollis said, to the fire. "A liar doesn't show the coat."

"A truthful man doesn't bring fifty riders to show a coat," Sera answered.

"Both things are true," Bramwen said. "That's what makes him dangerous. Sit down, girl, you're dripping on my floor." She lowered herself into her chair with the groan of old joints and looked at Quentin for a long moment. "Well, child. He offered you everything you've ever wanted. A room where you don't have to hide. Did it feel like freedom?"

Quentin turned Marisol's letter over in their hands. Outside, the wind had begun to rise, dragging the first fat flakes of a real storm across the window glass — the storm the shepherds had been muttering about for days, the one the old-timers called the Long Winter when it came bad, and it came bad more often now.

"It felt like a greenhouse," Quentin said finally. "Warm. Glass all around. And him holding the only door."

Bramwen nodded slowly, as if that were the correct answer to some older question. "Then tomorrow we talk about the rite," she said. "All of it. What it costs. What it requires." Her eyes went to the window, to the thickening dark. "Because I don't think we have three days. I think the snow is going to make that decision for us."

Sera stood, went to the window, and was quiet for a long time.

"Bramwen," she said at last. "How long has it been since anyone got a message out of this valley?"

The old woman's silence was answer enough.

Outside, the Long Winter began to fall.
