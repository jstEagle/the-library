## 13. The Long Winter Falls

The snow came on the third night of the new moon, and it did not stop for nine days.

Quentin stood at the greenhouse door and watched it fall, a gray curtain that erased the mountain passes one ridge at a time. By morning the road to Caerlum was gone. By the second day the road to the lowlands was gone. Thornhollow sat in its bowl of white like a coin at the bottom of a well, and Quentin understood, with a cold clarity that had nothing to do with the weather, that no one was coming.

"We've flour for six weeks," Bramwen said behind them. She said it the way she said everything—flat, unhurried, as if reciting an inventory she'd already grieved. "Less if we're honest about how much six weeks of flour feeds forty-three people."

"Forty-four," Quentin said. "I'm still here."

"You're here." Bramwen looked at them sidelong, and there was something in her face Quentin couldn't read—or could read and refused to. "That's what worries me."

* * *

They rationed by lamplight in the meeting house, forty-three names read aloud and portions measured into cloth bags with a grain merchant's scale older than anyone using it. Quentin watched the villagers take their shares without complaint, because these were people who remembered hunger from other winters, who knew the etiquette of scarcity. Old Wenna split her portion with her sister's orphans before anyone asked her to. The cooper, Hallam, gave his oil ration to the chapel lamp so the children wouldn't have to sit in the dark.

It would have been moving, Quentin thought, if it weren't arithmetic. Generosity didn't change the sum. It only decided who got hungry first.

And under the floorboards of the smallest greenhouse, wrapped in Marisol's old charm-cloth, the seed pulsed like a second heart.

Quentin felt it all through the rationing. Felt it lean toward every empty bag, every hollow cheek, the way water leans toward a crack. It wasn't hunger exactly. It was more like the seed *noticed*. All winter it had been noticing—the frost-killed orchard, the frozen well, the goat that didn't make it through the second week—and each time it noticed, it offered, quiet as breath: *I can fix this. You know I can.*

Quentin had said no for eleven days.

On the twelfth, they went out to the greenhouses after midnight and knelt in the snow, and pressed both palms against the frozen glass of the south house where the seedlings had gone black in their trays, and said, "Just this once."

The lie tasted familiar. They'd been telling versions of it their whole life. *Just this once I'll use a little magic. Just this once I'll be careful. Just this once no one will see.*

The seed opened like a hand.

Warmth moved up through Quentin's arms—not heat, something older than heat, sap and sunlight and the slow green insistence of things that grow toward what feeds them. The black seedlings shivered. Unblackened. Stood up in their trays like small tired soldiers called back to their feet. Frost retreated from the glass in a widening circle, and outside, Quentin saw, the snow over the greenhouse roof had softened and slid away, and beneath it the soil steamed faintly, breathing.

It cost them a tooth's worth of dizziness and a night of dreams full of roots. Cheap, Quentin told themselves, walking home through the blue dark. That was cheap.

They did not let themselves think about the word *yet*.

* * *

By the feast of Saint Corvin—midwinter, the year's darkest hinge—the village should have been down to seed grain and boiled leather. Instead there were greens.

Bramwen stood in the greenhouse doorway with a head of cabbage in her hands like evidence at a trial. Behind her, rows of winter vegetables grew fat and impossible under glass while the world outside lay white and dead to the horizon.

"I asked who," she said.

"The greenhouse faces south," Quentin said. "Good light."

"It's been snowing for a month, child. There is no light. There is barely a *sun*." She set the cabbage down on the workbench with terrible gentleness. "Who."

Quentin held her gaze and lied, which was easier than it should have been, and that ease frightened them more than the cabbage did. "I don't know."

Bramwen studied them for a long moment. She was old enough, Quentin thought, to have seen every kind of hiding there was; she'd spent a lifetime keeping old rites alive under a throne that burned people for them. Maybe that was why she let it go. Or maybe she saw something in Quentin's face—a grayness under the skin, a tremor in the hands—that told her the cost was being paid somewhere, even if not where she was looking.

"Eat," she said finally, pushing the cabbage across the bench. "You look like something left out in the rain."

* * *

The pattern established itself the way bad habits do: invisibly, then completely.

Every third night, Quentin went to the greenhouses. Every third night, the seed opened, and warmth poured out of Quentin into the frozen ground, and things grew that had no business growing, and Quentin paid. They learned the currency quickly, because the seed kept honest accounts. A night of keeping the whole south house alive cost them their sense of smell until noon. Two nights cost color—the world arriving bleached and gray, meat looking like stone, blood looking like ink. Once, after forcing the well spring unfrozen so the village wouldn't have to melt snow, Quentin woke unable to feel their own feet, and lay in bed testing their toes like a man checking whether he still owned them, and did not tell anyone, and got up anyway when the bell rang for morning chores.

The worst part wasn't the weakness. The worst part was how good it felt to be needed.

Odile used to say, back in the night market, that charity was the most addictive drug in Caerlum—cheaper than wine, better respected, and twice as hard to quit. Quentin understood now. Every morning someone thanked them for the greens, for the warm well water, for the fresh bread that appeared when the flour ran low (and where *did* the flour come from, someone wondered aloud, and Quentin studied their boots). Every morning the gratitude went down warm, and every morning the debt it covered grew, and every morning the price came due in some small amputated piece of themselves, and Quentin found—to their horror—that they were willing. Willing to go gray. Willing to go numb. Willing, if it came to it, to go blind, deaf, dumb, if the alternative was watching Wenna's orphans' cheeks go sharp.

*That's how it starts,* Marisol had written, in a letter Quentin hadn't understood until now. *Not with cruelty. With usefulness. No one ever grasps the Crown because they want to rule. They grasp it because someone is hungry, and they can help, and helping feels like love. Then one day you look down and your hand has become the thing everyone needs, and you cannot open it, and neither can they.*

Quentin read that letter four times in the dark of their room, by the light of a lamp they were too weak some nights to light themselves.

Then they went out to the greenhouses anyway.

* * *

In the fifth week, Sera caught them.

Quentin heard her boots on the frozen path too late—she moved quietly for a woman her size, a hunter's habit that would never leave her—and by the time they turned, she was standing in the greenhouse doorway with her arms crossed and her breath smoking, watching the impossible lettuce grow.

"How long?" she said.

Quentin considered lying. It was remarkable, really, how easy it had gotten. "Since the first week."

"And?"

"And what?"

"And what's it costing you." Sera stepped inside, and the warmth closed around her, and she flinched slightly, the way she always did around Quentin's working—as if it reminded her of something she'd rather forget. She took Quentin's chin in one gloved hand and turned their face to the light. Whatever she saw made her jaw tighten. "You look like a corpse that's been promoted to clerk."

"Thank you."

"Your eyes are wrong. Your color's wrong. Last week you fell asleep at supper, face-first in your bowl, and Bramwen covered for you, which means Bramwen knows, which means everyone knows except whoever you think you're fooling." She dropped her hand. "How much can you give before there's nothing left?"

Quentin looked at the seedling trays, the fat cabbages, the steam rising off living soil in a dead white world. "I don't know," they said, and it was almost true. "But I know what happens if I stop."

"People starve."

"People starve *slower*." Quentin heard their own voice come out hard and strange. "Sera, I have seen what hunger does. I sold fever-charms in the night market to mothers whose children were already past saving. I know the arithmetic better than anyone in this village. Forty-three people. Six weeks of flour. And I—" they spread their hands, gray-nailed, trembling faintly, "—I am a granary that walks. What kind of person hoards herself when she's a granary?"

"The kind who wants to still be a person in the spring."

"That's cowardice dressed up as wisdom."

"No," Sera said quietly. "Cowardice is what I did with my sister. This is different. This is worse." She pulled a crate over and sat, elbows on knees, close enough that Quentin could see the scar through her eyebrow, the one she'd never explained. "Listen to me. I've seen what power looks like when it eats its owner. Cassel's whole apparatus is built on people who started out wanting to help. Every interrogator in that citadel believes he's protecting the realm. Every informer believes he's keeping the peace. Nobody joins the Hunt thinking *I want to hurt people.* They join thinking *someone has to*, and then ten years later they can't remember who they were before someone had to." She gestured at the greenhouses, at the miracle growing in the dark. "This is the same door. You're just walking through it kindly."

"So I should let them starve."

"So you should tell Bramwen. Tonight. Let the village decide what you're worth, instead of deciding for them." Sera stood. "That's the difference between power and service, Quentin. Service gets a vote. Power doesn't ask."

She left, and Quentin stayed among the green things a long time, listening to the seed hum its patient offer, and could not make themselves move.

* * *

They told Bramwen two nights later. Not because Sera was right—they still weren't sure she was—but because Quentin fainted carrying water to the goats and woke with three villagers staring down at them, and the choice stopped being theirs.

Bramwen listened without interrupting. When Quentin finished—the seed, the greenhouses, the nights, the prices paid in senses and strength and whatever else they couldn't name yet—the old woman was silent for a long moment, staring into the meeting-house fire.

"My mother," she said at last, "kept bees. When a hive ran short in a hard winter, she fed them sugar water, and the bees lived, and in the spring they made weak honey, and the colony declined a little every year, and no one could say why, until one year there were no bees." She looked up. "You understand? It isn't the feeding that kills. It's that the feeding lets everything continue as if nothing is wrong. The village plans around your miracles now, child. Rations stretch because the greens exist. No one salts meat because the well stays sweet. We have stopped preparing for the spring because you have promised us a winter that doesn't end." Her voice gentled. "I'm not ungrateful. I'm telling you what my mother's bees taught me. Kindness that costs the giver everything and changes nothing is just a slower famine."

"So what do we do?"

"Now?" Bramwen rose, joints cracking like the ice on the eaves. "Now we do what villages do. We count honestly, we cut rations to truth, we send Hallam's boys through the pass with skis before it closes for good, and we pray the lowland roads open early." She put a hand on Quentin's shoulder, light as a bird landing. "And you rest. That's not a kindness to you. It's a kindness to us. A village that builds itself on one person's burning is a village waiting for a funeral."

* * *

For six days, Quentin rested, and it was agony.

They lay in their room listening to the village tighten its belt, hearing the smaller portions served, the quieter suppers, and every hour the seed whispered its offer, and every hour refusing got harder, and on the sixth day Hallam's sons came back from the pass with bloodied faces and a report: drifted solid, ten feet deep in the narrows, no way through till thaw. Thaw was eight weeks away. The flour would last five.

That evening Bramwen stood in the meeting house and told the village the truth, all of it, the numbers plain, and Quentin watched forty-three faces do the arithmetic and arrive, one by one, at the same answer. No one said *use the boy*. No one said *use the witch*. But the silence had a shape, and the shape was a question, and everyone in the room knew Quentin could hear it.

Wenna's youngest orphan, a girl of six named Tilly with a chilblain-scarred face, tugged Quentin's sleeve as the meeting broke up.

"If you get sick from helping us," she said, with the terrible directness of children, "do you die?"

"Maybe," Quentin said. "Probably not."

"Grandmother says you shouldn't burn yourself up for other people. She says that's how saints get made and saints are dead." The girl considered this. "But I'm hungry."

Quentin knelt. Up close, the child's cheeks were already sharper than they'd been a month ago, and something in Quentin's chest tore along a seam they hadn't known was there.

"I know," they said. "I know you are."

* * *

That night they went back to the greenhouses.

Not the gentle trickle of before—this time Quentin pressed both hands flat to the earth and *opened*, and the seed roared up through them like spring itself breaking a dam, and they poured it out in a flood: warmth into the root cellars, life into the seed grain so it would sprout weeks early in flats by every hearth, strength into the goats, green into the winter garden until the whole south slope of Thornhollow glowed faintly under the snow like a coal under ash. They gave it everything the seed asked to give and more besides, and the price came due all at once, and Quentin paid it standing up.

When they finally staggered out, dawn was graying the east, and the snow on the path had melted in a ring around them, and Sera was sitting on the woodpile watching with an expression Quentin couldn't read.

"Well," she said. "That was either the bravest thing I've ever seen or the stupidest."

"Both," Quentin said. Their voice came out sanded thin. "Probably both."

They took two steps and the world tilted, and Sera caught them before the ground did, and the last thing Quentin saw before the dark took them was the seed's light shining up through their own skin, green as new leaves, green as a promise, green as a trap—

—and the last thing they heard, far off, down the valley road that no rider should have been able to cross, was the sound of many horses.
