## 14. Bramwen's Price

The snow came down like a verdict.

Quentin sat in Bramwen's kitchen—the warmest room in Thornhollow, the room where all the hard conversations happened, Quentin was learning—and watched the old woman grind something in a mortar with the patience of someone who had done it ten thousand times. Dried yarrow. A sprig of something resinous Quentin didn't recognize. The smell filled the low-ceilinged space like a memory trying to surface.

"You've been avoiding me," Bramwen said, without looking up.

"I've been keeping greenhouses alive."

"That too." She set the mortar down and finally looked at him. Her eyes were pale as river ice and just as hard to see through. "But you know what I mean. Three days since I told you there were three paths. You've walked past my door eleven times. I counted."

Quentin had no answer for that. He wrapped his hands around his cup of barley tea and let the heat bite into his palms. Outside, the storm that had sealed the passes for a week showed no sign of mercy. The village had buried two goats and half its turnip crop under the drifts, and every morning the line at the greenhouses grew longer—families coming to stare at impossible green things growing in impossible cold, and then looking at Quentin with an expression he couldn't bear. Gratitude. Hope. The kind of hope that eats the hoper alive.

He'd been paying for every sprout out of his own marrow. He hadn't told anyone that either.

"Say it," he said. "Whatever you've been waiting to say. Say it."

Bramwen wiped her hands on her apron, slowly, the way she did everything. Then she sat across from him, and her spine straightened, and Quentin understood that whatever came next had been rehearsed for three days—or perhaps for forty years.

"The rite of Returning," she said, "requires a willing death."

* * *

The words went into him like a nail driven slow.

Quentin had known. That was the terrible thing—he had known, somewhere below language, since the night Marisol's seed first pulsed against his pulse. Power like this did not leave a body politely. It did not slide out like a splinter. It had roots. And you could not pull up roots without tearing open the ground they held.

Still. Knowing a thing in your bones and hearing it said aloud in a warm kitchen over barley tea were different animals entirely.

"A willing death," he repeated. His voice sounded far away, like someone else speaking through a wall.

"Yes." Bramwen didn't flinch. He respected that, even now. Even as the floor tilted under him. "The Crown is not an object, boy. You know that by now. It's a will. A very old, very stubborn will that spent centuries wearing bodies like coats and leaving them rotted from the inside. It cannot be destroyed while it wants to live. It cannot be given away while it wants to be kept. The only thing it cannot argue with—" she tapped the table once, sharp, "—is a soul that chooses to stop."

"So I die."

"You pour yourself out. Every drop. The Crown goes into the land because you go into the land. There's no seam between them. That's what Marisol never got far enough to learn, and what I learned the hard way." Something moved behind her face then, something old and drowned. "I performed the rite once before. Forty-one years ago."

Quentin looked up. In all the weeks since Thornhollow—in all the stories Bramwen had told about the old rites, the hidden wells, the goddess whose name nobody spoke—he had never heard this.

"There was another vessel?" he asked.

"My daughter."

The word landed in the room and stayed there, enormous.

"Alwen," Bramwen said, and saying the name cost her; Quentin watched her hands go still on the table. "She was born carrying a fragment. Not the whole Crown—Marisol wasn't the only one who worked that old magic, though she was the best of us. A shard. Enough to make Alwen hear the fields singing when she should have been sleeping. Enough to make the wrong people curious." Her jaw tightened. "A hunter came. This was before the Hunt had a name, when it was just men with warrants and fire. We decided to end it ourselves rather than run forever. I built the circle. I read the words. Alwen lay down in the snow at the standing stone above the village, and she was willing—gods forgive me, she was so willing, she smiled at me right up until the end."

Quentin's mouth had gone dry. "What happened?"

"She poured herself out." Bramwen's voice stayed level, but only the way a frozen river is level—one crack from catastrophe. "And the shard went into the ground, and the ground drank it, and for one moment the whole valley bloomed. Midwinter, and the snow melted back ten paces in every direction, and every seed in the soil woke up thinking it was May." Her eyes closed. "But I stopped it."

"Stopped it?"

"I couldn't watch her die." The words came out flat, stripped of excuse. "The rite takes everything, and I saw her going—saw the light running out of her like water from a broken jug—and I reached in and pulled her back. Halfway. I caught her soul before it finished leaving and dragged it home." She opened her eyes, and they were terrible and clear. "She lived nine more days. Neither alive nor dead. The shard torn in half, half in her, half in the earth, neither whole enough to do anything but hurt. She begged me to finish it, near the end. I couldn't do that either. So I have lived forty-one years knowing that my cowardice bought my daughter nine days of agony instead of one clean moment of peace."

The kettle ticked on the stove. Snow pressed against the window like it wanted in.

"Why tell me this?" Quentin asked quietly. "You could have just told me the price."

"Because you deserve to know both halves of it." Bramwen leaned forward. "The price, and the way a loving hand can make it worse. When the moment comes—and it will come, boy, the Crown is waking faster now, I can feel it humming in the well water—you will want someone to catch you. Everyone does. It's the oldest instinct there is. And the person who loves you most will be the most dangerous person in that circle, because their love will tell them to reach in and pull you back." She held his gaze. "So you must choose your circle carefully. And you must choose it honestly. No one in it may love you more than they love the world. Do you understand me?"

Quentin thought of Sera, out in the byre sharpening knives that hadn't needed sharpening, the way she did when her thoughts got loud. He thought of the look on her face after the bounty hunters' ambush in the pass—after she'd seen what lived inside him, seen a man unmade by green light—and how she had stayed anyway. How she'd sat beside him that night and said nothing, which from Sera Vane was a speech.

"I think so," he said.

"Don't think. Know." Bramwen rose and began moving around the kitchen, pulling things from shelves—chalk, a bundle of dried herbs, a knife with a bone handle worn smooth by generations of hands. "Because we don't have until spring. The Crown is straining against you. Every time you feed those greenhouses, you loosen the knot Marisol tied, and the knot doesn't retie itself. Another month of this and it won't matter whether you're willing or not. It'll take what it wants and wear you out like a coat." She set the items on the table between them. "We do it at the standing stone, at the dark of the moon. Nine nights from now."

Nine nights.

Quentin sat very still and felt the number settle over him like snowfall—soft, and cold, and total.

* * *

He didn't cry. He'd expected to, and the not-crying frightened him more than the crying would have. He walked out of Bramwen's kitchen into the blue dusk of the storm's edge and stood in the lane between the houses, and the village moved around him—smoke from chimneys, a dog barking, someone hauling a sled of firewood—and none of them knew. None of them knew that the boy who kept their greenhouses green had nine nights left.

He thought: *I should tell them.*

He thought: *No. They'll look at me differently. Every meal, every kindness, will become a farewell, and I can't—I can't eat nine days of goodbyes.*

He thought: *That's exactly what Marisol did to me, isn't it. Twelve years of silence so I wouldn't have to carry it early.*

He thought: *Gods, Marisol. Did you know? All those years, did you sit across from me at supper counting down something you never named?*

The seed answered him before he was ready. It did that more often now—a slow green warmth behind his sternum, like a second heartbeat learning his rhythm, patient as roots. It wasn't malevolent. That was the trap of it. It felt like being loved by something vast. And Bramwen had just taught him the word for what that love wanted: *everything.*

He walked to the byre.

Sera was exactly where he knew she'd be, working a whetstone along a hunting knife with the mechanical rhythm of a woman refusing to think. She glanced up when he came in, took one look at his face, and set the knife down.

"Bramwen talked to you," she said.

"Yeah."

"And?"

Quentin leaned against the stall door. Somewhere beyond the byre wall, the horses shifted in the dark. "It needs a willing death," he said. "That's the price. The whole price. I pour myself out and the Crown goes into the ground and magic stops belonging to anyone with the strength to take it." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "All those years hiding. All those charms sold for copper, all those nights checking the door latch twice. I survived the Hunt, and Cassel, and the bounty hunters in the pass. And it turns out the exit was inside me the whole time."

Sera was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was careful, the way it always was when she was handling something that might detonate. "You're telling me calmly. That worries me."

"I'm not calm. I'm—" He searched for it. "I'm standing very still on ice and listening to it crack. But underneath that." He looked at his hands. They were a farm boy's hands, a hedge-witch's apprentice hands, ink-stained and chapped from greenhouse work. Ordinary hands. "Underneath that, some part of me has been waiting for this since the chapel. Since I read Marisol's carvings and understood what she'd done. Nobody carries a crown like this and gets to keep their life. Every story agrees on that much. I just—" His voice snagged. "I just found out I get nine nights first. Most people don't get to know. Maybe that's a mercy. Maybe it's the cruelest thing anyone's ever done to me. I genuinely don't know."

Sera crossed the byre and did something she almost never did: she put her hand on the back of his neck, brief and hard, the grip of someone anchoring another person in a gale. Then she stepped back and became practical again, because that was her armor and he loved her for it.

"Nine nights," she said. "Then we use them. What do you need?"

"What?"

"You heard me. If you're doing this—if we're doing this—then we do it properly. Not a scared kid lying down in the snow because an old woman with regrets is rushing him. You said the rite needs willingness. Fine. Then spend nine nights becoming willing. Whatever that means for you. Say your goodbyes or don't. Settle your accounts. Eat the food you like. Write letters. Get furious about it if you need to get furious." Her jaw set. "My sister had one night between the warrant and the pyre. One night, and half of it wasted on my cowardice. You have nine. I'll be damned if any of them get wasted."

Quentin blinked hard and looked away, out the byre's cracked door, at the last violet light dying over the white hills.

"Will you be in the circle?" he asked. "Bramwen says no one in it may love me more than the world. And you—" He turned back. "You're the most stubbornly decent person I've ever met, Sera Vane. But I've seen what happens to people who try to hold onto me."

Something flickered across her face—her sister's name, maybe, or the memory of a falsified blood vial, all the ways she had already failed and saved people in the same motion.

"I'll stand at the edge," she said at last. "Not in the circle. At the edge. Where I can keep the rest of the world off you while you do what you have to do." A ghost of her old dry humor. "Keeping watch is the one thing I've never failed at."

* * *

That night Quentin climbed to the standing stone alone.

The storm had blown itself out; the sky was scoured clean and brutal with stars, and the snow squeaked under his boots, and his breath hung around him like a spirit reluctant to leave. The stone rose out of the whiteness ahead—older than the village, older than the war, older probably than the goddess whose death had made the Crown. Lichen traced grey-green script up its flank. He put his palm against it, and it was cold, and it was utterly indifferent to him, and somehow that helped.

*Nine nights,* he thought. *Then I come here and I don't get up.*

Behind his sternum, the seed stirred—not resisting, not pleading. Just turning toward him, attentive, the way a great animal turns toward the one who feeds it. And for the first time Quentin didn't flinch from it. He let himself feel the whole weight of it, the vast slow wanting, and he said, aloud, into the starlight:

"I know what you are. And I know what I am. And in nine nights, neither of us gets to keep the other."

The green warmth pulsed once, slow, like a question.

He took his hand off the stone and started walking back down toward the lights of Thornhollow, and behind him, where his palm had rested, the lichen had begun—very faintly, in the deep cold—to glow.

Below in the village, a bell began to ring. Not the chapel bell. The watch-bell, fast and ragged, the sound Thornhollow used for riders on the road.

Quentin stood in the snow and counted the strokes, and his blood went cold, because the bell wasn't stopping.
