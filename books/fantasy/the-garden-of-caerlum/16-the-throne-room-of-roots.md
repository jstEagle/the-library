## 16. The Throne Room of Roots

The citadel of Lord Cassel rose out of the frozen plain like a black tooth, and Quentin walked toward it with their hands empty and their heart hammering a rhythm that had nothing to do with fear and everything to do with decision.

They had made the offer at dawn, standing in the ruined doorway of Bramwen's longhouse while smoke still curled from the burned greenhouses. Cassel's herald had come under a white banner, and his terms had been simple: the vessel comes alone, or the prisoners die one by one until it does. Sera among them. Odile among them. Eleven of Thornhollow's own, taken in the raid, held in the citadel's lower cells.

So Quentin had washed, dressed in the clean wool Bramwen pressed on them without a word, and eaten a breakfast they couldn't taste.

"You don't have to do this," Sera had said, when they told her. Her hands were bandaged from the fire; she'd been pulled from the burning greenhouse half-conscious, and the guilt of that sat on Quentin like wet snow. "There are other ways. We could—"

"There aren't." Quentin had said it gently, because she needed gentleness just then, and because they meant it. "You taught me something on the aqueduct, Sera. You said trust is a loan nobody expects repaid. Well. This is me spending everything I've got."

"That's not what I—"

"It's exactly what you said." They had almost smiled. "I'm going to walk in there and give him what he wants. And then we'll see what he does with it."

She had gripped their arm hard enough to bruise. "If he kills you—"

"He won't kill me. He needs me alive. That's the whole point of me." The words had come out steadier than they felt. "That's been the whole point of me since before I was born. I'm finally going to use it."

Now, hours later, the gates stood open — an invitation and a threat in equal measure — and Quentin walked through them into a courtyard swept clean of snow, as if even here, in the seat of Caerlum's power, someone was afraid of what might grow if the ground were left alone.

* * *

They took Quentin's coat at the door. Then the knife in their boot. Then, after a hesitation that told Quentin everything about how much Cassel feared them, the laces from their shoes.

"Protocol," the guard said, not meeting their eyes.

"For the prisoner or for the guest?" Quentin asked.

The guard didn't answer. That was answer enough.

They were escorted through corridors of grey stone hung with banners so old their colors had faded to ghosts, past servants who looked through them as though looking too long might cost them something. Quentin counted doors, turns, staircases — not to escape, but to know where they were going. Marisol had taught them that much: *know the shape of your cage, little sprout, before you decide whether to rattle it.*

At last they came to a set of double doors banded in iron, carved with the royal sigil — a tower above crossed swords — worn nearly smooth by centuries of hands pushing against it.

Beyond lay a hall that took Quentin's breath away.

It should have been a throne room. It had clearly once been one: the dais still stood at the far end, the high vaulted ceiling still arched overhead like the ribs of some enormous beast. But no throne sat on the dais. Instead, roots grew there.

They came up through the stone floor itself — thick, pale, gnarled things, silver-grey and dead-looking, spreading across the dais in a tangle like the fingers of a buried hand. They climbed the walls in places, traced the vaulting, and in the center of the chamber they converged on a raised basin of black iron, wide as a cartwheel, its rim etched with script Quentin recognized with a jolt: the same flowing characters Marisol had carved inside the hidden box, twelve years ago, in a language she'd never taught them to read.

Old magic. Older than the kingdom. Older, maybe, than the war the kingdom was built on.

"You're wondering about the roots."

Cassel stood at the base of the dais, and Quentin saw him properly for the first time: a spare man in dark green, grey at the temples, with the kind of face that had been handsome once and had since been worn down to pure function, like a coin rubbed featureless by handling. His eyes were the color of shallow water over stones.

"They were here when the citadel was built," he went on, gesturing at the pale tangle without touching it. "The masons cut around them. Every architect since has proposed removing them. Every architect has died within the year. Superstition, my predecessors called it." He smiled thinly. "I call it evidence. Something under this hill remembers what this land used to be. And something in you, Master Joubert, is kin to it."

"I'm not your master anything," Quentin said. "And I didn't come here for a lecture."

"No?" Cassel descended the two steps from the dais, unhurried. "Why did you come, then? Truly. I've read every report on you — Vane's reports especially, before her loyalties became... flexible. You're not brave. Bravery is a performance for an audience. There's no audience here." He tilted his head. "So why?"

Quentin held his gaze. "Because you have eleven people in your cells who did nothing but feed their neighbors through a winter you made crueler. Because Sera Vane is one of them. And because—" they paused, and decided, freely, to say the true thing, "—because I'm tired of being a hiding place. Whatever happens next, I want it to happen *to* me, not around me."

Something flickered across Cassel's face — respect, perhaps, or its close cousin, appetite.

"Well," he said softly. "Marisol trained you better than I expected. Come. Let me show you what you've agreed to."

* * *

He led them up onto the dais, between the pale roots, to the iron basin. Up close Quentin could see that the script on its rim wasn't carved but *grown* — the metal had flowed around the letters like wax around a seal, as if the basin had been poured over something living and the living thing had left its signature behind.

"The rite is called Extraction," Cassel said. "It predates the Mage War. It predates the Crown itself, if the fragments are to be believed. The goddess — the one your mentor's order served — was not killed, despite what the chapels teach. She was *harvested*. Slowly. By people who believed, sincerely, that they could hold her power better than she could." He watched Quentin's face as he said it, watching for flinching, cataloguing each one. "The Crown was the instrument. A vessel to hold a vessel. Your Marisol reversed the process — poured the harvest into a newborn instead of an object. Clever. Cruel. Necessary, she would have said."

"You talk about her like you knew her."

"I interrogated her for six years." He said it flatly, without pride. "She never gave me your name. Never gave me this city. She gave me nothing but corrections to my grammar." For a moment — only a moment — something moved behind the shallow-water eyes. Grief, maybe. Or its fossil. "Then she died, and I started over, and twenty years later a hedge-witch's luck-knots turn up in a night market working miracles, and here we are."

"Here we are," Quentin echoed. Their mouth was dry. "And the ritual. What does it do to me?"

"Honestly?" Cassel met their eyes, and Quentin gave him credit for not lying. "I don't know. No one has extracted a willing vessel before. The texts assume resistance. Resistance burns the vessel out — ash, in the old accounts. Willingness..." He spread his hands. "Perhaps it will be gentle. Perhaps you'll simply sleep, and wake lighter. Or perhaps the Crown will take everything you are on its way out, the way ivy takes a tree. I intend to find out. With your help."

"And if I refuse now? Walk out?"

"The eleven prisoners die at dusk. Starting with Vane." He said it without heat, a man reading a ledger. "But you already knew that. You did the arithmetic on the road here. I recognize the look."

Quentin looked at the basin. At the pale roots converging beneath it like veins feeding a heart. At the script glowing faintly now — faintly, unmistakably — in time with their own pulse.

*It knows I'm here,* Quentin thought. *It's been waiting longer than I have.*

"Then let's not keep it waiting," they said.

* * *

They prepared Quentin in a side chamber off the dais — stripped, washed, dressed in a plain white shift that smelled of cedar and old smoke. An old woman with ink-stained fingers painted lines along their arms and throat in a pigment that tingled like frostbite. She worked in silence, but her hands shook, and when she finished the last line she whispered, so quietly the guards couldn't hear:

"The roots remember mercy. Stand on them."

Then she was gone, and Quentin was alone with the sound of their own blood moving under the painted skin.

They brought Quentin back into the hall at dusk. Candles had been lit — hundreds of them, lining the walls, and their flames all leaned slightly inward, toward the dais, as if drawn by breath. Cassel stood beside the basin in a robe of deep green, reading from a book whose pages looked like bark. Six robed figures — his own hunters, faces bare — took positions around the circle of roots.

Sera's voice was still in Quentin's head. *Trust is a loan.* Bramwen's too: *the land gives back what is given freely.* And underneath both, older than either, Marisol's voice from a dozen ordinary evenings: *Little sprout, the strongest magic I ever knew was knowing which door to walk through.*

This door. This one, chosen with open eyes.

Quentin stepped onto the roots. They were cold as riverbed stone — and then, as the inked lines on their skin began to warm, not cold at all. Something far below shifted, slow as a season turning.

"Lie down," Cassel said.

Quentin lay down in the iron basin. It should have been freezing. It was warm as bathwater, warm as blood, and the last thing they thought before the chanting began was that Marisol had known this basin, or one like it, had known what it was for — and had spent her whole life making sure Quentin would never lie in one.

*I'm sorry,* Quentin thought. *And thank you. Both. Always both.*

The chant deepened. The candle flames bent flat. The script on the rim blazed green, and the green climbed into Quentin's veins like spring climbing a hillside — unstoppable, ancient, vast beyond any imagining they'd had in a night market selling luck-knots for copper.

And somewhere beneath the pain, beneath the light, Quentin felt the Crown begin to wake — and felt, with terrible clarity, that it was waking *them* too, opening doors inside their memory that had been sealed since before they could speak—

The first door opened.

And Quentin fell.
