## 17. What the Crown Shows You

The extraction did not hurt, at first. That was the worst of it.

Quentin had braced for pain—had imagined knives of light, hooks in the marrow, the seed torn out like a tooth. Instead there was only cold, and then a falling sensation without any ground beneath it, and then—

Green.

Not the green of leaves or glass or river water. A green that was older than color, older than the word for it. It opened around them like an eye.

*Come and see,* said something vast and patient and terribly sad. *Since you are here at all, come and see what you have been carrying.*

And Quentin, who had spent twenty-two years learning to look away from their own reflection, looked.

* * *

They stood on a hillside that no longer existed, under a sky the color of unripe plums. The grass came to their waist and every blade hummed faintly, like a struck string still ringing. Below the hill spread a city—white walls, gold roofs, ten thousand chimneys breathing sweet smoke—and beyond the city, fields so fertile they seemed to glow faintly in the dusk, crops standing heavy and ripe though it was high summer's end.

A woman walked up the hill toward them.

She was not beautiful in any way Quentin had words for. She was *plenty*. Her hair moved like water finding its level; her hands were stained to the wrist with soil; her dress was homespun and mended and she wore it the way queens wear crowns. When she smiled at Quentin, the grass around her feet flowered out of season, just because she had passed.

"Hello, little vessel," she said. Her voice was the voice Quentin had heard all their life at the edge of sleep and never dared name. "You're wondering what I am."

"You're the goddess," Quentin said. "The one Marisol—the one they took the Crown from."

"I'm the one who *was*." She sat down in the grass, and the ground received her gladly. "Sit. You'll want to be sitting."

Quentin sat. The earth was warm as a living body.

"The war hadn't started yet," the goddess said, gesturing at the city below. "That's the thing your histories get wrong, if they bother with me at all. There was no war when they came to me. There was only hunger. Mortal mages who wanted what I had—not my power, understand, they didn't want to *be* me—they wanted the *having* of it. The certainty. The way everything grows toward me and never away." She turned her stained hands over. "So they built a crown. Did you know that? Your scholars think the Verdant Crown fell from heaven or grew from some sacred tree. No. Mages forged it. A vessel shaped like sovereignty, because they believed power naturally pools upward, into a single head. They brought it to me and asked me to pour myself in."

"Why would you?"

"Because they asked nicely." She laughed, and the laugh was terrible, because underneath it Quentin could hear weeping. "Because I loved them, child. That was my whole nature. I could not refuse them anything. And because they promised—" her voice caught on something two thousand years old— "they promised they'd only borrow it. To end a famine. One famine. One borrowing."

The hillside shivered. The sky darkened by degrees, like a bruise spreading.

"They took more than one borrowing," Quentin said.

"They took until there was nothing left to take." She held out her hands, and Quentin saw them now as they truly were: not stained with soil but *becoming* soil, the fingers going gray and granular at the tips, crumbling gently even as she spoke. "I poured myself into that circlet of iron and green fire until there wasn't enough of me left to hold a shape. And do you know what the mages did with a dying goddess in a crown? They put it on a king's head and marched him west."

The world tore open.

* * *

War, then. Not described—*lived*, in fragments, the way a drowning person remembers water.

A battlefield where the grass died in a perfect circle around a crowned man, expanding outward with each order he gave, until the dead ground stretched past the horizon and the soldiers began to call him *the Witherer* behind his back and *majesty* to his face.

A tower room where three mages argued over the Crown while it sat on a table between them, humming, and Quentin understood suddenly that the Crown was listening, that it always listened, that every word spoken near it went into it forever. One mage said, *We can still take it off him.* Another said, *He'll kill us.* The third said nothing, and later that third one burned the tower down with the other two inside it, and put the Crown on his own head, and lasted eleven days before his heart gave out mid-sentence, and the men who found him said he was smiling.

A cellar. A woman with Marisol's jaw and none of Marisol's kindness holding the Crown at arm's length, weeping, saying to someone out of sight: *It doesn't want to rule. That's the horror of it. It wants to be *used well*. It will make any hand feel worthy. It made me feel worthy.* Then the cellar again, years later, different torchlight, and the same woman driving an iron spike through her own palm to pin the Crown to a table while she spoke words that unraveled it into seven pieces, and the pieces screaming—

Quentin surfaced, gasping, into the plum-colored nowhere. The goddess—or what remained of her attention—was beside them still.

"Now you've seen the wearing," she said. "Would you like to see the holding? Yours is the longer story."

* * *

The fragments came gentler after that, which somehow made them worse.

A hedge-witch in a fen country, four hundred years gone, who found one piece buried in a peat bank and carried it home wrapped in her apron, meaning to destroy it, and instead lived beside it forty years, talking to it every evening like a husband, growing stranger and kinder and more alone, until the village drowned her for it and the piece sank back into the peat, *satisfied*.

A soldier in the Mage War's last winter who swallowed a splinter of it rather than surrender it, and fought afterward like something not quite human, and whose descendants for six generations were born knowing things they shouldn't, one of whom was a woman named Marisol.

Marisol. Younger than Quentin had ever known her, standing in the ruined chapel with the whole Crown reassembled on the altar before her, and Quentin watched through eyes that were not eyes as she pressed both bleeding palms against it and said: *No. Not again. Not one more head.* And the Crown had answered her—not in words, in *offers*, in visions of Quentin grown tall and wise and gentle, ruling a healed world, and Marisol had wept and refused it, and refused it, and refused it, and finally done the only thing left: she had bound it small, bound it sleeping, bound it into the blood of a newborn nobody would ever look at twice.

Bound it into Quentin.

"It hid itself in you so thoroughly that even I lost track of it," the goddess said quietly. "For twenty-two years you were only a hiding place. But hiding places wake up, little vessel. Every time you used your gift—every luck-knot, every fever-charm—you weren't drawing on yourself. You were letting it breathe. And each breath, it dreamed a little closer to waking."

"That's why the testing," Quentin said. "Cassel wasn't hunting mages. He was sifting for *me*."

"He was sifting for it. You he would have stepped over." Something in the vast sad attention sharpened. "But here is what your Marisol never learned, because she died before she could learn it, and what I can give you now, at the end: the Crown does not corrupt. Everyone says *corrupt*, as if it were poison in wine. It is worse and simpler than that. The Crown *agrees with you*. Whatever you most want, it shows you how the world looks once you have it. It never lies. It only removes the word *no*."

* * *

Then Cassel was there.

Quentin felt him arrive the way you feel a change in weather—a pressure, a wrongness, a great cold intelligence fumbling along the ritual's edges, linked to the extraction whether he willed it or not. He had built this rite, or bought it, or stolen it, and he had assumed he would stand outside it, directing. But the Crown did not recognize directors. The Crown recognized *hands*.

*Lord Aldous Cassel,* it said, warm as a hearth. *Twenty years. Come in. Come and see what you've earned.*

"No," Cassel said, somewhere far away and very close, in the citadel above, in the dark. "I am the operator of this rite. I am not a subject of it."

*Everyone says that first.*

And Quentin, suspended in the green, got to watch what the Crown showed him.

It showed him a garden. Of course it did—it knew what it was doing; it had been doing this since before gardens had names. It showed Lord Cassel a garden in Caerlum where no child had died of the flux in sixty years, where the Gift-Hunt had never been necessary because there had been nothing to hunt, where Sera Vane's sister—*Elira, her name was Elira, he knew it, he'd signed the order himself*—stood alive among the herb beds, laughing at something, forgiven, restored, her name clean in every ledger in the kingdom.

It showed him Marisol alive. It showed him the famine year solved with a gesture. It showed him a throne occupied by no one, because no one needed it, because *he* had arranged the world so wisely that thrones had become unnecessary—and it showed him this with such tenderness, such meticulous loving detail, that Quentin understood with a lurch that the Crown was not tempting Cassel at all.

It was *comforting* him. The way you comfort a man at a funeral. *There, there. None of it was your fault. Look how it could have been.*

"No," Cassel said again, but his voice had changed. It had the texture of a man arguing with his own heartbeat. "This is manipulation. This is the artifact defending itself. I have read the accounts. I know what it does—"

*Yes,* the Crown agreed pleasantly. *You know exactly what it does. That's why you're worthy. Only the worthy resist it, and only the resisters are worthy. Shall I show you the others who said those words?*

And it showed him. All of them. The Witherer smiling on his dead field. The mage smiling in his tower. The fen-witch waving goodbye. Seven centuries of hands, each one certain, each one *reasonable*, each one able to explain precisely why this time the power would be used well, and Quentin watched Cassel watch them, and felt the exact moment the spymaster's certainty developed its first crack—not doubt of the Crown, but doubt of *himself*, which was infinitely more dangerous and infinitely more honest.

"There has to be a way," Cassel whispered. "Twenty years. There has to be a controlled extraction. Bound instruments. Safeguards. If I could simply—study it—"

*Of course,* said the Crown. *Start with a small study. They all start small.*

* * *

Quentin hung in the green between the goddess's fading warmth and the sound of a great man beginning, very quietly, to come apart.

"Is he lost?" Quentin asked.

"Not yet." The goddess's voice was thinner now; the vision was costing what remained of her. "Lost takes years. He's only *opening*. Which means, little vessel, that right now—in this minute—he could still close. Someone could say the true thing to him, plainly, with no power in it at all. Power can't reach him anymore; I've shown him too much. But a plain word might." A pause, and Quentin felt her attention turn on them fully, gravely, for the last time. "Or he might close around the crack and harden. Men do. Then he'll take you apart to fill the hole where his certainty used to be. So decide quickly, and decide *small*: not how to save the world. How to save the next five minutes."

The green began to thin. Through it, Quentin heard—faint, real, outside—the ring of steel, a woman shouting a name that was not Sera Vane's, and the ritual circle shuddering like a door being forced.

*Elira,* the shout said again. *Her name was ELIRA—*

The goddess laughed once, softly, almost fond.

"Oh, *good*," she said. "Someone brought a plain word."

And the last thing Quentin felt, as the vision released them and the extraction chamber rushed back in—Cassel on his knees among his own instruments, the circle guttering, the air full of smoke and the smell of scorched iron—was the seed inside them turning over, wide awake now, and asking, in the goddess's voice:

*Well, little gardener. What will you plant?*
