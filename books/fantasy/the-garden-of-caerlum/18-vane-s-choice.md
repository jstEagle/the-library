## 18. Vane's Choice

The chains broke before Sera understood she had moved.

One moment she was on her knees in the gallery above the ritual floor, iron manacles biting her wrists, watching the extraction circle turn beneath Quentin like a millwheel of light. The next, the manacles were on the stone, split clean through, and her hands were free and bleeding and hers.

She would learn later that it was the Crown itself—loosened from Quentin's body by Cassel's rite, spilling its edges into every soul in the chamber—that had answered the oldest prayer she knew: *let me out.* But in the moment there was no time for learning. There was only the fall.

She went over the gallery rail without deciding to. Twelve feet onto the marble of the ritual floor, rolling as Marisol's old training had taught her—*land with your weight already leaving, Vane, land like you were never here*—and came up inside the outer ring of Cassel's hunters before any of them had finished being surprised.

The chamber was chaos wearing ceremony's clothes. The extraction rite required nine acolytes at nine points around a central dais where Quentin hung half-lifted out of their own body, suspended in a lattice of green fire that Cassel's machines drank and channeled into a vessel of black glass. The vessel sat on a tripod of silver at the circle's heart, filling with light like a jar filling with honey, and Lord Cassel stood before it with his hands raised, his face lit from below, wearing the expression of a man watching God arrive on schedule.

Sera put a knife through the nearest acolyte's ward-anchor and kicked the second one off his mark.

The lattice flickered.

Every head in the chamber turned toward her. She felt the attention of them all like weather, and beneath it, fainter but unmistakable, she felt *Quentin*—a thread of awareness still threaded through the burning scaffold of the rite, thin as a pulse under snow. Still there. Still choosing.

"Vane." Cassel did not lower his hands. His voice carried the mild disappointment he'd once used on her over a desk, twenty years ago, when she'd been young enough to think service meant something. "You were always sentimental. It's why I never advanced you further than I had to."

"You advanced me exactly as far as you needed," Sera said. "Far enough to find things. Never far enough to matter."

"And yet here you are," Cassel said, "mattering."

He gestured—not at her, worse, at the floor—and six hunters moved to close the gap she'd opened in the circle. The lattice steadied. The vessel drank again. On the dais, Quentin made a sound that Sera would spend the rest of her life not describing to anyone.

Her body knew what to do; it had been trained into her by men like these. Break the anchor, break the line, break the caster. She could kill three of them before they brought her down. Maybe four. It wouldn't be enough. The rite only needed one unbroken minute and the Crown would be in the glass, and everything Quentin had bled through Thornhollow's winter to protect would belong to a man who kept ledgers of names the way other men kept ledgers of coin.

So don't fight the circle, said a voice in her head that sounded like Bramwen, calm as well-water. *Hold it open.*

She looked at the lattice. Really looked, for the first time, not as a hunter cataloguing weaknesses but as what she had been before the Hunt had remade her—a girl in a village outside Caerlum whose sister could make bread rise without yeast, who had grown up believing the gift was a garden and not a crime.

The lattice wasn't a cage. That was the trick of it, the thing Marisol must have understood and Bramwen had tried to teach and Quentin had finally learned alone in the dark under the citadel: the Crown didn't want to be held. Every cage built around it became a weapon pointed at whoever built it. Cassel's rite wasn't containing the power. It was standing in front of an open door and calling himself the doorframe.

All it needed was someone willing to stand in the doorway instead—and hold it wide.

"Sera." Quentin's voice came from everywhere and nowhere, from the lattice itself, frayed to threads. "Run. Please. I need you to run."

"I've done a lot of running this winter," Sera said. She pulled the second knife. "I'm bad at it. Ask anyone."

* * *

They came at her in pairs, because she'd trained them and she'd taught them pairs. She gave ground toward the eastern arc of the circle, letting them think they pressed her, and when the first pair committed she dropped low and cut the ward-line at the eighth point with a stroke that took two fingers off the acolyte holding it. He screamed. The lattice lurched. Green fire spat sideways and caught a hunter's cloak, and the man went down slapping at flames that weren't hot—they were *growing*, small white flowers opening along the burn—and Sera felt the whole room flinch back from a power none of them had ever seen do anything gentle.

That was the moment she understood the shape of what she was doing. Not fighting the rite. *Feeding* it. Every break she forced, every acolyte dragged off his mark, widened the channel instead of closing it, because the Crown poured toward whatever opening it was given and Cassel's machine could only drink so fast. She wasn't dismantling the trap. She was turning the trap into a door.

"Hold the ninth point!" Cassel's composure cracked at last, and what showed underneath was older and hungrier. "Hold it or I will have your families' names read aloud in your parishes by morning!"

The ninth-point acolyte held. Of course he did. Fear was the strongest mortar the throne ever mixed.

Sera reached the dais. Up close, Quentin was terrible to see—their body translucent, veins lit green like leaves against sun, their face slack with an effort that was visible as pressure, as a person holding a river shut with their shoulders. Their eyes found her.

"You're supposed to be gone," Quentin whispered.

"Yeah." Sera set her bleeding hand flat against the edge of the lattice. The fire didn't burn her. It *recognized* her—she felt it move through her like spring moving through frozen ground, finding every buried seed of the girl she'd been before, the sister, the liar, the coward, the witness. "Change of plan. Bramwen told me about the Returning. She told me what it needs."

"It needs—" Quentin's breath hitched. "It needs me dead. And it needs no one interfering, and everyone in this room wants to interfere, and I can't hold them all off and pour myself out at the same time—"

"So don't." Sera met their eyes. "Pour. I'll hold the door."

"You'll die."

"Probably." She smiled, and found to her surprise that it wasn't a hard smile to wear. "My sister died in a cell smaller than this room because I signed a paper. I've spent eleven years telling myself the paper was coerced. It was. It's also true that I signed it." Her voice didn't shake, which surprised her most of all. "I'm done paying my debts in words, Quentin. Let me pay this one in stone."

The lattice surged. Across the chamber, Cassel had abandoned dignity entirely—he was shouting the rite forward, driving his acolytes, dragging the vessel closer to the dais with his own gloved hands, because he could feel it too, the door swinging wider than his machine was built for. Black glass filled with light until the light began to leak from the seams, and where it leaked onto the marble, moss grew. In the throne room of the mage-hunters' citadel, moss grew, soft and impossible, and the hunters near it stepped back as if from plague.

"Sera." Quentin again, and now the voice was less frayed, more vast, as if something enormous were speaking through a keyhole. "If you hold it open, you'll feel everything. All of it. What it showed me. The goddess. The war. Every hand."

"I know."

"It doesn't show mercy."

"I'm counting on it," Sera said. "Someone in this city should see it clearly. Might as well be me."

She planted both feet at the northern point of the circle—the empty one, the one whose acolyte she'd broken first—and drove her knife into the seam between the flagstones, and spoke the only words of power she had ever learned, the ones her sister used to whisper over rising bread, the ones the Hunt had beaten out of her vocabulary and never out of her blood:

"*Grow through me.*"

* * *

The world opened.

Later, she would not be able to say how long she stood there. Time went strange inside the lattice, the way it does in fever. She felt the Crown's memory pour through her and did not look away, because looking away was the one thing she had promised herself she wouldn't do.

She saw the goddess—Caerlum's own, older than the city's name, a woman-shaped season walking through forests that had never been cut. She saw the mages come to her in the war's last winter, starving, desperate, asking for enough power to end it, and the goddess giving it because she loved them, because love was the whole of her nature and she had never learned suspicion. She saw them bind her dying body into an artifact rather than let her pass, because grief is greedy, because even good hands want to keep. She saw the artifact end the war in a single night of green fire, and she saw what it cost, and she saw the first hand reach for it afterward, and the second, and the hundredth, each one rotting a little faster than the last.

She saw Marisol, young and terrified, carrying the seed out of a burning archive. She saw Marisol choose a child. She saw twelve years of small kindnesses—fever-charms, luck-knots, copper counted out in the dark—as the shape a great love takes when it has no room left to be great.

And she saw her sister. Not in the Crown's memory—in her own, which the lattice held just as faithfully. Lottie, nineteen years old, flour on her hands, saying *they can't punish the whole village, Sera, they're not monsters,* and Sera, twenty-two and newly sworn, saying nothing, which was its own signature.

The lattice held her up. That was the miracle of it, the thing she would carry out of this room if she carried anything: it didn't ask her to be worthy. It asked her to stay. Everything else—the grief, the guilt, the seeing—it carried for her, the way soil carries whatever is planted in it, without judgment, without refusal.

Across the chamber, Cassel screamed.

She heard it distantly, through the roar of the pouring power. He had linked himself to the rite to force its pace, and now the Crown's memory was in him too, and he was discovering what every grasping hand before him had discovered: that the Crown shows its owner the truth, and the truth is that ownership is the rot. He was seeing his twenty-year hunt laid bare—not justice, not restoration of order, just appetite wearing justice's coat, ledger after ledger of names traded like coin, and at the bottom of the last ledger his own name, written in a hand that shook.

Good, Sera thought, and was not ashamed of it.

Quentin's voice rose through the lattice, no longer a keyhole voice but a full one, doubled and trebled, human and land speaking together: "*It belongs to no one. It belongs to everyone. It goes home.*"

The vessel of black glass shattered.

Not broke—*shattered*, burst outward into a thousand fragments that hung in the air a heartbeat and then fell as seeds, as ash, as rain, and the green fire that had been caged in it came loose all at once, a flood with the doors finally, finally open, and Sera held her stance and held her arms wide and *held the door.*

The flood went through her.

It did not hurt. That was the last thing she expected and the truest thing she learned: it went through her the way spring goes through a field, taking nothing, waking everything. It passed down through the marble, through the citadel's foundations, down into the bedrock of Caerlum itself, and she felt it go—felt the land receive it the way dry ground receives the first thaw, greedily, gratefully, with a sound too deep for ears, a sound like a held breath released after a hundred years.

The hunters fled. She barely noticed. The acolytes fled. The moss spread across the marble in rivers. Somewhere above, the citadel's ancient stones groaned and settled, roots pushing up through cellars that hadn't seen growth since the Mage War, and the whole vast rotten edifice of the Hunt's power shuddered on foundations that no longer belonged to it.

Cassel did not flee. She saw him at the last, through the blaze—still reaching, both hands outstretched toward the pouring light, refusing to the final instant to take back his grasp. The light took him instead. There was no scream at the end. There was a sound like a tree falling in a forest where no one is left to hear it, and then there was a shape of green-veined stone where the royal spymaster had stood, and then, slowly, flowers.

Lilies. White ones. Growing out of what he'd become.

Sera's knees gave. She went down onto the wet marble among the moss and the lilies and the shattered glass-seeds, and the lattice closed gently around her like a hand releasing a bird, and the last of the Crown's light sank into the earth beneath the city and was gone—everywhere now, and nowhere, and no one's.

In the center of the dais, Quentin Joubert lay still.

Too still. The translucence was fading, and what it faded toward was gray.

"No," Sera said, and crawled to them, and gathered them up, and found no pulse, and said it again, louder, to the room, to the land, to whatever had just passed through her and gone home: "No—you don't get to—*you don't get to leave like this—*"

The ground beneath the citadel breathed.

She felt it—one long, slow inhalation, rising up through the marble, through her knees, through Quentin's still body—and with it came a scent she knew from her sister's kitchen, from Thornhollow's greenhouses, from every spring of her childhood: thaw. Loam. The first green push of something that has decided, against all evidence, to grow.

Under her hands, Quentin's chest rose.

Once. Shallow. Then again, deeper, and color came back into their lips like dawn coming into a valley, and their eyes opened—not quite the eyes she remembered, something older in them now, something patient, the eyes of a person who has heard the land's slow voice and will hear it forever—but theirs, still theirs, still squinting up at her with the ghost of the market-stall charm-seller's wry exhaustion.

"Told you," Quentin rasped. "Told you to run."

Sera laughed, or sobbed, or both, and pressed her forehead to theirs, and above them the ruined ceiling of the throne room cracked open along an old fault, and through it, impossibly, out of season, out of reason, out of the deepest winter Caerlum had known in a century—

—snowmelt dripped. And where it dripped, grass grew.

She got Quentin upright, an arm across her shoulders, and they stood together in the green-fire gutted hall, two liars, two survivors, surrounded by lilies and ruin and the smell of spring.

"Thornhollow," Sera said. "Bramwen. Odile. Everyone Cassel locked up. They're still in the cells below."

Quentin looked toward the stairwell, and the green light that lived in the stones now—everyone's light, no one's light—flickered once, like a nod.

"Then we'd better hurry," they said. "I think the locks are going to open on their own. And I think we should be there when they do—to tell them what happened. Before the stories start telling themselves."

From far below, faint through the roots that were even now splitting the citadel's deepest walls, came a sound neither of them could mistake: iron, giving way. Cell doors, opening.
