## 19. The Returning

The ritual circle was a wound in the floor.

Quentin saw it that way from the moment the hunters dragged them into the heart of Cassel's citadel—a great ring of silver inlay set into black stone, older than the throne above it, older than the city itself. Someone had built this room around the Crown's absence. Someone had been waiting a very long time.

Chains of cold iron held their wrists. Runes crawled up their forearms where the blood had been drawn, drinking, mapping, learning the shape of what lived inside them. Around the circle stood nine robed figures—Cassel's extracted choir, faces hidden, voices already beginning a low drone that Quentin felt in their teeth.

And at the circle's edge, on her knees between two guards, Sera Vane was screaming Quentin's name.

*Hold on,* Quentin thought at her. *Hold on, hold on—*

The thought had no words behind it. Only the seed, awake now, turning over in its sleep beneath their sternum like something vast remembering it had a body.

Lord Aldous Cassel stepped into the light.

He looked smaller than he had in Thornhollow. Smaller and older and more human, which somehow made him worse. His coat was immaculate. His hands were steady. He carried a bowl of clear water as if it were a chalice, and when he spoke, his voice was almost gentle.

"You could have come to me years ago," he said. "You could have spared us all this."

"Us," Quentin repeated. Their voice came out cracked. "There's no *us*, my lord. There never was."

"There is now." He crouched to their level, and for one obscene moment his eyes were kind. "I have read every account of the Mage War. I have read what the Crown did to the men who wore it—the way it burned through them like fire through paper. I am not a fool, Joubert. I don't intend to *wear* it. I intend to study it. Contain it. Make sure no one ever holds that power again—not a goddess, not a king, not you." He straightened. "That is not tyranny. That is the only mercy left."

"You buried Marisol."

"I questioned Marisol." Something flickered across his face—grief, or its well-practiced imitation. "She refused to cooperate for eleven days. On the twelfth, she stopped breathing. I did not touch her." He turned away. "Begin."

The choir's drone deepened. The runes on Quentin's arms began to burn.

* * *

It started as cold.

Not the cold of winter—that was a surface thing, a skin-thing. This cold came up from underneath, from the silver ring, from whatever engine Cassel had spent twenty years building beneath his beautiful city. It reached into Quentin's chest and closed around the seed like a fist around a lantern.

And the seed *fought*.

Green light bled through the seams of Quentin's skin—at the wrists, at the throat, along the old scar over their ribs where Marisol's charm-knife had slipped twelve years ago. The chains smoked. One of the choir screamed and fell back, hands blistered. The circle held anyway. Of course it held. It had been built to hold.

Quentin felt themselves being *opened*.

Not torn—worse than torn. Unpicked. Stitch by stitch, the rite found the boundary of what they were and began to loosen it, and everything they had ever been spilled toward the light: the night market, the luck-knots, Odile's laugh, the fever-charm that worked too well, Marisol's hands folding theirs around a sprig of rosemary, *this is how you hide, little root, this is how you stay small and safe*—

And then the Crown showed them what lay beneath all of it.

* * *

They were standing in a field that had been green before there were fields.

A woman knelt in the soil with her hands buried to the wrist, and she was dying, and she was enormous—her hair was rivers, her breath was weather—and she was looking at Quentin with eyes the color of new leaves.

*I am the last of nine,* she said, without sound. *We made the world by accident. We will unmake each other on purpose. Take what's left of me. Hide it. Bury it small.*

"Who are you?" Quentin asked, and their voice was a child's voice.

*Does it matter?* The goddess smiled, and the smile was tired beyond endurance. *Everyone who takes me becomes me, briefly. And then they become less. Watch.*

The field changed. A king in bronze, crowned in green fire, laughing as his armies marched under a banner of living vines—and then the vines turning inward, drinking him, and the laughter going wrong, and the field going gray. Another figure. Another. A queen who wept as she burned a forest to prove she could. A hedge-witch who only wanted rain for her village and took too much and drowned it. Every hand that grasped. Every hand that rotted.

*Do you understand yet?* the goddess said. *I am not a gift. I am not a weapon. I am a hunger wearing the shape of a garden. They will tell you otherwise. They will always tell you otherwise.*

"I'm not going to use you," Quentin said. "I'm going to give you back."

The goddess went still.

For a long moment the green field held its breath. Then she laughed—a real laugh, cracked and astonished, the first true thing in this whole buried memory.

*Give me back.* She shook her head, rivers moving in her hair. *Twelve of them tried to take me. One tried to spend me. You would be the first to return me.* Her eyes sharpened, and suddenly they were not kind at all. *Do you know what returning costs?*

"I die."

*You die.* No flinching in it. *And the land takes you. All of you. You will hear roots growing for the rest of your death. You will feel every season forever, in a body that is no longer only yours. Is that a price you can pay, little root?*

Quentin thought of Sera on her knees, screaming their name. Thought of Bramwen's failed rite, and the grave she'd dug for herself and climbed out of. Thought of Odile in a cell somewhere, and the boy healed in the market mud, and Marisol spending eleven days refusing to speak so that this—*this exact moment*—could stay possible.

"Yes," Quentin said.

*Then do it quickly,* said the goddess, *before you're afraid enough to stop.*

* * *

The memory released them and the rite was halfway done.

Cassel stood at the circle's rim with both hands raised, and his face—his careful, controlled, twenty-years-patient face—was coming apart. Because the rite ran both ways. The choir had linked him to the extraction, and the extraction had opened Quentin's memories into his, and Lord Aldous Cassel, spymaster of Caerlum, architect of the Gift-Hunt, was seeing the field of green and the dying goddess and every rotting hand, and he was seeing *himself* in the procession—he was seeing himself reach—

"No," he whispered. "No. I'm not like them. I want to *contain* it—"

The seed answered him.

It surged up through the loosened stitches of Quentin's self, half-extracted now, half-free, and it met Cassel's grasping will the way the sea meets a man who walks in determined to own it. He had built a machine to hold it. He had not built a machine to hold *himself*. The green light poured into the channels he'd opened and filled them, and kept filling, and Cassel's scream joined the choir's drone and then outlasted it.

His coat smoldered. His shadow grew leaves.

"Let go!" Quentin shouted at him—actually shouted, actually begging. "Let go and live!"

But letting go was the one thing he had never learned. Twenty years of wanting had grown into his bones like roots into stone, and when the power finally pulled, he pulled *back*, and the Crown—patient, ancient, honest about what it was—took him at his word.

He came apart quietly, in the end. Not burned. *Unmade*—unraveling outward into threads of green and gold that drifted up past the vaulted ceiling and did not come down. The last expression on Lord Aldous Cassel's face was not fear. It was wonder, terrible and total, the look of a man who finally holds exactly what he wanted.

Then the choir broke and ran, and the circle's silver began to crack, and Quentin was alone inside their own unraveling body with a goddess's hunger in their hands.

*Sera,* they thought. *Sera, get out. Get out now.*

Sera Vane did not get out.

* * *

She had broken free in the chaos—one guard down, a knife taken, blood on her sleeve that might have been hers. She should have run. Every instinct she'd honed in ten years of hunting told her to run, to get clear, to survive and testify and bury her sister's name under a respectable career.

Instead she crossed the cracking circle and put herself in the gap where a rune had failed, and she pressed her bare palms against the silver, and she *held it open*.

"I've got the circle!" she screamed at Quentin. "Whatever you're doing—do it!"

"You'll burn—"

"I know what I'll do!" Her grin was wild and wet-eyed and absolutely certain. "My sister's name was Liora! Say it! Everyone in this rotten city will say it! *Liora Vane!*"

The name went into the circle like a key.

And Quentin stopped fighting the rite and started *steering* it.

This was the part Marisol had never known, the part even the goddess hadn't shown plainly—because it couldn't be taught, only chosen. The Crown wanted out. It had always wanted out; it had been buried alive for a thousand years, hidden small, kept safe, passed hand to grasping hand like a prisoner moved between cells. Quentin was its last cell. And now, with the rite tearing the walls down anyway, Quentin turned the breach downward—through the citadel's foundations, through the bedrock, through the frozen roots of Caerlum itself.

*Take it,* they told the land. *All of it. Not a vessel. Not a crown. Just—rain. Just spring. Just yours.*

They let go of everything.

It did not feel like dying. It felt like being *poured*. Every stitch of self unpicked at once, and instead of spilling into a silver ring, they spilled into the dark earth below the city—into aqueduct and cellar and grave and field, into the frost-bound veins of a whole country waiting under snow. The green fire followed them, racing outward through the foundations in branching rivers, up through stone, through soil, through the frozen gardens of Thornhollow miles away, through the parish wells where neighbors had denounced their neighbors, through the prison where Odile slept, through everything, everywhere, patient as water, gentle as rot, absolute as spring.

The last thing Quentin knew was Sera's hands leaving the circle to catch them as they fell.

The last thing they heard was the goddess, everywhere now, laughing like thaw.

* * *

Green fire stood in the snowfields for three days.

They said afterward—you could see it from the walls of Caerlum, from the passes, from the fishing boats far out in the gray water—that the whole land glowed under the white, as if something enormous had turned over in its sleep and dreamed of summer. Rivers unlocked. Buds split bare branches in midwinter. In the citadel's deepest vault, where the ritual room had collapsed into a garden of silver shards and flowering moss, the hunters found no body of Lord Cassel, only a tree growing through the throne-room floor, young and green and impossible, its roots wrapped around a signet ring it had grown through and past.

They found Quentin where Sera had caught them: not breathing, not bleeding, heart stilled like a clock run down, held in the arms of a disgraced hunter who refused to let anyone else touch them and refused, with equal ferocity, to believe.

"Come back," she said, to the body, to the ground, to whatever was listening. "You don't get to give everyone else spring and skip it yourself. Come *back*."

The land heard her.

And somewhere beneath the citadel, beneath the city, beneath the long white silence of the world, something that had been Quentin Joubert turned over—slowly, the way seasons turn—and began, very far down, to grow toward the light.
