## 20. Spring Out of Season

Quentin woke to the smell of turned earth and the sound of someone crying two rooms away.

Both arrived at once, without distance. The crying was in the next house—Bramwen's granddaughter, weeping over a burned greenhouse—and Quentin heard it as clearly as if the girl were kneeling beside the bed. And beneath it, deeper, slower, came another sound entirely: a voice like water moving under ice, patient and vast, saying nothing that could be called words but everything that could be called *here*.

*Here,* said the land. *Here. Here.*

Quentin opened their eyes.

The ceiling above them was Thornhollow timber, smoke-darkened beams, a crack running from the chimney corner like a river on a map. Someone had built the fire up high; the room was warm. Their body felt like a borrowed coat—present, serviceable, not quite theirs. When they flexed their fingers, they felt not only the fingers but the grain of the wood in the floorboards below, the frost still locked in the shaded north side of the house, the roots of the old oak out back turning over in their sleep.

They remembered dying.

Not the way you remember a dream. The way you remember a door you walked through. Green fire pouring out of them through the citadel's foundations, down through stone and clay and frozen loam, spreading through Caerlum like dawn through a valley. The last thing they had felt was Sera's hand on their face, and her voice—rough, furious, alive—saying their name over and over as though repetition could anchor a soul to a body that was emptying itself into the ground.

Then nothing. Then this.

They sat up too fast and the room tilted—not the room, their perception of it, because for one vertiginous moment they perceived it from four directions at once, from the rafters and the root-cellar and the well and the ridge above the village. They gripped the blanket until the world agreed to be small again.

"You're awake." Bramwen stood in the doorway with a bowl in her hands, steam rising off it. She looked older than she had nine days ago—or perhaps Quentin simply saw her differently now, saw the years in her the way they saw the rings in the oak, layered and legible. "You've been gone a long while."

"How long?"

"Nine days. We buried you once already, did you know that? Dug the grave and everything. Bramwen's stubbornness kept it open." She set the bowl on the stool beside the bed. Barley broth. A sprig of something green floating in it that had no business being green in midwinter. "Sera sat with you three days and nights before Cassel's prisoners needed tending and I made her go. She'll deny she cried. Don't let her."

"Is she—"

"Alive. Loud. Insufferable with relief." Bramwen lowered herself onto the edge of the bed, joints cracking like kindling. She studied Quentin's face with an expression they couldn't read, which was itself strange; since waking, they had found most faces easy to read, the way you read weather. "What do you remember?"

"Dying."

"And after?"

Quentin hesitated. How to describe it—the long dark that hadn't been dark, the sense of being poured out and yet remaining, of becoming the thing poured into? "I heard the land," they said finally. "I think—I think I'm still hearing it."

Bramwen nodded slowly, unsurprised, and that unsurprise frightened them more than anything else.

"I performed the Returning once before," she said. "Forty years ago. A girl named Ede, whose mother carried a splinter of the Crown's light the way Marisol's line carried yours. It didn't take. Or rather—it took her, all of her, and gave nothing back. I've spent forty years wondering what I did wrong." Her eyes were wet and steady. "Marisol knew, you understand. That's why she bound the seed into you instead of leaving it loose. A vessel can survive the Returning. A vessel is *made* to give something up. But no one has ever come back from it whole, and no one has ever come back unchanged. So tell me true, child: what are you now?"

Quentin reached for an answer and the land answered first—a slow pulse through the soles of their feet, through the bed frame, through the frozen ground beneath the house: *here, here, here.*

"I don't know," they said. "But I'm here."

* * *

Walking was a revelation and an ordeal.

Their legs worked. That was the ordeal part—nine days of stillness had left them weak as a foal, and Sera caught their elbow twice on the way down the stairs, saying nothing either time, just steadying them with a grip that lingered a half-second longer than balance required.

The revelation was everything else.

Thornhollow in winter should have been gray and silent, snow-muffled, smoke-stilled. It was those things. But underneath, Quentin heard—or felt, or knew—the village the way they'd once known their own heartbeat: the hens' dim animal contentment in the coop, the frost heaving and settling in the lane like a sleeping thing breathing, the apple trees at the village edge dreaming their tight brown buds toward spring with a patience that made human longing look frantic. The land's voice wasn't language. It was older than language. It was the hum beneath a held note.

And threaded through it, faint but unmistakable, came other voices. Small ones. Human ones.

The baker's boy, hauling water, humming a tune that bent strangely at the edges—bent, Quentin realized, because the tune was doing something, a tiny working, warming his hands against the cold without knowing he was doing it. Old Tam the smith, muttering over his forge, and the fire leaning toward him like a dog toward its master. Bramwen's granddaughter, still sniffling by the ruined greenhouse, pressing her palm flat against a scorched beam—and where her palm rested, a thread of green moving under the char, tentative as a pulse.

Magic. Not hunted, not hoarded, not owned. Just—stirring. Rising in ordinary people like groundwater after rain.

"It started three days ago," Sera said quietly. She'd fallen into step beside them, arms crossed against the cold, watching Quentin watch the world. "Bramwen calls it the seeping. Kids doing things they shouldn't be able to do. Small things. A woman in the lower houses mended a cracked jar by holding it. Nobody taught them. Nobody *could* teach them—they're just doing it, like breathing." She glanced sideways. "It's you. What you poured out didn't die. It went into everything."

Quentin stopped walking. The lane stretched ahead of them, snow-scabbed, ordinary, and beneath it the land hummed its slow syllable, and inside that hum they could feel themselves—not a self standing *on* the land but a self woven *through* it, one bright thread among roots and frost and sleeping seed.

"Everyone," they said. "It's in everyone."

"In everyone near enough to feel it, anyway. Which is why—" Sera stopped. Her jaw worked. Quentin watched her decide to say the thing plainly, because that was Sera: she lied with documents, never with her face. "Which is why you need to hear the rest before you walk into the square looking like Lazarus. Riders came yesterday. Not Cassel's—he's ash, everyone knows that much, whatever happened in that citadel. These wore no colors. A herald and six swords, from the capital. They're going village to village asking about 'the event at Lord Cassel's seat.' Asking who survived. Asking—" her voice flattened, "—about a person of unknown description seen entering the ritual chamber."

"The Hunt is dissolved. You told me—"

"The Hunt is dissolved. The throne isn't. A dead spymaster with a burned citadel and a story that won't hold—that's not an ending, Quentin, that's a question, and questions make kings nervous." She looked at them then, fully, and Quentin saw what the land had already shown them: exhaustion down to the marrow, grief worn smooth by use, and underneath it all a stubborn, banked tenderness she'd have denied under torture. "You're not hunted anymore. But you're not *safe*. There's a difference, and it's thinner than you'd like."

* * *

The village gathered in Bramwen's hall that evening, and Quentin learned what rebirth looks like when it isn't triumphant.

It looks like barley soup and hard bread. It looks like Odile—Odile, freed from Cassel's cells along with the other taken charm-sellers, arriving two days ago on a requisitioned cart, thinner and grayer and swearing with undiminished artistry—sitting across the table and staring at Quentin like a woman auditing a miracle. It looks like old Tam asking, awkwardly, whether the warmth in his hands was going to fade, and looking almost disappointed when Bramwen said probably not. It looks like children peeking around doorframes at the person the grown-ups keep calling *the Returned*, and Quentin catching one small girl's eye and feeling, instantly and completely, the child's whole uncomplicated wonder, and having to leave the table for a moment because being *seen* like that—without concealment, without cost—was harder than dying had been.

"They want to know what you are," Odile said, when the hall had emptied to embers and the three of them—Quentin, Sera, Bramwen—sat in the quiet. "Officially. For when the riders come back. Are you a saint? A weapon? A new Cassel waiting for her crown?" She turned her cup. "And don't tell me none of the above, because I sold charms for thirty years and I know a loaded question when I hear one, even when I'm the one loading it."

Quentin considered the fire. Through the floorboards, through the foundation stones, through the frozen earth, the land murmured its patience. *Here. Here.* Somewhere south, Caerlum's ten thousand chimneys breathed smoke into the dark, and beneath the city they could feel the Crown's gift lying in the soil like seed-corn, waiting on a spring that had come early and would keep coming early, year upon year, a small permanent alteration in the world's arithmetic.

"I'm a gardener," they said at last. "That's all I want to be. The power isn't mine. I don't want it back, I couldn't hold it if I tried, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something." They looked up at Odile, and then at Sera, and then at Bramwen. "But I can't promise the riders a category that makes them comfortable. I can't promise them anything. That's the truth of it. I died to make sure no one would ever own this again—including me. If the throne needs a word for that, they'll have to invent one."

"Sovereign nuisance," Sera offered.

"Public vegetable," said Odile.

Despite everything—despite the riders on the road and the grave that had been dug and refilled and the strange new ears full of the earth's slow voice—Quentin laughed. It came out rusty and real, and the fire leaned toward the sound, and outside, under the snow, the apple trees turned over in their sleep.

* * *

Later, alone, Quentin walked out past the ruined greenhouses to the chapel path, because there was something they had to check, and they were afraid to check it.

Marisol.

Through the Returning, through the Crown's vast memory, they had touched every hand that ever grasped the artifact—and somewhere in that torrent, briefly, they had felt a presence they couldn't name, a hedge-witch's stubborn warmth, there and gone. Since waking they had told themselves it was nothing. Grief dressing itself as hope.

They knelt at the edge of the path and pressed both palms flat to the frozen ground, and opened themselves the way you open a window, and listened.

The land spoke. Frost and root and stone and the deep slow *here*. And threaded within it, faint as a name on a worn gravestone, they found her—not Marisol's soul, nothing so simple, nothing so cruel or so kind—but her work. Twelve years of careful binding, of charms laid into a child's blood like stitches into cloth. The shape of her hands was still in it. The shape of her love was still in it, worked so deep into Quentin's making that the Returning itself had carried it down into the soil, so that now, when the land said *here*, it said it partly in Marisol's voice.

She wasn't there. But she was everywhere Quentin was.

Quentin knelt in the snow a long time, and the snow did not feel cold, and when they finally rose, they understood the change in themselves at last—not the power, not the hearing, but the simpler thing underneath. All their life they had been a hiding place. A box with a secret in it. Now they were a field. Open, exposed, belonging to whatever weather came.

It should have terrified them. Some of it did.

But the rest—the larger part, the part that surprised them—felt like setting down a weight they'd forgotten they were carrying.

Behind them, boots crunched on the path. Sera, wrapped in a borrowed cloak, breath steaming, stopping a few paces off with the particular stiffness of someone who has rehearsed a speech and intends to deliver it.

"The riders reach the crossroads tomorrow," she said. "There's a herald named Corvane—I know him, he owes me, and more to the point he *fears* me, which lasts longer. I can meet him at the crossroads and shape the story before it shapes us. But I'll need to know exactly what to tell him you are." She paused. "So. One more time, for the record. What do I call you?"

Quentin looked south, toward Caerlum, where spring was coming out of season through frozen streets, where ten thousand people were about to discover small impossible warmths in their own hands, where the throne's riders were already asking questions no honest answer would satisfy.

They thought of Marisol's garden. Of seeds. Of names.

"Tell him," Quentin said, "that the garden is awake. And that it remembers who tended it—and who burned it."

Far off at the crossroads, a lantern swung, and the land felt the footsteps of seven horses, and whispered their weight to Quentin like gossip.
