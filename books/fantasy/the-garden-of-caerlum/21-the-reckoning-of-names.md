## 21. The Reckoning of Names

The tribunal met in the Hall of Ledgers, because that was where Cassel had kept his files, and there was a certain justice in it that Sera Vane did not find funny so much as necessary. The room where twenty years of names had been sorted into *harmless* and *useful* and *burn* now held three magistrates, a clerk with ink-stained fingers, and Sera standing at the witness table with a stack of confiscated papers taller than her forearm.

She had slept four hours in two days. It didn't matter. She had spent years learning to function on less, back when functioning meant pretending.

"State your name and former office for the record," said the senior magistrate, a dry woman named Corvel who had once signed off on Sera's sister's arrest warrant and had not, as far as Sera could tell, apologized to anyone in her life.

"Sera Vane. Formerly First Hunter of the Gift-Hunt, Caerlum district."

"And you come before this tribunal voluntarily?"

"I come before it armed," Sera said. "With documents. Which is worse for everyone involved."

Corvel's mouth twitched. The clerk wrote something down.

Sera had thought about how to do this for a long time — through the long walk out of Cassel's citadel, through the days when Quentin lay green-veined and barely breathing in Bramwen's guest room, through the ride back down the pass when the snow was still melting in ways no almanac could explain. She had considered simply burning the archives. There was an appeal to it. Fire was clean; fire didn't ask her to say things aloud.

But burning the archives would have burned Odile's name too, and the names of every charm-seller and hedge-healer and quiet village granny who'd ever been entered into Cassel's ledgers under *asset*, *suspect*, or *pending*. You couldn't repair people by erasing the paper trail of what had been done to them. So she had carried the ledgers out herself, crate by crate, and put them on the tribunal's table like a woman laying out a body.

"The Hunt did not operate on conviction," she said. "I want that understood first. It operated on inventory. Lord Cassel kept lists of every person in this city with even a trace of the gift, going back decades. Most were never arrested. They were *filed*. Watched. Leverage collected against them — debts, illnesses, family members who could be pressured. When he needed a hunter to meet a quota, he pulled a name. When he needed an informant, he pulled a name. When he needed someone to disappear quietly, he pulled a name."

The clerk's pen scratched. Corvel said, "You're describing systematic extortion by the Crown's own spymaster."

"I'm describing Tuesday," Sera said. "For twenty years."

She opened the first ledger. Her hands were steady. That surprised her; she had expected them to shake. But it turned out that saying true things in a room full of officials was easier than saying false ones had ever been.

"This page is the Vane entry. My sister, Lira Vane, listed here as *confirmed gift-bearer, minor craft, non-threatening*. Note the assessment. Non-threatening. She made bread rise faster and could find lost buttons. Someone in this building decided that was worth a warrant anyway, and I want to know whose signature is at the bottom."

The clerk turned the ledger around. His face went carefully blank.

"That's not Lord Cassel's hand," Sera said. "Whose is it?"

Silence, of the kind that has weight.

"Read the name," Corvel said quietly. "Or we will."

The clerk read it. It belonged to a magistrate who was, at that moment, sitting on the tribunal itself — an old man named Halloway who had been very kind to Sera during the proceedings, who had brought her tea on the second day, who had told her that what she was doing took courage.

Halloway stood up. He looked at Sera, and whatever he saw in her face made him stop composing his defense halfway through the first sentence.

"I'll wait outside," he said, "for the constables."

He left. Nobody stopped him. Sera found she felt nothing at all about it, which she suspected was its own kind of wound, and which she decided she could examine some other year, when there was time.

* * *

They freed Odile on the fourth day of testimony.

Sera went to the holding cells beneath the Hunter's Hall personally, because she did not trust the process to anyone else, and because she owed the woman a debt she intended to pay in person. The cell block smelled of wet stone and old straw. Odile sat on the bunk with her arms crossed, silver rings dull with grime, looking exactly as unimpressed by imprisonment as she had been by everything else Sera had ever seen her do.

"Well," Odile said. "The famous traitor-hunter. Come to gloat?"

"Come to open the door."

"You'll forgive me if I don't weep with gratitude. Your colleagues took my stock, my stall, and six weeks of my life. One of them broke my lucky knot-charm. Do you know how long it takes to tie one of those properly? Three hours. And it has to be done on a Thursday."

"It's Thursday," Sera said.

Odile blinked. Then, grudgingly: "Huh."

Sera unlocked the cell. Odile walked past her, then stopped in the corridor, and for a moment the armor slipped and Sera saw underneath it — a tired woman in her fifties who had spent six weeks not knowing if she'd hang.

"They told me you'd turned," Odile said. "In here. The guards. They said Vane went over to the witches, that she helped burn the citadel, that she's the reason the spymaster's dead." She turned. "Is it true? All of it?"

"Some of it. I never burned anything. That was—" Sera paused. "That was Quentin."

"The luck-knot boy." Odile snorted. "I sold that child their first charm kit. Charged them double, too, they were so obviously desperate." Her voice cracked slightly on the last word, and she covered it by walking again, fast, toward the light at the end of the corridor. "Where are my people? The others taken at Ash Wednesday market?"

"Being released today. All of them. Records expunged. Compensation from seized Hunt funds — it's in the tribunal's ruling, I made sure of it."

"*You* made sure of it."

"I know how Cassel worked. He starved people slowly and called it mercy. If the city just opens the cells and shrugs, nothing's repaired. People need their stalls back. Their names cleared. Their Thursday charms re-tied." Sera held the door at the top of the stairs. Grey daylight fell across the threshold. "It's not enough. I know it's not enough. But it's a start, and starts are all I've got."

Odile stepped out into the daylight, squinting. Then she reached up, took one of her silver rings off her finger, and pressed it into Sera's palm.

"For luck," she said. "And don't argue, it's rude. You look like a woman who could use some."

* * *

Quentin came down from Thornhollow for the last day of testimony, because Sera asked, and because there was a question only they could answer.

They arrived quietly — no escort, no announcement, wearing a plain brown coat and carrying a satchel of seeds, because Bramwen had sent them with seeds for the chapel garden and Quentin saw no reason to make two trips. The city had changed in the months since the Returning. Not dramatically. That was the thing people kept getting wrong, in the pamphlets and the street-corner sermons: they expected transformation, green fire, miracles on demand. What they got instead was earlier springs. Wells that stayed sweet. A stubbornness in the soil that hadn't been there before, gardens growing through cracks in flagstones, orchards fruiting a month ahead of schedule. Magic, loose in the world, doing small patient work.

And everywhere Quentin walked, people stared.

They tried not to. Quentin could feel them trying — the averted eyes, the sudden intense interest in shop windows. But word had traveled, as word does, distorted as it goes: *the vessel of the Verdant Crown, the one who killed the spymaster, the one who died and came back.* Some of them knelt. One woman in the fish market had actually pressed her forehead to the cobbles as Quentin passed, and Quentin had stood there mortified, holding a satchel of turnip seeds, unable to think of a single thing to say.

At the Hall of Ledgers, Sera met them at the door.

"You look terrible," Quentin said.

"You look like springtime. We can't all be seasonal phenomena." But she was smiling, tiredly, and she gripped their shoulder once, hard, which from Sera was roughly equivalent to an embrace. "They're going to ask you questions. You don't have to answer any of them. Remember that. You walked into a citadel and unmade a tyrant; you owe these people nothing."

"I know."

"Do you?" She studied them. "Because half the city wants to crown you, and the other half wants to canonize you, and I've watched you flinch from both, and I want to hear you say it in there. In front of the magistrates. Because they'll ask, Quentin. They'll ask what you intend to *do* with what you are. And whatever you answer will be quoted for a hundred years."

Inside, the tribunal chamber was fuller than before. Word had gotten out. Magistrates, clerks, observers crammed along the walls, and at the witness table a chair had been set with almost ceremonial care, as if the furniture itself expected a queen.

Quentin sat down in it like a person waiting for a delayed coach.

Magistrate Corvel presided. "Quentin Joubert. You are not accused of anything. Let the record show that clearly. You are asked to speak because you are the only living person who witnessed the events inside Cassel's citadel, and because—" she hesitated, the first hesitation Sera had ever seen from her, "—because the city needs to hear from you directly. There are many claims circulating. That you carry the Verdant Crown still. That you intend to rule. That magic should be restored to the old bloodlines, with yourself at their head. That you are a god returned." She folded her hands. "We would prefer facts."

"Facts," Quentin said. "All right."

They told it plainly. The seed Marisol had bound into them at birth. The flight, the ambush, the winter, the choice. The rite of Returning, and what it cost — they did not dress it up; they described dying, briefly, in a burst of green light, and the room went very still. And then they described waking up changed, hearing the land's slow voice, feeling seasons move in their bones.

"So the Crown's power remains in you," said one of the junior magistrates, leaning forward. "Distributed, perhaps, but centered. You are its vessel still. Its— administrator, shall we say. Which raises the question of governance. Who governs the gift, if not you?"

"No one," Quentin said.

A murmur ran the length of the room.

"No one governs it," Quentin repeated. "That's the entire point. That's what I learned inside the Crown's memory — I saw every hand that ever held it. A goddess tore it out of herself to end a war, and within a generation men were killing each other over who got to wear it. Kings. Archmages. Spymasters. Every single one of them started by believing they'd use it well. *This time*, they all thought. *By me.*" They shook their head. "Power that belongs to one person gets used for that person, eventually. Always. The only way to keep it from rotting is to make sure it doesn't belong to anyone."

"Then what do you propose?" Corvel asked. "Anarchy? Every hedge-witch and charm-seller free to work whatever craft they please, with no oversight, no law?"

"I propose what already happened," Quentin said. "Marisol taught me charms for copper. Odile tied luck-knots. Bramwen's village kept the old rites alive for two hundred years without conquering anything. Ordinary people doing small magic for their neighbors — that's been happening the whole time, under the fear, and nobody was hurt by it except by the Hunt that feared it. The gift isn't a throne. It's a garden. You don't appoint a king over a garden. You tend it, and you share what grows."

The junior magistrate frowned. "And if someone misuses it? If some future Cassel rises, or some hedge-mage decides to make himself a tyrant with the power now loose in the land?"

"Then you deal with him the way communities have always dealt with harmful people," Quentin said. "Together. Not with a machine built to test everyone's blood and file them away. I understand the temptation — believe me, I understand it better than anyone in this room. I *am* the temptation. Put me on a throne and I could probably make the crops never fail and the winters stay mild and the wars stop. I could also become the thing Marisol died protecting the world from, and I wouldn't even notice it happening, because that's how it always happens. Slowly. Kindly. For everyone's good." They looked around the chamber. "So no. I won't be your prophet. I won't be your queen. I'm going back to Thornhollow tomorrow to plant a herb garden, and if the city wants my advice on magic, it can send a letter, and I'll answer it the same week, weather permitting."

Silence. Then Corvel, dry as ever: "Weather permitting."

"The land talks slower than tribunals do," Quentin said. "But it keeps its promises better."

Someone in the gallery laughed — Odile, Sera realized, sitting among the freed charm-sellers with her rings restored and her arms crossed, laughing like a woman who had decided the world might yet be worth the trouble. And the laugh broke something open in the room, and the tension went out of it, and the tribunal passed, that afternoon, the first of the new statutes: the dissolution of the Gift-Hunt, the expungement of all blood-records, and the recognition of "common craft" as lawful throughout Caerlum and its territories.

Not vengeance. Repair. It moved slowly, and it satisfied no one completely, and Sera thought it was the most beautiful thing she had ever watched a government do.

* * *

That evening, Sera found Quentin on the roof of the Hall of Ledgers, of all places, sitting on the slates with their coat collar up, watching the city lights come on below.

"Couldn't resist one last dramatic rooftop," she said, hauling herself up beside them.

"It's where Marisol and I used to sit, when I was small. On our own roof. She'd point out which chimneys belonged to which families, and tell me whose fevers she'd treated, whose babies she'd charmed safe." Quentin's breath fogged in the cold. Below, somewhere, a bell rang the hour. "She never told me any of it, you know. The Crown. The war. Why she hid me. I used to lie awake hating her for it — for making me small, for making me afraid. Now I know she was carrying the whole world on her back and couldn't tell a child without crushing them under it." They were quiet a moment. "I miss her differently now. That's strange, isn't it? Grief changing shape after the fact."

"No," Sera said. "That's just understanding arriving late. It happens more than people admit."

Below them, Caerlum glowed. Somewhere in the tangle of streets, Odile was reopening her stall. Somewhere, a family was taking down the little lead sigil of the Hunt that had hung by their door for a generation, and arguing about whether to throw it away or melt it into something useful. Somewhere, a child was tying their first clumsy luck-knot, badly, on the wrong day of the week, and it would work anyway.

"Halloway's trial starts next week," Sera said. "Then the rest of them. It'll take a year, maybe more. There are a lot of names in those ledgers, and some of them belong to people still in office." She pulled her cloak tighter. "I've been asked to stay on. Rebuilding the office from the inside — new mandate, new oversight, the works. They want someone who knows where all the bodies are buried to dig them up."

"Will you?"

"I don't know." She said it honestly, which was still, after everything, a practice rather than a habit. "Part of me wants to go north. Find a farm. Grow something. Be a person whose hands smell like dirt instead of ink." She glanced sideways. "What about you? Back to Thornhollow?"

"Tomorrow. Bramwen says the chapel roof won't fix itself, and the land's been muttering at me about drainage ditches all week, which is a sentence I genuinely never imagined saying." Quentin smiled, but it faded into something quieter. "Sera — the other night, in the citadel, when you held the circle open. You could have run. Everyone would have understood. Instead you stood in the fire and spoke Lira's name out loud while the whole place came apart."

"She liked her name spoken," Sera said. "She used to say a name was the only thing that was truly yours, and even that you had to lend out to people who loved you, or it went stale." She looked out at the city. "I owed her that much. More than that. But that much, at least, in that moment."

"Well," Quentin said. "For what it's worth — I'm glad you stayed. And I'm glad you're staying on, if you do. This city's going to need someone stubborn keeping the reckoning honest."

"And you'll be up in your hills, talking to drainage ditches."

"Someone has to. The ditches have opinions."

They sat a while longer in the cold, two people who had lied to each other once upon a frozen aqueduct and never needed to again. Then Quentin stood, brushing slate dust from their coat, and looked east toward the dark line of the hills where Thornhollow waited, and the chapel, and the garden plot already staked out and waiting for spring.

"Come see it when the herbs come up," they said. "Marisol's garden. I'm planting it on the chapel ruins — feverfew, sage, the blue camomile she loved. First harvest's yours, if you want it."

Sera looked at them — this person who had been a vessel, a fugitive, a weapon, a corpse, a miracle, and had chosen, out of all of it, to be a gardener.

"I'll bring the tea," she said.

Far below, the bells rang out across Caerlum, and in the thawing fields beyond the walls, green things stirred in soil that remembered, now, whose it was.
