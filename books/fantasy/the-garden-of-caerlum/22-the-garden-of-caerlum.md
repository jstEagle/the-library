## 22. The Garden of Caerlum

The frost broke on the ninth day of Thawmonth, three weeks before it had any right to.

Quentin felt it happen the way they felt everything now—not through their skin, but through something older and slower underneath it. A loosening in the soil. A long exhale from roots that had been holding their breath since autumn. They woke in Bramwen's guest room with dirt under their fingernails that hadn't been there when they'd gone to sleep, and lay still for a moment, listening.

*Wake,* said the land, in no language at all. *Wake. Wake.*

By noon, word was coming down from the high pastures: crocuses split the snow on the south slopes. The Thornhollow creek ran loud and brown with meltwater. Old men who hadn't wept since the war years stood at their gates with their hats off, watching green things move where green things should not have been for another month.

Quentin walked out to the orchard and put their palm flat against a bare apple tree. It shivered under their hand like a horse being gentled.

"I know," they murmured. "I know. Go on, then."

It blossomed overnight. So did half the valley.

They were not sure, anymore, whether they had done it or merely permitted it. That distinction had become the whole of their life.

* * *

Caerlum, when Quentin finally went back to see it, wore its scars like a soldier wears them—openly, without apology.

The citadel's eastern face was still black where the green fire had climbed it. No mason would touch the stone; the guilds said it had been changed at the root, and stone that has been changed does not take mortar kindly. Vines had already found it anyway. By midsummer the burned quarter looked less like ruin than like a wall someone had decided to stop fighting and start gardening.

The wells were open again. The parish testing stations had been pulled down by the same crowds that had once queued at them, patiently, vial in hand, ready to be measured and found wanting or found guilty. Quentin watched a woman use one of the dismantled testing tables as a fish stall in the Copperlane market, hawking eels off the scarred wood, and thought: *Marisol would have laughed until she cried.*

Not everything healed so easily. The cells beneath the spymaster's wing had given up forty-one prisoners when Sera forced them, and Odile had come out of the last one thin as a lath and twice as sharp, blinking in the daylight, saying only, "Well. You look terrible." Then she had hugged Quentin hard enough to crack a rib, which Quentin let her do, because some debts are paid in pain gladly given.

Odile was back at her stall now. Her charms sold openly these days—luck-knots braided in plain sight, fever-sachets labeled honestly, prices chalked on slate where everyone could argue about them. Half her stock didn't work anymore, and she said so cheerfully. "The land gives out what the land gives out," she told her customers. "I'm just the wrapping."

But sometimes a knot came off her table warm, and the buyer would come back three days later with news of a safe birth, or a fever broken, or rain arriving exactly when the hay needed it. And Odile would look across the market at Quentin, wherever they stood, and raise an eyebrow, and Quentin would shrug, because they genuinely did not know. The gift moved now like weather. It settled on some people and passed over others, and no one could predict it, and no one owned it, and this was precisely the point.

Cassel's network had not dissolved quietly. There had been trials—Sera had insisted on trials, real ones, with witnesses and ledgers and the names read aloud, all of them, including her sister's, spoken into the record so that no clerk could ever lose it again. Some of Cassel's people had wept. Some had lied. Two had been hanged, and Quentin had not attended, and had slept badly anyway, and had decided that this too was part of having a conscience rather than a crown.

The throne had wobbled and then, remarkably, held. The young queen—who turned out to have opinions of her own and a spine the court had underestimated—had signed the Repeal of the Hunt in the spring of her second year, with Sera Vane standing at her shoulder in a uniform she no longer quite belonged to and had not yet decided to leave. "Someone has to make sure the next Hunt never gets funded," Sera said. "Bureaucracy is the only weapon I trust now."

She visited Thornhollow every few weeks. She always arrived complaining about the road and left reluctantly, and she never once asked Quentin what it felt like, being part of the ground. She just sat with them in the evenings while they listened to things she couldn't hear, and told her stories about the city, and let the silence do its work.

That was friendship, Quentin had learned. Not understanding. Attendance.

* * *

The chapel ruins had not been touched since the war either, but for different reasons. Marisol's old training chapel stood on a hill above Caerlum's western gate, half-swallowed by ivy, its roof open to the sky, its carved warnings worn smooth by twelve years of weather. People avoided it out of habit. Haunted, they said. Cursed. Which meant, in practice, that it was the quietest piece of land in the city, and the land itself had begun whispering to Quentin that it was lonely.

So in the first warm week of the new spring, Quentin went up the hill with a wheelbarrow, two spades, a sack of manure, and a packet of seeds wrapped in oilcloth that had waited twelve years in a hidden box for exactly this.

Marisol's seeds. Saved from her own garden, the one she'd kept behind the boarding house before the first Hunt took her, labeled in her cramped, impatient hand: *feverfew — don't overwater, fool girl*. And *yarrow — stubborn, like you.* And one unlabeled envelope that Quentin opened last, sitting on a fallen roof beam in the sun, and found full of poppy seeds, red as embers, with a single line inside:

*For the grave I don't intend to have. Plant me somewhere useful.*

Quentin sat with that for a long time. Then they got up and started turning soil.

The work was good in a way magic had never been. Magic, even now, even shared, even gentle—it asked things of Quentin that words couldn't hold. But digging was simple. Digging had an honest arithmetic to it: one spade-depth per hour, one row per morning, sore back, clean mind. The land hummed up through their knees as they worked, pleased, chatty, offering suggestions (*more sun there, less water here, the worms know best*) and Quentin mostly ignored it, the way you ignore a helpful grandparent, fondly and with firm boundaries.

By the end of the first week there were four beds laid out along the chapel's broken nave, following the old lines of the floor stones, so that the garden grew in the shape of the church that had once stood there. Feverfew by the west wall. Yarrow along the aisle. Comfrey, chamomile, sage, and a row of poppies down the center where the altar would have been, because Marisol had never had much reverence for altars and Quentin suspected she'd have found the placement hilarious.

They planted a rosemary bush at the door, low and scrubby, the kind that survives anything. *Rosemary for remembrance* was a mortal custom, not a magical one, and Quentin had decided, somewhere in the long winter, that the mortal customs were the ones worth keeping.

* * *

The children found them within a month, the way children find everything.

First it was one boy—the same boy, though neither of them knew it yet, whose blood Quentin had healed in the trampled market two winters ago—climbing the chapel wall to retrieve a kite. He'd stared at the garden, at the person in the dirt with the strange stillness about them, and asked, "Are you a witch?"

"No," Quentin had said. "I'm a gardener."

"Same thing," the boy had said with the devastating certainty of nine-year-olds, and stayed to help, and told his friends, and by midsummer there were eight of them, and by harvest there were more than Quentin could count without taking off a boot.

They came after lessons, dusty and loud, and Quentin taught them the way Marisol had taught them: nothing that looked like power. How to braid a luck-knot with your attention instead of your fingers. How to hold a seedling and *mean* it, which was ninety percent of any charm ever made. How to listen to a sick goat. How to tell when bread dough was happy. Small magics, domestic magics, the kind that had survived every purge in history precisely because no one could burn a woman for making bread rise well.

"The old law says this is illegal," Quentin told them once, early on, because they refused to lie to children. "The new law says it isn't. But laws change. So here is the only rule that matters: you do not use what you know to make yourself bigger than someone else. Not ever. The moment you do, you're Lord Cassel. Understood?"

"Understood," they chorused, and then immediately used a growth charm to win a bean-growing contest against each other, which Quentin allowed, because rivalry between children over beans was a very different thing from empire, and knowing the difference was most of wisdom.

One gray afternoon, a girl named Tansy—small, serious, gap-toothed—sat back on her heels in the yarrow bed and frowned at Quentin with the particular ferocity children reserve for unfairness.

"My gran says you died," she announced. "She says you poured yourself into the ground and died, and the ground gave you back wrong."

The other children went quiet. Even the wind seemed to lean in.

Quentin considered several answers. *Your gran is right. Your gran is mistaken. It's complicated.* All true. None sufficient.

"What do you think?" they asked.

Tansy studied them the way she studied seedlings, looking for the true thing underneath. "I think you're like compost," she said at last.

A pause.

"Go on," said Quentin, hiding a smile.

"Well—" she gestured at the beds, earnest now, committed. "Compost isn't dead stuff. It's stuff that's been *changed* so it can feed things. You're not a person and you're not dirt. You're both, mixed up, and that's why everything grows better around you."

Silence. Somewhere below the hill, Caerlum clattered and rang, ten thousand lives going about their business, none of them knowing that the future of magic had just been accurately described by a seven-year-old using kitchen vocabulary.

"That," said Quentin, "is the finest theology I've ever heard, and I'm putting it on the chapel wall."

They did, too. Carved it themselves, into the stone above the door, in letters deep enough to outlast weather:

**COMPOST ISN'T DEAD. IT'S CHANGED SO IT CAN FEED THINGS.**

Marisol would have hated it—too earnest, too public, far too sentimental—and would have defended it to the death against anyone else who criticized it. Quentin knew this the way they knew the turn of seasons now: completely, and without evidence.

* * *

On the anniversary of the Returning—a date no calendar marked but Quentin carried in their bones—they climbed the hill alone at dusk and sat among the poppies, which had gone up red and reckless along the old altar line, exactly as ordered.

The city glowed below. Lamplighters working the western streets. Smoke from suppers. A fiddler somewhere near the gate playing badly and loudly. The scars on the citadel black against the last light, wearing their vines like a borrowed coat.

*I did that,* Quentin thought, looking at the burned stone. *And this.* A poppy nodded in the breeze. *Both. Always both.*

The land stirred beneath them, drowsy with evening, and spoke its slow wordless word, and for once Quentin let themselves ask the question they usually declined to ask.

*What am I?*

The answer came the way the land answered everything—not in sentences but in sensation. Rain soaking into a field. A worm turning heavy earth. Green pushing up through a crack in a paving stone because cracks are what stones are *for*, eventually. Nothing that named them. Everything that included them.

Not the vessel of a dead power. Not the heir of a goddess. Not a weapon that had chosen peace, or a martyr who had gotten lucky.

A gardener. Of a living thing. One pair of hands among millions, tending what belonged to everyone, answerable to the seasons and to Tansy's gran and to no throne at all.

Quentin laughed—quietly, so as not to wake the children's beans—and lay back among the poppies as the stars came out over Caerlum.

Below, faint on the wind, they heard the chapel bell in the lower town ring the hour, and beneath it, thinner, stranger, a sound only they could hear: the whole city's foundations humming, root-deep and content, like a garden settling in for the night.

Spring had come early. Spring, as far as Quentin could tell, intended to keep doing so.

There was work tomorrow. Seeds to sort. Children to teach. A world, changed so it could feed things.

Quentin closed their eyes, and the garden breathed, and that was enough.
