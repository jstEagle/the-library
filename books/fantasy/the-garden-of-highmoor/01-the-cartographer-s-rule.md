## 1. The Cartographer's Rule

There were three ways over the Thornfell mountains that the Duke's surveyors never found, and Casper Hartwell had drawn all of them.

He had not drawn them anywhere a reasonable man might look. The first lived in the stitching of his left boot, a wandering seam that doubled as the Shepherd's Cut when read against the grain of the leather. The second was worked into the border pattern of a wool scarf he wore only in winter, each knot a switchback, each fringe-length a distance in furlongs. The third—the most dangerous of the three, the one that made his palms sweat even now, six years after he'd committed it—was not written down at all. It existed only in the architecture of his memory, and every morning when he woke he walked it once, end to end, the way other men said their prayers.

The Kettlewoman's Stair. Forty-one steps of smuggler-cut granite behind a waterfall that froze in a shape no waterfall should freeze. He could have climbed it blindfolded. He intended to die without ever telling anyone that he could.

This was the rule, and the rule had kept him alive: *never be memorable.*

It was not a dramatic rule. It did not ask him to be brave or clever or fast. It asked him to be dull, which was harder than any of those things and infinitely more useful. Be dull in your face—average browns, average height, a jaw that suggested nothing. Be dull in your habits—same bread from same baker, same nod to same neighbors, an opinion about weather offered at intervals so regular it became furniture. Be dull in your work, which was the trickiest part, because Casper was very good at his work, and excellence is a kind of shouting.

So he was good in the way that was expected and no better. His official maps—sold openly through the guild office in Lowmark—were competent, tidy, and wrong in forty small places. A stream moved fifty yards east. A ridge shown where there was a col. A mine marked abandoned that was anything but. The errors were his signature, though no one would ever know to look for one, and they were worth more to certain clients than everything else he drew combined.

Tonight's client was one of those. Tonight was a delivery.

* * *

The rain had come up the valley like something with intent, driving sideways off the high crags and turning the cart-track to soup, and Casper walked the last two miles to the Drover's Rest with his collar up and his satchel strapped tight against his ribs. Inside the satchel, wrapped in oilcloth between a spare shirt and a bag of dried plums, lay a single sheet of vellum. To anyone who seized it—and someone had tried, once, three years ago on the Low Road, and had been disappointed—it was a grazing chart for the high summer pastures. Sheep numbers. Watering rights. The sort of document a land-steward might carry and a bandit might bother to steal and then burn out of spite.

The sheep were a cipher. So were the watering rights. The true map sat underneath them like a body under a sheet, legible only if you knew that every third number was a bearing and every fifth a pace-count, and that the little crosses marking wells were in fact the locations of cache-sites along a route that bypassed the Duke's toll-gates entirely.

At the Drover's Rest, the fire was low and the room smelled of wet dog and tallow. Casper took the corner table because he always took the corner table, back to the wall, sightline to both doors, and ordered barley broth because he always ordered barley broth. The innkeeper's wife brought it without comment. That was the point of routine; it made you invisible to the people who saw you every day.

His client arrived twenty minutes late, shaking rain from a hooded cloak of good grey wool—too good for this room, and the man knew it, and wore it anyway, which told Casper something before a word was spoken. He was soft through the middle, forty or fifty, with the ruddy complexion of a man who drank indoors rather than out, and rings on three fingers that caught the firelight.

"You're the mapman." Not a question.

"I'm a man eating soup," Casper said. "If you're here about grazing charts, I have one. If you're here about anything else, you've mistaken me."

A flicker of irritation crossed the client's face, then smoothed itself with visible effort. He understood the game. Good. Men who didn't understand the game tended to get loud, and loud men got remembered, and remembered men got visited by people whose visits Casper had spent a lifetime avoiding.

"The chart," the man said, sitting heavily across from him. "And the reading."

"There is no reading."

"Vess said there'd be a reading."

Casper kept his spoon moving. Madame Vess dealt in many things—tobacco, texts, rumors, revenge—but she did not deal in carelessness, and sending a client who said her name aloud in a public house was either a test or a mistake. "Madame Vess sells cloth," he said mildly. "From Marrowgate. Very fine cloth. I wouldn't know her otherwise."

The client leaned forward. Up close he smelled of wine and something sharper beneath it, a medicinal bite, willowbark maybe, or something less legal. "Look," he said, lower now, "I've paid. Vess has paid. Everyone's paid. I need the route by Thursday or my cargo sits at the toll-gate and the toll-gate eats a third of it, and I'll tell you what, mapman, I don't fancy giving the Duke's men a third of anything."

Casper finished his soup. He set the spoon down parallel to the bowl's rim, wiped his mouth, and only then reached for his satchel. Ritual mattered. It slowed him down, and slowness looked like boredom, and boredom was armor.

The vellum changed hands under the table, folded small, passed with the casual clumsiness of a man handing over a bill of sale. The client palmed it, and Casper watched his eyes drop to it, watch the pupils widen as the cipher resolved for a man who'd been given the key in advance—*thirds are bearings, fifths are paces, follow the crosses*—and Casper felt the familiar cold satisfaction of work done cleanly. Forty years of routes in the Thornfells, and he had never once lost a delivery. Not to weather, not to wardens, not to the two men who had tried to rob him on the High Pass and now, presumably, farmed sheep somewhere south with no memory of why their ambush had gone so strangely wrong.

"Thursday," the client murmured. "Good. Good." He tucked the vellum away and sat back, and Casper began, quietly, to calculate how long he should wait before leaving, because the rule said never leave with a client, never arrive with one, never be seen adjacent—

The client was looking at him.

Not at the satchel, not at the table, not scanning the room the way buyers did after a handoff, checking for watchers. Looking at *him*. At his face. With an expression Casper had no category for—not suspicion, not greed, but a kind of dawning, puzzled recognition, like a man trying to place a face from a dream.

"What?" Casper said.

"Have we met?"

"No."

"No, I—" The client frowned. He rubbed his thumb across the rings, one, two, three, a nervous habit. "You have one of those faces. You know? One of those faces you've seen somewhere." He tilted his head. The firelight moved across it. "Somewhere important."

"People say that," Casper said. It was a lie. No one had ever said that. In thirty-one years, no one had ever looked at Casper Hartwell twice, and he had cultivated that anonymity the way other men cultivated gardens—patiently, daily, with an eye to the long harvest. "I have a common face. It's why I'm good at my job."

But the client wasn't listening anymore. He was leaning in again, and there was something almost frightened in his squint now, some instinct firing in the oldest rooms of him, the rooms below language. "The tower," he said slowly. "No. No, that's mad. But—" He snapped his fingers, once, softly. "You'd have been a boy. There was a boy. They brought him through the halls and everyone was told not to look at him, and everyone looked anyway, and he had—" The client stopped. Blinked. Shook his head like a man clearing water from his ear. "Huh," he said. "That's nothing. That's nothing at all. Forget it. Old man's nonsense."

Casper's heart was doing something quiet and terrible against his ribs.

"I don't know what you're talking about," he said, and his voice came out level, and he was proud of that, proud the way a drowning man is proud of keeping his boots dry.

"Course you don't." The client laughed—a short, uncomfortable sound—and pushed back from the table. "Rain's letting up. I've a long ride." He stood, dropped coins beside the empty bowls, too many of them, and paused with one hand on the chair-back. For a moment he seemed about to say something else. Then he pulled his hood up and went out into the dark, and the door swung shut behind him, and the fire popped, and the innkeeper's wife asked if Casper wanted another broth, and he said yes, because saying yes was the routine thing, the dull thing, the memorable-in-its-unremarkableness thing.

He sat with the second broth going cold in front of him for a very long time.

*A tower. A boy. Don't look at him.*

He turned the words over the way he'd turn an unfamiliar coin, checking the weight of them, testing the metal. The client had been drunk, or half-drunk, or addled by whatever bitter medicine he smelled of. People invented memories the way rivers invented bends—constantly, carelessly, following the path of least resistance. That was all. That had to be all.

Except.

Except that Casper had no childhood. Not in the way men usually lacked one—not faded, not half-lost, but absent, clean as a page scraped with a knife. He had woken at fourteen in a waystation loft above the Low Road with a name in his head—*Casper Hartwell*, supplied, he'd assumed, by whoever found him—and a set of skills in his hands he couldn't account for: how to hold a pen, how to read bearings, how to speak three languages and swear fluently in a fourth. No mother's face. No father's voice. No scar with a story. When he'd asked, early on, the carter who'd supposedly found him had frowned and said, *You came down off the Thornfell road, lad, don't you remember?* And Casper had said yes, because the alternative was to keep asking questions, and questions were memorable, and memorable was death.

For seventeen years he had built himself around that absence the way a tree builds around a lightning scar. He had made the absence into a method. *Never be memorable* was not just survival. It was a kindness to himself. Because a man with no past cannot be haunted by one, and a man no one remembers cannot be erased, and Casper Hartwell had arranged his whole life into a single sustained act of pre-emptive forgetting.

Now a fat stranger with wine on his breath and rings on his fingers had looked at his face and seen a tower.

* * *

He did not go home. Home was a stone cottage two miles east with a false floor and a decade of careful mediocrity inside it, and the rule said never go straight home after a delivery, especially a delivery that had gone strange. Instead he took the shepherd's track north, up into the black shoulder of the hills, walking hard through the thinning rain until the Drover's Rest was a smudge of orange light far below and the wind was the only company he had.

He told himself he was being cautious. Standard procedure. A client spooked is a client who talks; let the night cool him off; circle back by dawn. All true. None of it was why he was walking.

He was walking because his body wanted altitude, and his body had learned things in fourteen missing years that his mind had never been given.

The clouds tore open as he climbed, and the stars came out hard and cold, and Casper did what he always did when the sky cleared: he fixed his position. It was reflex, breathing, prayer. Polaris, low and steady. The Wanderer climbing past the Horn. The pale scatter of the Sisters, which he used for easting because they rose, in these latitudes, at a known drift off true east, and a man who knew the sky's errors could use them like a key.

Only the sky was wrong tonight.

He stopped. Stood very still on the wet grass with the wind pulling at his coat, and checked himself the way he'd check a suspect map—against the horizon, against the compass in his pocket, against the second compass hidden in his boot heel. Three instruments, three confirmations.

Polaris was fine. The Wanderer was fine. The Sisters were fine.

But in the southeast, low over the pass where no star should be—over the direction of Highmoor, the dead estate, the ruined garden he'd sheltered in a hundred times and mapped, officially, as *overgrown grounds, no feature of interest*—there was a light. Not a star. Stars did not sit still while the sky turned around them. This one sat. It hung above the broken walls of Highmoor like a lamp held up by someone patient, someone waiting, and it was the color of nothing Casper had words for—not white, not green, but the color the snow has just before dawn, the color of a page.

He watched it for a long time. His breath smoked. Somewhere below, a fox screamed and went silent.

The rule said: unexplained lights are other people's business. The rule said: go home, bar the door, draw the curtains, be dull until the world loses interest in you. Seventeen years of the rule, and the rule had never once failed him.

But the client's voice was in his ears—*there was a boy, they brought him through the halls, everyone was told not to look at him*—and for the first time since he was fourteen years old, Casper Hartwell understood that the rule had been protecting the wrong thing. He had spent his life making sure no one remembered him. He had never once asked the question underneath it, the question a cartographer of all people should have asked:

*What if someone already had?*

The light over Highmoor pulsed, gently, once, like a thing noticing him back.

Casper pulled his collar up, turned downhill, and started walking—not east toward home, but northwest, toward the ruin, telling himself with every step that he was only going to look, only to stand at the wall and see what it was, only to satisfy the itch, and knowing, in the cold honest room behind his eyes where he kept the maps of himself, that he was lying.

The storm that had chased him all evening regathered behind the peaks, and the first fat drops came down as the garden walls rose out of the dark ahead of him, black against that impossible, patient light.
