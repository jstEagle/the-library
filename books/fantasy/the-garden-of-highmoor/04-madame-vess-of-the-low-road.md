## 4. Madame Vess of the Low Road

Three days after the stranger died on the pass exactly as the book had written it, Casper Hartwell did the one thing his rule forbade: he made himself memorable.

He went looking for Madame Vess.

The Low Road wasn't on any map he'd ever drawn, which was saying something, because Casper had drawn maps of things that officially didn't exist—goat tracks that crossed the Thornfell border at night, cellar doors in Kellbridge that opened into other cellars two streets away, a chapel whose crypt connected to a wine merchant's stockroom by a passage so narrow you had to exhale to walk it. He knew the mountains' hidden grammar better than any man alive. But the Low Road was different. The Low Road was a rumor that moved.

You didn't find it. You let it find you, and then you paid for the privilege.

He started at the Drowned Bell in Harrowgate's lower town, a tavern built into the bones of an old tide-mill, where the river ran loud enough beneath the floorboards that conversations could be conducted at a whisper and still be private. Casper hadn't been inside in four years. Four years ago he'd delivered a map there and left before his cup was half empty, and no one had looked up from their drinks when he came or went, and that was precisely how he liked it.

Tonight he walked in and let the door bang shut behind him.

The room did what rooms full of smugglers always do when a stranger enters with purpose: nothing visible, and everything else. Dice kept rolling. A fiddler kept sawing. But eleven separate pairs of eyes weighed him, catalogued him, and passed judgment along some silent chain of custody until it reached the barkeep, a slab-shouldered woman with a burn scar down one side of her face who was polishing a mug that had been clean for an hour.

Casper sat at the end of the bar, put three silver crowns on the wood, and said, "I need to buy something that isn't for sale."

The barkeep didn't look at him. "Everything here is for sale."

"Then I need to buy something that isn't *here*."

A long moment. The river muttered under the floor. Then the barkeep took the crowns, slid them into her apron without counting them—a courtesy, or a test—and said, "There's a coal barge leaving the weir at moonrise. Barge hands wear grey wool. Don't be one of them. Don't be anything at all."

"I'm good at that," Casper said.

"Everyone says so," she said, "right up until they aren't."

* * *

The coal barge smelled of tar and old water, and Casper spent the six miles downstream wedged between crates of salted eels with his hood up and the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like a second heart. He'd tried, over three sleepless nights, to convince himself of every reasonable explanation. A coincidence of names. A forgery planted by someone who wanted to frighten him. A cruel joke by a client with a grudge over a misdrawn contour line.

But the book had known the date. And the man on the pass had died with a broken rope-line across his chest and frost in his open eyes, and Casper had stood over the body at dawn and read the entry again by grey light, and the words had matched the corpse the way a key matches a lock—not approximately, not in spirit, but *exactly*, down to the detail that the man's left boot had come off in the fall.

Books didn't know about boots. Coincidences didn't either.

So: Vess. Every forbidden thing in the Thornfells eventually passed through Madame Vess's hands—heretical pamphlets, pre-Conquest star charts, love letters between people who legally didn't exist. If anyone could tell him what the book was, she could. And if anyone would sell him out afterward, it would also be her. He knew this. He went anyway, because the alternative was going back to his workshop and waiting for whatever the book had scheduled next, and Casper Hartwell had spent thirty-one years learning that waiting was just dying at a slower speed.

The barge put him off below a collapsed aqueduct where the river split around a gravel island. From there he walked upstream along a dry millrace until the walls closed in, until the stars narrowed to a ragged seam overhead, until he reached the door.

It was a door set into the aqueduct's own stonework, iron-banded oak, unmarked except for a knocker shaped like a closed hand. No handle on the outside. Casper raised the knocker, hesitated, and knocked twice—the way the barkeep's silence had told him to, though she'd never said a word about knocking.

A slot scraped open. One eye, brown and unimpressed.

"Business?"

"A question."

"Questions are free. Answers cost."

"That's why I brought money instead of questions."

Something that might have been amusement shifted behind the eye. The slot closed. Bolts turned somewhere inside the stone, more of them than seemed structurally sensible, and the door swung inward onto lamplight and the smell of paper, ink, tallow, and beneath it all something older—dust that had never seen sun, the particular staleness of rooms that exist only underground.

* * *

Madame Vess received him in a chamber that had clearly once been a cistern. The vaulted ceiling dripped mineral stains like frozen waterfalls; the floor was laid with rugs three deep to kill the echo; and the walls—

The walls were books. Not shelves of books. *Walls.* Stacks rising fifteen feet, laddered on rails, crammed spine-outward in no order Casper could discern, some chained shut, some wrapped in waxed canvas, one enormous folio displayed open on a lectern like a saint's relic, its pages covered in script that hurt to look at directly, the letters seeming to rearrange themselves whenever he almost focused on them.

Vess herself sat behind a desk made from a ship's hatch, small and broad and sixty-ish, with iron-grey hair cropped short and hands folded like a woman who had done violence with them once and found she preferred arithmetic. She wore a plain brown dress and a single ring of black metal that Casper's professional eye immediately wanted to sketch: it bore no sigil, but its band was engraved with characters in a script he'd seen exactly once before.

In the prophecy book.

"You're the cartographer," she said. Her voice was low and unhurried, a river under floorboards. "Hartwell. The one who draws roads that don't exist."

"They exist," Casper said. "They're just not supposed to."

"Mm." She studied him the way he studied terrain—with attention to what was missing. "Sit. You've been walking since moonrise and you have the posture of a man carrying something heavy against his sternum. Take it out. Put it on the desk. Slowly."

His pulse ticked up. Every instinct he owned screamed at him to lie, to deflect, to be forgettable. But she'd already seen through the coat, through the hood, through thirty-one years of practiced dullness, and lying to Madame Vess in her own vault struck him as roughly as survivable as lying to an avalanche.

He unwrapped the oilcloth and set the book down.

Vess didn't touch it at first. She leaned forward and looked at the cover the way a physician looks at a wound—assessing, unhurried, and with something underneath the professionalism that Casper took a moment to identify because he'd never seen it on a smuggler's face before.

It was fear.

"Where," she said quietly, "did you get this."

"Highmoor. The old garden above Kellbridge. There's a greenhouse behind a fallen wall—"

"I know the garden." She cut him off with two fingers, a gesture small and absolute. "Everyone knows the garden. Nobody goes into the garden. The Ascendants sealed it eighty years ago and even they don't go in anymore, and they sealed it for reasons that are written nowhere, which for the Ascendants means the reasons are very bad indeed." Her eyes came up to his. "So I'll ask again, cartographer, and I'd like the true answer this time. Where did you get it."

"It was already there. In a reading stand, in the middle of the greenhouse, like someone had just set it down. Like someone was coming back for it." He hesitated. "It has my name in it."

"Of course it does."

The casualness of it knocked the air out of him. "You knew?"

"I knew such books existed. I've held two in my life. Both burned my buyers' houses down within a year—one literally, one in other ways." She finally touched the cover, flat-palmed, careful, the way you'd steady a sleeping animal. "This is an Ascendant relic. Pre-Conquest, if the binding's original, and it is. They call them ledgers of intent. The common word, among the few of us who use it, is a *scheduling book*."

"Scheduling," Casper repeated. The word landed in his stomach like a dropped stone.

"The Ascendants don't predict the future, Hartwell. Prediction implies uncertainty. What they do is—" she searched for the shape of it, fingers flexing, "—they write ahead. Memory is their medium. Yours is ink; theirs is remembering. And a mind that can rewrite what everyone remembers can also write what everyone *will* remember. An event recorded in a ledger like this tends to occur, and then to be remembered as having always been inevitable." She tapped the cover once. "The book doesn't tell fortunes. It issues orders. To reality."

Casper thought of the man on the pass. The boot coming off. Frost in the open eyes. *Exactly.*

"There's an entry," he heard himself say. His voice sounded far away, like someone else speaking through the aqueduct wall. "Dated three days ago. It describes a traveler falling on the White Stair. It happened. Word for word. Boot and all." He swallowed. "And there's another entry. Dated tonight. It names me. It calls me—" the word felt absurd in the lamplight, absurd and enormous, "—the Bearer of the Unwritten."

For the first time, Madame Vess went completely still.

Not thoughtful-still. The stillness of a woman recalculating everything, fast, behind a face that had learned not to show math. When she spoke again, the river-under-floorboards quality was gone from her voice, and what remained was flatter and harder.

"Read it to me. Tonight's entry. Exactly."

"I don't have it memorized—"

"Then open the book and read it, and be quick about it, because if it says what I think it might say, we are both standing in a room full of kindling and neither of us knows who holds the match."

Casper opened the book. The pages fell to the marked place as if the binding remembered his hands. He read aloud, and the vault drank his voice:

"*On the third night of the thaw moon, the Bearer of the Unwritten comes down the Low Road bearing the book. He seeks the woman of the low road, who deals in what is forbidden. She will know him by his asking. Let her decide whether he is weapon or kindling.*"

Silence. Somewhere deep in the stacks, a chain swayed and ticked against a shelf, though no draft moved.

"He wrote me into it too," Vess said at last. There was no awe in it. There was fury, banked and old, the fury of someone seeing an old scar reopened. "*Let her decide.* As if I were a line in someone's inventory." She pushed back from the desk and stood, and up close Casper saw that her hands were shaking, very slightly, and that she was clenching them to stop it. "Do you understand what you've done by bringing this here? Do you understand what you've done by *reading it aloud*?"

"No," Casper admitted. "That's rather why I came."

"Every time a scheduling book is read aloud by the person it names, the writing sets deeper. That's how they work—it isn't enough to write the event. The event must be *witnessed into memory*. You've just witnessed yourself into your own page. So have I." She began to pace, three steps, turn, three steps, a caged animal measuring its cage. "Eighty years ago the Ascendants won a war. Not with armies—with erasure. There was a whole people, Hartwell. A nation. Roads, songs, kings, graves, mothers. And the Ascendants rewrote the world's remembering until none of it had ever been, and the ones they couldn't fully erase they hunted down and burned out of every ledger, every gravestone, every grandmother's tale. The Unwritten, the survivors called themselves, before even that name was taken from them." She stopped pacing. "And the rebels who fought them—the archivists, the memory-smugglers, my predecessors in this trade—they built counterweapons. Books like yours. Books that wrote *ahead*, past the Ascendants' edits, so that no matter how thoroughly the world was rewritten, certain events would still arrive on schedule. Seeds buried in tomorrow."

"And I'm in one."

"You're *in* one." She laughed, short and without humor. "Cartographer, listen to me, because I will only say this once and then I will decide your fate while you stand there sweating. People named in these books come in exactly two kinds. Weapons—the book moves them toward something, uses them, spends them against the enemy. Or kindling—the book moves them toward something, and their burning lights the way for the weapons. There is no third kind. There is no page in any of these books where the named person simply goes home and lives a quiet life drawing maps. I have looked. Believe me, in forty years, I have *looked*."

Casper's mouth was dry. "Which one am I?"

"If I knew, I'd be a richer woman." Vess returned to the desk but didn't sit. She looked down at the book, and her expression did something complicated—hunger and dread fighting to a draw. "What I do know is that the phrase *Bearer of the Unwritten* hasn't appeared in any text I've ever handled. Which means your entry isn't a copy of an old campaign. It's new. Original. Someone wrote it recently, or—" her jaw tightened, "—it wrote itself, which is worse, and rarer, and I've only ever heard of it happening once."

"When?"

"Never you mind." She waved it off, but her eyes flicked, just for a heartbeat, to the great chained folio on its lectern, and Casper filed that away the way he filed everything: precisely, and for later. "Here is what matters. The Ascendants have instruments that feel these books the way you'd feel a lit stove in a dark room. Somewhere in a grey coat, an inquisitor has already felt yours light up, and is already riding toward Thornfell, and every day you carry that book openly is a day you're painting a target on everything within ten miles of you. Including, as of twenty minutes ago, this vault, my people, and forty years of work."

"So give me directions to somewhere far away and I'll be gone by dawn."

"No." She said it flatly, and then, seeing his face, something in her own shifted—reluctance, calculation, and beneath both, unexpectedly, pity. "No, because you'll die alone on some mountain with a book in your pack and the world will be exactly as rewritten as it was yesterday. And no because—" she exhaled through her nose, annoyed at herself, "—because I have spent forty years moving forbidden texts through these mountains, cartographer, and in all that time I have operated on one principle: the Ascendants erased people, and somebody has to remember them, and remembering is smuggling too. Your book remembers something. Something big enough that they hid it inside a *person*, which is a trick I've heard of and never once believed." She sat down heavily, and for a moment she looked every one of her sixty-odd years, and tired down to the marrow of them. "I shouldn't help you. Every sane instinct says put you out in the millrace and let the river vote. But the book says I get to decide, and I find I resent being *told* I get to decide, and the fastest way to spite a prophecy is to make my own choice. So."

She pulled a sheet of paper toward her, and a pen, and did not yet write.

"My price. Two parts. First: you travel under my network from tonight forward. My routes, my couriers, my rules. You touch nothing, arrange nothing, trust no one who isn't mine. Second—" and here her voice changed, went quieter, and Casper understood with a cold slide in his gut that they had arrived at the real transaction, the one beneath the transaction, "—second, in that book, somewhere, there may be a page. A name. A girl who was written out of the world before she was old enough to be in it. If it exists, you bring it to me. Not a copy. The page itself."

"What girl?"

"My daughter," Madame Vess said, in the tone of a woman reporting the weather, which is the only tone in which such things can be survived. "Erased twenty-two years ago. I don't even remember her face anymore—I remember that I used to remember it, which is a wound the Ascendants specialize in. If your book schedules futures, maybe it also keeps records. Maybe somewhere in those pages is proof she existed." Her eyes came up, and they were hard as river stones. "Bring me that page, and my whole road is yours. Fail me, or betray me, and I promise you the Ascendants will be the least of what's hunting you."

Casper looked at her—at the iron-grey hair and the shaking hands clenched still, at the walls of stolen memory rising into the dark—and felt the last door of his old life swing shut behind him with a sound like a bolt turning.

"I'll bring you your page," he said.

"Good." Vess dipped the pen, wrote three lines, folded the paper, and held it out. "Safe passage. Show it to no one but the people I'll describe. Now go and sleep, because at first light you leave for Highmoor."

Casper blinked. "Highmoor? Why would I go back? The book was there, the garden is sealed, the Ascendants—"

"Because," Madame Vess said, taking the book off the desk and holding it out to him, and now her voice had gone strange, careful, the voice of a woman handing over something that might detonate, "when you read me that entry, cartographer, I asked you to read it *exactly*. And you did. And the book said you'd seek me tonight, and it said I'd decide, and it got every word right." She pressed the book into his hands and did not let go of it immediately, so that for a moment they both held it, like men lowering something heavy into a grave. "But it never said what I decided. Look for yourself. The line ends at *weapon or kindling*. The rest of the sentence—the part where I choose—is blank."

Casper stared down at the open page. She was right. After *kindling*, the vellum lay empty—not worn, not damaged, simply blank, in the way of a page awaiting ink.

Except that as he watched, in the lamplight, in the space of a breath, faint grey letters surfaced on the blank vellum like frost forming on glass, arranging themselves into a single fresh line:

*She sends him back to the garden, where the flowers bloom out of season, and the garden knows his name.*

Vess snatched the book, read it, and went white to the lips.

"It's writing itself," she whispered. "Gods preserve us all—it's writing itself, and it's writing *you*."
