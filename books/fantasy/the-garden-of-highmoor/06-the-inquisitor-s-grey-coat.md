## 6. The Inquisitor's Grey Coat

The grey coat was the first thing they gave her and the last thing she would ever be allowed to take off.

Sera Ilvane had worn it for eleven years. She knew its weight the way other people knew their own heartbeat—the stiffened collar that sat against her jaw like a hand reminding her to hold her head level, the hem cut precisely at mid-calf so it would not drag in snow or mud or blood, the seven buttons of bone-white lacquer that Ascendant doctrine said represented the Seven Sealed Truths but which Sera privately thought of as seven small locked doors down the front of her body. Inquisitors did not wear black. Black was for mourners and for the Wardens who did the Spire's rougher work. Grey was the color of a page on which nothing had yet been written, and an inquisitor's whole purpose was to keep the world's pages clean.

She stood now at the window of the waystation inn at Thornfell's foot, watching sleet come down the mountain like the sky shedding old paper, and beneath her coat, folded flat against her ribs, lay the order that had brought her here.

*Disturbance detected: Highmoor province, category unreconciled. A text has woken. Retrieve the Bearer intact. If retrieval proves impossible, erase.*

The words were written in Ondrell's own hand. That was what troubled her—not the order itself; orders were weather—but the hand. Archivist Prime Ondrell had not signed a field commission in nine years. Whatever had stirred in Highmoor was important enough to bring his pen out of retirement, and Ondrell's pen, in Sera's experience, was a thing that left graves shaped like sentences.

Behind her, Warden Breck was arguing with the innkeeper about beds.

"We'll take the two rooms facing the road," Breck said. He was a big man with a voice built for cellars, patient as a landslide. "And my colleague will want hot water. Don't trouble yourself about which of us pays. The Ledger covers it."

"The Ledger," the innkeeper repeated, in the way people said such words when they wanted you to know they were afraid of them and too proud to show it.

"Everything is in the Ledger," Breck agreed pleasantly. "Your name. Your mother's name. The debt your father left in Carrowford in the year of the Ash Fair. We're not here for any of that. We're here for quiet."

They got their rooms.

Sera did not sleep. She sat in the chair by the window with the sleet ticking against the glass and opened the memory-ledger across her knees—a slim volume bound in slate leather, its pages dense with the small exact script of a recording archivist. It was the disturbance report, copied from the Spire's central reading room before she'd been given her coat and her warrant and her two wardens and sent north.

*A text has woken.* Four words that should have meant nothing. Books woke all the time, in the loose sense—old letters surfaced, diaries turned up in dead men's attics, and the Archive collected them the way a river collects leaves. But the report's phrasing was precise, and precision was the Spire's native language. *Category unreconciled.* There were categories for everything else. A found hymnal was category reconciled: read, weighed, filed, forgotten. A heretical tract was category burned. *Unreconciled* meant the book had resisted classification, which meant it had resisted reading, which meant—

Which meant someone had written something the Ascendants could not unwrite.

She turned the page. The report's final paragraph was short, and she had read it eleven times since Carrowford, and it still produced in her chest the same sensation as stepping off a stair that wasn't there.

*The text identifies a living bearer. Name withheld at the Prime's instruction. Designation: the Bearer of the Unwritten.*

Name withheld. She had served the Ledger her whole adult life and she had never once seen a name withheld from a field officer. Names were the Spire's currency; withholding one was like sending a merchant to market with empty hands. Unless the name was dangerous. Unless knowing it changed something in the knower.

Unless, said a small dry voice at the back of her mind, the Prime didn't trust you with it.

Sera closed the ledger and looked at her reflection in the dark window: a woman of thirty-four, sharp-faced, grey-coated, her hair pinned back so severely it might have been evidence. Behind the reflection, the room. On the table, her kit: irons, vials of blank-slate tincture, the long needle-nosed stylus used for extraction, wrapped in oilcloth like a surgical instrument because that was exactly what it was. Everything in its place. Everything accounted for.

Everything except the year.

There it was again, the thought she kept catching like a splinter under a fingernail. *The year.* Her missing year—the gap in her own record, the twelve months between her graduation from the Archive school and her first posting, a span the ledgers covered with a single bland line: *Assigned to internal duties.* Every inquisitor's life was documented down to the meals they ate on the road north. Everyone's except hers, for one year, when she was twenty-two.

She had asked about it once, early, carefully, framing the question as diligence rather than doubt. Her instructor had smiled with real warmth and told her that internal duty years were sealed as a matter of course, to protect ongoing operations, and that a good inquisitor trusted the Ledger the way a good swimmer trusted the water. And Sera had nodded, and stopped asking, and for eleven years the sealed year had sat inside her like a room in her own house whose door she walked past every day without opening.

Tonight, with sleet coming down and a nameless Bearer waiting somewhere above her in the mountains, she found herself standing in front of that door in her mind, hand raised, not quite knocking.

*You failed one already,* said the dry little voice. *Remember?*

She remembered.

* * *

His name had been Aldous Renk, and he had been a choir-master in the fen-town of Miren Low, and he had been forty-one years old, and Sera had been twenty-three and four months into her first field posting when the order came down: a text had surfaced in Miren Low, a book of songs that were not only songs, and the man who kept it was category unreconciled. Retrieve intact. If retrieval proved impossible, erase.

She could still walk the streets of Miren Low in her memory if she let herself—the smell of peat smoke and brackish water, the eels hung in pink ropes outside the fishmonger's, the bell of the drowned chapel tolling hours across the marsh. She had gone to hear him sing first. Protocol recommended observation before contact; the manual said a subject's public face told you how he would wear his private one. So she had stood at the back of the chapel of Miren Low among wet wool coats and coughing children and listened to Aldous Renk teach his choir a song about the flooding of the fens, three hundred years ago, before the Ascendants, before the Ledger—and the song was beautiful, and it was also accurate, and accuracy was the crime.

Because the fens had not flooded, according to every map and record the Archive maintained. The fens had always been fens. The town that drowned there—Corvess, its name survived nowhere but in his song—had never existed. Aldous Renk was teaching forty children to sing the history of a place the world had been made to forget, and he sang it in a bright clear tenor with his eyes shut, and Sera had stood in the back with her grey coat buttoned to the throat and felt something move in her chest that she had no category for either.

She had watched him for six days. She had learned his routines: the market at dawn, the choir at noon, the long evenings in the chapel loft where he kept the book. She had drafted three retrieval plans, each cleaner than the last. And on the seventh night she had climbed to the loft to take him, and found him waiting, sitting calmly with the songbook open on his knees, as though he had expected her the way you expect weather.

"You're one of theirs," he said. Not accusing. Almost gentle. "I wondered when the grey coat would come. My grandmother told me one always comes."

"Give me the book, Master Renk." Her voice had been steady. She had practiced it being steady. "Come south with me. Cooperate fully and the Spire will classify you as a source, not a subject. You'll live."

"And the children?" He turned the page. "Will they live remembering? Because that's the thing about a song, inquisitor. I can burn the book. I've got six copies hidden and I'll tell you where five of them are. But the song's in forty heads now, and heads are not the Archive's to search." He smiled, and it was a terrible kind smile, the smile of a man who had already done his arithmetic. "So we both know how this ends. The question is only whether you do it quickly."

What happened next, Sera had spent eleven years arranging into a shape she could carry. The wardens had come up the loft stairs behind her—she had signaled, of course she had signaled, procedure demanded it—and Aldous Renk had thrown the songbook into the stove, and then he had done the one thing the manual had no section for: he had begun to sing. Loudly. The drowning song, the true one, at full voice, in a wooden building full of sleeping townsfolk, and the wardens had moved to stop him, and in the struggle—

In the struggle, a lantern went over. That was the official finding. *Lantern overturned during subject restraint; fire spread through the chapel loft; subject deceased; structure lost.* Eleven words, and not one of them false, and together they made a lie the way eleven stones make a wall.

Forty children had sung that song. Forty children had watched the chapel burn with their choir-master in it. And Sera Ilvane had stood in the square in her grey coat with ash coming down like grey snow, doing her duty, which was to stand there, visible, while the Ledger's meaning settled over the town: *this is what accuracy costs.*

The Spire had commended her. The commendation was in her file. *Officer Ilvane displayed exemplary composure under adverse conditions.* She had read it a hundred times, hunting for irony, finding none. The Spire did not do irony. The Spire did not even see what there was to forgive.

But at night, in the years after, in whatever town or waystation or billet held her, she sometimes heard the song. Not the melody—she had never learned the melody, had refused to learn it, some stubborn cellar of herself keeping the door shut. Just the fact of it. A man singing truth at the top of his lungs because it was the only pocket large enough to hide it in.

And the thought that came with it, always, arriving like the third knock of a nightmare: *the next one won't burn a book. The next one will run.*

* * *

"Sera." Breck's knock, two soft blows. "Word from the pass station. There's a cartographer works these mountains. Hartwell. Maps the smuggler roads for anyone who pays and asks no questions otherwise. Station master says Hartwell came through two nights back, headed up toward Highmoor. Alone. In weather no sane man travels in."

Sera opened the door. Breck filled it, snow melting in his beard, his round honest face arranged around the particular satisfaction of a man delivering useful news.

"A cartographer," she said.

"Knows every track, cave, and smuggler stair in the Thornfells. If our Bearer's heading anywhere, this fellow could draw us the route—or sell us the man." He paused. "Station master says there's something odd, though. Says Hartwell's been coming through for years, regular as rent, friendly enough, forgettable as bread. But two nights back he paid for his tea with a coin from a mint that closed sixty years ago."

Something cold walked down Sera's spine on small precise feet.

"Sixty years," she repeated.

"So the station master says. Old coin, bright as new. Like it hadn't spent a day in a pocket." Breck shrugged. "Could be a collector's piece. Could be nothing."

Nothing, in Sera's experience, was a word the mountains used to mean *everything*. A man who mapped hidden roads, carrying money that predated its own owner's plausible possession of it, walking alone toward a ruin where a book had woken after a century of sleep. She thought of the withheld name. She thought of Ondrell's pen coming out of retirement.

Or she thought, in the cellar where she kept the things she did not say aloud: *a man who has been hidden so well that even his money is older than his story.*

"Tomorrow we go up," she said. "Both wardens, full kit. We find the workshop first. A man keeps his truths where he keeps his maps."

Breck nodded and went to make ready, his boots loud on the stairs, and Sera stood a moment longer in the doorway, looking north through the inn's leaded window at the Thornfell massif, black against a sky the color of her coat, sleet hardening toward snow.

Highmoor's ruined gardens lay somewhere up in that darkness. The reports called the site abandoned for a hundred years. But the disturbance had originated there, and disturbances of category unreconciled did not originate in dead places. Something up there was awake. Something up there had handed a forgettable cartographer a coin sixty years out of its grave and pointed him at the mountains.

She touched the seven buttons of her coat, one to seven, the way she did before every operation, a habit she had never examined and did not intend to begin examining now.

*Retrieve intact,* said the order in Ondrell's hand. *If retrieval proves impossible, erase.*

Eleven years ago she had stood in a burning square and learned what the second word cost. Tomorrow she would climb toward a man who might be Aldous Renk all over again—or might be something the Spire feared enough to hide his very name from the woman sent to take him.

She found, buttoning the grey coat to the throat, that she could not decide which possibility frightened her more.

Outside, the first true flakes of the storm began to fall, slow and deliberate, like pages coming down out of a torn book—and far up the mountain, unseen, a light moved in the windows of a workshop that would, by tomorrow night, be burning.
