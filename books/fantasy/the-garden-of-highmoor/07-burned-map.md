## 7. Burned Map

Casper came down off the mountain three days after the garden, and the first thing he noticed was the smoke.

Not chimney smoke—chimney smoke was domestic, a grey thread rising straight and lazy from a hearth someone tended. This smoke leaned. It dragged along the valley floor like something wounded trying to crawl home, and it was coming from the direction of his workshop.

He stood at the tree line above Thornfell's lower road, the prophecy book heavy against his ribs where he'd strapped it beneath his coat, and let himself believe, for exactly four breaths, that it was nothing. The tannery. A kiln gone wrong. Old Man Ferrow burning brush out of season, as old men did.

Then the wind shifted and brought him the smell underneath the woodsmoke: oil, hot iron, and something sweeter and worse—the smell of paper burning by the ream. He knew that smell intimately. He had spent his life making things that could burn.

"Tam," he said, to no one, and started down the slope at a run.

* * *

The workshop sat at the end of Cooper's Lane, tucked between a cooperage that hadn't coopered anything in twenty years and a widow's cottage whose garden Casper had mapped once, for free, because she'd asked nicely. It was deliberately unremarkable: a long low building of stone and timber, its windows fogged with the steam of Casper's ink-boiling, its door always unlatched because Tam could never remember to drop the bar and Casper had stopped reminding him.

It was burning from both ends.

Flames stood in the east windows like orange curtains drawn back, and the roof over the drying room had already gone—that was where he kept the finished maps, months of work, the smuggler routes through the Thornfell passes rendered in his own careful hand. He watched ten years of invisible labor curl up into the darkening sky and felt something in his chest go very quiet and very cold, the way a river goes cold under ice while still moving underneath.

There were figures in the lane. Four of them, maybe five, standing back from the heat in a loose semicircle, watching the fire with the patience of men doing a job. They wore grey coats, long ones, buttoned to the throat despite the warmth of the blaze, and they didn't shout orders or pass buckets. Nobody in Thornfell ran toward fires anyway; that was the point of living in a town built on forgetting which neighbors owed which favors. But even so, there was something wrong about how still they were. Men watching a fire want to warm their hands. These men kept them folded behind their backs.

Inquisitors. He'd never seen one, but he knew the shape of them the way a rabbit knows the shape of a hawk—not from experience but from something older than experience, written into the blood.

Casper dropped flat behind the low stone wall of the widow's garden and did what he had trained himself to do his whole life: he became part of the landscape. He made himself small against the wall's shadow, slowed his breathing, let his eyes go soft and unfocused so they wouldn't snag on details. *Never be memorable.* Ten years of practice, and now he used all of it for one purpose—to stay alive long enough to count the men who had burned his life.

Four in the lane. One more on the road beyond, mounted, holding the horses' heads. And—

His heart stopped.

A fifth figure, smaller, being held by the arm at the edge of the lane. Not held gently.

Tam.

The boy was sixteen, all elbows and ink stains, with a laugh like a door falling off its hinges and a habit of asking questions in threes because two never satisfied him. Casper had found him three winters ago sleeping in the workshop's map chest, an orphan off the Low Road with frostbitten fingers and a head full of half-remembered songs, and instead of turning him out Casper had taught him to grind pigments, then to stretch vellum, then to draw a bearing line true. Tam wanted to be a cartographer. Casper had told him, only last week, that next spring he'd start teaching him the mountains themselves.

Now Tam was struggling against the grip of a grey-coated man who held him the way you hold a sack of grain you intend to set down and forget, and Casper heard—faintly, across the lane, across the roar of the fire—one clear boyish voice cracking with fury:

"He isn't here! I told you, he went up-mountain, he won't be back till—"

The man holding him didn't strike him. That was somehow worse. The man simply looked past Tam at the burning workshop, at the flames eating the drying room, and said something too quiet for Casper to catch, and the other inquisitors turned their heads toward him in unison, like birds.

Casper understood then, with a clarity that felt like falling, what kind of men these were. Not men looking for a fugitive. Men completing an inventory. They had come to burn the workshop not because they thought Casper was inside it, but because everything inside it was evidence—and Tam, Tam who slept in the map chest and sang off-key and asked questions in threes, was not evidence.

Tam was a witness.

"No," Casper breathed into the dirt. "No, no—"

He was already moving before he decided to move, sliding along the wall, mind sprinting ahead of his body the way it did when he charted unknown ground. The cooperage backed onto the lane. Its side door hung broken—he knew, he'd mapped it, he mapped everything, and for the first time in his life the knowledge felt like a weapon instead of a hiding place. If he came through the cooperage he'd come out ten paces behind the man holding Tam, downwind of the smoke, in the blind spot every guard forgets because it faces away from the thing worth guarding.

He didn't have a plan past the first ten paces. He told himself that was fine. Maps were just plans, and plans were just guesses wearing good handwriting.

* * *

Later, Casper would try to remember the moment itself and find it had been sanded smooth, the way trauma sands things. He remembered pieces. The cooperage's dead-cold interior, the smell of old oak shavings. The broken door giving under his shoulder without a sound. The lane, the smoke, the grey coats seen now close enough to see the stitching—fine stitching, expensive, government stitching. The man holding Tam turning at the last instant, too slow, his face a pale surprised oval under a hood.

And Tam.

Tam seeing him over the man's shoulder. Tam's face doing three things in one second—relief, terror, and then something else, something Casper would spend the rest of his life failing to name. Not forgiveness. Tam had nothing to forgive. It was more like *permission*. Like the boy was saying, with his whole stricken face: *go, get out, don't you dare ruin your one chance for me.*

"Run, Cas!" Tam shouted, and twisted free—or tried to.

What happened next happened fast and badly. The inquisitor caught Tam's collar. Another stepped in. There was a sound Casper's memory refused to file properly—a short, wet, wrong sound, nothing like the stories—and Tam went down in the lane like a puppet with cut strings, and the man who had struck him didn't even look at what he'd done. He looked at Casper.

That was the worst of it. Not the blow. The look. The inquisitor's face held no anger, no cruelty, not even contempt. It held recognition—the mild, professional satisfaction of a clerk finding the last entry on his list. *There you are,* the face said. *Item five.*

Casper ran.

He hated himself for it even as his legs carried him, hating it, running anyway, because Tam's face had said *go* and because staying would have bought nothing except a second body in the lane. He ran through the widow's garden, trampling her winter kale, over her back fence, down the drainage cut behind the tannery, through bramble and black water and up the far bank, and behind him the town stayed silent, doors shut, shutters closed, everyone in Thornfell performing the oldest survival skill the mountains taught: hearing everything, seeing nothing, remembering less.

Only when he reached the tree line, lungs full of knives, did he stop and turn.

The workshop was fully alight now, a bright wrong sunrise in the middle of the dusk, and the grey coats were mounting their horses. One of them lifted something small from his saddlebag—Casper couldn't see what—and touched it briefly to his own forehead, some gesture, some rite, and then they rode north, unhurried, toward the pass, leaving the fire for the town to discover and grieve and, in time, forget.

They left Tam lying in the lane.

Nobody went to him. Casper watched through the trees for an hour, frozen by the oldest rule of his life—*never be memorable, never be seen, never draw the eye*—watching the boy he loved like a younger brother lie in the mud of Cooper's Lane while the fire roared and the town hid, and the rule that had kept him alive for thirty years became, in that hour, the most obscene thing he had ever known.

When full dark came, he went back.

* * *

He buried Tam himself, in the hours before dawn, in the little rise behind the widow's cottage where the ground was soft and nobody walked. He had no shovel. He dug with a broken tent-stake and his hands, and his hands were ruined by the end of it, split and black with earth, and he did not care. He wrapped Tam in his own cloak, the good one, the oiled wool one that had kept him dry through nine years of mountain rain, and he laid the boy down and covered him, and he could not think of any words. He was a man who worked in words—labels, legends, marginalia, the precise vocabulary of terrain—and he had none. In the end he said only:

"You asked questions in threes. I should have answered better."

Then he sat beside the grave until the sky went the color of ash, and somewhere in those grey hours the cold thing in his chest that had been moving under ice all night finally broke through the surface, and it was not grief anymore.

Grief was a weight. This was a blade.

* * *

Dawn found him back at the ruins of the workshop, because there was one thing he needed and he meant to have it.

The building was a black ribcage, still smoking, the stone walls standing where timber had fallen inward. The heat hit him from twenty feet away, a wall of shimmering air that made the ruins wobble like a bad drawing. He circled to the north wall, to the spot he knew better than his own heartbeat: the drying room, the false-bottomed chest where he kept the field kit. Every cartographer worth his salt kept a duplicate kit hidden, because maps were the first thing bandits burned and the last thing a survivor needed. He'd built the cache into the foundation itself, a stone-lined box under the floor slates, and stone, thank every patient god of small prudent men, does not burn.

The slates were cracked and hot. He wrapped his hands in strips torn from his shirt and levered them up, and there it lay, scorched around the edges but whole: the oilskin roll. Inside—compass, pens, the brass scale, three sticks of ink that had survived by being waxed, a folding knife, flint, a week of pressed travel-bread, and forty feet of good cord. And beneath it all, wrapped in waxed linen, the thing that mattered most: his master map of the Thornfell high passes, the one he'd never shown anyone, the one with the smuggler roads and the hollow ways and the places where the mountain forgot to be a mountain. Singed at one corner. Otherwise perfect.

He took it, and the kit, and stood up in the ashes of his life.

Everything else was gone. His bed. His books. The drawings Tam had pinned to the wall—bad perspective studies, a map of the boy's imaginary homeland with its coastline shaped like a whale, each one labeled in Tam's sprawling hand. Gone. The Ascendants had taken ten years of Casper Hartwell and reduced them to smoke, and they had taken Tam, who had never been part of anything, who had only slept in a map chest and asked questions in threes, and they had spent him like a coin.

Casper stood in the wreckage and let himself feel the fury all the way down, without flinching, the way he'd once forced himself to survey a cliff face in a storm—because if you looked away, the numbers lied.

*Invisible*, he thought. *I made myself invisible. I drew my whole life in pencil so no one could trace it in ink.* And it hadn't saved him. It hadn't saved Tam. Being unremarkable had only guaranteed that when the grey coats came, no one in Thornfell would remember to be angry. The rule hadn't protected him. The rule had disarmed everyone who might have stood beside him.

Well. The invisible life was over. Whatever he was now—the Bearer of the Unwritten, item five on an inquisitor's list, a man whose name was written in a book older than his grandfather—he was visible, and hunted, and done hiding in plain sight.

He took the prophecy book from its strap against his ribs. The leather was warm from his body, unnaturally warm, as it always seemed to be. He opened it to the page with his name and read the lines again in the firelight, and this time he read them not as a victim but as a surveyor reading terrain. *The Bearer walks the low road to the woman of many doors.* He'd assumed it was riddle. Now, with his workshop ash and his friend in fresh earth and grey coats riding the high passes, it sounded less like a riddle and more like instructions.

Madame Vess. The smuggler-queen of the Low Road, dealer in forbidden texts, the one person in the Thornfell country who might know what an Ascendant relic was and why it had chosen to grow his name like a flower.

Going to her meant walking out of the shadows he'd lived in and into something far more dangerous: alliance. Debt. Witness. Everything the rule forbade.

Casper closed the book. Behind him, the sun cleared the eastern ridge and lit the ruin of Cooper's Lane, and the smoke of his old life rose straight and thin into a sky that was already, at the edges, in the far north where the peaks stood white, beginning to fill with cloud.

He settled the field kit on his shoulders, pulled his hood up, and turned south, toward the Low Road, toward the woman of many doors.

"They wrote me into their book," he said quietly, to Tam, to the ashes, to whoever or whatever was listening. "Fine. I'm going to go read the rest of it."

And he walked out of Thornfell without looking back, a man with no house, no trade, no name worth keeping—and, for the first time in thirty years, no intention of being forgotten.
