## 8. The Long Winter Begins

The snow came to Millbrook on the eve of Harvesthome, when the orchards were still heavy and the cider presses had not yet been oiled.

Old Wenna Tull was the first to see it. She had lived seventy-one years in the same stone house beneath the mill race, and she knew the sky the way other folk knew their own hands. The clouds that rolled over Thornfell's shoulder that evening were wrong—too low, too grey-green, carrying a smell like iron and old paper. She stood in her doorway with a basket of late plums and watched the first flakes come down fat and slow as goose feathers, melting against the warm cobbles, then not melting, then lying thick enough to hold a footprint.

"Early," she said to her granddaughter, who was setting the table. "Too early by six weeks."

Pell didn't look up from the spoons. "It's weather, Gran. Weather doesn't keep a calendar."

But Wenna did, because her mother had kept one, and her mother's mother before her, a leather-bound almanac of first frosts and last thorns going back past the Ascension itself. She fetched it down from its shelf that night, blew off the dust, and opened it to write the date—and stopped.

The page for this year was already filled in.

The same handwriting as every other year, her own cramped script, but she hadn't written it. *First snow, Harvesthome eve,* it read. *Six weeks early. The tithe comes twice.* And beneath it, in ink so fresh it shone: *Remember to forget.*

Wenna sat very still for a long time. Then she closed the book, put it back on the shelf, and went to bed without supper, and by morning she could not have said why the almanac frightened her, only that she no longer wished to open it, and that somewhere in the night she had lost the name of the plum tree in her garden—the one her dead husband had planted the spring they wed. It was just a tree now. Just a tree.

* * *

Two hundred miles south, in the salt-terraced city of Candlemere, the memory-tithe wagons came through the Fishgate at dawn.

They always came in autumn—one wagon per district, grey-canvassed, drawn by mules whose eyes had been clouded deliberately, because a beast that remembered too much was worse than useless. The tithe-takers wore the dove-grey coats of the Ascendant Order and moved door to door with their brass censers and their ledgers, and the people of the Salt Quarter lined up in the cold with the patience of long practice.

It was a small thing, the tithe. That was what everyone said. A memory here or there—a face from childhood, a song, the smell of a particular kitchen. The Ascendants called it *civic maintenance*, the pruning of dangerous recollections, and in exchange the Order kept the peace, healed the sick, held back the floods with workings no living engineer could match. Everyone gave a little. Everyone forgot a little. The city ran smooth as poured glass.

But this year there were two wagons.

"I don't understand," said the cooper's wife, Odile Ferran, standing in her doorway with her arms wrapped around herself while a tithe-taker consulted his ledger. "We paid at midsummer. I gave them my grandmother's lullaby. I still miss it."

"The schedule has deepened." The tithe-taker did not look unkind. That was somehow worse. He was young, with chapped hands and a voice like a clerk reading freight manifests. "By order of the Archivist Prime. There's been a... disturbance. A disruption in the records. Until it's resolved, all districts move to double tithe."

"A disturbance. What kind of disturbance?"

He looked at her then, and behind his eyes something flickered—not fear exactly, but the careful blankness of a man standing near a hole in the ice. "You won't remember asking," he said gently, and raised his censer.

Odile woke an hour later on her own kitchen floor with a bruise on her temple and a taste like copper pennies in her mouth, and her youngest daughter was standing over her crying, saying *Mama, Mama, you asked me my name three times*, and Odile reached up to touch the girl's face and found, for one horrible sliding moment, that she was looking at a stranger. A pretty child. Someone's daughter, surely. Whose?

Then it came back, flooding in like water finding its level, and Odile held her daughter so hard the girl squeaked, and neither of them spoke about it again. But that night Odile took a knife and scratched her children's names into the underside of the stair—*Bram, Liss, Tam-who-was-not-hers-to-mourn*—no. She scratched *Bram* and *Liss*, and below them, though she could not say why, she scratched a single letter: *C.*

* * *

In the high passes above Thornfell, where the smuggler roads stitched between the peaks like dark thread, Madame Vess stood at the mouth of the Hollow Stair and watched snow fall upward.

Her lieutenants saw it too—Kestrel and the twins and old Dree, eleven of them huddled under the overhang with their breath smoking—and none of them said anything for a long while, because there is a limit to what hardened criminals will admit to witnessing. The flakes rose. Slowly, steadily, out of the black gorge and into the low clouds, as if the mountain were exhaling everything it had ever caught and held.

"That's not weather," Dree said finally. "That's taking."

"It's the Spire pulling in," Vess said. Her voice was flat. She had seen the Order do strange things in thirty years on the Low Road, but never this, never the sky itself behaving like a ledger being balanced. "They're calling something home. Or calling something *off*."

She thought of the girl in the prophecy book. She thought of the page she wanted—the page she had dreamed about for twenty years, the one that might tell her whether the daughter the Ascendants had scraped out of the world was anywhere at all, whether remembering her was even possible, whether Vess's grief was for a person or for a hole shaped like one. She thought of the cartographer, Hartwell, with his careful eyes and his careful nothing-of-a-life, walking around with a relic in his coat like a man carrying a lit fuse through a powder store.

"Double the watches," she said. "And send word down every road we hold: anyone moving north toward Highmoor gets passage, no questions, no price. Anyone flying the grey coats gets a blade."

Kestrel frowned. "Since when are we running charity?"

"Since the world started forgetting which way is down." Vess turned back to the rising snow. "Move."

* * *

In a village called Ashford Reedy, on the flats below the Thornfell foothills, the schoolteacher Mr. Colm lost his pupils' names all in the same hour.

He was writing the day's lesson on the slate board—arithmetic, harmless arithmetic—when he turned to call on a boy in the third row and found his mouth empty. Not hesitating. Empty. The boy sat there with his hand half-raised, freckled, gap-toothed, waiting, and Colm looked at him the way a man looks at furniture. Around the room, twenty-two faces waited, and they were faces, merely faces, the way stones in a wall are stones.

"Master Colm?" the freckled boy said. "It's me. Jem."

"Jem," Colm repeated, and the sound of it unlocked something, a hairline crack of recognition—but when he turned to the next row it sealed over again, and the next, and the next, and he stood at his board with the chalk dying in his fingers while twenty-two children realized, in the particular silent way children realize things, that their teacher had become a stranger among strangers.

It passed in a quarter hour. Names returned like birds to a hedge. But the children walked home that day in tight quiet clusters, and that evening, without any adult suggesting it, they began a game—or not a game—of repeating each other's names at intervals through the day. *Jem. Jem. You're Jem.* They did not know why. It simply felt necessary, the way scratching feels necessary, and within a week every village in the valley was doing it, and the custom would outlive the winter and the Order both, though none of them would ever recall starting it.

* * *

Sera Ilvane felt the winter begin in her teeth.

She was three days out of Thornfell, riding hard along the King's Road with her warrant folded against her heart and the disturbance-report burning a hole in her mind: *anomalous memory-surge, Highmoor district, source unidentified, retrieval priority absolute.* Behind her rode four wardens; ahead lay the frozen passes and a cartographer who had touched something he should not have known existed.

At the coaching inn at Grey Ford, she noticed the innkeeper's wife pausing over the guest register, pen hovering, staring at her own husband across the room as if trying to place him. At the ferry over the White Water, the ferryman charged her twice and apologized twice, having forgotten between crossings that she'd already paid. Small failures. Frayed stitches. All across the realm, she thought, the great tapestry of recorded memory was coming loose at the edges, and the Order's answer was to pull the threads tighter.

She told herself it was duty that kept her riding north. The Order had raised her, educated her, given her purpose after—after—

After what? The thought arrived and slid away, the way it always did, like a fish slipping under dock pilings. There was a year missing somewhere in her, a seam she had never been permitted to examine, and she had learned young that examining seams was how inquisitors ended up as subjects.

At the last waystation before the high pass, she wrote her nightly entry in her own ledger, the private one, the one no archivist was supposed to know she kept:

*Day 12. Snow in the lowlands. Impossible snow. The tithe has doubled and the people are afraid in the specific way of people who cannot name what they've lost. I am pursuing a mapmaker who found a book. It occurs to me—I record this only because recording is my discipline—that I do not actually know why the book matters. I know only that I was ordered, and that the order frightens someone above me badly enough that they doubled the tithe of an entire realm to pay for it.*

*What are we so afraid he'll remember?*

She read the line back. Then, hands steady with long practice, she struck it through, blotted the page, and slept with the ledger beneath her pillow like a child guarding a stolen sweet.

* * *

Beneath Highmoor, in the hollow where frost bloomed in patterns no ice had made, Maren the elder counted her people and found them dimming.

There had been two hundred and forty of the Unwritten when Casper Hartwell first stumbled down among them—two hundred and forty souls the Ascendants had erased, sustained now only by the garden's stubborn remembering, names kept alive the way embers are kept alive, cupped and fed and shielded from wind. Maren had tended the count for sixty years. Each dawn she walked the hollow and spoke every name aloud, and each dawn the names answered in faces.

This morning, three did not.

Old Sorrel was still there, sitting by the warm root-door where he always sat, but when Maren said *Sorrel* he looked up with the mild blankness of a man hearing a word in a foreign tongue. Little Iva, seven years erased and eight years old forever, did not come to the counting at all; they found her by the underground pool, watching the water, and when Maren knelt and said her name the child tilted her head and asked, politely, *Are you talking to me?*

Maren did not weep. Weeping was a luxury the Unwritten had traded away generations ago. She took Iva's cold hands in hers and began, quietly, to tell her a story—about a girl who loved frogs, who had once smuggled a live frog into the Ascendant tithe-house in her shoe, who had laughed so hard at the resulting chaos that she snorted, and how her mother had sworn never to let her forget it, and how her mother had been made to forget it anyway, and how the story survived regardless, because stories are harder to kill than names.

"And the girl's name," Maren finished, "was Iva."

The child considered this with grave brown eyes. "Is that me?"

"That's you."

"Will it stay me?"

Maren looked up, through forty feet of earth and root, toward the ruined greenhouse where a book lay open on a stone shelf, its pages stirring in air that did not move. Toward the surface, where the first impossible snow was falling on the garden walls. Toward the north, where a young man with a borrowed name was learning what he carried.

"Not unless we're quick," she said. "Not unless we're very, very quick."

* * *

And in a rented room above a chandler's shop in the river town of Marleford, a woman named Betha woke at midnight from a dream of her son.

He had been taken by the Order nineteen years ago—she knew this the way she knew her own heartbeat, though the official record, when she had once dared to request it, showed no such child ever born to her, no such seizure ever conducted, no such woman as Betha of Marleford ever filing a grievance. Nineteen years, and every night she dreamed him: the gap in his smile, the cowlick, the way he'd hummed smuggler tunes learned off the passing barges.

Tonight the dream was different. Tonight he was older, and standing in a garden full of out-of-season flowers, and turning toward her as if he'd heard his name spoken from very far away.

Betha woke with tears already drying on her face and the certainty, sudden and total, that the winter outside her window was not weather. It was a theft in progress. Something vast was being carried off in the night, and the snow falling six weeks early was the thieves covering their tracks.

She got up, dressed, and took down the bundle she had kept for nineteen years beneath a floorboard: her son's swaddling cloth, a lock of his hair tied with thread, and a scrap of parchment on which, in a younger woman's desperate hand, was written the only thing the tithe-takers had never managed to find and take.

A name. His real one. The one they had erased.

She blew out the candle, tucked the bundle inside her coat, and set out before dawn, walking north, toward the mountains, toward Highmoor, following a pull she could not explain and did not try to.

Behind her, the snow fell thicker. Ahead of her, in a walled garden that remembered everything, a book waited with its pages open—and on the road between, unseen by any watcher, the threads of a dozen small lives were already drawing tight around one man who had only ever wanted to be forgotten.
