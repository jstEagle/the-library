## 9. Vess's Price

The Low Road smelled of tallow and wet stone, and it went down farther than any road had a right to.

Casper counted the turns as they descended—forty-one switchbacks, three flooded galleries, a staircase carved by hands that had never seen daylight. He counted because counting was what he did. A cartographer who stopped measuring the world around him was a man asking to be lost in it, and Casper Hartwell had spent thirty years making certain he was never lost, never found, and never remembered longer than it took to pay him.

Madame Vess walked ahead with a lantern she didn't need. The smuggler-queen of Thornfell was sixty if she was a day, broad as a door and twice as hard to open, and her eyes caught the light like coins at the bottom of a well. She hadn't spoken since the tunnel mouth. That, more than anything, told him how seriously she was taking this.

"You knew," he said finally, his voice bouncing off wet rock. "When I came to you about the book. You already knew what it was."

"I knew it was Ascendant work." Her boots rang on the stone. "I didn't know it would have your name in it. That part's your own bad luck."

"Is it luck?"

She stopped so abruptly he nearly walked into her. When she turned, the lantern threw her shadow huge against the tunnel wall—a giantess made of tallow-light, holding him in one fist.

"You want to ask questions, cartographer, or you want to live long enough to stop asking them?" She let the silence sit between them like a blade laid flat on a table. Then, softer: "Questions are fine down here. It's up there they get you killed."

They walked on.

* * *

Her hall was a drowned cathedral.

Some forgotten king had built it into the mountain's root before the Ascendants ever learned to write their names in other people's heads, and now smugglers moved through its colonnades with crates of unlicensed ink, memory-tithe exemptions forged on good vellum, and the occasional person who needed to become someone else by morning. Candles burned in every niche—not for light, Vess explained, but because "the dark remembers things you'd rather it didn't."

She fed him first. Salt mutton, black bread, tea brewed from something that tasted like pine needles and regret. He ate like a man who'd been running for four days, because he had been. The workshop was ash behind him. Tam was ash behind him. He kept not thinking about that, and kept failing, and kept eating anyway, because grief was a country too and even grief required provisions.

Vess watched him eat from across the table, chin propped on one fist, and when he finally set down the spoon she said:

"The book. Show me."

He almost refused. Every instinct honed over thirty years of staying invisible screamed at him to keep the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth and pressed to his ribs, to lie, to run. But Tam was dead because Casper had been careless, and the garden had chosen him for reasons he didn't understand, and there was nowhere left in Thornfell where an invisible man could stand still. So he unwrapped it.

The book sat on Vess's table like a sleeping animal. Its cover was grey leather gone soft with age, and the pages inside were not paper—he'd checked, with a lens and a knife-tip and all the professional curiosity of his trade—but something thinner and older, like skin cured in moonlight. The writing shifted when you weren't looking directly at it. He'd learned to read it sideways, out of the corner of his eye, the way you read stars.

Vess didn't touch it. She leaned close instead, hands flat on the table on either side of it, and read the way he imagined a priest reads scripture—with hunger held on a very short leash.

"Where did you find it."

"Highmoor. The old garden. There's a greenhouse behind a wall of hawthorn—"

"I know where the greenhouse is." Something crossed her face then, quick as a fish under ice. "Everyone on the Low Road knows Highmoor. We just don't go there."

"Why not?"

"Because the flowers bloom in winter, cartographer, and nothing that blooms in winter blooms for free." She straightened. "This book is a rebel relic. Pre-Conquest. Do you understand what that means? Before the Ascendants won their war of forgetting, before they taught the world that memory is a tax to be paid rather than a thing to be owned—someone fought them with this. Someone wrote down what was going to happen, or what they wanted to happen, and bound it so it couldn't be unwritten." Her finger hovered above the page without touching. "And now it has your name in it, which means the Ascendants' inquisitors will burn half a mountain range to get it back. Which means I am currently sitting in the blast radius of a war that ended two hundred years ago."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." She looked at him, and for the first time he saw past the coin-bright eyes to something tired underneath, something that had been carrying a weight for decades and had learned to walk bent so no one would notice. "You came to me because I move things through mountains. Fine. I can move you. There's a route—the Pass of False Stars—that doesn't exist on any map, including yours, including mine. It exists because people walk it and remember it, and right now that's the only kind of road left worth trusting."

"And the price?"

Vess smiled. It was not a comforting sight.

"There it is. The cartographer's question. Everything has coordinates; everything has a cost." She turned the book toward herself with one finger, gently, the way you'd turn a card you suspected was rigged. "There's a page in here. Near the middle. I need it."

"A page."

"One page. You'll know it when you see it—it'll be the only entry written in a different hand. Older. Angrier." Her voice did something complicated on the word *angrier*. "Bring it to me, and you walk the False Stars free of charge, with my people watching your back the whole way."

"And if I don't?"

"Then you can try the high passes in early winter with inquisitors behind you and wardens ahead. I hear the snows are starting early this year." She said it lightly, but the lightness had edges. "Look at me, Casper Hartwell. I'm not threatening you. I'm telling you the shape of the world. The Long Winter is coming—the real one, the one the Ascendants have been feeding for two centuries—and when it closes the passes, no one moves, no one runs, and everyone forgets. You want to cross before that happens? This is the toll."

He studied her. Thirty years of reading faces—the client whose eyes lingered a half-second too long on his cheekbones, the courier whose smile didn't reach the muscles around them—thirty years of that had made him good at seeing what people hid. And what Madame Vess hid, beneath the greed and the granite, was grief. Old, specific, load-bearing grief.

"Who's on the page?" he asked quietly.

The silence stretched long enough that somewhere in the colonnade a candle guttered and was relit by a passing hand.

"My daughter," Vess said at last.

Casper blinked. In all his years on the Low Road he'd heard everything about Madame Vess—her empire, her ruthlessness, the man she'd allegedly fed to his own ledgers—but never a daughter. No one had ever mentioned a daughter.

"I don't—" he began.

"No," she agreed. "You don't. Nobody does. That's rather the point." She pulled out a chair and sat, heavily, like a woman setting down cargo. "Twenty-six years ago I had a girl. Bright as a struck flint. And the Ascendants came through Thornfell doing one of their periodic corrections—a village here, a family there, anyone whose records didn't reconcile. My girl's name didn't reconcile. Some archivist with neat handwriting decided she was a discrepancy, and discrepancies get erased. Not killed. Erased. One morning I woke up and my neighbors smiled at me with pity, because as far as everyone remembered, Madame Vess had lost a baby years ago and never quite recovered. Grief they allowed me. A living daughter, they did not."

The candles flickered. Somewhere far off in the tunnels, water dripped with the patience of mountains.

"They took her name out of the world," Vess said. "Out of parish rolls, out of my own head—I can't tell you her birthday, cartographer. I can't tell you the color of her eyes. I know I loved her the way you love a wound, which is to say constantly and without being able to show anyone the injury. And then, eleven years ago, a runner brings me word of a book in a ruined garden, a book of prophecies, and I send men to look, and they come back saying there's a page in it written in a hand like a knife-cut, and it names a girl." Her jaw worked. "It names my girl. Says she's alive. Says she's *written somewhere else*. Whatever that means."

"You believe it?"

"I believe the book cost me three good men to retrieve and nearly a fourth to carry, and I believe nothing that expensive lies." She fixed him with those coin-bright eyes. "But I'm old, and I'm careful, and I don't touch Ascendant relics with my own hands. Things named in that book end up either weapons or kindling, and I've spent twenty-six years choosing neither. So. You're already named. You're already damned. One more page in your arms won't change your trajectory, but it will change mine."

"That's why you need me to take it."

"That's why I need *you* to take it." She leaned forward. "If my people steal it, and the inquisitors trace the theft, they trace it to me, and everything I've built—every route, every safehouse, every soul I've smuggled out from under the tithe—burns. But if the Bearer of the Unwritten takes it? If the man the book itself chose lifts the page?" A grim smile. "Then the theft isn't a crime. It's prophecy. Even Ondrell's hounds have to respect that."

The name landed in Casper's chest like a dropped stone. Ondrell. He'd heard it only once before, whispered by the dying stranger on the pass—the stranger who had died at midnight exactly as the book scheduled, whose last breath had been spent gasping a warning: *the Archivist Prime sees everything written.*

"You know Ondrell," Casper said.

"Everyone on the Low Road knows Ondrell the way sailors know a storm front. You watch it coming and you adjust your sails and you pray." Vess stood, and the audience was clearly over. "The page is yours to lift, cartographer. Tonight, tomorrow, on the road—I don't care. But we don't move until I hold it."

* * *

He read the book that night in the room she gave him—a cell, honestly, with a straw pallet and a candle stub and a door that locked from the outside, though he pretended not to notice that last part.

He read it sideways, out of the corner of his eye, and the entries swam up at him like fish rising to feed.

*A cartographer shall shelter from rain among the hawthorns.* True. *The bearer shall watch a death scheduled under false stars.* True, and it still turned his stomach. *The workshop burns; the apprentice pays the debt of visibility.* He'd stopped reading after that one, the night it happened, and picked the book up again only because the alternative was screaming.

Now he turned the pages toward the middle, hunting for a different hand.

He found it near midnight. The entry was shorter than the others, and the script was jagged, urgent, gouged into the strange skin-paper as if the writer had been afraid of being interrupted—or erased—before finishing. Out of the corner of his eye, the letters steadied just enough to read:

*The girl sold twice, beloved and bartered, waits beneath the roots of remembering. Her mother's bargain buys her passage. Her mother's doubt unmakes it.*

Casper read it three times. Then a fourth, slower, because the cartographer in him had noticed something the grieving son in him wished it hadn't: the entry wasn't a prophecy. Not really. It was a *condition*. The others described events—fixed, scheduled, inevitable. This one described terms. *Her mother's bargain buys her passage. Her mother's doubt unmakes it.*

A contract, with Vess's faith as the signature.

And contracts, in Casper's experience, always contained clauses the signer hadn't read.

He thought about waking her. He thought about the way she'd said *beloved and bartered*, and the fish-under-ice expression when he'd mentioned the greenhouse, and the fact that a woman who ruled the Low Road had sent three men to die retrieving a book she refused to touch herself. He thought about Tam, who had trusted him, and who was ash.

He thought about the shape of the world, as Vess would say, and the shape of the world was this: he could not cross the mountains alone, and she could not cross her own history alone, and they were both standing at the same toll gate pretending the price was simple.

In the morning, he knocked on her door and held out the page.

Vess took it with both hands. He watched this woman who commanded a hundred tunnels and could buy and sell the gentry of three valleys look down at a scrap of moonlight-cured skin as if it were a newborn—which, he supposed, in every way that mattered, it was. Her lips moved. No sound came out. For one unguarded moment Madame Vess of the Low Road looked exactly like what she was underneath: a mother who had been robbed, holding the first receipt she'd gotten in twenty-six years.

Then the moment closed over, like water over a dropped coin.

"It doesn't say what I hoped," she said quietly.

"It says she's waiting. Beneath the roots of remembering." He hesitated. "Vess—the entry calls you *beloved and bartered*. And it says your doubt unmakes her passage. That's not a prophecy. That's terms. Someone wrote a bargain into that book with your name on it, and I don't think the writer was the Ascendants."

"No," Vess said softly. "The Ascendants erase. They don't leave receipts." She folded the page into her bodice, over her heart, with the care of a woman arming herself. "Which means whoever wrote it wanted me to find it. Wanted me to *send* you to find it." Her eyes narrowed, coin-bright again, and for a moment Casper saw the mind behind the granite turning like a millwheel. "And that means, cartographer, that the book has been playing us both from the start—and I intend to find out what it wants before it finishes getting it."

She turned for the door, then paused.

"We leave for the Pass of False Stars at dusk. Eat. Sleep. And Casper—" she didn't look back at him, and her voice was quieter than he'd ever heard it, "—whatever the book has written for you next, remember that I've read enough prophecies to know they only come true for people who cooperate."

The door shut behind her.

Casper stood alone in the candlelit cell, and out of habit—the oldest habit he had—he reached for the book to check the next entry.

The page fell open on its own.

And there, beneath the entry about the scheduled death, beneath the burning workshop, beneath all the lines that had already come true, a new line was surfacing through the skin-paper like a bruise rising, in handwriting he was beginning to recognize as the book's own:

*The bearer crosses the False Stars with a traitor at his side.*

Casper stared at it for a long time.

Then he blew out the candle, because some things you're better off not reading by light.
