## 11. Sera's Ledger

The village of Lowmere had forty-one souls before the tithe-takers came through last autumn. Now it had thirty-eight, and one of the missing was a girl of nine whose mother could no longer say her name.

Sera Ilvane learned this in the first hour, standing in a kitchen that smelled of woodsmoke and boiled barley, watching a woman's face go smooth and blank as poured wax when she tried to answer.

"Your daughter," Sera said. "Tell me about her."

"She—" The woman's hands twisted in her apron. Her eyes went to the window, to the frost-killed garden beyond, and something moved behind them like a fish under ice. "There was a cradle. I kept a cradle by the fire. I don't know why I keep it. It's empty. It has always been empty."

"Has it?"

"I—" The woman blinked, and the fish vanished beneath the ice again. "It has always been empty, Inquisitor. Forgive me. I'm tired."

Sera closed her ledger. She did not write anything in it. There was nothing to write; the woman remembered nothing because there was nothing left to remember, and the extraction had been clean work, no ragged edges, no bleeding grief. Whoever had taken the girl had taken her *tidily*.

That was what bothered Sera. Not the theft itself—she had witnessed a hundred tithes in nine years of service, and she had long ago stopped tasting copper in her mouth when she watched them—but the craftsmanship of this one. Sloppy extractions left scars. This was surgical. This was the work of someone who cared about the quality of the forgetting.

Someone with archive access.

She thanked the woman, who did not thank her back, and stepped out into the grey morning. Lowmere clung to the lower slopes of Thornfell like lichen to stone: crooked roofs, one well, a shrine to the Ascendants' Nine Faces that nobody tended anymore except out of fear. Snow was coming down in fat, unhurried flakes, though the almanacs said winter was still three weeks out. The whole realm had been saying that for a month now, and the whole realm had been wrong.

Her junior, Warden Pell, waited by their horses, stamping his feet and breathing on his gloved fingers. He was twenty-three, earnest, and terrified of her in a way she found useful and occasionally exhausting.

"No luck?" he asked.

"The mother's been scraped." Sera mounted. "Clean job. Archive-grade."

Pell's eyebrows rose. "You think the Order took a child from its own tithe district? That's not— we don't do that, Inquisitor. Tithes are for surplus memory. Old grudges, dead loves, things people *want* to be rid of. We don't take children."

"We take whatever the ledger says is owed." She said it flatly, without inflection, because inflection invited questions, and questions were how wardens started becoming problems. But the words sat strangely in her mouth as she rode out of the village, because she had spoken them a thousand times and never once heard how they sounded.

*We take whatever the ledger says is owed.*

The disturbance that had pulled her to Thornfell was three weeks old now: a ripple in the memory-field centered somewhere near Highmoor, the kind of anomaly that happened when forbidden texts were handled by untrained minds. Her orders were precise. Locate the source. Retrieve or erase the Bearer. The word *Bearer* had been capitalized in her orders, which meant it came down from above the regional level, which meant someone in the Spire itself considered this important enough to spend an inquisitor on a mountain province where nothing had happened for a hundred years.

She should have felt flattered. Instead, all morning, riding through snow that fell too early and too gently, like ash, she had felt the particular unease of a map that doesn't match the territory.

* * *

The confiscated ledgers were stored in the tithe-house at Greyford, two days' ride north—a squat stone building with iron-bound doors and a cellar that smelled of lamp oil and cold paper. Sera had requisitioned access dozens of times. Confiscated memory-ledgers were the raw material of the Order's work: transcriptions of extracted memories, bound and catalogued, waiting to be archived or destroyed according to classification.

She told Pell to see to the horses. She told him she would be several hours. She did not tell him why, because she did not yet have a why. She had only a splinter.

The splinter had come from the mother's kitchen. When the woman had looked at the empty cradle, for half a heartbeat—less than half, a sliver of a heartbeat—her expression hadn't been confused. It had been *familiar*. The look of someone greeting an old acquaintance. And Sera had recognized the look because—

Because she knew it.

Because there was a room in her own childhood she could not enter. Not a trauma; the mind-healers of the Spire academy had examined her twice and found nothing. Just a gap, smooth-walled, like a tooth socket long since healed over. A year, roughly. Her ninth year. She had grown up in the Ascendant nursery-foundling system, as most inquisitors did, and the records said she'd spent that year at a satellite school in the eastern provinces recovering from a fever. She had seen the records herself, once, as a cadet. Neat handwriting. Official seal.

She had never thought about it again until this morning, in a poor woman's kitchen, watching a poor woman's face greet an absence like a friend.

Now she stood in the Greyford cellar with a lamp turned low and four hundred ledgers shelved around her, and she told herself she was here to cross-reference the Highmoor disturbance against older extraction records from the region. That was even true. It was simply not the whole truth, and the distance between those two things was exactly the width of a healed-over gap in a nine-year-old's memory.

Ledgers were organized by extraction date and district. She pulled the volumes for the eastern provinces, the correct years, and began.

* * *

Three hours later, her lamp had burned down twice and her hands were cold to the wrist, and she found it.

Not what she was looking for. That was the thing she would remember afterward—the cruelty of it, the precision. She had been searching for her own name, her own record, some entry marked *Ilvane, S., age 9, fever, relocation approved.* She had expected, if she found anything, to find a mundane explanation. A clerical error. A quarantine.

What she found instead was a different ledger entirely. One shelf over. A volume from the western districts, the year she would have been eight, misfiled—or not misfiled, she realized, her stomach tightening, but *shelved among the sealed classifications*, the ones that required a countersignature she didn't have. Except someone had shelved it carelessly, or deliberately, one finger's width outside the sealed row, close enough to be reached by a tall woman with cold hands and a lamp.

She should have put it back. Procedure was procedure; touching a sealed classification without authorization was a flogging offense, possibly worse. Her hand was already on the spine.

She opened it.

The entries inside were ordinary tithe-work at first: farmers, ferry-men, a midwife who had seen something during a difficult birth that the Order preferred unseen. Transcribed memories, rendered in the flat second-person voice the archivists used, as if the rememberer were being addressed across a table:

*You are standing in a doorway. The rain has just stopped. You are happy, though you will not remember why.*

Sera turned pages. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above her, Pell's boots crossed the floorboards, and she stilled like a thief until they faded.

Then, near the middle of the volume, the transcription changed character. The hand grew smaller, tighter. The memories were not tithes. They were observations. Field notes, almost—and the subject of the notes was a child.

*Subject demonstrates unusual retention despite repeated application. Age seven. Asked today where she goes in her dreams. Answer: "the garden." No garden within forty miles of the facility. Recommend escalation.*

Sera's breath stopped.

She knew that answer. She knew it the way you know a scar's shape with your tongue. When she was small—before the academy, before the white corridors and the memory-hygiene drills—she had dreamed, every night, of a garden. Walled. Green. Full of flowers that bloomed in defiance of the season, and paths that rearranged themselves when no one watched, and a smell of warm earth and old paper. She had described it once, eagerly, to a caretaker. She had never described it again.

She had forgotten describing it. Until now.

Her hands were shaking. She made them stop, the way she'd been trained, and read on.

*Subject continues to reference the garden. Application increased to full suppression. Note: suppression holds but does not take cleanly. Subject retains affective residue. Recommend reassignment rather than continued effort. Some material resists extraction without damage to the vessel.*

Affective residue. That was what the healers had called her gap-tooth year, wasn't it—not in those words, but that was the shape of it. They had assumed the gap was the wound. It had never occurred to her, in twenty-six years, that the gap might be the *bandage*. That underneath it there might be something so stubborn the Order's own craft had failed to dig it out, and so they had simply buried it and paved over the grave.

She turned the page. The next entry was dated nearly a year later—the year of her fever, the year of the satellite school, the year that wasn't.

*Subject transferred per recommendation. Final note before transfer: residual dream-content persists at low intensity. Garden imagery stable. Recommend follow-up in five years. If residue strengthens, subject may require full erasure. Signed—*

The signature was a cipher, the archivists' mark, unreadable to anyone without the key. But below it, in the margin, in a different hand—hastier, almost furtive—someone had written a single line:

*If she asks about the garden again, tell me before anyone else.*

Sera stood very still in the cold cellar with the lamp guttering and the ledger open in her hands, and she listened to her own heart, and she understood, with the calm clarity that comes at the center of an avalanche, that she had spent her entire life inside a sentence someone else was writing.

They had taken her year. They had taken her garden. They had taken whatever she had been before she was Sera Ilvane, inquisitor, and they had done it not to punish her but to *manage* her—because a child who dreamed true dreams was a liability, and a managed child was an instrument. She was an instrument. She had always known it in the way instruments know their handles, and she had called it discipline, and devotion, and duty.

She closed the ledger. Opened it again. Checked the district, the date, the classification code. Memorized everything, because she could not take the volume—it would be missed within the day, and a missing ledger was a confession.

Then she sat down on the cold floor with her back against the shelves, and let herself shake, briefly, thoroughly, the way she allowed herself nothing else. Two minutes. She counted them.

When she stood, her face was smooth again. Poured wax. She had learned that trick from the best.

* * *

Pell was waiting in the yard, brushing snow off the horses. He brightened when she emerged. "Any joy, Inquisitor?"

"Regional extractions show nothing relevant to the disturbance." Her voice came out level, bored, perfect. "I want copies of the tithe schedules for the Thornfell districts going back ten years sent to my field desk. And Pell—"

"Yes?"

"If anyone from the regional office asks what I consulted in the cellar, tell them the Greyford land-dispute rolls. Nothing else."

He blinked. "Is there something wrong with the—"

"There's always something wrong with the land-dispute rolls. That's why no one ever checks them." She swung into the saddle. "Ride."

He rode. He was a good boy, Pell. She hoped, distantly, that whatever happened next would not require her to ruin him.

They climbed out of Greyford through falling snow, up the switchbacks toward the high passes and the ruined garden at Highmoor that her maps insisted was abandoned and her instincts now insisted was the opposite of abandoned. The Bearer was up there somewhere. A man named Casper Hartwell, according to the parish rolls—a cartographer, unremarkable, the sort of man the world was built to overlook. Her orders said retrieve or erase.

But her orders came from the same hand that had signed off, twenty years ago, on burying a dreaming child alive inside a stranger's name. And the margin-note in that sealed ledger—*tell me before anyone else*—had been written by someone who wanted the garden found, and had been waiting a very long time for a hunter foolish enough to go looking for her own past in the Order's cellars.

Snow thickened. Behind them, Greyford sank into the white. Ahead, the pass toward Highmoor opened like a door.

Sera rode toward it with a stranger's memories folded against her ribs like stolen letters, and for the first time in nine years of service, she understood what the villagers felt when the tithe-takers came.

She was not the hunter. She had never been the hunter.

She was simply the hound who had finally caught the scent of her own missing year—and the leash ran all the way back to the Spire, to a hand she could not yet see, holding it.
