## 12. The Pass of False Stars

Vess woke him an hour before dawn by pouring cold water over his head.

"Up," she said. "The wardens reached Lowmarket at midnight. By now they'll have your description in every garrison from here to the coast." She tossed him a bundle of grey wool — a shepherd's coat, patched and reeking of tallow. "You're a drover named Pell. You're stupid, you're slow, and you complain about your feet. Can you manage that?"

"I've been playing unremarkable my whole life," Casper said, wringing water from his hair. "Stupid is a stretch."

"Then act." She was already moving through the waystation's single room, checking straps on her pack with quick, economical hands. In the lamplight he could see the map of scars along her forearms, each one a story she'd never told him. "We take the White Road as far as the cairns, then cut north into the pass. No one uses the pass."

"Why not?"

"Because the stars are wrong there," Vess said, as if that were an answer to everything, and in a way it was. "Smugglers are superstitious people, and superstition keeps us alive. The pass is bad luck. Which means it's empty. Which means it's ours."

*Ours*, he noted. Not *yours*. Somewhere between Lowmarket and this freezing waystation, Madame Vess of the Low Road had stopped talking about moving cargo and started talking about moving *him*, and Casper didn't know what to do with that except carry it carefully, like something breakable.

They left before first light, when the frost lay so thick on the stones that their boots printed silver. Behind them the Thornfell peaks stood black against a sky just beginning to bruise with colour; ahead, the road climbed through pine shadow into country Casper knew only from other men's maps — maps drawn by drovers who'd never gone higher than the cairns, because above the cairns was the pass, and above the pass was nothing anyone wanted.

He'd spent three days with Vess now, long enough to learn her rhythms. She walked like a woman reading a book only she could see: every bend anticipated, every loose stone known before her boot found it. Twice she stopped without explanation, tilted her head, listened, and moved on. Once she pressed two fingers to the trunk of a lightning-split pine and stood there for a long moment, eyes closed, and Casper had the strange certainty that she was asking the tree something.

"You can feel them," he said. "The wardens."

"I can feel *wrongness*," Vess said. "Wardens are mostly wrongness wearing coats." She glanced back at him, and for once there was no mockery in her face. "Your friend Tam. He died quick?"

Casper's throat closed. He looked at the road.

"That's not an answer," Vess said quietly.

"He burned," Casper said finally. "The workshop. They set it and they waited outside for whoever ran." He heard his own voice go flat, cartographer-flat, the voice he used for distances and elevations. "So I don't know if it was quick. I know he didn't run."

Vess said nothing for a long time. Then: "The Ascendants call what they do *correction*. Like we're errata." She spat, precisely, off the edge of the trail. "Keep walking, Pell. Stupid drovers don't cry on mountainsides."

* * *

They reached the cairns at midday.

There were nine of them, stacked by hands long dead, each stone furred with lichen the colour of old bone. Beyond them the pines gave out all at once, as if a line had been drawn that living things respected, and the pass opened ahead: a grey throat of rock rising between two walls of cliff, scree underfoot, wind keening down from the icefields with a sound almost like speech.

Vess stopped at the ninth cairn and did something Casper hadn't seen her do before. She took off one glove, pressed her bare palm flat against the topmost stone, and murmured — not words, or not words he caught, something older and lower, a sound like a name being said backwards.

"What are you doing?" he asked.

"Paying the toll." She pulled the glove back on. "Every road has a keeper. Most roads' keepers are dead, but dead doesn't mean gone. Up here especially." She looked up at the sky, and Casper followed her gaze, and felt the first true cold of the day slide down his spine.

The sun stood high and white, but the stars were visible around it — faint, needle-thin points scattered across the blue like frost on glass. That alone would have been strange enough. But Casper had spent thirty years reading skies. He had navigated by stars through cloud, through storm, through nights so black he'd trusted a single point of light with his life. And these stars were wrong. Not dim, not displaced — *wrong*. They sat in patterns no sky had ever held. Constellations he half-recognized and then couldn't recognize at all, shapes that seemed to shift when he wasn't looking directly at them, the way words in a dream refuse to be read twice the same way.

"They're not our stars," he said.

"No." Vess started up the pass. "This valley took a wound, long ago. Before the Ascendants, even. Something happened here that the world never finished healing, and the sky over it never quite agreed to be the sky again." She walked with her head slightly bowed, watching her feet, and Casper noticed she did not look up again. "Old smuggler wisdom: navigate by the ground in the false-star pass, never by the heavens. The ground at least pretends to tell the truth."

Casper looked up anyway. He couldn't help it. One star near the zenith pulsed, slow and deliberate, like a lamp being signalled — and he had the sudden, irrational conviction that it was looking back.

"The garden knows things like this," he said. "Places where memory pooled wrong. Maren called them scar-country."

"Maren says a lot of things." But Vess's voice had tightened. "Walk, Hartwell."

* * *

They made good distance through the afternoon, and the pass narrowed, and the wrongness deepened.

It wasn't only the stars. It was the echoes. Sound behaved strangely in the gorge — their footsteps came back to them a half-beat late, then a full beat, then not at all, and once Casper heard his own boot-crunch return from *ahead* of them, from somewhere up the trail, as though someone else were walking the same path in the same boots a few minutes in the future. The light itself had a quality he couldn't name, a thinness, as though the whole valley were viewed through old glass. Shadows fell at angles that didn't match anything.

And twice, Casper saw figures on the ridgelines. Still, upright, watching. When he stopped to look, they weren't there. When he walked on, the prickle between his shoulder blades returned.

"Don't look at them," Vess said, the second time, without turning her head.

"There's *people* up there—"

"There's nothing up there. That's the trick of it. This place remembers travellers who aren't travelling anymore, and it likes to show them off." Her hand rested on the knife at her belt, casual as breathing. "A smuggler who stops to wave at the ridge becomes a story the ridge tells. Keep your eyes on the scree and your mouth shut."

But it was Vess herself who broke the rule, an hour later, when the light began to fail.

She stopped so abruptly Casper nearly walked into her. Ahead, the pass bent around a shoulder of black rock, and beyond the bend — Casper saw it too — a glow. Faint, orange, flickering. Firelight, where no fire should be. No honest traveller camped in the false-star pass. No dishonest one either.

Vess raised a fist. Casper froze. For ten heartbeats neither of them moved, and the wind moaned down the gorge, and the echo of the wind arrived late and wrong, and somewhere above them the false stars burned on, indifferent.

Then Vess moved — fast, silent, flowing along the rock wall like water finding its level — and Casper followed, copying her, putting his feet where hers had been. They rounded the bend low and slow.

The campfire was real. Three bedrolls. A cook-pot steaming. And standing at the edge of the firelight, looking directly at them, a man in a grey coat.

Not a warden's coat — Casper's stomach dropped and then rose again, confused. Grey, yes, but travel-worn, hooded, the coat of a pilgrim or a pedlar. The man was young, hollow-cheeked, and he was staring at them with an expression of such naked terror that Casper took a step forward before he could think better of it.

"Please," the man said. His voice echoed strangely, arriving before his lips moved. "I've been walking for days. I got turned around. Please, I just need—"

"*Down!*" Vess roared, and hit Casper between the shoulders with the flat of her hand, and the world exploded.

* * *

Later, Casper would try to reconstruct those minutes and find them full of holes, like a map of a coastline that keeps eroding.

He remembered the fire going out — not dying, but going *out*, snuffed between one heartbeat and the next, and the young man's face in the sudden dark changing, smoothing, becoming serene and terrible. He remembered the bedrolls emptying, the cook-pot tipping, the whole camp folding away like scenery, because it had been scenery, it had been bait dressed in a dead man's memory. He remembered Vess screaming a word that wasn't a word, and the cliffs answering her, and the false stars overhead flaring all at once like a skyful of struck matches.

He remembered the wardens coming off the ridgeline.

Six of them. Maybe eight. Grey coats, grey hoods, faces like unfinished sketches — he could see them clearly in motion and could not describe a single feature afterward. They came down the scree without disturbing a single stone, which was somehow worse than if they'd thundered. And the lead warden raised one hand, palm out, and spoke a syllable that Casper felt in his teeth, in the roots of his hair, in the memory of his mother's voice —

— and Casper's name began to leave him.

He felt it go. Felt *Casper* loosening like a knot pulled from wet rope, felt the shape of himself start to drain out through some wound in the air. This was what the tithe was, he understood suddenly, viscerally, kneeling in the scree with his mind unravelling: not forgetting, not exactly. Being *edited*. Someone else holding the pen.

Then Vess was there, in front of him, arms spread wide, and she shouted — not the backwards-name from the cairn but plain Low Road cant, filthy and furious and utterly human — and she *cut* the air with her knife, a gesture like signing her name on something, and the syllable broke apart.

"RUN!" she screamed. "North fork, the split rock, don't wait, DON'T WAIT—"

A warden closed on her. Casper saw the grey sleeve sweep out, saw light bend around it wrong, saw Vess turn to meet it with her knife low and her weight forward, a duellist's stance from a lifetime ago—

—and a second warden came out of the dark behind her, and its hand went through the space between her shoulder blades like a hand through smoke, and Vess made a sound Casper had never heard a human being make, and Casper ran.

That was the thing he would carry afterward, the thing that sat on his chest at night like a stone: he ran. His legs decided before his heart finished arguing. North fork, split rock, her voice still tearing the air behind him — *don't wait* — and he ran, because she had spent three days teaching him to survive and he was going to insult her by dying anyway.

The gorge blurred past. Scree slid under his boots. Above him the false stars wheeled in patterns that hurt to follow, and behind him the sounds of the fight came back wrong — delayed, doubled, arriving from ahead of him as well as behind, so that he seemed to run through the battle's ghost even as it receded. Once he heard Vess laugh, wild and triumphant, and once he heard nothing at all, and the nothing was worse.

The split rock rose out of the dark like a doorpost — a granite wedge taller than a house, cloven by some ancient violence, the gap beyond barely shoulder-wide. He squeezed through, tore his coat, kept going, down and down through switchbacks the smuggler maps had never recorded, until the wrongness thinned and the stars overhead resolved, slowly, grudgingly, into constellations he knew, and he realized he was crying, and that his feet had carried him two miles past any sensible stopping point.

* * *

He went back.

Of course he went back. He told himself it was tactics — you don't abandon an ally's body for the Ascendants to read — but the truth was simpler and he let it stay simple. He climbed through the dark with the prophecy book a hard rectangle against his ribs and guilt a harder one, following his own footprints in the frost, and found the north fork silent and empty and scrubbed.

No blood. No bodies. No scuff-marks, no traces of the fight at all — the scree lay smooth as a swept floor. The Ascendants were thorough even with their leavings. Only one thing marked that anything had happened at all: at the split rock, wedged in the crack at shoulder height, a scrap of grey wool, torn, with a mended burn-hole Casper recognized, because he'd watched Vess patch it by firelight two nights ago while telling a story about a customs house in Vail and a tax collector who'd made the mistake of being clever.

He took the scrap. He held it in his fist hard enough to hurt.

She wasn't dead. He refused — deliberately, willfully, the way you refuse to look at the ridge — to believe she was dead. Wardens didn't leave bodies, but they also didn't leave *scraps*, and scraps meant haste, and haste meant she'd been taken alive, or taken *somewhere*, or had cut her own coat free and gone to ground the way a woman with forty years of Low Road in her bones absolutely would. Taken alive. That was the story he chose. Wardens took people alive when the people were useful, and Vess was useful, Vess knew every road and every name, and the thought of what they might want her to remember, and how they might make her remember it, sent him stumbling down the last miles to the treeline with his jaw clenched so tight his teeth ached.

Dawn found him in the shelter of the pines below the cairns, shaking with cold he hadn't bothered to feel until now. He built no fire. He sat with the scrap of wool in one hand and the prophecy book in the other, and after a long time, with fingers that wouldn't quite obey, he opened the book to the page that bore his name.

The entry had changed.

He was almost sure it had changed. The lines describing the ambush on the pass were still there — *the Bearer flees north through the wounded sky; the keeper of roads stands against the grey and falls* — but beneath them, in ink that looked newer, darker, as if it had been written more recently than everything around it, a fresh line had appeared:

*The keeper of roads is not ended. What is taken can be taken back.*

Below that, the page was blank. Waiting.

Casper stared at it until his eyes burned. The book had scheduled Tam's death. It had scheduled the ambush, the wardens, Vess falling — he'd read enough of it by now to understand that much, that this wasn't prophecy but itinerary, a route already mapped by some hand he couldn't see. So why write a line of hope? Unless hope was also scheduled. Unless even his grief was a waypoint on someone else's map.

"Who's writing this?" he whispered to the pines, to the pass, to the wrong stars fading into morning. "Who's holding the pen?"

The wind came down out of the gorge and moved through the branches, and for half a heartbeat — he would never be sure, afterward, whether it was imagination or the pass leaking its strangeness downhill — the sound it made was almost like a name. Not *Casper*. Something older. Something that fit him the way the prophecy's ink fit the page.

He folded the scrap of grey wool into his breast pocket, over his heart, where the book rode. Then he stood, checked his bearings against the honest sun, and turned his face north — toward the hollow beneath Highmoor, toward Maren and the Unwritten, toward whatever the next line of the map intended for him.

Vess had bought his escape with her blood. The least he could do was make the purchase mean something.

Behind him, far up in the wounded sky, one false star pulsed once, slow and deliberate, like a lamp being answered — and went out.
