## 13. A Name in Frost

The waystation at Hollowmere had been a chapel once. Casper could tell by the ribs of the roof, the way the beams arched overhead like the bones of some enormous patient animal, and by the alcove in the eastern wall where a saint's statue had been chiseled away—not broken, but *removed*, carefully, the stone smoothed to blankness as though the face had never been there at all.

The Ascendants did that everywhere, Vess had told him on the pass. They didn't smash what they erased. They made it smooth. Made it look like nothing had ever stood there.

He'd thought about that for most of the evening while he nursed a fire down to embers and ate hard bread softened in melted snow. His shoulder still ached where he'd wrenched it diving behind the rocks when the wardens' crossbow bolts came hissing out of the false starlight. Vess's blood was on his sleeve. He hadn't washed it off. It felt like the only honest thing he owned.

Now he lay in his bedroll under the empty arches, the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth and tucked against his chest like a second heartbeat, and sleep took him faster than he wanted it to.

* * *

The tower first.

It rose out of white mist the way a tooth rises out of a gum—sudden, pale, rooted in something soft and wrong. Casper stood on a causeway of black glass that stretched toward it across a frozen lake, and the lake was not water. He knew that without knowing how he knew. The surface shimmered with faint moving lights, like fish beneath ice, like thoughts beneath a skull, and when he looked down he saw faces drifting there. Not drowned faces. *Filed* faces. Rows upon rows of them, serene and slightly blurred, arranged in a grid that receded into depths no lake should have.

He looked up quickly. Cartographer's instinct: don't stare at the thing you're crossing or it will pull you off the path.

The tower had no door. But the mist opened for him anyway, folding aside like a curtain drawn by a courteous hand, and there was an entrance where no entrance had been, and steps inside going up, and he climbed them because in dreams climbing is not a choice but a grammar.

The stairwell walls were lined with shelves.

That was the first impossible thing—or the first thing he noticed being impossible, which amounts to the same. Shelves in a stairwell, floor to ceiling, every one crammed with books whose spines bore no titles. Instead each spine held a small oval portrait, painted in miniature, and every portrait was a face, and every face was asleep, and some of them were breathing. He could see the spines rise and fall. Faintly. Like sleeping birds.

"Don't touch them," said a voice above him. "They bruise."

Casper's hand froze an inch from a spine. He looked up the spiral of shelves and stairs, and far above, at a landing lit by a lamp that burned without flickering, stood a figure in grey. An Ascendant's coat—he knew the cut now, had learned it the hard way on the White Road—but older in style, the collar higher, the silver clasps shaped like open hands rather than closed eyes.

"You're late," the figure said. "We've been keeping your page warm."

"I don't know what page," Casper said, and heard his own voice come out younger than it should have. Higher. A boy's voice.

"You will." The figure descended three steps, and the lamp-light slid across its face, and the face was smooth. Not featureless—the features were all there, eyes, mouth, the whole architecture of a person—but *uncommitted*, the way a sketch is uncommitted before the ink goes down. "Everyone says that, at first. Everyone says *I don't know*. And then they remember, and then they wish they didn't, and then—" the figure smiled, gently, terribly, "—then we file them somewhere comfortable."

"Where is this?" Casper asked. His voice cracked on *this*.

"The Archive," the figure said, as if naming a village. "Or what the Archive dreams when it's hungry. You've been here before. That's rather the problem."

And the stairs went on up, and Casper climbed, and the breathing books watched him pass with their closed painted eyes, and somewhere below him, deep in the foundations, something began to burn.

* * *

Smoke has a taste in dreams. That was the second thing he understood: real smoke stings the eyes first, but dream-smoke arrives on the tongue, bitter and sweet at once, like burnt sugar, like a name dissolving.

The library was on fire and no one was screaming.

That was the horror of it. Casper stood in a hall vast enough to swallow the whole of Highmoor garden, shelves rising in cliff-faces on either side, and fire moved through them like weather—rolling, deliberate, unhurried—and the people among the shelves were not fleeing. They were *choosing*. He saw a woman tear a book in half and press one half to her chest and swallow something—a word, a syllable, light—that leapt from the pages into her mouth. He saw her hair catch, and she did not flinch; she walked into the flames reading aloud, and the flames bent around her voice like grass around wind.

"They're taking it into themselves," someone said beside him. "You can't burn what's remembered in flesh."

Casper turned. The speaker was an old man with ink-stained fingers and a scholar's stoop, and his face—

His face was familiar in the way a map is familiar of a country you've never visited. Every line correct. Nothing you've seen.

"You're the author," Casper said. He didn't know how he knew. In dreams knowledge simply *settles*, like snowfall.

"I'm a librarian," the old man said, with the mild pedantry of the dead. "Authors are just librarians who got lost. Now listen, because we haven't long, and you never listen the first time—you make me say everything twice, across forty years, and I am tired, boy, I am so tired of saying it twice."

The fire roared closer. Heat pressed against Casper's cheek like a palm.

"When they come—and they will come, they always come—they will offer you a trade. Your name back. All of it, every stolen year, your mother's voice, your father's hands, the room you slept in before the rewriting. They'll put it in a mirror and let you look until you weep. And all they'll ask in return is that you let them finish the sentence." The old man gripped Casper's wrist, and his grip was cold as the frost on the waystation windows. "A sentence isn't finished when the words run out. It's finished when it can't be argued with. Don't let them finish the sentence."

"What sentence?" Casper demanded. "What sentence? Tell me—"

But the old man was already turning away, already walking back into the burning stacks, and over his shoulder he said the last thing clearly, the only thing that would survive the night intact:

"*The last line is yours to write. That's the whole secret. That's why they fear a cartographer.*"

Then the shelf beside him burst, and the fire took the old man's shape and folded it away, and Casper was running—down through smoke, past falling shelves, past a case of glass jars in which tiny landscapes floated like specimens, a village, a wedding, a winter market, each one dimming as he passed—and the heat at his back became a hand between his shoulders, shoving, and the floor tilted, and—

* * *

Snow. A courtyard. Night.

He was small again. He knew it by the height of the wall, by the way the fountain's rim met his chin, by the enormous dark sky pressing down. Snow fell upward here, drifting from the ground to the clouds, and each flake was a letter. He could read them as they passed his face. Most were in no alphabet he knew. One spelled *Hartwell*. Another spelled a name that made his chest ache before his mind caught up, a name like a hand closing around something soft.

There were voices beyond the courtyard door. Two men arguing in low tones, the argument of professionals who agree about the outcome and are haggling over method.

"—the girl's already placed. This one's harder. Too many people saw the birth."

"Then fewer people will remember seeing it. That's what we do."

"It's not clean. Rewrites this young take root crooked. He'll feel it all his life. A shape where something used to be."

"Good. A shape keeps a man looking. Better than a hole, which lets him stop."

A pause. Then, quieter, almost kind:

"He won't suffer. We're very good at mercy. It's our best product."

The door opened. Lamplight spilled across the snow, and Casper—small, four years old perhaps, wearing a nightshirt embroidered with initials he couldn't read—looked up at two grey coats, and one of them crouched, and its face was the smooth unfinished face from the stairwell, and it smiled the gentle terrible smile and held out its open hands.

"There's a family," the Ascendant said softly. "In the mountains. Thornfell. They lost a son to fever last spring. Grief makes such wonderful empty rooms, little one. We're going to put you in one."

"I don't want to go," said the child's voice, and it was Casper's voice, and it was not Casper's voice, and both of those were true at once and the truth of it split him down the middle like a seam giving way.

"Nobody does," the Ascendant agreed. "That's why we hold you while it happens. You won't remember my hands. You'll remember warmth. Everyone remembers warmth. Memory keeps what it likes and calls the rest a life."

Hands closed around him. Warm hands. He was lifted, and the courtyard fell away, and the snow-letters swirled faster, and he read them desperately as he rose—*remember, remember, remember*—and then a flake landed on his lips and dissolved, and it tasted of the dream-smoke, of burnt sugar, of a name dissolving, and the name was—

* * *

He woke with frost on his eyelashes.

Not metaphor. Actual frost, feathered across his lashes and brows, though the fire was down to a red eye and the chapel's broken windows had let in nothing worse than the ordinary cold of the Thornfell high country. He sat up too fast and his wrenched shoulder screamed and he bit down on the sound, because even now—even alone, even hollowed out—some stubborn shard of the old rule held: *never be memorable.*

Except the rule was ash. Tam was ash. The workshop was ash. He breathed until his hands stopped shaking, and then he unwrapped the prophecy book and held it in the firelight, and the frost on his lashes began to melt and run down his cheeks, and he let it, because there was no one to see and it wasn't crying anyway. It was condensation. It was physics.

The book lay open—it had not been open when he'd wrapped it—to a page he had never studied closely. The entry was dated thirty-nine years before his birth, and it described a fire in a library, and it described a child carried out through a courtyard of falling snow, and the handwriting changed halfway down the page, shifting from the careful script that filled the rest of the volume to something ragged and urgent, as if written by someone running, or burning:

*The Bearer will dream the seams. When he wakes with frost on his lashes, he will know the tower, though he has never seen it. He will know the library, though it died before him. He will know the hands that carried him, though they wore mercy like a coat. Let him wake angry. Anger is a map. Anger finds the road when hope cannot.*

Below that, in the careful hand again, smaller, almost an afterthought:

*If you are reading this and your lashes were wet when you woke: I wrote this for you. I could not sign it. Signing it would have ended us both. Look for me in the garden. I left myself where the flowers keep what the world was made to forget.*

Casper read it three times. Then a fourth, slower, tracing the words with a fingertip gone numb from cold.

Outside, the wind moved through Hollowmere's dead bell-cote, and the broken bell gave a single cracked note, soft as a tapped fingernail on a window. Somewhere south of him, he knew without knowing how, an inquisitor in a grey coat was opening ledgers. Somewhere north, the garden waited under the frost, blooming things it should not be able to bloom, growing paths toward buried things.

And in his mind, laid over the memory of the dream like one map laid over another, the lines matched. The tower's position relative to the lake. The courtyard's orientation to the northern stars. The angle of the lamplight from the door.

He was a cartographer. He had spent twenty years learning to reconstruct terrain from fragments—a smuggler's boast, a burned bridge, a shepherd's secondhand directions—and every instinct he owned was now telling him, calmly and precisely, that he could draft the floor plan of a place he had never been.

He picked up his charcoal stub and, on the back of a spare survey sheet, began to draw the tower.

Halfway through, his hand drew a corridor he had not planned, leading to a room he had not seen, and he stopped, charcoal hovering, heart slamming, because he recognized the room. He had slept in it. Before. Under different initials, in a nightshirt stitched by a mother whose face the frost on the window was slowly, patiently, beginning to return to him—

The charcoal snapped in his fingers.

Casper sat very still in the ruined chapel, listening to the wind, holding half a tower in his hand, and understood that the dream had not been a dream at all.

It had been a delivery. And someone, forty years dead, had finally gotten it through.
