## 16. The Page Vess Wanted

The page fought him for three days.

Casper worked by candlelight in the back room of Vess's waystation at Hollowmere, a low-beamed space that smelled of tallow and old smoke, with the prophecy book open before him on a table scarred by a hundred years of knives and coin-weights. The page Vess had demanded—the one naming her erased daughter—was bound into the book's final quire, sewn in with thread so fine it might have been spider silk. He had not cut it out. Not yet. Vess wanted the page itself, whole and unharmed, and Casper was a careful man; he did nothing to a document until he understood what it was.

He understood now why she'd been so insistent.

The script was Ascendant cipher—of course it was—but it was an early form, the kind the archivists had used before they'd stopped bothering to hide what they wrote from anyone who might read it. Casper had spent two years of his life copying smuggler ledgers written in half-ciphers, and before that, longer still learning to read the marginalia of forbidden maps: route-notes, dead-drops, warnings scratched in codes that changed every season because someone was always watching. Cipher was just a map of language. You found the landmarks first.

The landmarks here were names.

Most of the prophecy book was exactly what Maren had described—a rebel counterweapon, a branching record of futures the Ascendants had tried to prune away. But this page was different. It was older than the rest, or made to look older, and its cipher was built on substitution rather than memory-work. Someone had wanted it readable by a person who knew how to look and unreadable to a person who merely glanced. That distinction mattered. That distinction was a message in itself.

*You are meant to find this,* Casper thought, for the hundredth time, and for the hundredth time he pushed the thought away, because the alternative—that no one was meant to find it, that he was simply the first fool stubborn enough to try—was somehow worse.

He copied the substitutions onto scrap paper, cross-referenced them against the glyph-tables Maren had sketched for him in the hollow beneath the garden, and by the third night the text began to come apart like wet paper in his hands.

*When the Bearer walks the white road under false stars—*

His candle guttered. He trimmed the wick without looking up.

*—the one who guides him shall turn his shadow toward the Spire.*

Casper sat very still.

He read it again. Then again, as if repetition might change the words, the way a map redrawn often enough will eventually show you the road you wish existed instead of the one that does. It didn't change. The letters sat where they were, patient and black, and meant what they said.

*The one who guides him.*

There was only one person guiding him anywhere. There was only one person who *could* guide him through the high passes, through the routes the Ascendants couldn't watch because even they didn't know all of them. Madame Vess. Vess, who had fed him stew and called him *little cartographer* in a voice like gravel wrapped in velvet. Vess, who had looked at the book with something raw moving behind her eyes and said, *There's a page in there with my daughter's name. Bring it to me and I'll carry you over the mountains myself.*

Vess, who had a daughter?

Casper realized, sitting in the tallow-smell dark, that he had never once asked the daughter's name.

* * *

He should have gone to her that night. He knew that later, turning it over and over like a stone with a wrongness hidden on its underside. A man who finds rot in a ship's hull doesn't wait for calm seas. But it was past midnight, the waystation quiet except for the wind working at the shutters, and some animal part of him—the part that had kept him alive for thirty years by never being memorable, by never being noticed, by never, ever being the one who told an uncomfortable truth at an inconvenient hour—counseled patience. Sleep on it. Copy the page first. Be sure.

So he copied it. Every word, every glyph, tracing the cipher substitutions in a second column so that any reader could see both the lock and the key. If he was going to accuse Vess of treachery, he would do it with evidence, not suspicion. That was the cartographer's discipline: you didn't declare a river impassable because it looked wide. You sounded it. You measured. You were right before you spoke.

And then, because he was thorough, because thoroughness had kept him alive when cleverer men died, he read the rest of the page.

There was more beneath the betrayal. A second passage, smaller, cramped against the bottom margin as though the writer had been running out of room—or out of courage.

*And the Bearer, knowing, may yet choose mercy, for the betrayer betrays out of love, and love is the oldest chain. Mark this: the shadow she turns is not his death but his capture, and the Spire has need of him living. What she buys with his blood will keep forty souls through the winter. The book does not judge. The book only remembers what will be.*

Casper set down the pen.

His hand was steady. He noted that with distant surprise, the way you note the weather. Forty souls. Medicine for forty souls, or food, or passage—something that could keep forty people alive through a winter that was already falling wrong across the whole realm. He thought of the Unwritten in their hollow beneath the garden, sustained by remembering alone, fading like flowers pressed between pages. Forty of them. Maybe fewer, after the cold came.

*She betrays out of love.*

It would have been easier if the page had named her a monster.

* * *

Morning came grey and thin. Casper tucked the original page inside his coat, against his ribs, where the book usually rode, and went down into the common room with the copy folded in his pocket and no plan at all beyond *look at her face*.

Vess was already there, which was unusual; she kept night hours the way smugglers keep everything, backwards and by preference. She sat at the long table near the hearth with a cup of something steaming and a stack of tallies before her, doing accounts in a small fierce hand. Her grey-streaked hair was braided back tight. She looked up when he came in, and for one unguarded instant—before the smile assembled itself—her face was tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

"Little cartographer." She pushed a chair out with her boot. "You've got the look of a man who's been arguing with a book. Sit. Eat. Books win those arguments nine times in ten."

"I finished it," Casper said. "The page."

The room was empty except for them and a boy asleep in the corner who was supposed to be minding the door. Vess set down her pen. Very slowly, the way a hunter sets down a rifle within reach rather than away from it.

"And?"

"It doesn't name your daughter."

Something crossed her face—quick, controlled, gone—and Casper watched it go and hated himself a little for cataloguing it the way he catalogued everything. Relief? No. Not relief. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman hearing a thing she had already heard somewhere, in some other life, and hoped never to hear again.

"No?" Vess said. Her voice was level. Too level, the way ice is too smooth. "Then what does it name?"

"You want me to say it."

"I want you to stop standing in my doorway like a debt collector and say whatever you climbed down here to say." She gestured with her chin at the chair again. "Sit down, Casper Hartwell. I don't interrogate standing men. It feels too much like work."

He sat. He didn't take the tea she poured him. She noticed that too; she noticed everything, that was the whole architecture of the woman, and it occurred to him that a person who noticed everything would also know precisely how to arrange a room, a rumor, a route, so that a man walked exactly where she wanted him to walk.

"The page says," Casper said quietly, "that the one who guides me through the pass will turn my shadow toward the Spire. That I'll be taken alive. And that what my capture buys will keep forty souls alive through the winter."

Silence. The fire popped. In the corner, the sleeping boy snored and turned over.

Vess looked at him for a long moment. Then she laughed—one short, hard bark with no humor in it at all, the sound of a door slamming.

"Well," she said. "There it is."

"There it is," Casper agreed. His heart was going hard against the page hidden under his coat. "Is it true?"

"Is it true." She repeated it slowly, tasting each word, and then she stood up and walked to the window, arms folded, looking out at the frozen yard where two of her people were loading sledges with covered crates. Frost ferned the glass around her silhouette. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped its performance entirely, and what was underneath was worse than anger. It was exhaustion.

"Do you know what the tithe-takers took from Low Road families last year, little cartographer? Not coin. Coin we hide. They took *names*. Memory-tithe, they call it. A woman in Marrowgate woke up and didn't know her own children, and the Ascendants wrote it down in a ledger as *administrative adjustment*. Forty souls in my network are holding on right now by threads thinner than that page you're hiding under your coat—I can see the shape of it, boy, I've been reading men's coats since before you could write—and every one of those threads snaps the moment the Long Winter closes the passes and my caravans stop moving." She turned. Her eyes were dry and terrible. "So yes. If there were a price that could keep them—if there were a trade—I'd consider it. Any woman worth the name would. That's not treason. That's arithmetic."

"But you haven't decided."

"I haven't decided," Vess said, "because I hadn't finished deciding whether to believe a book written by dead rebels about a future nobody's lived yet." She came back to the table and leaned on it, close enough that he could smell woodsmoke and some bitter medicinal herb. "But you have. Look at you. You've already tried me, convicted me, and buried me, all before breakfast, and you're sitting there wondering whether to run."

"Are you going to stop me?"

"From what?"

"From leaving. From taking another road. From—" He stopped, because saying *from warning the others* would make it real.

Vess straightened. For a moment she looked almost amused, in the way of a storm that finds your preparations inadequate. "Boy. I have moved stolen goods, stolen people, and stolen memories through these mountains for thirty years. You think I can't move one frightened mapmaker by a different road if I choose to?" She tapped the table once, sharply. "Here's what happens now. We leave tomorrow for the Pass of False Stars, same as planned, because it's the only road the wardens don't watch and the only road that gets you to Highmoor before the snows seal it. And somewhere on that road, you and I are going to settle what this page means—whether it's a sentence or a warning. Because there's a difference, and the difference matters."

"What difference?"

"A sentence tells you what must happen." Her voice went low. "A warning tells you what to avoid. Your dead rebels wrote that page for a reason, and I don't think it was to hang me. I think it was to put us both in a room together, forced to look at the worst thing either of us might do, *before* either of us does it." She picked up her tea and drained it. "That's what books are for, little cartographer. Maps too. They show you the flood plain so you don't build your house in it."

"And if I'm wrong about you?"

"Then you'll owe me an apology and a bottle of the good brandy." She smiled, and this time it almost reached her eyes, and it was almost worse than the other look. "And if you're right—" She shrugged, a small economical motion. "Then you'll owe me nothing, and you'll have forty lives to weigh against one, and you'll finally understand why I drink."

She left him sitting there with the untouched tea going cold, and Casper stared at the frost on the window and thought about the second passage, the one crammed into the margin, the one he hadn't shown her.

*The book does not judge. The book only remembers what will be.*

But the garden had shown him otherwise, hadn't it? Branching lines. Choices. Futures that bent when a man bent them. Maren had said the prophecy was a weapon, and weapons pointed somewhere—but a map wasn't a weapon, a map was an offer, and Casper had spent his whole life believing that the territory always got a vote.

Except.

Except that the page had known things it shouldn't have known. It had known he would copy it in secret. It had known he would hide it under his coat. It had known Vess would see the shape of it through his jacket, because of course she had, because he'd wanted her to, some traitorous honest part of him had wanted her to know he knew—

Or the book had arranged it.

He thought of the entry that had started everything, his own name and tonight's date in a century-old hand, and felt the vertigo come back, the sick lurch of a man standing on a bridge that turns out to be drawn on paper.

* * *

They left at dusk the next day.

Vess gave him furs, a sledge-driver named Oskar who never spoke, and a route north through country Casper had mapped twice and still barely recognized under the snow. The world had gone white and silent in a way that felt less like weather than like erasure—the Long Winter settling in for good, eating the roads one pass at a time. Behind them, Hollowmere's lights shrank to embers. Ahead, the mountains stood up black against a sky with too few stars in it.

At the mouth of the pass, where the trail narrowed between walls of blue ice, Vess reined up beside him. Her breath smoked. Somewhere above, wind moved through stone with a sound like pages turning.

"False Stars starts here," she said. "From this point on, trust the road and nothing else. The stars lie. The compasses lie. Even your maps will lie to you a little—it's why I brought a mapmaker." She glanced sideways at him, and for a heartbeat her expression was nakedly, unbearably human: a woman carrying a decision she hadn't made yet, in a book she couldn't stop reading. "Whatever you think you know about me, little cartographer—keep it in your pocket until we're through. Roads have a way of shaking loose what people carry."

"That's what worries me," Casper said.

Ahead, the pass swallowed the last of the light. And far behind them—so far it might have been imagination, except that Casper had learned, these past weeks, never to trust that word—something glinted on the southern ridge. Grey figures. Moving fast.

Not following the road.

Following *them*.
