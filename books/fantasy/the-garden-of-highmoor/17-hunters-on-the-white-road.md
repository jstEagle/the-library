## 17. Hunters on the White Road

The road out of Vess's territory was called the White Road because no one who named it had ever come back to say otherwise.

Casper walked it with his collar up and his breath freezing in his beard, the prophecy book strapped against his chest beneath two layers of wool, and he told himself—again, in the flat voice of a man reciting a rule he'd invented for exactly this kind of night—that a road was only a line on a map until someone made it into a story. He was good at roads. He had spent thirty years making himself into terrain: unremarkable, unmemorable, the kind of face that slid off memory like water off slate.

But the book against his ribs made him a story now. The book made him a *line*, and lines could be followed.

He heard the horse before he saw it—or rather, he heard nothing, which in the deep snow of the pass was its own kind of sound. The wind had died. The flakes were falling straight down, fat and slow, filling his own footprints behind him as fast as he made them, and into that perfect hush came the soft crunch of a hoof finding ice.

She rode out of the snowfall the way a sentence completes itself: grey coat, grey hood, a sword at her hip she made no move toward. Her horse was white enough to be a rumor. For a moment Casper thought the storm had simply grown a shape, and then the shape spoke.

"Casper Hartwell."

His hand went to the knife at his belt and stopped there, useless, honest. Running was pointless; the road behind him was already erased by snow, and the road ahead belonged to her. So he did the thing his whole life had trained him never to do. He stood still, and let himself be seen.

"You have me at a disadvantage," he said. "You know my name."

"I know what it says in your ledgers." She dismounted, unhurried, and Casper marked the way she moved—not a soldier's economy but an archivist's, precise, cataloguing. "Born in Lowmark. Apprenticed to a mapmaker. No living kin. A life eleven pages long, and not one word of it true."

"Then you know more about me than I do."

Something flickered across her face—too quick to name, gone before it landed. She stopped six paces away, close enough that he could see the frost caked in her eyelashes, the exhaustion under her eyes that no amount of Ascendant discipline could quite erase. Up close she looked less like an inquisitor than like a woman who had been awake too long with a question she couldn't put down.

"My orders," she said, "are to retrieve you or erase you. Those are the only two outcomes the Spire recognizes."

"And yet here we are, admiring the weather."

"Here we are." She looked past him, down the long white throat of the road, and when she spoke again her voice had changed—lower, stripped of its official cadence, almost private. "I'm going to ask you one question, Hartwell. One. And then I'm going to let you walk, or I'm not, depending on how you answer."

Casper's heart was a fist against the book. "Why would you give me anything?"

"Because I've been hunting you for three hundred miles," she said, "and every mile, the hunt has felt less like duty and more like being read aloud." She pulled off one glove, finger by finger, as if the small ritual steadied her. "So. My question."

The snow fell between them. Somewhere below the pass, a ridge cracked under the weight of winter, a long groan like the mountain clearing its throat.

"A year ago," Sera Ilvane said, "I lost a year."

* * *

Three days earlier, in the archive annex at Candlemere, Sera had sat alone with a lamp and a ledger bearing her own name, and had understood, with the calm horror of a woman watching water rise past her knees, that the handwriting was hers and the memories were not.

That discovery lived in her now like a splinter worked too deep to draw. It was why she had falsified nothing yet, reported everything late, ridden ahead of her own wardens through the White Road pass alone. It was why she had taken off her glove in front of a fugitive, which protocol called surrender and which she was beginning to suspect was something closer to honesty.

"One year," she said now, to Hartwell, to the falling snow. "Between my nineteenth birthday and my twentieth, there is a gap. Not blank—the Spire's editors don't leave blanks, blanks invite questions. They left a *seam*. A year of garrison duty at a fort I can describe in perfect detail and cannot, no matter how I try, remember entering or leaving. I know the names of everyone in my company. I cannot tell you the color of the sky over it. When I dream, I dream around it, the way a tongue finds the socket of a missing tooth."

Hartwell said nothing. His eyes—grey, watchful, a cartographer's eyes, always measuring the distance to the nearest exit—had not left her face.

"The Spire teaches us that gaps in memory are symptoms," she went on. "Corruption. The mark of the Unwritten's touch, of forbidden texts, of sedition. Inquisitors who find seams in themselves report them immediately, for excision." Her mouth curved without warmth. "I reported mine. Do you know what the Archivist Prime said to me?"

"No."

"He thanked me. He put his hand on my shoulder, the way you'd steady a piece of furniture, and he said, *'A clean mind is a loyal mind, Ilvane.'* And then he ordered the seam smoothed, and I signed the consent form myself, because I had been raised since childhood to believe that my own memory was a document owned by the realm, and documents get corrected." She flexed her bare hand in the cold, watching the blood return to the fingertips. "I didn't sign it. I forged the signature and I kept the seam. Three hundred miles I've carried it, and every mile it gets louder."

The wind stirred, then died again. Hartwell's breath hung between them in small ghosts.

"So here is my question," Sera said. "And understand that I have crossed a frozen pass alone, in violation of half a dozen protocols, to ask it, so consider what your answer costs me. You've seen the Unwritten. You've read the rebels' book. You know things about editing that the Spire denies and the garden remembers." She stepped closer, and this time it wasn't menace in it—it was need, raw and badly hidden, the need of a starving person smelling bread. "When they cut a year out of a person—when they take it and smooth the edges and leave a seam—does anything remain? Underneath. Does the year survive somewhere, or is it ash? Is it *gone*?"

* * *

Casper had imagined this meeting a hundred times since Tam died. In most versions he ran. In some he fought. In none of them—and this was the thing that undid him, standing in the snow with an inquisitor's question hanging in the air—in none of them had she asked him something he *wanted* to answer.

Because he knew about seams. God help him, he knew about seams from the inside.

"There's a valley in Thornfell," he said slowly, "that I mapped when I was twenty-three. The Smuggler's Cut, we called it. I drew it twice because the first survey disagreed with itself—the stream flowed one way on Monday and another on Thursday, and I assumed I'd made an error, because I always assume I've made an error. It's safer that way." He huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "It took me years to understand the surveys weren't wrong. The valley had been edited. Someone had rewritten the water, and the land remembered both versions, the way a bone remembers a break."

Sera had gone very still.

"When the Ascendants cut a year out of you," Casper said, "they don't burn it. Burning leaves evidence—ash, absence, a hole other memories collapse into. They're too careful for that. What they do—" he searched for the words, and found them in the garden, in Maren's voice, in the hollow where the forgotten people waited— "is they fold it. They fold the year flat, the way you'd fold a letter, and they tuck it into the seam, and they write over it. But paper holds the crease forever, Ilvane. Folded hard enough, long enough, the old writing starts to show through. Your dreams go around the gap because part of you is *walking around the fold*. The year isn't ash. It's in there. Pressed flat, but whole."

The silence after that was long enough that the snow filled their footprints and made strangers of them again.

"That's not possible," Sera said, but her voice said *please*.

"Maren told me the Unwritten call it the pressed flower," Casper went on. "A memory folded flat keeps its shape. That's why the garden can restore people. That's why the Long Winter terrifies the Spire—because every edit they've ever made is a crease, and creases hold, and someday—" He stopped. Careful. Even now, even here, the cartographer's rule whispered: *never be memorable.* But the rule was dead; Tam had burned with it. "Someday someone unfolds them all at once."

"And that's what you're carrying." Her eyes dropped to his chest, to the shape beneath the wool. "The book. The means to unfold."

"I'm carrying a lot of things," Casper said. "Guilt. A dead friend's name. A page I promised to a smuggler queen who'll gut me if I lose it. And yes—a book that schedules the future instead of predicting it, which I would happily give to someone else if anyone else could carry it." He met her gaze. "But you asked me one question, and I've answered it honestly, and now I'd like to know what happens next. Because you're an inquisitor standing alone in a pass with a fugitive, and either that's a trap or it's a defection, and I've survived this long by knowing the difference."

Sera looked at him for a long moment. Then she laughed—one short, broken note, startled out of her, frost-smoke in the air.

"A trap," she said, "would be smarter. A trap I'd know how to report." She pulled her glove back on, finger by finger, closing the ritual. "Do you know what I realized, three hundred miles ago, reading your ledger? Eleven pages. Neat, complete, boring. A life with no creases anywhere." Her jaw tightened. "No one has a life with no creases, Hartwell. Not even the Archivist Prime. Your file wasn't written. It was *typeset*. Someone built you a past the way the Spire builds all its lies—smooth, seamless, and perfectly, deliberately forgettable. I've spent my career learning to spot forged documents." She turned toward her horse, and paused. "You're the best forgery I've ever seen. Which makes you either the Spire's greatest asset or its greatest crime, and I no longer trust myself to judge which."

"And so you're letting me walk."

"I'm letting us both walk," Sera said quietly. "For now. Because I think we're in the same cage, you and I—I just got a look at the bars before you did." She mounted, gathered the reins, and looked down at him one last time through the falling snow. "They'll send wardens behind me when I fail to report. Grey cloaks, four days, maybe five. Don't be on this road when they come. And Hartwell—" the hesitation was small, human, quickly mastered— "if you ever learn how to unfold a year. There's a fort in the eastern passes I'd very much like to visit."

She turned the horse. The snowfall took her the way it takes everything on the White Road—gradually, then completely—and Casper stood alone in the hush with his heart hammering against the book, understanding that something had just happened that he had no map for.

An enemy had asked him a true question. He had given a true answer. And neither of them had died of it.

He walked on. Behind him, unseen, Sera Ilvane did the same, and both of them carried the same new weight: the knowledge that the cage had a door, and that the door had been unlocked all along, and that walking through it was going to cost more than either of them yet knew.

Four days later, in the high country above the pass, Casper woke in a waystation to find frost flowers blooming on the inside of the shutters—white petals opening in patterns he recognized, patterns the garden had shown him, spelling out a single urgent line in a language older than the Ascendants:

*The Spire knows the road she rides. Move.*
