## 20. Sera Breaks Protocol

The order came at dusk, folded inside a grey envelope sealed with the Ascendant sigil—a closed eye above an open book—and Sera Ilvane stood in the doorway of the relay station at Coldharrow holding it for a long moment before she broke the wax.

She had been expecting it. That was the worst part. For three days she had known, in the way one knows a storm is coming from the smell of the air, that the Spire would ask her the question she could not answer honestly. The dispatch riders had passed her twice on the high road without stopping, and each time she had felt the weight of their indifference like a held breath.

*Inquisitor Ilvane. Report your current position relative to the Bearer of the Unwritten. Confirm pursuit status. Respond by rider within one day.*

The words were precise, bureaucratic, almost gentle. That was how the Ascendants wrote everything—soft hands around hard intentions. She had read a thousand such orders in her eleven years of service. She had written some herself.

Sera looked up from the letter. The relay station was a low stone building hunched against the mountain's shoulder, its windows fogged with woodsmoke, its stable smelling of hay and horse sweat. Two junior wardens waited inside by the fire, playing cards, their grey coats steaming faintly where they'd come in from the snow. One of them, Pell, glanced up when she entered.

"Orders, ma'am?"

"Routing instructions." She tucked the letter into her coat. "We move at first light."

Pell nodded and went back to his cards. He was twenty-three years old and believed entirely in the order he served, and Sera found that she could not look at him for longer than a few seconds without feeling something crack quietly behind her ribs.

She walked out to the stable instead, on the pretext of checking her horse, and stood in the straw-dark among the animals breathing their white breath, and let herself admit what she had been refusing to admit since Candlemere.

She was going to lie to the Spire.

* * *

It had started with the ledger. Of course it had. Everything in her life now traced back to that ledger—the confiscated memory-record from the village of Ashen Mill, the neat columns of extracted recollection, and there, on the third page, in an archivist's careful hand: *Ilvane, S. Age nine. Witness to boundary incident. Memory adjusted per protocol 7.*

Her own name. Her own childhood, itemized like inventory. *Adjusted per protocol.* Four words that meant someone had reached into the small room of her nine-year-old mind and rearranged the furniture, and she had walked out of that room believing she still knew where everything stood.

She had gone to Candlemere to verify it—had pulled her own file, had found the surgical excisions, had found the gap where a year of her life should have been. A year. Three hundred and sixty-odd days of rain and bread and voices and fear, simply gone, cut away with such precision that even the scar tissue had been smoothed over. She remembered being eight. She remembered being ten. Between them lay a smooth white nothing that her mind papered over with borrowed scenery, the way a liar papers over a story he hasn't fully rehearsed.

And then there was Hartwell.

Casper Hartwell, the cartographer who should have been nothing—a smuggler's mapmaker, a grey little man in a grey little trade—who had looked at her across a frozen road and answered her question about her missing year with a truth so plain it had felt like a blade laid flat against her palm. *You were in the tower country,* he'd said. *You were visiting someone. A woman who kept bees. You cried when you left and you didn't know why.*

He couldn't have known that. No record contained it. No ledger. It lived only in the excised year itself—which meant either Hartwell had access to things no fugitive should possess, or the garden had shown him, or—

Or he was exactly what the prophecy said he was. The Bearer of the Unwritten. Keeper of the secret the Ascendants had spent a century burying.

She had reported the encounter, as protocol demanded. She had reported it accurately, too—that was the thing she kept returning to, the thing that sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. In that moment, standing on the White Road with her hand on her sword, she had still been loyal. She had given the Spire everything: the location, the direction of travel, the description of Vess's wounded smuggler guide. And the Spire had used her accuracy to send wardens to the pass at Thornfell's knee, and the wardens had found not Hartwell but a waystation full of ordinary travelers, and they had done what wardens did when a Bearer slipped through their fingers.

Sera had read the after-action report by candlelight in this very relay station. Eleven dead. Two of them children. The report called them *collateral memory-losses*, a phrase that managed to make murder sound like weather.

That had been four days ago. Since then she had not slept more than two hours at a stretch, and every time she closed her eyes she saw the waystation, reconstructed in her imagination from the report's dry language—a door kicked open, a table set for supper, a pot of something still warm.

Tonight the Spire had asked her where Hartwell was. And Sera Ilvane, eleven years a faithful instrument of the Ascendant order, stood in a stable full of horses and realized that she knew exactly where he was going—and that she would rather cut out her own tongue than write it down.

Because she did know. That was the other thing. Two nights ago, a garden flower had appeared on her windowsill. A frost-bloom, impossible in this cold, petals like veined glass, and when she touched it she had seen—not dreamed, *seen*—a green door in a ruined wall, and beyond it a garden growing riotous under snow, and Hartwell standing among the flowers looking lost, and an old woman with river-grey hair saying words she couldn't quite hear but whose shape she understood anyway: *come home, come home, come home.*

Highmoor. He was going back to Highmoor. The garden was calling him home, and Sera was the only inquisitor in the Thornfell reach who knew it.

She took out her field ledger then, sitting on an overturned feed bucket in the straw-smelling dark, and opened it to the current entry page. Her handwriting stared back at her from three days prior—neat, disciplined, the handwriting of a woman who had never once doubted what she served:

*Day 41. Subject sighted White Road, traveling southeast with armed escort (Vess, M., wounded). Pursuit viable. Requested reinforcement denied; proceeding alone per doctrine of quiet approach.*

Below it, blank lines waiting for tonight's truth.

Sera uncapped her ink. Her hand did not shake. She noted that with a kind of distant clinical interest—*look at that, I'm calm, I'm actually calm*—and began to write.

*Day 44. Trail lost at the Thornfell knee. Weather deteriorating; passes closing early this season. Subject likely sheltering south of the divide but all sign ends at the rockfall. Recommend suspending active pursuit until thaw. Will hold at Coldharrow and re-establish contact with informants.*

Every word a lie. Every word chosen with the same care she gave to real reports, because a false report that read like a false report was a death sentence, and a false report that read like truth was—what? Freedom? Treason?

She wrote the lies in her best hand and signed her name beneath them, and her signature came out steady, and that steadiness frightened her more than any trembling could have.

* * *

But the ledger was only half of it. A forged entry bought her days, maybe a week—but the surveillance records were the true danger. Every inquisitor's kit carried memory-wafers: thin discs of treated glass that recorded what the wearer saw, keyed to the Spire's archive, reviewed at random intervals by archivists who looked for exactly the kind of hesitation Sera had been carrying in her chest for four days. If an archivist replayed the last week of her life, he would see her face when she read the after-action report. He would see her standing too long over the frost-bloom on her windowsill. He would see her lips move on the White Road, repeating a stranger's words about bees and tears.

They would see her doubt. And doubt, in the Ascendant order, was the one crime that could not be adjusted away—because doubt meant the adjustment had failed, and failed adjustments meant the adjuster had erred, and archivists did not forgive evidence of their own errors. They erased the evidence. Usually along with the person carrying it.

So Sera sat in the stable until the house above went quiet, until Pell's laughter faded and the coals in the relay station's chimney burned down to a red memory of themselves, and then she built a small fire in the corner of the stable yard, shielded by the woodpile, and fed it her wafers one at a time.

Treated glass did not burn easily. It softened first, going milky, then translucent, and in those last seconds before it slumped into slag the recorded images would surface on its face like drowned things rising—ghost-pictures, half-formed, flickering. Sera watched them go. A market day in some southern town. A corridor in the Spire, all white stone and silence. Her own face, younger, taking an oath with her hand on a memory-ledger. The frozen road. Hartwell's tired eyes.

"You were in the tower country," the slag seemed to whisper as it died. "You cried when you left."

The last wafer was the hardest. It was the one from Candlemere—the archive visit, the excised pages, the moment she had stood in the reading room holding her own amputated year and understood that the people she served had done to her precisely what they had done to the villagers of Ashen Mill, to the Unwritten, to everyone. Evidence. Proof. The kind of proof that got inquisitors disappeared.

She held it over the fire for a long time.

If she destroyed it, she destroyed her only corroboration. If anyone ever asked her to prove what had been done to her, she would have nothing but her own unreliable, edited mind. She would be a woman claiming a wound no one could see, in an order that specialized in invisible wounds.

If she kept it, she was dead. Ondrell's archivists ran random audits, and hers was overdue, and a wafer showing an inquisitor browsing restricted files about her own past would end her career and probably her life before she ever found Hartwell again.

*Before she ever found Hartwell again.*

There it was. The thought she had been circling for days, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. She wasn't planning to close the case. She was planning to find him first.

Sera dropped the wafer into the fire.

It flared a strange greenish color as it died, and for one instant the flames threw a picture into the night air above them—a tower, impossibly tall, wrapped in cloud, and a small figure at a high window—and then it was gone, and the fire settled into ordinary orange, and Sera Ilvane knelt in the snow beside the ashes of her own recorded life and felt something in her chest unclench that had been clenched for eleven years.

Fear was still there. Fear would always be there; she was too much an inquisitor ever to stop calculating the cost of what she'd done. By morning the Spire would have her false report, and for a week or two it would hold—weather was plausible, the season was turning, even Ondrell's machine needed rest. But eventually the audits would catch up, or another inquisitor would pick up the trail she'd deliberately muddied, or Hartwell himself would do something loud enough to bring the whole grey apparatus of the Ascendancy down on Highmoor like an avalanche.

She should feel ruined. Instead she felt—light. Terrifyingly light. As if the wafers hadn't been the only things burning; as if some other weight had gone into the fire with them, some grey coat she'd been wearing so long she'd mistaken it for skin.

A sound made her turn.

Pell stood at the stable door in his nightshirt, cardigan thrown over it, watching her. The firelight caught his young face. On the ground between them: ash, slag, the unmistakable residue of burned memory-glass.

Sera rose slowly. Her hand did not go to her sword, though every trained instinct screamed at her to control the scene, to invent a story—*contaminated wafers, standard disposal*—to do what inquisitors did, which was manage perception until reality bent around it.

Instead she heard herself say, quietly: "How long have you been standing there?"

"Long enough." Pell's voice cracked on the second word. He looked terrified, and underneath the terror, barely visible, like a vein of ore in grey stone, was something else. "Ma'am—the waystation. The report said eleven dead. My sister lives near the Thornfell knee."

The wind moved through the stable behind him, and the horses shifted, and somewhere far off on the mountain a snow-slope settled with a long sighing sound, like the world exhaling.

"I lied in my report," Sera said. It came out simply, without ceremony. Eleven years of discipline, and when the moment finally came, the truth cost her no effort at all. "Hartwell is alive. I know where he's going. And I'm not going to tell them."

Pell stared at her. Snow began to fall between them, fat and slow, catching in the young warden's hair, melting on the warm ashes of the fire.

"They'll kill you," he whispered.

"Yes."

"And if I report you—"

"You'll be commended. Promoted, probably." She held his gaze. "I won't beg, Pell. And I won't run. Whatever you decide in the next minute, I'll accept it."

The boy—for he was a boy, whatever grey coat they'd hung on him—looked down at the ashes. His jaw worked. When he spoke again, his voice was so low the snow nearly swallowed it.

"The woman who kept bees," he said. "In the tower country. Was that in the records too? Because my mother used to sing about bee-wives. Before the tithe collectors came. She doesn't remember the song anymore. She thinks she never knew it."

Sera's breath stopped. Somewhere in the realm below, the Long Winter was tightening its grip, and old songs were dying in old mouths, and this child of the Ascendancy was standing in falling snow asking her whether the thing his mother had lost was real.

"It was real," Sera said. "It's all real. They took it, and I helped them take it, and I'm done helping."

Pell was silent for a long moment. Then he crouched, scooped a double handful of snow, and dropped it over the ashes, grinding it down with his boot until the slag and the ghost-green light were buried white.

"First light, you said we move," he murmured, not looking up. "Coldharrow's garrison rotates east tomorrow. Nobody checks the western passes till the thaw." He straightened, and his young face had aged ten years in as many minutes. "I never saw a fire, ma'am. I never saw anything at all."

He turned and went inside, and the stable door closed behind him, and Sera stood alone in the falling snow with her forged report in her pocket and her past in ashes and no title left in the world except the one she chose for herself in that moment, silently, the way a pen chooses a line:

Deserter. Ally. Hunted.

She looked west, toward Highmoor, where a ruined garden grew flowers through the frost and an unwritten man was learning what he was. The snow thickened. Behind her, the relay station's windows glowed dull orange, and somewhere inside, a boy was lying awake deciding what kind of man he would be.

Sera pulled her collar up and began to walk.
