## 21. Vess Collects

The waystation at Greyhollow was a smuggler's courtesy: a stone bothy sunk into the mountainside, half-buried in scree, with a chimney that smoked sideways so the wind would shred the evidence of habitation. Vess had left them there three days while her runners went ahead to scout the White Road, and Casper had spent those days learning the shape of the woman who had pulled him out of an ambush at the Pass of False Stars.

She slept little and never lying down—dozing upright against the wall like a soldier, one hand always near the knife at her hip. She ate last and least. She spoke to her people in a low, clipped cant that Casper's cartographer's ear slowly began to parse: *grey coin* meant Ascendant silver, *wet work* meant anything done above the snowline, and *the debt*—the debt meant something older than all of it, something she said only once, when she thought he was asleep.

He hadn't been asleep. He'd been mapping her, the way he mapped everything, because mapping was the only prayer he knew.

"The debt," he said now, on the third morning, watching her count a pouch of coins by the light of a shuttered lamp. "You mentioned it. In your sleep."

Vess didn't look up. Her hands kept moving, each coin turned and tested with a thumbnail, bitten, weighed. "I don't sleep-talk."

"You do. You talk about a girl. A name that starts with *S*." He watched her thumb stop on a coin. "And you keep a page folded inside your coat, next to your heart, and you take it out at night when you think no one can see, and you read it, and then you put it away like it bit you."

The silence stretched long enough that the wind outside seemed loud. Then Vess set the pouch down, deliberately, coin by coin, and looked at him with eyes the grey of glacier ice.

"You're a dangerous man to travel with, Casper Hartwell."

"I've been told I'm not memorable at all."

"That's the dangerous part." She almost smiled. It didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "A man nobody remembers can stand in a room full of enemies and walk out again. But a man who *notices*—" She shook her head. "Noticing gets people killed. Usually other people."

"Whose name is it? The girl's?"

Vess stood, joints cracking, and went to the narrow window slit. Beyond it the world was white and getting whiter; the Long Winter had settled into the high passes like a tenant who intended to stay. Frost flowers—the garden's signature, the impossible bloom that followed Casper now like a scent—had begun creeping up the outside of the stone, delicate white petals threading through cracks, and Vess looked at them the way a woman looks at weather she cannot argue with.

"Sable," she said finally. "Her name was Sable. Is Sable. Depends which side of the ledger you read." She tapped her chest, where the folded page lived. "The book says things about me I don't believe. That's all you need to know."

"And if the book is right?"

"Then the book and I are going to have words." She turned from the window. "Runners are back within the hour. We move at dusk. There's a route through the Hollow Teeth that the wardens haven't cracked in twenty years, and we'll be through it before Ondrell's hounds know which way the wind points."

Casper wanted to ask more. He was learning, though, that Madame Vess of the Low Road gave information the way the mountain gave water: in exactly the amount the terrain allowed, and never a drop more.

* * *

They moved at dusk, eleven of them strung along a goat track so narrow that Casper stopped thinking of it as a path at all and started thinking of it as a line he was drawing with his own body—a contour map of terror, elevation gain measured in heartbeats. Below them the valley filled with blue shadow. Above, the first stars came out, and Casper checked them out of habit, the way a man checks a wound.

The stars were right here. Small mercies.

Vess led from the front, sure-footed despite her years, and behind her came Brannic, her lieutenant, a slab of a man with frost-scars on his knuckles, and behind him the others—smugglers, exiles, two of the Unwritten who had joined at Greyhollow with the hollow-eyed patience of people who had already lost everything once. Casper walked near the middle, the prophecy book wrapped in oilcloth against his back, and tried not to think about how much he trusted these people, because trust was a debt too, and debts got collected.

It was near midnight when they reached the Teeth proper—a labyrinth of stone spires where the wind played harmonics through natural flutes in the rock, moaning notes that made speech impossible. They camped in a hollow between two pillars, fires small and shielded, and Casper sat with Maren's teachings turning over in his head like stones in a river. *Whoever holds memory holds power.* He was beginning to understand that he himself was a kind of ledger, and that everyone around him was reading different entries.

He woke before dawn to the sound of Vess's voice, low and urgent, coming from beyond the windbreak.

"—the whole shipment, or nothing. Fever's taken four children this week. Four. And the elder's coughing blood."

Casper lay still, breath shallow, listening.

A second voice answered, and every hair on his arms rose. It was a voice like dry paper, like a ledger being opened, and even muffled by wind and stone Casper knew what it was: an Ascendant. Not a warden's bark—an archivist's murmur, patient, reasonable, terrible.

"The Spire honors its agreements, Madame Vess. The medicine will be delivered to your people within the week. All we require in return is the route. Where the Bearer crosses the White Road, and when."

"He's just a man."

"He is the most dangerous object in the realm wearing the shape of a man. But yes. Just a man." A pause, paper-dry. "You've read the page we discussed."

Silence. Long enough for the wind to sing three full notes through the Teeth.

"I've read it," Vess said at last, and her voice was flat as slate. "And I'm telling you it lies."

"Of course it does. Prophecy always lies—that's what makes it useful. But consider, Madame: if it does *not* lie. If the Bearer's road ends in fire either way. Would you not rather choose which fire?" Another pause. "Your daughter's name could be written back into the world. First among the restored. The Spire keeps its word on restorations—we have done it before, for those who served."

Casper's blood went cold and colder. He should move. He should wake Brannic, raise the alarm, run—but his body wouldn't obey, because some traitor part of him needed to hear the end of it, needed to know how deep the knife went.

"When," Vess said, and it wasn't a question anymore. It was surrender dressed as one.

"Dusk tomorrow. The narrows below the Old Sentinel. Wardens will take him clean, no blood, no fuss. Your caravan will be miles clear. No one need ever know."

"No one ever knows," Vess repeated softly. "That's the Ascendant creed, isn't it."

"It's the Ascendant *gift*," the paper voice said, and Casper heard footsteps crunching away through frost, unhurried, a man whose business was concluded.

He lay in the dark with his heart hammering and did the only thing his life had trained him to do: he mapped. The narrows below the Old Sentinel—he knew it, had drawn it twice, a defile where the track pinched between a cliff face and a frozen waterfall, the one place in thirty miles where a man couldn't go off-path. An ambush ground. Perfect ambush ground. Which meant—

Which meant he had until dusk tomorrow. One day. And a choice: confront Vess and shatter the only force standing between him and the open White Road, or say nothing, alter the plan quietly, and let her believe her betrayal had worked.

He stared up at the black underside of the stone and felt something crack in his chest that wasn't quite anger. Because he'd heard the fever, the four children, the blood. He'd heard what she was buying. And underneath the fury, unwelcome as frost flowers under stone, he understood her completely—and understanding her was worse than hatred would have been.

* * *

He said nothing at breakfast. Neither did she.

They ate hard bread and frozen cheese, and Vess met his eyes across the fire with a steadiness that would have fooled anyone who hadn't spent the night listening to her sell him. Casper held the look, nodded, passed her the bread, and hated himself a little for how well he'd learned to lie—it was Tam's workshop burning behind his eyes whenever he did it now, and he did it anyway.

"We'll reach the narrows by midday tomorrow," Vess announced to the group, all business. "Water the animals at the falls, then single file through the pinch. Brannic takes point after the Sentinel. Questions?"

Casper raised his hand like a schoolboy, and something flickered in Vess's face—fear, quickly buried.

"Alternative," he said mildly. "There's a shelf route above the falls. Steep, but it bypasses the pinch entirely. I mapped it years ago—never published it. If the weather holds, it saves us half a day and keeps us off the low ground."

"Off the low ground," Vess repeated carefully.

"Frost's thick down there. Ice under the snow. Shelf's safer." He shrugged, the picture of a cautious cartographer. "Unless you know a reason to prefer the narrows."

The silence lasted three seconds. Around the fire, the smugglers glanced between their queen and the quiet mapmaker, sensing some current they couldn't see. Vess's jaw worked once.

"Shelf route," she said. "If you're certain of it."

"Certain enough to bet my life on it. Which is, I believe, the standard rate."

Someone laughed. The tension broke—or seemed to. And Casper caught Vess's eye one last time and saw, beneath the granite, something that looked horribly like relief, and horribly like grief, and horribly like gratitude, all fighting for the same inch of face.

* * *

The shelf route was real. He'd drawn it seven years ago, ink fading now, and it held—narrow, vertiginous, the drop beside them falling away into white mist where the waterfall thundered under its ice shell. They crossed the Old Sentinel's shoulder at noon, the great leaning pillar of stone frowning over the valley, and Casper looked down into the narrows below and saw what he'd known he would see.

Wardens. Six of them, grey-cloaked, motionless against the rock, positioned with professional patience around the pinch point. Waiting for a caravan that had, without explanation, grown wings.

Beside him, Vess had gone very still. She looked down at the ambush she had arranged, at the men she had sold him to, and Casper watched her realize that he had known—had known and said nothing and simply walked them all around her sin, leaving it waiting in the cold below like a trap set for ghosts.

"You knew," she breathed.

"I listen in my sleep," Casper said. "Apparently."

Below, one warden raised his head, puzzled, scanning the empty narrows. Then another. Then, with a discipline that was almost eerie, all six turned as one and looked *up*—and Casper understood that the game was over, that grey coats did not stay bought and betrayed, that the paper voice in the dark had surely planned for this too.

"Scatter formation!" Vess roared, and the smugglers moved like water, but the wardens were faster than they should have been, flowing up the scree with impossible grace, and the air filled with the high singing sound Casper had learned to dread: memory-hooks loosed from crossbow-like rigs, glinting lines of pale light arcing through the cold.

One took Brannic in the shoulder. The big man didn't fall—he *stopped*, mid-stride, eyes going wide and blank as a page, and then he sat down in the snow and began to weep without knowing why, reaching for memories that were being reeled away on silver lines.

"Go!" Vess shoved Casper toward the far ridge. "The book matters more than any of us! GO!"

He ran. Behind him the battle dissolved into shouts and the smell of ozone and old paper, and the last thing he saw, turning at the ridgeline, was Vess standing her ground between two wardens and her fleeing people, knife drawn, grey hair whipping in the wind—not fighting to win, he realized. Fighting to *buy time*. Paying, with her body and her freedom, on a debt she'd tried to settle with his life instead.

And pinned under her boot heel, torn loose in the struggle, a fold of paper skittered across the snow toward the cliff edge—the page, the page she'd carried next to her heart, flipping open in the wind like a small white mouth.

Casper had forty feet, maybe less, and wardens between. He went anyway, because he had read enough of the book to know that pages mattered, and because some stubborn cartographer's instinct told him that this page—this one—was a map of everything.

He slid, grabbed the paper as it lifted, rolled, came up running. The wardens' hooks sang past his ears. Ahead, the ridge dropped into fog, and somewhere in the fog the frost flowers were blooming wild along the rocks, marking a path only he could see, the garden calling its own home.

Behind him, Vess screamed—not in pain but in fury, a name torn out of her, and whether it was *Sable* or *run* he never learned, because the fog took him, and the last sound of the Low Road's queen was the sound of chains.

In his fist, crumpled and damp, the page pressed against his palm. He didn't dare read it here. But as he ran blind through the flowering fog, one line showed itself, ink bleeding in the wet:

*...and the betrayer shall give the Bearer to the grey, for love of that which was taken, and shall not believe her own name until—*

The rest was torn away.

Ahead, through the fog, lights were appearing. Lanterns. Many lanterns, arranged in a patient ring, closing like a hand.

Casper ran toward the only door left open—the garden's—and prayed the greenhouse still remembered him.
