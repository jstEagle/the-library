## 22. Captured

They did not bind his hands. That was the first thing Casper noticed, and the noticing frightened him more than irons would have.

The wardens had taken his field kit at the pass—his inks, his brushes, the rolled maps he'd spent eleven years perfecting—and they'd taken the prophecy book last, lifting it from inside his coat with a reverence that made his stomach turn. But his hands they left free, wrapped only in rough wool against the cold of the warden's sledge. Free hands meant they weren't afraid of what he could do with them. Free hands meant they already knew there was nothing he could do.

He sat between two wardens in grey coats as the sledge climbed out of the White Road and onto a causeway he had never mapped, because no smuggler had ever found it and lived to sell it. The road was not stone. It was something smoother, paler, veined faintly with light like frost seen from beneath. It hummed under the runners, a low note that Casper felt in his teeth more than heard.

"Where are you taking me?" he asked, for the third time.

The warden on his left—a woman with a face like a closed ledger—did not answer for the first two asks. On the third she turned her head slightly, enough that he could see the pale seam of an Ascendant sigil inked behind her ear.

"You're being taken to be read," she said.

"I don't understand."

"No," she agreed. "You won't. Not until it's happening."

* * *

The Spire rose out of the mountains like a needle driven through the world's skin.

Casper had charted every peak in Thornfell, every ridge and scree-slope and hanging valley, and there was no place in any of his maps where this tower stood. It was impossibly tall, its summit lost in cloud, its walls a white so uniform it hurt to look at—as if someone had taken the sky after a heavy snowfall and taught it to stand upright. No windows. No door that he could see. The sledge glided up to a seam in the wall that opened before them without a sound, exhaling air that smelled of nothing at all.

That was the thing about the Spire, he would think later. Everything about it smelled of nothing. No smoke, no stone-dust, no tallow, no people-smell. A building scrubbed of itself.

Inside, corridors of the same bloodless white unspooled in silence. Their footsteps made no echo. Casper tried, automatically, to map the route—left at the first junction, down a stair of nineteen steps, left again—but the corridors refused to hold their shapes in his mind. He would look back the way they'd come and find the passage subtly wrong: the stair now fifteen steps, the junction angled differently, as though the building were quietly rearranging itself behind them.

"Stop counting," said the ledger-faced warden, without turning around.

He hadn't realized he'd been doing it aloud.

* * *

The cell was white.

Not painted white, not plastered white—white the way the inside of an egg is white, seamless and total, curving gently where walls met floor met ceiling so that there were no corners to orient by. The light came from everywhere and nowhere. When Casper pressed his palm against the wall it was warm, faintly, like skin.

There was no bed. There was no bench. There was no door—or rather, there had been one, and then there hadn't; it had simply ceased to be a feature of the wall, leaving no seam, no hinge-shadow, nothing.

He sat in the center of the room because sitting anywhere else felt like standing too close to an edge.

Time passed. Or it didn't. Without windows, without hunger, without any change in the sourceless light, he could not say whether he had been there an hour or a day. He tried to keep count by heartbeat and abandoned it when his heart began racing for reasons that had nothing to do with timekeeping. He spoke his own name aloud—"Casper Hartwell, cartographer, of Thornfell"—and heard the words fall dead into the white, making no echo even here, even against his own ears.

When the voice came, it came from everywhere, the way the light did.

"Subject is settled," it said. It was a pleasant voice. That was somehow the worst part. "We'll begin with the outer layers. Routine."

"Begin what?" Casper stood, turning in place, looking for a speaker, a grille, anything. "Who's there? What are you—"

"Name," said the pleasant voice, ignoring him entirely.

"Casper Hartwell."

A pause. Then, in a different tone—the tone of a clerk noting a discrepancy—"That's what you believe your name is. We'll come back to that. Occupation?"

"Cartographer. I make maps. Smuggler roads, mountain passes, the Thornfell range. Eleven years."

"Mm. And before that?"

"Before that I—" He stopped. The answer should have been simple. Before that he had been an apprentice; before that a boy; before that—

The thought slid away from him like water off oiled cloth. He reached for it again and again it slipped, leaving behind only a smooth blank surface and a faint, sickening sense that something had just been taken out of his hand.

"Before that," the voice prompted gently.

"I don't—" His own voice sounded strange to him, thin in the white room. "I don't remember. Why don't I remember? I've always known—I've always known I didn't know, but I never wondered why—"

"That's the edit working as intended," the voice said, with something almost like warmth. "You're not broken, Casper Hartwell. You're very well made. Now. Hold still. This next part is easier if you don't fight it."

"What part? What are you going to do to me?"

"We're going to read you."

* * *

It started with smells.

The voice spoke them aloud, unhurried, and each one arrived in Casper's head a half-second before it was named, so that he experienced the horror of hearing his own interior recited back to him like an inventory.

"Pine resin. Tallow and cheap paper. The oil you use on your good brush—linseed, cut with something you bought from a Vess woman's people and never told anyone about." A pause, delicately amused. "Wet wool. Your mother's kitchen."

"My mother's kitchen smelled of wet wool?" Casper said, and hated how his voice cracked. "I don't—there is no kitchen. I told you, I don't remember before—"

"You don't remember it," the voice agreed. "But it's in you. Memory isn't a book, Casper Hartwell. It's sediment. They can strip the top layers, the ones with names attached, but the deep strata stay. The smell of a room. The weight of a hand on your hair. Grief with no object. You're full of grief with no object, did you know that? Layers and layers of it. Someone wept over you when you were very small, and whoever edited you wasn't thorough enough—or wasn't cruel enough—to take that too."

Casper discovered he was crying. He had no memory of starting.

"Stop," he said. "Stop it. Stop reading me."

"This is what we do here," the voice said, not unkindly. "This is all we do here. Everything the realm is, everything it remembers, passes through this Spire to be weighed, corrected, archived. You've been carrying contraband, Casper Hartwell. Contraband memory. We're only doing our work."

And it went deeper.

* * *

They took Tam next.

He knew it was coming—he felt the voice gather itself, felt the direction of its attention shift like weather—and he braced every wall he had, and it did not matter at all. The voice went through his defenses the way water goes through sand.

"The apprentice. Tam. Red-haired. Whistled while he worked, badly, always the same three bars of the same song. You let him ink the coastlines because he had a steadier hand than yours and you never told him that, because telling him would have meant admitting you needed him." The voice slowed, savored. "The workshop burning. You watched from the treeline. You counted the seconds between the flash and the sound of the roof falling. Fourteen. You will count fourteen for the rest of your life, at odd moments, in quiet rooms. Fourteen."

"Stop." He was on his knees now. He didn't remember getting there. The warm white floor was against his palms and he was pressing them flat as if he could push himself through into somewhere else. "He was sixteen. He was sixteen years old, you burned him alive for drawing lines on paper—"

"For drawing lines that shouldn't exist," the voice corrected. "For a world with fewer secrets in it than the Ascendants permit. Yes. And now—" a rustle, the sound of pages, though there were no pages, "—we arrive at the interesting stratum. The book. The garden. The name that isn't yours."

"There is no other name."

"Oh, but there is." The voice drew close, closer than sound should be able to come, intimate as a whisper directly against the bone of his skull. "Do you want to hear it? We have it, you know. Or the shape of it. Deep down, under everything they buried, there's a place where your name used to live, and the edit didn't fill the hole—they sealed it. Sealed things can be unsealed. It's why you were brought here alive, Casper Hartwell. Do you understand? Anyone else who carried what you carry would have been erased on the roadside. You are cargo. You are the most valuable cargo to enter this Spire in a hundred years, and every layer we peel brings us closer to the seal."

Casper knelt in the white and made himself breathe.

Tam was dead. The garden was far. Vess was a traitor or a martyr depending on which page you believed, and Sera Ilvane was somewhere above him in this white throat of a tower, wearing her grey coat, and no one was coming. He held those facts the way a drowning man holds stones.

"If you open it," he said, "what happens to me?"

Another pause. Longer. When the voice resumed, the warmth was gone from it, replaced by something procedural, which was worse.

"The seal and the self are the same structure. Opening one opens both. In practical terms: the man called Casper Hartwell ends. What remains afterward—" the voice hesitated, and for the first time it sounded genuinely uncertain, genuinely human, and that terrified Casper more than everything before it combined, "—the records are unclear. There has never been a successful extraction of a sealed name. There have been attempts. The subjects stopped being people partway through. We filed them under structural losses."

"Filed them," Casper repeated.

"They were given numbers," the voice said, as if that were a kindness.

* * *

They read him for a long time after that. Or a short time. Time had stopped meaning anything hours or years ago.

They read him the taste of river water in high summer. They read him the exact weight of the prophecy book against his ribs, and the precise quality of his dread each dawn when he checked that it was still there. They read him Madame Vess's laugh, and Maren's hands, and the smell of the greenhouse the night the flowers bloomed through frost. They read him Sera Ilvane's face on the frozen road, and the question she asked him, and the honesty of his answer, and they lingered over that one, turning it over like appraisers with an unexpected coin.

"Interesting," the voice murmured. "An inquisitor asked you a true question and you gave her a true answer. That's not in either of your files. That's new behavior. New behavior gets reported upward."

"Report it," Casper rasped. His throat was raw. He had been talking, arguing, pleading, for a span of time he could not measure, and the voice had never once tired, never once paused except to savor. "Report everything. Report that I cried. Report that I miss my friend. Report whatever makes you feel like a person instead of a drain."

Silence. Then, softly: "Anger. Good. Anger sits near the seal. Anger is load-bearing."

And it dove again, and this time it went past anger, past grief, past the smell of pine resin and the weight of a hand in his hair, down into strata so old they barely had images attached—just pressure, just a great dark weight of *before*, and at the bottom of it, faint as a star through stormcloud, something that was almost a word. Almost his.

The voice reached for it.

And the whole white room shuddered.

It was small, at first—a tremor in the floor, a flicker in the sourceless light, gone so fast he might have dreamed it. But the voice had stopped. For the first time since the cell, the voice had stopped mid-word, and in the silence Casper heard something he had not heard anywhere in the Spire until this moment:

Water. Dripping. Somewhere below.

Then, faint and impossible and unmistakable, threading up through the warm white floor like scent through a keyhole:

The smell of green things growing.

The voice returned, and it was no longer pleasant. "Seal integrity fluctuation," it said, rapid now, clipped, speaking to someone else, to many someones. "Confirm. Confirm. Something is—how is something *growing* on sublevel nine—"

Under Casper's knees, through the seamless white, a single thread of root pushed up through solid nothing, tender and green and patient, and split itself open into a flower the color of frost at sunrise.

The cell grew a door.
