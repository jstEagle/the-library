## 24. Interrogation of the Unwritten

The cell had no corners.

Casper had noticed it on the first hour—or what he assumed was the first hour; time moved strangely in a room without windows—and once he'd noticed it, he couldn't stop noticing it. The walls curved where they met the floor and the ceiling, seamless as the inside of an eggshell, white without being bright, lit from nowhere. No door seam. No drain. No mark of any tool that had built it.

He had spent thirty years reading rooms. A smuggler's cellar told you by its damp which roads flooded in spring. A client's parlor told you by its dust which lies they told their neighbors. Rooms confessed everything if you knew how to listen.

This room said nothing. It had been made to say nothing. Which meant someone had been afraid of exactly the kind of man Casper Hartwell was—the kind who listened to rooms—and had spent a great deal of money ensuring there was nothing to hear.

He sat cross-legged on the floor with his field kit open beside him, because they had let him keep it, and that was the most frightening thing of all. They wanted him calm. They wanted him *settled*. You did not settle livestock before slaughter; you settled it before milking.

His ink was nearly gone. He'd been drawing maps on the inside of his own forearm with a stub of charcoal, working from memory: the pass above Thornfell, the switchbacks of the Low Road, the drowned stair at Candlemere. Not because he needed them. Because his hands needed to be doing what his hands knew how to do, or else the fear would climb up through his wrists and into his chest and stay there.

*You are a cartographer,* he told himself. *You have mapped places no one was supposed to find. Map this.*

The light changed.

It didn't brighten or dim so much as *thicken*, the way air thickens before a summer storm, and then the wall opposite him wasn't a wall anymore. It was a window—no, a mirror—and in the mirror stood a woman in grey with a face like a closed ledger.

He knew her. Sera Ilvane. The inquisitor who had cornered him on the frozen road and asked him about her missing year instead of taking him. He had answered honestly, and she had let him walk, and he had thought about that for eleven days afterward, turning it over like a stone looking for the worm beneath.

But this wasn't her. The woman in the mirror wore Ilvane's coat and carried herself with Ilvane's economy of motion, but the face behind it was older, colder, arranged around an expression of patient appetite. Her eyes were the pale grey of paper left too long in the sun.

"Casper Hartwell," she said. Her voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way voices do in dreams. "Cartographer. Son of Marta and Aldous Hartwell, deceased. Resident of Lowmark village, eleven years. Apprentice to the surveyor Denner, three years before that. Do you confirm these particulars?"

"I confirm I've heard them before," Casper said. "Usually from people selling something."

"We are going to read your memories now," she said, as though he hadn't spoken. "The process is painless if you do not resist. Resisting is painful. This is not a threat. It is simply how memory works."

"And if I refuse?"

"Memory cannot be refused," she said, and there was something almost gentle in it, the gentleness of a woman describing weather to a farmer whose crops were already drowning. "Only endured."

* * *

They began with his mother.

Not a question—a *presence*. The white room filled with the smell of bread and woodsmoke, and there was Marta Hartwell's kitchen rendered in impossible detail, every scratch on the table, every chip in the blue bowl, and Casper felt the extraction like a hook set gently behind his eyes, reeling the scene out of him thread by thread.

Here was the trap, and he understood it only because he had spent his whole life being unremarkable on purpose: they would take everything. Every true thing. And when they were done, they would know him completely, and a man who is known completely can be rewritten completely.

Unless what they took was false.

The hook pulled. The kitchen shimmered, waiting to be read.

And Casper Hartwell did the only thing he had ever been good at, the thing he had practiced in secret for thirty years until it was less a skill than a spine:

He lied.

He reached into the memory of his mother's kitchen and *redrew it*. The table had been oak? Now it was pine, scarred with a burn from a dropped lamp—he invented the lamp, the night of the burn, his mother's shout, the smell of scorched pine that had never happened. The blue bowl became green. The window faced east; he turned it west, and gave the western view a crooked fence and a dead pear tree, and a neighbor named Odell who climbed it as a boy and fell and broke his arm, and Odell's mother's anger, and the taste of the pears—sour, always sour, why had they never ripened—

The hook stuttered.

It was subtle, but Casper felt it, the way a fisherman feels the line go slack: the extraction losing its grip on a memory that refused to hold still, that kept *changing under examination*. Memory was supposed to be sediment. His was behaving like water.

The woman in the mirror frowned. "Your recollection is inconsistent."

"My recollection," Casper said, "is thirty years old. Yours is a rumor you got secondhand." He spread his hands, the picture of a tired, frightened man—which cost him nothing, because he was tired and frightened, he simply declined to be only those things. "I was six when my parents died. You're asking me to audit a child's kitchen. Of course it's inconsistent."

Something flickered across her face—not belief, but the consideration of belief, which was enough. Inquisitors, he guessed, were trained to catch resistance. He doubted anyone had trained them for cooperation this detailed. For a man who wept over the wrong color of a bowl.

"Continue," she said.

* * *

So he built her a life.

He did it the way he drew maps: starting from terrain he knew and inventing only what he had to. The extraction wanted Lowmark village, and Casper had passed through Lowmark twice, so he gave them Lowmark—the well with the leaning post, the miller's crooked chimney—but he salted it. He populated it with people who didn't exist and events that hadn't happened: the winter the river froze solid and they held a market on the ice; the fiddler with the missing finger who played at every wedding; his own childhood fever, the week he'd nearly died, the doctor with onion breath who'd bled him twice and been paid in cheese.

Each invention had to be load-bearing. That was the craft of it. A single lie floats away; a lattice of lies holds. So he wove them tight: the fiddler's missing finger tied to the ice market, where he'd lost it to a fisherman's knife in a dispute over a wager; the wager tied to the miller's son; the miller's son tied to the fever, because it was the miller's family who'd carried Casper through the snow to the doctor. Every false thread knotted to two others. Let them pull. The net would hold, and the more they pulled, the more entangled they'd become in a village that had never existed, full of people who had never lived, grieving losses that had never been suffered.

And underneath the net, quiet as a smuggler's road beneath a shepherd's track, ran the truth: the garden, the book, Maren, the Unwritten, his own stolen name. He buried it deep and covered it with the ordinary.

Because that was the other lesson of a lifetime of being forgettable: no one digs below the boring.

Hours passed. Maybe days. The mirror-woman came and went, and each time she came, Casper fed her another layer: his apprenticeship with Denner, rebuilt with a limp Denner had never had and a dog named Bristle that had never lived; his first commission, mapping the Vell orchards, which he relocated forty miles south and gave a haunted cider press; Tam.

That one hurt. He could have skipped Tam, left the boy out entirely, but absence was a shape too—inquisitors noticed holes—and so he included Tam and falsified him gently, changed the color of his coat, moved their meeting from the workshop to a tavern, gave their friendship a quarrel it had never had, so that even his grief was counterfeit, even his mourning led them down a road with no body at the end of it.

He wept while he did it. He let himself weep, because the tears were real even if the memories weren't, and he suspected—hoped—that the machine drank tears as eagerly as anything.

"You loved him," the mirror-woman observed, during the fourth session or the fourteenth.

"He was my friend," Casper said, and that much was true, and he hated how good it felt to say one true thing, and he noted the feeling and filed it away as a vulnerability and built a wall around it.

* * *

It was during the fifth session—or the fifteenth—that he made his mistake.

They had gone back to the garden. Highmoor, the ruined greenhouse, the book. And Casper, exhausted, fraying at the seams after so long a performance, gave them the garden *too accurately*. He described the broken glass in the eastern panes as green-tinted, because it had been. He placed the book on the third shelf of the potting bench, because that was where it had been. He let slip the smell of the place—wet stone and something sweeter, something blooming out of season—and the sweetness was true, all the way down true, and the hook caught on it like a nail catching silk.

The mirror-woman went very still.

"The greenhouse," she said slowly. "Describe the glass again."

"Green," Casper said. "Greenish. Sea-glass colored. Old glass goes that way, impurities in the sand—" He heard his own voice picking up speed, the cartographer's tell, the sound of a man filling silence with detail, and he made himself stop. Made himself shrug. "I was cold and wet and hiding from a storm. I remember the shelter, not the joinery."

But she was no longer listening to him. She was consulting something outside the frame of the mirror, a ledger or a screen of light, and her stillness had changed quality—from patience to attention, the way a hawk changes from perching to stooping.

"Your file says you entered Highmoor on the ninth of Frostmonth," she said. "Alone. On foot. In a storm of unusual violence." A pause. "There is no record of what you did there. The disturbance signature begins that night. It has not stopped since."

"Disturbance signature," Casper repeated. "Is that what you call it?"

"That is what we call a place remembering against its orders." Her pale eyes came back to him, and for the first time there was something in them besides appetite. Something almost like fear, which he found, to his own surprise, encouraging. "Gardens do not bloom in winter, cartographer. Stone does not grow doors. Unless something is feeding them."

"Then you should feed your prisoners better," Casper said. "I've seen the portions."

She ignored that. She leaned closer to the mirror, close enough that he could see the fine lines around her eyes, the faint tremor in her jaw that she probably believed he couldn't see. "Every mind resists extraction, Mr. Hartwell. It is natural. It is expected. But resistance looks like static. Like screaming. Like a man clawing at his own skull." Her voice dropped. "What you are doing is not resistance. What you are doing is *cartography*. You are redrawing the territory faster than we can survey it."

The white room was silent.

"I have read a thousand memories," she said. "I have never once been *narrated to*."

Casper held her gaze and did the hardest thing he had done in the cell, harder than the lying, harder than the weeping: he said nothing at all. He let the silence sit between them, vast and white as the room itself, and he watched her decide—watched the decision move through her face like weather—whether she had caught him or merely unsettled herself.

Then the mirror darkened, and she was gone, and the room was only a room again, eggshell-smooth and silent, and Casper sat alone with his charcoal stub and his shaking hands.

* * *

He drew maps on his arm until his fingers cramped.

Not real ones anymore. False ones. He filled his forearms with the geography of a country that did not exist: the village of Lowmark with its ice-market square, the Vell orchards forty miles south of anywhere, the dead pear tree, the fiddler's grave. If they took his body, let them take a body tattooed with fiction. If they read his skin the way they read his mind, let them read a man from a place that had never been, walking roads he had invented in a white room to keep from being erased.

*They erase people,* he thought, charcoal whispering over old scars of ink. *So I will become a person made of erasures. A palimpsest. Write on me. There's nothing underneath but more writing.*

It was somewhere past exhaustion, in the grey nowhere-time when the light neither dimmed nor brightened, that he felt it.

At first he thought it was a hallucination—the body's rebellion, the mind's small mutinies. A coolness against his bare foot, where the floor met the curve of the wall. Then a prickling, delicate pressure, like a feather made of needles.

He looked down.

Between the flagstones that were not flagstones—between the seamless white floor that had confessed nothing for days—something green had split the surface. A stem, thin as wire, trembling slightly, crowned with a bud that opened as he watched: a flower, white-veined with frost, breathing out a sweetness he knew down to the marrow.

Highmoor's out-of-season bloom. The garden's signature. The scent that had betrayed him to the mirror-woman now rising up through the Spire's foundations like a promise.

More stems followed. A dozen. A hundred. Splitting the flawless floor in fine cracks that spread outward from his crossed legs like the lines of a map being drawn by a hand far older and more patient than his own, and the white room—the room built to say nothing—began, very softly, to fill with the smell of a garden that remembered.

Casper started to laugh. It came out cracked and half-sobbed and it was the truest sound he'd made in weeks.

"They told you I was here," he whispered to the flowers. "Didn't they. The garden doesn't lose its own."

The outermost crack reached the wall—and stopped. Held. As if waiting.

As if something on the other side of the eggshell had just laid its ear against it, listening—and was deciding whether to knock.
