## 26. Escape Across the Rooftops

The frost-flowers were still blooming when Sera pulled him out of the cell.

Casper had heard her coming before he saw her—boots on stone, then a sound he hadn't heard in the Spire before: running that wasn't afraid of being heard. The white door had dissolved into a lattice of ice-crystal and green, petals forcing themselves through seams that had no seams, and through it came a grey coat with a drawn blade and a face he'd last seen across a frozen road.

"You," he'd said.

"Me." She'd cut his bindings without ceremony. "Can you run?"

He'd laughed—something cracked and involuntary. "I've been reading my own memories aloud to strangers for a day and a half. I can *crawl*."

"Then crawl fast."

Now they were climbing.

The Spire's interior was chaos behind them—alarms that sounded less like bells and more like a choir singing one wrong note forever, wardens shouting in corridors where the floors had turned to winter gardens, archivists fleeing with armfuls of ledgers they clearly valued more than their lives. But above it all, above the archive levels where Ondrell's people kept the world's stolen memory in ordered rows, there were the rooftops: a spine of slate and copper running the length of the Spire's lower wings, down toward the city wall, toward the mountains, toward anywhere that wasn't here.

Sera went first. Casper learned quickly that she climbed the way he mapped—with total, unglamorous competence. No flourishes. Hand here, foot there, weight forward. She moved like someone who had done this before and been punished for doing it badly once.

"Where are we going?" he called, low, as they flattened against a chimney stack while a searchlight swept past below—some Ascendant device that threw a column of pale light like a finger pointing at sin.

"Away from the light," she said.

"That's not a plan."

"It's the only plan I have left." She glanced at him, and in the dark her eyes looked almost young. "Everything else was written for me. I'm improvising. It's terrible. Keep up."

* * *

They crossed the first gap between buildings on a plank of rotted scaffolding that Casper would not have trusted with his own weight alone. Halfway across, Sera stopped.

"The night you answered my question," she said. "On the White Road. About my missing year."

Casper gripped the plank. Below them, forty feet down, warden lanterns moved along the wall like slow fireflies. "Is this the moment you decided to interrogate me again? Mid-air?"

"I decided nothing. That's the point." Her voice was steady but her knuckles were white on the wood. "You told me about the ferryman. The woman who paid him in buttons. You told me because it was true, and you had no reason for it to be true except that you'd seen it—in the garden, or in yourself, I don't know. And everything I'd been told about you said you were a lie wearing a man's shape."

"And now?"

"Now I know the lie is me." She moved again, fast, before he could answer, dropping onto the far roof in a crouch. He followed, legs shaking, and found her waiting by a dormer window, watching him with an expression he couldn't read.

"I falsified my ledger tonight," she said. "I burned six years of surveillance records. Do you understand what that means? There is no version of Sera Ilvane, Inquisitor Third Rank, after this hour. Whatever name I carry next, I carry as a fugitive."

"So don't carry it."

She stared at him.

"You said it yourself," Casper said. "It was written for you. By someone with access. Someone who edited your year out and gave you a smooth, seamless life in its place—and you *felt* the seam, didn't you? All these years. Like a tooth that isn't yours."

Her hand went, unconsciously, to her jaw. Then she caught herself doing it and something in her face broke open—not dramatically, not weeping, just a hairline fracture running through the cold.

"They took a whole year," she whispered. "Who does that? Who takes a *year*?"

"Someone who needed you not to remember what you did in it." He said it gently, and watched the words land like stones in still water. "Or what was done to you."

The searchlight swept past again. They pressed flat against cold slate until it passed, shoulder to shoulder, and Casper became aware of her breathing—quick, controlled, the breathing of someone holding a great deal at bay.

"When we're clear," she said finally, "I'll give you something. Not yet. When we're clear."

* * *

The second wing of the Spire was worse. The garden had reached it—frost-flowers bursting through the rooftop tiles in clusters, silver-white blooms opening in the freezing dark, and where they grew the slate was slick with rime. Casper nearly went down twice. The second time, Sera caught his wrist and held him over the drop for one long, absurd moment, both of them suspended between the garden's bloom and the city's black fall.

"Don't," she said, "die on a roof. It's undignified."

"Noted," he gasped, and she hauled him up.

But the flowers had shown him something. He crouched by the nearest cluster, ignoring her hiss of *we don't have time*, and touched one bloom with a fingertip. Warmth. Actual warmth, radiating off a flower growing out of frozen stone in the middle of the Long Winter's first true snowfall.

"It's following us," he said. "Look—the blooms trail off toward the east roofs. Toward the wall. It's making us a path."

"Your garden is helping us escape?"

"It's not my garden." He stood, and felt the truth of it settle into him like a key turning. "But it knows me. Better than I know myself, apparently. Come on—where the flowers grow thickest, the tiles hold. Where they're dead, the roof's rotten. It's mapping for us."

Sera looked at the trail of silver blooms snaking away across the rooftops, and Casper watched her make a decision he recognized, because he'd made it himself in a ruined greenhouse a lifetime ago: the decision to stop disbelieving.

"All right," she said. "Lead on, Bearer."

* * *

They lost their pursuers at the bell tower.

Not truly lost—wardens didn't lose people, they simply redistributed them across the search grid—but for eleven minutes, eleven minutes Casper counted against his own heartbeat, there was no one behind them. They crouched in the tower's shadow among rooks' nests and old bell-ropes, catching breath that wouldn't come, and Sera did something he didn't expect.

She laughed.

It was a small sound, startled out of her, half hysteria. "We're on the roof of the most secure building in the realm, fleeing the order I served for nine years, guided by *flowers*, and I just—" She pressed the back of her hand to her mouth. "I haven't laughed since I was—I don't know when. I don't know when I last laughed. Isn't that a terrible thing not to know?"

"There's a lot of that going around."

"Tell me something true." She turned to face him fully, and the moonlight caught the sharp line of her cheekbone, the exhaustion underneath the discipline. "That's the currency now, isn't it? Between us. Truth for truth. You know things about my missing year. So tell me something true about Casper Hartwell. Something real. Not the cartographer. Not the Bearer. You."

Casper leaned back against the cold brick and thought about it. The strange thing was how hard it was. A day and a half of having his memories read aloud had scraped him thin; he felt like one of his own maps, worn soft at the folds.

"Tam taught me to whistle," he said at last. "Before he could talk much. His mother died when he was small and he went near-mute for a season, and the way he came back to speech was through whistling—bird calls first, then tunes, then words. So every map I ever drew has little whistle-marks in the margins. Notes. Songs he liked. Anyone who ever studied my work closely enough would think they were survey marks." His throat tightened. "They weren't survey marks."

Sera was quiet for a long moment.

"He died because of orders I carried," she said. "Three years ago. A workshop in Thornfell. I didn't strike the match—but I drew the route for the men who did. I remember drawing it. I remember thinking it was a smuggler cache." Her voice didn't waver; that was somehow worse. "And then my ledger says I spent that winter in Vellmark, and I have no memory of Vellmark at all. None. Just a smooth place where a year should be."

"Sera—"

"No. Let me finish, because if I stop I won't start again." She pulled something from inside her coat—a small tin locket, dented, the kind soldiers carried. "When we're clear of the wall, I promised you something. Here it is early. My name. My real one. I found it two weeks ago in a confiscated memory-ledger, in a record of a village that doesn't exist anymore. Before the Ascendants made me Sera Ilvane, before the academy, before any of it—"

She opened the locket. Inside, on a scrap of paper gone brown with age, in a child's careful hand:

*Seryth.*

"My mother wrote it," Sera said. "My mother, whose face I cannot remember, whose village the tithe erased, who named me Seryth before someone decided that girl was inconvenient and built an inquisitor in her place." She closed her fist around the locket hard enough that Casper heard the tin creak. "Say it. Please. I need to hear it in someone else's voice. If it only exists in mine, it might be another edit. Another thing they planted."

"Seryth," Casper said.

The name hung in the freezing air between them, small and enormous. Sera—Seryth—closed her eyes, and a single tear cut a hot line down her cold-flushed face, and she let it, and did not wipe it away, and Casper understood that this was the bravest thing he had seen anyone do in the entire long nightmare since he'd opened a book in a dead garden.

"Again," she whispered.

"Seryth."

* * *

The wall crossing nearly killed them both.

The garden's path led to the eastern bastion, where old scaffolding from some abandoned repair clung to the stonework like a skeleton. Below, the city sprawled under curfew-dark, and beyond it the passes, white and closing. Wardens patrolled the wall-top in pairs, lanterns swinging. Sera studied their rotation with an inquisitor's eye and hated herself for how easily it came.

"Fourteen seconds between patrols at this stretch," she murmured. "The scaffold takes longer than fourteen seconds to descend. So we don't descend. We go over the gatehouse and drop to the coal store roof—twelve feet, snowdrift against the north wall, survivable if we land right."

"If we land wrong?"

"We won't land wrong." She looked at him. "Because you're going to trust me, and I am going to be worth trusting. That's the whole plan. That's all any of us have now."

They went at the gap between patrols. Over the parapet, along the gatehouse ridge—slippery, wind tearing at them, the whole city laid out below like a map with no legend—and then the drop, together, into the drift. The impact knocked the world white and silent. For a moment Casper lay in the snow looking up at falling flakes and thought, absurdly, of Tam's whistle-marks, of maps with songs in the margins, of all the small true things that survive even when the large ones burn.

A hand reached down into his field of vision. Seryth's hand.

"Up," she said. "The Unwritten will want to meet the woman who burned her own leash."

* * *

They ran the low roads east, through streets where the Long Winter had already begun its quiet thefts—doors standing open, a table set for a meal no one remembered cooking, an old man standing in a square asking passersby, politely, endlessly, if they had seen his wife, describing her in loving detail and then, halfway through, faltering, because he could no longer recall whether the face in his mind was hers or his daughter's or his own.

Seryth stopped for him. Took his hands. Told him: *Go to the temple of candles on Harrow Lane. Tell them Seryth sent you. They'll help you remember.* It was a lie—there was no such refuge, not yet—but it was a kind lie, a direction to walk in, and the old man walked it, grateful.

"You can't save everyone on one street," Casper said quietly.

"No," she agreed. "But I can aim them." She looked back the way they'd come, at the Spire rising against the stars with frost-flowers now visibly blooming up its flanks, silver veins spreading across black stone. "Ondrell will have felt the garden by now. He'll know exactly what it means. And he'll know the Bearer escaped with an inquisitor who just burned six years of records." She started walking again, faster. "Which means by dawn, every warden in the realm has our faces and a new set of orders."

"What orders?"

"The ones I would write, if I were still writing them." Her jaw set. "*Take the woman alive. She knows the routes. Kill the man on sight—he's more dangerous loose than captured.*"

"You don't know that."

"I wrote those orders twice before," Seryth said, "for other names. Other Bearers." She didn't look at him. "There was a girl, four years ago. Named in a fragment of prophecy. I tracked her for a season. I was ordered to bring her in alive, and I intended to. And then the order changed—one line, new seal, Archivist Prime's own cipher—and the team that went up the mountain after her came back down without her, and without their memories of her, and I woke one morning unable to remember her face though I'd eaten breakfast across from her a hundred times." Her breath smoked in the dark. "I've been hunting ghosts so long I forgot I was one of them."

Casper caught her arm, stopped her, in the middle of the empty white road with the Spire burning cold behind them and the mountains waiting ahead.

"Then we go get her back," he said. "Her name. Out of whatever vault they put it in. Yours too. Everyone's. That's the whole point of me, Seryth—that's what the book actually says, I've read it now. The last line is unwritten. Nobody's written it yet. Not Ondrell. Not the prophecy. Us."

For a moment she searched his face the way she'd once searched it across a frozen road, looking for the lie. There wasn't one. There never had been.

"East," she said at last. "Vess's people hold the low road through the White Gap. If she'll take us."

"She'll take us," Casper said. "She owes me a debt she doesn't know how to pay yet."

Behind them, faint across the miles, the Spire's wrong-note choir began to sing again—and beneath it, impossibly, threading through the alarm like green through stone, came something else. A smell on the freezing wind.

Thyme. Rosemary. Growing things.

The garden was coming with them.

Seryth heard it too. She turned, and Casper watched the frost-flowers open along the road behind them, one by one, marking their path east like a trail of small silver lamps lit by a hand that had been waiting a hundred years for someone to follow.

"It's not guiding us anymore," she said slowly.

"No," Casper agreed, and felt the truth of it settle over him like snowfall—terrifying and warm at once. "Now it's *following*."
