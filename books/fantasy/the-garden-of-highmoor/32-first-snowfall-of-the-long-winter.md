## 32. First Snowfall of the Long Winter

The snow came to Millbrook in the hour before dawn, and it did not fall so much as arrive—each flake hanging in the air a moment too long, as though it had forgotten what falling was for.

Old Wenna Hask was awake to see it. She was always awake before dawn; sixty years of baking had put the habit in her bones deeper than any spell could reach. She stood at her window with her hands still dusted in flour and watched the white come down over the rooftops, and she thought, *first snow*, and reached for the name of the year that had taught her to love first snow.

It wasn't there.

She reached again. There should have been a winter—a specific winter—with a boy and a sled and a hill behind the mill, laughter she could almost hear, a child's red mittens. She knew such a memory existed the way you know a room exists behind a locked door. She could feel its shape. She could not open it.

"Wenna," said a voice beside her, warm with sleep, and she turned to the man in her bed, this stranger with grey in his beard and a scar on his knuckles that spoke of decades of work, and her heart did something terrible: nothing. No flood of tenderness. No history. Just a woman looking at a man and knowing, intellectually, that she must have loved him.

"What's your name?" she asked.

He smiled at first, thinking it a joke. Then he saw her face. "Aldric," he said. "Wenna. It's Aldric. We've been married thirty-one years."

"Thirty-one." She said it like a distance. Like a country she'd never visited.

Outside, the snow kept arriving, flake by patient flake, and down the lane other windows began to light up one by one, and in each of them someone was asking someone else a question no one should ever have to ask.

* * *

In the high pasture above Thornfell, Casper Hartwell woke with frost in his eyelashes and Maren's hand shaking his shoulder.

"Get up. Get up, boy—it's starting."

The hollow beneath the garden was dim, lit by the greenish glow that seeped through the roots overhead, and that glow was wrong. Casper had been three weeks among the Unwritten long enough to know its rhythms—the way it brightened at dawn as if the garden above were breathing light down into the earth. This morning it flickered. This morning it was thinner, like a candle burning down to its last finger of wax.

"The snow?" he said, sitting up. His breath hung white even here, underground, which had never happened before.

"Not just snow." Maren's face, seamed and ancient, looked older than it had yesterday, as if the years were catching up with her all at once. "Come and see."

They climbed the root-stair into the greenhouse, and Casper stopped on the threshold.

Snow lay on the glass roof of the walled garden, thick and silent, and where it touched the panes the glass had begun to cloud—not with ice but with a kind of greying, as though the garden's color were being drawn out of it. The roses along the south wall, which had bloomed defiantly through every frost since autumn, were still blooming. But their red had gone pale at the edges. Washed out. A flower remembered rather than seen.

And the paths—the living paths of moss and flowering thyme that shifted through the garden like slow green rivers, opening ways toward buried things—had gone still. Frozen mid-curve, like a sentence someone had stopped writing.

"It's spending itself," Maren said quietly. "All these months, keeping us alive. Keeping *remembered*. Remembering is not free, Casper Hartwell. Nothing is free. The garden has been paying out of a purse that only ever held so much, and now the Long Winter has come to collect from everyone at once—including us."

Casper walked to the nearest rose and touched one pale petal. It felt cold. Not dead—nothing in this garden died, that was the whole horror and mercy of it—but *thinner*. He thought of the prophecy book hidden inside his coat, the branching lines, the final line that waited blank as fresh snow. He thought of Ondrell's voice coming through the captured mirror two nights ago, silk over iron: *Your name, Bearer. Give us the name, and we will spare what can still be spared.*

"How long?" he asked.

Maren didn't answer at once. She looked out at the whitened garden with an expression Casper had learned to read among the Unwritten—the look of people who had already lost everything once and recognized the shape of losing it again.

"A week," she said finally. "Perhaps less. When the last of the remembering goes, the Unwritten go with it. All of us. Every soul the Ascendants erased, gone the second time—and this time there will be no garden to hold the ghost of us, no book, no boy with a stolen name. Just snow." She turned to him, and her eyes were fierce and wet. "Whatever you're going to decide, decide it soon."

* * *

Sera Ilvane rode through the village of Harrowgate at midmorning and knew, before she dismounted, that something had broken.

She had passed through Harrowgate twice on her hunt for the Bearer. Both times, the village had greeted her with the wary hostility of people who remembered what inquisitors were. Today they simply stared. A woman dropped a basket of apples in the square and did not stoop for them; she stood looking at the scattered fruit as if she had never seen an apple before and could not imagine why anyone would want one. Two men argued outside the smithy—but it was a strange argument, circular, both of them saying *who are you* and *what is this place* and *why am I here*, the same questions wearing grooves in the air.

An old dog trotted up to Sera and pressed its head against her knee, whining, and she understood with a lurch that the animal was the only creature in the square who still knew who it was.

"Inquisitor." A girl of perhaps twelve tugged at her coat hem. The girl's eyes were dry and frightened in the particular way of children who have lost something they cannot name. "Mama woke up and didn't know me. She asked me my name. I told her and she said it was pretty, like I was a stranger. Papa says it'll pass, the fever, but nobody's got a fever, have they?"

"No," Sera said. Her voice came out flat. "No fever."

"Then what is it? What's happening to them?"

What was happening was written in the orders Sera carried, in the tithe ledgers she had spent her career enforcing, in the archive pages she had burned with her own hands eleven days ago in a small room that smelled of ash and liberation. The Ascendants had been drawing memory out of the realm for a century—one tithe at a time, a cupful from every life, poured into the Spire's great reservoirs. They had called it maintenance. Balance. The price of peace after the war.

But reservoirs drain. And whatever Ondrell had begun—whatever final rewrite he was feeding with a hundred years of stolen remembering—it had torn something open. The realm's memory was running backward into the Spire like water finding a hole in the world, and the Long Winter was what remained behind. Snow without warmth. Lives without pasts.

"I don't know," Sera told the girl, because it was true, because every lie she had ever told in a grey coat sat in her throat like a stone, and because the truth—that the order Sera had served since childhood was eating the world's mind and calling it order—was more than a child should carry. "But I'm going to try to stop it."

The girl studied her with the merciless clarity of the young. "You're one of them, aren't you. The ones who did it."

Sera held her gaze. "Yes."

The girl nodded slowly, accepting this the way children accept weather. Then she picked up Sera's stirrup and led her horse toward the well, because someone had to water it, and whoever else these people had forgotten how to be, they were still, underneath everything, people who watered a stranger's horse.

Sera stood in the square a moment longer, watching the snow come down on Harrowgate, and made herself a promise with no ceremony attached: she would reach the Spire before the reservoirs finished draining, or she would die on the road trying. There was no third option anymore. There hadn't been, really, since the night she burned her ledgers.

* * *

Vess of the Low Road woke in a smuggler waystation with blood on her bandages and a knife in her hand, which was normal. What was not normal was that she could not remember whose blood it was.

She lay still and took inventory, the way she did every morning of her life: knife, yes. Wound in her side from the wardens' crossbow on the White Road, yes, hot and deep and bad. The page from the prophecy book, folded against her heart where she had kept it since taking it back from Casper's kit while he slept—yes, still there, still heavy, still unread since the night she'd deciphered enough of it to stop reading.

*Betrayal,* the page named her. She had not needed to read further. Some words you recognize the way you recognize your own face.

She swung her feet to the floor and found a young smuggler sitting across the room, watching her with wide eyes. Denny. She knew that—knew it the way you know a fact in a ledger, *Denny, seventeen, good climber, afraid of the dark.* But when she searched for the memory beneath the fact—the night she'd pulled him half-frozen off the Kettle Pass, the way he'd cried and pretended he hadn't, the ten thousand small mornings since that had made him hers—there was nothing. A locked door. A room she owned but couldn't enter.

"Vess?" Denny said. "You're looking at me funny."

"How long have you run with me, boy?"

He blinked. "Six years. Since Kettle Pass. You know that."

"Say it again."

"Six years. You fished me out of a snowdrift and I've been yours ever since." He hesitated. "Haven't I?"

The question hung between them, and Vess heard what lived under it: *you're looking at me like you don't know, and if you don't know, then who tells me who I am?*

"Six years," she confirmed, and her voice was steady, because a queen's voice is always steady or it is nothing. "And you'll be mine six more if we live that long. Now saddle the horses. We ride for Highmoor."

"Highmoor?" Denny frowned. "That's where the Bearer went. That's where you sent him."

"Yes."

"You sold his route to the wardens." Denny said it carefully, testing the words. "For the medicine. For the fever in the low camps. Everyone knows. Nobody blames—" He stopped, because her face had changed.

"Everyone knows," Vess agreed softly. "And now I'm going back. Saddle the horses, Denny."

Alone, she drew out the folded page and held it over the waystation's small fire, close enough to feel the heat, far enough to keep it whole. She had meant to burn it a dozen times. Betrayers shouldn't get to keep their evidence. But the page was the only proof in the world of what she'd done, and destroying it felt like lying, and Vess had built her whole kingdom on one law: *a smuggler who lies to herself dies poor.*

Besides. If the page said what she feared it said—if it named her the hinge on which the prophecy would break—then it also meant she mattered. That somewhere in the great machinery of fate, a low-road smuggler with a crossbow wound was a load-bearing wall.

She put the page away, unburned, and went to see about the horses.

* * *

In a village called Emberly, on the far side of the Thornfells, a schoolteacher named Pell woke and could not remember why the twenty-three faces looking up at him trusted him.

In the Spire, in a cell of blank white stone, wardens reported to their superiors that the prisoner in the seventh level had stopped speaking—not from silence, but because he had begun narrating memories so vividly false that the extraction engines choked on them, and the archivists argued in the corridors about whether this constituted resistance or madness.

On the White Road, a warden patrol found their own footprints from the previous day filled with snow, and one of them, a young man named Corren, looked at those footprints and could not remember making them, and said nothing, because the others would report him, and he had a sudden, sourceless certainty that being reported was the worst thing in the world.

And everywhere, everywhere, the snow came down—patient, white, total—covering the roads and the roofs and the graves, covering the world the way a clean page covers everything written on it before.

* * *

Casper stood in the fading garden as the light went, and the last thing the day gave him was a single rose, on the south wall, flushing suddenly, briefly, gloriously red—as if the garden were spending its final coin on one perfect act of remembering, one last declaration: *I was here. This was real. We were real.*

Then the color drained away, and the snow came down through the open glass roof, landing on the pale petals, and did not melt.

Maren appeared at his shoulder. Behind her, gathered in the greenhouse doorway, stood the Unwritten—forty-odd souls the world had erased, watching their sanctuary fade around them, and none of them weeping, because they had practiced for this their whole deathless lives.

"Well, Casper Hartwell," Maren said. "Tomorrow the council meets. Hollow Names, we call it, when the end gets close and every voice must be counted. There are those among us who say you should take Ondrell's offer—take back your name, destroy the Ascendants from inside their own machine, damn the cost. There are those who say any use of the rewrite is the crime itself, no matter whose hands hold it. And there are some—" her voice gentled, "—who say there is a third road, if a man is brave enough and foolish enough to walk it. Tomorrow we will ask you which man you are."

Casper looked out at the whitening garden, at the frozen paths, at the rose that had given its last red for nothing—or for everything, depending on what he did next. He thought of Tam's workshop burning. Of Sera's face on the frozen road, asking her one honest question. Of Vess riding back through the snow with her betrayal folded against her heart. Of the blank line at the bottom of the prophecy, waiting.

"Tomorrow," he said.

But that night, alone in the hollow with the book open on his knees, he read the branching lines one more time—and stopped, cold, at a passage he had somehow never seen before, though he must have read this page a dozen times:

*When the first snow falls and does not melt, the Bearer will understand that the choice was never his to make alone.*

Beneath it, in ink that had not been there an hour ago, a new line was writing itself.
