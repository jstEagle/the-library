## 39. The Gardener of Highmoor

The thaw came in the night, and by morning the world smelled of wet stone and green things.

Sera Ilvane stood at the broken gate of Highmoor and did not go in. She had been standing there for a long time—long enough that the frost on her grey coat had melted to dark patches at the shoulders, long enough that her boots had begun to sink into mud that a week ago had been iron-hard snow. The gate itself was a ruin of black iron, half-swallowed by a hedge that had no business being alive in this season or any season, and beyond it the garden rose in terraces she remembered from reports, from ledgers, from a life spent cataloguing other people's memories while her own sat in someone else's archive like evidence in a locked drawer.

She had come to find a dead man's grave. That was what she told herself. Casper Hartwell had fed himself into the garden-spell the way a cartographer feeds paper into a fire—one corner at a time, watching it curl—and when the light had gone out of him there had been nothing left to bury. No body. No name, even. Ondrell was ash and ruin; the Spire's archive heart was a crater of fused glass and blooming frost-flowers that no one could explain; and across the whole breadth of the realm, people were waking up weeping, holding their children, screaming the names of neighbors who had never existed until yesterday morning.

And Casper Hartwell was nowhere. Not in the records—not that the records meant anything anymore, half of them rewritten overnight as ten thousand stolen lives poured back into their owners. Not in Vess's smuggler networks, though the old woman had turned every road in Thornfell inside out looking, and had finally stopped answering Sera's letters. Not in the garden, where Maren and the Unwritten had walked for days among the blooming terraces calling his name—or the other name, the true one, the author's name that had started all of it—until their voices gave out.

So: a grave. A place to stand and say the things she should have said while he was alive. *I'm sorry. I burned your workshop. I put the wardens on Tam's trail years before you ever knew my face. I am the reason your invisible life ended, and I am the reason it mattered.* She had rehearsed it the whole way up from Candlemere, three weeks of walking through a countryside drunk on returned memory, and now that she was here the words sat in her chest like swallowed stones.

She went in anyway.

* * *

The garden did not look like a ruin anymore.

That was the first thing that undid her. In the reports—in her own memory of the reports, which she had checked and rechecked against the recovered ledgers—the Highmoor garden had been a wound: collapsed greenhouse glass, walls furred with moss, hedges gone feral and blind. What she walked through now was order. Not the rigid order of Ascendant architecture, all axes and imposed symmetry, but something older and more patient, the kind of order a river has, or a flock of birds. Paths curved where feet had worn them. Fruit trees heavy out of season leaned over low stone walls. And everywhere, climbing the ruins, threading through the cracks, growing straight up through flagstones that should have killed anything green a century ago—flowers.

Frost-flowers. White petals veined with silver, cold to the touch, the same impossible blooms that had erupted through the Spire's floors the night the garden came for its own. They grew thickest along the central path, as if the ground itself remembered where he had walked.

Sera followed them without deciding to.

Her coat was wrong here. She knew that. Three weeks ago she had stood in front of a mirror in a Candlemere inn and cut the grey braid from her collar with a paring knife, and the woman in the mirror had not looked free, exactly—she had looked undressed. But she hadn't been able to wear it another day, not after the ledger, not after everything the restored archives had shown her about the woman she used to be before Ondrell's scissors. Her real name sat in her mind now like a coin found in an old coat pocket: familiar in shape, strange in weight. *Seretha.* She was still learning to answer to it. Most days she didn't try.

The path climbed past a dry fountain choked with watercress, past a sundial whose gnomon had been snapped off by some ancient frost, and came out at last onto a walled terrace where the air went suddenly warm, the way air goes warm near a hearth you can't see.

He was on his knees in the dirt.

For one heartbeat she thought—absurdly, hopelessly—that it was him. Casper Hartwell, alive, whole, his memory folded back into him like a letter resealed. But no. This man was younger. Twenty, perhaps twenty-two, with brown hair sun-streaked at the ends and hands that moved through the soil with the unhurried certainty of someone who had done this work forever, except that he couldn't have, because—

Because when he looked up at her, his eyes were kind and completely empty of recognition.

"Morning," he said. "You'll want to wipe your boots. The east beds are seedlings."

His voice was easy, unhurried, faintly amused—as if strangers arrived at the top of a ruined mountain garden every day. As if the world had not ended and begun again within living memory. Sera stood very still.

"Do I know you?" she asked.

"No." He said it without embarrassment, without grief—a simple fact, reported the way he might report the weather. "Nobody does. It's rather restful, actually." He brushed soil from his knees and stood, and she saw that he was tall, and lean, and that there was dirt under his fingernails and a smudge of it along his jaw, and that none of it made him look less like a man who had once drawn maps in a workshop above a chandler's shop in Thornfell town.

"You're the gardener," she said. "Maren told me—I mean, I heard there was a gardener."

"There's me," he agreed. "Whether I'm a gardener is a matter of opinion. I woke up here eleven days ago with dirt on my hands and no idea how it got there." He smiled. It was a good smile. It was, she thought with a lurch, *Casper's* smile—the same crooked, self-deprecating tilt, the smile of a man whose entire strategy for survival had been to seem harmless. "Maren says I'm the garden's. Whatever that means."

"What do you remember?" The question came out sharper than she intended. She softened it. "Before. Anything."

He considered it honestly, head tilted, and the gesture was so precisely Casper's that she had to look away, out over the terrace wall, at the white peaks of Thornfell going gold in the morning light.

"Warmth," he said at last. "A tower. Not this tower—" he nodded toward the ruined folly at the garden's far end, "—a taller one, white stone. Someone reading to me. Ink. The smell of ink, a great deal of ink. And a tune." He hummed it then, four bars, lilting and off-key in a way that suggested he'd learned it from the inside of someone else's head.

Sera's breath left her in a thin white stream.

It was a smuggler's tune. *The Low Road Reel.* Every runner in Vess's network whistled it on the passes; Vess herself claimed to have written it, though she'd also claimed, in the same hour, to have invented snow. Casper Hartwell had hummed it constantly—she knew that from the surveillance transcripts, from the weeks she had tracked him through other people's eyes before ever standing in front of him on a frozen road and asking her one forbidden question.

"That song," she said carefully. "Where did you get it?"

"I don't know." He frowned, and the frown was Casper's too. "It's just *there*. Like the maps."

"The maps?"

"Oh—" He crouched, swept his hand across a patch of bare earth between the beds, and drew. Five strokes. Six. Sera felt the ground tilt beneath her.

It was the Thornfell high passes. Perfectly. The switchbacks above Hollowmere, the false col that fooled every government surveyor, the smuggler's cut through the scree that appeared on no map but the ones Casper Hartwell had sold to Madame Vess for six years. Drawn freehand, in seconds, in dirt, by a man who claimed to remember nothing.

"You've never seen these mountains," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I've never left the garden," he said. "Maren won't let me yet. She says the world is still settling." He studied his own drawing with mild curiosity, the way a man studies handwriting he doesn't recognize as his own. "But I know where every path goes. Isn't that strange? I could draw you the whole range. I could draw you Candlemere." His finger hovered, then traced a small, precise square south of the mountains. "There's a building there with a heart of glass. There was a fire in it recently. I don't know how I know that either."

The fire in which she had burned her own surveillance records. The fire in which Sera Ilvane had ceased, officially, to exist.

The frost-flowers along the wall had begun, very quietly, to open.

* * *

They walked the terraces together, because she could not make herself leave and he seemed to have no concept that a guest might want anything besides company. He talked the way Casper had talked when he was nervous—which was to say, he filled silence with precise, useful observations, as though the world were a document he was annotating. Which plants had come back wrong after the thaw. Which bees had returned to the hives. How the eastern wall's foundation stones were laid in a pattern that predated the Ascendants by centuries, and how someone ought really to write that down before it was forgotten again.

"Forgotten things are dangerous," he said, apropos of nothing. "Or maybe they're only dangerous when they're forgotten on purpose. Accidental forgetting is just weather. Deliberate forgetting—" he shook his head, "—that's a wound. Somebody did that to somebody."

"Yes," Sera said. "Somebody did."

"Maren says I helped stop it." He said it without pride, the way he might report a rainfall. "She says there was a man who gave everything so everyone else could remember. She won't tell me his name. She says names have weight, and mine is light as a feather right now, and she doesn't want to crush me with somebody else's." He glanced at her sidelong. "Do you know him? The man from the story?"

Sera stopped walking.

The terrace overlooked the lower garden, and below them the frost-flowers ran in a river down the central path, thousands upon thousands of white blooms, and every single one of them, she realized, was oriented toward the man beside her. Not toward the sun—there was hardly any sun through the cloud. Toward *him*. As if the whole garden were a field of small pale faces, all turned to listen.

"I knew him," she said. "Not well enough. Not soon enough." She made herself go on, because he deserved honesty even if he would never know why. "He was a mapmaker. He spent his whole life making sure nobody noticed him, and then he found out the world had been lying to him since before he could walk, and instead of running—he ran, at first, he was smarter than anyone gave him credit for—but in the end he stood in a room full of people who wanted to erase everything, and he opened himself up like a book, and he let them take his name so that everyone else could have theirs back."

The gardener listened with his head slightly bowed, and when she finished he was quiet for a long moment.

"That sounds lonely," he said finally. "Being the one who remembers for everybody." Then, softer: "Did he have someone? At the end?"

"He had people fighting for him. A smuggler queen who loved him like a son and betrayed him like a mother. An elder named Maren who taught him what the world used to be. And—" Sera's throat tightened. "And an enemy who became something else. On a frozen road, she asked him a question she shouldn't have, and he answered honestly, and after that they weren't really enemies anymore. She got him out of the Spire. She was supposed to protect him afterward, and she—" *failed*, she did not say. *I failed. I let him walk into the archive heart alone because he asked me to hold the door, and holding the door was all I could do, and it was not enough.*

"He must have trusted her," the gardener said.

"He must have," Sera said, "been a fool."

The gardener shook his head slowly. "No," he said, with the mild, immovable certainty of a man stating that water runs downhill. "Trusting someone with your worst truth isn't foolishness. It's the only map worth drawing." He looked at her then, fully, and his empty eyes were somehow less empty than any eyes she had ever met. "Whoever she was, I expect she's carrying it still. The trust. People do that. They carry it whether or not they believe they've earned it."

Sera discovered that she was crying.

Not dramatically—she had never permitted herself drama, even now, even unfrocked and unnamed—but simply, quietly, the way frost melts: all at once, from the edges inward. She turned her face away toward the mountains and let it happen, and the gardener had the grace to pretend to be interested in a bee.

* * *

She stayed three days.

She told herself it was to rest, to recover from the road, to wait out a storm that never quite arrived. In truth she stayed because the garden would not let her leave—or perhaps because she would not let herself, because every time she reached the gate the frost-flowers along the path thickened, leaning, and something in her chest refused to walk away from a miracle still in progress.

On the second day she watched him teach two of the Unwritten's children to graft apple stock, patient and precise, laughing at his own mistakes. On the third day she found him at dawn standing at the terrace wall, humming the Low Road Reel, staring at Thornfell with an expression she recognized from a hundred miles and a lifetime ago: the expression of a man reading a landscape the way other men read letters from home.

"It pulls," he said, without turning. "The mountains. Like a hook behind my ribs. Maren says I'm not to go." He paused. "What's up there?"

"Smugglers," Sera said. "Grief. A woman named Vess who will feed you until you can't move and lie to you the entire time, and love you harder than anyone you'll ever meet." She came to stand beside him. Below, the garden breathed its impossible warmth into the cold morning. "Roads that don't appear on any honest map."

"I'd like to see them," he said. "The roads. I think I'd be good at roads." He smiled that crooked smile. "I don't know why."

The frost-flowers at their feet shivered in a wind that touched nothing else.

On the afternoon of the third day, Maren found Sera packing her kit in the shelter of the old greenhouse—the walled one, the one where a cartographer had once sheltered from a storm and opened a book that should never have been opened. The old woman moved slowly now; the winter had taken tolls from everyone, even the Unwritten, even the remembered. But her eyes were as clear as ever, and they went straight to Sera's face and read it like a ledger.

"You're leaving."

"I have to deliver a report," Sera said. "To people who are trying to build something out of what's left. They want testimony. About the Spire. About Ondrell. About—" she gestured vaguely at everything, the garden, the thaw, the boy in the dirt, "—all of it."

"And about him?"

Sera's hands went still over her bedroll.

"I don't know what I'd say," she admitted. "He's not Casper Hartwell. Casper Hartwell is dead; I watched the light go out of him. And he's not—" she couldn't say the true name; it still felt like handling a live coal, "—not who he was before, either. He's new. Blank pages." She looked down. "I keep thinking I should tell him everything. Who he was. What he did. What it cost."

Maren was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was gentle and absolutely firm, the voice of a woman who had watched a civilization erase a people and had spent a hundred years refusing to forget it.

"A page that's already written can't surprise you," she said. "Let him write himself. If the truth wants him, it knows where he gardens." She laid one weathered hand briefly on Sera's arm. "Besides. You of all people should understand. You spent thirty years as someone else's draft. Would you have wanted a stranger arriving to read you your original?"

Sera thought of Seretha, the girl in the pre-edited ledger, the child who had trusted the archivists. She thought of the woman she had chosen to become in the ashes of Candlemere. Neither one was her. Both of them were.

"No," she said. "I wouldn't have."

* * *

She left at first light, before he woke.

It was cowardice, probably. Or mercy. She had stopped being able to tell the difference somewhere on the frozen road outside Thornfell, and she suspected she never would again. She went down through the terraces in the grey half-dark, past the sleeping beds, past the fountain, past the sundial with its broken shadow, and at the gate she stopped—because the frost-flowers had grown overnight, and now they lined the path all the way to the iron arch, a corridor of small white lights, and every bloom was open and turned toward the garden's heart.

Toward him. Even now. Even asleep.

"Goodbye," Sera said aloud, to the flowers, to the garden, to the man she could not bring herself to wake. "Thank you. For the door. For the question you answered honestly." She pulled her collar up against the cold. "If you ever draw a map of yourself, I hope it's a good one."

She stepped through the gate.

Behind her—she did not turn, but she heard it, felt it in the soles of her boots, in the roots of her teeth—a sound like a held breath released. When she finally glanced back from the ridge, half a mile down, the whole of Highmoor's upper terrace had come into bloom: a cascade of white and silver spilling over the ruined walls, blazing against the snowfields like a signal fire, like a beacon lit for someone still expected home.

And in the center of the terrace, small in the distance, a figure stood at the wall with the dawn breaking gold behind him, looking not at the mountains, not at the garden, but down the road she had taken.

Watching her go. Or waiting for her to come back. With those eyes, she could not tell the difference, and perhaps neither could he.

Sera Ilvane—who had been Seretha, who had been nothing, who was learning daily to be someone new—lifted one hand, then let it fall, and walked on into the waking world.

Behind her, in the garden of Highmoor, a young man with no past knelt in the dirt, picked up a fallen frost-petal, and began—without knowing why, without stopping to ask—to draw a map of a road he had never walked, leading to a door he had never opened, in a city he had never seen.

The petal was white. The lines were true.

And at the edge of the drawing, unbidden, his hand added a small square building with a heart of glass, and beside it, in handwriting he did not recognize, a single word:

*Soon.*
