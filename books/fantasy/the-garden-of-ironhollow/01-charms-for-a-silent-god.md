## 1. Charms for a Silent God

The trick to selling charms in a city that had outlawed magic, Jude Pemberton had learned, was to sell them honestly.

Not *actually* honest — that would get a man hanged — but honest in the way that mattered. He never claimed his charms worked. He sold them the way a grandmother sells soup when the cook's been dead a decade: with warmth, with ceremony, and with the unspoken understanding between buyer and seller that what was being purchased was not the thing itself, but the feeling of it.

"Protection against bad dreams," he told the fishmonger's wife, wrapping a knotted cord of grey wool in brown paper. "Old family recipe. My gran swore by it."

His gran had sworn by nothing. His gran had died before the Silence, back when charms were charms and the god of Ironhollow still answered prayers with something better than weather.

"That'll be two coppers."

The woman paid, clutched the parcel like an amulet, and left his stall smiling. Jude watched her go and felt the familiar hollow ache behind his ribs — the one shaped exactly like a prayer nobody answered anymore.

Three years. Three years since the ember in his chest had last flickered warm. Three years since he'd felt anything from the god who had, once upon a time, chosen him — or so the old monks at Saint Verre had claimed, pressing their hands to his sternum and weeping about *vessels* and *wards* until he'd run away to become a mercenary instead, which had seemed at the time like the more sensible career.

Now he sold wool knots and river-stones and little tin medallions stamped with symbols that meant nothing, in a city where meaning anything could cost you your tongue.

Vell's Crossing didn't hate magic, exactly. Vell's Crossing hated what magic did to property values. The Crown's Wardens enforced the Edict of Quiet Ash with a bureaucratic thoroughness that made burning people feel almost like paperwork, and so the city had learned to want its miracles in safe, deniable forms: a knot against nightmares, a stone for luck, a blessing whispered over a newborn that everyone agreed afterward had just been words.

Jude was very good at words.

He arranged his stall for the afternoon market — cords and stones and medallions, each priced to be affordable and useless — and tried not to think about the ember sleeping behind his breastbone. It sat there like a banked coal, patient as debt. Some nights he pressed his palm flat against it and asked, the way you'd ask a locked door, *Are you there?*

Some nights he almost thought he heard breathing on the other side.

Mostly he heard nothing at all.

* * *

The child came at dusk, when the market lamps were being lit and the Wardens changed shift along the Processional.

She couldn't have been more than seven or eight, small enough that she had to stand on her toes to see over the edge of his stall, with a smudge of soot on one cheek and eyes too old for the rest of her face. She looked left. She looked right. Then she looked at him with the particular desperation of someone about to do something illegal.

"I need a charm," she whispered.

"You've come to the right criminal," Jude said gently. "What sort?"

She glanced over her shoulder again. Down the lane, a Warden patrol was making its rounds, torchlight sliding across shuttered windows. When she turned back, her voice dropped so low he had to lean in.

"For my brother. He's going to be taken."

Jude's hands went still over the cords.

"Taken," he repeated.

"The Wardens came to our street yesterday." Her fingers twisted in her skirt. "They took the cobbler's daughter. Mama says they only take the ones who're *touched*. But my brother — he makes things move. Little things. Spoons. He doesn't mean to. He cries after, and Mama put him in the cellar, but they'll come back, I know they'll come back, and—" Her breath hitched. "I heard you sell real ones. Under the fake ones. That's what Tomas said. He said you're the only one in the Crossing who sells *real*."

*Tomas,* Jude thought, with a weariness that went all the way down to his boots, *is going to get us all hanged.*

Because here was the truth he kept folded beneath the wool knots and the river-stones, the truth he'd carried for three years like a stone in his shoe: every charm he sold was hollow, yes — except that they weren't. Not entirely. Because the ember in his chest, silent as it was, still leaked. A trickle. A candle-end of something divine that had never quite gone out, and which Jude, in a moment of weakness roughly two years ago, had begun folding into his work without quite admitting what he was doing.

One charm in ten held a whisper of true warmth. He never knew which. He never let himself know. It was safer that way — for him, and for whoever bought them. If he didn't know, then no Warden could torture it out of him, because he genuinely had nothing to tell.

Except now a child with soot on her face was asking him to choose.

"I don't sell real ones," he said quietly. "Tomas is a liar and probably a thief."

Her chin trembled. "Please."

"Kid—"

"They're going to burn him." The words cracked out of her like something breaking. "I *saw* them practicing. In the square. They build the pyres on Restday and everyone stands and watches and claps when it's done, and my brother is six years old and he just makes spoons move."

The market noise seemed very far away. Jude looked at this small furious terrified girl and felt the ember stir behind his ribs — a slow, warm roll, like a dog lifting its head.

*Don't,* he told it. *Don't you dare.*

He reached under the stall, past the honest junk, to the false bottom where he kept the one charm he'd never intended to sell: a plain iron ring on a leather cord, cold as a promise. He'd made it the week the Silence began, back when he still believed the god might answer if he just built the door wide enough. It was the warmest thing he'd ever made. He'd kept it the way you keep a letter you can't send.

He set it in the girl's palm and closed her fingers over it.

"This goes around your brother's neck," he said. "And then your family gets out of Vell's Crossing. Tonight. Not tomorrow. Tonight. There are smugglers' paths through the Fenmarch — ask for Odile's people at the Broken Keel, say Jude sent you. Can you remember that? The Broken Keel. Say it back."

"The Broken Keel," she whispered. "Jude sent me."

"Good girl." He pushed the cord into her hands properly. "No charge."

"Everything costs," she said, with the flat certainty of a child raised in Vell's Crossing, and something in his chest twisted sideways.

"Not this," he said. "Go."

She went — quick as a mouse, vanishing between the stalls — and Jude stood alone in the lamplight feeling the ember settle again, warm and satisfied, like a cat that had gotten its way.

"You shouldn't have done that."

The voice came from behind him, low and level, and Jude knew it before he turned around. He'd have known it blindfolded, deafened, dead. Some voices you carry with you whether you want to or not.

Sera Ilvane stood at the edge of his stall in full Warden black, torchlight catching the silver badge at her collar. Three years hadn't softened her. If anything, the years had honed her — cheekbones sharper, mouth harder, eyes the same storm-grey they'd been the night she'd found him kneeling in the ruins of Saint Verre with blood on his hands and a lie in his mouth.

"Sera," he said. His voice came out steadier than he deserved. "Warden Ilvane. Come to buy a luck charm? Special rate for the Crown."

"Don't." She stepped closer, and up close he could see the exhaustion under her composure, the way her hand rested near her baton without quite touching it. "I watched you give that child something from under your stall, Jude. I've been watching this stall for two months."

"Two months," he said. "Flattered. Most men have to buy a girl dinner first."

"*Jude.*"

"What do you want, Sera?" And there it was — the crack in his voice, small but real, the first in three years of careful silence. "You want me to confess? Fine. I sell knots and stones and lies to frightened people. Arrest me. Read me the Edict. I'll even help you spell 'hollow' for the report."

"I don't think they're hollow." Her voice dropped. "That's the problem. Three weeks ago, a woman in Copperlane survived a house fire with no burns. She was wearing one of your cords. Last month a fever broke in a whole tenement the night after a peddler bought a river-stone off you. I keep a list, Jude. Eleven incidents. All traced back here."

The ember pulsed behind his ribs, slow and traitorous, and Jude prayed — uselessly, habitually, to a god who wasn't listening — that his face gave away nothing.

"You should arrest me, then," he said.

"Yes," Sera said. "I should."

Neither of them moved. Around them the market emptied, lamps guttering, the city settling into its nightly performance of innocence. Somewhere far off, a bell tolled the hour, and Jude thought he felt — faintly, impossibly, for the first time in three years — the ember turn over in his sleep.

Like something beginning to wake.

Or something trying to warn him.

Sera's eyes flicked toward the lane where the girl had vanished, then back to his face, and when she spoke again her voice was barely above the wind.

"The wards on the border failed today," she said. "All of them. Every wardstone from here to the coast went dark at noon, and Command is saying it's a technical fault, and Command is lying. So before I decide what I am tonight — Warden or woman — you're going to tell me what you know about the god of Ironhollow, Jude Pemberton. And you're going to start with why I think your charms have been working."

Above them, unseen behind the smoke-hazed clouds, the first star of the evening failed to appear.
