## 2. The Night the Stars Went Out

Jude had learned to read a city by its silences, and Vell's Crossing had gone quiet in all the wrong places.

The market bell hadn't rung for evening. The lamplighters' cart, which rattled down Copperlane every dusk like a drunk rolling home, was absent. Even the dogs had stopped — and dogs always barked when something was wrong. That was their whole profession.

He stood in the doorway of his stall, counting his charms one last time before packing them away. Hollow things, most of them. Bits of river-glass wrapped in copper wire, knotted thread sold as "warding cord," dried lavender that promised restful sleep because lavender did that anyway, no gods involved. Three years ago he could have made any of them sing. Now he made them *look* like they might, if you squinted, if you wanted it badly enough.

People bought wanting. It was the one commodity Vell's Crossing never outlawed.

"Closing time," he told the empty street, and began stacking his trays.

That was when he saw the girl.

She couldn't have been more than eight or nine, standing at the edge of the stall's shadow with her hands folded tight, the way children stood when they'd been sent somewhere they shouldn't be. Her eyes were fixed on the tray of river-glass charms.

"You're not supposed to be out after moonrise," Jude said. "New ordinance. Very serious. Wardens drag you off to—" he waved vaguely "—somewhere unpleasant."

"I know." She didn't move. "My brother's sick. Mam said — she said maybe a charm would help. She gave me this." A copper coin sat in her palm, worn nearly smooth, worth perhaps a quarter of what he charged.

Jude looked at the coin. He looked at the girl. He thought about the ember sleeping behind his sternum, the last true scrap of magic in his body, warm as a banked coal and just as forbidden.

*Don't*, he told himself. *You've survived three years by being nobody.*

He picked out the largest charm on the tray, pressed it into her hand along with her coin, and closed her fingers over both. "For your brother. And keep the money — tell your mam I overcharged you and you argued me down. She'll believe it. Everyone does."

The girl's face did something complicated and bright. "You're kind," she said, with the devastating accuracy of children.

"I'm tired," Jude corrected. "It looks similar from a distance."

She laughed — and then the sky went wrong.

It wasn't sound first. It was the light. Every lantern in Copperlane guttered at once, not blown out but *drunk down*, as though the dark itself had opened a mouth. Jude looked up on instinct, the way everyone did, the way people had looked up at night since the first night.

And there were no stars.

Not clouds. Not fog. The sky above Ironhollow was clear and black and utterly empty, a vast smooth absence where stars should have been, and Jude felt — he would swear this later, gripping a doorframe while the city screamed around him — he felt the absence *notice him*. Like an eye opening somewhere beyond the black, patient and enormous, looking down through the hole where the heavens used to be.

The wards. The border wards that had held for four hundred years, that every child learned were older than the Crown itself. They hadn't flickered. They had *shattered*, and whatever they'd been keeping the sky honest with had gone with them.

Then the screaming started, and the bells, all of them at once, and Vell's Crossing stopped pretending to be safe.

"Go home," Jude told the girl, shoving the whole tray of charms at her. "All of them. Run, don't look up, don't stop."

She ran. He watched until she turned the corner, and then he did the thing three years of survival had trained into his bones: he packed nothing, took everything, and went out the back.

* * *

Sera Ilvane had arrested forty-one mages in her career, and she remembered every name, which was the problem with her.

She stood in the Wardenhall courtyard watching the pyres being built and thought: *this is wrong, this is wrong, this is wrong* in the flat, exhausted way of a woman who has thought exactly this before and done her duty anyway.

The orders had come within the hour of the sky failing. *The wards fell because someone broke them. Mages. Burn them out.* It was nonsense — Sera had helped write half the containment protocols herself, and no hedge-witch with a stolen grimoire could crack wards that old — but nonsense moved fast when people were frightened, and fear was the one thing the Crown had never needed to legislate.

"Captain Ilvane." A young Warden, barely shaving, holding a list. "We've got names for the Copperlane sweep. There's a charm-seller—"

"No." The word came out of her before she'd decided anything. "Copperlane's charm-sellers are frauds. River glass and string. You want real mages, check the registered houses."

The young Warden hesitated. "The list says Pemberton, J."

Sera's hand closed around the edge of her gauntlet hard enough to hurt. *Pemberton.* Of course. Of course the universe, having spent three years ignoring her prayers, would choose tonight to be efficient.

"Sweep starts at the river," she said. "I'll take Copperlane myself."

It wasn't mercy. She told herself it wasn't mercy. It was jurisdiction, procedure, the simple fact that if anyone was going to put Jude Pemberton in irons it should be the person he'd betrayed, because strangers did it clumsily and she at least knew where he kept his knives.

The streets were madness. People stood in the middle of roads staring upward, mouths open, pointing at nothing. A man wept into his hands outside a shrine to the Silent God — shrines to the Silent God had been springing up for three years now, candles lit for a divinity who had stopped answering, and Sera had always found them morbid. Tonight the candlelight looked less like grief and more like apology.

She cut through the tanner's row and caught the flash of a familiar coat vanishing down the alley behind the charm-stall — moving fast, moving *away*, moving like a man who had seen this night coming years ago.

Sera ran.

She cornered him at the wall where Copperlane met the drainage cut, and she had her hand on his shoulder and her blade drawn before he finished turning, and then they were face to face for the first time in three years, and neither of them said anything for a long, stupid moment.

Jude looked terrible. Grey-faced, breath ragged, a pack half-slung off one shoulder. His eyes went from her blade to her badge to her face, doing arithmetic.

"Sera," he said finally. "You look well. Terrifying, obviously, but well."

"Don't." Her voice came out harder than she intended. "Don't do the thing where you talk until I forget why I'm angry."

"It's never worked once."

"It hasn't." She pressed him against the wall, checked his wrists out of pure habit, found no weapons — which meant he'd hidden them, which meant he was still Jude. "Forty-one arrests, Pemberton. I remember all their names. I was going to make yours forty-two."

"And now?"

Above them, through the gap between rooftops, the starless sky hung like a held breath. Somewhere across the city another pyre caught, and the orange glow crawled up the walls, and Sera heard herself say, quietly, in a voice she didn't recognize:

"Now I need to know what you know."

Jude blinked. "I don't—"

"The wards didn't fall, Jude. Wards *break*. They crack, they fray, they get chewed through. These shattered. All of them, at once, at moonrise, like something reached across the whole border and snapped its fingers." She leaned closer. "And you've been selling dead charms in a dead city for three years, ever since your god went quiet, and you flinched *before* the sky went dark. I saw it. You knew."

The lie assembled itself behind his eyes — she could watch it happen, could see the charming deflection taking shape — and then he let it fall, and what was underneath was so tired it broke her heart a little.

"I don't know what happened," he said. "I know my chest has been burning since moonrise like something's trying to climb out. I know the silence in my head got *louder*, which shouldn't be possible, silence is silence. And I know—" he swallowed "—I know that whatever's up there instead of stars, it isn't empty. Empty doesn't *watch*."

Sera released him. Stepped back. Looked at her own hand on the sword hilt, the oath-brand on her wrist, the pyre-light on the walls.

"There are Wardens sweeping Copperlane right now," she said. "Your name is on a list. Mine is on the order authorizing it."

"So arrest me. Get it over with. You've clearly been practicing the speech for years."

"Shut up, Jude." She dragged a hand down her face. When she spoke again it was barely above the noise of the burning city. "There's a woman. Odile Ferrant. Former royal cartographer. Struck off the rolls two years ago for maps that wouldn't stay accurate — everyone thought she was mad or fraudulent." Sera met his eyes. "Her maps have been redrawing themselves every night this week. Roads disappearing. Coastlines shrinking. She sent a message to the Hall and they burned it unread. I didn't."

Jude stared at her. "You're telling me the country is *shrinking*."

"I'm telling you I'm committing treason, and I'd like to stop talking about it in an alley." She jerked her chin toward the drainage cut, toward the dark. "Can you fight?"

"I can run beautifully."

"That'll do. Stay behind me, don't look up, and Jude?" She was already moving, blade drawn, pyre-light sliding along its edge. "If this is some long game of yours — if you knew what was coming and said nothing—"

"Sera." For a moment, under the dead sky, he looked younger than she'd ever seen him, and more afraid. "Three years ago I betrayed you for a god who then abandoned me. I'm out of games. I'm just trying to keep breathing."

"Fine," she said. "Breathe behind me. Move."

They went together into the burning dark, two people with every reason to kill each other, running toward the only person in Ironhollow who might know why the sky had stopped telling the truth.
