## 3. A Cartographer of Vanished Roads

The house at the end of Candlemaker's Row had once been the Royal Cartographic Office's western annex, which meant it had once mattered. Now its shutters hung crooked, its brass plaque read *FERRANT — SURVEYS &c.* with the *and* scratched out and replaced by nothing at all, and a cat of no fixed allegiance slept on the windowsill like it paid rent.

Sera knocked twice, then a third time in the pattern she'd been taught years ago, back when she'd still believed patterns meant something.

"Go away," said a voice through the door. "Unless you're the flour delivery, in which case I forgive you and also you're late."

"It's Sera."

A long pause. "The flour thing was a joke, Ilvane. You can't be flour."

"I've got someone who needs to see your maps."

"Everyone needs to see my maps. Nobody wants to *believe* them." The door opened a hand's width, revealing one eye, sharp and grey as a well-used pencil. The eye traveled from Sera to Jude and back again, and Odile Ferrant made a sound like a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like. "Oh, wonderful. A Warden and a charm-seller. If you're here to arrest me for cartographic heresy, do it quickly; I have ink drying."

"We're not—" Sera began.

"He's not—" Jude said at the same moment.

They looked at each other. It was the first time they'd managed it since the alley, since the sky had gone out, since Sera had pointed her blade at his throat and then, in an act that would haunt her oath-ledger forever, lowered it.

"The stars," Jude said quietly. "She thinks you might know why they went out."

Odile's eye narrowed. Then the door swung wide.

"Well," she said. "Come in before the whole street sees you. Not because I care about my reputation — that died beautifully, thank you — but because yours is about to, and I'd hate to share the experience."

* * *

Inside, the annex smelled of lamp oil, old paper, and something faintly burnt, though not unpleasantly — like toast remembered rather than toast present. Every wall was papered with maps. Not framed, not filed: *papered*, edge to edge, floor to ceiling, so that standing in the room felt like standing inside a country that had been turned inside out.

And every map was wrong.

Sera knew Ironhollow's roads the way she knew her own scars. She'd marched them, patrolled them, bled on three of them. But the maps on these walls showed coastlines where there should be farmland, forests where there should be cities, and — most disturbingly — great swathes of pale grey where *anything* should be. The grey patches weren't blank. They were somehow more than blank: a color that suggested absence the way a scream suggests silence.

"They change," Odile said, watching them look. "Every night, around the third bell. I wake up, and the country has redrawn itself. Smaller. Always smaller." She picked up a teacup from a stack of seven identical teacups and offered it around without much hope. "I've measured it. Forty miles lost off the western reach this month alone. The village of Harrowmere is gone — not destroyed, not abandoned. *Gone.* My map shows grey where I drew a church spire eleven years ago, and when I rode out to check, I found the road simply... ended. Not washed out. Ended, the way a sentence ends. And past it—"

"Grey," Jude said.

"You've seen it." Something flickered across Odile's face — relief, or its exhausted cousin. "Three days ago nobody had seen it. Now everyone's seeing it, or everyone's dead, or both. Sit down. Both of you. The tea is terrible but it's hot, and I find terrible hot things are underrated in a crisis."

They sat. Sera took her tea mostly to have something to hold. Across the little table, Jude cradled his cup like it might be evidence against him.

"Now," Odile said, pulling a chair around backward and straddling it with the ease of a woman who had stopped performing respectability some years ago, "you'll tell me exactly what happened last night, from the beginning, and you'll leave nothing out, because I have spent six years being called mad by the Royal Survey and I would very much like to spend tonight being called mad by *informed people* instead."

So Sera told it. The wards shattering at moonrise — she'd felt it in her teeth, every Warden had, a soundless snap like the world biting down on nothing. The stars going out all at once, not clouded, not veiled, *removed*. The panic in Vell's Crossing. The pyres already lit in Wardenhall Square before midnight. And then the part she still hadn't sorted into any drawer of her mind: finding Jude Pemberton in the smoke, charm-bag over his shoulder, running — and letting him run beside her instead of behind bars.

"And you," Odile said to Jude. "The famous silent god. Three years quiet, yes? That's what the gossip said before the gossip got people burned."

Jude's jaw tightened. "He wasn't quiet because he left," he said finally. "He was quiet because he was *finished*. Saying something. I just didn't know what until the sky went dark."

"Which was?"

"I don't know. But when the wards broke, I felt it here." He pressed two fingers to his sternum, and Sera saw — really saw, for the first time — the way he did it. Not like a man touching an old wound. Like a man touching a coal he was afraid of dropping.

Odile was quiet for a moment. Then she stood, crossed to the largest map on the eastern wall, and pulled it down with both hands.

"This," she said, "is Ironhollow. As drawn by my grandmother. As drawn by her mother. As drawn, if the family records are honest, for nine generations of Ferrants, all of whom served the Crown and all of whom noticed the same thing I'm about to show you and all of whom kept their mouths shut about it."

She laid the old map over a fresh one. Held them up to the lamp.

"Look at the borders."

Sera looked. The old border and the new border were the same shape — the same impossible shape, a closed line with no gate, no pass, no seam. Ironhollow's frontier didn't border anything. It bordered itself.

"A country has neighbors," Odile said softly. "Even an island has a sea. Ironhollow has *nothing*. No trade routes in. No migration records. No war we didn't invent ourselves. Nine generations of cartographers, and not one of us ever asked the obvious question, because the obvious question was: *neighbors to what?*" She set the maps down. Her hands were steady, which Sera suspected cost her something. "Ironhollow isn't a place. It never was. It's a *spell*. A held breath. A room someone built and locked from the inside, and everything in it — us, the roads, the taxes, the Wardens, the god — everything is furniture in that room."

The vertigo hit Sera like a wave hitting a seawall. She gripped her teacup. "That's not possible. I grew up here. I buried my father here. There's a *graveyard*."

"There's a graveyard-shaped spell," Odile agreed, not unkindly. "It feels real because it was made to feel real. That was the whole point. Whatever's outside — whatever the walls were built to keep out, or keep *in* — it couldn't be allowed to touch us. So someone wove a world instead." She tapped the grey patches on the new map. "And now someone, or something, is unwinding it. From outside. Thread by thread. The stars going out wasn't an attack, Warden Ilvane. It was the ceiling coming down."

Silence. Somewhere in the walls, the cat rearranged itself with the moral indifference of cats.

"That's madness," Sera said — but her voice came out wrong, thin, and they all heard it.

"Yes," Odile said cheerfully. "Welcome to my last six years. The Royal Survey voted me insane by a margin of eleven to nothing. I got a cake."

Jude hadn't spoken. He was staring at the grey on the map with an expression Sera recognized from the alley — the look of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like. When he finally spoke, his voice was very quiet.

"The ember in my chest. It flares when I use what's left of him. And every time it does..." He looked up. "The grey spreads faster. I've felt it. Like the fire's eating the same thing the darkness is."

Odile went still. "Say that again. Slower."

"I burn the country to keep people alive. That's the arithmetic."

"Oh," Odile said faintly. "Oh, you *poor* man. You're not carrying a relic. You're carrying a *wick*."

Sera stood up so fast her chair skidded. "Then we cut it out of him. There are surgeons—"

"With what hands?" Jude asked, not unkindly. "With what *country*? You felt what I am, Sera. Back in the alley, when you grabbed my arm. Don't pretend you didn't."

She had felt it. Warmth like a hearth through a wall, like a voice humming under floorboards. She'd told herself it was adrenaline. She was running out of things to tell herself.

Odile was already moving, sweeping maps aside, digging through a chest beneath the window. "If he's a wick, then the flame has a source, and if the source is the god, and the god is finished—" She came up with a scroll case, black leather, sealed with wax stamped by a crest Sera didn't recognize. "Then there's a place where he finished. Everything that ends, ends somewhere. Even spells. Especially spells."

"What's that?" Sera asked.

"My family's sealed survey. Ninth generation. The one document my grandmother ordered burned and my grandfather hid instead, because he loved her more than he obeyed her, which is the only reason our family survived at all." Odile turned the case in her hands. "There's a garden at the center of every old map. Before the Crown redrew them. Before the Office 'corrected' them. An iron-walled garden, marked with a symbol no surveyor would use for topography." She looked up, and for the first time the humor fell away entirely, leaving something older and sadder underneath. "We used it for graves. For the *dead god's* grave."

Jude rose slowly. On the wall behind him, forty maps of a dying country rustled in a draft that shouldn't have been there, and for half a heartbeat Sera could have sworn they all shifted at once — turning, like flowers toward a sun that wasn't in the sky anymore.

"The Door," Jude said.

Odile blinked. "You know that word?"

"No," Jude said. "But something in me does, and lately I've learned to listen." He looked at Sera, and then at Odile, and something in his face settled — the weary arithmetic of a man deciding what he's willing to spend. "How fast can you pack?"

Odile was already stuffing maps into a satchel. "I've been packed for six years, dear. I only stayed for the tea." She paused at the door, cat now winding between her ankles, and added, almost gently:

"You should know the road east is gone as of last night. We'll be walking off the edge of the map. Literally." A beat. "I've always wanted to say that."

* * *

They left before dawn, three shadows and a cat that invited itself, moving south along the canal path while Vell's Crossing behind them glowed orange with fires that had nothing to do with warmth. Sera walked last, as she always had, watching the road behind.

At the first bend, she looked back one final time. The city sat under its starless sky like a patient under a sheet. And above the rooftops, where the northern horizon should have shown the grey haze of the unwritten lands pressing closer —

she saw, for just a moment, a single point of light.

Not a star. Stars didn't come back. This was lower, warmer, steadier — somewhere far to the east, past the dead villages and the ending roads, a small light burning in the dark like a lamp left in a window for someone expected home.

Pip, she would learn later. But standing there on the canal path with her oaths in ashes and her orders hunting her, Sera Ilvane only knew two things: the light was real, and it was waiting for them.

"Hurry up," she called softly to the others. "We're expected."
