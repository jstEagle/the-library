## 4. The Child Who Remembers Light

The smugglers' road out of Vell's Crossing smelled of wet wool and secrets.

Jude had walked it twice before in his mercenary years—once hauling contraband salt, once hauling a wounded man he'd been paid to abandon and hadn't—and both times the path had felt like a held breath. It wound down through the gorse hills behind the tanneries, past a shrine to some minor saint whose face had been chiseled off during the Reforms, and ended at a door built into the side of a hill that was not, by any honest measurement, a hill at all.

"Keep your hood up," Sera murmured beside him. She'd traded her Warden's coat for a fishwife's shawl somewhere in the first mile, and it suited her about as well as a crown on a cat. She kept touching her hip where her badge used to sit, like a tongue probing a missing tooth. "And whatever they offer you to drink, drink it slow."

"I know how smugglers work, Sera."

"You knew how Wardens worked, too, and look how that went."

He had no answer for that, so he said nothing, which was becoming his primary method of conversation with her. Behind them, Odile Ferrant trudged with her map-case hugged to her chest like an infant, muttering coordinates under her breath. Every few hundred paces she'd stop, unroll a corner of parchment, frown, and roll it up again.

"It's moved again," she announced, for the fourth time since dawn. "The river bend. It's *moved*."

"Rivers do that," Jude said.

"Not east. Rivers don't move *east*." She caught up with them, breathless. "Jude. The country is folding. I've charted every league of Ironhollow since I was nineteen years old, and I am telling you that the space between here and the coast is getting *smaller*."

"Then we should hurry," Sera said flatly, "before we run out of country," and Odile made a small wounded noise and said nothing else for a while.

* * *

The hill-door opened before they knocked, which was either very good hospitality or very bad news.

The woman who filled it was broad as a barn door and missing three fingers on her left hand, which she used to gesture them inside with the economy of someone who'd learned gestures were cheaper than words. "Maren sent you," she said. Not a question. "Wardens behind you?"

"Two days, maybe three," Sera said. "They'll have dogs."

"They'll have worse than dogs if the sky keeps doing what it's doing." The woman stepped aside. "In. Boots off. And if any of you is carrying magic, leave it at the threshold or I'll leave *you* there."

Jude felt the ember in his chest shift, warm and guilty as a stolen coin. He thought, very hard, *stay quiet*, and it settled, sullen but obedient. Three years of practice at hiding it had made him fluent in silence.

Inside, the smugglers' den was nothing like he expected—which was to say it was exactly like every smugglers' den he'd ever seen: low ceilings, too many people, a stew pot that had clearly never been fully empty in a decade, and children everywhere. That was the part that always surprised him. People imagined smuggling as knives and shadows, but mostly it was logistics, and logistics meant families, and families meant small feet thundering across floorboards overhead while their parents argued tariffs over cards.

A boy of maybe ten skidded past carrying a cat. A girl was teaching a younger one to cheat at dice, badly, with the teacher cheating worse. And in the corner nearest the fire—

That was where Jude saw her first.

She was small. Six, perhaps seven, though she had the stillness of someone much older, sitting cross-legged on a barrel with her chin tipped up toward the ceiling as if the ceiling were something else entirely. Her eyes were closed. Her lips moved without sound.

"She does that," said a voice at his elbow—one of the smuggler women, flour to the wrists, tired around the eyes. "Born the winter after the Silence, that one. Never seen a star in her life. But you ask her about the sky and she'll tell you things." The woman lowered her voice. "Things she shouldn't know."

"What kind of things?"

"Come see for yourself. But gentle, mind. She startles."

Jude crossed the room without quite deciding to. It was a strange sensation, walking toward something instead of away from it; he'd spent three years perfecting the art of retreat, of keeping everyone at charm's length, selling them little brass charms and taking their coin and never once letting them close enough to notice that the merchant's hands shook.

He crouched near the barrel. Up close, the girl looked ordinary—scraped knees, a cowlick, a smudge of stew on her chin. Ordinary, except that her face, tilted toward the invisible ceiling, wore an expression Jude recognized from mirrors. From his own worst nights. The look of someone listening for a voice that isn't coming.

"Hello," he said softly.

Her eyes opened. They were brown, unremarkable, and then—not. For half a heartbeat, something moved behind them like light through deep water.

"You're loud," she said.

"I—" He glanced back at the room. Nobody was looking at them. "I haven't said anything."

"Not out loud." She studied him with the frankness of children and cats. "In the other place. You're very loud there. Like a kettle." She wrinkled her nose. "A sad kettle."

Behind him, he heard Sera shift her weight—the particular creak of a woman deciding whether to reach for a weapon. "Pip," the flour-woman called warningly from across the room. "Be polite."

"I'm being *accurate*," Pip said with dignity, and then, to Jude: "What's your name?"

"Jude."

"That's not what I asked."

His mouth went dry. "It's what I have."

She considered this, apparently finding it acceptable, and patted the barrel beside her—an invitation no adult had offered him in longer than he cared to count. He sat. The barrel wobbled. Neither of them acknowledged it.

"Do you remember stars?" she asked suddenly.

"No," he said, because lying to her felt like kicking a dog. "Not really. I was young when they went."

"I do." She said it simply, the way another child might say *I can whistle*. "Everyone says I'm making it up. Marta says I'm making it up. But I remember them. There was one that blinked, all green-ish, low over the water. And a whole river of them, white, spilled across the black like milk. And one—" her voice dropped, went reverent, "—one that wasn't a star at all. It was a *doorway*. A hole shaped like home."

Jude's chest hurt. He told himself it was the ember, pressing against his ribs, but the ember had never hurt like this. This was older. This was grief wearing a child's face.

"Pip," he said carefully. "Who told you about the doorway?"

"Nobody." She frowned at him. "I *remembered* it. You're not listening. That's the whole point of remembering, that nobody tells you."

"I'm sorry. You're right." He rubbed his face. "Can you—do you remember anything else? Sounds? Songs?"

Her whole face changed. Light came into it, actual light, or the memory of it, and she began to hum.

Jude stopped breathing.

He knew the melody. Of course he knew it—he'd known it before he had language for knowing, the way you know your own heartbeat. It was the song the god used to hum through the mouths of its gardeners, the song old Pemberton family records described and Jude had heard once, only once, in a dream the night before his grandmother died: a rising phrase like dawn over water, a falling phrase like a hand closing gently around something small. The name of the god, sung rather than spoken, because the true name could not survive being spoken.

No one alive knew that song. The archives that recorded it had burned decades ago. Jude himself couldn't have hummed four notes of it sober.

Pip hummed all of it. Perfectly. Without hesitation, without knowing, the way a stream knows downhill.

When she finished, she looked at him expectantly. "You know it too," she said. "I can hear you almost singing along. In the other place."

"Pip." His voice came out wrecked. He cleared his throat and tried again. "Where did you learn that?"

"Nowhere. I told you." She leaned closer, conspiratorial, and her eyes did the deep-water thing again, and this time Jude saw it plainly: a fleck of gold in the brown, burning steady, the exact color of the ember sleeping in his own chest. "It's just *in* me. Like a pebble in my shoe. Has been since forever." She tapped her sternum. "Right here. Doesn't it feel like that for you?"

The room went very quiet, or Jude went very deaf to it; he couldn't say which. Somewhere behind him Sera said his name and he didn't answer. Odile had gone pale in the way of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't want finished.

Because he understood now. Understood what had woken in him three years ago when the god's voice cut off mid-sentence, understood why the ember flared when he protected people, understood the terrible mathematics Odile had been circling for days. The god hadn't simply died. A dying thing scatters. And its last breath—the breath that held Ironhollow together—hadn't dispersed into air.

It had landed. In vessels. In a mercenary who sold hollow charms and an orphan born after the Silence who remembered light she'd never seen.

They weren't survivors of the god. They were the god, in pieces, wearing coats.

"Jude." Sera's hand on his shoulder, real and warm. "You've gone grey. What is it?"

He looked at Pip. Pip looked back, unbothered, and stole the last heel of bread off the plate between them with the shameless speed of childhood.

"We need to talk," he said hoarsely. "Not here. But Sera—she comes with us. Wherever we go next. Whatever we find."

Sera followed his gaze, read his face the way she'd once read battlefields, and he watched her arrive at the same conclusion by different roads. "You know what you're asking. Carrying a child into—"

"I know exactly what I'm asking." And he did, and it terrified him, because three days ago the correct answer—the survival answer—would have been to walk away from the child, the woman, the mapmaker, all of it, alone and invisible and safe. Instead he heard himself say: "Nobody gets left. Not anymore. I'm done being safe if safe means leaving people in the dark."

Pip, mouth full of stolen bread, nodded solemnly, as though he'd passed some test.

That night, wedged between strangers in the smugglers' common room, Jude slept deeper than he had in three years—and dreamed, for the first time since the Silence, not of silence, but of a garden. Black glass trees. A door standing open. And a small hand slipping into his, warm as an ember, holding on.

He woke before dawn to shouting above—the sentry's voice, cracked with fear:

"Wardens! Wardens on the ridge—and they've got *fire*!"
