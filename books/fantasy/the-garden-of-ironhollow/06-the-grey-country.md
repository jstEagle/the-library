## 6. The Grey Country

The world ended in patches.

That was the thing nobody warned you about—not the Wardens with their burning brands, not Odile with her maps that wept ink, not even the silence where God used to be. The end of a country didn't come like a wave or a fire. It came like moths in a wool coat. You didn't notice until the holes were everywhere, and by then you could see straight through to nothing.

Jude walked at the front of their little procession because walking at the back meant looking at what followed them, and what followed them was grey.

Three days out of Vell's Crossing, they'd crossed into it. Not a border—there were no borders anymore, Odile said, only seams. One moment the road had been honest dirt with honest weeds shouldering through it, and the next it was the color of old dishwater, flat and textureless, as if someone had painted the world but forgotten the world underneath. Pip had stopped humming. That was how Jude knew it was bad. The child hummed everything—bawdy smuggler songs she absolutely should not know, funeral dirges, the nameless melody that made Jude's chest-ember ache like a bruised tooth—but over the grey land she went quiet, one small hand fisted in Sera's cloak.

"Is it dead?" Pip had asked.

"It's resting," Jude had said, which was a lie, and Pip had looked up at him with those too-old eyes and let him keep it, which was worse.

Now, on the fourth morning, they came to the village of Harrow's Bend—or what had been Harrow's Bend, according to the map Odile consulted with the grim focus of a woman reading her own death warrant.

"Should be forty souls here," she murmured. "A well. A shrine to—" She stopped. Everyone stopped saying *the god* now; they said *the shrine*, *the old faith*, *him*, as if naming it might cost something. "A shrine."

There was a well. There was a shrine. And there were people, if you could call them that—a woman frozen halfway through hanging laundry, a sheet billowing in a wind that no longer moved; a man mid-stride toward her with a basket of apples; two children chasing each other around the shrine, forever almost-tagging, never tagging. All of them grey. All of them still. Their faces weren't frightened. That was the horror of it. They looked *patient*, like they'd been told to wait and intended to be very good about it.

"They're not dead," Odile said quietly. She'd explained this before, at the last village, and would explain it again at the next, because explaining was how she kept her hands from shaking. "They're *unwritten*. The words that held them are gone. If the country is restored—if anyone restores it—they'll finish their gesture and never know."

"And if nobody does?" Sera asked, though she knew.

Odile didn't answer. She didn't have to. Everyone in Ironhollow knew what happened to sentences left unfinished.

Pip broke away from Sera and ran to the children at the shrine. Before Jude could shout, she'd planted herself directly in their path, arms out, and stood there being tagged. Nothing touched her. The grey boy's hand passed through her shoulder like smoke through smoke, and Pip laughed—the first sound any of them had made in an hour—and then her face crumpled, and she came back to Sera and pressed her whole small self against the Warden's side.

"He can't see me," she whispered. "He's trying so hard and he can't see me."

Jude's ember flared.

It happened before he decided anything, the way it always did now—a surge of heat behind his sternum, a taste of copper and summer, and light leaking through the seams of him. He felt it go out of him like warmth leaving a cup, felt it spread across the village square, and for one impossible heartbeat the sheet snapped full in the wind, the woman's hands moved, color flooded back into the world like water into cracked earth—

—and then the grey rushed back in, deeper than before, and beyond the village, past the hills, something vast exhaled. Jude felt it as a tug behind his navel. Somewhere out there, another mile of Ironhollow simply stopped.

"Jude." Sera's voice, low and careful. Warden-careful. The voice she used on spooked horses and suspects. "What did you just do?"

"Warmed my hands," he said.

"Don't." She crossed to him, and up close her eyes did that thing they'd done since the night the stars went out—searched him like a locked room she used to have the key to. "Every time you do that, Odile's map gets smaller. I've been watching. Don't tell me you haven't noticed."

He had noticed. That was the rot at the center of him these days: he *had* noticed, three villages ago, when he'd pulled Pip back from a river running upward into blankness and the northern coast had ceased to exist by suppertime. He was spending the country to buy them hours. Every flare of the ember—every act of care, and gods help him, he kept caring—was a withdrawal from an account that belonged to everyone.

"I don't know how to stop," he said. It came out more honest than he'd planned. Honesty kept ambushing him around these people.

"You could start by not standing near danger," Odile offered from the shrine steps, dry as her maps. "Radical strategy. Fleeing things. Very fashionable among survivors."

"I'm serious, Ferrant."

"So am I. You're a mercenary, Pemberton. Your instincts are all wrong for this journey. Your instincts say *protect*, and protection is exactly what's killing us." She tapped the map tube against her palm. "The entity unwinding this place isn't attacking. It's *collecting*. And every time you spend divinity to shield us, you're handing it loose thread."

The word *divinity* sat in the air between them. Nobody had said it plainly until yesterday, and now Odile said it the way she said *river* or *road*, and Jude hated how much steadier it made him feel, being named.

They ate a cold lunch in the shadow of the shrine, facing away from the patient grey people because Pip asked nicely and everyone discovered they'd do most things if Pip asked nicely. Then they walked on, and the afternoon gave them the river.

It had been called the Liss, once. Odile had it on three generations of her family's maps, a fat silver ribbon bending east toward the sea. What they found instead was a river running backward—water climbing out of its own bed in long glassy ropes, rising past the banks, past the hills, up into a sky that wasn't sky anymore but a pale absence with suggestions of clouds, and pouring itself into nothing. It made no sound. That was the worst part. A river should roar. This one ascended in silence, like a congregation filing into church.

"We cross upstream," Odile said, studying the current's impossible direction. "Where it's still behaving. There was a ford at—"

"There was," Sera agreed grimly. "Was is doing a lot of work lately."

They found the ford anyway, or the memory of it—stepping stones half-dissolved, the water there merely confused rather than ascending, swirling in slow doubtful circles. Sera went first with Pip on her back, testing each stone. Odile followed, hugging her maps. Jude came last, because he'd insisted, because if the stones went he wanted to be the one holding the far end of things.

Halfway across, the river remembered what it was becoming.

The confusion left the water all at once. It rose—not violently, nothing so kind—with the smooth inevitability of a tide coming in, and the stones began to soften beneath their feet like sugar in tea. Pip shrieked. Sera turned, hip-deep now in water that was starting to pull *upward*, and thrust out her hand, and Jude was already moving, already burning, the ember roaring up through his ribs and out along his arm—

He caught Sera's wrist. Light poured from the contact, gold-white, and the river *stopped*. Held. A wall of water hung around them, curved and glittering, fish suspended mid-flee, and for four heartbeats they stood inside a bell of miracle while Jude's knees shook and his vision went white at the edges.

Then he let go, and they scrambled the rest of the way, and behind them the river resumed its ascent with a sound like a long sigh, and somewhere north, Jude felt a piece of the country come loose in his chest. He felt it the way you feel a tooth leave. He knew, without asking, without looking at Odile's face, which piece.

"The Saltmarshes," Odile said softly, unrolling the map with hands that weren't quite steady. A whole region of it had faded to the color of nothing. "That was... that was where my mother was born."

Nobody spoke. Pip took Odile's sleeve. Odile let her.

They made camp that night in the lee of a hill that was still fully written, and Jude sat apart from the fire doing arithmetic he couldn't win. Each flare cost miles. Miles meant villages, meant the patient grey people, meant Odile's mother's country dissolving a little faster every time he loved somebody hard enough to burn for them.

Sera sat down beside him without asking. She did that now. Three years of silence between them, and now she treated his silence like furniture she had a right to move.

"Do the math out loud," she said. "Whatever it is. It's worse in your head."

"If I stop spending it, we die on this road. If I keep spending it, the country dies faster, and everyone in it goes unwritten, and we die anyway, just later, having killed more." He stared into the fire. "There's no version where I'm not the disaster, Sera. I'm the leak in the boat bailing with a cup."

"That's not the math."

"It's exactly the math."

"No." She leaned forward, elbows on knees, firelight catching the scar along her jaw—the one he'd given her, the night he betrayed her, though neither of them had said so aloud yet. "The math is: the Door is ahead of us. Whatever's unwinding the country started at the Door, and whatever ends it happens at the Door. Out here you're just bleeding. Get there, and maybe you get to matter." She glanced sideways at him. "Also, you're not a leak. Leaks don't choose. You choose. Every time. That's different, and it's harder, and it's why I'm sitting here instead of arresting you."

"You tried to arrest me once."

"You made me chase you across a rooftop in the rain. You deserved it."

Despite everything—despite the grey hills and the ascending river and the piece of the Saltmarshes gone quiet in his chest—Jude laughed. It startled him, the sound, rough and rusty. Pip's head popped up from her bedroll across the fire like a suspicious owl, assessed the situation, found it acceptable, and lay back down.

"Get some sleep, Pemberton," Sera said, standing. "Tomorrow we walk. And tomorrow—" she paused, and something flickered across her face that wasn't humor at all, "—tomorrow we reach the monastery at Saint Verre. Odile says her family's sealed maps are there. Says they'll explain what you actually are."

"And if I don't want to know?"

"Then you'll hate the next chapter of your life." She said it lightly, but her eyes didn't, and she walked back to the fire, and Jude sat alone a while longer, watching the horizon where the grey met the dark.

Out there, faint as breath on glass, the land was glowing. Not with starlight—there were no stars. With lines. Thin silver seams crisscrossing the hills ahead, converging toward a point beyond Saint Verre, like the country was showing its stitching. Like something enormous was pulling a thread.

And under his sternum, the ember pulsed once—in rhythm, he realized, with the seams. In rhythm, he understood with cold certainty, with the Door.

It knew he was coming. And it was reeling him in.

* * *

He didn't sleep much after that. None of them did, really, though Pip pretended, sprawled dramatically across her bedroll with her boots on, because pretending to sleep was apparently one of the games she played when the world got too big. Around midnight she gave up the pretense and crept over to sit between Jude and Sera, which had become her spot, her declared territory, marked by sheer force of personality.

"Tell me about the stars again," she said.

"You remember them better than I do," Jude said. "You were born after they went out. That's the whole impossible thing about you."

"But I only remember pieces. Like—" she scrunched her face, hunting for it, "—like somebody else's dream stuck in my teeth. Tell me your pieces."

So he told her. About winter constellations over the Ironhollow ranges, the Hunter with his crooked belt. About how sailors swore the stars sang if you listened from the water, though he'd never managed it. About the last night he'd seen them, three years ago, the night his god went silent—he'd been drunk and miserable on a rooftop in Vell's Crossing, and the sky had been so thick with stars it looked like snow falling upward, and he hadn't known to say goodbye.

Pip listened with her whole body, the way children do, and when he finished she nodded solemnly, as if confirming inventory.

"That matches," she said. "Mostly. Except the singing part. They don't sing, silly. They *hum*." And she demonstrated—three low notes, round and warm, and Jude's ember answered before he could stop it, a soft pulse of gold through the blanket over his chest, and above the grey hills, for one second, one single seam of silver light brightened like a vein remembering blood.

Everyone saw it. Nobody spoke. Pip looked at the seam, then at Jude's chest, then at Jude, with an expression far too old for her face.

"Oh," she said quietly. "It's *you* they've been waiting for."

Far off toward Saint Verre, the silver threads tightened, and the night pulled one stitch closer.
