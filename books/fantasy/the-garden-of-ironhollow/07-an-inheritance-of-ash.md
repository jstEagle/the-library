## 7. An Inheritance of Ash

The monastery of Saint Verre had been dead for two hundred years, which made it, in Odile Ferrant's professional opinion, the most honest building left in Ironhollow.

Everything else was pretending. The villages they'd passed were pretending to be alive. The roads pretended to go somewhere. Even the sky had given up the pretense of stars and now hung overhead like a held breath. But Saint Verre did not pretend anything. It stood on its hill with its roof fallen in and its saints faceless with erosion, and it said, plainly: *everything ends.* Odile found that almost comforting.

"Almost," she muttered, hauling her map-case up the last of the stairs. "Comforting is a strong word for a ruin full of pigeon filth."

Behind her, Jude Pemberton climbed with the wary gait of a man who expected stairs to betray him personally. Sera came last, one hand resting between Pip's shoulder blades, steering the child away from the gaps in the balustrade. The smugglers' boat had cost them a day and most of their coin; the walk inland had cost them something harder to name. Twice they had crossed land gone grey at the edges, and twice Pip had hummed that little tuneless song until the grey stopped creeping.

Odile did not let herself think about what that meant. She was a cartographer. She dealt in what could be measured.

The scriptorium still had its great south window, though the glass had long since become a memory and a few stubborn shards. Evening light came through sideways, gold and forgiving, and fell across rows of empty lecterns where monks had once copied out psalms by candlelight. In the corner stood an iron cabinet, black with age, its doors sealed with wax the color of dried blood.

"There," Odile said, and her voice came out smaller than she intended.

"You knew it would be here." Sera set Pip down on a bench, and the child immediately began investigating a dead beetle with the focused intensity of a scholar.

"I hoped." Odile knelt before the cabinet. "My grandmother's grandmother was Verrenne. That's not a common name. When I was expelled from the Royal Cartographic Office—*disgraced*, thank you for asking—they let me keep my family papers because no one else wanted them. Sealed maps. My grandfather called them the shame of our line. He wouldn't say why." She drew out the topmost sheet, heavy vellum gone soft as cloth. "I've carried them for eleven years and never broken the seals. Do you know why?"

"Because you're a coward?" Jude offered.

"Because I was afraid of what I'd find," she said, "and because some part of me already knew. Cartographers always know. We just need the map to admit it first."

She broke the first seal. It crumbled like old bread.

The map beneath was of Ironhollow—but not her Ironhollow. The borders were drawn differently, wider, wilder, and at the center, where every schoolchild learned there was nothing but the King's Reserve and the old royal garden, someone had inked a walled enclosure with obsessive, loving detail. Every hedge. Every path. And at the heart of it, drawn in silver leaf that still gleamed after centuries, a door.

Beneath it, in her ancestor's hand:

*The Garden of Ironhollow. Here the Gardener keeps the Door. Here the god sleeps in his tree. Let no Pemberton forget.*

"Pemberton," Sera read aloud, slowly.

The silence in the scriptorium had weight. Odile looked up and found Jude very still, the way he went still when he was deciding whether to run or fight. She had known him three weeks and she had catalogued all his stillnesses the way she catalogued coastlines.

"That's a coincidence," he said.

"It's never a coincidence," Odile said. "Coincidences are what we call patterns we haven't finished mapping." She broke the second seal. This map showed the garden alone, vast, filling half the country's heart, annotated in a cramped, furious hand: *the Door must be tended. The Ward must be fed. The line of Gardeners does not choose; it is chosen for.* And then, smaller, as if written late at night by someone who had stopped believing their own duty: *God forgive us for what we agreed to.*

"The Pembertons weren't farmers," Odile said gently. "They were gardeners. Capital G. Keepers of the Door at the heart of the country. Your family didn't serve the god, Jude. They served the *spell*. They kept the cage locked from the inside so the thing outside couldn't get in—or so nothing inside could ever leave."

"My family sold charms," Jude said. His voice was flat. "My mother sold charms. Bad ones. My father hauled freight. Nobody gardened anything except a hatred of mornings."

"And yet." Odile spread the third map, and this one she had to unfold twice, so large it covered four lecterns pushed together. It was a genealogy—not of people, though people were on it, but of inheritance. A single unbroken line running down through centuries of Pembertons, each name connected by a thread of silver, and at the end of the thread, the ink still wet-looking after all this time, a final name.

Jude Pemberton.

Below it, in letters that seemed to have been pressed hard enough to scar the vellum: *THE LAST WARD. THE LIVING KEY.*

"No," Jude said.

"Jude—"

"*No.*" He stepped back from the table as if the maps were fire. "You don't get to do this. You don't get to dig through your family's guilt and hand me mine. I didn't ask for this. I didn't ask for any of it—the ember, the silence, the—" He gestured at everything, at the ruined monastery, the dying country, the child humming softly over her beetle. "Three years my god said nothing to me. Three years I thought I'd failed, that I'd done something wrong, and the whole time he was *dead*, and I was his—his *spare key*? His leftover lock?"

"Yes," Odile said, because lying to him would have been crueller.

"He chose me before I was born. Before my mother was born. Some monk or priest or *gardener* sat at this table two hundred years ago and decided that a man who didn't exist yet would die for a country that isn't even real." His laugh came out cracked. "It's not even real, Odile. You told us yourself. It's a spell wearing a country's shape. He wants me to die for a *lie*."

"He wants you to tend a door," Odile said. "There's a difference. Doors are honest. They only do what hands make them do."

"Well, my hands are closed."

Pip had come over at some point during the shouting—children always drifted toward shouting the way moths drift toward lamps—and now she stood beside the great genealogy map, frowning at it with her whole face. She reached out one small finger and touched the silver thread above Jude's name.

"This is the song," she said.

Everyone turned.

"What song?" Sera asked quietly.

"The one I hum." Pip traced the thread from name to name, up and up, generations of it. "It goes all the way back. It's the same all the way back. It's just—" she scrunched her nose, searching for the word— "*tired* at the bottom. At his name it's tired. Like it walked a really long way."

Jude stared at her. Something in his fury hit a wall and cracked, and behind the crack Odile saw the thing he actually was: not a mercenary, not a ward, but a tired man who had been walking a very long way himself.

"I didn't agree to this," he said again, but softer, and the argument had changed shape without anyone noticing. It was no longer *this isn't true.* It was *this isn't fair,* and unfair things, unlike untrue ones, can be survived.

"No," Odile agreed. She began rolling the maps with careful hands. "Neither did your grandmother, probably. Neither did hers. That's the trouble with inheritances—you get the debt without the signature. Believe me, I know. My family spent six generations making these maps and hiding them, too ashamed to look at what they'd done and too loyal to destroy it. Cowardice, handed down like china." She tied the roll with cord. "But here is what I've learned about maps, Jude, and it took losing everything to learn it: a map is not a sentence. It's a description. It tells you where things *are*. It doesn't tell you where they must *go*."

He looked at her. "The map says I'm the key."

"The map says you're the key," she agreed. "Maps don't say what keys are for. Locks open. That's all a key does—it opens. Whoever told your family that opening meant dying was reading the map wrong. Or wanted it read wrong."

Outside, the light was failing, and failing strangely—the way it had done since the stars went out, dusk arriving like a lid closing rather than a sun setting. Sera lit a stub of candle and set it on the lectern, and the flame bent toward Jude, just slightly, the way flames had begun bending toward him lately. Toward the ember.

Pip yawned enormously, leaned against Sera, and murmured, half-asleep: "The song says the garden misses him. The tree part. It says come home, but it also says—" another yawn— "it says *careful*."

"Careful of what?" Sera asked.

But Pip was asleep.

They made camp in the scriptorium, because the alternative was the grey country outside, and grey country at night was not a thing any of them suggested aloud. Odile sat by the window with her maps across her knees, checking and rechecking the route to the garden's heart, and watched Jude sit apart with his elbows on his knees, staring into the dark where the garden lay.

She got up once, near midnight, to fetch water, and paused beside him.

"For what it's worth," she said, "I raged too. When I understood what my family had sealed away. Eleven years of rage. It's a fine fuel, rage. Burns hot and clean and gets you absolutely nowhere new." She settled onto the step beside him. "Then one morning I woke up and realized the maps hadn't changed while I hated them. They were waiting. They'll wait forever. Maps are patient. The question was never whether I'd accept them. The question was what I'd draw next."

"I'm not a mapmaker," Jude said.

"No. You're worse. You're a *territory*." She smiled at his expression. "Get some sleep, Master Pemberton. Tomorrow we cross the King's Reserve, and if my ancestors' ink is true, tomorrow night we reach the wall."

He didn't answer. But when she rose, he said, quietly, to the darkness more than to her: "If I open it—if I'm the key and I open it—what comes out?"

And Odile, who had spent eleven years studying the blank places at the edges of her maps, who had watched roads dissolve into grey nothing, who knew better than anyone alive what waited outside the world, gave him the only honest answer a cartographer could give.

"Nothing," she said. "That's the problem. Nothing comes out. Nothing has been knocking for three hundred years."

Above the ruined monastery, where stars should have been, the absence seemed to lean closer, listening.

And far off, past the sleeping hills, past the frozen villages, at the iron heart of the country, something answered the knock from the inside—and opened.
