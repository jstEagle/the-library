## 8. The Garden Where God Died

The last road to the heart of Ironhollow was not a road at all. It was a seam.

Jude felt it under his boots before he saw it—a line in the earth where the grey stopped pretending to be grass, where the unwritten country ended and something older began. Beyond the seam, the land was still itself: brown hills, bent pines, a stream arguing its way downhill like water was supposed to. Ordinary ground. After six days of villages frozen mid-prayer and rivers running upward into nothing, ordinary ground made his eyes sting.

"Is that it?" Pip asked, pointing with her whole arm, the way children point, as if her finger alone couldn't carry the weight of the question.

Ahead of them, rising out of a shallow valley, stood a wall of iron.

It wasn't built so much as grown—great black ribs of metal curving up from the soil like the skeleton of some enormous patient animal, taller than any cathedral spire Jude had ever robbed or defended. Rust-orange lichen crawled across it in continents. Vines had threaded themselves through the gaps over centuries, green insisting on the iron the way moss insists on a tombstone. And set into the wall, maybe forty feet up, hung a door.

The Door.

Even from here, even at this distance, Jude's chest hurt looking at it. The ember he'd carried for three years—the god's last breath banked down inside him like a coal wrapped in wet cloth—had begun to glow through his ribs since they'd crossed the seam. He could feel it pressing outward, toward that wall, the way a dog presses toward a door it knows.

"It's open," Odile said quietly.

She had her maps spread on the ground between them, weighted with stones, though she hadn't looked at them in an hour. She didn't need to anymore. The cartographer who had spent thirty years drawing a country that lied to itself now stood at the place where all her maps converged, and her hands were shaking.

"The Door is open," she said again, as if repetition might make it less true. "In every account, in every sealed record my family kept—it was never supposed to be open. That was the entire point of everything. The gardeners. The wards. The Pembertons." She glanced at Jude, apologetic and fierce at once. "You."

"Well," Jude said, "I've been meaning to have a word with whoever left it unlocked."

Nobody laughed. He hadn't really expected them to. But Sera's mouth did that thing it did when she was swallowing a smile for professional reasons, and that was almost enough.

They walked down into the valley as the light went long and gold. There was no gate in the iron wall—or rather, there were many, all of them fused shut, seams of metal melted together like candlewax, except one. A postern door near the base, human-sized, standing ajar. Through the gap Jude could see a path of white gravel winding away into green shade.

Sera drew her sword anyway. Old habits. New world.

"You don't need that," Jude said.

"I know."

"I mean it. Whatever's in there, a sword is going to be about as useful as strong opinions."

"I know," Sera said again, and sheathed it, and put her hand briefly on his shoulder instead—which, from Sera Ilvane, was roughly equivalent to a sonnet.

Pip took his hand without being asked. She'd been doing that more lately, reaching for him in the way of someone who has decided, unilaterally and permanently, that this person is hers now. Jude had stopped flinching from it somewhere around day four. He wasn't sure when exactly. He suspected it was around the time she'd hummed three notes of a song no living person knew, and the ember in his chest had answered like a struck bell.

They went through.

* * *

The garden inside was dying beautifully.

That was the only way Jude could describe it. Rows of fruit trees heavy with black glass fruit that caught the sunset and held it, glowing faintly amber, like lanterns. Hedges trimmed by no living hand into shapes too precise for nature. A fountain at the center that no longer flowed but still somehow sounded like water—you could hear it, clear and cool, if you listened, though the basin held only dust.

And at the far end of the garden, where the paths all led whether you wanted them to or not, stood the tree.

It had been a god once. Jude understood that now not as information but as sensation, the way you understand a wound. The tree was vast—its trunk wider than the Wardenhall, its branches spreading over half the garden—and it was made entirely of black glass, smooth and seamless, shot through with veins of dim gold light that pulsed slowly, slowly, like something breathing at the bottom of a deep sleep. Or trying to.

Like something almost finished breathing.

"That's him," Jude said. His voice came out strange. "That's what's left."

He had known, in the abstract, since Odile's maps. Since Saint Verre. My family tended this, the records said; your family died keeping it. But knowing a thing and standing beneath the corpse of the god who had spoken to you every day of your life until three years ago—until the morning you woke up and the warmth behind your sternum had gone quiet as a shut door—that was different. That was kneeling-in-the-dirt different.

"He's beautiful," Pip said simply.

"Yes," Jude said. "He is."

The ember in his chest flared, and for one impossible moment Jude felt the tree feel him back. Recognition. Not words—he'd gotten no words in three years—but something older than words. The sensation of a hand resting on the top of your head. *There you are. I wondered which of them you'd turn out to be.*

Then the light in the glass-veins guttered, and the feeling passed, and Jude was left standing in the dusk with wet eyes and an ache in his throat he refused to examine in company.

"We should camp," Sera said gently. "Whatever happens next, we shouldn't do it exhausted."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the wall, under a glass tree whose fruit glowed soft enough to read by. Odile sketched by firelight—not maps anymore, but the tree, over and over, as if her hands needed to understand it. Sera cleaned a sword she wouldn't use. Pip fell asleep against Jude's side halfway through a story about a smuggler's cat named Admiral, mid-sentence, trusting as gravity.

Jude sat awake.

Which was why he heard the fountain start flowing.

He looked up. Across the dark garden, the dry fountain was running—water silvering down through moonlight that shouldn't exist, because there was no moon anymore, because the stars were gone, because the sky above Ironhollow was a vast watching absence. But light fell on the fountain anyway, sourceless and pale, and a figure stood beside the basin with its sleeves rolled up like a gardener washing off the day.

It looked like a man. That was the first lie, and Jude caught it immediately, the way you catch a counterfeit coin—not by what's wrong with it, but by how *eager* it is to be right. Too symmetrical. Its shadow fell the wrong direction. When it turned its head, the movement had a smoothness no joints could manage, like a reflection deciding to move independently of the face.

"Jude Pemberton," it said. Its voice was warm. That was the second lie. "You came. I confess I hoped you would, but hope is such a vulgar word for what I felt. Certainty, perhaps."

"What are you?" Jude asked. He kept his voice low. Behind him, Sera had gone still in the way that meant she was awake and armed and choosing not to be either yet.

"I'm the one who was here first." The thing smiled. "Before your god planted his little hedge-maze of a country. Before he gathered up the frightened and the faithful and walled them in with borrowed starlight. I am what's outside, Jude. I am what the sky used to hold, back when the sky was honest. And I am—" it gestured gracefully at the black glass tree, at the dimming gold veins, "—hungry."

"You killed him."

"I *waited*," the thing corrected, with the mild patience of a teacher. "There's a difference. Your god spent himself holding a door shut against me for a thousand years, and doors only hold while there's someone willing to stand behind them. He grew tired. They always grow tired. And then—" it spread its hands, "—I opened it myself. Three years ago. You may remember the evening. Something in your chest went quiet?"

Three years ago. The Silence. Jude had built his whole ruined life in the shape of that absence, sold hollow charms in its shadow, hated himself for surviving it. And it hadn't been abandonment. It hadn't been death, even. It had been a siege ending.

"You've been eating the country ever since," Jude said. "The grey. The unwritten places."

"I've been *tasting*," the thing said. "The meal comes later. Or—" and here its voice changed, softened, became something horribly gentle, "—it doesn't come at all. For you."

It crossed the garden in three steps that covered thirty feet and stopped just outside the firelight, close enough that Jude could see it had no face, only a smooth suggestion of one, features arriving as needed.

"Here is what I offer, heir of the gardeners. Walk away. Take the child, the soldier, the mapmaker—take them all, I'll allow it, I'm generous with small things. There are other countries. Real ones, beyond the walls of this spell, with real stars overhead and no god bleeding out in the middle of them. You could live there. Grow old. Sell bread, if you like—I understand you've developed a taste for honest work." It tilted its suggestion of a head. "All you must do is forget. This place, this duty, the ember in your chest. Leave it here with the rest of the corpse, and walk out into a life that costs nothing."

And oh, it was sweet. That was the horror of it—how sweet. Jude felt the offer settle over him like a blanket after years of cold. No more running from Wardens. No more guilt with every flare of the ember, every mile of country burned away to keep his people alive. No more carrying a dead god's last breath like a debt he'd never agreed to owe. Just bread. Just Pip's laughter. Just Sera's rare, crooked smile across a market stall in some sunlit city that had never heard of Ironhollow. Forget. Live. Be only a man, finally, instead of a key.

He wanted it so badly his hands shook.

"You're crying," the thing observed, kindly.

"I'm not," said Jude, who was.

And then a small hand slid into his.

Pip stood beside him in her nightshirt, hair sleep-tangled, having crossed the dark garden barefoot without him hearing. She was looking up at the faceless thing with the unflinching directness of the very young, and her grip on Jude's fingers was firm and warm and utterly without doubt.

"She can't come with you if you forget," the entity said. "Forgetting isn't transferable."

"I know," Jude said.

His voice came out steady. That surprised him. Somewhere in the last six days—somewhere between a frozen village and a burning archive and a child humming a dead god's name—his voice had learned to be steady.

"But here's the thing," Jude said, and the ember in his chest rose like a sunrise, gold light spilling between his ribs, lighting the garden, lighting the black glass tree until its dead veins flickered, "you got one detail wrong. You think he spent himself holding this door because it was his duty. His burden. His thousand-year sentence." He squeezed Pip's hand. "But I've been a mercenary. I know hired work when I see it, and that wasn't it. He *loved* this. Every year of it. That's why it lasted so long."

The thing's face smoothed utterly blank.

"So no," Jude said. "I'm not walking away. And I'm not forgetting. And I'm not doing whatever it is you're hoping I'll do next, either—because I've been thinking about wards, and I don't think a ward is a sacrifice. I think a ward is a choice, made fresh, by as many hands as are willing."

Behind him, steel whispered from a scabbard. Pages rustled. Small bare feet shifted in the gravel, planting themselves.

The entity looked at the four of them—at the disgraced cartographer, the oath-breaker, the child, the heir—and for the first time, something in its perfect stillness *hesitated*.

"Oh," it said softly. "Oh, you're going to try *that*."

The gold light in the black glass tree flared—and held.
