## 9. The Last Ward

**Jude**

The Door was open, and it had been waiting for him the way a noose waits for a neck.

Jude stood at the edge of the iron-walled garden, the black glass tree looming behind him like a wound that had learned to grow, and looked into the thing that had silenced his god. It did not have a face. It had something better than a face: it had an offer.

*Walk away,* it said, in a voice like the space between stars. *Leave through me. Live. Forget Ironhollow ever held you. Forget the silence. Forget her.* A pause, delicate as a knife being sheathed. *Forget Sera.*

"That last part's a bit rude," Jude said, because his mouth had always been faster than his sense of self-preservation.

The Door rippled. Somewhere beyond it, he could feel the shape of elsewhere — green country, real rain, a sky that had never heard of wards. All he had to do was step through and be a man instead of a vessel. All he had to do was stop being the last thing his god ever made.

Three years ago, that offer would have cost nothing. Three years ago he'd been selling hollow charms to frightened people and calling it survival.

But Pip's hand found his. Small. Sticky. Absolutely certain.

"You're not going," she said. Not a question. Children born after the Silence didn't ask questions about things they already knew.

"I'm not going," Jude agreed, and felt the ember in his chest flare so bright it hurt, and understood — finally, stupidly, mercifully — what he was for.

He stepped forward, away from the Door, toward the tree, and said the words he'd been carrying since Vell's Crossing without knowing their weight:

"Then I'll be the ward. Take me instead. I'm willing."

* * *

**Sera**

"No," Sera said.

She said it before she'd decided to say it, which was how most important things left her mouth. She crossed the garden's black grass — grass that chimed faintly underfoot, like the whole place was holding its breath — and planted herself between Jude and the tree.

"You don't get to do this alone. That's not how oaths work. That's not how *anything* works."

"Sera." He had the decency to look pained. "It has to be one person. That's the whole architecture. One ward, one hinge, one—"

"One *martyr*," she snapped. "You've been rehearsing this since the border fell. Don't think I haven't noticed. You walk like a man reading his own eulogy and hoping nobody checks the spelling."

Odile laughed from where she stood among the roots, maps spread across her knees despite there being nothing left to map. "She's right. And worse — she's quoting doctrine at you, which is my job." The old cartographer pushed herself up, joints crackling like green wood in fire. "Listen to me, boy. I've spent forty years drawing borders, and I'll tell you the one truth of them: a wall built by one hand falls when that hand tires. Your god knew that. Why do you think He died *surrounded*? Gardeners, keepers, saints, fools — He gathered people the way other gods gather worship. Because a ward isn't a sacrifice. A ward is a *promise kept by many hands*."

The Door stirred at the edge of hearing, and its voice slid into the garden like cold water: *One is simpler.*

"Everything cruel is simpler," Sera said flatly.

And then she understood it — all at once, the way Wardenhall's archives had burned, the way understanding arrives too late or exactly on time. She turned to Odile. "The oath. When I broke mine, it should have killed me. The law said so. But the law died with Him."

"Yes," Odile breathed.

"So the Door still thinks in the old grammar. It wants a ward bound by oath, by offering, by—" She stopped. Looked at her own hands. Hands that had burned mages. Hands that had refused to burn a child. "It wants someone *willing*. Not one someone. Just... willingness. As much as it can hold."

Jude stared at her. "You're suggesting we hot-wire a divine seal."

"I'm suggesting," Sera said, "that for once in your insufferable life, you let people help you."

* * *

**Pip**

Pip knew what she was carrying. She'd always known, the way you know a song you can't remember learning.

It lived behind her ribs and it hummed, and sometimes at night it hummed loud enough that the smugglers had asked her to stop singing in her sleep. It was starlight. It was the name of somebody enormous who had loved the world enough to spend Himself on it. And now the enormous somebody's last breath was standing in front of her wearing Jude's face and looking terrified, which was funny, because Jude was the biggest thing she knew.

Except he wasn't going to be big if he did this alone. Grown-ups were like that. They carried everything until they broke, and then everyone acted surprised.

So Pip walked to the black glass tree, put both palms against it — it was warm, warmer than anything in the grey country, warm like bread, like Jude's stall had been — and she opened her ribs the way you open a window.

"Here," she said. "I saved some. In case."

The starlight came out of her gently, not torn but *given*, streaming up through the glass branches, and the whole garden lit like dawn remembering itself. Pip felt lighter and sadder and taller all at once. Somewhere inside the light, very faintly, something enormous said *thank you*, in the voice of a man who had once sold charms in a city that outlawed magic.

"That's the offering," Odile whispered. "That's the first hand."

Pip turned back to Jude and held out her hand again, because grown-ups needed instructions even at the end of the world.

* * *

**Sera**

Sera took the hinge.

That was the only word for it. When she stepped to the Door's edge and pressed her broken oath against it — spoke aloud every vow she'd sworn at nineteen and every vow she'd unsworn in the burning of Wardenhall — the Door shuddered, because a broken oath was still an oath, still *weight*, and weight was what hinges were made of.

"I held this order together with my obedience," she told it, and her voice didn't shake, which surprised her. "I held Jude at arm's length with my duty. I held the law over a child with my fear. I am done holding things shut." She set her shoulder against the Door's frame as though it were a door in a farmhouse, as though it were ordinary, as though stubbornness could outlast divinity. "So I'll hold *this*. Not to keep it closed. To keep it *honest* while it opens."

The Door screamed — a soundless scream, pressure without sound, the whole sky leaning in — and Sera held.

Odile was beside her then, maps abandoned, one weathered hand flat against the frame next to hers. "Cartographer's privilege," the old woman said wryly. "I get to decide what stays on the map. And I'm striking the cage."

Four hands now. Four wills. The Door, built for the arithmetic of one, began to come apart under the arithmetic of many — and it was, Sera thought wildly, almost funny, how the great devouring entity that ate gods could be undone by the thing it never bothered to learn: that people are unbearable in groups.

* * *

**Jude**

Jude placed his hand over Pip's starlight in the glass branches and did the opposite of everything the ember in his chest begged him to do.

He didn't pour himself in. He didn't become the ward. He *unmade* the wanting — reached into the Door's architecture with three years of silence and one night of family, and simply... let go. Of the seal. Of the spell. Of the beautiful lie that a country could be held together by one dying god's last breath forever, that anything could.

Ironhollow ended the way a held note ends: not destroyed, but released.

The grey country sighed. The frozen villages finished their gestures. The rivers remembered downhill. And above them — above everything — the sky, empty for so long it had become a kind of scream, began very quietly to practice.

One star. Then two. Then a scatter, tentative, like seeds thrown by an uncertain hand.

"It's not saving us," Jude said, watching. His voice cracked and he let it. "It's just... stopping pretending. Everything after this is real. The good and the bad. We don't get the spell anymore."

"Good," said Pip, leaning against his side, heavy and warm and alive.

"Yeah," Jude said. "Good."

Behind them the Door came apart like frost in sunlight, and Sera caught him as his knees gave, and Odile was laughing — actually laughing, the old fraud — and somewhere in the new dark that wasn't absence but merely *night*, honest and ordinary and theirs, the first true dawn in three years began deciding whether to come up.

It did.

And far off, at the edge of the ruined garden, in soil that had never been anything but soil, Jude saw something impossible and small: a seed from the glass tree, fallen and split, putting out one green leaf.

"Well," he said. "Someone's got to plant the rest of those."

Sera squeezed his hand. "We will."

*We.* He'd spent three years forgetting the word existed. It fit like a coat he'd outgrown and grown back into.

They walked out of the garden together, four silhouettes under a sky relearning its oldest language — and behind them, in honest dirt, the second seed was already waking.
