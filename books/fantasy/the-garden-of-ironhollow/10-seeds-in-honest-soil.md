## 10. Seeds in Honest Soil

The first star came back on a Tuesday, and Odile cried about it for three days.

Not the loud kind of crying—Odile Ferrant did nothing loudly, except argue. It was the quiet kind, where she'd stop mid-sentence while sketching the new coastline and just stare up through the gap in the ruined roof until her ink dried in the well. Then she'd wipe her eyes with the heel of her hand, mutter something about *dust*, and keep drawing a world that was finally holding still long enough to be drawn.

Jude understood. He'd done his own version of it, that first morning after the Door came down, when he'd walked out of the garden's broken iron wall into grey ash that was already—already—going green at the edges, and had to sit down on a dead god's root because his legs simply declined to hold him.

Three years of silence. And then, in one night, an ending so complete that even the silence had ended.

* * *

They planted the seeds in spring, or what passed for spring now, which was a season still learning its own name.

The glass-tree had shattered when the Door unmade itself—when Jude put his hands against it and Sera held the hinge with her broken oath and Pip gave back everything she'd carried—and what remained was a drift of black shards across the garden floor, thin as fingernails, each one holding a faint warmth like a stone left too near a fire. Seeds, Odile called them. She'd mapped their scatter pattern twice, cross-referenced it against her family's sealed maps of Saint Verre, and announced with the particular satisfaction she reserved for being right about terrible things: "They want soil. Real soil. Not spellwork. Not wards."

"Then we'd better find some," Sera said.

Finding real soil in a country that had spent four hundred years being a lie turned out to be harder than killing a god's murderer, which at least had the courtesy of being a single task. They walked for weeks through land that was busy becoming itself again—rivers running the correct direction now, mostly, though one outside Vell's Crossing still went uphill on foggy mornings out of what Jude could only assume was stubbornness—and everywhere they found people doing the same slow work. Farmers testing whether wheat would grow without the god's breath under it. (It would. Slower. Uglier. Better.) A Warden remnant in the north who had traded their white cloaks for shovels and were rebuilding a bridge they'd once burned, on the theory, as their captain put it, that they owed the road an apology.

Pip rode most of the way on Sera's shoulders and asked approximately nine thousand questions per mile.

"Why does dirt smell different now?"

"Because it's honest dirt," Jude said.

"Honest how?"

"It doesn't have to pretend to be anything."

Pip considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "I liked pretending better when pretending was all there was."

"You did not," Sera said. "You cried for a month about the stars."

"I cried because they *left*," Pip said, with the devastating precision of children. "Now they're coming back one at a time like they're sorry. That's different."

Jude had no answer for that, so he gave her a dried apricot, which was the currency of their household and settled most disputes.

* * *

The place they chose was a hillside above a river that ran downhill in every weather, two days' walk from what remained of Vell's Crossing. The old city still stood, half of it, and people still lived there—but nobody called it outlaw anymore, because there was no Crown left to outlaw anything. The Wardens' law had died with the god who wrote it, and whatever grew in its place was going to be written by hand, slowly, by people who kept getting it wrong and starting over.

The hillside had real earth under the ash—dark, dense, smelling of worms and iron and rain. Jude dug the first hole himself, with a spade, the ordinary mortal kind, and dropped in a single shard of black glass.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. That was rather the point.

But when he covered it over and pressed his palm flat against the soil, he felt—not the ember, not divinity, not the vast pressure of a god's last breath. Just warmth. The same warmth the shard had carried. Faint, patient, willing to wait.

"He's not coming back," Jude said quietly. He hadn't meant to say it aloud.

"No," Odile agreed, crouched beside him with her maps spread out on a board despite the fact that the ground no longer moved. "Dead is dead. We checked very thoroughly." She tapped the soil. "This isn't him. This is what he *planted*. There's a difference, and I've made a career of knowing the difference between a thing and its map."

Sera stood behind them with her arms crossed, watching the sky, where evening was assembling itself out of honest colors. Three stars were already visible. By summer, Odile predicted, they'd have constellations again—wrong ones, new ones, ones nobody had names for yet.

"We'll need names," Pip said, appearing between them like she'd been listening from inside Jude's coat pocket, which was not entirely impossible; she had a gift for small spaces and smaller eavesdroppings.

"For what?"

"The stars. Somebody has to name them or they'll just hang up there being embarrassed."

"You name them," Jude said. "You're the only one who remembers what light looked like before."

Pip beamed and immediately named the brightest one *Bread*, which everyone agreed was a tragedy of nomenclature and which stuck permanently.

* * *

The stall went up in autumn.

It was Odile's idea, delivered in the tone she used for ideas she wanted credit for but not responsibility: "You were good at selling things. Sell something that exists."

So Jude built a stall—rough pine, slightly crooked, one leg shimmed with a hymnal from Saint Verre that nobody had the heart to burn—at the crossroads below the hillside, where the road from Vell's Crossing met the river ferry. He sold bread. That was all. Dark rye, mostly, and a morning loaf with honey that Pip had strong opinions about, and on festival days a seed-cake that Sera claimed not to like and ate four pieces of.

No charms. No wards. No whispered promises that a scrap of blessed cloth could hold back the dark. The dark, these days, was mostly just night, and night turned out to be survivable if you had a lamp and someone to sit with.

People came anyway. That was the part that undid him, some mornings—that they came. A woman whose brother had been burned in the purges bought bread every week and never spoke, and Jude never asked, and one day she touched the stall's crooked post and said, "My mother used to buy charms off a man with your hands," and then bought her loaf and left, and Jude had to sit down among the flour sacks for a while.

He wasn't sorry, exactly. He'd sold hollow charms to frightened people for three years, and he wasn't sorry, because frightened people needed *something* to hold and he'd been too much of a coward to give them truth instead. But he was tired in a way that had nothing to do with walking. So he sold bread. Bread didn't promise anything except that you wouldn't be hungry for a few hours, and it always kept its word.

Sera worked the ferry landing most days, organizing the crossing, arguing with smugglers who were discovering that honest trade paid worse but let you sleep. Her oath-breaking had killed nothing in her after all—or rather, it had killed the thing that deserved to die, and left the rest standing, and she was still learning what the rest was. Some evenings she sat on the stall's counter with her boots swinging like a girl's and told him about the new council forming in Vell's Crossing, the arguments, the compromises, the letter they wanted her to write about Warden reform.

"They think because I broke my oaths I understand freedom," she said.

"Do you?"

"I understand that it's heavy," she said. "That's most of it."

* * *

Winter came, the first winter with no wards holding the cold politely at bay, and it was bitter, and the roof leaked, and Pip got a cough that scared them all far more than the end of the world ever had. But the house held—it was a real house, timber and stone, built by their own hands on the hillside above the planted field—and Sera's broth and Odile's grim medical certainty saw the child through, and on the worst night, when Pip's fever climbed, Jude sat by the bed and did the only magic left in him, which was none, and stayed anyway.

Staying, he was learning, was most of what power had ever been.

In the field below, under snow, forty-one shards of black glass waited in honest soil. In the spring, the first shoot came up: a stem like smoked crystal, unfurling leaves that caught the light and threw it back in colors that had no names yet either. Not a god. Not a ward. Something else. Something that had never existed before and owed nothing to what came before it.

Odile measured it weekly. Sera pretended not to be moved by it and failed completely. Pip named it *Steve*, which was somehow worse than Bread.

* * *

On an evening in late spring, with the sky going violet and five new stars pricking through where the old heavens used to be, Pip climbed onto the counter of the stall while Jude swept flour into the air and Sera argued cheerfully with the ferryman about tariffs.

She was taller. That was the thing that got him, sometimes—she was growing, which meant time was passing, which meant the world was continuing, which was the entire miracle in miniature.

She watched him work for a while, chin on her fists, the way she did when she was building up to something.

"Jude."

"Mm."

"Are we going to be all right?"

He set down the broom. Outside, the river ran downhill. The bread smelled like bread. Somewhere up the hill, Odile was swearing at a map that had finally, blessedly, stopped moving, and Sera's laugh carried over the water, and the tree called Steve stood in the field throwing light that belonged to no god and no crown and no spell at all.

He thought about lying, because lying had been his trade for years and he'd been good at it. He thought about the honest answer, which was *nobody knows, the country is unwritten, we are all making it up as we go.*

He thought about the loaf cooling on the shelf, which had promised nothing and would deliver.

"Yeah," Jude said. "We're going to be all right."

And for the first time in his life—in any of his lives, vessel or mercenary or gardener's heir—he meant it.

Pip studied his face for a long moment, checking, the way children check, for the seam where the lie would show. Finding none, she nodded once, satisfied, and hopped down to go harass Sera about dinner.

Jude picked up the broom. Above the crossroads, the star Pip had named Bread rose over the honest hills, and the evening smelled of woodsmoke and rising dough, and the world—unwritten, unfinished, entirely real—went on.
