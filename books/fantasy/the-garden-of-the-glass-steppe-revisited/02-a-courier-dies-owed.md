## 2. A Courier Dies Owed

The snow came to Corveth in the way debts came: quietly at first, then all at once, then without any intention of leaving. Ivo Zhou noticed it the way they noticed everything—sideways, while doing something else. They were balancing a column of figures for the third quarter's tallow futures when the light through the counting house windows went the color of old pewter, and by the time they looked up properly, the world outside had been erased and rewritten in white.

*Erased,* Ivo thought, and did not know why the word snagged on something behind their ribs like a fishhook.

They let it go. That was the discipline of the Ledger Guild: you let things go that did not balance. You entered what was owed and what was paid, and you did not enter the difference between them that lived in your chest at night.

The counting house on Candlemaker's Row was quiet in the way only guild buildings were quiet—not empty but *held*, every sound accounted for. The scratch of forty pens. The tick of the great regulator clock over the assay room, which kept time not in hours but in interest accrued. Somewhere below, in the vaults, the bottled grief of six hundred households sat shelved in the dark, each vial labeled with a name and a sum, waiting to be sold back to its owners at rates they could never afford. Ivo had stopped letting themselves think about the vaults years ago. Thinking about the vaults was how clerks went strange.

"Zhou." Maren's voice from the senior desk, dry as ledger paper. "You're bleeding ink."

Ivo looked down. A blot had spread beneath their pen like a small black wound. "Sorry," they said, and blotted it, and corrected the figure, and for one absurd moment their hand hesitated over the page as though it wanted to write something else entirely—a word with too many letters, a name with no entry code. Then the moment passed, as moments did, and Ivo was competent again, and invisible again, and safe.

That was the arrangement Ivo had made with the world. Competence for invisibility. They were good—better than good; the auditors said Ivo Zhou could smell an error through three layers of forgery—and being good meant being left alone, and being left alone was the closest thing to happiness the guild sold.

It was nearly the closing bell when the courier came.

Ivo heard him before anyone else did, because Ivo heard everything: not footsteps but the *absence* of them, a wrongness in the rhythm of the porter's rounds, and then a weight against the outer door, a soft terrible thud like a body deciding the floor was owed to it.

The door swung in on a gust that scattered loose receipts across the entry hall like startled birds. Snow came in with it—real snow, whirling—and in the middle of the whirl stood a man who had been outdoors far longer than any living thing should be. His coat was courier's gray, guild-adjacent but not guild-issue, crusted with ice along the seams so that he seemed to have been sewn into winter itself. His lips were the blue of ink left out overnight. Frost had grown into his beard in little crystalline ferns, beautiful and obscene.

He looked at the forty clerks bent over their forty desks, and his eyes—Ivo would remember this, would carry this, the way one carries a splinter too deep to dig—his eyes went past all of them as if they were furniture, as if they were *entries*, and found Ivo.

Found Ivo the way a creditor finds a debtor.

"No," Ivo said, under their breath, to no one, to the universe. *Not me. I'm no one. I'm the one who isn't seen.*

But the man was already moving, lurching down the aisle between the desks, shedding meltwater, and the nearest clerks drew back with the particular disgust of the comfortable confronted by the uncomfortable. Brother Anselm wasn't there yet—that came later—but old Pellam rose from his stool saying something about protocol, and the man pushed past him, and fell.

He didn't fall so much as *arrive*, kneeling, then folding sideways, and Ivo was somehow already there—Ivo, who never moved first, who moved only when the ledgers required it—was kneeling in the slush of him, catching his shoulders before his skull could meet the marble.

His skin burned cold. Not cold the way stone is cold; cold the way a brand is hot. It bit through Ivo's sleeves and made their teeth ache.

"Fetch the house physician," someone said, uselessly, distantly.

The man's hand came up. It took both of Ivo's hands to hold it, it shook so badly, and into them he pressed a thing wrapped in oilcloth and tied with cord gone stiff as wire. Small. Flat. Heavy in the way that meaning is heavy, not the way lead is.

"For the debt," he whispered. His breath smelled of iron and pine smoke. "It's all—the whole country owes—it has to be *entered*, do you understand, it can't just—it has to be written down or it never—"

"I don't understand," Ivo said. "Who are you? What debt?"

His eyes focused on Ivo then, truly focused, and there was such relief in them, such awful gratitude, that Ivo felt it like a slap. He had been carrying this thing across a country that did not exist, through winters that should have killed him ten times over, and now he could put it down, and the person he put it down on was Ivo Zhou, who had spent thirty-one years ensuring that no one would ever put anything down on them.

"You'll know," he said. "When you read it. You'll—you knew before. Everyone from there knew before." A bubble of blood appeared at the corner of his mouth, bright against the frost-blue. "Vessany."

The word went through Ivo like a needle finding the one nerve they didn't know they had.

*I've never heard that word.*

And underneath the thought, quieter, older, in a child's voice speaking a language Ivo did not know they knew: *you have, you have, you have.*

"Say it again," Ivo whispered.

But the courier's eyes had gone past them, up, to the high dark windows where the snow swirled beyond the glass, and whatever he saw there made him smile—a small, cracked, terrible smile, the smile of a man settling accounts. "She's still falling," he said. "All this time. She's still—" And then the breath went out of him and did not come back, and his hand relaxed around nothing, and the counting house of the Ledger Guild held its newest silence.

* * *

They took the body to the dead room off the assay hall, because the guild had a form for everything, even this. Form 9-C: Recovery of Deceased Contractor's Effects. Ivo filled it out in their own hand, because Ivo always volunteered for the forms nobody wanted, invisibility being a craft practiced in small motions.

Name: unknown. Employer: unregistered. Cause of death: exposure, pending physician's certification. Effects: one (1) item, oilcloth-wrapped, contents unverified.

The physician pronounced death and departed. The porters departed. The snow kept falling beyond the dead room's single high window, fat and slow and patient, and Ivo stood alone with a dead man and a parcel and the growing certainty that they had just become the most visible person in Corveth.

*Don't open it here,* said some instinct older than training. *Don't open it anywhere.*

They opened it there, of course. Curiosity in a clerk is like damp in a wall: you don't see it working, but everything it touches rots.

The cord cut their fingers coming off—or rather, the cord did not cut them, but the *cold* of it did, a thin bright line across the pad of their thumb that welled red and then froze, instantly, into a bead of garnet. Inside the oilcloth lay a map.

It was drawn on skin. Ivo knew leather, knew vellum, knew the fourteen grades of parchment the guild stocked, and this was none of them. It was skin, human skin, cured soft as cloth, and someone had drawn a country onto it in ink that shifted color when Ivo tilted it—brown to green to a green so vivid it hurt, summer-green, impossible-green in a city locked in November.

The country had mountains like a spine down its west, rivers braiding eastward, towns marked with tiny careful stars. One star on the coast was larger than the rest, drawn with a kind of devotion, and beside it, in a script Ivo's eye slid off of twice before it caught, was written a word:

*Vessany.*

There it was. There was the word again, and this time Ivo looked straight at it and their mind did the thing minds do with impossible sums: it refused. Vessany. The letters sat on the skin-map, plain as a price, and somewhere in Ivo's memory there should have been—an atlas, a schoolmaster, a shipping chart, *anything*—and there was nothing. No country by that name had ever been entered in any ledger Ivo had ever touched, and Ivo had touched, in eleven years of service, essentially all of them.

A country that appears in no ledger does not exist. That was the first law of the guild, older than the founding, older than the vaults. Value must be recorded to be real. Debt must be written to be owed. And here was a whole nation, unwritten.

Except.

Except that the map was warm in one place. Near the coast, near the devoted star, the skin held a heat like a sleeping animal, and everywhere else it was so cold it steamed faintly in the dead room's air, and when Ivo's bare fingers rested on the cold part—

The cold went *up*.

It went up through the bones of the hand, the wrist, the arm, and it carried something with it, the way a river carries silt: images. A kitchen with blue tiles. A woman singing two notes over and over, some lullaby worn down to its skeleton. Snow falling upward, impossibly, from the ground into a sky the color of a bruise healing. A door. A door standing free in a field of snow with nothing on either side of it, and someone small holding Ivo's hand—Ivo's *smaller* hand, a child's hand—and saying, in the language Ivo did not know they knew: *whatever happens, count us, count us, we are real, count us—*

Ivo snatched their hand back and sat down hard on the dead man's table, because the floor had developed opinions about staying still.

The map lay on the oilcloth, steaming gently, patient as a creditor.

"That's not possible," Ivo told it. Their voice sounded very small in the dead room. "I was born in Corveth. My mother kept a stall on Tanners' Lane. I have papers. I have *entries*."

The map said nothing, in the way that true things say nothing.

Ivo sat with it for a long time while the regulator clock ticked interest in the next room. And slowly, clerk-fashion, the terror cooled into procedure, and procedure hardened into decision, and the decision was this: the courier had said *for the debt*. Everything was owed to someone. Therefore the map was owed to someone. Therefore there was a record, somewhere, of the someone—and Ivo Zhou was the best finder of records in the guild, and nobody watched the best finder of records, because watching people was for people who mattered.

For the first time in eleven years, Ivo prayed—without knowing to whom, without believing in whom—that they could stay invisible just a little longer.

They wrapped the map. They signed Form 9-C. And as they blew out the dead room's lamp, they noticed the last line of the courier's effects inventory, written in their own hand minutes ago, which they had no memory of writing:

*Contents: one (1) map of Vessany. Entered against the founding debt. It remembers being stolen.*

Below it, in ink still wet, in handwriting that was theirs and yet not quite theirs, someone had added:

*So do you.*
