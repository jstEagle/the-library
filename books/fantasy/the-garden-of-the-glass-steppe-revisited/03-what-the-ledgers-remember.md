## 3. What the Ledgers Remember

The founding ledgers lived in the vault beneath the counting house, and the vault was cold in a way that had nothing to do with stone. Ivo had been down there twice before—once as a probationer, carrying lamp oil under Maren's eye, and once alone, sent to fetch a valuation of a widow's borrowed courage—and both times they had felt it: a stillness in the air, like the held breath of a room that was listening. The clerks joked that the vault was where the guild kept its conscience, since no one could account for its whereabouts elsewhere.

Tonight the joke did not comfort them.

Ivo descended the iron stair with the courier's map folded against their chest, inside their shirt, where it lay like a plate of ice laid over the heart. It had not warmed all day. They had carried it through inventory, through supper, through the long gray hours when the counting house emptied and the lamps were trimmed and the great ledgers were closed with sounds like coffins shutting. The map burned cold. That was the phrase that kept arriving, uninvited, in Ivo's mind—not *grew* cold, not *felt* cold, but *burned*, the way frost burns skin, the way grief burns when you bottle it too long without decanting.

The vault door took two keys and a word spoken flatly, without feeling. Ivo had stolen the second key from Maren's ring three nights ago, an act so far outside their character that they had done it almost to prove they were capable of it. The word was *solvency*. Of course it was.

Inside, the founding ledgers waited on their lecterns like sleeping animals.

There were nine of them. Every clerk knew this the way every clerk knew the guild's catechism: *Nine books, one debt, all accounts settled at the end of things.* No one read them. No one was permitted to read them. Their spines were bound in leather gone dark as old blood, and their clasps bore a stamp Ivo had never thought to question—a circle with a line struck through it, which the catechism called the Mark of Closure but which, looking at it now by lamplight, looked suddenly like something else. A mouth. Closed. Or made to be closed.

Ivo set the lamp on the reading stand and drew out the first ledger.

The paper was wrong. That was the first thing. Guild paper was uniform, milled, faintly scented with juniper to discourage moths and forgers alike. This paper was thick and rough-edged, handmade, and it smelled of nothing at all—which was stranger than any scent, because everything in the counting house smelled of something. Paper that had been scrubbed of scent. Paper that had been made to forget what it was.

The script inside was beautiful. Not guild-hand, with its rigid columns and its merciless arithmetic, but a flowing, generous hand that seemed to delight in the act of writing, looping its capitals, flourishing its terminals. And the entries—

Ivo's breath went shallow.

They were entries. That was the horror of it. Ivo knew entries the way a fisherman knows water, and these were entries, real ones, dated in a calendar the guild did not use, recording transactions in terms the guild did not trade. *Received in trust: one summer, whole, from the people of the valley below.* *Held in keeping: the names of the drowned, until such time as they are claimed.* *Borrowed against winter: forty days of birdsong; interest to be paid in kind.*

This was not commerce. This was custodianship. These were the accounts of something that *kept* things for people, that held what could not be held, that lent the impossible against the promise of return. The guild traded in bottled grief and borrowed luck and called itself the master of impossible commodities, and here, in its own founding book, was proof that every commodity it sold had been taken from somewhere. From someone. From some *thing* whose whole nature had been to hold such things in trust.

Ivo turned pages faster. The dates marched backward, or forward—the calendar meant nothing to them—and then, near the middle of the first ledger, the handwriting changed. The loops tightened. The flourishes died mid-stroke, like a bird shot in flight.

And there were gaps. Pages torn out—not removed cleanly, but torn, hurriedly, the stubs left behind like snapped teeth. Ivo ran their thumb along the ragged edge of one stub and felt the paper tremble—or felt their own hand tremble, they could no longer say which. Nine ledgers. In each one, the same wound: a cluster of missing pages, always in the same place, always where the entries spoke most tenderly of trust.

In the seventh ledger, something worse. Where a name should have been—where, judging by the spacing and the deep initial stroke, a name *had* been—someone had scraped the vellum raw. Ivo tilted the page toward the lamp. Beneath the scraping, if one held the light low enough, the ghost of letters rose like bruises:

Ves—

Nothing after. Just the two letters, and the scraped hollow where the rest had been murdered.

*Vessany.*

The map against Ivo's chest went so cold it ached, and Ivo gasped aloud in the vault's silence.

And then the memories came.

Not returned—*arrived*, the way weather arrives, all at once and from everywhere. Ivo gripped the lectern. A smell filled the vault that the vault could not contain: cut hay and river stones warming in sun. A sound: bells, but not guild bells—bells rung loose and joyful, out of time with one another, the way children ring them. A voice, singing in a language Ivo did not know and understood perfectly, a harvest song about carrying sheaves up the hill before the frost, *before the frost, little sister, before the frost comes down to stay—*

"Before the frost," Ivo whispered, in a language that was not the guild's tongue, in a voice that was not entirely their own. "Before the frost comes down."

They had never sung that song. They had never seen a hill like the one rising behind their eyes, gold to the ridgeline, with a white door standing open at its crest. They had never—but they *had*. They had run up that hill. They had been small. Someone had carried them on their shoulders, someone with a laugh like—

Like nothing. Like a hole. The memory had a person-shaped absence in it, scraped clean as the vellum, and Ivo understood, with a vertigo so total it felt like falling upward, that whatever had been done to the name in the seventh ledger had also been done to them. To their childhood. To everyone's. The guild had not only stolen a god's accounts. It had scraped the god out of the world, and the world had healed over the wound with forgetting, smooth as scar tissue, and Ivo had lived twenty years on the scar without once pressing on it.

Until tonight.

"I wouldn't read further."

Ivo spun. Maren stood at the foot of the iron stair, lamp in hand, her gray braid undone over her shoulder, her face lit from below into something older than her years. She did not look angry. She looked tired in a way Ivo had never seen—tired the way stone is tired, worn down by water over centuries.

"You followed me," Ivo said. Their voice came out strange. Half guild-flat, half something softer, accented with hills.

"I watched you steal my key," Maren said. "I've been watching you since the courier died in our doorway. I hoped you'd burn the map unread." She climbed the last steps slowly. "Some debts are better unpaid, Ivo. Some accounts are better unopened. What you're holding is not a ledger. It's a wound that learned to keep books."

"Whose name is scraped out?" Ivo demanded. The hill-song was still in their mouth; they could taste it, green and golden. "Whose name did they take?"

Maren set her lamp beside Ivo's. The two flames leaned toward each other. For a long moment she said nothing, and Ivo saw her look at the torn stubs, at the bruised ghost-letters, at Ivo's face—with what? Pity. Recognition. Something that might have been guilt wearing pity's coat.

"Forget it," Maren said quietly. "That is the only mercy left in this building, and I am asking you to take it. Close the book. Burn the map. Come up tomorrow and falsify another entry, save another debtor, live your small good life inside the forgetting. The alternative—" She stopped. Her hand rose toward the seventh ledger and did not quite touch it. "The alternative is remembering. And Ivo, I have remembered for twenty years, and I would give both my hands to stop."

"Then tell me," Ivo said. "Tell me what I'm remembering. Tell me who sang that song."

Maren's composure cracked—one hairline fracture, quickly sealed. But Ivo had spent a lifetime reading small deviations in large accounts, and they saw it plainly: Maren knew the song. Perhaps had sung it.

"No," Maren said. "Go home, Ivo. Sleep. By morning the forgetting will have smoothed over again—it always does, if you let it." She turned toward the stair, then paused, her back to them. "But if you won't—if you go looking for the door that isn't in any atlas—then know that the guild sends enforcers after lost property. And know that what was taken from Vessany was not taken fairly, and that the ones who took it are still alive, and still afraid, and fear makes clerks do arithmetic that has no bottom."

She climbed the stairs. The vault door did not close behind her.

Ivo stood alone among the nine wounded books, the lamps leaning together, the cold map burning over their heart. Slowly, hands shaking, they turned back to the seventh ledger—to the scraped hollow, to the two surviving letters.

*Ves.*

And beneath the hollow, in the margin, in that dying, tightening hand, someone had written one final line, small as a prayer, in the language Ivo should not have known and did:

*It does not sleep. It waits. Do not mistake its patience for consent.*

Outside, far above the vault, snow had begun to fall on the city—the first snow of the season, early, wrong, falling straight down without wind, as if the sky itself were holding its breath.

Ivo closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a coffin, and began to plan a theft.

* * *

By the time Ivo climbed back into the counting house, the snow was thickening past the windows, and the lamps along the Salt Gate road were going out one by one, as though the night were being erased ahead of them. Ivo stood a moment in the dark hall, the map against their chest, the memory of a hill burning behind their eyes, and made themselves one promise, in the language of the scraped-away country:

*I'm coming back before the frost comes down to stay.*

Somewhere across the city, in the debtors' dormitory behind the counting house, a smuggler named Sefa slept badly, dreaming of a door she had never seen.
