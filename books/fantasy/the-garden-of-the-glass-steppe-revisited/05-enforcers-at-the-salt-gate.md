## 5. Enforcers at the Salt Gate

Ivo heard the raid before they saw it: the counting house's great brass door groaning on its hinges at an hour when no honest clerk should be awake, and beneath that, the particular silence of boots that had been taught to walk quietly. Twenty years in the guild had given Ivo a taxonomy of sounds—the wet click of abacus beads, the sigh of vellum, the small dry cough of a ledger being opened—and this sound did not belong to any of them.

They were still at Maren's desk, three hours past midnight, the founding ledger's spine laid bare before them like a dissected animal. Ivo had pried it loose from its binding an hour ago, working by candle stub, because the pages could be replaced but the spine could not. The spine was where the guild kept its oldest marks: columns of figures pressed so deep into the leather they seemed less written than grown, and threaded through them, a name scraped away so thoroughly that only its shape remained—a hollow in the record, a country-sized absence.

*Vessany.* The courier's word. The map's cold weight, still folded against Ivo's ribs.

The candles went out all at once, though there was no draft.

Ivo did not think. Thinking was for clerks with time. They swept the spine into their coat, felt it settle against their chest—cold as the map, cold as something that remembered being alive—and blew out toward the scriptorium's back stair as the brass door gave way below with a sound like a struck bell.

Lamplight bloomed up the stairwell behind them. Voices, clipped and procedural, the way enforcers spoke even when breaking things: *Seal the counting floor. Wake the night auditors. If anyone runs, note the direction; running is itself an entry.*

Ivo took the stairs two at a time and came out through the coal chute into the alley behind the salt sheds, where the air tasted of brine and old fish and freedom of a very low grade. Behind them, the counting house windows began to light one by one, floor by floor, until the whole building glowed like a ledger being read aloud.

They ran.

* * *

Sefa had been awake because sleep was a commodity she'd stopped being able to afford around the same time the guild had bought her family's debt. She sat in the loft above her mother's chandlery, whittling a wick-trimmer she didn't need, when the first runner went past in the street below—not running from anything, but running *toward*, which in Salt Gate meant trouble with a warrant attached.

She was down the stairs and out the door before she'd decided to move. That was the smuggler's curse: the body always knew before the mind agreed.

The counting house was lit up like a festival nobody would admit to holding. Enforcers in grey wool stood at the corners, unhurried, writing in little books. Sefa knew those books. Everything you did became a line in them, and every line accrued interest.

And there—cutting across the square with the terrible straightness of someone who had never learned to loiter—Ivo Zhou, coat bulging wrong at the chest, face pale as tallow, walking fast in the manner of a person who has realized that running is itself an entry.

Sefa did the arithmetic in half a second. Ivo had falsified her mother's entry once—she'd found the alteration weeks later, a transposed digit, a debt shrunk just enough to buy a season. She had never said thank you. Saying thank you made it a transaction between them, and some mercies were better left unacknowledged, like gifts left on doorsteps.

But arithmetic didn't care about delicacy. Two enforcers were drifting toward Ivo from the fountain side, casual as weather.

Sefa stepped out, hooked her arm through Ivo's, and said, loudly, "You're late, love. Mother's been asking."

Ivo's eyes went wide, then—credit where due—narrowed into something almost convincing. "The ledgers," they said.

"Always the ledgers." She turned them both down Candlemaker's Row, past the tallow stink, past the shuttered shrine where her mother still left offerings to saints whose names the guild had taxed out of use. The enforcers' footsteps did not follow. Not yet. But she could feel them the way you feel a change in pressure before snow.

"Where are we going?" Ivo whispered.

"That," Sefa said, "depends entirely on what's in your coat."

* * *

Ivo told her everything in the chandlery cellar, by the light of a single candle Sefa refused to trim properly because a guttering flame was harder to see from the street. The map. The courier dying in the doorway with frost in his beard though it was high summer. The scraped name in the founding spine. The way Ivo's own memories had begun arriving like debts called in early—a white field, a bell with no clapper, a woman singing in a language Ivo's mouth had forgotten but their throat apparently hadn't, because they'd caught themselves humming it over the grief-shipment audit and had to stop, hands shaking, mid-note.

"And now they're raiding the counting house at midnight," Ivo finished, "which means Maren talked, or Maren didn't talk fast enough, or—" They stopped. Their hand lay flat against their coat, over the spine, the way you'd rest it on a sleeping child or a wound.

Sefa looked at the candle for a long moment. Then she got up, dragged a crate from under the eel-smoking rack, and pried up a board Ivo would never have found in a hundred years. Inside: a go-bag, packed and repacked so many times the canvas had gone soft as cloth-of-gold. Rope. A forged pass under a name that wasn't hers. A dried apple, absurdly, still red.

"I've been ready to run since I was fourteen," she said. "I just never had anywhere worth running *to*." She slung the bag over her shoulder. "North?"

"North," Ivo said. "There's a door. The map says there's a door."

"Doors out of the country don't exist."

"The country doesn't exist either. Apparently that helps."

It was almost a laugh. Neither of them managed it fully, but the shape of it passed between them, and Ivo thought: *this is what it feels like, then. Not being alone in a cellar at the end of your life.* It was a strange thing to learn at such an hour.

Then the candle went out again. No draft. And this time, from the top of the cellar stairs, came a voice—not an enforcer's clipped procedure, but something older, worn smooth as a river stone:

"You'll want the north road, then. And you'll want me on it, or you'll die at the Salt Gate crossing without ever knowing why."

* * *

He came down the stairs with his hands open and empty, a big man gone soft at the edges, wearing a cassock that had been grey once and was now the color of surrender. Around his neck, where a holy symbol should have been, hung nothing but a leather cord with a hole punched in it—as if the symbol had been cut away and the cord couldn't bring itself to notice.

"They defrocked me eleven years ago," he said. "Brother Anselm, formerly of the Order of the Quiet Bell. Formerly of anywhere." He looked at Ivo the way a man looks at a fire he thought had gone out. "You've been humming it. In the square. I heard you across forty feet of crowd and my knees nearly went. That's a Vessany hymn. There are maybe nine people in this city who could hum it and not know what they're humming, and I've spent eleven years listening for every one of them."

Sefa had put herself between Anselm and Ivo, knife out, low and easy. "Prove it."

Anselm knelt—knees cracking like green wood—and held out his open palm. On it he laid a small object: a bell, no bigger than a thumb, cast in dull pewter. It had no clapper. It had never had a clapper.

"We rang these with our voices," he said. "Humming into the metal until it answered. Our god doesn't speak, child. Our god *listens*. That was the whole of the faith. You give it your words and it keeps them safe, and in exchange—" his voice caught, and he steadied it like a man setting down something heavy "—in exchange, winter stayed gentle. Snow enough for the wheat to sleep under. Cold enough to kill the blight. We called it the Long Kindness. Until the year the clergy decided kindness wasn't profitable, and sold the listening to men who wanted to trade in it. Bottled grief. Borrowed luck. Last words." He looked up at Ivo, and his eyes were terrible and calm. "Your guild didn't invent its commodities. It *harvests* them. From Him. He's been silent twenty years because they took His voice away in installments, and wrote the terms in a book whose spine I can see the outline of through your coat."

The cellar was very quiet. Somewhere above, Sefa's mother turned over in her sleep, and the floorboards spoke.

Ivo drew out the spine with both hands. Against the candleflame—Sefa relit it, hands steady, smuggler's nerves of iron—the scraped hollow of the name seemed to deepen, to widen, until Ivo felt they were looking not at a gap in a record but at a mouth, opened, waiting to be filled.

"He's still there," Ivo said. Not a question. They could feel it, the way you feel a hearth through a wall. "Under the winter. Under the erasure. Still listening."

"To no one," Anselm said. "For twenty years. Can you imagine it? A god built entirely out of attention, and no one attending." He rose, slowly, and gathered his ruined cassock around him like a man dressing for a long road. "I swore an oath once, to attend. They took my vestments and my name from the rolls, but oaths aren't guild property. They can't be seized for debt." He nodded at the spine, at the map, at Ivo's face. "Take me north. I know the crossings. I know which priests along the way still remember, and which ones report. And when we reach whatever door is standing in whatever snowfield—" his jaw set "—I intend to be the one who knocks."

Sefa looked at Ivo. Ivo looked at Sefa. Between them passed the whole silent negotiation of people who had already decided and were only checking the other had decided too.

"Three of us," Sefa said. "A disgraced priest, an indentured smuggler, and a runaway clerk carrying stolen scripture." She snorted, shouldered her bag, and kicked dirt over the candle. "We sound like the beginning of a bad joke."

"It's not a joke yet," Anselm said gravely, moving toward the stairs. "Give us till the border."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, came the tolling of the counting house's alarm bell—one long note, then another, spreading outward through Salt Gate like a searchlight made of sound. The enforcers were writing names in their little books. Every line accruing interest.

Ivo pressed the spine to their chest, felt its cold patient weight, and followed the others up into the dark.
