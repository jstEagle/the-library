## 6. The Country That Isn't There

Anselm had spent eleven years learning to say nothing, and now he could not stop talking.

It was the road that did it. Roads always had. In the old country, before the old country stopped being anywhere at all, the pilgrims used to say that a road was a kind of prayer with its knees bent—every mile a syllable, every traveler a word in a sentence the god was too patient to finish. Anselm had walked a thousand such sentences as a young brother of the Silent Order, and he had learned to listen to them. Now, walking north out of the guild's reach with a stolen ledger spine wrapped in oilcloth against his chest and two strangers at his heels, he found his mouth opening like a door long kept shut.

"You should know what you're carrying," he said, on the third morning, when the pines had thickened and the salt smell of the lowlands was finally gone from the air. "Before the enforcers catch us and it stops mattering."

Ivo looked up from the map they were following—the courier's map, which burned cold even through Sefa's glove—and said nothing, which Anselm was beginning to understand was Ivo's way of saying *yes*.

Sefa said, "Finally," and sat down on a fallen log like a woman settling in for a story she'd already paid for.

Anselm unwrapped the ledger spine. It was only a fragment, truly—a strip of binding and perhaps forty leaves, torn from the founding ledgers by someone who had known exactly where to cut. The pages were vellum gone the color of old teeth. He laid them open on the log between them, careful as a man handling relics, because that is what they were.

"Vessany," he said. The name came out of him strangely, half-swallowed, the way a word does when it has not been spoken aloud in twenty years. Even now, even here, three days north of the Salt Gate, he felt the syllables snag on something invisible. "Say it back to me."

"Vessany," Sefa said easily. She'd never heard of the place; there was nothing in her to resist.

Ivo tried. Anselm watched their lips move, watched them shape the first syllable and lose the second, watched them frown and try again. *Ves-sa—* and then nothing, a small blankness behind the eyes, like a lamp guttering.

"It gets easier," Anselm said quietly. "The further north we go. It's not you. It's the erasure. It's thickest where the guild is, because the guild maintains it." He tapped the vellum. "This is how."

* * *

He told it the way he had once told scripture, walking slowly while they ate cold rice, because the old brothers had believed truth went down easier in motion.

"There was a god in Vessany who did not speak. Not a god of silence—that's a mistake people make, thinking silence is the same as absence. This god spoke constantly. It spoke in snowfall and in the ripening of plums and in the exact weight of a debt forgiven. It simply never used words. We called it the Quiet Neighbor. Every household left its ledger open on the table at night—not accounts of money, accounts of *owing*. What you owed your neighbor, your dead, the season, the road. And in the morning, sometimes, an entry would be answered. A debt struck through. A kindness entered that no one had written. The god kept the country's books, and the country trusted it absolutely, because the god never took anything without entering it fair."

Sefa snorted softly. "A god that balances."

"A god that *is* balance," Anselm said. "And then came the famine winter. Ninety-one years ago, but it might as well be last year; grief doesn't compound interest, it just sits. The harvest failed twice. The Quiet Neighbor gave everything it had—poured itself into the granaries, if you want the truth of it, though we didn't know that then. And when it was nearly empty, when the snow wouldn't stop and the people were boiling bark, the clergy did the thing clergy do." His mouth twisted. "We bargained."

"With who?" Sefa asked.

"With the god. That's the horror of it. There was no devil at the door, no rival power. We went to our own Quiet Neighbor with ink and vellum and we wrote terms. The terms are on these pages—I've read them so many times I could recite them in my sleep. The god would keep the country fed through any winter, endlessly, without emptying itself. In exchange—" Anselm's voice caught, an old splinter working loose. "In exchange, the god would go silent. Truly silent. No more answered entries. No more speech in snowfall or plums or forgiven debts. And the country would forget it had ever been heard. The clergy thought they were being clever. They thought they were saving their people and paying with something the people would never miss, because the people would never remember missing it."

"And the god agreed?" Ivo asked. Their voice was very quiet.

"The god agreed," Anselm said, "because gods keep their word. That's the whole difference between gods and us. But it wrote one condition into the terms, one line, and the clergy were so relieved and so ashamed that they signed without reading it closely." He turned the vellum toward them. The script was old Vessany hand, the letters tall and patient. "Here. Read this line."

Ivo leaned over. Anselm watched them read, watched their lips move, and this time the name came whole: "*Vessany.*" They said it twice, tasting it, and their face did something complicated—recognition arriving like weather. "It says... the silence holds 'until the ledgers are returned to their author.' Until someone brings the books home."

"The guild's founding ledgers," Anselm said. "Your masters didn't invent their trade, Ivo. Twenty years ago the Order fell apart—shame, mostly, and the enforcers came, and the surviving brothers scattered. The guild bought what we sold. Bottled grief. Borrowed luck. Last words of the drowned. Do you understand what those commodities actually *are*? They're the god's speech, harvested. Everything the Quiet Neighbor ever said, pressed into inventory and priced by the ounce. The guild has been selling my god's voice for twenty years and calling it commerce." He closed the vellum gently. "And the silence itself—that's the winter. The long winters that have crept south year by year since the signing. The god can't speak, so the seasons have forgotten how to turn properly. Your guild isn't rich off tragedy, Ivo. Your guild *is* the tragedy."

* * *

They walked. The pines gave way to birch, the birch to bare grey slopes with snow lying in the hollows like spilled flour. Anselm noticed that Ivo had stopped asking questions somewhere past midday, and that their silences had changed quality—from the competent, armored silence of a clerk to something rawer, a person listening to a house settle inside themselves.

It happened at dusk, at a shrine.

There was no reason for a shrine to be there. That was the point. It stood alone at a bend in the road: a waist-high stone post, snow-capped, carved with the sign of the open book. No village within a day's walk. No road worth blessing. Just a post, standing faithful in the middle of nowhere, because nowhere had once been Vessany's northern march and the stones remembered even when the maps didn't.

Ivo stopped as if they'd hit glass.

Anselm watched them approach the stone slowly, the way one approaches a sleeping animal. Watched them lay two fingers on the carved book. Watched their breath go strange.

"I know this," Ivo said. It wasn't a question. "I know this stone. There was—" Their brow furrowed, hard, like someone hauling a net out of deep water. "There was a village. Down that slope. My mother sold—she sold *bottled things*, no, wait, she *made* candles, she made candles that burned with the color of whatever you'd lost, and there was a boy, there was a boy who—" Ivo's hand came off the stone as if it had bitten them. They stared at their own fingers. "Anselm. Why do I remember candle-wax?"

"Because you were born there," Anselm said. Gently, because some truths must be set down like eggs. "Under the sign of the open book. You're Vessany-born, Ivo. The erasure took the country off the maps, but it couldn't take it out of the people who came from it—it could only bury it. And every mile north, the burial gets shallower."

Ivo sat down in the snow. Not gracefully, not dramatically—just sat, the way a person sits when their legs have been reassigned to someone else. Sefa crouched beside them without touching, which Anselm noted was exactly right.

"My parents put me on a cart," Ivo said, to no one, to the stone, to the snow. "South. I was six. There was a woman in grey who paid the driver and she said—she said *forget until it's safe*—and I thought that was her name, Forget-until-it's-safe, I called her that for years in my head, and everyone laughed at me for having an imaginary friend with such a stupid name." A sound came out of them that was almost a laugh and was not one. "She was real. She was one of yours. One of the Order. She wiped me and sent me south and the guild took me in and taught me numbers, and I've spent my whole life keeping accounts for the people who stole my own."

The wind moved through the birches with a sound like pages turning. Anselm knelt in the snow across from them, old knees cracking, and did the only thing his order had ever actually been good at: he waited.

"They erased a whole country," Ivo said at last. "Twenty years. Millions of entries. Debts and weddings and funerals and candle-makers, all of it struck through, and nobody south of the mountains even noticed the hole. How do you grieve a place that officially never existed? Where do you even put it?"

"In the body," Anselm said. "That's why we're walking north. That's why the map burns cold instead of hot—cold is how the Quiet Neighbor says *yes*." He looked up at the darkening sky, where the first flakes were coming down, early and wrong, snow in a season that should be turning green. "And I'll tell you where else you put it. In the terms. Because I've read that clause a hundred times, Ivo, and there's a part I haven't told you yet, because I wanted you to hear it standing on Vessany soil rather than sitting in the snow."

Ivo lifted their head.

"The condition cuts both ways," Anselm said. "'Until the ledgers are returned to their author.' Returned *by whom* is not specified. Any hand will serve. But there's a price written under it, in the god's own hand, and the god's handwriting is—" he searched for it, and found it, and it frightened him the way it had frightened him at nineteen—"*fair*. Whatever it asks, it will be exactly what was taken. No more. No less." He rose, stiffly, and held out his hand. "Come. Snow's starting, and I don't trust snow anymore, and neither should you."

Ivo took his hand and stood. For a moment the three of them stood at the bend in the road, at the shrine of the open book, while the wrong-season snow came down around them fine and dry and endless.

And then, in the space between one flake and the next, Anselm saw it—saw all three of them see it, because Sefa swore aloud and Ivo went white to the lips.

Out on the slope below the shrine, where there had been nothing, there was now a light. A single window's worth of warm yellow, burning in the middle of trackless wilderness, and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, carried up the hill on air that should have smelled only of pine and snow—

the smell of candle wax.
