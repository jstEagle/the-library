## 7. Winter's Long Falling

The village had no name on any map, which was fitting, because the maps had eaten it.

Sefa saw it first from the ridge—a scatter of turf-roofed houses crouched in a bowl of snow, smoke rising thin and straight, as if the whole settlement were holding its breath. No walls. No watchtowers. Nothing worth defending except what couldn't be seen.

"They trade in memories here," Anselm said. He had gone quiet since the salt gate, quiet the way a man goes quiet after setting down something heavy he'd carried too long. "It's how they survive the erasure. A country can be struck from paper. It can't be struck from everyone at once—not if they keep passing the pieces around."

"Like coin," Sefa said.

"Worse than coin." Anselm's breath plumed white. "Coin you can counterfeit."

Ivo said nothing. They hadn't said much in three days—just walked with the ledger's stolen spine wrapped in oilcloth against their chest like a second heart, and touched the map through their coat at intervals, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Sefa watched them do it now and felt the old familiar itch of assessment: *what is this one worth, what will they cost me.* Except the arithmetic kept coming back wrong. Ivo Zhou, who falsified entries to save strangers from indenture, who had offered to erase her family's debt before she'd even agreed to guide them—that was not a sum she knew how to carry.

Her mother's debt still sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. The guild's offer stood: erase it, and deliver these two to whatever door they were looking for. She had told herself it was only that. Only business. But she had been walking north for three days and had not once thought of the terms, only of the way Ivo checked the map, and the way Anselm prayed without words, moving his lips over names that had been scraped out of every book that held them.

They went down into the village as the light failed.

* * *

The woman who met them at the first house was old in a way that made Sefa think of riverbeds—worn down but still carrying water somewhere deep. Her name was Odile, and she looked at Anselm for a long moment before she spoke.

"You're the priest's boy," she said. "Tomas's novice. You used to sweep the chapel at Vessany-Under-Hill."

Anselm's face did something complicated. "You remember me?"

"I remember everything." She said it flatly, without pride. "That's my burden in this place. Come in before the cold takes your stories. Cold takes stories first, you know. Teeth and toes second."

Inside, the house was warm and dim and crowded with things that made no sense until they did: jars on shelves, each labeled in a careful hand. Not preserves. Sefa leaned close to one—*First snowfall, the year the bells changed*—and when she uncorked it, just a breath, the smell of some childhood winter she had never lived flooded her so hard she had to sit down.

"Careful," Odile said, not unkindly. "Those are strong ones. The weak memories we spend freely. The strong ones—" she shrugged, "—the strong ones are dowries. Funerals. Wars."

The villagers came by through the evening, drawn by word of strangers, and Sefa learned the economy of the place by watching. A man traded an hour of his mother's voice for a week of bread. Two sisters split a single summer between them, each keeping half, neither able afterward to say why they flinched at the smell of cut grass. A boy of maybe ten paid for a hot meal with the memory of his own name, and got it back the next morning from a neighbor who had been keeping it safe, and wept when it was returned to him like a gift.

"That's the trick of it," Odile told Sefa, later, by the fire. "A memory alone rots. Shared, it keeps. We are each other's cellars." She looked at Sefa with those riverbed eyes. "And what do you keep, smuggler? What do you pay with?"

"Nothing," Sefa said. "I don't have anything worth bottling."

Odile smiled, and it was not a kind smile, but it was not unkind either. "Everyone says that. Everyone's wrong." She nodded toward the corner where Ivo sat, asleep sitting up, the oilcloth bundle against their chest. "Your clerk there has been dreaming loud enough to fill this room. Do you know what they dream?"

"No."

"Snow. A garden under snow. And a voice saying *come home* in a language they claim not to speak." Odile poked the fire. "We heard that voice twenty years ago, all of us, the day the country closed. Then silence. If your clerk is hearing it again—" she looked up, and for the first time she seemed afraid, "—then the door isn't just opening. Something on the other side is pulling."

Sefa slept badly, and dreamed of nothing at all, which somehow felt worse.

* * *

Maren came at dawn.

Sefa woke to Anselm's hand over her mouth and his other hand pointing at the window, where a figure stood at the edge of the village in a guild cloak gone gray with road-dust. Senior Clerk Maren. Sefa had seen her twice from across the counting house floor—a woman like a closed ledger herself, spine straight, pages shut.

"She'll have reported our route," Sefa whispered. "Enforcers will be behind her."

"She came alone," Anselm said. "Look at her tracks. One set. And look at her hands."

Her hands were empty. Raised slightly away from her body, palms out, the way you approach a dog that has been kicked.

Ivo was already awake, already standing, the map in one hand and something terrible and calm in their face. They went out into the blue morning cold, and Sefa followed because following Ivo Zhou had apparently become a thing she did now.

Maren stopped ten paces off. Up close she looked wrong—hollowed, like a jar someone had uncorked and forgotten to refill. Frost had gotten into her eyebrows. She had walked a long way badly dressed for it.

"Ivo," she said.

"You brought enforcers to the counting house," Ivo said. "You caught me at the ledgers and told me to forget. Now you've found us. Say what you came to say."

"I tore the pages."

The wind moved. Somewhere behind them a villager's door opened a crack, then another. The exiles were coming out, silent, gathering along the houses' edges like a congregation.

"The founding ledgers," Maren went on, and her voice did not shake, which Sefa understood was costing her everything. "The terms of the silencing. I tore them out forty years ago—I was a junior clerk, I was sent to copy them, and I read what the guild had done, what *we* had done, and I couldn't—" For the first time her voice cracked. "I hid the pages. In the chapel wall at Vessany-Under-Hill. I thought I was protecting them. I thought someday someone would find them and set it right. Instead the guild simply rewrote the terms elsewhere, and my theft became part of the cover, and I spent forty years telling myself I was a secret ally when really I was just—" she breathed, "—a coward with good handwriting."

"Why come now?" Ivo asked. "Why follow us?"

"Because you burned cold when you touched the map. Because I've watched you for years, Ivo, and you have mercy in you like a splinter working toward the skin. And because last month I finally opened the jar." She reached into her coat and drew out a small glass vessel, stoppered, holding what looked like a curl of smoke or a lock of pale hair. "My memory of tearing the pages. I bottled it myself, forty years ago, so that I could live with what I'd done. I sold pieces of my guilt to the guild's buyers over the decades whenever it grew too heavy. This is the last of it. The original sin, whole." She held it out. "I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm asking to be entered in the account. Whatever comes next—let me be written down as what I am."

Nobody moved. The exiles had come closer, and Sefa realized with a chill that they recognized her—not Maren the senior clerk, but Maren the young woman who had visited the border villages in the years before the closing, buying memories cheap, cataloguing names. Some of them had sold her pieces of themselves. Some of those pieces had gone into the founding ledgers. Into the god.

An old man stepped forward—the boy grown old? No; someone else—and spat in the snow. "She priced our summers," he said. "Four coppers a memory. My wife sold her wedding day to feed our children and never got it back."

"I know," Maren said. "I wrote the entry."

"And you want what? Absolution?"

"No. I want a page."

Silence, then. The long falling of snow filled it, fat flakes drifting down without hurry, the winter that had lasted twenty years settling over all of them equally.

It was Odile who finally spoke, from the crowd's edge, in the tone of a woman making a trade she intended to honor. "Give me the jar," she said to Maren. "Not to forgive you. To hold. That's what we do here—we keep what people can't carry yet. When you've earned it back, if you ever earn it back, you may buy it home at fair price." She extended her hand. "Until then you travel with us, sleep apart, eat last, and work hardest. Those are the terms. Take them or walk back into the snow."

Maren looked at the jar a long moment—her guilt, her forty years, the last true copy of what she'd done—and put it into Odile's palm.

"Terms accepted," she said.

Ivo turned away first, back toward the fire, and Sefa fell into step beside them. At the doorway Ivo paused, and Sefa saw that their hand had gone to the map inside their coat, and that they were shivering though the cold could not explain it.

"It's stronger," Ivo murmured. "The pulling. Last night it wasn't words anymore." Their eyes, when they lifted them, were full of snowlight and something older. "Last night, Sefa, I dreamed in Vessany. And the door knows we're coming. I felt it open—one inch, like a held breath let go."

Behind them, the exiles were already dividing the morning's work, and Maren stood alone in the falling snow with empty hands, waiting to be given something to carry.
