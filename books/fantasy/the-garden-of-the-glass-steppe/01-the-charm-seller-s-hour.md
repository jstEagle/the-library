## 1. The Charm-Seller's Hour

The trick to selling magic that doesn't exist is to believe in it harder than your customer does.

Bram had learned that in her first week behind the counter of the Saltglass Shop, back when her hands still shook every time the bell over the door rang. Three years later they were steady. She could weigh a river-stone in her palm, look a widow dead in the eye, and say, *This one keeps bad dreams from crossing water*, and mean it—not because the stone did anything, but because grief needed somewhere to sit, and a stone was as good a place as any. Better, maybe. Stones didn't ask questions.

She arranged the morning stock with the practiced indifference of a woman who had nothing to hide: knucklebones polished smooth and sold as luck-charms, copper wire twisted into knots "against fever," little glass beads blown in the shape of tears for mothers who'd lost children to the gray cough. None of it worked. All of it was honest. That was the joke she never told anyone—that in a city where real magic carried a Warden's brand and a slow death, the only safe way to traffic in wonder was to sell things that were completely, verifiably dead.

Corvane didn't mind superstition. Corvane minded power.

Outside the shop window, the city was doing its morning impression of itself: gray stone, gray sky, gray light off the canal where the barges came in heavy with grain and left light with coin. The lamplighters were snuffing the last of the night-flames along Tallow Row. Somewhere a bell counted seven, its voice flattened by fog. A good hour. The Charm-Seller's Hour—early enough that the desperate came before pride woke up, late enough that the Wardens' night patrol had gone home to sleep off whatever Wardens drank.

Bram lit the lamp behind the counter anyway, because shadows made customers nervous, and set out her ledger. The ledger was the most dangerous object in the room and she treated it accordingly: names written small, purchases described in the dullest language she could manage. *River-stone, one. Beads, blue, six.* Nothing about what the stones were *for*. Nothing about the woman from Weir Street who bought a knot of copper for her son's fever and came back three days later to buy six more, which meant either the fever broke or it didn't, and either way Bram would take her coin and her silence together.

The bell over the door rang at half past the hour.

"Open?" said the man in the doorway, though he could see perfectly well that she was. He was broad through the shoulders, dock-worker's hands, and he stood like someone used to being told to wait.

"For another minute or an hour," Bram said. "Depends on what you're buying."

He crossed the floor in four strides and put something on the counter between them. A boot heel. Small—a child's size—and worn through at the sole, caked with canal mud.

"My girl can't sleep," he said. "Walks the floor till the bells. My wife says—" He stopped, jaw working. "My wife says there's a woman on Tallow Row sells charms that work."

Bram looked at the boot heel for a long moment. It was a test, she understood. Not his—the city's. This was how it always went: some frightened parent repeating a rumor they shouldn't have heard, standing in her shop like a lit fuse, and Bram had to decide, in the space of a breath, whether this was a sale or a trap.

It was almost never a trap. Almost.

"I sell trinkets," she said. "Knickknacks. Pretty rubbish for people who like pretty rubbish."

"My wife says—"

"Your wife," Bram said, not unkindly, "is grieving something, and grief makes people hear things." She reached under the counter without looking and came up with the tray she kept for exactly this: river-stones, each one wrapped in gray cloth, each one identical. She set one down beside the boot heel. "This is a rock. I found it in the canal shallows. It does nothing. But if you put it under her pillow, and you tell her—it has to be you, not your wife, fathers count different—that this rock holds the walking dreams so she doesn't have to, then some nights it'll work. Because she'll believe you. Children believe their fathers."

The man stared at the stone. Then at her. Something in his face loosened, the way faces did when they realized they weren't going to be cheated and weren't going to be saved, only helped, which was rarer than both.

"How much?"

"Two coppers."

"It's a rock."

"It's two coppers' worth of a rock." She held out her hand. "And a secret, which is free."

He paid. He took the stone. At the door he paused, and she watched him decide not to say thank you, because thanking a charm-seller out loud felt too much like confessing, and instead he touched two fingers to his brow—an old gesture, Veyli, from before the covenant shattered, from before anyone in Corvane had a reason to remember it—and was gone into the fog.

Bram stood very still until the bell's last tremor died.

*Fathers count different.* Where had she learned that? Not here. The knowledge sat in her like a splinter too deep to dig out, one of a thousand small certainties she carried about things like charms and thresholds and the hours when spirits walked, none of which she could account for, all of which she knew the way she knew her own hands. She had woken up in Corvane three years ago with coin in her pocket, a trade in her fingers, and a past with a hole in it the exact shape of everything that mattered.

That was the story she told herself, anyway. The other story—the one she kept in a locked box beneath the floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth, never opened—told itself differently. Some nights the hole in her memory ached like a lost tooth, and she caught herself tracing maps on the countertop with one fingertip: coastlines she'd never sailed, mountain ranges no atlas in Corvane contained, a steppe that gleamed like a frozen sea.

She put the thought away. She was good at putting thoughts away. It was, in fact, her single greatest professional skill.

* *

Midmorning brought the regulars, and Bram moved among them like a woman turning a grindstone: Mrs. Halloway for her monthly knot of copper ("for the joints, dear, don't tell me it's nothing, I can feel it working"), a pair of apprentices from the chandlers' buying luck-bones before their licensing exams, a thin clerk who bought a glass tear every time the gray cough took someone on his street and never said whose name. Bram priced kindness low and ignorance high and kept the ledger current, and if any of them noticed that the charm-seller never once claimed a charm actually *worked*, they were polite enough—or frightened enough—not to mention it.

The trouble came at noon, and it came wearing gray.

She heard it before she saw it: boots on Tallow Row, too many, too matched. Six sets, moving in step. In Corvane only one kind of person walked in step, and they didn't come to buy river-stones.

Bram's body reacted before her mind did—three years of vigilance had built reflexes deeper than thought. Her hand went to the counter's underside and flipped the false panel; the tray of "working charms"—the ones she kept for show, carved with symbols copied from a book she'd bought precisely because it was wrong—slid into the hidden drawer even as her other hand swept the day's coins into the till. By the time the door swung open she was weighing dried lavender with the air of a woman interrupted mid-chore.

Six Wardens filled her doorway like a wall deciding to be rude.

Their armor was the color of wet slate, unadorned except for the sigil at the breast: a circle broken by a vertical line. The Seal of Severance. Under it, their faces wore the particular blankness of men who had been trained out of curiosity, which was worse than cruelty, because cruelty could be reasoned with.

The one in front—sergeant's bars, young, jaw like a drawn bow—let his gaze walk the shop slowly. Shelves of trinkets. Bundles of herbs. The ledger, closed, sitting square on the counter.

"Charm-seller," he said. It wasn't a question, but it wasn't quite a greeting either.

"That's the sign outside," Bram said. "You read it right."

"We're making inquiries." He produced a folded paper, official cream, the Seal stamped in black wax. "There was an incident last night. Docks district. A man was found dead with—" his eyes flicked down to the paper, and something flickered across his face too fast to name, "—unusual effects on his person. We're asking merchants in the trade whether anything unusual has been offered for sale."

"Everything I sell is unusual," Bram said. "That's rather the point of a charm shop."

The sergeant's jaw tightened. One of the others, older, gray at the temples, shifted his weight, and Bram catalogued him automatically: tired, bored, the one most likely to want this over with. The youngest one at the back hadn't blinked enough. She filed that away too.

"The incident," the sergeant said, with the patience of a man reciting scripture to a child, "involved suspected trafficking in proscribed materials. The penalty for such trafficking is forfeiture of goods, forfeiture of premises, and branding." He let the word sit. "I'll ask again. Has anyone offered you items of genuine arcane character? Charms that function? Objects that move, glow, sing, bleed, or otherwise behave contrary to their nature?"

*Bleed.* The word snagged on something in her, a hook finding old scar tissue, and she didn't know why. She smiled through it.

"Sergeant, if I had an object that bled, I'd have sold it to a surgeon by now."

Someone behind him exhaled through their nose—not quite a laugh. The sergeant heard it. His ears went pink, and Bram understood she'd just spent a small coin of goodwill, and that the exchange rate on Warden goodwill was ruinous.

He stepped forward. Close enough now that she could smell the cold-iron tang of his armor, close enough that she saw his eyes drop—to the counter, to the ledger—and stay there a beat too long.

"You keep careful accounts," he said.

"I keep accounts."

"May I?"

Every instinct she owned stood up and screamed at once. She kept her hands loose on the counter and her face pleasant and her heartbeat nailed down by main force. Refusal was confession. Compliance was exposure. There was no third door, and the sergeant knew it, and the pause he allowed before she answered was long enough to be a lesson.

"Mind the ink," she said. "It smears."

He opened the ledger. He turned pages. His finger traveled down columns of the dullest prose ever committed to paper—*river-stone, one; beads, blue, six; copper knot, two; lavender, bundle*—and Bram watched him hunt for magic in arithmetic and find nothing, because there was nothing to find, because she had spent three years building a life so gray and so dull that boredom itself was her best defense.

He closed the book.

"Keep your records current," he said. "If anything unusual comes into your possession—anything at all—you're required to report it within a day to the district office. Failure to report is treated as possession." He straightened. "Enjoy your afternoon, charm-seller."

They left. The boots faded down Tallow Row, in step, six hearts beating in six gray cages, and Bram stood in her shop in the ringing quiet and discovered that her hands, steady through all of it, were shaking now that it was over.

She laughed once, short and ugly, at herself. Adrenaline with nowhere to go. She put the kettle on and waited for it to pass, the way she waited for all of it to pass—the patrols, the questions, the nights when she dreamed in a language she couldn't speak awake. Vigilance wasn't a skill you finished learning. It was a tax you paid daily, forever, on a fortune you couldn't remember earning.

* *

The afternoon crawled. Rain arrived around three, soft and persistent, silvering the window and emptying the street. Bram used the lull to do the thing she did every few weeks when the itch got unbearable: she took out the locked box.

Not the one under the floorboards. She never touched that one. This was a smaller box, a permitted box, the decoy she kept for moments exactly like this—when the wanting got loud and she needed to feed it just enough to keep it quiet. Inside: a stub of pencil, a sheet of good paper, and three years of accumulated nothing.

She drew the steppe.

Her hand knew it without her. That was the horror and the comfort of it—she couldn't have described the place in words if you'd held a knife to her throat, but her fingers remembered every contour. The glass flats stretching to a horizon that curved the wrong way. The singing spires in the far distance, drawn in strokes so fine they seemed to hum off the page. A river that ran backward, marked with the notation *veyl-script*, in characters she recognized and could not read.

She was adding shading to the eastern flats—her pencil moving faster now, hungry, the paper filling with a country that had no business existing inside a Corvane shopfront—when the bell over the door rang.

Bram's hand slammed flat over the drawing on pure reflex.

"Closed," she called, not looking up. "Weather's foul, come back tomorr—"

"It's raining," said a voice, "and I'm dying, so I'll risk it."

She looked up.

A stranger stood in her doorway, dripping. Young, maybe, though it was hard to tell—young people aged fast when they were running. His coat was dark with more than rain along the left side, and there was blood on the mat beneath his boots, already diluting pink in the wet. His eyes were doing something she'd seen twice before in her life and never forgotten: fixed on her with the terrible clarity of a man spending his last coins.

"Shop's shut," Bram said, rising slowly. "There's a surgeon on Bell Lane, two streets—"

"He's Warden-paid." The stranger took a step inside. The bell shivered. "They're all Warden-paid. You know that." Another step. His hand came up, and in it was a fold of something—paper, leather, she couldn't tell—wrapped tight with cord. "Three years you've been safe. I'm sorry. I ran out of road."

"Don't—" Bram's voice came out strange to her own ears. "Whatever you think I am—"

"I don't think." He was swaying now. The blood was keeping pace with him, tapping the floorboards. "I *know*. I've carried this since the Spire fell silent. It wouldn't let anyone else carry it. It's been waiting for you, Bram Joubert. It's been waiting three years."

Her name. Her whole name, spoken aloud in her shop, in a city where that name was supposed to be buried under three years of gray wool and harmless trinkets. The room tilted. Behind her, on the counter, her hand was still pressed flat over a drawing of a country that did not exist, and she felt the paper warm against her palm like a living thing.

The stranger reached the counter. He put the folded thing into her free hand—it was heavier than paper had any right to be, and it pulsed once, faintly, like a heartbeat—and then his legs gave out and she caught him, or mostly caught him, easing him down behind the counter as the rain thickened against the glass.

"The Spire remembers you," he whispered. Blood at the corner of his mouth. "Reforge it. Before the glass—"

He didn't finish. Outside, the rain kept falling on Tallow Row, patient as everything else in Corvane, and in Bram's hand the map beat once more against her fingers—alive, warm, and written in a script she had spent three years pretending she couldn't read.
