## 2. A Stranger at the Door

The rain came down on Corvane like a verdict.

Bram watched it from behind her counter, the shop dim around her, lamps turned low because low was safe. Bright windows invited eyes. Eyes invited questions. Three years in this city had taught her that light was a currency you spent carefully, and she had learned to live poor.

The shop itself was a masterpiece of harmless nothing. *Montrose's Curiosities*, said the hand-painted sign outside—her third false name in as many cities, chosen because it sounded dull enough to forget. Inside: dried lavender bundles, river-stone pendants strung on waxed cord, little clay bells that chimed when the wind found them. Charms, the sign promised. Protection against ill fortune, bad dreams, unfaithful lovers.

All of it dead. All of it stone and clay and string. She sold hope in a city that had outlawed everything else, and the hope worked exactly as well as any placebo ever had—which was to say, well enough to keep her fed.

She was restringing a pendant for the fourth time when the bell above her door rang.

"We're closed," she said without looking up. "Come back at—"

He fell through the doorway instead of walking through it.

* * *

He was tall, or he would have been if he'd been standing. As it was he caught himself on her display shelf, sent three lavender bundles and a tray of river stones clattering to the floorboards, and stayed upright by what looked like pure spite. His coat was dark with rain and darker with something else. Blood. It ran from under his arm in a slow, steady line, pooling at his heel, and Bram's first thought—not noble, not kind, but honest—was *the floorboards are old and blood stains.*

Her second thought was *Wardens.*

"Please." His voice was wrecked, gravel dragged over glass. "I need—I was told—" He pressed a hand to his side and it came away shining black in the lamplight. "You're the cartographer."

The word went through her like a blade finding the gap in armor.

"I'm a charm-seller," she said. Her voice came out flat and steady, and she was grateful to it, because her hands were not. "I sell rocks. You've got the wrong door."

"No." He shook his head, and the motion cost him; he swayed, caught the counter's edge. Up close she could see him properly now—youngish, thirty maybe, Veyli features softened by half-blood mixing, cheekbones like her own mother's family, eyes gone fever-bright. "No, I don't. I walked four days with the wrong door in my head and every one of them wrong, and then I saw your window and I knew. The way you arrange things. Symmetries. You can't help it. Cartographers see the shape of a room before they see the room."

"Get out." She moved toward him, meaning to shove him back into the rain, meaning anything except what actually happened, which was that she got close enough to smell the wound on him—copper and rot and underneath it, faintly, impossibly, the green-and-thunder scent of Veyli magic, real magic, the kind that hadn't existed in Corvane for three years because she had made very sure of it. "Whatever you're carrying, whatever they told you, I'm not—"

"The barrier is failing."

She stopped.

Outside, the rain kept falling. Somewhere down the street a Warden patrol drummed past in their iron-shod boots, four beats and a pause, four beats and a pause, and both of them stood silent until the sound faded. That silence told her more than anything he'd said. He wasn't afraid of the Wardens the way a criminal is afraid. He was afraid of them the way a man is afraid of the thing hunting him specifically, personally, by name.

"You're bleeding out in my shop," she said quietly, "telling me fairy stories."

"It's not a story." He fumbled at his coat, at some inner pocket, and she should have run then. She knew that later, turning it over in the small hours—the moment to run was then, while his hands were occupied, while the door was still an option. But her feet had rooted. Because there was a look on his face she recognized from mirrors, from three years ago, from the last day of the old world: the look of someone carrying news too large for their body.

He drew out a folded square of paper—or what she thought was paper—and held it out between them.

"Take it," he said. "Before I drop it. Please. I didn't walk four days to drop it."

Against every instinct that had kept her alive since the covenant shattered, Bram took it.

It was warm. That was the first wrongness. Paper shouldn't be warm, shouldn't hold warmth like skin, but this did, and the second wrongness came when she felt it shift against her palms—a flutter, faint as a moth's wing, as though the folded thing were breathing. The third wrongness was weight. It weighed more than paper had any right to, more than its size allowed, as though it were dense with everything drawn upon it.

"Don't open it here," the stranger whispered. "Open it where you'll know what you're seeing. Open it home."

"I don't have a home. I have a lease."

Something that might have been a laugh cracked in his chest and turned into a cough, and the cough brought up blood, bright red this time, freckling her clean counter. He slid down the front of the shelf and sat hard on her floor, back against the wall beneath the hanging clay bells, and looked up at her with those fever-bright eyes going dimmer by the second.

"Help me," she heard herself say, already moving, already dragging the stool over, already pressing her hands to the wound under his arm the way Odji had shown her a lifetime ago, back when she'd been allowed to learn field-medicine alongside field-cartography because Veyl had assumed it would always be whole. "What cut you? What did this?"

"Wardens." He laughed again, wetter. "Not the gray ones. Not your city's little book-burners. The other kind. The ones who remember what the covenant was for, and want to make sure it never comes back." His hand rose, trembling, and gripped her wrist with surprising strength. "Listen. Listen to me. There isn't time for the story. There's only time for the map."

"I don't want the map."

"Nobody wants the map." His smile was terrible. "That's how I know you're the right one. The right one never wants it."

* * *

He died the way travelers die in border towns: quickly, badly, and talking.

Bram knelt beside him on her floor with her hands stained red and listened, because there was nothing else to do, and because some traitor part of her had been starving for this—for Veyl-words, for the old cadences—for three years, and would take them however they came.

His name, he said, was Erevan Sill. He had been a courier of the Veiled Road, one of the last, running messages between the scattered enclaves of Veyl's survivors—those few thousand who had been inside the steppe-realm when the covenant shattered and the borders sealed, trapped in a land whose magic was guttering like a lamp out of oil. He had carried this map from the far settlements to Corvane because the map had insisted. Maps did that now, he said. The living ones. They chose their bearers.

"The covenant is failing," he said, and each word cost him visibly, breath by breath. "You understand what that means? Not the wall between realms. The covenant. The *agreement*. The steppe was the treaty made solid, the pledge between worlds given body—and the body is dying, and when it dies, both worlds bleed into each other. Your gray city will drown in wild magic. Our dying realm will be picked apart by yours. Everyone loses. Everything."

"Then let it fail," Bram heard herself say, and hated herself even as she said it. "Let it all fall. What did the covenant ever do for anyone? My mother died for it. Whole bloodlines erased for it. Let it rot."

Erevan Sill looked at her with pity, which was somehow worse than anger.

"That's why it has to be you," he said. "Someone who hates it enough to be honest about what it costs. Someone who loved it enough to learn every mile of it." His grip tightened on her wrist. "The Spire remembers you. Do you hear me? The Covenant Spire remembers your hands. It's been waiting three years for a cartographer of the founding line to come home."

"I'm not—my mother was the cartographer, not me, I just—"

"You just mapped Veyl at nineteen years old," he said, "finer than anyone since the founders. You think we don't know? You think the maps forgot?" Blood flecked his lips. "The Spire remembers you, Bram Joubert. Go home. Reforge it. Or watch both worlds burn slow."

Then his eyes fixed on something past her shoulder—nothing, the empty air, the dark window—and he exhaled once, long, like a man setting down a burden carried across four days and forty years, and did not inhale again.

* * *

Afterward, Bram sat on the floor of her shop with a dead stranger's head in her lap and the warm folded thing in her hand, and did not move for a long time.

She catalogued the problems, because cataloguing was what she did instead of panicking. Problem one: a dead man on her floor. Problem two: blood on her counter, her floorboards, her hands. Problem three: whatever was in the folded thing, which pulsed gently against her palm like a second heartbeat. Problem four, largest of all: somewhere out in the rain, the people who had cut him open were looking for what he'd carried.

And problem five, the one she circled back to no matter how she tried to reorder the list: he had known her name. Her true name. The name she'd buried under three years of Montrose and two names before that. Nobody in Corvane knew it. Nobody alive outside Veyl should have known it at all.

The past had found her. It had walked through her door bleeding and dying, and it had put itself in her hands, and it was warm.

"Damn you," she said softly, to Erevan Sill, to her mother, to the whole ruined architecture of fate. "Damn every one of you."

She laid him out straight, closed his eyes, covered his face with a linen cloth. Practicalities. She could be practical. She fetched the bucket and the brush and scrubbed the counter while her mind screamed at her to stop stalling, and when the floor was as clean as old wood ever got, she lit the lamp at her worktable, sat down, and unfolded the map.

* * *

It opened like a flower.

There was no other way to describe it. The folds loosened at her touch—not unfolding so much as *blooming*, creases smoothing themselves flat, the square spreading across her table until it filled it corner to corner, and the ink began to move.

Veyl-script. True Veyl-script, the flowing root-and-river lettering that predated Corvane's alphabet by a thousand years, the script that had been outlawed, burned, and beaten out of every schoolbook in the gray city. Most people alive couldn't read it. Most people alive had never seen it.

Bram read it the way she read her own heartbeat.

*To the daughter of Ilse Joubert, who bound the western reach. To the hands that drew the steppe true. Come home. The veil thins at the Spire. The glass remembers. We are ending.*

Below the words, the drawing itself: the glass steppe rendered in silver ink that shimmered when she breathed on it, every ridge and resonance-line she had once charted at nineteen, and beyond its edge—Corvane, her adopted city, drawn in the same living ink. And between them, where the border should have stood as a clean firm line, the ink was *fraying*. Threads of silver lifting off the page, drifting, dissolving. As she watched, a hairline crack spread through the border region with a sound she felt more than heard, a tiny crystalline tick, like ice settling on a pond.

The map was showing her the future. Or the present. She honestly couldn't tell which was worse.

And there—she leaned closer, heart hammering—in the margin, in a hand she knew better than her own, small and sharp and unmistakable after all these years: her mother's handwriting. *If the veil fails, the third path remains. Look for my notes at the Spire. Forgive me what it will cost.*

Bram shoved back from the table so fast the stool went over.

"No," she said aloud, to the map, to the room, to the dead man under the linen. "No. I'm done. I paid. I paid more than anyone, and I'm *done*."

She snatched the map off the table—it writhed in her grip, folding itself against her fingers like a captured bird—and carried it to the hearth, where embers still glowed from the morning's fire. She fed it in. Watched the flame take it. Felt something in her chest loosen, finally, finally—

The map reformed in her hands.

One moment ash and curling ember; the next, cool paper pressed against her palms, whole and unburnt, warm as skin, pulsing with that patient second heartbeat. On its face, the fraying border had spread another inch. In the margin, beneath her mother's note, new letters were surfacing through the silver ink like fish rising in a pond, written in a script she recognized as her own:

*Bram. Please.*

She stood in the lamplight holding the impossible thing, shaking, ash still drifting down around her from a fire that had failed to consume it, and understood with cold clarity that denial was no longer on the menu. The map would not burn. The courier had not stopped walking. Her mother's handwriting was rising out of the ink to beg.

Three years of hiding, and the past had simply waited until tonight, and knocked.

A knock sounded at her door.

Bram went rigid. Through the rain-streaked window she could see nothing but darkness and the streetlamp's weak halo—but the bell above the door hung perfectly still, and yet she had heard, distinctly, three knocks. Slow. Deliberate. Patient as the map's pulse in her hands.

Then, through the wood, a voice she knew. A voice she had last heard three years ago, saying goodbye in a way that had felt like both of them dying a little.

"Bram," said Sef. "I need to talk to you about the man who just died in your shop."
