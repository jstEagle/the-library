## 4. The Map That Bleeds

The map did not sleep.

Bram had told herself it would. She'd wrapped it in oilcloth, buried it under a false floorboard beneath the counter where she kept the worthless charms—copper rings sold as luck-binders, knotted thread sold as fever-wards—and she'd gone upstairs and lain in her narrow bed above the shop listening to Corvane's rain against the shutters, telling herself that by morning the thing would be only paper again.

It was not paper. It had never been paper.

She knew this the way you know a heartbeat is yours: not by looking, but by feeling. All night the floorboards above it had hummed faintly, a frequency just below hearing, like a struck tuning fork pressed to bone. Twice she'd risen to check the locks on her door, which were already checked. Once she'd stood at the window watching the street below, half-expecting Wardens in their ash-gray coats to come pouring out of the dark with lanterns and irons.

They hadn't come. Not yet.

By gray morning light she gave up pretending and went down, pried up the board, and unwrapped the map on her worktable with the grim tenderness of someone handling a wound.

It lay there, folded into eighths, and even folded it moved. A slow pulse traveled through the creases, like breath through a sleeping animal's flank. The parchment—no, not parchment; she'd touched it once and it had been warm, blood-warm—was inked in Veyl-script, the flowing cartographer's hand she'd learned at her mother's knee before she could write her own name in Common. Lines of it crawled along the borders of the drawn world, annotating, measuring, *counting down*.

She unfolded it.

The city of Corvane spread across the lower third, rendered in fine gray strokes: the aqueduct, the Warden citadel, the crooked lanes of the charm-quarter where she'd spent three years being no one. But the gray was wrong this morning. Along the eastern edge of the city, near the old tannery district, thin veins of white had spread through the streets like frost climbing a windowpane. Glass. She recognized it instantly, with a lurch that had nothing to do with memory and everything to do with dread—the glass of the steppe, the petrified covenant, reaching into the world of iron and law.

And past the city's edge, where the steppe began, the border line itself was drawn in something that looked like a hairline crack in fired clay. Running along it, in her mother's hand—or a hand so like it that Bram's throat closed—were words in Veyl-script she could read as easily as breathing:

*Thinning. Thinning. Thinning.*

Below that, smaller, almost an afterthought:

*Where it fails, it spreads.*

Bram sat down hard on her stool.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty shop, to the map, to whoever had died on her floor two nights ago pressing this thing into her hands. "No, I didn't ask for this. I sold trinkets. I was careful. I was *careful for three years*."

The map pulsed. Patient. Indifferent to her grief.

She thought about the courier then—not his face, which she'd barely seen, but his hands. Burned, both of them, the skin split and weeping, as if he'd carried something alive for too long. He'd known what he was carrying. He'd run from wherever he'd found it all the way to a charm-seller's back-alley shop in Corvane because somewhere, somehow, he'd learned that a descendant of the original cartographers lived here. *The Spire remembers you*, he'd said, and died with his blood soaking into her floorboards, and she'd scrubbed that floor until her knees ached and still told herself it meant nothing, that anyone could say anything with their last breath, that dying men grasped at straws—

But the glass on the map was spreading toward the tannery district, and last night, walking home from Odji's clinic with her collar up, she'd seen it. A shimmer in a puddle that wasn't rainwater. A cobbler's step gone strangely smooth and reflective where everyone knew the cobblestones were rough. She'd told herself it was ice. It was late spring. There was no ice.

"You're lying to me," she told the map. Her voice cracked on the second word. "Or I'm lying to me. One of us is."

The map did not answer, but the white veins crept another finger's width while she watched.

* * *

She tried to burn it that afternoon.

Not in the shop—she wasn't a fool, whatever else she was. She took it wrapped in oilcloth to the lime-kilns behind the dyers' row, where the heat came off the mouths of the kilns in visible waves and nobody asked questions because everybody there was doing something illegal or nearly so. She bought a moment alone by paying a kiln-tender too much for a cup of bad tea, and when he shuffled off she held the folded map over the open mouth of the nearest kiln.

The heat should have taken it instantly. Oilcloth first, smoking, then the map itself curling to ash.

Instead the map grew cold in her hands. Cold as riverbed stone in winter, so cold her fingers ached, and the pulse beneath the folds quickened—not frightened, she realized. *Offended.*

"Stop it," she whispered. "Just be paper. Please. Just be paper and let me be nothing."

She thrust it into the fire anyway.

The flames bent around it. She watched, mouth open, as the fire parted like water around a stone, licking at the air an inch from the oilcloth without ever touching it, and the kiln-tender twenty feet away saw nothing amiss because the magic made sure no one would see, because the magic was *protecting itself*, or protecting her, or—worst thought of all—protecting its own purpose, and Bram snatched it back before the man turned around, heart slamming, and walked away fast with her hood up and the map cold against her chest like a second heart.

* * *

That evening she tried water. The rain barrel behind her shop, black and still. She weighted the map with a brick and pushed it under, and stood there in the dark watching bubbles rise, and felt—this was the part that undid her—she felt it *looking back up at her*. Not with eyes. With intent. The way a dog watches you from the bottom of a well.

She left it there for three hours. When she hauled it out, dripping, the ink was brighter than before. The white veins in the tannery district had reached the aqueduct.

And there—she crouched closer, wet map trembling in her hands—there was new writing. Fresh strokes, still glistening, appearing letter by letter in that hand like her mother's hand, in Veyl-script:

*You cannot un-know what you know, daughter-of-the-line. The barrier does not care whether you believe in it. It is failing whether you watch or sleep. Come home.*

"I don't have a home," Bram said. Her voice came out small and vicious. "I had one. You people burned it out of me. I have a shop full of lies and a floor with a dead man's ghost in it and—" 

Her voice broke entirely.

"—and I was *happy*," she finished, barely audible. "I was almost happy."

The map lay in her lap, warm now, pulsing gently, and if she didn't know better she'd have sworn it was sorry.

* * *

She didn't sleep. Around the coldest hour of night she lit one candle and studied the thing properly for the first time, cartographer to cartographer, because if she couldn't destroy it she could at least *read* it, and reading was the one weapon she'd never surrendered.

The steppe filled the upper two-thirds. She'd mapped it once herself, at nineteen, young and arrogant and brilliant, walking the glass with surveyor's instruments and her mother's old star-charts, believing the covenant was eternal because she'd never known a world without it. Now she traced the border crack with one fingertip and understood what she was seeing, and understanding was worse than ignorance.

The steppe wasn't land. It had never been land. It was the covenant itself—the treaty between realms, forged in living memory and sealed in glass, a wall built out of everything the first cartographers had agreed to forget. And it was cracking, and where it cracked, the glass leaked into Corvane like water through a failing dam, and on the far side—she tilted the map to the candlelight—on the far side of the crack, tiny figures had been inked near the border. Fading. Rendered in strokes so pale they were barely there at all, as if the map itself was losing the ability to hold them.

Veyli. Her mother's people. Dying on the other side of a wall her family had helped build.

"Come home," she repeated bitterly. "Come home to what? To fix your wall? To die for it?"

Then she saw the third annotation. Small, tucked into the fold-crease near the Spire, in handwriting that was *not* her mother's—sharper, hurried, the courier's own dying scrawl translated somehow into script:

*If the bearer will not come, the glass will come to her.*

Bram set the map down very carefully, the way you set down something venomous, and looked at her hands. Steady. Cartographer's hands. Hands that had once drawn worlds.

Outside, far off toward the tannery district, something glinted. Not lamplight. Lamplight flickered; this gleamed steady and cold, a long smooth streak down a street that should have been cobblestone, catching the moon like the spine of a fish.

Glass. In her city. On her street's horizon.

She had spent three years learning to be no one, and the world had spent those same three years refusing to let the lesson take. The past hadn't found her by accident. It had been crawling toward her the whole time, patient as frost, and now it was in her streets, and every hour she denied it the white veins crept further, and the courier's burned hands rose unbidden behind her eyes.

She couldn't burn it. She couldn't drown it. She couldn't unread it.

Which left one thing she'd sworn never to do, and the thought of it sat in her chest like swallowed glass: she would have to find someone who still remembered how the covenant worked. Someone disgraced, underground, dangerous to know.

Someone like Odji.

Bram looked at the map one last time—at the fading figures beyond the crack, at the word *home* in her mother's hand—and blew out the candle.

In the dark, the map pulsed on, keeping its own time, counting down.
