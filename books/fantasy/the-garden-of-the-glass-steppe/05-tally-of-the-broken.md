## 5. Tally of the Broken

The clinic was where Odji had always said it would be: nowhere.

Bram found it by walking past it three times, as instructed. The entrance was a dead-end alley off Tanner's Row, behind a shuttered tannery that hadn't processed a hide in a decade. The door was a slab of rusted iron with no handle and no lock, which should have meant nothing was inside. Instead, when she pressed her palm flat against the metal—Odji's instruction, too—it gave way like a held breath released.

Warm air rolled out, smelling of boiled willow bark, lamp oil, and something older underneath: the green, rain-soaked scent of Veyl. Bram stood in the doorway with her heart hammering and let herself breathe it in for one stolen second before she stepped through and pulled the door shut behind her.

She hadn't smelled home in three years.

The stairwell descended farther than the building above could possibly have allowed. Corvane was full of architecture like this—spaces that didn't match their shells, left over from the years when magic had been legal and builders had gotten ambitious. Now the city pretended those places didn't exist, the way it pretended a lot of things didn't exist.

At the bottom of the stairs, lamplight spilled across a long cellar room. It had been a cistern once; the arched ceiling wept mineral stains down the walls like old tears. Cots lined both sides of the space, two dozen of them, most occupied. A woman with bandaged hands slept sitting up. A boy no older than Pip's age—no, she didn't know any Pips yet, she corrected herself, the courier's face still haunted her—a boy with a fever lay shivering under three blankets while an apprentice pressed cool cloths to his forehead. At the far end, behind a curtain of dyed canvas, someone moaned low and rhythmic, the sound of pain being endured rather than treated, because treatment cost more than anyone here had.

And moving between the cots, sleeves rolled to the elbow, hands stained yellow-green with some poultice or other, was Odji.

He looked worse than he had three years ago. Thinner. His hair, which had been black shot through with silver when they'd last spoken, was now mostly silver, cropped short in the Veyli mourning style he'd sworn he'd never adopt. But his hands were the same—long-fingered, precise, moving over a sleeping man's chest with the unhurried confidence of someone who knew exactly where everything was supposed to be.

He saw her before she'd made it halfway down the room. He didn't stop working. He just said, quietly, in accented Corvani, "You're late."

"You said come after dark."

"It's been dark for an hour." He finished whatever he was doing, drew the blanket up over the sleeping man's shoulders, and beckoned her toward the back of the room with two fingers. "Walk. Don't gawk. Gawkers make my patients nervous, and nervous patients die on me out of spite."

She walked. A few of the awake ones watched her pass—watched her *clothes*, she realized, the good wool coat, the shopkeeper's neatness—and looked away again. She was used to being invisible. Here, she was conspicuous, and she hated how much she'd missed being seen.

* * *

Odji's workspace was a alcove behind the canvas curtain, screened from the worst of the moaning. A table cluttered with mortars, glass vials, and a scale whose weights were coins from four different currencies. A cot in the corner with a blanket that had clearly never been slept under. On the wall above it, pinned with a bent nail, hung a strip of woven grass—the kind of charm every Veyli child learned to braid before they learned their letters.

Not a charm. A reminder. In Corvane, displaying one would get him arrested. He kept it anyway.

"Sit," he said, dragging a stool out with his foot. "Before you fall down. You look like you've been carrying something heavy for three days."

"I've been carrying something heavy for three years. It got heavier Tuesday night."

"So I heard." He poured water from a clay jug into a cup and pushed it into her hands. "A man died in the Lantern Quarter. Warden patrol found him in an alley behind a charm-shop. They ruled it knife-work, common robbery." His eyes came up to hers, steady and unreadable. "But the body was gone by morning. Wardens don't move bodies fast unless there's something about them they don't want seen. And the shop the alley backed onto—" he let the silence sit, then finished it himself— "was yours."

Bram set the cup down without drinking. "You have ears everywhere."

"I have patients everywhere. Sick people hear everything. Nobody guards their tongue around a dying man." He sat down across from her, elbows on knees, and for a moment the healer's briskness fell away and she saw what was underneath: fear, worn smooth by use, like a tool handle. "Tell me what he gave you."

"How do you know he gave me anything?"

"Because you're here." He spread his stained hands. "Three years, Bram. Three years I've told you the covenant is failing and you've told me I'm being dramatic. You crossed half the city tonight to sit in a hole full of plague and broken bones because a stranger died outside your door. So. What did he give you?"

She reached into her coat.

The map had spent two days folded in the inner pocket, wrapped in linen, weighted down with a river stone she'd taken from her windowsill for no reason she could name except that it felt right. She unwrapped it now, unfolded it, and laid it flat on the table between them.

Odji went very still.

The map breathed. There was no better word for it. The parchment rose and fell in a rhythm too slow to be wind, too regular to be imagination, and beneath its surface the ink moved—not flowing, not shifting, but *circulating*, the rivers of Veyl-script running their courses like blood through veins. Where the script pooled thickest, at the map's center, a shape had emerged overnight that hadn't been there when Bram first opened it: a tower, drawn in strokes so fine they might have been made with a single hair, rising out of the steppe like a needle out of cloth.

"The Covenant Spire," Odji whispered.

"You can read it?"

"A little. Enough." He didn't touch it. His hands hovered above the parchment, trembling slightly, and Bram understood—he was afraid to touch it, the way you're afraid to touch a bird that might be only stunned. "This is living cartography. True work. I haven't seen true work since—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Since your mother."

The name landed in Bram's chest like a dropped stone. She let it sit there. She'd had practice.

"The man who brought it to me," she said. "He was Veyli. Dying already—I think the crossing did it, or whatever carried him through. He said—" Her voice caught, and she hated that, and pushed through anyway. "He said *the Spire remembers you*. Then he died. And the Wardens came, and I ran, and I've been running for two days trying to convince myself I imagined all of it." She turned the map so Odji could see the edges, where the ink had begun to fray into gray. "I can't. Look at the borders. Tell me I'm imagining that."

Odji leaned close. Along the map's rim, where the boundary between realms should have been drawn as a clean unbroken line, the line had gone sick. It stuttered. It doubled back on itself. In three places it simply stopped, and beyond the stopping point the parchment wasn't parchment anymore but something thin and translucent and faintly glassy, and through it Bram could see—she had checked eleven times, hoping to be wrong—Corvane streets. Tanner's Row. The Lantern Quarter. Her own shop's roofline, rendered in miniature, sitting on the far side of a border that was supposed to hold it out.

"The barrier is failing," Odji said. No drama in it now. Just diagnosis. "It's been failing since the covenant broke. Slowly, the way a wound fails—you know, seeps, scars over, seeps again. For three years the scar has been holding. It isn't holding anymore." He tapped the glassy patch with one fingernail, gently, the way you'd tap a bruise. "Where the barrier thins, the steppe bleeds through. Glass in the streets. Cold spots. Dreams that aren't yours. You've heard the reports same as I have. The Wardens call it contamination. Burn the affected blocks, relocate the survivors, pretend it's contained."

"And it isn't."

"And it isn't. Contamination spreads from the steppe side, yes. But it *starts* from ours." He straightened up and looked at her with an expression she couldn't parse until he spoke again. "Bram. Do you know why the covenant needed cartographers? Not mages. Cartographers."

"Because a border is a drawing," she said. The answer came up from somewhere below memory, somewhere her mother's voice still lived. "A border is a line someone agreed to draw. Magic is just ink. The agreement is the paper."

"Your mother taught you well." Something moved through his face—grief, or pride, or both wearing each other's clothes. "When the covenant shattered, the lines shattered with it. But lines can be redrawn. That's what the Spire is. Not a prison. Not a wall. A *drafting table*. The place where the original accord was written into the world. And it doesn't open for mages, or armies, or Wardens." He laid his hand flat on the table, beside the map but not touching it, and met her eyes. "It opens for the hand that holds the pen. The bloodline of the original cartographers. Your bloodline, Bram. Yours."

The cellar sounds receded—the moaning, the boiling pots, the drip of the weeping walls—all of it fading under the roar of her own pulse.

"No," she said.

"Bram—"

"*No.* My mother is dead. Erased, actually, which is worse than dead, because at least dead people get graves. Whatever she built, it killed her, and whatever the Spire wants, it can want it from someone else. I sell buttons and lucky horseshoes to people who can't afford either. That's my whole life. That's what I have." She was standing now; she didn't remember standing. "I didn't ask for this map. I didn't ask for any of it."

"No," Odji agreed mildly. "Nobody asks. That's rather the definition."

"Don't be clever with me."

"I'm a disgraced healer running an illegal clinic under a tannery. Cleverness is the only thing I have left." But there was no bite in it. He rose too, slowly, joints cracking, and stood on the far side of the table with the breathing map between them like a third person neither of them wanted to look at directly. When he spoke again, his voice had gone quiet. "May I tell you a tally? Since you came to a clinic. We keep tallies here."

She didn't answer. He took it as permission.

"Forty-one patients under this roof tonight. Nine of them are sick in ways Corvani medicine can't name, because it isn't Corvani sickness. It's bleed-through. Glass-lung, we call it. They breathe fine air and cough frost. Two will die this week no matter what I do. Last month it was one. The month before, none." He held up a second finger. "In the Lantern Quarter, the Wardens burned six buildings on Fallow Street because frost grew on the inside of the windows. Eleven families displaced. One grandmother arrested for 'harboring contamination,' which means harboring Veyli blood, which means existing. Third tally mark: the crossings are getting easier. Things come through now that aren't people. Small things. Hungry things. My apprentices won't walk the lower tunnels alone anymore."

He lowered his hand.

"I could go on. I have pages of tallies. But here is the last one, and it's the one that matters." He turned his palm up, open, an old healer's gesture: *see, there is nothing hidden in it.* "Every week the barrier thins, more of my people sicken, and more of yours burn. Both realms are bleeding out of the same wound. Sealing it saves one and kills the other. Breaking it kills both slower. Those are the only choices anyone has offered in three years." His eyes flicked down to the map, to the needle-thin tower at its center. "Unless somebody walks into that Spire and draws a new line. And there is exactly one person alive who can."

The silence stretched. Somewhere beyond the curtain, the feverish boy's breathing had gone easier; the apprentice hummed tunelessly as she worked.

"I sold charms today," Bram said finally, absurdly. "Little glass birds. A woman bought one for her daughter's birthday. She asked me if it would bring her luck and I said luck is mostly showing up." She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I'm not my mother, Odji. She was—she was the best of us. I ran. I hid for three years behind a counter selling lies to people who wanted them to be true. What makes you think there's anything in me worth a whole realm?"

Odji came around the table. He took her hands—her clean shopkeeper's hands, his poultice-stained ones—and held them the way he must have held a thousand patients' hands, firmly, without ceremony, as if hands were simply things that were meant to be held.

"When you walked in tonight," he said, "you counted my patients. Don't argue, I watched your eyes do it. Twenty-three cots, forty-one souls, you had the number before you reached me. That's what cartographers do. They count what's in the territory. They refuse to leave anyone off the map." His grip tightened. "Your mother drew worlds. You, I think, draw the people in them. The Spire doesn't need the best of us, child. It needs someone who'll stand in front of a wound and refuse to look away."

Bram's throat was too tight to speak. She looked down at their joined hands, and at the map beside them, and saw that the ink had changed again—the frayed gray edges had darkened, the stuttering border-line steadied, as if the mere fact of her decision, not yet made, had already begun to mend it.

Or as if it were listening.

"The map led him to me," she said at last. "The courier. Which means it can lead others. If I can find it, the Wardens can find it. Find *me*."

"That," said Odji, "is the other thing we need to discuss. Because if you mean to do this—if there's even a chance you mean to do this—you can't stay in Corvane, and you can't cross the steppe alone, and you cannot, under any circumstances, trust the Wardens." He released her hands and began rolling down his sleeves, brisk again, decisions already assembling themselves behind his eyes. "There's a market that opens at midnight. Under the old aqueduct, past the tannery district. Everything Corvane outlawed, sold in the dark by people who have nowhere else to be. If anyone knows a way across the glass, they'll be there."

"Now?" Bram said. "Tonight?"

"Tonight. The barrier won't wait for a convenient hour, and neither will the Wardens." He snuffed the lamp over his workspace and pulled a hooded cloak from a hook, throwing her a second one. "Stay close, touch nothing, and if anyone asks, you're my niece and you're simple."

"I am *not*—"

"Perfect. You're learning to lie like a local." He lifted the canvas curtain and paused, glancing back at her with an expression that was almost, nearly, a smile. "For what it's worth—I'm glad you came, Bram. I'd begun to think I'd tend these tallies alone."

Above them, faint through the weeping stone, a bell began to ring. Not the midnight bell—it was too early, and it rang wrong, fast and ragged, the alarm pattern the Wardens used when they were sweeping a quarter.

Odji's face went grim. "That's Tanner's Row," he said. "They're close. Hood up. We go now."
