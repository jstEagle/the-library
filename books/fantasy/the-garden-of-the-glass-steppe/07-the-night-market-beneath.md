## 7. The Night Market Beneath

The map had been folded into Bram's coat for six days, and she could feel it breathing.

That was the word for it. Not pulsing, not shifting—breathing. A slow expansion against her ribs when the shop went quiet, a contraction when the Wardens passed in the street. It slept when she slept. It woke when she dreamed of glass. She had tried burning it on the second night, and it had reformed between her fingers like a moth shaking off soot, and after that she'd stopped trying to kill it and started trying to understand it, which was its own kind of surrender.

"You're late," Odji said.

He was waiting at the mouth of Tanner's Run with a lantern hooded to a coin of light, his gray braid tucked under a fishmonger's cap that fooled no one. He looked the way he always looked—tired, amused, faintly disappointed in the world—and beneath that, the way only she seemed able to see now: frightened.

"The patrol changed routes," Bram said. "I had to go around by the tannery."

"I know. I watched you go around." He lifted the lantern. "Come down before someone else watches."

The entrance to the night market was a drainage grate behind a shuttered dye-house, and it smelled exactly as glamorous as that suggested. Odji went first, boots finding rungs worn smooth by decades of feet that didn't want to be counted. Bram followed, one hand pressed flat over the map, and the dark closed above them like water.

She had heard rumors of the market beneath Corvane for three years—every charm-seller had—but rumors were a currency she'd never been able to afford. Rumors led to questions. Questions led to Wardens. So she had sold her harmless trinkets and kept her head down and told herself the city under the city was a story people told to feel less alone.

It was not a story.

The tunnel opened into a vaulted cistern the size of a cathedral, and the cistern was full of light. Hundreds of lanterns hung from iron chains at every height, some swaying, none casting quite the same color—amber, green, a violet that made her teeth ache in a way she hadn't felt since Veyl. Stalls climbed the walls in wooden tiers, selling things that had no business existing in an outlawed city: bottled birdsong, keys cut for locks that hadn't been built yet, rain collected from storms that hadn't happened. The crowd moved through it all in masks and hoods and half-faces, and nobody looked directly at anybody, and somehow that was the most courteous thing Bram had seen since she came to Corvane.

"Odji," said a woman at the first stall, without looking up from a scale she was weighing nothing on. "You brought a stranger."

"I brought a customer," Odji said. "There's a difference. Strangers are free."

Bram's skin prickled. The map had gone warm against her ribs—not alarm, exactly. Interest. Like a dog lifting its head.

They moved deeper into the cistern, past a fortune-teller whose cards were small squares of mirror, past children selling roasted chestnuts that steamed in colors. And everywhere, threaded under the noise of commerce, Bram began to hear it: a sound she had spent three years pretending she'd forgotten. Faint, fractured, coming up through the stone floor itself.

Singing.

Thin and broken, like a bell rung underwater. But singing.

She stopped walking. "Odji. Do you hear that?"

He watched her face with something that wasn't surprise. "Hear what?"

"That. Under the floor. Like—" She struggled. "Like glass. Like a lot of glass, very far away, all trying to be in tune."

Something moved through the market then—a stillness, spreading outward from where she stood the way ripples spread. Conversations faltered. Lantern-light seemed to lean toward her. A dozen hooded faces turned, not fully, just enough.

Odji took her elbow gently and drew her into the shadow of a pillar stacked with crates of contraband candles. "Three years," he said quietly, "you've kept that ear of yours shut. I wondered if you still could hear it at all."

"Hear what? What is it?"

"Corvane is built on the steppe's edge. Everyone knows that. What they don't know is that the barrier isn't a wall, girl. It's a membrane. And membranes thin." His eyes were steady and sad. "What you're hearing is the far side. Leaking through. And if you can hear it here, in the deepest part of the old city, then it's thinner than even I thought."

The map pulsed once against her ribs, warm as a held hand.

"We need a guide," Bram said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Across the steppe. To the Spire. Someone who knows the glass."

"I know," Odji said. "Why do you think I brought you to the one place in Corvane where such a person might exist?"

* * *

The guide turned out to be a thief.

Odji asked three people before he got a straight answer, and each time the answer came wrapped in the same expression—the particular wince of someone about to recommend a bad idea. The name, when it finally surfaced, was given by a knife-grinder who wouldn't say it aloud, only write it on a scrap of paper and burn it in his own brazier immediately after.

*Pip.*

"Pip," Odji repeated, reading the ash. "Of course it's Pip."

"You know her?"

"I've stitched her back together twice. Once for a knife wound, once for something she refuses to describe." He sighed. "She's maybe sixteen. She lives in the walls. She steals from everyone, including me, including you, probably including this conversation while we're having it. Check your pockets."

Bram checked her pockets. Her spare coin purse was gone. In its place was a folded scrap of paper bearing a single charcoal-drawn eye, and beneath it, in a spidery hand: *you talk loud.*

"She knows we're looking for her," Odji translated.

"Is that good or bad?"

"With Pip? Unknowable. Come on. There's a place she haunts when she wants to be found."

The place was a dead fountain at the edge of the cistern, dry for a century, its basin cracked into a mosaic of green-stained tiles. Someone had strung it with stolen lanterns and cushions that had been stolen more recently, and in the middle of it, cross-legged atop the fountain's central statue—a weatherworn mermaid missing both arms—sat a slight figure wrapped in three coats, eating an apple.

Pip was small and sharp-boned, with hair cropped raggedly short and eyes the color of wet slate. She finished the apple, considered them upside down from her perch, and tossed the core at Odji, who caught it without looking.

"You're the healer," she said. Her voice was quick and light, a pickpocket's voice. "And you're the one who talks loud." Her gaze fixed on Bram with unsettling directness. "You're the one who hears it."

"Hears what?" Bram said carefully.

"Don't do that." Pip dropped from the statue, landing soft as a cat, close enough that Bram caught the smell of coal smoke and apples. "Don't do the thing where you pretend. Everyone down here pretends, it's the whole economy. But you *heard* it. Back there. You stopped walking and your face did a thing, and everybody saw, because you talk loud even when you're quiet." She tilted her head. "So. What'd it sound like?"

Bram hesitated. Lying to this girl felt like lying to a blade.

"Bells," she said. "Underwater. Broken ones."

Pip's whole face changed. The wariness didn't leave, but something underneath it shifted—recognition, hunger, relief, all fighting for space. "Broken bells," she whispered. "Yeah. Yeah, that's it exactly. Nobody else gets it right. They say wind, they say pipes, they say I'm cracked." She stepped closer. "But it's bells. And lately they've been going *wrong*. Faster. Higher. Like something's winding down." Her slate eyes narrowed. "You know why."

It wasn't a question. The map chose that moment to pulse again, hard enough that Bram pressed a hand to her ribs, and Pip's gaze snapped to the motion like a hawk's.

"You've got a piece of it on you," Pip breathed. "A piece of the song. I can hear it humming through your coat. What *is* that?"

"A map," Bram said.

"A map that hums."

"A living map. From Veyl."

The word landed in the dry fountain like a stone in still water. Pip went very still. Around them, Bram noticed, the nearest stalls had gone quiet again—that ripple of attention, the market listening.

"My gran was Veyli," Pip said at last, and her voice had lost its flippancy entirely. "Before the Purge. She used to sing songs in the old script and say they were counting songs. Counting what, she never said." She looked at Bram, and there was something raw and young under all the armor. "If you're going across the glass—and you are, anyone carrying that thing is going across the glass—I'm coming."

"Why?" Odji asked mildly. "You don't know her. This is suicide with extra steps."

"Because I hear the bells going wrong," Pip snapped, "and everyone I ask says I'm cracked, and *she* heard them too, first try, no questions." She jabbed a finger at Bram. "That's worth more than knowing somebody. That's worth everything down here."

Bram studied her. Every instinct honed by three years of hiding said: *too young, too wild, too loud, a liability.* Every instinct had also kept her alone in a gray shop for three years, selling lies to strangers, dying by inches.

"You stole my purse," Bram said.

"Obviously."

"If you come with us, you stop stealing from us. We watch each other's backs or we don't go. And when I say run, you run. No arguments, no heroics."

Pip's grin was sudden and feral and, despite everything, bright. "Deal. Mostly."

"*Pip.*"

"Fine. Entirely. When you say run." She spat in her palm and held it out, and after a heartbeat Bram took it, and the bargain was struck the way bargains were struck beneath Corvane—messy, binding, real.

"One condition of mine," Pip added, swinging her pack onto her shoulder—it was already packed; Bram would wonder later how long she'd been waiting. "We go tonight. The bells have been getting worse every day, and I want to hear them right before they stop being anything at all."

* * *

They left through the market's far gate, into tunnels Odji clearly knew better than he admitted, and it was somewhere in the third turning, in the dark between two lanterns, that Pip went rigid and grabbed Bram's arm.

"Listen," she whispered.

Bram listened. Footsteps? Warden boots? No—

"There," Pip breathed. "Do you hear it? It changed."

And Bram, straining, caught it: the broken bells beneath the city, the song leaking through the thinning membrane—and threaded through it now, unmistakable, a new note. Not wrong. Not broken.

*Answering.*

The map burned hot against her ribs, and far above them, muffled by stone and street, a bell in a Warden tower began to ring the alarm.

Odji's face in the lantern light had gone grim. "They've found something," he said. "Or someone."

Bram thought of Sef—of the way he'd let her walk away, and the lie that must have cost him—and her blood ran cold.

"It's us," she said. "They've found us."
