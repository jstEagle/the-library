## 9. Out Through the Ash

The fires started in the Tanner's Quarter and ate their way uphill toward us like something with an appetite.

I stood in the doorway of Odji's clinic and watched the sky turn the color of a bruise. Embers rode the wind over the rooftops, settling on awnings, on laundry lines, on the shoulders of people who had stopped pretending they had anywhere to be. Somewhere below us, Wardens were going door to door with lanterns and long iron pikes, flushing out the map-bearer the way you'd smoke a hive of bees.

"The aqueduct," Odji said behind me. He was already moving through his clinic, pressing bandages and a clay jar of poppy resin into Pip's arms, not looking at any of it, trusting her to hold what he couldn't carry. "The old Veyli channel under Foundry Hill. It runs dry for six miles and comes up past the cisterns. If we're quick."

"If we're quick," I repeated. It was all anyone said anymore. *If we're quick. If we're quiet. If we live.*

Pip appeared at my elbow, small and sharp-eyed, the map's case strapped across her chest where I'd told her never to let it leave. She was chewing on something that might have been leather or might have been fear. "They're burning the Night Market first," she said. "I heard the runners. They're starting there because they think we'll go back for it."

"Would we?"

"No." She spat. "But it felt rude not to consider."

Odji came out last, carrying nothing but his healer's satchel and a look on his face I recognized from three years ago, from the night he'd been dragged out of the Healers' Conclave with his credentials torn in half — the look of a man leaving a life behind and refusing to let his face know about it.

"Foundry Hill," he said. "Stay off the main streets. And Bram—" He caught my arm. His hands were steady; his voice wasn't, quite. "Whatever happens down there, the map matters more than any of us. You understand that. Even more than me."

"I understand that," I lied, because it was what he needed to hear, and because some part of me knew it was true, and hated that it was true, and would have traded every soul in Corvane for it not to be.

* * *

We went down through the city's bones.

The aqueduct gate was a rusted hatch behind a tannery that no longer existed except as heat shimmer and ash. Odji knew the lock — of course he did; half the underground of Corvane owed him stitches or silence — and we dropped into a stone throat that smelled of old water and older magic.

The channel was Veyli work. I felt it before I saw it: the faint hum in the masonry, the way the dark seemed less like absence and more like held breath. My mother's people had built these channels when the two realms still touched, carrying clean water from the steppe's hidden springs into the gray city that would one day outlaw them. Three years I'd walked above this without knowing. Or knowing, and choosing not to.

The map stirred against Pip's chest. In the dark I could see the seam of the case glowing faintly, green-gold, like a firefly in a jar.

"Keep it quiet," I whispered.

"It's not me making noise," Pip hissed back. "It's *singing*. It's been singing since the fires started. It knows where we're going."

"Maps don't know things."

"Then why's it singing?"

I didn't have an answer, so I put my hand flat against the case until the glow dimmed. Under my palm, the leather was warm. Warm like skin. Like something alive that had been running beside us this whole time and wanted, very badly, to get home.

We walked. Six miles of walking in the dark, ankle-deep in places where the last rain had pooled, our footsteps swallowed by stone. Odji counted the side-channels aloud in Veyli script, an old cartographer's habit I hadn't taught him and didn't ask how he'd learned. Pip ranged ahead like a ferret, listening at junctions. Twice she held up a fist and we stood motionless while boots passed overhead, iron-shod, unhurried, the sound of men who had all night and permission to use it.

Somewhere around the fourth mile, the air changed.

Not the temperature. The *texture*. The way it does when glass is near — that faint sweetness, that pressure behind the eyes. I stopped. Ahead of us, the channel opened into a vaulted chamber where the old Veyli engineers had met the steppe's edge, and the far wall was not wall anymore.

It was glass. A sheet of it, floor to ceiling, veined with light, and beyond it — blurred, enormous, patient — the steppe stretched away into a horizon that glowed without a sun.

"That's new," Pip said.

"It shouldn't be here." My voice came out flat. "The barrier runs along the surface. This is thirty feet underground. It's *spreading*."

"Is that bad?" Pip asked, in the tone of someone who already knew.

"It means the covenant isn't just failing. It's collapsing inward. The realms aren't drifting apart — they're bleeding together, and the glass is the scar tissue." I pressed my hand to the pane. It was cold, then warm, then cold again, pulsing slow as a sleeping heart. Through it I could see the steppe's surface far above, refracted, doubled. "When it reaches the streets—"

"It already has," Odji said quietly. "You saw the Tanner's Quarter. They're calling it fire damage."

* * *

The cistern exit was supposed to be clear.

It was not clear.

We came up the maintenance stair into gray pre-dawn light and the smell of smoke, and found the yard beyond the cistern gates full of Wardens — not many, six, seven, a checkpoint strung across the road where the city thinned into scrubland. Lanterns. Pikes. One of them had a shard-cage on his belt, the kind they used to store confiscated charms, and even from forty yards I could see it glowing with everything they'd ripped out of the Night Market before they burned it.

We froze in the stairwell shadow. Odji pulled us back a step. Pip's breathing went thin and fast.

"There's another way," she breathed. "There's always another way."

"Not through them, there isn't. Not before full light." Odji closed his eyes. "They'll sweep the aqueduct by noon. We can't go back and we can't wait."

"We can't fight them either," I said. "Three of us. Seven of them. One of us is sixty years old and one of us is—"

"I'm fifteen and I've been robbing Wardens since I was nine," Pip snapped. "Don't count me out."

"Pip—"

But she wasn't listening. She was looking at the map case, and the map case was looking back — glowing now, bright enough to throw shadows, bright enough to be seen from the yard if anyone glanced toward the stairwell. Bright enough to be a beacon.

"Pip," I said slowly. "Why is it doing that."

"I don't—" She fumbled with the straps. "It's been getting hotter since the chamber, it wants to go, it wants *out*, it doesn't like the cage-glow, it's pulling—"

The strap tore.

Later, I would replay this moment a hundred times and never see it clearly: whether the buckle failed, whether she wrenched it, whether the map itself chose that heartbeat to lunge. What I remember is the case hitting the stones, springing open, and the map rising — actually rising, a foot of living parchment unfurling in the air like a wing, blazing green-gold, singing now audibly, a high sweet note that cut through the smoke —

— and every Warden in the yard turned toward us.

"*Run!*" Odji shouted, and shoved me toward the gap in the wall, but Pip dove the other way, straight into the open, straight toward the map, small body flat-out across forty yards of killing ground while the first crossbow bolt cracked past her ear and buried itself in the cistern wall.

"Pip!" My voice tore something in my throat.

She slid the last five feet on her knees, snatched the map out of the air, and rolled — and the second bolt took her through the shoulder.

The sound she made was small. That's what I'll never forget. Not a scream. A small, surprised sound, like a child stung by a nettle, and then she was up and running again with the map crushed against her chest and blood sheeting down her arm, and Odji was hauling me backward into cover, and the world had gone narrow and ringing.

Then the glass saved us.

I don't know what else to call it. The Warden with the shard-cage raised his hand and the confiscated charms inside flared — and the ground between him and Pip, the packed earth of the yard, went white. Glass. A sheet of it, sudden and screaming, spreading from his boots in a starburst that threw two of his own men off their feet. The barrier-bleed, reaching up through the soil, answering the map's song the way a struck string answers its octave.

The Wardens broke. Men don't stand in front of magic they were trained to believe was dead.

"GO!" Odji roared, and we went — through the gap in the wall, up the scrub slope, Pip between us with her legs going wrong and her jaw set like iron, the map glowing against her chest like a second heart, and behind us the checkpoint dissolved into white light and shouting and the terrible soft crackle of earth turning to glass.

* * *

We ran until the city was a smudge of orange behind us and the dawn came up properly, and then we ran because stopping meant thinking, and thinking could wait.

Pip lasted another mile on pure fury. Then her knees buckled and Odji caught her, and we laid her down in the lee of a fallen boundary-stone — an old Veyli marker, its script worn smooth, the first honest thing I'd touched in three years — and he worked on her shoulder with shaking hands and a voice that never once shook.

"Bolt's out. Clean entry, thank every merciful god. You'll keep the arm." He bound it tight. "You absolute idiot. Magnificent idiot. Why?"

Pip looked up at me, gray-faced, bloodied, fifteen years old and ancient. Her free hand found mine and gripped it hard enough to hurt.

"You said the map mattered more than any of us," she said. "I figured somebody ought to make sure it got carried right."

I turned away so she wouldn't see my face, and failed, because there was nowhere left to hide. The city was burning behind us. The girl was bleeding in front of me. And ahead—

Ahead was the steppe.

* * *

We crested the last rise as the sun cleared the eastern haze, and there it was.

Endless. Blinding. A plain of glass running to every horizon, catching the dawn and shattering it into ten thousand colors, gold and rose and a green so deep it looked like the memory of forests. It moved when the wind moved — ripples of light chasing each other across leagues of frozen sea. No roads. No landmarks. No shade. Nothing that had ever been alive.

Nothing, except the singing.

Faint, at first. I thought it was the wind. Then I thought it was the map, still warm against Pip's bandages. Then I understood it was neither, and both, and more: the whole plain was humming, a chord too vast and too low for ears, felt in the teeth, in the sternum, in the scar tissue where my old life used to be.

The covenant. Petrified. Stretched out beneath our feet for a hundred miles, holding two worlds apart by force of will and grief, and singing to itself in the dark because no one had listened in three hundred years.

Behind me: smoke, ash, a city that had never once been home and had still deserved better than to burn. I grieved it anyway. That was the trick nobody warned you about — you don't get to choose what you mourn. Corvane had given me nothing but hiding, and I stood on the edge of its ruin and felt it tear something loose.

Beside me: a bleeding thief who'd taken a bolt for a piece of paper. An exile healer with nothing but a satchel. A map that sang.

"Six miles of glass to the first waystation," Odji said. "If the maps are right."

"They're right," I said. "My mother drew them."

Pip lifted her head, squinting against the blaze of morning, and grinned through the pain. "Then it should be easy. It's not like the glass is going to *talk* to us."

Under our feet, low and patient, the steppe began to sing louder.
