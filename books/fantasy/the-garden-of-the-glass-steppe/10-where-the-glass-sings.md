## 10. Where the Glass Sings

The first thing you learn on the glass is that it talks.

Not with words. Words are for people who need them. The glass sings — a thin high note under your boots, different every hundred paces, like someone running a wet finger round the rim of a cup that never empties. I've heard it since I was small enough to sleep in a drainpipe off Tanner's Row. Everyone said I was mad. Mad Pip, hearing music in the cobbles. But the cobbles of Corvane were dead things, crushed stone, and they only ever hummed one bored note.

This is not that.

I stop walking and hold up my fist, the way Odji taught me to signal halt back when he was still deciding whether to poison me or feed me. Behind me the line stops: Odji with his healer's satchel clacking against his hip, Sef with his Warden sword wrapped in sacking so it won't catch the light, and Bram at the rear, her hood up against the glare, the living map pressed flat against her chest like something she's afraid will bolt.

"Listen," I say.

They listen. They're bad at it, all three of them. City people listen with their ears and then start talking about what they heard. I make them stand there a full minute while the sun climbs out of the glass horizon and sets every facet east of us burning white-gold, and the note under our feet slides up, up, up until Odji's eyebrows climb his forehead.

"You hear that?" I ask.

"I hear nothing," Sef says. "Which worries me more."

"That's because you're standing wrong." I crouch, put my palm flat on the glass. It's warm already, though the morning's barely started, and the vibration comes up through my arm bones into my teeth. "Whole body. Heels down. You don't hear it, you *drink* it." I look at Bram. "You especially. You're the worst listener I've ever met and you're supposed to be the great cartographer."

Bram pulls her hood back. Three days on the steppe have burned the shopkeeper softness off her face; she looks carved now, all planes and squint. "Teach, then," she says. "Since you're the expert."

"I am, actually." I grin at her because it annoys her, and she needs annoying. She's been walking like a woman going to her own funeral since we came out through the ash, and funerals are boring. "Right. Lesson one."

* * *

Here's what I know, and I know it in my bones before I know it in my head, which is the only way to know anything true:

The glass isn't flat. It only pretends to be. Under every pane there's a vein, a seam where two sheets fused wrong, and each seam carries its own note. Low notes run deep — those panes are thick as cathedral floors and you could march an army over them and never crack a thing. High notes mean thin ice, thin glass, hollows underneath where the old world fell in and got glazed over. And between the notes, when two seams cross, there's a chord. A bright, complicated chord, like three bells arguing.

Chords are doors. That's what nobody understands who hasn't lived their whole life listening to dead city stone. Where the notes cross, the glass remembers being something else, and if you put your hand down at the crossing-point and hum back the right pitch — I can't explain how you find the right pitch, you just do, the way you find the right word — the memory opens like a well.

I teach them the low notes first. March on the lows, always. Then the highs, and how to read the tremble in your own shinbones: a shiver means hollow, a shudder means drop, a long smooth slide means you can run, actually run, and for once on this cursed steppe running is safe.

Odji takes to it fastest, which surprises everyone except him. "I spent twenty years diagnosing by touch," he says, kneeling, pressing his brown fingers to a seam. "A pulse tells you everything if you shut up and let it speak." He finds a hollow thirty paces ahead of us that would have swallowed Bram to the waist, and after that nobody calls it madness anymore.

Sef is slower. His problem is he doesn't trust anything he can't arrest. He walks like the glass owes him money. But he's stubborn in the useful way, and by midday he's reading the seams without dropping his hand, just by the flex of his boot-soles, and I catch him almost smiling at it before he remembers he's a disgraced traitor and kills the smile.

Bram — Bram is the strange one.

She listens the way I do. Not learning it. *Remembering* it. I watch her at the third chord-crossing we find, watch her kneel and lay both palms down without me telling her to, and her lips move, humming a pitch I never taught her, and the well opens — a circle of glass going soft and dark as pond water, showing a flash of green, of moving water, of a sky with two moons in it — and she stares down into it like she's looking at her own grave.

"What do you see?" I ask.

"Veyl," she says. Her voice comes out scraped. "It's Veyl. Underneath. All of it."

"Yeah," I say. "That's the trick of it, innit."

And then I tell her the thing I've been carrying since the night market, the thing I didn't say because saying it felt like handing someone a knife handle-first. "It's not that the steppe sits *on* Veyl. It's not a lid. Bram — the steppe *is* the covenant. Somebody poured the whole promise out here and it cooled. Every pane. Every seam. We've been walking on the treaty this whole time."

* * *

She goes quiet after that. Properly quiet, not sulking-quiet, which I know from experience because I invented sulking-quiet. She walks to the front of the line and stays there, and every hour or so she stops and puts her hands on the glass and listens with her whole body the way I showed her, and whatever she hears makes her mouth go thinner.

By late afternoon the light has gone the color of old honey, and the singing changes.

I feel it before I hear it — a wrongness in my back teeth, a sourness, like biting foil. I've felt it once before, years ago, out past the ash-line on a dare, and I turned around and ran and didn't stop running until Corvane's walls. Now there's no turning around. There's only the Spire ahead, a gray needle on the horizon that hasn't gotten any closer in three days, mocking us.

"Storm," I say.

Odji looks up. "Sky's clear."

"Not that kind." I'm already moving, scanning the panes, hunting the low notes. "Memory-storm. The glass holds everything that was promised, yeah? Promises break. Broken promises don't sit still. When the wind gets up—" I don't finish it, because the wind does get up, all at once, out of a clear sky, and the singing across the whole steppe bends flat and ugly, a thousand cups dragged off-key by the same dead finger.

The horizon shivers.

That's the only word for it. The horizon *shivers*, like a reflection in disturbed water, and out of the shiver come shapes. Not wind-shapes. Not sand. Faces. Hands. A doorway that hangs in the air forty feet tall with nothing behind it. A woman's voice calling a name I don't know in a language that hurts my fillings. Fragments of other people's yesterdays, torn loose from wherever they were stored, blowing toward us across the glass like burning paper.

"*Down!*" Sef shouts, and tackles Odji flat as the first fragment scythes overhead — a slice of somebody's wedding, gold light and music and a bride's laugh, gone in a blink, and where it passes the glass beneath frosts white and stays frosted.

We crawl. I lead them along the deepest low-note seam I can find, shouting the turns over the rising howl, and the storm keeps coming apart above us — a child's birthday, a hanging, a harvest, a funeral, someone's first kiss and someone's last breath all tangled together, screaming silently. One fragment lands close enough to touch: a kitchen, whole and warm, bread on a board, and for half a second I smell it, real bread, real warmth, and I want it so badly I nearly reach out, and Odji grabs my wrist hard enough to bruise.

"It's not yours," he says. "Pip. Look at me. It is not yours, and it is not true anymore. Let it pass."

It passes. The frost where it touched spreads toward my knee and I scramble back and keep crawling.

Then Bram screams.

I twist around and she's on her feet — on her *feet*, in the middle of it, arms out, hood blown back, hair whipping — and a fragment has found her. It's bigger than the others, a whole room of memory wrapped around her like a bell jar, and inside it I can see another version of her: younger, laughing, ink to the elbows, bent over a drafting table in a tower room while a woman with Bram's same grey eyes points at a map spread wide, pointing and pointing, mouth moving, saying something over and over that none of us can hear.

"Bram!" Sef is up too, wading toward her, frost blooming around his boots with every stride, and the storm is bending toward her now, all of it, every broken piece on the horizon curving in like iron filings to a magnet, because she's a cartographer's daughter standing on a covenant made of memory and the storm knows its own.

I do the stupid thing. I do the thing I'd do even if I'd never met any of them, because feral is feral.

I sing back.

I drop to my knees at the nearest chord-crossing, palms down, and I hum the opening pitch as loud as my lungs will give, and the well opens under my hands — that soft dark circle, that flash of two-mooned sky — and I push everything I've got into it, every note I know, the whole song of the steppe at once, and the glass *answers*. A chord rolls out of the crossing-point like a struck bell, huge, and the nearest fragments shatter into harmless glitter. I drag the pitch higher. Another crossing catches it. Another. The steppe starts ringing, dozens of wells singing together, and the storm staggers, tears, thins—

—and Bram falls out of the bell jar of her mother's voice like a puppet with cut strings, and Sef catches her, and the last of the fragments blow past over our heads and away east, chasing the sunset, taking their terrible cargo of other people's lives with them.

Silence. Real silence, the glass gone still as a held breath.

I stay on my knees, shaking, my palms printed red on the cold pane. Odji's hand settles on the back of my neck, steady as a splint.

"Well done," he says quietly.

"I nearly died."

"Yes. Well done anyway."

* * *

We make camp in the lee of a ridge of fused glass taller than a house, some ancient fracture frozen mid-surge. No fire — nothing up here burns, and light draws things we haven't met yet — so we huddle in blankets and share dried plums and hardtack, and nobody talks for a long time.

Bram sits apart, staring at her hands.

I go sit next to her, because someone has to and I'm the shortest journey. Up close she looks wrung out, translucent, like the storm took a bite and spat most of it back.

"What did she say?" I ask. "In there. Your mum. She was saying something. Over and over."

Bram's jaw works. "I couldn't hear it. That's the worst part. She was right there, telling me — telling me *something* — and it was like listening through a wall." She looks at me, and her eyes are wet and furious. "Three days ago I thought the steppe was just ground. Today I learn it's the covenant itself, petrified, and my mother's voice is somewhere inside it, and I can't hear a single word."

"Maybe you're not meant to hear it yet," I say, and immediately regret it, because it sounds wise and I hate sounding wise.

"Or maybe the storm was a warning." Sef has drifted near, arms crossed, the sacked sword across his knees. He says it flat, but his eyes aren't flat; they keep going to Bram and coming back. "The barrier's failing. If the covenant is the steppe, then the steppe is failing too. Those fragments weren't random. They were *leaking*."

"Leaks before the flood," Odji murmurs.

Nobody says the rest of it. Nobody has to. If the steppe is the covenant and the covenant is cracking, then every day we walk is a day the cracks widen, and the Spire ahead — that gray needle that won't come closer — is either the patch or the wound.

I lie back on my blanket and look up. The stars over the glass are wrong, doubled, each one with a faint twin beside it, and I've decided not to mention that to anyone yet because we've had enough for one day.

But before I sleep, I press one palm flat against the ground, the way I've done every night since I was a kid in a drainpipe, just checking.

The glass is still singing.

But tonight, for the first time, it's singing *faster* — a quickening, urgent, like a heart before a race — and far off to the north, so faint I might have dreamed it, something is singing back.
