## 12. Sef, Unarmored

He had been following them for two days before he let them see him.

That was the discipline of it. Sef had spent eleven years learning how to be unseen in a city that watched everything, and the steppe was easier—open ground, long sightlines, a horizon that gave away every silhouette. He moved at dusk and dawn, when the glass threw its light sideways and shadows ran long enough to hide a man. He drank from the condensation traps the old surveyors had left, ate the ration bars he'd carried out of Corvane sewn into his coat lining. He kept three hundred meters between himself and the four figures picking their way across the singing plain, and he told himself it was surveillance.

It was not surveillance. Surveillance ended with a report.

On the second night he watched them make camp in the lee of a shattered waystation wall, and he watched Odji move among them with his healer's hands, checking Pip's bandaged arm, pressing water into Bram's grip the way you press water into someone who has forgotten they're thirsty. He watched Bram sit apart, the living map open across her knees, its light the color of deep water, her lips moving as she read script no one else could see.

She used to read like that over her mother's charts, back when there had been a workshop above the harbor and the smell of ink and hot wax, back when he'd been allowed through the door. He had loved her for exactly that—the way she bent toward knowledge like a plant toward light, unguarded, certain the world would keep being worth understanding.

Then the covenant broke, and Corvane taught them both what the world actually did to people who understood things.

*You could still turn around,* he thought. *Walk east. Report that you lost the trail in the storm fields. Take the demotion. Live.*

But the commander had taken the shards. He had seen what she did to the prisoner in the holding cells—not interrogated, *unmade*, peeled open like fruit while the other Wardens looked anywhere but at her—and whatever Sef had been serving since then, it wasn't the law. It was just the shape of the law, worn by something else.

He checked his gear one last time. Then he took off his sword belt, unslung the crossbow, drew the knife from his boot, and laid all of it on a flat shelf of glass where the dawn light would find it.

If this went wrong, he wanted to die unarmed. It seemed only honest.

* * *

Pip heard him first, of course. The girl heard everything the glass said, and the glass said *man approaching, alone, no metal singing on him* about forty minutes before he came within bowshot—which was fine, because he had no bow anymore.

They spotted him as a dark figure walking upright across the open steppe with his hands visible, which was either the bravest or stupidest thing any of them had ever seen, and given that Pip had once stolen a Warden's boots off his feet while he wore them, the competition was stiff.

"Stop there," Odji called, when Sef was twenty paces out. The healer had planted himself between Sef and the camp, arms loose, weight forward. Behind him, Bram had risen, and the map in her hands had begun to glow like a struck match.

Sef stopped. He knelt—slowly, palms out—and pressed both hands flat against the glass.

"I'm surrendering," he said. "My weapons are back at the ridge, on the flat stone. Sword, crossbow, knife, the Warden-issue signal flare I never used. You can send the girl for them. She's fast."

"You're Warden-blood," Odji said. Not a question. His voice had the flat calm of a man who had treated wounds made by that bloodline's work for years. "You tracked us from the ash line. Don't insult me by pretending you got lost."

"I tracked you from the ash line," Sef agreed. "Because if I hadn't, someone worse would have. Commander Vess has the covenant-shards now. Four of them. She's been using them to scry the map's resonance." He nodded at the glow in Bram's hands. "She knows roughly where you are. She's maybe half a day behind me, moving with twelve Wardens and a captured resonance-engine. And she is not coming to arrest anyone."

The silence stretched. Somewhere underfoot, faint as a pulse, the glass sang.

"And why," Odji said quietly, "should we believe a single word of that?"

"You shouldn't." Sef stayed kneeling, hands on the cold glass, feeling its hum come up through his palms like a second heartbeat. "Belief is yours to give or withhold. I'm not here to earn forgiveness. I'm here because she—" he looked past Odji, at Bram, and made himself hold her stare even though it burned, "—is walking into Vess's hands without knowing what Vess has become, and I would rather she hate me alive than mourn me ignorant."

Bram spoke for the first time. Her voice was steady, which somehow made it worse.

"The cells under the Warden citadel. Three years ago. There was a charm-seller named Ferro—an old man, harmless, his charms were *dried flowers*, Sef, I knew him, he bought bread from my neighbor—" Her breath caught and steadied. "You signed the transfer order. I saw the ledger when I bought my way into the records office. Your name. Your hand."

"Yes."

"He died in transit. They said he fell."

"They said he fell," Sef said. "I've spent three years not knowing whether that was true, and three years knowing that I put him in the cart, and both of those facts together have been enough." He didn't look away. He owed her that much—to be looked at while she decided what he was. "I won't ask you to forgive it. I'm asking you to use me. Point me at the worst job and I'll do it. Give me the rearguard and I'll hold it. When Vess comes—and she's coming—I know her tactics, her formations, the engine's resonance frequency, and the way she thinks, which is the most dangerous thing I know. Kill me after, if you want. But let me be useful first."

"That's very tidy," Bram said. "A man arrives with an enemy at his heels and offers to be a shield. It's almost like he's made himself impossible to refuse."

"It is exactly like that," Sef said. "I learned tactics from the same people you're running from. I know what surrender looks like when it's a trap. This isn't one. But you can't verify that, and I can't prove it, and so we're at the place where trust gets extended before it's earned or not at all." He spread his empty hands. "So. Extend it or don't. Those are the options. I'll kneel here until you decide."

"He will too," Pip said from the rocks, sounding delighted. "I've been watching. He's got, like, *kneeling stamina*. It's honestly impressive."

"Pip," Odji said.

"What? Someone should take notes."

* * *

Odji crouched in front of him, close enough that Sef could see the gray coming into the healer's beard, the tremor in his left hand that he hid inside his sleeve and thought no one noticed.

"You knew what I was," Odji said. "In Corvane. Before the clinic. When the Wardens swept the Tannery Quarter and I was treating people in a cellar full of dye vats. You were the lieutenant on that sweep."

"I was."

"You found the cellar."

"I found the cellar," Sef said. "I reported it empty. There were nineteen people in it."

Odji studied him for a long moment. "Nineteen," he said. "I counted eighteen. One of them was a woman who wouldn't stop thanking me, and I lost track." Something moved behind his eyes—grief and arithmetic and, unwillingly, a door opening a crack. "Why?"

"Because the man who wrote the covenant's enforcement doctrine was my grandfather," Sef said. "And because I read it. All of it. Every clause. Do you know what's in the margins of the original? In his own hand? *This will require cruelty indefinitely.* He knew. He designed it knowing, and he did it anyway, and then the Wardens built a religion on top of it and called the cruelty duty." He looked down at his own hands, scarred from eleven years of the work those hands had done. "I carried out that duty until I couldn't pretend I didn't know what it was. Vess finished the job. She showed me what we'd always been, underneath the uniforms."

Odji rose. He turned to Bram, and his voice changed—healer's voice, the one he used to deliver hard truths gently and true ones plainly.

"Bram. I'm going to say something you won't like."

"Don't," she said.

"Nineteen people are alive because this man lied to his own Wardens three years ago, and none of us knew, and none of them ever thanked him, and he never told anyone. That's not the act of a man performing remorse. Performers want witnesses." He held up a hand before she could answer. "I'm not telling you to forgive him. Forgiveness isn't medicine and I don't prescribe it. I'm telling you what I know, and what I know is that we need every pair of hands that's willing, and his are willing, and he just knelt on frozen glass for an hour to prove it. We can hate him later. Right now Vess is half a day out with an engine that can crack the steppe, and pride is a luxury we stopped being able to afford somewhere back at the burning quarter."

Bram looked at Sef for a long time.

The map had gone quiet in her hands, its light banked low, and he wondered—not for the first time—if it could read hearts the way it read terrain. If it was showing her something. If that was why her expression shifted, minute by minute, from hatred toward something more exhausted and more terrible: recognition. Because that was the cruelest part, he thought. She didn't hate him cleanly. She hated him *specifically*, with memory, with the whole ruined harbor workshop of their history stacked behind her eyes, and specific hatred is just love with nowhere to go.

"Ferro had a daughter," she said finally. "In Corvane. If we live through this, you find her, and you tell her yourself. Everything. On your knees if you have to—you seem good at that."

"Yes," Sef said.

"And you walk at the back. Where I can see you. And if the map flinches when you touch anything, you drop it and step away, and you don't argue."

"Yes."

"And Sef?" Her voice didn't rise at all. "I don't forgive you. Maybe I will. Maybe I won't. Don't wait for it. Don't fight for it. Just do the next right thing until the ledger balances or it doesn't."

"I can live on that," he said, and found, to his surprise, that it was true. It was more than he'd expected to be offered. It was more than he'd offered himself, some nights, staring at the ceiling of safehouses with his rank gone and his name struck from the rolls.

Odji helped him up. His grip was strong despite the tremor. "Welcome to the tally of the broken," the healer said dryly. "We're a small chapter. Dues are paid in labor."

Pip appeared at his elbow, having fetched his weapons from the ridge without being asked, and dropped them at his feet in a clatter. "These are evidence," she announced. "I'm keeping the crossbow bolts, though. They're good bolts."

"They're yours," Sef said.

"I know," said Pip. "That's why I took them."

* * *

They walked.

He took the rear, as ordered, and found that the position suited him—it always had; a lieutenant's instinct was to face the direction danger came from—and by midday he had mapped their pace, their water discipline, the way Bram favored her left ankle on the descents. Small things. The inventory of people you intended to keep alive.

In the afternoon the glass began to sing differently.

Sef felt it before he understood it—a new note under the steppe's constant hum, lower, wrong, like a string tightened past its breaking point. Pip stopped dead in the middle of the column, head cocked, and her feral grin drained off her face entirely.

"That's not the steppe," she said. "That's *behind* us. That's—" She turned, and Sef was already turning, already reading the horizon with eleven years of hunter's eyes, and what he saw made his stomach drop through the glass and into whatever lay beneath the world.

Far to the southeast, a smudge of dust. Above it, a light that flickered in a rhythm no storm had ever had—regular, mechanical, pulsing like a searchlight sweeping a prison yard.

The resonance-engine. Half a day, he had told them. Vess had always been faster than her estimates.

"She pushed through the night," he said. "She's not trying to catch us anymore. She doesn't need to. She's driving us." His mouth had gone dry. "Straight ahead. Along the path of least resistance. Toward the Spire—because the engine needs the Spire's foundation to anchor the shards, and she's been planning to arrive there since before I defected. We weren't fleeing, Bram. We were herding ourselves."

Bram stood with the map raised against the wind, its light flaring hard, and when she lowered it her face had gone pale and set.

"Then we stop being a herd," she said. "Odji—how far to the veil-village?"

"Two days by the marked route," Odji said. "One, if we cut through the storm fields."

"The storm fields eat memories," Pip said. "Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows that," Bram agreed, "and Vess's engine can't follow us through interference that dense without tearing itself apart. So we go through the fields." She looked back at Sef, and there was no warmth in it, but there was something better: use. Purpose. A place in the line. "You know her formations. Tell me exactly what she'll do when we turn off the route."

Sef watched the pulsing light crawl along the horizon and felt the last of his old life fall away behind him like a shed skin.

"She'll split her force," he said. "Six to follow our trail into the fields and die there. Six to run the long way around and meet us at the Spire. And she'll go with the six, because Vess never sends anyone to do what she wants to watch." He rolled his shoulders, weaponless by choice and lighter for it. "Which means we have two days to cross ground that unmakes people, and when we come out the other side, she'll be waiting."

"Then we'd better be worth waiting for," Pip said, and grinned into the singing wind, and they turned north, all five of them together, toward the storm fields and the memory-eating dark.
