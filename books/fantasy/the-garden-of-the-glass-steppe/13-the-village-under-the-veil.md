## 13. The Village Under the Veil

The village did not exist until it decided to.

One moment we were walking a valley of gray grass that chimed faintly underfoot, and the next the air ahead of us shivered like heat off a stove and there were rooftops—low, round-shouldered houses roofed in pale tile, clustered along a stream that ran the color of weak tea. No walls. No gate. Just a place that had been holding its breath for three years and finally let it out because Odji whistled three notes, low and rising, into the hush.

"They'll have seen us coming since dawn," he said. "The veil doesn't hide you from people. It hides people from everything else."

Pip had gone still in the way she went still when something mattered, which was to say she'd stopped moving entirely and was listening with her whole body, eyes half-closed, head tilted toward the stream. "It's quieter here," she said. "The glass. Quieter than anywhere else on the steppe." Her face did something complicated. "Quieter isn't good."

I knew what she meant before she explained it, because I could feel it too—not through my bloodline's old gift, which I had spent three years pretending was a scar rather than an organ, but through the map. It rode against my ribs inside its oilcloth wrap, warm as a sleeping animal, and all day it had been growing heavier. Not physically. Heavier the way grief is heavy. The way a name is heavy when you say it over a grave.

Sef walked at the rear of our little line, hands empty by choice, his Warden coat traded somewhere outside Corvane for a gray traveler's cloak that made him look like a man who had never held authority over anything. He hadn't spoken in two hours. When I glanced back at him he was watching the village appear out of nothing with an expression I couldn't read, and when he caught me looking he looked away first, which was new, and which I refused to let mean anything.

Odji led us down between the houses. They were beautiful in the way Veyli things were always beautiful—impossibly proportioned, doorways tall as trees, windows set at angles that caught light from directions the sun wasn't in. And they were dying. I saw it in the first thirty seconds: moss the color of ash creeping up the white walls, tiles cracked and not repaired, a garden gone to seed where someone had clearly once kept it ruthlessly. A child's toy lay in the middle of the lane, a carved bird on a string, and no one had picked it up.

"People," Pip whispered, and there they were—emerging from doorways, from the fields beyond, gathering in the lane with the unhurried inevitability of water finding its level. Maybe forty of them. Fifty. Veyli faces, which is to say faces like mine would be if I stopped flinching: high-boned, dark-eyed, marked faintly at the temple with the pale tracery of covenant-lines that every trueborn Veyli carried and that I had spent my whole Corvane life hiding under my hair.

They looked at me the way starving people look at bread.

An older woman came forward through the crowd. She moved carefully, deliberately, like someone conserving a finite resource, and I understood with a lurch that she was—that they all were. That this was what Odji had been trying to tell me since the night market, what the map had been screaming in its silent way, what I had been refusing to hear because hearing it would make it mine.

Veyl was running out.

"You're the cartographer's daughter." The elder's voice was dry and clear, a reed over water. "Maren's girl."

"My mother's name was—" I started, and stopped, because the lie I'd lived inside for three years didn't even come up out of my throat. There was nothing there to say. *My mother was a charm-seller's widow. My mother died in the quarantine years.* Ash. All of it ash.

"Maren Joubert," the elder said gently. "I am Sefa Vane. I sat across the table from her eleven nights while she drew the last true map of the covenant. You have her hands."

I looked down at my own hands as if they belonged to a stranger. Behind me, very quietly, Sef exhaled.

* * *

They fed us first. This was Veyli custom, Odji murmured—grief and argument both waited until everyone had eaten—and so we sat in the longhouse at trestle tables worn smooth by generations, eating a soup of steppe-grain and root vegetables that tasted almost, almost like the food of my earliest buried memories, while fifty fading people watched me eat and I understood that the meal itself was a kind of plea.

The longhouse was built around a single tree. That undid me more than anything else. A living tree, indoors, its trunk rising through a gap in the roof, its leaves pale gold and thinning—thinning visibly, I realized, watching one detach and drift down onto the table near my bowl. Someone had raked fallen leaves into a neat pile by the wall. The pile was enormous. The tree was nearly bare.

"It was green when the covenant broke," Sefa Vane said, following my gaze. "Covenant-trees don't drop leaves for winter. They hold them until the world ends or the world doesn't." She folded her hands. "We are down to four hundred leaves, cartographer's daughter. By my count. I count them every morning."

Pip had gone gray around the mouth. Odji put a hand briefly on her shoulder.

"Tell her," Sefa said—to Odji, not to me. "You've been carrying it since the clinic. Put it down."

So Odji told it, in his clinic voice, the flat gentle voice he used for wounds: how the barrier between realms was not a wall but a membrane, fed from both sides, sustained by the covenant that Maren Joubert and nine others had forged in the Spire; how when the covenant shattered, the feeding stopped; how Veyl had been living on reserves ever since, a body burning its own muscle; how the glass steppe was the visible edge of it, the realm petrifying outward from the wound like frost climbing a windowpane. How the villages had gone dark one by one. How this was the last.

"How many were there?" I asked. My voice came out strange.

"When I was born? Two hundred and eleven settlements." Sefa Vane didn't flinch from the number. "This morning there is one."

The soup turned to stone in my stomach. Across the table, Sef was staring at the covenant-tree with the fixed intensity of a man doing arithmetic he hated, and I knew—he'd been a Warden, he'd been raised on the other side of the doctrine—but knowing a thing in your bones and hearing it said aloud over soup are different countries.

"And Corvane?" I said. "If the barrier fails completely—"

"The glass comes through." Pip said it. She was staring at her bowl. "That's what the singing is. Out on the steppe, where it's thinnest—I can hear it pushing. Like water behind a dam. It's not angry. That's the worst part. It's just *coming*."

"The Wardens know," Sef said quietly. Everyone looked at him. He didn't apologize for speaking; he'd stopped doing that somewhere on the steppe. "Commander Ashe has fragments of the old covenant-shards. She can feel the thinning too. She's not trying to stop it. She's trying to be standing in the right place when it breaks."

"Yes," Sefa Vane said. "Which brings us to why you are here, and why I will now ask you for the hardest thing anyone has ever asked of you, and I want you to hear all of it before you answer, because you are young, and because Maren's girl deserves the same courtesy Maren got: the whole truth, however long the telling."

She rose. The room rose with her.

* * *

She took me up the hill behind the village alone. I think that was deliberate—the others watched us go, Odji with his healer's helplessness, Pip with her fists in her pockets, Sef with an expression I catalogued and refused to interpret—and at the top of the hill stood a watchtower, or the bones of one, and from its broken crown you could see everything.

The steppe in every direction, white and glittering to the horizon. And far away, maybe two days' walk, a smudge on the sky that I knew without being told was the Covenant Spire.

And below us, past the veil's shimmer, if you knew where to look—a thread of gray that was not steppe. Corvane's outer farms. Smoke from chimneys. Tiny, distant, oblivious human life.

"There," Sefa Vane said. "Look at both. Look at both for as long as you need. Then listen."

I looked. I listened.

"Your mother faced this choice," she said. "Not this choice exactly—hers was larger, hers was ten realms' worth of arithmetic—but the shape of it. Two goods that cannot coexist. She chose binding. Binding costs the binder everything, and it bought us thirty years, and those thirty years were worth having, and I will never dishonor her by saying otherwise." The wind moved her white hair. "But bindings fail, cartographer's daughter. Everything woven wears. And now the loom is breaking again, and there is no second Maren Joubert, and there is no second covenant of ten, because the other nine are dead and their lines are scattered or spent. There is only you. One cartographer, half-trained, standing at the end of everything."

"What are you asking me?"

"I am asking you to seal it." She said it plainly, without pleading, which somehow made it worse. "Seal the rift permanently. Cut Veyl loose. Let this realm close like a book and let Corvane live. The barrier would hold forever—a sealed thing needs no feeding, no covenant, no cartographer bleeding herself into the weave. Your city goes on. Its children grow up. The Wardens disband or turn to banditry, either way the burnings stop." She turned to face me fully. "And we die. Four hundred souls, give or take the ones too proud to admit they're counting. We die slowly, or quickly if the sealing is rough, and the realm that made your mother's hands goes dark, and in a hundred years no one remembers there was ever anything on the other side of the glass except stories."

The wind came up the hill and I heard it pass through the valley below, through the village, and beneath it—faint, at the very floor of my hearing, the sound Pip heard all the time—the glass, singing. Not menacing. Not mournful. Just singing, the way a tuning fork sings, holding its note, waiting.

"If I refuse," I said. "If I try for something else—"

"Then you gamble both worlds on a hope I cannot see and do not share, and if you lose, the glass takes Corvane street by street, and it will not be quick, and it will not be clean, and I will have watched the last city of men drown because a girl loved a story about third options." Her voice didn't rise. "I have buried this village eleven times, cartographer's daughter. Eleven settlements. I have dug the graves and sung the songs and walked away. I am asking you to let me stop digging. I am asking you to let the dying be *finished*, instead of endless. Do you understand the mercy of that? Do you understand that what I'm begging you for is not my life but an ending to it?"

I understood. That was the horror of it. Standing on that hill with both worlds laid out beneath me, I understood her completely, and some cold clear part of me—the cartographer's part, the part that drew borders and knew that every border is a wound agreed upon—whispered that she was right. Seal it. Cut the loss. Save the many. It was clean. It was arithmetic. It was everything my mother's work had taught me to respect.

And then I thought of Pip, half-Veyli, standing in the lane below with her fists in her pockets, and what sealing meant for everyone whose blood ran to Veyl on the far side of the barrier, and the clean arithmetic developed a crack straight down the middle.

"I need time," I said.

"You have until the tree loses another hundred leaves." Sefa Vane looked out at the white horizon, and for one moment the elder fell away and I saw only a tired woman who had counted leaves every morning for years. "I will tell you something else, since you must decide with a full heart. Your mother, on her eleventh night here, told me the same thing. *I need time.* And then she went down the hill and she chose binding, and it cost her everything, and I have spent thirty years wondering if she chose rightly." She touched my shoulder, brief and light. "Whatever you choose, choose it as your own. Inherited choices are chains. Chosen ones are wings. Even when they burn."

* * *

I came down the hill at dusk and they were all waiting, and none of them asked, which was its own kindness.

The longhouse fire was low. The covenant-tree stood bare-limbed against the roof-gap and the stars came through where its crown used to be, and I sat beneath it with the map unwrapped in my lap at last, because I was done pretending I could put it down. It pulsed against my palms like a second heart. Where the rift was, its inked lines had begun to bleed—thin threads of red seeping from the Spire's mark, spreading slow as frost.

Pip sat down on my left without asking. After a while Sef sat on my right. Odji stood behind us, arms folded, keeping the vigil he'd apparently appointed himself.

"The red's worse," Pip said.

"I know."

"It's going to touch the edge of the page soon. Then what happens?"

I looked at the map, at the thinning barrier rendered in my mother's hand, at the red crawling toward the border of the parchment where Corvane was drawn in small gray strokes, streets and steeples and the aqueduct we'd fled through, all of it no bigger than my palm, all of it real, all of it full of people who had never consented to be ink.

"Then it starts coming through," I said.

And as if the map had been waiting for permission—as if it had heard the elder's plea and my silence and weighed them both—the bleeding lines shivered, and new script surfaced in the margins, rising through the parchment like drowned things coming up for air. Veyli script. My mother's hand. I read it before I could stop myself, and the words went through me like cold water:

*If you are reading this, little mapmaker, you have found the choice I prayed you'd never face. I left you a third road. Find Odji's eyes. Trust the thief's ears. And forgive the Warden—he was always going to follow you.*

Below us, out on the darkening steppe, the glass began to sing louder.

Pip's head snapped up. "Someone's coming," she said. "Fast. A lot of someones."
