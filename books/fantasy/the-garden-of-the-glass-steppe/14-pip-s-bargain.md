## 14. Pip's Bargain

Pip heard the argument through the wall of the elder's house, which was not really a wall but a woven curtain of silvergrass that had never once in her life kept a secret.

"—seal it," the elder was saying. Her voice was old paper and old grief. "Seal it clean, cartographer. Cut Veyl loose and let Corvane live. We have known this was coming since the covenant cracked. My people have made their peace."

"And you?" That was Odji, low and careful. "Have *you* made yours?"

A silence with weight in it.

Pip sat on the step outside with her knees pulled up to her chin and her back against the wall so she could feel the words come through it like bees under the plaster. She had gotten good at listening through walls. Three years of rooftops and crawlspaces in Corvane would do that to a person. You learned that every important thing anyone said was said somewhere they thought was private.

Bram's voice came next, quiet enough that Pip had to hold her breath to catch it. "If I seal it. If I do what your elder asks. What happens to the people on the other side? The half-bloods?"

Another silence. This one had teeth.

"You know what happens," the elder said at last. "The veil closes all the way. Everything of Veyl that lives in Corvan blood goes with it."

Pip stopped hearing after that.

Not because the voices stopped — they went on, Bram asking something sharp, Odji saying something soothing, the elder repeating her litany of *peace, peace, we have made our peace* — but because Pip's own heartbeat got very loud, and underneath it she could hear the glass fields singing, and for once the song sounded like it was singing about her.

Half-blood. She'd been called worse. She'd been called worse by better people, back when she was smaller and quicker and slept in the bell tower of Saint Verren's because nobody looked up. Half-blood was just what you were when your mother ran from Veyl with a Corvan soldier's baby in her belly and died anyway two winters later, leaving you nothing but a name you couldn't pronounce right and ears that heard things other people's ears didn't.

Ears that heard the glass sing.

She'd thought it was a trick. A gift, maybe, if she was feeling generous — the kind of gift that let a thief walk a glass roof without a sound, because the glass told her where it would bear weight and where it would crack. She'd thought it was hers. Just hers, a private little magic in a city where magic got you burned.

But it wasn't hers. It was Veyl's. It was the covenant leaking through her mother's blood like water through a cracked jug, and if Bram sealed the rift — if Bram did the merciful, sensible, save-Corvane thing that everyone in that room wanted her to do —

Then Pip would go out like a candle.

Not die. Worse than die. Erased. Every half-blood in Corvane unwritten, unremembered, their mothers' names scraped off the world. And nobody would even mourn them, because there'd be nothing left to mourn *with*. You can't grieve someone who never existed.

Pip stood up. Her legs were shaking. She noticed this the way you notice rain — distantly, like it was happening to somebody else.

Inside, Bram was saying, "There has to be another way. There's always—"

Pip didn't wait to hear the end of the sentence. Sentences like that always ended badly or not at all.

She ran.

* * *

The village hid itself under a veil of folded light, some last working scrap of Veyli craft, and the moment Pip passed through its edge the whole world opened up white and enormous and singing.

The glass steppe at midday was a country of knives laid flat. Light came off it in sheets. Pip squinted against it and ran anyway, out past the marker stones, out past the place where the villagers dared to farm their thin sad gardens in imported soil, out onto the open glass where nothing grew and everything sang.

She could hear it better here. That was the stupid part, the part that made her furious — she'd spent three years in Corvane learning to *shut it out*, clamping down on the hum that lived under the city's cobbles, and now that she finally let herself listen, really listen, the glass was saying things she understood.

It said: *we were a promise.*

It said: *we were meant to be a door.*

And underneath, fainter, in a voice that sounded horribly like her own: *and you are made of us, little one, and when we close, you close.*

"Shut up," Pip said out loud, to a field of glass, because she was alone and no one could see her being ridiculous. "Shut up shut up shut up."

The glass didn't shut up. It rose instead. She felt it before she saw it — a pressure change, a thickening in the air, the way Corvane felt an hour before a Warden raid. Far off to the west, where the horizon shimmered wrong, a storm was building. Not weather. She'd seen the real storms crossing the steppe, thunderheads stacked like siege towers. This was different. This was the broken memory-storm, the one Sef had described with his jaw tight, the one that had nearly taken Odji's mind apart in the first week of the crossing. Shattered pieces of the old covenant, still spinning in the wound where the Spire used to stand whole.

It was coming toward her. Or she was running toward it. From up here, on the singing glass, it was honestly hard to say who was hunting whom.

Good, thought some small vicious corner of Pip's mind. Let it take me. Better erased by accident than erased on purpose.

But her feet, traitorous feet, kept carrying her sideways along the storm's edge instead of into it. Survival habit. Three years of staying alive by instinct, and the instinct didn't care how tired her heart was.

That was when she heard them behind her.

"Pip!"

Sef's voice. Of course it was Sef — the man moved like a Warden even exiled, all long strides and terrible efficiency, and he'd apparently been the one to notice her empty spot on the step. Behind him, fainter, Odji calling her name in that healer's calm that fooled nobody, and Bram—

Bram's voice cut across the glass and the glass *answered* it. Pip felt it happen: a ripple in the song, a shiver of recognition, the whole plain leaning toward the cartographer like flowers toward sun. And the storm leaned too. It turned, slow as a wheel, away from Pip and toward the four small figures chasing her.

Oh, Pip thought. Oh, that's bad.

Because she'd been listening to the glass for days now, really listening, and she knew something the others didn't. She knew the storm wasn't random. It was the covenant's broken pieces, yes, but broken pieces remember what they were for, and what they were for was *judging*. The old covenant had weighed every soul that crossed between realms — her mother used to hum about it, a lullaby that was really a warning: *the glass knows your blood, little bird, the glass knows what you owe.* And the storm was going to weigh Bram Joubert, daughter of the woman who forged it, and Pip had a horrible clear feeling about what the weighing would find.

She turned around. She ran back.

Later, she wouldn't be able to explain why. Fear made people stupid; that was the official story she'd tell. But the truth, the truth she kept in the locked drawer where she kept her mother's name, was simpler: those idiots had come after her. Nobody came after Pip. In three years of Corvane, nobody had ever once come after Pip. And if they were going to be idiotic enough to run into a memory-storm for a feral little half-blood thief, then the least she could do was be idiotic right back.

"Pip, stop—" Sef again, closer now, and then the storm reached them all at once.

* * *

It didn't look like a storm up close. It looked like looking at your life through the wrong end of a spyglass.

The light went strange and grainy. The air filled with fragments — not shards of glass, exactly, though they glittered like it, but pieces of *something else*, moments, echoes, the covenant's shed skin. Pip felt them start to sift through her like sifting flour, and each fragment carried a memory that wasn't hers and tried to become hers anyway:

— a market day in Veyl, banners the color of dawn, everyone laughing in a language she almost knew —

— her mother's hands, younger than Pip had ever seen them, folding a paper bird that flew —

— the cracking. The great tearing sound, the sky splitting along a seam, and the Spire falling, and voices screaming in two languages at once —

"No," Pip said, and clamped her hands over her ears, which was useless, because the storm wasn't coming through her ears. It was coming through her blood. Through the Veyl half of her, the half that was scheduled for erasure, the half that apparently made a lovely open door for any passing shard of dead covenant.

She went down on the glass. It was warm under her palms, warm and singing, and somewhere above her Sef was shouting her name and Bram was shouting something else and the storm was pouring memories into her faster than she could refuse them, and the worst one, the very worst one, was small:

— a woman with Pip's eyes standing at a window in Corvane, holding a baby, whispering, *"They'll never know what you are. I'll make sure they never know."* Hiding her. Hiding her the way Bram had hidden. Hiding her so well that when the rift sealed, no one would even notice she was gone.

"That's not fair," Pip said to the storm, and her voice cracked in the middle. "That's not FAIR—"

Hands on her shoulders. Real hands, solid, smelling of ash and cold air. Sef, kneeling over her, shielding her body with his the way you shield a candle flame, which was funny because he was the one with Warden blood, he was the one whose whole lineage was built to enforce the sealing, and here he was using his traitor body as a windbreak for a girl the covenant wanted to delete.

"Pip! Look at me. Look at me. You're Pip. You stole my boots our second night out and sold them back to me at a markup—"

"They were good boots," Pip gasped.

"They were *terrible* boots. Odji says you hear the glass. So hear it now — tell me what it's saying."

And that, that absurd Warden question, cut through the storm like a knife through fog. Because it gave her something to do. Something that was hers.

Pip pulled her hands from her ears. She listened past the shards, past the memories, down to the deep note underneath — the covenant's true voice, the one the storm was drowning out.

"It's scared," she said. Wonder crept into her voice despite everything. "It's not attacking. It's *scared*. It's been broken for three years and everybody keeps trying to fix it by breaking it more — seal it, burn it, cut it — and it doesn't want—" She grabbed Sef's wrist. "It doesn't want to be a wall. It wants to be a *door*. It keeps saying door. It's been saying door since we crossed."

The storm shuddered around them. And then, like a tide finding its channel, it began to move — flowing past them, around them, drawn away toward something. Toward someone.

Toward Bram, who stood ten paces off with the living map open in her hands, its inked rivers crawling, its Veyl-script blazing, taking the storm's full attention the way a lightning rod takes lightning. Odji had his arm around her, chanting something in the healer's cadence, and Bram's face was wet and set and absolutely certain, and she was saying, over and over, to the storm, to the map, to the whole broken machinery of two worlds:

"I know. I know. I'm listening. Tell me the third way."

The storm settled over her like a flock of birds coming home.

* * *

Afterward they walked back to the village in a line, the way you walk when nobody wants to be the first to talk.

Pip's legs worked. That was about all she could say for them. Every few steps she checked, without meaning to, that her hands were still there, that her shadow still matched her, that she hadn't started fading at the edges. Still there. Still annoyingly solid.

Bram fell into step beside her. Up close her eyes were red-rimmed and her hair was full of static from the storm, standing out in a pale halo, and she looked, for the first time since Pip had met her, less like a legend and more like a tired woman in her thirties who hadn't slept properly in weeks.

"The elder told you," Bram said. Not a question. "About the sealing. About what it would mean for—"

"For everyone like me." Pip kept her eyes forward. "Yeah. She told me. Very peaceful about it. Very *we have made our peace.* Easy to make peace when it's somebody else getting erased."

"Yes." Bram didn't flinch from it, which Pip grudgingly respected. "I'm not going to seal it, Pip."

"You don't know that. You might have to. Maybe there's no third way, maybe your mum's marginalia is a dead end, maybe—"

"Pip." Bram stopped walking. The others stopped too, a little way ahead, pretending very badly not to listen. "When that storm hit you, I felt it. Through the map. It went through you because you're *part of what it's made of* — and it didn't erase you. It showed you your mother. Do you understand what that means? The covenant doesn't want you gone. The covenant is trying to keep you." She held up the living map, and there, in the crawling ink, new lines had appeared — fine bright threads running out from the Spire's ruin, threading through the steppe, threading, Pip realized with a jolt, directly through the spot where Pip had fallen. "You're not collateral damage in this. You might be part of the answer. Half-bloods might all be part of the answer. Doors need hinges, Pip. Maybe that's what you are."

Pip stared at the map until her eyes stung. Then she scrubbed her face with her sleeve, fast and rough, before anyone could call it crying.

"If you're lying to me," she said, "if this is a kindness lie, I will steal everything you own and sell it in the night market under a fake name, and I'll do it *well*."

"Noted," Bram said solemnly. "Odji, witness that she threatened me."

"Witnessed," Odji said gravely. "Terrible boots, by the way, Sef. I told you at the time."

They walked on. The village veil shimmered ahead, and behind them the glass fields sang on in the late light, and Pip found — carefully, tentatively, like testing thin ice — that the song didn't sound like a threat anymore. It sounded like a crowd before a performance. Like waiting.

She was almost through the veil when the glass spoke one more time. Clear, distinct, unmistakable, in the voice of the deep note beneath all the others:

*She comes,* it said. *The one with the stolen shards. She comes with the Spire's broken crown, and she will be here by tomorrow's dark.*

Pip froze. Turned. Looked west, where the horizon lay flat and innocent and lying.

"Bram," she said. "We've got a problem."
