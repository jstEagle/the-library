## 16. The Warden's Hunt

Sef heard the glass before he saw them.

It had been singing all morning—a thin, high note like a wet finger circling the rim of a cup—but now it changed pitch, dropped into something lower and uglier, the way a dog's growl changes when it stops being warning and becomes intent. He stopped on the ridge of fused glass and turned back the way they'd come.

Three days behind them, maybe less. He'd hoped for five. He'd hoped, stupidly, that Commander Vess would waste time consolidating her hold on Corvane, that the Wardens' chain of command would slow her the way it had slowed everything else in his old life. But Vess had never been slowed by anything except ambition, and ambition, given shards of a broken covenant to feed on, moved like fire through dry grass.

"There." Pip pointed, crouched low against the glass so her shadow flattened out behind her. "The shimmer. That's not heat-haze."

It wasn't. Heat-haze didn't move in ranks. The distortion on the southern horizon rippled in disciplined lines, a dozen figures walking where no one should have been able to walk—because Sef knew this stretch of steppe, had crossed it once as a young Warden on a mapping survey, and the glass here sang wrong. It rejected weight. Ordinary people found their steps skidding, their balance betraying them, until they learned to move with the resonance instead of against it. Pip could do it. Bram was learning. Even Odji, with his healer's sensitivity, managed an awkward grace.

Vess's soldiers walked across it flat-footed, unbothered, because each of them carried a shard.

Sef counted twelve, then gave up counting. His stomach did something cold and complicated. "That's half a garrison," he said. "She emptied Corvane's Warden-house for this."

"She emptied more than that." Odji stood beside him, hands folded into his sleeves, squinting south with the clinical detachment he brought to everything—even, apparently, the army hunting them. "Look at their spacing. Too even. They're not walking, they're being carried. Whatever she's using to anchor them, it isn't their own discipline."

"It's the shards." Sef flexed his right hand, remembering the weight of a Warden's suppression gauntlet, the way it had felt like wearing someone else's certainty. "Covenant-shards don't just store power. They impose order. She's got enough of them to make her whole column march in step with the glass instead of against it."

Bram came up the ridge last, the map wrapped against her chest under her coat. She looked tired in a way sleep didn't touch—the kind of tired that lived behind the eyes, where the map had taken up residence. Three days since the waystation, since she'd told them about her mother, since the marginalia Odji found had rewritten every plan they had. *A living cartographer's memory.* Since then she'd barely spoken, and when she did, her voice had the flat calm of someone reading their own eulogy.

"How many?" she asked.

"Twelve or more. Plus Vess." Sef kept his tone level. Reporting bad numbers was a skill he'd learned young; you stripped the emotion out so the person hearing them could keep theirs. "We can't outrun them to the Spire. Not with the pace we've been keeping."

"We're two days out," Bram said. "Maybe less if we push."

"And they're one day behind us and gaining, because they don't stop to sleep. The shards will carry them through the nights." He hated saying it. He hated more what it meant. "Someone has to slow them down."

* * *

They argued about it in the lee of a glass formation shaped like a frozen wave, its curl hollowed out into a shelter that hummed faintly when the wind passed through. Pip wanted to run—wanted it badly, Sef could see it in the way she kept glancing south, her whole body angled toward flight like a compass needle. She was fourteen and half-Veyli and she had survived Corvane's streets by knowing exactly when to bolt, and every instinct she owned was screaming at her now.

"If we split up, they chase the biggest group," she said. "I can take the map and cut north. I'm fastest on the glass. You all know I am."

"You're not taking the map anywhere alone," Bram said, and there was something in her voice that made Pip's mouth snap shut—not anger, but the fierce, exhausted protectiveness of someone who had already decided how much more she was willing to lose. "Nobody carries it alone. That's not negotiable."

"Then what? We stand here?"

"No." Odji lowered himself onto a shelf of glass, moving carefully. Sef noticed then—he'd noticed for a day, actually, and said nothing, because saying it would make it real—that the healer's left hand wasn't folding properly into his sleeve. The fingers wouldn't close all the way. "We choose ground. If they're going to catch us, better they catch us where the glass works for us and not for them."

Sef looked at him sharply. "You have something in mind."

"The narrows." Odji nodded east, toward a place Sef remembered from the survey maps: a corridor where two vast sheets of steppe-glass had buckled against each other, rising into walls thirty feet high, the space between them barely wide enough for three abreast. The resonance there was chaotic—Pip had complained about it for an hour when they'd skirted its edge two days ago. "Sound behaves strangely in there. Echoes stack. If the shards work by imposing order—by making everything march to one frequency—then a place where frequencies tangle might be the one ground where they're vulnerable."

"It's a theory," Sef said.

"It's a very good theory," Odji said mildly, "and I am a very good theorist, and also we do not have a second option."

* * *

The narrows were worse than Sef remembered. The glass walls leaned overhead, fused and refused, streaked with colors that had no names—veins of violet and burnt gold running through the gray-green like something trying to bloom and petrifying mid-attempt. The sound hit them before they were fifty feet in: a layered drone, dozens of overlapping tones sliding against each other, none resolving. Pip clapped her hands over her ears and then, visibly deciding that was weakness, took them away again and set her jaw.

"It's arguing with itself," she shouted over it. "The glass. All these different songs at once and none of them agreeing."

"Can you read it?" Bram asked. She had to lean close, lips nearly touching Pip's ear.

"A little. Enough." Pip's eyes were narrowed, tracking something none of the rest of them could see. "There's a chamber ahead where the noise folds back on itself. Dead center of the narrows. If we stand there—" she made a shape in the air, "—everything that comes in gets scrambled. Sound, resonance, whatever the shards use. It'll be loud for us too. Really loud. Like standing inside a bell while someone rings it."

"For how long?" Sef asked.

Pip shrugged. "Until your ears bleed or theirs do. Whichever comes first."

They made their preparations with the economy of people who had stopped pretending this was anything other than desperate. Sef distributed what remained of their supplies into lighter packs. Bram unwrapped the map and studied it by the strange refracted light, her finger tracing the route to the Spire, and Sef watched her face do the thing it always did when she touched the map—that flicker of recognition, of homecoming, quickly buried. She caught him watching and looked away first. He let her. There were debts between them that couldn't be paid with apologies, and he'd stopped trying to collect.

Odji sat apart, working on his left hand with small precise motions, kneading the fingers straight. When Sef came to check on him, the healer didn't look up.

"When did it start?" Sef asked quietly.

"Three days ago. The night in the waystation." Odji flexed the hand; the fingers trembled, then obeyed, mostly. "Nerve damage. Old exposure—I spent years handling covenant-tainted materials in my clinic before the Wardens burned it. It accumulates. The steppe is aggravating it." He smiled, and it was his usual smile, wry and unhurried, the smile of a man who diagnosed himself the way he diagnosed everyone else: honestly, and without drama. "It's manageable."

"Odji."

"It's manageable, Sef." He said it gently, the way he said everything, and Sef understood that the gentleness was the wall. "Besides. If we survive tomorrow, I'll have a Spire full of covenant-magic to fix it. And if we don't survive tomorrow, my hand will be the least of anyone's problems."

* * *

They came at dusk.

The light on the glass went amber and long, stretching their shadows east toward the Spire, and out of that light the column resolved—twelve Wardens in gray, marching in perfect unison, and at their center a figure Sef had saluted for nine years.

Commander Vess had changed. He'd known her as a woman carved from duty, spare and hard, her authority resting on the fact that she never asked a soldier to bear what she wouldn't. Now she moved like something puppeted from within, too smooth, her stride eating distance without effort, and around her neck and wrists and belt hung shards—dozens of them, covenant-fragments the size of knuckles, drinking the dying light and giving back nothing. Her face was the same face. That was the worst part. She looked at Sef across four hundred yards of singing glass and her expression held no hatred, only assessment, the way she'd once assessed tactical maps.

He raised his hand—the old signal, palm out, *hold position*—and hated himself for the habit even as he did it.

"Bram," he said. "She wants to talk first. That's Vess. She always offers terms."

"Then I'll listen to terms." Bram stepped forward, and Sef's chest tightened, because of course she would, because she was a cartographer and cartographers believed terrain could be negotiated with, that every confrontation had a readable map. "Stay ready."

Vess stopped forty yards out. Behind her, the twelve halted as one body, boots striking glass in a single click.

"Sef." Her voice carried impossibly well across the resonance, or perhaps the glass carried it for her, eager for any new sound to multiply. "You're looking thinner. Exile doesn't suit you."

"Neither does corruption, Commander. Though you wear it with your usual efficiency."

Something flickered across her face—hurt, quickly filed away. That was Vess. Every wound became a data point. "I'm not corrupted. I'm *armed*. There's a difference, and you'd know it if you'd stayed." Her gaze shifted past him to Bram. "Cartographer. You have something that belongs to the Wardens. A relic of the covenant. Surrender it and the quarter-burn order is rescinded. Your companions walk free. You walk free. Corvane forgets you ever existed—which, given what you're planning at the Spire, may be the kinder fate."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I take it from you, and I take the Spire, and I hold both realms' ruins in my hand." Vess spread her arms slightly, and the shards along them caught the last light and pulsed, once, in unison. "I've seen what's coming, Sef. I've *felt* it through these. The barrier failing isn't a tragedy—it's an opportunity. Two realms, no covenant, no law but whoever holds the fragments. Someone has to rule the ruins. Better a Warden than chaos."

"That's not ruling," Sef said. "That's looting a burning house."

"That's *order*," Vess snapped, and for a moment the smoothness cracked and Sef saw the woman underneath, exhausted, terrified, clinging to the only framework that had ever made sense to her. "You think I wanted this? I held Corvane together for twenty years with rules and patrols and burn orders I hated signing. The covenant breaks, the magic comes back wild, and you want me to just—stand aside? Let the world dissolve? No. No. I will hold the line the only way left. Give me the map."

Bram's answer was quiet, and the glass carried it anyway. "This map isn't a weapon. It's a memory. And memories don't surrender."

Vess sighed—the sigh of a commander whose terms had been refused and who now had to spend lives she'd rather not spend. "Then I'm sorry, Sef. Genuinely." She raised her fist, and the twelve Wardens drew as one, and the shards flared white. "Take the map. Take them alive if you can. The healer and the girl are expendable."

They charged—and the narrows swallowed them.

* * *

What happened next, Sef would remember in fragments for the rest of his life, which was itself suddenly uncertain in length.

The Wardens hit the mouth of the narrows in perfect formation, and the formation died. The stacked echoes took their marching cadence and multiplied it, bent it, threw it back against itself until the twelve were stumbling into rhythms that didn't exist, their shard-imposed unity shattering against the tangled frequencies like a wave against rocks. Two fell immediately, their shards overloading, screaming steam. The others pressed on, firing suppression bolts that ricocheted off the glass walls and came back at angles that made no sense.

"Center chamber!" Pip screamed, hauling Bram by the sleeve. "Now! Move!"

They ran. Sef covered the rear, and it was covering the rear that saved his life, because he saw the bolt coming—saw Vess herself aiming through the chaos, her arm steady despite everything, her aim true despite everything—and he twisted, and the bolt meant for his spine took him across the shoulder instead, searing through coat and flesh, spinning him into the glass wall.

He slid down it. Got up. The pain was enormous and clarifying.

Ahead, the center chamber opened around them like the throat of a bell, and they crowded into it—Bram clutching the map, Pip white-knuckled against the resonance, Odji standing calm at the middle of the storm—and the sound closed over them like water.

Inside the chamber, the world was noise made solid. Sef felt it in his teeth, his bones, the wet architecture of his ears. The pursuing Wardens staggered at the threshold, their shards shrieking against the discord, and one by one the shards began to crack—hairline fractures spreading, light bleeding out of them like yolk from shells. Vess stood at the entrance, her own shards blazing, holding herself together by will and corruption, and she looked at Bram across the chamber and mouthed something Sef couldn't hear over the roar.

Then she did the thing Sef should have predicted and didn't, because love—or whatever his feeling for Bram had become, this stubborn ember that outlasted exile and betrayal and three years of silence—made him stupid.

She threw a shard.

Not at Bram. At the ceiling of the chamber, at the keystone of fused glass above them, and the impact rang the entire narrows like a struck gong, and the chamber's delicate tangle of frequencies collapsed into a single catastrophic note.

The resonance wave hit them all at once. Sef was thrown flat. Through watering eyes he saw the Wardens scattered like dolls, shards exploding off them in showers of dead light. He saw Vess standing untouched in a bubble of her remaining shards, watching, calculating, already retreating with what was left of her force. He saw Pip dragging Bram toward the far exit. He saw—

He saw Odji still standing at the center of the chamber, upright, arms half-raised, and the wave passing *through* him, and the healer's mouth open in a soundless cry as the resonance that should have killed them all found in him instead a channel—a body already tuned by years of tainted materials, nerves already frayed, a conductor waiting for the current—and took him.

When Sef reached him, the fight was over. Vess was gone, withdrawing south with six survivors and a look on her face that promised she'd learned from this failure the way she learned from everything. The surviving Wardens' dead shards littered the glass like shed scales. And Odji lay in Sef's arms, breathing in shallow, terrible pulls, his left side slack, his clever hands—both of them now—twitching with static.

"He channeled it," Pip whispered. She was crying without any of the noise she usually made about everything, tears cutting tracks through the dust on her face. "The wave. It needed somewhere to go and he—he stood in front of it. On purpose. I saw him. He *knew* and he stood there."

"Why?" Sef's voice came out wrecked. "Why would he—"

"Because the wave would have reached the map." Bram knelt beside them, and her voice was the flat calm again, but underneath it something was breaking. "Through me. The map is bound to me now—it would have poured all of that through me and burned the binding out, or burned *me* out. He did the arithmetic faster than any of us and chose the answer where he was the variable."

Odji's eyes opened. They found Bram first, and he smiled—that same unhurried smile, though it cost him visibly now, dragging itself up through the damage.

"The Spire," he rasped. Each word was a negotiation. "One day. Don't... let my hand be the reason... you're late."

"Shut up," Pip said fiercely, pressing her jacket under his head. "Shut up, you're fine, you're going to be fine, I'll carry you myself—"

"Pip." He turned his head with tremendous care. "Watch the northern... approaches. Vess learns fast. She won't come... the same way twice."

Sef tore open the healer's pack with shaking hands and found the poppy concentrate, the nerve-tonics, everything Odji had packed with the foresight of a man who had diagnosed his own decline weeks ago and said nothing. He dosed Odji with hands that wouldn't steady, and Odji watched him do it with an expression of mild professional criticism.

"Leftward tilt," the healer murmured. "On the needle. Honestly, Sef. Nine years... a Warden, and still..."

"Hold still."

"...amateur."

But his eyes were closing, and his breathing was going shallow in a way that Sef's field training recognized and hated, and the narrows hummed around them with a new note now—softer, almost tender, the glass singing something that sounded, horribly, like mourning.

They made a litter from packs and coats. Pip scouted ahead, moving with a fury that looked like grief's older sister. Bram walked beside the litter, one hand resting on Odji's blanket, the map pressed against her ribs with the other arm, and she didn't speak for a long time.

When she finally did, it was to Sef, quietly, without looking at him.

"He stood in front of it for me. For the map. For all of it." Her jaw worked. "And Vess will reach the Spire first if we slow down. She'll turn my mother's work into a throne."

"Yes."

"So we don't slow down." Bram looked at the horizon, where the Covenant Spire was visible now even at this distance—a thread of light against the darkening east, humming its patient, terrible invitation. "We go tonight. We push through the dark. And we pray the glass is kinder to a man being carried than it was to a man standing."

Sef adjusted the litter straps against his shoulder, feeling Odji's weight settle into the fabric, feeling the ache of his own burned shoulder and the larger ache beneath it that had no name he was willing to say aloud.

"One day," he said. "Then we end this."

Behind them, far to the south, a single point of white light flared against the dusk—Vess, regrouping, feeding her remaining shards, coming on.

Ahead, the Spire waited, and the third path waited inside it, and the price of the third path waited deepest of all.

They walked into the dark, carrying what was left of their family, toward everything that remained.
