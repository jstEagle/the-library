## 19. Aftermath of Light

The light did not explode. That was the first thing Sef would remember afterward, when he tried to piece the moment back together in the dark behind his eyes: it *bloomed*.

He was braced against a resonance-chamber pillar on the Spire's third landing, one arm locked around Pip's collar to keep her from running back into the heart-room, and the light came up through the floor like dawn through water—slow, total, green-gold at the edges where it passed through the fused memory-glass of the walls. It had no heat. It had no sound. It simply arrived everywhere at once, and every shard of glass in the tower lit like a struck bell made visible.

Then the sound came anyway.

Not an explosion—a chord. A single sustained note that Sef felt in his teeth, in the old Warden-blood that his grandmother had sworn ran thin in him but that he now understood had never been thin at all, only waiting. The note rose through the soles of his boots and up through the marrow of him, and somewhere inside it he heard voices—not words, not yet, but the shape of words, the way you hear a crowd through a wall.

Pip stopped fighting his grip. Her head tipped back. "It's singing," she said, and her voice cracked down the middle. "Sef. It's singing *back*."

"Bram," he said. It was all he could say. It was the only word left in him.

The heart-room door had blown open—or dissolved; he couldn't tell, the edges of everything were soft with light—and through it he saw them.

Bram stood at the center of the chamber with her arms out and her palms open, and the map was not a map anymore. It was a river of ink and light pouring off her hands, off her arms, off her face, streaming upward into the great fractured keystone of the covenant that hung above the chamber like a shattered moon held together by stubbornness. She was feeding herself into it. He could see it happening. Every second, something went out of her—the set of her shoulders changed, the line of her jaw softened, as if someone were gently erasing her from the inside out, feature by feature, memory by memory, and she was letting it happen with her chin lifted like a woman walking into weather.

And across the chamber, Commander Vess Rell was burning.

No—that wasn't right either. Vess was being *unmade*, and there was a difference, and the difference was choice. The corrupted shards she'd torn from the Spire's outer vault were fused to her gauntlets, her breastplate, the skin of her throat, drinking her the way she had drunk from them for years, and now the reforged covenant was calling its stolen pieces home. The shards were leaving her. They lifted off her body one by one, peeling away like scales, trailing threads of light back into the keystone, and each one that went took more of her with it. Not her flesh. Her *weight*. Her history. Sef watched twenty years of service go out of his commander's eyes, watched the ambition drain, then the certainty, then the grief underneath the certainty that had always driven her harder than any order ever had—until what stood in the ruined armor was a woman who looked around the chamber with the blank, blinking bewilderment of someone waking in a stranger's house.

She saw Bram. She opened her mouth.

"Who—" Vess said.

The last shard left her. The armor fell. And Vess Rell, who had burned three quarters of Corvane's low district to flush out one map-bearer, who had hunted Sef across the glass steppe with covenant-shards strapped to her fists like brass knuckles for God, sat down on the floor of the collapsing Spire and began to cry without knowing why.

* * *

The tower was coming apart around them, but gently, the way a fist comes unclenched.

Walls that had been fused memory-glass for three hundred years were remembering how to be something else. Sef watched a section of the eastern wall lose its transparency, cloud over, darken, crumble—not into shards but into grains, into soil, into a fine black loam that sifted down through the light and piled soft against the floor. The Spire was dying and composting in the same motion. Above them the keystone blazed whole now, seamless, a small white sun turning slowly on its axis, and from it the chord went out and out and out, through the steppe, through the veil, through whatever lay on the far side of both.

"Odji," Pip said suddenly, wrenching free of Sef's hand. "Where's Odji—he was on the second landing, he wouldn't leave the second landing—"

"Go," Sef said. "I've got her."

"You don't have anyone, look at her—" But Pip was already moving, scrambling down the stairwell with her feral grace, shouting Odji's name into the singing dark.

Look at her. Sef did.

Bram had lowered her arms. The river of light had thinned to a trickle, then to nothing, and the map was gone—truly gone, he realized, not folded away, not hidden, *gone*, spent, every line of it poured into the keystone above. She swayed on her feet. Her face was turned up toward the sun-wheel and it was the face of a stranger wearing her features: open, unguarded, faintly smiling, with none of the vigilance that had lived in it for three years, none of the flinch, none of the arithmetic of exits that Bram Joubert performed every waking hour of her life in Corvane. All of that was gone too. Everything was gone. She had paid with everything, exactly as Odji had warned, exactly as her mother's marginalia had promised, and she had done it without hesitation, and now she stood in the heart of the thing she had rebuilt and did not know its name or hers.

She turned. She looked directly at him.

For one insane half-second hope flared in Sef's chest—that some part of her would surface, that she'd say something dry and cutting about how long he'd taken to get here, that she'd call him by the name she'd used before he became a Warden, before either of them became what they'd become.

Her brow furrowed slightly, the way a person's does at a familiar smell they can't place.

"Do I know you?" she asked. Her voice was steady. Curious. Kind, even. It was the kindest cruelty he had ever endured.

"No," Sef said. His voice came out wrecked. He cleared his throat and tried again. "Not anymore."

"That's sad," she said, with such simple sincerity that he had to turn away. "I feel like it should be sad."

"It is," he said. "Come on. We need to move. This whole tower is coming down."

"Is it?" She looked around with genuine interest, taking in the crumbling walls, the raining soil, the weeping former commander sitting dazed in her underthings among the ruins. "It feels like it's just changing."

Of course it did. She would know better than anyone. She was the change.

The floor shuddered. Somewhere below, a load-bearing arch of glass gave way with a sound like a glacier clearing its throat, and the whole chamber tilted two degrees, and Sef stopped having the luxury of grief. He crossed the room in four strides, caught Bram around the waist, and hauled her toward the stairwell as the heart-room's ceiling let go in a long silent avalanche of black soil and golden dust.

Vess was still sitting there.

Sef looked back once. Every instinct drilled into him over fifteen years of service said *leave her, she'd have left you, she ordered your death twice.* But the woman on the floor wasn't his commander anymore. She was nobody's commander. She was a hollowed-out old soldier crying in a ruin, and the covenant above them hummed with the memory of everyone it had ever held, and Sef found he could not be the man who walked away, because he had spent three years being exactly that man and it had nearly killed the world.

"Pip!" he bellowed down the stairwell. "I need help! I've got two of them!"

* * *

They got Vess down eleven flights of a staircase that was becoming progressively less staircase and more hillside, the glass steps softening underfoot into packed earth, the walls sweating condensation that smelled like rain and cut grass. Bram climbed under her own power, which surprised him until he understood why: she didn't know enough to be afraid. Fear required a past. She moved through the disintegrating tower with the calm of a child in a garden, pausing twice to touch the walls where they were warmest, saying things like *"oh, that's lovely"* and *"listen, it's almost finished"* while Sef gripped her elbow and counted landings and hated himself for noticing, again, that she still tilted her head the same way when she listened.

At the fifth landing they found Odji.

He was propped against a wall with his leg at an angle legs don't go, gray-faced, conscious, and Pip was crouched over him with her hands pressed to the wound in his thigh and tears cutting tracks through the glass-dust on her cheeks. The shard-fragment the commander's men had put in him on the steppe had gone deep. He'd been holding the resonance-chamber's western anchor when it blew, and he'd held it long enough, and now the bill had come due.

"I'm fine," Odji said, which from a healer meant the opposite. "Mostly fine. Cosmetically fine."

"We're carrying you," Sef said.

"You're carrying me *carefully*. If you rupture the—yes, fine, carry me, but if I die I want it noted that I died complaining, which is the traditional Veyli manner of death."

Between Sef and Pip they got him up and moving, one of his arms over Sef's shoulders, Bram walking ahead with Vess's arm looped through hers—because Bram had taken one look at the weeping stranger and decided, with the uncomplicated compassion of a person with no history to complicate her, that no one should come down alone. Sef watched the two of them descend together, the woman who had erased herself and the woman whose self had been taken, and thought: *there's a symmetry there that would have interested her. There's a symmetry she'll never see.*

The last three flights were earth. Actual earth, dark and damp, threaded with pale roots that hadn't existed an hour ago. The Spire's foundations were transmuting beneath them, three centuries of petrified covenant thawing back into living ground, and the chord overhead had softened into something almost like breathing. By the time they reached the base, the great doors had already gone—dissolved into a stand of saplings growing through the doorway, their leaves translucent gold, trembling in a wind that smelled like the world before wardens.

Sef carried Bram the last hundred meters because she kept stopping to look at things.

* * *

Outside, the steppe was ending.

They came out onto what had been, six days ago, an endless plain of singing glass stretching to the horizon in every direction—blinding, lethal, beautiful, the petrified corpse of the old covenant. Sef had crossed it expecting to die on it. Now he stood at its edge and watched it die instead, and the dying was the most alive thing he had ever seen.

The glass was breaking up. Not shattering—*thawing*. Great panes of it slumped and softened and sank into the ground like ice on a spring pond, and where each pane went down, soil came up, black and wet and steaming faintly in the cool air. Grass followed the soil. Not slowly, not tentatively—grass came up like a tide, green waves rolling outward from the Spire's roots across the plain faster than anything growing had a right to move, and wildflowers punched through behind it, and somewhere out in that impossible advance a bird sang, a real bird, a bird that could not possibly have been there ten minutes ago unless the reforged veil was reaching into Veyl itself and pulling life through.

Pip stood hip-deep in new grass with Odji's weight forgotten on her shoulder, and she was laughing and sobbing at the same time, and the sound she made was answered—from very far away, beyond the shimmering line where the air bent and the other realm began—by voices. Human voices. Veyli voices, raised in something between a hymn and a cheer, drifting through the thinning veil like music through a wall finally opening into a door.

"They can see us," Pip whispered. "Sef. They can *see us*. There are people on the other side and they can see us."

The barrier wasn't gone. That was the miracle of it, the thing Sef would spend the rest of his life trying to explain to people who hadn't been there: Bram hadn't broken the wall between the realms and she hadn't sealed it. She had made it a *membrane*. Alive. Permeable. A threshold instead of a scar. The veil shimmered out there like heat-haze over a summer field, and through it the shapes of hills and towers and torchlit crowds wavered, close enough to touch, separate enough to stay whole.

Two worlds, no longer strangling each other. Both of them saved by a woman who couldn't remember saving them.

Bram stood beside him at the edge of the glass-that-was-soil, barefoot—she'd lost her boots somewhere in the tower and hadn't noticed—and the new grass was growing up around her ankles as she watched, actual grass, curling toward her like it knew. Her face was wet. Sef realized she was crying and that she didn't know why, and that this was somehow worse than if she'd known, worse than grief, because grief at least has an object.

"Why am I crying?" she asked him. Not dramatic. Just wondering. She wiped her cheek and looked at her wet fingers with mild scientific interest.

"Because you did something enormous," Sef said, "and the part of you that did it doesn't live in your head anymore. But it lives in everything else." He gestured—at the grass, the veil, the sky, the whole reborn world. "You're standing inside yourself. All of it. You're just the part that got to walk out."

She considered that for a long moment. The setting sun—it was evening, he realized; they'd gone into the Spire at dawn—caught her face and turned it gold.

"That sounds like a lot of responsibility," she said, "for someone I don't remember being."

"She'd have laughed at that," Sef said. His voice broke on the last word. He let it break. There was no one left to hide it from.

"Was she funny?"

"Ferociously. When she was terrified, especially. Especially then." He looked at the horizon, at the veil, anywhere but at her open, empty, waiting face. "She was a cartographer. The best there ever was. She mapped a country that didn't exist anymore and then she brought it back."

Bram was quiet. Then, softly, testing the shape of it:

"Bram," she said. "That's my name. Someone said it, down in the tower. It fits. Like a key." She flexed her fingers, looking at her own hands as though checking for ink stains. "I don't remember anything. But my hands feel like they know how to do something. Does that make sense?"

Sef's chest hurt. He put his hand into his coat and drew out the one thing he'd carried since Corvane burned, the thing he'd taken off her worktable that night without letting himself examine why: a stub of charcoal pencil, worn to a nub, tooth-marked at the end where she chewed it when she worked.

He put it in her palm.

Her fingers closed around it instantly, automatically, with the sureness of thirty years of habit, and something flickered across her face—not memory, not quite, but the ghost of memory's posture, the way a missing limb itches.

"Yes," she said slowly. "Yes. That makes sense."

Behind them, Pip called out—Odji needed binding, the leg was bad, and the light was going, and there was a village's worth of Veyli exiles starting to pick their way through the veil toward the new shore, drawn by the singing, and someone was going to have to meet them before they panicked. The world was demanding everything, immediately, as it always did.

But Sef stood one moment longer with Bram at the edge of the garden that used to be a graveyard, watching the first stars come out over two reconciled worlds, and she tugged his sleeve and pointed at the veil and said, "There are people coming through there. I think—I think I'm supposed to draw them. Is that strange? I feel like I'm supposed to draw everything."

"No," Sef said, and found, to his astonishment, that he was smiling through the wreckage of himself. "That's not strange at all. Come on. Let me introduce you to your family."

She frowned. "I don't have a family."

"You do," he said. "You just haven't met them yet. Give them a week."
