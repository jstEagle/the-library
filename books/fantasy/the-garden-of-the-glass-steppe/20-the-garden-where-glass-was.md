## 20. The Garden Where Glass Was

Pip had never been good at sitting still. Three years of thieving in Corvane's underbelly had taught her that stillness was for people with something to lose, and she'd spent most of her life making sure she wasn't one of them. But here, in the garden-country where the glass steppe used to be, she'd learned a new kind of stillness—the kind that came from holding a cup of water steady while someone who didn't know your name drank from it.

Bram sat propped against the crooked trunk of the first tree any of them had ever seen grow out of true soil. It was a spindly thing, all pale bark and new leaves, and Pip had named it *Stubborn* on the day it broke through the ground because it reminded her of herself. Bram leaned against Stubborn now with her hands open in her lap, palms up, the way she always sat when she was letting the world arrive without asking it questions.

Her eyes were clear. That was the worst part. Not empty—clear. Like windows washed so thoroughly there was nothing left on either side of the glass.

"Good morning," Pip said, for the hundredth time.

Bram looked at her with polite attention, the way you'd look at a stranger who'd greeted you warmly and whose name you'd already lost. "Good morning."

"I'm Pip."

"Yes." A small smile. "You told me yesterday. I'm sorry I've forgotten again."

"You haven't forgotten," Pip said, too fast, the way she said it every time. "It's not forgetting. Forgetting means it was there once and slipped away. This is—" She hunted for the word Odji used. "This is a room where the furniture hasn't arrived yet. We're just going to keep carrying pieces in until it looks like home."

Bram considered this with the grave patience she apparently hadn't lost along with everything else. "That's kind of you. To keep carrying things."

"Yeah, well." Pip shrugged and looked away, out across the garden. "I'm a thief. Carrying things is what I do."

* * *

The garden went on for miles now. Where the steppe had been a sheet of singing glass stretching to every horizon, there was grass, deep green and soft as moss, and wildflowers in colors Pip didn't have names for—colors that had leaked through the reforged veil from Veyl, probably, or maybe from somewhere new entirely. The barrier between realms held. Sef said it hummed when he pressed his palm flat to the ground, steady as a heartbeat, and Sef was a Warden even in exile; he knew what barriers were supposed to feel like.

People came through now. That was the miracle nobody had quite gotten used to. Veyli families crossed over in ones and twos, blinking in Corvane's gray light, and Corvani farmers walked out onto soil that had been deadly glass three months ago, and everyone stood around looking stunned and polite, like guests at a wedding where neither family spoke the same language. Pip had watched a Veyli child teach a Corvani farmer's daughter to whistle up a wind-spirit no bigger than a thumb, and the farmer had watched from the fence line with his fists clenched like he wanted to arrest the air itself, and then he'd unclenched them, slowly, and let his daughter laugh.

That was the world Bram had bought. She wouldn't remember buying it. She'd never know the price had been her.

Pip carried water from the stream—actual water, running over actual stones, where the glass had been—and thought about how unfair it was that grief required a person to be dead. Nobody had written rules for mourning someone alive. There were no rites for it. You couldn't burn a candle or say the words. You just got up every morning and brought them breakfast and they smiled at you like you were a stranger doing something inexplicably nice, and you smiled back and died a little inside, quietly, where nobody could see.

Except everybody could see. That was the other thing about found family: no privacy in grief.

* * *

Odji found her at midday, down by the stream, scrubbing a bandage that didn't need scrubbing. His clinic tent stood behind him—a real structure now, canvas and salvaged timber, with a queue of patients from both realms. He lowered himself onto a rock beside her with the careful movements of a man whose body had been broken on the glass and never quite forgiven it.

"You're doing the angry-washing," he observed.

"I'm doing helpful washing."

"Mm." He watched the water run red-tinged off cloth that had been clean an hour ago. "In Veyl we had a saying. *The river does not grieve the mountain it carves.*"

"That's a stupid saying."

"It is," he agreed comfortably. "Most sayings are. But it means the shaping is not the same as the loss. She shaped us, Pip. What she poured into the covenant didn't vanish. It became the banks that hold the river."

Pip wrung out the bandage hard enough to hurt her own hands. "She doesn't know my name."

"No."

"She doesn't know she has a name. She doesn't know she *had* one."

"No," Odji said again, gently. "But watch her face when Sef speaks. Watch her hands when she touches the soil. Some things went deeper than memory. The Spire took the story. It could not take what the story grew into."

"That's not enough."

"It isn't," Odji said. "You're right. It isn't. And yet." He gestured with his chin toward the tree on the rise, where Sef had appeared, walking up from the boundary stones with his Warden's coat finally gone, replaced by a plain traveler's cloak that made him look younger and more lost than the uniform ever had. "And yet."

* * *

Pip watched from a distance, the way she watched everything worth watching.

Sef sat down beside Bram under Stubborn, and Bram turned to him, and her whole face changed. Not recognition exactly—something older than recognition, something that lived below memory, in the marrow. Her shoulders came down. Her hands, which stayed carefully folded whenever anyone else approached, opened.

"You," she said, sounding pleased.

"Me," Sef said.

"I don't know your name."

"Sef."

"Sef." She tried it out, tasting it. And then she smiled—that smile, the one that undid Pip every single time, because it was the smile of a woman falling into comfort with someone she believed she'd just met, and it was also, unmistakably, the smile she'd worn in the waystation at the frozen pass, and on the glass at dawn, and in every small moment Pip had catalogued on their crossing without meaning to. The smile survived. The story around it was gone, but the smile remained, like a footprint after the walker has gone.

They talked. Pip couldn't hear the words, but she knew the shape of it: Sef telling her pieces of herself, carefully, the way you feed a fire one stick at a time so it doesn't gutter. He did it every day. He'd been doing it every day since he carried her out of the collapsing Spire with her eyes open and empty and her blood on his hands, and he would do it every day after, forever, if that was what it took. Pip had seen men hold walls under siege with less devotion.

Once, weeks ago, drunk on fermented Veyli honey-wine, Pip had screamed at him about it. *She looked at you like that before she ever met you, and you get to have it every day, and she doesn't even get to have YOU—* and Sef had taken it, all of it, standing in the rain, and when she finished he'd said, quietly, "I know. I'd give it back to her if I could. Every piece. I'd trade my whole history for one of her days." And then he'd gone back to sitting with Bram, every morning, telling her who she was.

Pip had apologized with stolen pastries. It was the best she could do. He'd eaten all six.

* * *

Evening came violet and gold over the garden-country. Pip climbed the low hill above the settlement with her supper wrapped in cloth, because this was the hour she kept for herself and for Bram—the hour when she didn't tend her, didn't carry things for her, but just talked. Told her the stories whether she retained them or not.

Bram liked the hill. They'd discovered that early. Something in her leaned toward high places with long views, some cartographer's instinct buried below the erasure, and so Pip brought her up here while the light went long and the two realms showed themselves in the distance: Corvane's smoke-gray skyline to the west, and to the east, faint as breath on glass, the shimmer of Veyl.

"Tell me what I did," Bram said tonight, unprompted.

Pip's heart did its familiar drop. "You want the whole thing?"

"I want the parts people won't tell me. Sef tells me the gentle parts. The old healer tells me wise parts. You—" she pointed at Pip with a stalk of grass, "—you look at me like I'm a debt you're trying to repay, and I'd like to know what I'm owed for."

Pip laughed despite herself. It came out wet. "You're not owed anything. That's the whole point. You paid everything."

"So tell me what everything was."

So Pip told her. She told it the way she knew how—not chronologically, because Pip had never respected chronology—but in pieces, the way a thief cases a room. The charm shop with its fake trinkets. The dying courier and the map that bled. The Wardens burning the quarter. The night market beneath the city, and how Bram had looked at Pip—feral, half-Veyli, thirteen kinds of trouble—and said *you can hear the glass sing? Show me,* and it was the first time in Pip's entire life an adult had treated the thing inside her like a gift instead of a disease.

"You picked me," Pip said. "Out of everybody. You didn't know me, and you picked me anyway. That's the part nobody tells you about heroes. It's not the big sacrifice at the end. It's the picking. All along the road. You kept picking us."

Bram listened with her hands open in her lap, and when Pip ran out of words, she was quiet a long moment, looking east, where Veyl shimmered.

"The map," Bram said suddenly. "Everyone mentions a map. A living one. Where is it?"

Pip went still. "It burned with the Spire. Went into the covenant with everything else. Why?"

Bram's hand had drifted, without her seeming to notice, to the bare soil beside her. Her fingers moved on it—small, unconscious strokes, tracing lines only she could see. Pip watched, breath caught, as Bram drew in the dirt of the hilltop: a curve, a branching, a mark like a star at a junction.

Not random. Pip knew those marks. Every kid raised anywhere near the veil knew them. Waypoint signs. Cartographer's shorthand.

"Bram," Pip whispered. "What are you drawing?"

Bram looked down at her own hand as if surprised to find it moving. She frowned at the lines in the soil—frowned the way you frown at handwriting you almost recognize—and then the frown cleared, and she smiled that smile, and said, lightly, puzzled:

"I don't know. But it feels like the beginning of a road."

Far to the east, past the garden's edge, where the veil shimmered against the darkening sky, something flickered. Once. Twice. Like a signal. Like a door being tested from the other side.

Pip stood up slowly, her heart hammering a rhythm she recognized from the old days—the sound the glass used to make, translated into blood.

"Bram," she said. "I think we're going to need a mapmaker."
