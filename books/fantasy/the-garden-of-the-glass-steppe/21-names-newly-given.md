## 21. Names, Newly Given

The garden had a rule, though no one had written it down: Bram was never left alone with her own silence for longer than an hour.

It wasn't smothering. Pip had tried to explain it once, sitting cross-legged on the low wall outside the cottage while Bram shelled beans beside her, and the explanation had come out sideways, the way most of Pip's truths did. "It's like when you're learning a song," she'd said. "You can't just hear it once. You have to keep playing it until your hands know where to go without asking." She'd plucked a bean pod from the pile and split it with her thumbnail. "Your head's learning a song. We're the song."

Bram had looked at her then—this sharp-boned girl who could hear glass singing from three realms away—and said, quietly, "I don't remember learning anything."

"No," Pip agreed. "But your hands do." And she'd nodded at Bram's fingers, which were shelling beans in a fast, practiced rhythm that belonged to someone else's life.

So they told her. Every day, in pieces, like feeding a fire one stick at a time.

* * *

Odji took mornings.

He came up from the clinic below the garden terraces—he still ran one, because a healer stopped being a healer the way a river stopped being wet—and he brought tea and a chair and a patience that seemed geological. He would sit across from her at the little table under the trellis and begin wherever he'd left off, as if her memory were a book he'd simply paused in.

"You ran a charm shop in Corvane," he said this morning. "Gray Street, between a dyer's and a man who sold birds he couldn't feed. You sold trinkets dressed up as charms. Copper rings, knotted thread, bottles of colored sand."

"That sounds dishonest," Bram said.

"It was honest," Odji said. "That was the joke of it. People bought hope from you and you gave them something real instead—a thing they could hold. Half of Corvane's charms were lies that worked because people believed. Yours were true things that pretended to be lies so you wouldn't be caught." He sipped his tea. "You were very good at it. You were good at being invisible. You hated every day of it."

"Did I tell you that?"

"You didn't have to. You had a way of standing in a room like you were waiting for permission to leave it." He set down his cup. "You came to my clinic with a cut hand, three years ago. You paid me with a map you drew of the tunnels under the city, because I treated exiles and fugitives and we needed ways out that the Wardens didn't know. That map saved eleven people before the Wardens found the first entrance. Eleven. I counted."

Bram turned her hand over, studied the palm as if the map might still be written there. "I drew maps even then."

"You never stopped. That's what Sef says. You can stop a river but not the water in it."

* * *

Pip took afternoons, and Pip's lessons were nothing like Odji's.

Pip taught by doing. She dragged Bram out into the garden rows and made her name things—every plant, every tool, every bird that landed on the fence. When Bram got one wrong, Pip corrected her without mercy. When she got one right, Pip grinned like she'd won something.

"That's a trowel."

"Trowel."

"That's a hoe. Don't make that face, everyone makes that face at hoes."

"Hoe."

"And that—" Pip pointed at the far hedge, where a small brown bird was scolding them both, "—is a wren, and she thinks we're stealing from her, and she's right, because I am." She produced a handful of seeds from somewhere in her coat, because there was always somewhere in Pip's coat. "Here. You throw them. She'll take them from you eventually. Took me two weeks. You've got her beat by four days already."

Bram threw the seeds. The wren watched with bright, judgmental eyes.

"Now tell me about the shop again," Pip said. "The part with the Warden patrol."

Bram sighed, but she told it. She knew the shape of it now, the way you know a story you've heard many times—the near-catch, the customer who wouldn't leave, the moment she'd stood behind her counter with her heart hammering while gray-uniformed men examined her bottles of colored sand. It didn't feel like memory. It felt like a story she'd been given. But her body reacted anyway; her breath went shallow at the right moments, her shoulders tightened when the patrol's boots crossed the floor of the telling.

"See," Pip said, watching her face. "You flinch in the same places every time. That's how I know it's yours. A borrowed story doesn't make you flinch."

* * *

Sef took evenings, and Sef's tellings were the hardest and the gentlest, and Bram never quite knew which she was getting until she was inside one.

He came at dusk, when the light went long and gold over the terraces, and he sat with her on the bench by the wall where the first apple tree had taken root in soil that had been glass less than a season ago. He told her about Corvane. About the Wardens. About himself.

"You were hiding," he said tonight. "And I was hunting. Not you—not yet. But the same machine. I wore the uniform for nine years. I believed it kept people safe. Then I found traces of real cartography in a dead courier's effects, and the signature was one I'd known since I was nineteen years old, and I spent three days trying to convince myself I was wrong."

"Were you?"

"No." His voice was even, unhurried. He never rushed these stories; rushing them, she'd learned, made her feel like she was falling. "You draw your coastlines with a slight lift at the end of each stroke. Like the pen is reluctant to let go. I used to tease you about it. I called it your hesitation mark. You said it wasn't hesitation, it was respect—you were letting the land finish its own sentence."

Bram looked down at her hands. "Do I still do that?"

"I haven't seen you draw yet." Something moved through his voice, quickly, gone. "When you do, I'll tell you."

"What else?" she asked, because she always asked, because some part of her was greedy for herself in a way that frightened her a little. "Tell me the rest."

"The rest is hard."

"I know. Tell it anyway."

He was quiet for a while. The wren had gone to bed; the garden sounds had softened to wind and the creak of young trees.

"When the Spire ignited," he said finally, "you chose to pour everything into the covenant. Your memories. Both worlds' worth. You did it to save Veyl and Corvane together, and it worked, and the cost was—" his jaw tightened— "the cost was you. What woke up on the hillside was you, but new. Blank pages with the same handwriting underneath." He turned to look at her, and his eyes in the dusk were steady and full of something he was careful not to name. "We carried you out. I carried you. And I have sat on this bench every evening since and told you who you were, because nobody should have to be a stranger to themselves."

"Do you think I'll ever remember?" Bram asked. "Truly?"

"Some comes back as dreams," Sef said. "Odji says that's the covenant settling—your memories are in the veil now, holding it shut, and sometimes a piece leaks back through. You dreamed about the night market last week. You described the lanterns before anyone told you about them."

"I dreamed about a woman singing off-key," Bram said. "Was that real?"

A smile touched his mouth, brief and real. "That was you. In the aqueducts, escaping Corvane. Pip complained about it for days."

"I would never sing off-key."

"You absolutely would. You sang like a cartographer—enthusiastic, entirely unbothered by accuracy." He paused. "The dreams are yours. The rest—" he gestured gently at the garden, the terraces, the whole green country spreading beyond the wall— "the rest we retell until it feels true. And then, someday, it isn't a story anymore. It's just your life. The one you're living now counts too. It counts double, maybe. You're living it awake."

Bram was silent. Then: "The way you say certain things. When you talk about before. There's something underneath it."

"Yes," Sef said.

"Should I know what it is?"

His breath went out slowly. "Not yet. When you're ready—or when I am—I'll tell you. It's in the story. It keeps. Everything about us keeps."

She reached over, not knowing why except that it felt true, and put her hand over his on the bench between them. He didn't move. He didn't breathe, quite, for a moment. But he turned his hand up beneath hers, careful as a man handling a living map.

* * *

Some nights, the dreams did the teaching.

Bram dreamed of a tower of fused glass humming with a thousand voices, and of her own hands pressed flat against its heart, giving everything away. She dreamed of a mother whose face was always turned toward a horizon, drawing lines that held two worlds together. She dreamed of a dying stranger folding a map into her hands and whispering words she now understood: *the Spire remembers you.*

She woke from that one with tears on her face and no idea whose grief they belonged to, and found Pip already awake in the next room, because Pip always knew, somehow, the way she knew the glass's moods.

"Bad one?" Pip asked.

"A sad one. An important one, maybe." Bram sat up, wiped her face. "Pip. Did I know you? Before?"

Pip was quiet a moment, turning something over. "You pulled me out of a storm of broken memory on the steppe," she said. "I ran because I found out sealing the rift meant erasing half of what I am. And you came after me into the singing glass, and you held onto me, and you said—" Pip's voice did something complicated— "you said, 'Nobody gets erased while I'm holding the pen.' So yeah. You knew me. And I figure you still do. You just haven't met yourself yet."

"Is that how you all think of it? That I haven't met myself?"

"It's how *I* think of it." Pip shrugged, fierce and casual at once. "Meeting someone takes time. Look how long it took the wren to trust you."

* * *

On the first morning of autumn, Bram asked for paper.

They gave it to her without ceremony—Odji produced a folio from the clinic stores, Pip sharpened a charcoal stub against the doorframe, Sef said nothing at all but his stillness filled the room. Bram sat at the table under the trellis with a blank page in front of her, and the blankness looked back at her, vast and patient.

"I don't know what I'm going to draw," she said.

"Doesn't matter," Pip said. "Draw what's true."

Bram picked up the charcoal. Her hand hovered—and then moved, almost without her, the way her fingers had shelled beans that first week. A line went down. Another. A coastline, lifting slightly at the end of each stroke, hesitant, respectful, letting the land finish its own sentence.

Sef made a small sound, quickly swallowed.

"There," Bram murmured, half to herself, watching the shape grow under her hand. "There it is."

It was not the steppe. It was not Corvane or Veyl or any country she'd been told about. It was something at the edge of the reforged veil—something stirring, faint and far, past the garden's boundary where the old maps ended. New coastline. New world. Waiting to be named.

She lifted the charcoal, and at the end of the final stroke her hand hesitated—respectfully—and finished the sentence.

"Well," said Odji softly, from the doorway. "She's back."

"Not back," Bram said, and smiled at the page, at the family gathered around it, at the unnamed country rising in her mind like a tide. "Forward."
