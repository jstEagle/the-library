## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

The house on Candlewick Row smelled of beeswax and dying, which was, in Sable Pemberton's professional opinion, the most honest combination a wealthy household could manage.

She sat in the matriarch's bedchamber with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and her tools laid out on a folded linen cloth: three silver needles no longer than her smallest finger, a spool of thread that wasn't thread at all but spun moonlight—expensive, genuine, smuggled up from the coast at ruinous cost—and a small glass vial stoppered with wax. Everything gleamed. Everything was clean. Cleanliness mattered in her trade; a dirty needle left smudges in a memory, and nobody wanted their mother's last smile delivered with a smear of someone else's grief across it.

"Will it hurt her?" asked the son.

His name was Aldous Fenwick, he was fifty-two years old, and he had been crying in the corridor for the better part of an hour before working up the courage to knock. Sable had heard him through the door—she heard most things through doors; it was half her craft—and had used the time to arrange her needles by size, which was not necessary but gave nervous clients something to watch instead of weeping.

"Not a bit," she said. "That's rather the point. She'll feel as though she's dozing in a warm bath while I take down a picture from the wall. She may dream it again while I work. Most people find that pleasant."

"And afterward?" His voice cracked on the word. "Afterward she won't—"

"She won't remember it," Sable said gently. "That's what you're paying for, Mr. Fenwick. Your mother's happiest hour, whole and perfect, so you can keep it when she's gone. She'll have the peace of having lived it. You'll have the keeping of it. Nobody gets cheated."

He looked at his mother—the old woman propped against her pillows like a doll someone had loved too hard, her hands folded, her breath coming in long pauses between short arrivals—and then back at Sable, and asked the question they all eventually asked, usually in exactly this tone of ashamed wonder:

"How did you even get into this line of work?"

Sable smiled her professional smile, the one that cost nothing and revealed less. "Badly paid alternatives."

* * *

The extraction itself took forty minutes and required Sable to hold the old woman's hand the entire time, which was standard practice and also, though she would never have admitted it under torture, her favorite part of any job.

Memory-work was delicate in the way that surgery was delicate, but where a surgeon cut flesh, Sable navigated meaning. She'd slide a needle into the soft hollow below the wrist—not into a vein, never into a vein, amateurs went for veins and ended up pulling out whole years like rotten teeth—and follow the thread of moonlight down into the warm dark where a life was stored.

Inside, memories were not pictures. That was the first thing every thief learned and every client refused to believe. They were *rooms*. This one was a kitchen in high summer, all gold light and flour dust, and a young woman—sixty years younger, laughing so hard she had to grip the table—while a man off-frame said something Sable couldn't hear because the memory belonged to Odile Fenwick and only contained what Odile had noticed, which was the laugh, the light, the flour on her own hands, and the enormous private certainty that she was, in this moment, happier than anyone alive.

Sable stood at the edge of the room inside the memory, careful not to touch anything, the way you'd stand in a stranger's house. Then she began the harvest: loosening the memory from its moorings, gathering it like water in cupped hands, feeling it grow heavy and bright and warm against her palms.

It was beautiful work. That was the trap of it. You could not do this job well if you didn't love memories, and you could not love memories without wanting, sometimes, terribly, to keep one.

Sable drew the memory out, coiled it onto the spool, sealed it in the vial, and pressed the wax with her thumb. When she opened her eyes, Odile Fenwick was looking at her with mild, empty contentment, like a woman who had just woken from a nap she couldn't quite recall but felt good about anyway.

"There," Sable said softly. "All done."

* * *

Aldous Fenwick held the vial up to the window light and wept again, though these were different tears, the grateful kind, and he pressed a purse into Sable's hands that weighed considerably more than her fee. She returned the excess without comment. Overcharging the grieving was bad for business and worse for sleep.

"You're certain it's all there? The whole day?"

"The whole day," Sable confirmed. "Every detail she noticed. Mind how you store it—cool, dark, away from iron. And Mr. Fenwick?" She paused at the door. "When you play it back, don't do it alone the first time. Memories that big want witnesses."

He nodded, wet-eyed, and thanked her twice more, and the maid saw her out, and Sable Pemberton stepped down from the finest house on Candlewick Row into the grey afternoon of Hollowmere with thirty crowns in her purse and, as always, nothing else.

That was the rule. Her rule. The only rule she had never broken, not once, not ever, in eleven years of the trade:

*Never keep what you steal.*

Other memory-thieves kept souvenirs. She knew them—a woman in Lowmarsh who wore a dead lover's first kiss like a locket, taking it out on bad nights until it wore thin as gauze; a ferryman's boy who sold pieces of other people's courage because he had none of his own. It rotted you, keeping. A stolen memory didn't live in you right; it grew moldy edges, bled its color into your own recollections until you couldn't tell which sorrows were yours. Sable had seen thieves go hollow as gourds, stuffed with other people's summers and empty of their own.

So she extracted, she sealed, she delivered, and she walked away clean. Every time.

Well.

*Almost* every time.

* * *

Her rooms above the chandler's shop were cold, so she lit the small stove and made tea she couldn't really afford and sat at the narrow table by the window with her purse of thirty crowns and her single act of hypocrisy.

It lived in a plain tin locket, dented, worth perhaps two coppers, hanging on a nail behind the door where a picture might hang in a richer home. Inside the locket, folded small and tight as a moth in winter, was a memory: a woman's face, turning toward a little girl, smiling.

Her mother's face.

Sable did not open the locket often. Once a season, maybe. On her birthday. On nights when the wind came down off the boundary hills and moaned in the chimney like something asking to be let in. She told herself it was practical—an asset, contraband of unusual quality, the only memory she owned outright—but she knew the shape of a lie that well, at least, having handled ten thousand of them.

The truth was that her mother had died when Sable was nine, in the fever year, and grief had eaten the memory the way damp eats plaster—softly, gradually, everywhere at once. By the time Sable was grown, she could remember everything about her mother except the thing that mattered: the face. She remembered the smell of rosemary and lamp oil. She remembered rough hands braiding her hair. She remembered a voice singing something about a garden, low and off-key. But the face itself had gone the way of dreams at noon, and there are few terrors quieter than realizing you are losing someone you already lost.

Except—her mother had known. Mothers know. In the last week, feverish and frightened and fierce about it, she had pressed her thumb to Sable's wrist, in the very hollow where Sable would one day learn to place a needle, and had done something to her there, something quick and secret and desperate, and whispered: *"Keep this one, my love. Whatever rules you make later. Keep this one."*

So Sable had kept it. One theft. One treasure. The crack beneath the polish, the hypocrisy at the heart of her professionalism, and she guarded it the way misers guard coins and saints guard relics, which is to say badly, obsessively, and with constant prayer.

Tonight she took the locket down. She told herself it was because the wind was moaning and the tea was weak and the day had been full of other people's happiness, which it had—that golden kitchen, that laughter, that enormous private certainty, handed over to a son and gone now from the world except in glass.

She opened the locket.

The memory unfolded like a hand.

Rosemary. Lamp oil. Late afternoon light through a window she had never been able to place, in a house she could not remember living in—which was odd, when she thought about it, which she tried not to. Her mother turned, mid-motion, hair escaping its braid, and smiled at a small girl just off-frame, and the smile was the whole of it: unguarded, delighted, lit from inside like a lamp behind amber glass.

Sable watched it until it closed again, and put the locket back on its nail, and sat for a while in the cooling room with her hands around the cold teacup, being a professional woman with a clean ledger and one small sin, and telling herself—as she had told herself for eleven years—that this was enough, that this was safe, that the valley of Hollowmere was dull and warded and shut, and that nothing here would ever ask more of her than needlework and discretion.

Outside, far off toward the hills, the first boundary stone began to hum.

She wouldn't hear it. Not tonight. It was too low, too old, a note felt in the teeth rather than the ear, and Sable was tired, and the stove needed feeding, and the locket hung quiet on its nail behind her.

But the stone hummed anyway, wrong, like a bone creaking under a weight it had carried too long—and somewhere beyond the wards, past the hills, in a court she had never heard of, something ancient turned its attention toward a valley it had never forgotten, and began, patiently, to count the daughters of Lys.
