## 2. Wards Like Old Bones

The trouble with living in a valley that hated magic was that you learned to hate it too, the way you learn to hate a relative who ruined your mother's life and then had the decency to die before you could confront him.

Sable Pemberton felt this most keenly on market days, when she had to walk past the ward-stones at the foot of Hollowmere's western road. There were nine of them, one for each of the Ninefold Court's old provinces — or so the schoolmistress had claimed, though Sable suspected the woman had invented half her history lessons out of boredom. The stones were tall as men, gray as dishwater, and carved with spirals so worn they looked like fingerprints left by a giant who'd given up halfway through.

They were also humming.

Not singing. Not chiming. Humming — the way a struck bell hums after everyone has stopped listening, the way a hive hums when the bees have decided something. Sable had walked past those stones four thousand times in her twenty-six years, and in all that time they had been silent as furniture. Now, on a bright autumn morning thick with the smell of apples and horse dung, they were making a sound like a held breath.

She stopped. She listened. A cart rattled past behind her, and its driver called out a cheerful obscenity about people standing in roads, and she stepped aside without looking at him because she could not look away from the stones.

"Hm," said Sable Pemberton, which was, for a memory-thief, an unusually honest expression of feeling.

* * *

She told herself she'd imagined it. This was a professional habit. When you spent your days reaching into other people's heads and pulling out their brightest moments, you developed a healthy suspicion of your own senses. Half the time what people remembered wasn't what happened; the other half of the time, what happened wasn't worth remembering. Sable had extracted a man's treasured memory of his wedding day once and found, underneath the roses and the string quartet, the groom quietly counting the exits. People were unreliable narrators even to themselves. Why should she be any different?

So she went about her business. Her business that morning was Mrs. Halloway, the apothecary's widow, who wanted to forget the winter her husband coughed himself into the ground and was willing to pay a scandalous sum for the privilege. Sable took the money, did the work, and left the widow lighter by one bad memory and heavier by one suspiciously light purse, which was how these transactions always balanced out.

But all through the extraction — hands hovering over the widow's temples, the familiar taste of someone else's grief arriving on her tongue like copper coins — Sable kept hearing that hum. It threaded under the widow's sorrow like a second heartbeat. And afterward, walking home along the river path with her collar up against the wind, she realized the sound wasn't only at the stones anymore.

It was everywhere. Faint as a mosquito in a cathedral, but everywhere. In the water. In the ground. In the spaces between her own thoughts, if she was honest, and Sable prided herself on being honest in exactly one direction: inward.

The wards of Hollowmere were not holding.

They were *straining*.

* * *

There was exactly one person in Hollowmere older than the wards themselves, and he ran the ferry.

Mag had been ferrying people across the Mere since before Sable's mother was born, which made him either ninety or immortal, depending on which story you believed, and Mag told both stories himself with equal conviction. He was a barrel of a man with a beard like storm-clouds and hands like tree roots, and he occupied a small wooden hut at the crossing where he drank tea, cheated at cards, and dispensed wisdom at prices ranging from free (if he liked you) to ruinous (if he didn't).

Sable liked him, mostly. He liked her back in the way of two people who have each caught the other doing something disreputable and agreed, silently, never to mention it.

She found him mending a net on his dock, which was a performance, since no fish had willingly entered the Mere in living memory. The water there was black and still as spilled ink, and the locals swore nothing lived in it, and the locals were right, and nobody asked why, because asking why was how you got answers in Hollowmere, and answers were frowned upon.

"Mag," Sable said, sitting down beside him on the dock with the ease of long acquaintance. "Tell me about the ward-stones."

"No."

"I haven't asked anything yet."

"You were going to ask about the ward-stones. I heard it in your walk." He didn't look up from his net. "You've got your asking-walk on. Shoulders forward, eyes narrow, like a cat deciding whether the furniture owes it money."

"That is my normal walk."

"It's your asking-walk." He tied off a knot. "And the answer's no, because there's nothing to tell, because the stones are just stones, and anyone who says otherwise is either mad or selling something."

"Which are you?"

"Both, historically." But his hands had gone still on the net, and Sable had spent her whole life reading the pauses between words, and Mag's pause was long enough to drive a cart through.

She waited. Waiting was her best skill. You couldn't rush a memory loose any more than you could rush a plum ripe, and Mag was a very old, very tough plum.

The wind came off the Mere and smelled, for the first time she could remember, faintly of ozone. Of storms that hadn't arrived yet. Of something burnt.

Mag set down the net. He looked at the water for a while, then at the far shore, where the westernmost stone stood visible above the reeds, gray and patient.

"They used to sing," he said at last.

"The stones?"

"The stones." He said it the way you'd say *the dead*. "Every year, on the longest night. The whole valley would come out — every soul, from the mayor down to the beggars — and we'd bring offerings. Bread. Salt. Wool. A cup of wine from every household, poured into the roots." His voice had gone somewhere else, somewhere decades south of here. "And the stones would drink it in, and they'd sing. Low, mind. Not a tune you'd dance to. More like... like the sound a house makes settling. Like something enormous turning over in its sleep and being comfortable."

"What were they being fed *for*?"

Mag was quiet for so long that Sable thought he'd fallen asleep sitting up, which he did sometimes, mid-sentence, mid-meal, once mid-swim.

"To keep something fed," he said finally, "is to keep it asleep. Or to keep something else asleep. My mother said it was thanks. My grandmother said it was rent. The priest we had when I was a boy said it was superstition and then, I noticed, always came to pour his own cup first and last." He snorted. "Nobody agreed on what the feeding was *for*, girl. We just knew the valley stayed safe, and the safe thing to do was not ask why the safety needed feeding."

"And when did it stop?"

Another silence. This one had teeth in it.

"Before your time. Before mine, near enough. There was a year — I was a young man, strong as an ox and twice as stubborn — when the council decided the ritual was pagan nonsense. Wasteful. All that good wine going into dirt." He shook his head slowly. "Old Tam Fletcher stood up at the meeting and said, 'You don't stop paying a debt just because the collector's been quiet.' They laughed him out of the hall. Stopped the feeding that same winter."

"And the stones?"

"Went quiet." Mag's eyes came around to her at last, and they were not the eyes of a man telling stories on a sunny dock. They were the eyes of a man who had been waiting sixty years for someone to ask the right question, and hating himself for hoping nobody would. "Quiet as the grave. For forty years, quiet. And now you're telling me they're humming again."

"I'm telling you they're humming *wrong*," Sable said. "Like a bell that's cracked. Like something straining against—"

"Aye." He cut her off gently. "I know the sound. I heard it once before, the night before the feeding stopped working properly, the last year they did it. The stones sang sweet that night, but underneath the song there was this—" he tapped his chest, twice, over the heart, "—this knock. Like a guest at a door who'd been kept waiting too long and had stopped being polite about it."

The wind came again, and this time Sable tasted it clearly: burnt sugar and lightning. Somewhere across the valley, faint as a memory of a memory, the nine stones hummed their cracked-bell note, and the Mere lay black and still beneath them, and not one fish stirred in it, and Sable understood suddenly, with a thief's instinct for a locked door, that the stillness of the water had never been natural at all.

The Mere wasn't empty. The Mere was *held*.

Everything in Hollowmere was held.

"Mag," she said slowly, "what exactly were the wards keeping out? Or—" she watched his face, "—keeping *in*?"

And here was the thing she would think about later, in the terrible weeks to come, turning it over like a coin she should have refused: Mag didn't answer right away. He looked at the water, and he looked afraid, genuinely afraid, the first time she'd ever seen it on him, and then he reached into his coat and brought out a clay cup, chipped and ancient, and set it on the boards between them.

"My grandmother's pouring-cup," he said. "Kept it after they stopped. Foolish sentiment." He turned it in his root-knuckled fingers. "I've thought about filling it every winter since. Just once. Just to be polite. Never dared." He looked up at her, and his voice dropped to almost nothing. "You want to know what the wards keep in, girl? So do I. That's the truth of it. Nobody in this valley ever knew. We were told there was nothing out there worth fearing, and we were told it so often and so kindly that we stopped noticing we'd never once been allowed to climb the ridge and look."

"Never?"

"Ferries go north-south, girl. Always have. Roads go round the valley floor. The ridgelines belong to the stones." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, warm and broken. "We weren't refugees in a refuge. We were the contents of a jar, and somebody forgot to tell us we'd been pickled."

Sable sat with that. Around them the afternoon went on being ordinary — a dog barking, laundry snapping on a line, children shouting by the mill — and every ordinary sound now sounded to her like noise inside a sealed room.

"One more question," she said.

"You're spending your inheritance."

"When did the humming start? Exactly."

Mag considered. "Three nights past. Why?"

Because three nights past, Sable had done a job. A rich job. A strange job — a dying woman in the big house on the hill, who had gripped Sable's wrist with shocking strength as the extraction ended and whispered, *you have her eyes, child, you have Lys's eyes,* and then died smiling, and Sable had told herself the woman was simply confused, dying people always were—

She hadn't told Mag about that. She wasn't going to. Some doors you didn't open until you had to.

"No reason," said Sable Pemberton, lying smoothly, professionally, the way she did everything except inward. "Probably nothing. Old stones settle. Everything settles."

"Aye," said Mag. "And avalanches start with settling, too."

He picked up his net again. The conversation was over; she could feel it close like a shop shutter. But as she rose to go, he spoke once more, not looking at her, looking at the western stone above the reeds, which was humming its cracked, patient, held-breath note into the darkening air.

"Girl. Whatever your asking-walk is walking toward — walk faster. Things that have been kept waiting sixty years..." He tied a knot with unnecessary force. "They don't stay polite forever."

* * *

That night Sable climbed to her rooms above the chandler's shop, bolted the door, and took out the one thing she owned that broke her own rule.

One memory, kept. Her mother's face. Stored in a locket of dull silver, worn smooth by her thumb, illegal by her own law and priceless beyond any currency she dealt in. She held it every night the way other people prayed — briefly, guiltily, and with more need than faith.

Tonight, when she opened it, the memory flickered.

Just at the edges. Just for a heartbeat. Her mother's face — warm, laughing, half-turned toward a window full of green summer light — shivered like a reflection in disturbed water, and in that shiver Sable saw, impossibly, superimposed for less than a blink, something else. A garden. Vast and impossible, flowers the size of cartwheels, a sky with too many stars in it. A boy's hand in a woman's hand. A crown, far off, gleaming like a wound.

Then it was gone, and it was only her mother's face again, steady and dear, and Sable sat on the edge of her bed with her heart slamming and the locket burning cold in her palm.

Outside, far off at the valley's edge, the nine stones hummed.

And beneath the hum — faint, patient, no longer entirely polite — came a knock.
