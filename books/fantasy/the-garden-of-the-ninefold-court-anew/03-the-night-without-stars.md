## 3. The Night Without Stars

The trouble with a valley, Sable Pemberton had always thought, was that you could only leave it in so many directions. Hollowmere offered exactly three: the ferry road east, the shepherd's pass north, and the long switchback south that took two days and cost your knees. She had spent twenty-six years cataloguing these exits the way other people counted coins, and on the night every star went out at once, she was thinking about them again—specifically about how none of them would matter.

It began with the light going strange.

She was walking home from the Tallow house along the river path, her satchel heavy with a payment she hadn't yet decided how to feel about, when she noticed that the shadows had stopped behaving. Shadows were her trade, in a way; she spent her life in dim rooms watching what people's faces did when their memories left them, and she knew shadow the way a fisherman knows water. And the shadows tonight were wrong. They lay too flat. They pointed all in one direction, as though the whole valley stood before some enormous window with nothing behind it.

She stopped on the bridge above the millrace and looked up.

The sky was empty.

Not clouded—she knew clouded, clouded had texture, had weight, had the grey belly of weather moving somewhere. This was different. This was a ceiling. The stars had not been covered. They had been *taken*, all of them, in the same instant, the way a thief lifts a purse: no struggle, no witnesses, just the sudden absence of something you'd have sworn was fastened down.

Sable stood on the bridge for a long time. A minute. Five. Long enough for Mag's ferry to cross below her twice, its lantern swinging, the old man humming his tuneless hum. She didn't call out to him. Her voice seemed to belong to someone else, someone standing further off, watching a woman on a bridge fail to understand the sky.

Then the wards broke.

She felt it before she heard it—a sensation like a tooth finally giving way after weeks of ache, a release so total it was indistinguishable from catastrophe. The boundary stones, which she had passed ten thousand times without a second glance, which she had resented the way you resent walls around a garden you never planted—all of them, all at once, began to sing. Not hum. Sing. A high, clear note like struck glass, rising from every edge of the valley simultaneously, converging on the center, on her, on the bridge, on the millrace—

And then they shattered.

There was no debris. That was the terrible part. Glass breaks into shards; wards, it turned out, broke into *nothing*, into a brief rain of light that fell upward and was gone, and then Hollowmere was simply open, like a mouth, like a wound, like a door kicked in on a house whose owners had always believed themselves alone.

The silence afterward lasted three heartbeats.

Then the dogs started. Every dog in the valley, all together, a chorus of grief and confusion rolling up from the farms, and beneath it the church bell in Lower Fenring began to ring—not the measured alarm of fire or flood but frantic, arrhythmic, someone hauling the rope with both hands and no plan.

Sable ran.

Not toward the exits. Toward home, which told her something about herself she chose not to examine. Her cottage sat at the crook of the river where it bent north, half-hidden by an elder tree her mother had planted the year Sable was born, and she took the shortcut through the reed beds with her satchel banging against her hip and the coins inside it ringing like tiny bells, absurdly, obscenely ordinary.

Her mother's cache was under the hearthstone. It had always been under the hearthstone. Lys Pemberton had shown her daughter the trick of lifting it when Sable was nine—"in case I'm not here someday, little thief"—and in eleven years since the fever took her, Sable had lifted that stone perhaps four times, each time finding the same contents unchanged: a bundle of letters in a hand too elegant for a memory-thief's wife, a knife her mother had never explained, and a small iron locket that gave off, when held to the ear, a faint sound like distant music.

She had never opened the locket. Her mother had made her promise. *"When the sky changes,"* Lys had said, and laughed, because it sounded like madness, *"when the sky changes, little thief, not before."*

Sable knelt on her cold hearthstones with the sky changed beyond all recognition and lifted the stone with hands that shook only a little, which she counted as a professional victory.

The locket was warm. It should not have been warm. Nothing under a hearthstone is warm. She turned it over in her palm—the iron worn smooth, a nine-pointed star etched so shallowly you'd miss it unless you knew to look—and the warmth of it climbed her wrist like a small animal seeking shelter.

And that was when the riders came.

She heard them first on the ferry road: hooves, many, moving at a canter that was somehow worse than a gallop because it meant they were not hurrying. They were not fleeing anything. Nothing in Hollowmere could threaten them, and they knew it, and the unhurried rhythm of their approach said so more clearly than any herald.

Through the window she saw them enter the village square. Nine riders. Of course nine. Their horses were grey as river fog and moved without quite touching the ground, and the riders themselves wore cloaks the color of deep water at night, and on each breast was pinned a sigil that caught the lantern-light and gave back too much of it: a nine-pointed star, silver-white, turning slowly, though there was no wind and no mechanism she could see.

They drew up in the square outside the Tallow house, where twelve hours earlier Sable had drawn a dying woman's last happy afternoon out through her fingertips and sealed it in glass. One rider dismounted. He was tall, and when he removed his hood his face was pleasant in the way of well-made furniture—handsome, symmetrical, and utterly without warmth.

His voice carried to every corner of the village at once, which was not how voices worked.

"People of Hollowmere." A pause, courteous, dreadful. "We come in peace, and we come once. We seek the daughter of Lys."

Sable's whole body went cold and then hot and then cold again, cycling like a fever.

*The daughter of Lys.*

Not a name. A description. As if she were not a person but a debt outstanding, an item on an inventory, a line in a ledger kept for longer than she had been alive. Her mother's name in the mouths of these strangers was the worst thing she had ever heard, worse than the empty sky, because the sky had simply gone dark, but this—this meant her mother had been *known*. Had been *counted*. Had been part of whatever this was, whatever century-long lie the valley had been built on.

She pressed her back against the chimney breast and slid down until she was sitting on the floor, the locket clenched in her fist, its warmth beating against her palm like a second pulse.

Outside, the rider spoke again, patient as winter. "Lys of the Ninefold Court carried something out of the capital. We do not blame her—we grieved her passing, truly—but what she carried belongs to the throne, and the throne has need of it now. Produce the daughter of Lys, and Hollowmere will be remembered kindly. Withhold her, and we will search. We are very thorough, and the night is very long."

In the square, she heard her neighbors. Old Tam Wicker saying something brave and stupid. Mrs. Tallow's son, grief-drunk, shouting that his mother had died that morning and he'd thank them to come back never. The murmur of a hundred people who had believed, right up until this evening, that the world ended at the boundary stones.

And then, cutting through it all, Mag's voice. The old ferryman had come up from the river, and he sounded, of all things, almost cheerful.

"Daughter of Lys?" he called out. "Half the valley's somebody's daughter. You'll want to be more particular."

A pause. Then the pleasant voice, still pleasant: "Perhaps. Or perhaps the valley will tell us. Valleys remember what's fed to them."

Sable went very still.

*Fed.* Mag's word, from three nights ago, delivered across the gunwale of his ferry while the boundary stones hummed their wrongness: *they used to feed the wards something, girl. Once a year. Stopped before your time. Never asked what.*

The wards were broken now. But the valley itself—the earth, the river, the elder tree, the stones—had eaten a century of whatever ritual her mother's generation had abandoned, and apparently it had learned things in the eating. Apparently it could be asked questions.

Apparently it could answer them.

She looked down at the locket in her hand. Warm. Patient. Her mother's voice rose in her memory with terrible clarity, the way the dead get to be clear once they can no longer be argued with: *when the sky changes, little thief, not before.*

"Right," Sable whispered to the empty kitchen. "You could have mentioned sooner."

She did the thing her whole life had trained her for, the thing she was genuinely, professionally excellent at: she became no one. She banked the fire to embers. She took the letters, the knife, and the locket, and left everything else—her mother's shawl, her own good coat, the coins, the evidence of a person—to sit in the cold house like a shed skin. Through the back window she watched the nine-pointed stars turn on nine cloaks in the square, slow and silver and patient, and she understood with a clarity that felt like falling that the world she had lived in for twenty-six years had been a cell, and the jailers had finally noticed the prisoner's daughter missing from her crib.

The last thing she heard, slipping out through the reed beds with the river covering her steps, was the pleasant voice asking the darkness of the valley, gently, the way you ask a child:

"Now then. Where is she?"

And the ground beneath Hollowmere—fed for a hundred years, hungry for thirty, loyal to no one anymore—began, softly, to think about answering.

* * *

She made the shepherd's pass by moonrise—or by where the moon would have been, except the moon hung wrong now too, pale and slightly swollen, like an eye held open past bearing. Behind her, the valley glowed with torchlight. They were searching house to house, unhurried, thorough. Somewhere in that glow was Mag, who had bought her minutes with a joke, and Tam Wicker, and Mrs. Tallow's grieving son, and every neighbor who had ever paid her in eggs and gossip and never once asked where her money really came from.

She climbed. The locket beat its small warm pulse against her sternum where she'd tucked it inside her shirt, and with every step she waited for the voice behind her to say *there*, for the pleasant furniture-face to appear at the head of the trail, for the ground itself to give her up.

The ground stayed silent. Whether out of loyalty or indifference, she couldn't tell, and she suspected she'd never know, and she hated that.

Near the top of the pass, where the trail narrowed between two leaning stones, she finally let herself stop and breathe and look back one last time at the valley that had raised her.

Hollowmere lay below like a cup of spilled light, and above it—above the whole world—the sky remained a smooth black ceiling, starless, finished. And as she watched, she saw something that made her forget how to breathe.

Far to the east, past the mountains, past anywhere she had ever imagined going, a single point of light detached itself from the horizon and began, slowly, deliberately, to move. Not falling. *Traveling.* Trailing a faint silver wake, like a needle drawing thread across the dark.

Heading, she was almost certain, toward the capital everyone whispered about and no one in Hollowmere had ever seen.

Toward Virelay.

The locket pulsed against her chest, warm and insistent, and for the first time in eleven years Sable heard her mother's voice without grief attached—just the old dry humor, the little thief's inheritance:

*Well. You'd better follow it, hadn't you?*

She pulled her collar up against the cold, turned her back on the only home she'd ever known, and started climbing.
