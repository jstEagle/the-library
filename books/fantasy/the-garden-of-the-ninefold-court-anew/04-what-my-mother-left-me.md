## 4. What My Mother Left Me

Sable made it exactly half a mile up the mountain path before practicality caught her by the collar and dragged her back down.

It wasn't sentiment. She told herself that firmly, twice, the way you tell yourself a lie often enough that it starts wearing the shape of truth. It was *supplies*. It was *winter coming*. It was the fact that she had walked out of Hollowmere with one locket, one knife, three coins, and the particular pride of a woman who had spent eleven years planning for every contingency except the world ending.

The valley, when she crested the last rise and looked back down at it, was still burning in places. Thin threads of smoke rose from the Chandler farm, from the tithe-house, from the little shrine where old women left bread for saints nobody could name. The riders had moved on toward the town proper, their nine-pointed banners catching what little light the starless sky offered—which was none, really; the light came from their sigils themselves, a cold silver that hurt to look at directly.

*They're searching house to house,* Sable thought. *Which means they'll reach my mother's cottage by midday.*

Which meant she had until midday.

She went down through the orchard instead of the road, moving the way her mother had taught her—heel first, weight low, breathing through the nose—and let herself in through the loose board at the back of the root cellar, because doors had hinges and hinges had opinions, but boards only had dust.

The cottage smelled of ash and cold tea. Someone had already been through it—the good chairs were overturned, the mattress gutted, her mother's few books shaken out of their spines and left splayed on the floor like dead birds. Sable stood in the wreckage of the only home she'd ever known and felt something in her chest go very quiet and very hard.

*They didn't find it,* she realized. *They tore the place apart and they didn't find it.*

Of course they hadn't. Her mother had been Lys Pemberton, who once lifted a bishop's signet ring off his finger while confessing her sins to him. You didn't hide things from people like that in mattresses.

You hid them in the place nobody would ever look, because looking there required wanting to be there.

Sable knelt at the cold hearth, reached up into the flue past the second bend where no sweep had ever bothered to go, and pulled down the tin box wrapped in oilcloth.

Her hands shook. She noticed them shaking with a kind of distant professional irritation, the way she might notice a lock that was stiffer than advertised. Eleven years of steady hands, and grief undid them in a single morning.

She carried the box out to the orchard, because staying inside felt like being buried, and sat beneath the bare-limbed apple tree her mother had never gotten around to pruning. Then she unwrapped the oilcloth, folded it neatly beside her—habits—and opened the box.

Inside:

A knife. A good one, hollow-ground, balanced for throwing though her mother had always said throwing knives were for show-offs and cowards, which was why she'd taught Sable to throw them anyway. "In case you're ever both," she'd said, and laughed her dry little laugh.

A purse of coins—not Hollowmere scrip but real money, old money, stamped with a nine-pointed star that Sable had always assumed was some foreign king's vanity.

And letters. Seven of them, tied with string gone brittle as spun sugar. None addressed to her.

Sable untied the string with fingers that wanted very much not to be careful and were anyway.

The first letter was dated thirty-one years ago. The handwriting was her mother's, but younger—looser, less guarded, the loops of it practically singing.

*M.,*

*You were right about the capital. It's worse than you said. They light their lamps with stars here—actual stars, caged in glass, and the poor sell pieces of themselves to afford the glow. I got work easy enough. Nobody suspects a memory-thief in a city where everyone's desperate to forget.*

*But M., I found something I shouldn't have. Something I was paid to steal and couldn't sell, because selling it would be like selling a child.*

*I'm keeping it. I know what you'll say. Say it anyway. I'll still keep it.*

*—L.*

Sable read it three times. The wind moved through the orchard, and somewhere below, faintly, a building collapsed in on itself with a sound like a held breath finally released.

The second letter was shorter.

*M.,*

*They've found me. Not the guild—the Court. Riders with nine-pointed stars, asking after a thief who took something from the palace vaults. I'm going somewhere they can't follow. There's a valley two hundred miles north where the wards are old and hungry and nobody asks questions.*

*If anything happens to me, everything I am goes into the ground with me. Except one thing. One thing I'll hide so well that even I won't be able to find it without meaning to.*

*Tell me that's not sentimental. Go on. I dare you.*

*—L.*

Sable set the letter down on her knee and looked at it for a long moment.

Then she looked at the locket hanging against her chest—the one she'd taken from around her mother's neck as the woman died, the one that had hummed against her skin all night like a sleeping wasp, the one that had told her, in a voice that was almost her mother's almost-humor, to *follow it*.

"You absolute sentimental disaster," Sable said aloud, to a woman eleven years dead, in an orchard full of smoke. "You hid it in yourself."

Because that was the trick, wasn't it? That was the masterpiece. Lys Pemberton, memory-thief, had done the one thing no thief was supposed to do—she'd broken Sable's own rule, the rule Sable had built her whole life on, the rule carved into her like a ward-stone:

*Never keep what you steal.*

Except her mother hadn't kept it. She'd *planted* it. Somewhere in those long sick months at the end, when Sable had sat by her bed holding her hand and listening to her ramble about gardens and boys and a coronation that felt like a funeral, Lys hadn't been rambling at all. She'd been *transferring*. Slipping fragments of stolen memory across the border between dying mind and living daughter, disguised as deathbed talk, tucked into the folds of ordinary grief where no rider with a nine-pointed banner would ever think to search.

Not in a box. Not in a flue. In Sable herself.

"Contraband," Sable whispered. "I'm carrying contraband."

And not just any contraband. She closed her hand around the locket and let it open its memories to her again, the way it had begun doing since the wards fell—since whatever seal her mother had laid over these fragments had cracked along with the valley's borders.

*A garden.* That came first, always. Walled, ancient, impossibly green, roses the color of embers growing in shapes too deliberate to be wild. A boy in the garden—young, seven maybe, eight—with dark hair and a scowl far too serious for his face, kneeling in the dirt because he'd decided the gardener's boy needed help with the winter beds and no one, apparently, had the authority to stop him.

*"You're doing it wrong,"* the boy said, in a memory-voice that rang strangely familiar, though Sable couldn't place it. *"You have to talk to them. Grandmother says plants listen better than courtiers."*

*The gardener's boy—smaller, warier, with dirt under his nails and a look Sable recognized from her own childhood mirror—said nothing. The prince talked enough for both of them.*

*Aldric.* The name surfaced with the memory like a fish rising. *Prince Aldric. Ninefold heir. The king they crowned when the crown was already hungry.*

And then the other fragment, the one that had woken her screaming more than once over the years, back when she'd assumed it was just a nightmare her dying mother had leaked into her:

*A coronation hall vast as a cavern. A boy of fourteen walking down an aisle a mile long, alone, while ten thousand people watched in silence that wasn't respectful—it was *funereal*, the silence of a crowd watching something be buried. And on the cushion ahead of him, the crown: black metal worked into nine points, drinking the candlelight rather than reflecting it, and the boy looking at it the way a lamb looks at the knife.*

*"I don't want it,"* the boy-Aldric said, very quietly, to no one, knowing no one would hear. *"Please. I don't want it."*

*No one heard. No one ever hears, in coronations. That's rather the point of them.*

Sable came back to herself sitting under the apple tree with tears freezing on her cheeks and no memory of having started crying, which was, professionally speaking, mortifying.

She wiped her face with her sleeve, briskly, the way you'd wipe down a counter.

"So," she said to the locket. "You're a king. Or the pieces of one. And my mother stole you out of a palace vault thirty years ago and smuggled you north in her own head, and then—" her voice caught, and she pushed through it, "—and then she planted you in *me*, and then she died, and I spent eleven years thinking the strangest thing about my childhood was that I could remember a garden I'd never visited."

The locket pulsed. Warm. Insistent. Almost smug.

"Don't get pleased with yourself," she told it. "You're evidence. You're the kind of evidence that gets valleys burned."

Even as she said it, she was tying the letters back together with their brittle string, wrapping the knife and coins in the oilcloth, packing with quick sure movements. Because here was the thing—the ugly, undeniable thing she'd been circling since the stars went out:

The riders hadn't come for the valley. They'd come for *the daughter of Lys*. For her. For this. Which meant the thing in the locket mattered enough to burn a hundred homes over. Which meant either she ran and hid and waited for them to find her eventually, in some cellar, in some border town, alone—

—or she followed the locket's advice, and her mother's, and took the contraband straight to the heart of the Court that wanted it back.

*Well. You'd better follow it, hadn't you?*

"I hate that you're right," Sable muttered. "I hate it in a way that will outlast kingdoms."

She stood, brushed off her skirts, and had turned toward the mountain path when the sound reached her.

Horns. Not the riders' cold silver horns—she knew those now, had heard them all night, a sound like ice cracking. These were different: lower, rougher, human. Coming from the eastern ridge, from the old shepherd tracks above the valley.

And beneath the horns, voices. Several. Arguing. One of them rising above the others, clear and carrying and utterly furious, saying words that drifted down through the smoke-bright air and stopped Sable's breath:

"—told you the charts don't lie! The thirteenth binding is *real*, and if we don't—"

The voice cut off abruptly. Footsteps crunched on frost. Coming closer. Coming *down*.

Sable slipped the locket inside her shirt, drew her mother's knife, and stepped behind the apple tree—heart hammering, mind already running the arithmetic of escape routes—just as two figures stumbled out onto the orchard path below her.

One wore the mud-spattered grey of a scholar who had been running for days.

The other wore the torn remnants of a royal guard's cloak, with a nine-pointed star ripped cleanly from the shoulder.

Both of them were looking directly at her hiding place.

"Well," said the scholar, in the tone of a man whose week could not possibly get worse and had just managed it anyway. "That's a memory-thief's stance if I've ever seen one."
