## 5. The Astronomer Who Counted Nothing

Sable had been running for two days, and her body had filed a formal complaint.

It arrived in the form of a stitch beneath her ribs that felt like a knife left there by someone careless, and in the form of her boots—good boots, expensive boots, bought with the coin of a merchant who'd never miss the memory of his first kiss—filling with water as she waded up Miller's Creek toward the old survey road. The riders had swept the valley's south end twice. They were methodical. They had nine-pointed sigils on their cloaks and faces like men who had been told they were heroes so long ago that they'd stopped questioning it.

She climbed the creek bank and crouched in the bracken, breathing through her mouth, and did what she always did when terror got loud: she made an inventory. One locket-memory containing a dead king's childhood. One mother, dead eleven years, whose last act had been hiding contraband royalty inside her daughter's head. One valley, burning behind her in slow, patient increments. Zero plans.

"Two out of four," she whispered to herself. "Could be worse."

The stars were still gone. That was the part she couldn't stop checking, the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth. She'd look up and expect the sky to have remembered itself overnight, and instead it was just—blank. Not cloudy. Clouds had texture, intention. This was a ceiling. Someone had put a lid on the world, and Sable Pemberton, who had spent her whole life believing Hollowmere's wards kept magic *out*, was beginning to understand that a cage keeps things in just as well.

The survey road ran along the ridge above the creek, and where it crossed the stream stood the old counting house—a stone hut from before the wards, before even Mag's grandfather, built by some king's tax collectors who'd presumably frozen to death regretting their career choices. Its roof had half fallen in. Its door hung at an angle that suggested it had given up on being a door.

Smoke was coming out of it.

Sable went very still. Smoke meant fire, fire meant people, people meant either help or riders, and in her experience the ratio ran about one to nine. But her feet were bleeding, her bread had run out that morning, and desperation does arithmetic of its own.

She circled wide, came down through the alders, and looked through the gap where a window used to be.

A man sat inside at a rickety table covered in papers, writing by lamplight with the furious concentration of someone arguing with the paper itself. He was thin in the way of people who forget meals the way other people forget appointments, with ink on his fingers, his wrists, and—one smudge across his forehead like a brand—a general air of having lost a fight with his own quill. His hair stuck up in six directions. Around him, pinned and weighted and stacked, were charts. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Star charts, all of them wrong now, every one depicting a sky full of lights that no longer existed.

Sable knew him. Everyone in Hollowmere knew *of* him—the mad astronomer who lived up past the boundary stones, who'd been thrown out of the capital years ago for saying something treasonous about the stars. Wren Calloway. The man who counted nothing.

She should have walked away. Every instinct honed by fifteen years of thieving said: strange man, isolated hut, witnesses are how thieves die.

Instead she pushed open the ruined door and said, "I'll trade you half my bread for whatever you're cooking, because I can smell it, and I haven't eaten since yesterday."

The man jumped so hard he knocked over the lamp, caught it mid-fall with a reflex that suggested this happened often, and stared at her with enormous, startled eyes. "How long," he said, "have you been standing there?"

"Ten seconds."

"Good. Good." He set the lamp down carefully, as if it might bolt. "Because if it had been longer, I'd have said something incriminating, and then we'd be in an awkward position, you and I."

"You're already in an awkward position. You're living in a ruin making soup during an apocalypse."

"It's not soup, it's a decoction." He said this with the gravity of a man correcting a mispronunciation of his own name. Then he seemed to actually see her—the mud, the blood, the wildness—and his face changed. "You're running from them. The riders."

"How did you know?"

"Because everyone who comes here is running from them, and also because you keep glancing at the window the way rabbits glance at hawks." He gestured at the stool across from him. "Sit. There's bread. It's stale, but staleness is just patience made edible."

Against every rule she owned, Sable sat.

* * *

Wren Calloway had been watching the sky fail for three years, and nobody had believed him, which he had decided was the most predictable thing about the entire affair.

He'd written his first warning from the Observatory of Virelay itself, back when he'd still been allowed near instruments worth more than his life. A small paper, politely worded, addressed to the Court of Seasons: *Anomalies in stellar luminosity suggest systematic diminution. Recommend investigation.* He'd received no reply, which was fine; astronomers were used to being ignored. He'd written a second paper, less polite. A third, which contained the phrase "harvested" and the phrase "by whom, gentlemen?" and got him summoned before the Regent's chamberlain.

He remembered that meeting with unusual clarity, possibly because it ended his life. The chamberlain had been very kind. Kindness, Wren had since learned, was what power used when it wanted something done quietly. *You're tired, Master Calloway. You've been staring at the sky too long. Take a rest in the country. Hollowmere is lovely this time of year.*

He'd taken the rest. He'd stayed eleven years. And every night, he'd gone out with his battered brass quadrant and counted the stars, and every year there were fewer, and every year he wrote it down in a ledger that now held three thousand pages of the most depressing mathematics ever performed by a human being.

When the last star went out, Wren had been standing on the ridge with his quadrant raised, and he had done the only reasonable thing.

"I sat down," he told Sable, ladling the decoction—which was, she conceded, excellent—into a tin cup, "and I laughed until I cried, which took approximately four minutes, and then I cried until I laughed, which took considerably longer, and then I started recalculating."

"Recalculating what?"

"The rate." He pulled a chart from under a stack of others and spread it flat, weighting the corners with stones and a spoon. Ink lines crawled across it in his cramped hand—curves descending, year over year, like a staircase going down into dark. "Stars don't go out, Sable—" he'd asked her name within the first minute, because names mattered to a man who'd spent a decade talking mostly to himself— "stars don't go out. They dim over eons. What happened three nights ago wasn't death. It was *removal*. Something reached up and took them, in order, brightest first, exactly the way you'd strip a field if you intended to replant it."

"That's insane."

"Yes," Wren agreed cheerfully. "Insane things are frequently true. It's the sane things you can usually discount." He tapped the curve at its lowest point. "I tracked the harvest pattern for years without understanding it. Thirteen sectors of sky. The harvest moves through them in sequence, one per lunar turn. Twelve turns have passed. Tonight is the new moon that begins the twelfth."

Sable set down her cup. "And after the thirteenth?"

"After the thirteenth," Wren said, and for the first time the manic brightness dropped out of his voice and left something older and wearier underneath, "the harvest is complete. Thirteen bindings. In the old rites—and yes, I've read the old rites, exile is wonderful for your reading habits—thirteen is the number of permanence. Whatever they're building with that light, when the thirteenth binding closes, it will never come undone. Not by gods, not by kings, not by time itself." He looked at her across the lamplight. "They're not stealing the stars, Sable. They're making something eternal."

The locket against her sternum felt suddenly heavy, the way it had since the night the wards shattered—since the riders had ridden through asking after *the daughter of Lys*.

"And you know this," she said slowly, "because you're a court astronomer."

"*Disgraced* court astronomer. The disgrace is important. It means I'm the only person in this valley who isn't lying to himself about what's coming." He gathered the chart back into its stack with quick, precise movements. "Which brings me to my question. You've been running for two days. You're a memory-thief—I know the guild marks on your kit; my neighbor's son sold recollections to pay a debt, I know the craft when I see it. And the riders aren't searching the valley generally. They're searching *specifically*. For someone." His enormous eyes fixed on her. "So. Who are you to them?"

Every lie Sable owned lined up at the door of her throat, polished and ready. *Nobody. A thief in the wrong place. Let me pass.*

What came out instead was: "My mother hid something in me. Something of the king's. And I think they've come to collect it."

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere outside, wind moved through the alders, and the ruined roof ticked as it settled, and Wren Calloway stared at her the way he probably stared at a sky that had just produced a star where none should be.

"Well," he said finally. "*Well.*"

"That's your response? Well?"

"I'm recalibrating! You spend eleven years being the only madman in the valley, and then a bleeding stranger walks in and confirms your life's work in a single sentence, and you want eloquence?" He pressed both ink-stained hands flat on the table. "Say it again. Slowly."

"My mother was Lys Pemberton. Before Hollowmere, before the wards, she served in the palace at Virelay. When the king was bound—when the crown was put on him—she ran. She carried something out. Fragments of his memory, sealed in a locket. And before she died she hid them in me, the way only a memory-thief could hide anything—in the place where stolen things live." Sable touched her own temple. "I didn't know what they were until three nights ago. I thought they were just... noise. Dreams that weren't mine. A garden I'd never visited. A boy named Aldric. A coronation that felt like a funeral."

Wren had gone very pale. "Aldric," he repeated. "The Ninefold King. Aldric the Ninth." He began pulling charts off his stacks with sudden urgency, flipping through them, muttering. "Thirteen bindings. Thirteen turns. The crown devours him and the sky together—he's the anchor, don't you see, the light has to go somewhere, it's being *woven into him*—the king isn't just imprisoned, he's the raw material—"

"I know," Sable said quietly. "I've seen him. In the fragments. He knows what's happening to him. He counts the turns too."

That stopped Wren cold. He looked at her for a long moment, and something in his face shifted—from the scholar confronting data to the human being confronting another human being.

"He counts," Wren said softly. "Of course he counts. Counting is what you do when you can't do anything else." He laughed, short and bitter. "Eleven years I counted a dying sky and called it work. He's counting himself."

* * *

Here is what Wren Calloway knew about himself, and had known for a long time: he was a liar, and his lies were the respectable kind.

He lied when he said he'd stayed in Hollowmere by choice. He lied when he told the ferryman Mag that his exile hadn't broken anything in him. He lied most thoroughly when he claimed his work was pure—that a man who loved the stars could watch them vanish and feel nothing but scientific interest. Eleven years of lies, all of them tidy, all of them worn smooth with use, the way a coin wears smooth in a pocket.

And here is what he learned about Sable Pemberton in the three hours after midnight, sitting in a ruined counting house while the twelfth new moon rose invisible over a starless world: she was a liar too, but she knew it about herself, which made her either more dangerous than him or considerably more honest.

She'd caught him at it, too. Around the second hour, describing his expulsion from the Observatory, he'd heard himself say "I left voluntarily, ultimately," and she'd looked at him with those gray, weighing eyes—the eyes of a woman who appraised truth for a living—and said, gently, "You were thrown out. It's all right. I steal memories for money. We're neither of us what we tell people we are."

And the strange thing, the thing he would think about for years afterward, was that it hadn't stung. It had felt like setting down a bag he'd forgotten he was carrying.

"So what do we do?" she asked, near dawn. The lamp had burned low. Outside, the east stayed stubbornly dark—not even the false gray of approaching morning, because morning needed a sun, and apparently the sun was next on somebody's list. "You can't fight the Court with charts. I can't outrun nine riders forever. And the twelfth turn starts tonight."

"We go to Virelay."

Sable stared at him. "The capital. Where the regent lives. Where the riders come from. Where I am, I should mention, extremely recognizable to anyone holding my description."

"You have a piece of the king in your head. I have thirteen years of harvest data and the only complete record of the Binding rite in existence. Neither of us is safe anywhere, so we may as well be unsafe where the answers are." He was already sweeping his charts into a leather satchel, moving with the sudden decisive energy of a man who had, at long last, found a use for his madness. "Besides. There's someone in Virelay I need to see. An old colleague. If anyone can get us close to the king, it's—"

He stopped.

"What?" Sable said.

"Nothing. A complication. I'll explain on the road." He snuffed the lamp, shouldered the satchel, and paused at the ruined door, looking back at her with an expression she couldn't quite read—half apology, half dare. "Fair warning, Sable. Everything I've predicted for eleven years has come true, and every prediction has been worse than the last. Are you certain you want to travel with a man like that?"

Sable thought about the boy named Aldric, kneeling in a garden in someone else's memory while a crown was lowered onto his head like a lid onto a coffin. She thought about Mag the ferryman, who'd fed the wards once a year with his own small offerings and never asked why. She thought about the blank ceiling of the sky.

"No," she said. "But I'm certain I don't want to travel alone, and between the two of us, you're the better liar."

Wren grinned—ink-smudged, exhausted, entirely unheroic. "High praise. Come on, then. The road to Virelay runs past the burned farms, and if we move quickly, we'll reach the crossroads before the riders finish their sweep of the north valley."

They stepped out into the dark together, two liars with one satchel of doomed mathematics, and behind them the counting house stood empty for the first time in eleven years.

They made the crossroads by midmorning.

They did not make it alone. Waiting at the signpost where the survey road met the king's highway—leaning against the post with his arms crossed and his face arranged into something between boredom and threat—stood a rider in a cloak bearing the nine-pointed sigil, and when he saw them coming, he smiled.

"You'd be the astronomer," he said. "And you'd be the daughter of Lys."
