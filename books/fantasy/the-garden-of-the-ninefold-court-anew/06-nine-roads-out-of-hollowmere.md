## 6. Nine Roads Out of Hollowmere

The fire started at the mill.

Sable saw it from Mag's ferry landing, where they'd hidden in the reeds since midnight, and for one foolish heartbeat she thought: *the mill's burning, someone will have to fetch water.* That was the valley thinking in her, the old reflex of a place where disasters were small and neighborly—a barn fire, a broken axle, a wedding where too much plum brandy got drunk. Then she counted the other fires. One on Tanner's Row. One up the slopes of Windle Hill, where the orchards were. One that could only be the chapel, because the light it threw was gold-shot with green, and nothing burned green except what the riders carried in their lanterns.

Nine fires. Nine roads out of Hollowmere, and a rider on each one, walking flame along the valley like a hand trailing its fingers over a map.

"They're not searching," Wren said beside her. He'd said little all night—little even by his standards, which were already miserly—and his voice had the flatness of a man reading an instrument he didn't like the numbers on. "Searching is slow. This is *herding*. They want you to run, Sable. They're telling you which way."

"Then they've made their first mistake," Sable said, "because I don't run on command. Ask anyone." No one asked anyone. The reeds whispered. Somewhere across the water, a dog was barking itself hoarse at the sky, which had no stars in it to bark at and never would again.

Mag crouched at the ferry rope, one knotted hand resting on it like it was the neck of an old horse. He was seventy if he was a day, built like something the river had decided to keep, and he had not once in Sable's whole life been seen without his hat. Tonight the hat sat low over eyes that reflected nine fires and gave back none of them.

"Ferry won't cross to the north bank," he said. "Rope's cut on the far side. Cut clean, too—not weathered, not chewed. Somebody came through yesterday and did arithmetic." He spat into the water, thoughtfully, the way he did everything. "So we go south. Downriver. There's a fisherman's track past the old eel-weirs that doesn't show on any map worth having."

"You know a way off the river," Wren said, "that isn't on maps."

"I know a great many things that aren't on maps, astronomer. It's why I'm still alive and so interesting at parties." Mag pushed himself upright with a grunt that Sable suspected was forty percent theater. "Girl. A word before we walk."

She went to him. Behind them the chapel fire collapsed inward with a sound like a held breath let go, and she made herself not look, because looking was a luxury and luxuries got people killed.

"Whatever happens down this river," Mag said quietly, "you keep your feet moving. You hear me? Grief is heavy, but guilt is heavier, and guilt's the one that stops your legs. I've watched it drown better swimmers than either of us."

"Nothing's happened yet," Sable said.

"No," Mag agreed, and patted her shoulder once, twice, the way he used to when she was eight and crying about her mother, before he put his hat more firmly on and turned to the reeds. "Yet is a very honest word. Let's be moving."

* * *

They went south along the bank in the smoke-colored dark, single file, Wren navigating by a moon that was doing its level best to be useful and mostly failing. Twice they heard horses on the north road. Once, horribly, they heard singing—the riders, or some of them, singing in a language Sable didn't know but her bones did, some buried harmony that made her teeth ache and her eyes sting. Wren stopped dead when he heard it.

"That's Court liturgy," he whispered. "The Vespers of the Ninefold Throne. They only sing it at coronations." He swallowed. "Or executions."

"Cheerful lot," Sable managed.

"They think they're at a coronation," Wren said, and did not explain, and Sable found she preferred it unexplained, the way you prefer not to look directly at certain wounds.

It was near dawn when the first rider found them—or rather, when the first rider's lantern-light bloomed around the bend ahead of them, and there was suddenly nowhere left that wasn't lit. The man on the horse wore gray, and across his chest rode the sigil Sable had seen three nights ago when the world broke: nine points, like a star that had grown extra ambitions. His face beneath the hood was ordinary. That was the worst part. He looked like a tax assessor.

"There," he said, in the tone of a man finding a misplaced ledger. "The daughter of Lys."

Sable's whole body did the sensible thing, which was to try to become smaller than physics allowed. But Mag stepped forward—one step, unhurried, hat brim dipping—and said, "Evening," as though greeting a neighbor come about a fence dispute.

"The old man steps aside," the rider said.

"Now that's the strange thing," Mag said pleasantly. "I've been standing on this bank sixty years, and in all that time nobody's ever told me where to stand. Seems a poor night to start taking instruction." He glanced back at Sable, and though the lantern-light was cruel and total, she would swear until her dying day that he was smiling under that hat. "Fisherman's track's behind the third eel-weir, girl. Count them. Don't stop counting."

"Old man—" the rider began, and then Mag did something no one expected, least of all Mag, who grabbed the ferry rope's remaining anchor-stone and hurled himself and it both into the shallows where the horses would have to cross.

The splash was enormous. The horse screamed and shied. The rider's lantern went into the water with a hiss like a curse being swallowed, and in that sudden blessed darkness Sable heard Wren say, "*Now*," and then she was running, running along the bank with her lungs full of smoke and river-smell, counting eel-weirs—one, rotten; two, half-drowned; *three*—

Behind her, across the water, she heard shouting. She heard the gray rider's voice rise into that buried-harmony language, and she heard words in it she somehow understood anyway, the way you understand a blow: *obstruction of the Throne's design.* And she heard Mag's voice, clear across the whole width of the river, unhurried as ever, saying something she couldn't make out, followed by laughter—actual laughter, Mag's laugh, the one that had seen her through every bad night of her childhood.

Then the light came. Not lantern-light. Something older and whiter, and it took the north bank, and Mag's laugh, and everything else, and gave back silence.

Sable stopped running because her legs stopped being hers. Wren caught her arm. She didn't remember him crossing the distance between them.

"He's—" she said, and couldn't finish, because finishing meant believing it.

"Keep counting," Wren said, gentle and terrible. "One more weir. Keep counting, Sable. It's what he told you to do."

She counted. Her voice cracked on *three*, and she went through the gap behind the third eel-weir into a fisherman's track so narrow the willows closed over her head like hands, and she walked, because grief is heavy but guilt is heavier, and guilt's the one that stops your legs, and she had promised a dead man's living voice she'd keep moving.

* * *

They walked all day. The track wound south through country Sable had never seen, because no one in Hollowmere ever saw it—the wards had discouraged curiosity the way a well-disciplined dog discourages squirrels—and every mile of it was a mile further from the smoke, which did not help, because smoke follows you inside eventually.

Near dusk they sheltered in a hollow under a fallen oak, and Sable discovered she was holding the locket-memory against her sternum so hard it had printed its shape into her skin. She hadn't remembered picking it up. She hadn't remembered anything since the third eel-weir except counting.

Wren built no fire. He sat across from her, knees drawn up, and after a long while he said, "You should know what that light was. On the riverbank."

"Don't."

"It was harvested starlight. Contained, weaponized. What I spent eleven years telling the Court was possible and being exiled for saying." His jaw worked. "They used it to kill a ferryman."

"I said don't." Sable's voice came out flat and strange. "I heard you the first time. I hear everything. That's my whole cursed profession, Wren—I take what people feel and I carry it away and I give it to someone else, and do you know what I've learned in ten years of it? That carrying it doesn't lighten it. It just means two people are heavy instead of one." She looked at her hands. "He fed me stew when I was nine. Every winter. He pretended the pot was bigger than it was so I wouldn't feel like charity. Sixty years on that river and he died in ten seconds for a girl who steals memories and can't even keep the one that matters."

"Yes," Wren said.

She stared at him. "That's it? *Yes?*"

"What would you like me to say? That it wasn't your fault? You'd know I was lying, and worse, you'd know I knew you'd know." He leaned forward, and his eyes in the dark had the particular stubborn shine of a man who has been right at great personal cost and intends to remain so. "Here's what I'll say instead. Mag chose. Old men in burning valleys don't get many choices, and he took the only one on offer, which was *what my death buys*. And what it bought was you, walking, and me, walking, and that locket, walking, toward a king who—" Wren stopped. Started again, quieter. "Do you know what the last thing he said to me was? While you were running. He looked at me and he said, *tell her the king remembers being kind.*"

The hollow went very quiet. Somewhere overhead, in the starless dark, a nightjar called and got no answer from any sky.

"What does that mean?" Sable whispered.

"I don't know," Wren admitted. "But he said it like a man handing over a key he'd been keeping safe a long time. Mag knew things, Sable. Ferryman on the only river out of a prison valley, for sixty years? Nobody lives that long at a border post without knowing exactly what the border is for." He shook his head slowly. "I think he met the king. Before. Or whatever the king was, before the crown. And I think what he wanted you to carry south wasn't guilt."

Sable pressed the locket harder against her chest. Inside it, sleeping in stolen silver, was a boy named Aldric, a garden, a coronation that felt like a funeral. *The king remembers being kind.* As if kindness were a country the king had been exiled from too. As if somewhere under all that harvested light there was still a man who could be reminded.

"Guilt's lighter than purpose," she said at last. "That's the trick nobody tells you. Guilt you can just sit inside, like a room. Purpose makes you get up."

"Is that memory-thief wisdom?"

"That's orphan wisdom. We keep different collections." She stood, brushing leaf-rot from her knees, and looked south, where the track ran on through the willows toward a capital she'd never seen and a court that sang vespers at executions. "How far to Virelay?"

"On foot? Nine days." Wren rose too, stiffly, a man whose body had been filing complaints since the exile and had recently escalated. "Less, if we can beg a cart. Which reminds me—you're a thief. Can you steal us a cart?"

"Wren. I'm a *memory*-thief. Completely different guild. Different ethics entirely."

"Ethics."

"Rigorous ones. Rule one: never keep what you steal." She touched the locket, once, lightly—the crack beneath the control, the one kept thing, the debt she intended to pay back at any price the rite demanded—and then she lifted her chin toward the south road. "Rule two: always know what a thing's worth before you take it. So let's go find out what a cart costs in the first town, and whether anyone there remembers how to hope. I'm told it's a seller's market."

They walked. Behind them, far to the north, Hollowmere burned on the nine roads, and Sable did not look back—but she carried it, all of it, the mill and the chapel and Mag's laugh cut off mid-note, and she swore to the starless sky that she would spend every ounce of what it weighed.

Somewhere ahead, a king needed reminding that he had ever been kind.

She intended to deliver the message personally.
