## 7. A Court of Harvested Light

Wren Calloway had spent eleven years learning to read the sky, and in all that time he had never once imagined he would miss it this much.

The road to Virelay climbed out of the lowlands through a pass called the Shepherd's Tooth, and from its crest the capital revealed itself the way a stage magician reveals a lady cut in half: suddenly, and with an expectation of applause.

"There," Sable said beside him. She had gone quiet for most of the climb, which Wren was learning was not her natural state but her guarded one. "That's it."

Virelay lay in the bowl of the valley below like a spilled jewelry box. Ten thousand lamps burned in its streets—not flame-colored, not candle-warm, but white. White as frost. White as the inside of a closed eye. They hung from iron posts along every avenue, clustered at the bridges, crowned the towers, and pooled in the great plaza at the city's heart so brightly that even at midday they made the air around them look thin.

Wren stopped walking. He did not mean to; his legs simply decided the matter without consulting him.

"That's starlight," he said.

Sable glanced back at him. "It's lamps."

"It's *starlight*." His voice came out higher than he intended, and a mule driver ahead of them turned to look. Wren lowered it. "Do you know how I know? Because I have spent eleven years cataloguing the light of every visible star, and that—" he pointed, hand unsteady, at the nearest lamp-post, where a globe of glass held a small captive radiance— "that is Alcor. That is *Alcor*, specifically, third magnitude, spectral class A, and it should be over your shoulder right now on the northern horizon, four hundred miles away, and instead it is in a *lamp*."

Sable looked at the lamp. Then at him. Then at the lamp again, with the expression of a woman recalculating the price of everything she'd ever bought.

"They're harvesting them," Wren said. The words tasted like copper. "The stars aren't going out. They're being *collected*. Someone is picking the sky like fruit."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Below, Virelay glittered on, smug and bright, wearing the heavens as decoration.

"Well," said Sable at last, adjusting the strap of her bag. "At least we know we're not too late. You can't harvest what's already been eaten."

"That's either very comforting or very grim, and I genuinely cannot tell which."

"Neither can I. Come on. If we stand here gawking, someone will notice us gawking, and I'd rather be noticed for something more profitable."

They went down into the city together, and the stars went with them, bottled and burning.

* * *

Wren had expected gates. He got turnstiles.

The southern gate of Virelay was less a fortification than a marketplace with opinions. Guards in silver-and-black livery—the nine-pointed sigil stitched on their breasts, each point tipped with a bead of captured light—waved through carts of cabbages and wool and one alarming cargo of live geese, pausing only to collect a toll that seemed to depend entirely on how expensive your boots were. Wren's boots were terrible. He was waved through with a contemptuous flick of two fingers that he chose to interpret as professional courtesy between men who understood poverty.

Sable, whose boots were excellent and whose face was forgettable in the specific way of someone who had practiced being forgettable for years, paid her toll in coin and received no attention whatsoever.

Inside, the city swallowed them.

Wren had grown up in the capital—he sometimes forgot that Sable didn't know this about him yet, that there were whole decades of his life he hadn't confessed to her on the road—and he remembered Virelay as a place of bells and bookstalls and the smell of bread from the ovens on Tanner's Row. It still smelled of bread. But the light was wrong. Every lamp cast that flat, sourceless white, and under it people's faces looked washed-out, faintly translucent, like figures seen through vellum. Shadows fell strangely or not at all. It was a city lit by a sky that had been taken down and put indoors, and everyone in it had simply agreed to stop noticing.

"You're doing the thing," Sable murmured.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stare at everything and mutter. In Hollowmere we call that 'asking to be robbed.'"

"In Hollowmere you call everything asking to be robbed."

"Because everything is." She steered him left, down a street where the lamps grew sparser and the shadows grew honest again. "Where are we going, astronomer? You said you knew a place."

He did. That was the humiliating part. Eleven years at the Court Observatory, and the only person in Virelay still willing to give him a bed was a woman he'd once caught cheating at cards and had, in a fit of youthful integrity, reported to the gaming house—which had fired her, which was why she now ran a boarding house, which was why she hated him, which was why she charged him double.

"Madame Ostrey's," he said. "On Candle Lane."

"Candle Lane," Sable repeated. "In a city with no candles."

"The name's older than the lamps. Everything here is older than the lamps. That's rather the point of my entire grievance."

They turned onto Candle Lane and Wren stopped again, because the lane had changed since he'd last walked it, and the change was the kind that rearranged your understanding of the city around it.

There was a stall. Just a stall, striped awning, a woman behind it with kind eyes and quick hands. And on the stall, laid out on velvet like pastries, were memories.

Small ones. Glass lozenges, each holding a curl of pale luminescence—a taste, a color, a moment. The vendor was speaking softly to a boy of perhaps ten, who stood on tiptoe and counted coins onto the counter one at a time, agonized, deliberate, the way children count when the counting matters.

"—the fair one, the one with the dogs," the boy was saying. "Mam says I can have the dog one if I skip supper."

"And has your mam had hers today?" the vendor asked gently.

The boy nodded. "She had the wedding one. She cries happy now. Mostly."

Wren felt Sable go very still beside him. When he glanced at her, her face had done something complicated and then hidden it, the way water hides a stone.

"Selling memories," she said quietly. "To eat."

"To feel, mostly. Eating comes after." Wren's throat was tight. "I told you the Court taxed wonder. I undersold it. This isn't taxation. This is—" He gestured helplessly at the stall, at the boy, at the lozenge with the dogs. "This is the sky in a pawnshop. They take the stars, and when people start forgetting what the night used to look like, they sell the memory of it back to them. By the piece. By the *taste*."

The boy bought the dog memory. He held it up to the light, grinning, and pressed it to his temple, and for three seconds his face went soft and open with somebody else's joy—and then it faded, and he counted out his last coins for a heel of bread, and went home skipping.

"He'll do that every week," Sable said. "Until it's every day."

"Yes."

"And nobody stops it."

"Who would? The Regent's edicts say the Harvest is a gift. *The Court gathers light so that none need sit in darkness.*" He recited it flatly, the way you recite something you've heard so often it has worn smooth as river stone. "Technically true. None need sit in darkness. We've imported all the darkness into people instead."

Sable was quiet a moment longer. Then, in a voice carefully stripped of anything at all: "My mother sold memories. In Hollowmere. Before she died. I always thought she was hiding from something." She picked up her stride again, brisk, businesslike, a door closing audibly behind her eyes. "Come on. Madame Ostrey's. You can tell me the rest somewhere with a lock."

* * *

Madame Ostrey's boarding house was exactly as Wren remembered it: narrow, crooked, smelling of lavender deployed aggressively against the smell of the tanneries, and presided over by a woman built like a well-fed falcon. She took one look at Wren on her doorstep and said, "Absolutely not."

"Ostrey—"

"Eleven years, Calloway. Eleven years I sat in that gaming house waiting for you to come apologize, and instead you got yourself exiled for *arithmetic*." She folded her arms. "And now you're on my step with a stranger and a bag that clinks like it's full of stolen silverware."

"It's full of star-charts," Wren said.

"That's worse." But she stepped aside to let them in, because underneath the flint, Madame Ostrey had a heart the size of a cathedral, and both of them knew it, and she would have denied it under oath. "Two silvers a night. Each. And if the girl steals anything, I'm blaming you."

"She won't steal anything," Wren began.

"I might steal something," Sable said pleasantly, already inside. "But only from people who deserve it."

Ostrey looked at her for a long moment, and something passed across her face that Wren, who was bad at reading rooms and worse at reading faces, eventually identified as approval. "I like her better than you," she announced, and went to put the kettle on.

* * *

They took the back room, the one with the window facing the alley, because Sable insisted on windows facing alleys the way other people insisted on doors that locked. From it, above the rooftops, Wren could see the palace.

He had seen the palace a thousand times. He had worked within sight of it for a decade. But he had never seen it at dusk under the new lamps, and seeing it now was like seeing a portrait of a friend after the friend had died.

The Ninefold Court rose from the center of Virelay in nine ascending tiers, one for each of the old constellations, crowned by the Astral Spire where he himself had once kept his instruments and his faith. Every tier blazed. Light poured from it in rivers, fed by conduits that ran up the Spire's spine like luminous veins—and at the very top, above the highest terrace, something burned so bright that the clouds themselves had turned silver.

And standing on that highest terrace, visible even at this distance, was a figure in white.

Even from here, even across half a mile of evening air, Wren could see that she glowed. Not reflected light. Emitted. The Regent Vessia stood at the rail of her terrace like a lighthouse that had learned to walk, and the crowd in the plaza far below had stopped to look up at her, faces tilted, mouths slightly open, drinking her in.

"She does that every evening," Ostrey said from the doorway, setting down tea. "Sunset. Stands up there and lets them look. Half the city turns out for it now. They call it the Radiance Hour." Her mouth twisted. "Used to be you watched the actual sunset. Free, that was. Now they watch a woman."

Wren raised the spyglass he'd carried out of exile—his one possession of value, besides the charts sewn into his coat lining—and found her.

Vessia was beautiful the way a blade is beautiful, all clean lines and purposeful shine. White hair coiled high. Gown like poured moonlight. She smiled down at her city with perfect warmth, and Wren adjusted the focus, and the smile stayed perfect, and that was the wrong part. Nobody's smile was that symmetrical. He'd spent his life studying orbits, and orbits were honest things—they wobbled, they precessed, they betrayed their imperfections to anyone patient enough to look. Nothing alive was this smooth.

Then she turned her head, scanning the plaza, and the spyglass showed him her eyes.

One was dark. Warm brown, human, ordinary.

The other was filled with a small, slow-turning point of white light, like a star seen through deep water. Like a star that had been *put there*.

Wren lowered the glass very slowly. His hands had begun to shake, and he set them flat on the sill until they stopped.

"Wren?" Sable had come to stand beside him. She followed his gaze up to the distant bright figure. "What is it?"

"I predicted the starless night," he said. "You know that. It's why they exiled me. Everyone assumed I'd made an error—that I'd miscounted, or fudged my tables, or gone mad from too much time alone with telescopes." He laughed, and it came out wrong. "I didn't make an error. My calculations showed the stars dimming in sequence, brightest first, over thirteen lunar months. Thirteen. I thought it was absurd. Stars don't obey arithmetic like that. Stars don't get *spent* according to a schedule."

"But?"

"But they do," Wren said, "if someone is spending them. One constellation per new moon. Twelve taken, one remaining." He looked at her, and let her see it, whatever it was in his face. "Sable. She's wearing one. In her eye. She's not gathering the starlight for the crown. She's already begun *wearing* it."

Sable stared up at the terrace. The Regent Vessia, tiny and radiant at her rail, lifted one hand to her adoring city, and ten thousand throats below cheered the theft of their own sky.

"Thirteen turns," Sable said slowly. "Your exile was—when?"

"Fourteen months ago."

"Then the first turn already happened before you ever published. The count started before anyone knew to count." She turned from the window, and her jaw had set in the way he was beginning to understand meant she had finished being afraid and moved on to being dangerous. "How many nights until the next new moon?"

Wren did the arithmetic without meaning to—it lived in his head whether he wanted it there or not, faithful and horrible. "Nine."

"Nine nights." She was already moving, pulling her bag onto the bed, checking contents with quick fingers. "So we don't have time to be careful. Tomorrow I start working the city. There are people here who owe me, or owe my mother, or owe somebody who owes somebody—I can find a way into the Court. There's always a way in; rich people love showing off their locks to people who can pick them."

"And I'll need my old observatory access," Wren said. "The Spire archives. If the Binding rite is written anywhere, it's written there, and I still remember which floorboards squeak."

From the doorway, Madame Ostrey cleared her throat with the delicacy of a landslide.

"If the two of you are planning what I think you're planning," she said, "then you'll want to know that the Court is hosting a gala in six nights. Founders' Vigil. Every noble house, every guard captain, the Regent herself." She set down a second pot of tea, unasked, which was Ostrey's version of an oath of loyalty. "And invitations, as it happens, are something I can get. My late husband collected debts from half the peerage, and I've kept the ledger."

Wren blinked at her. "You'd help us? After the card incident?"

"After the card incident," Ostrey said, "I had time to open this boarding house, buy the building next door, and learn that honesty is a lovely quality in a man and a ruinous one in a business partner." She fixed him with a falcon's eye. "I'm helping the girl. You're just luggage she brought."

"Fair," said Sable.

"It is not—" Wren sighed. "Fine. It's fair."

Below them, the Radiance Hour ended. The Regent withdrew from her terrace, and the plaza emptied, and the city settled into its borrowed brightness—and high above it all, beyond the lamplight and the haze, the sky over Virelay was almost perfectly black.

Almost. Wren checked anyway, out of habit, out of grief, out of the stubborn refusal that had cost him everything.

One star remained. Low in the west, trembling, alone—the last unharvested light in the whole of the heaven, hanging on like a man gripping a ledge.

And as he watched, it flickered.

Not the way stars flicker. The way candles flicker when someone, somewhere, has opened a door.

"Oh no," Wren whispered.

"What?" Sable was at his shoulder instantly. "What is it?"

He handed her the spyglass and pointed, and waited while she found it, and watched her shoulders stiffen as she understood.

"That's not possible," she said. "That's the constellation of the *King's Crown*. Aldric's crown. Your charts said it goes last."

"It does." Wren's mouth was dry. "Which means either my arithmetic is finally wrong—"

"Or they're not waiting for the thirteenth moon."

Somewhere across the dark city, a bell began to ring. Not a festival bell. Not a warning bell, either—something stranger, older, a rhythm of nine strokes, pause, nine strokes, that Wren had never heard in all his years in the capital and that made Madame Ostrey, in the doorway, go white as flour.

"That's the Summoning," she said. "They haven't rung that since before I was born. Something's woken in the palace."

Sable set down the spyglass with a click, very quietly, the way people handle things when loudness has become unbearable.

"Get me that invitation," she said.
