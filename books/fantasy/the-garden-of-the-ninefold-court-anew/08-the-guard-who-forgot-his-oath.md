## 8. The Guard Who Forgot His Oath

Dain Ferros had been a captain of the Royal Guard for eleven years, and in all that time he had learned exactly one thing worth learning: that the Court of Virelay was a machine for making beautiful things out of ugly ones.

The gala was proof. The Lantern Hall had been strung with captured starlight—thousands of lamps burning with the cold white fire harvested from the emptied sky—and beneath them the nobility of the Court turned and glittered like fish in a net. Every face powdered. Every laugh pitched to carry exactly as far as it needed to and no farther. Somewhere above them all, behind the gilded doors of the Ninth Gate, the Regent Vessia held her court within her court, radiant and wrong-eyed, and everyone pretended not to notice that the sky outside had holes in it.

Dain stood at his post by the eastern colonnade with his hands clasped behind him and his face arranged into the expression the Guard called *the Wall*—polite, immovable, empty. He had worn the Wall so long he sometimes worried it had grown into his skull.

"Captain Ferros." Lady Merrowin drifted past with a glass of wine she would never drink. "You look grim tonight."

"I'm told it's my best feature, my lady."

She laughed the way people laughed at Court—quickly, checking over her shoulder first—and swept on. Dain watched her go and felt the familiar dull weight settle into his chest. Eleven years. He had taken the oath at nineteen, kneeling on cold marble before the old king's empty throne, hand on a sword older than his grandfather, swearing to protect the Crown and its wearer unto death.

He had never once seen the king.

That was the joke nobody at Court laughed about. There was a throne. There was a crown—it sat in the Regent's keeping, brought out on high holy days, blazing with a light that made your teeth ache. There were portraits of His Majesty Aldric the Ninth in every government office, painted from memory or invention, a stern handsome man with a jaw like a cliff. But the king himself had not been seen in public in thirty years. *The king is in seclusion,* they said. *The king is in prayer. The king is unwell.* And every guard who asked too many questions found himself reassigned to some border fort where the wind ate the paint off the walls.

Dain had stopped asking questions around year four. It was easier. Duty, he had come to understand, was mostly the art of not noticing.

The bell rang again across the city—nine strokes, pause, nine strokes—and the music faltered for half a beat before the musicians recovered, the way a horse recovers when it steps in a hole. Nobody mentioned it. Of course nobody mentioned it.

Dain resumed not noticing.

* * *

He noticed her because she was the only person in the hall moving *wrong*.

Everyone else at a gala moved in curves—sweeping arcs toward the wine, the dais, the exits, each trajectory planned like a dance figure. This woman moved in straight lines. She wore a gown of deep green that fit her well enough, though Dain's eye caught what it was designed to hide: she stood like someone used to standing in doorways and alleys, weight forward, close to walls. Her hair was pinned up in a style that took effort, but her hands—

Her hands kept doing something no lady's hands did. They were *counting*. Fingers brushing a candelabra here, a reticule there, a lord's signet ring as she passed him, light as a moth landing. Not taking. Measuring. Pricing the room.

A thief, then. A good one, dressed for the occasion, working the crowd while the Summoning bell had everyone's attention pointed upward and inward.

Dain should have signaled the under-captains. Two fingers off his left shoulder, and three men would have closed on her gently, professionally, and escorted her to a room where the questions were unpleasant. That was procedure. That was duty.

Instead he pushed off the colonnade and began to walk, unhurried, cutting the curve of the hall on a line that would intercept hers near the terrace doors. He told himself he simply wanted to handle it quietly—a captain's discretion, less fuss than a squad. He told himself a great many things that night, and believed almost none of them by morning.

She saw him coming. He watched her see him—the flicker of assessment, the recalculation—and instead of veering away she turned *toward* him, smiling, and suddenly she was just another guest, glass in hand, admiring the lamps.

"They're lovely," she said, nodding at the starlight lanterns overhead. "Do you know, I've never seen one up close? Where I'm from we make do with candles."

"Where are you from?"

"A long way north. You have an honest face, Captain. Are you supposed to be watching me?"

"Yes," said Dain.

"Oh good." She sipped her stolen wine. "I'd hate to be invisible. It's the worst feeling in the world, being somewhere no one sees you."

Something in the way she said it made him look at her properly for the first time. She wasn't flirting. Under the smile her eyes were doing arithmetic—distance to the doors, distance to the guards, whether he was alone—and underneath *that* was something tired. Something that had been running for days.

"You're not from any northern house," he said quietly. "I know every face on this guest list, and yours isn't on it."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't."

"Give me a reason not to call the squad."

She considered him. Then, absurdly, she leaned in as if sharing gossip and said, "Because in about ninety seconds, a man named Lord Cassel is going to discover that the memory he paid two hundred crowns for last month—the one of his wife, alive, laughing, on the day they met—is gone from his vault. And he'll make a scene. And while everyone is comforting him, I'll be gone, and you can be the only guard in this hall who didn't spend the evening chasing a ghost."

Dain stared at her. "You already took it."

"Mm." She touched her earlobe, where a small pearl earring hung—one of a pair, he now realized, and the other earring was nowhere, and had perhaps never been an earring at all. "Memory-work. Very delicate. You wouldn't approve."

"Thieving memories is a hanging offense."

"So I've heard." She smiled up at him, and it was a real smile this time, crooked and unafraid, and Dain felt the Wall crack somewhere along its foundation. "But you're not calling anyone. I can tell. You've got the look of a man whose oath fits badly, like boots borrowed from a taller brother."

"That," said Dain, "is treasonous talk."

"It's observational talk. There's a difference." She stepped back, gathering her skirts. "Well, Captain Ferros. It's been a genuine pleasure. Try not to arrest anyone on my account."

And that should have been the end of it. He let her walk six steps toward the terrace doors, telling himself he'd stop her at seven, at eight, at the very latest nine—

Then she paused beside the great mirror at the hall's entrance, ostensibly to adjust her hair, and pressed something small against the glass.

* * *

Later, Dain could never explain why he went to the mirror. Procedure said raise the alarm; the woman was walking out of a royal gala with stolen property and a confession. Instead he crossed the hall like a man sleepwalking, waited until the crowd's attention swung toward Lord Cassel's rising, bewildered voice—"it's *gone*, I tell you, the whole memory, like a page torn out"—and picked up what she had left.

It was a locket. Old brass, warm as skin, with a hairline seam where it opened. Inside, instead of a portrait, there was a sliver of something that caught the lamplight and held it wrong—not reflecting the hall but giving back a fragment of somewhere else entirely. Memory-glass. He'd seen confiscated pieces in evidence rooms, always dead, always dark. This one was *alive*, humming faintly against his palm like a bird's heartbeat.

He should have put it in his pouch. Evidence. Chain of custody. Duty.

He looked into it.

The garden hit him like weather.

Sunlight—but not the thin gray sunlight of Virelay, which came filtered through harvested haze. This was gold, thick as honey, falling through the branches of nine trees arranged in a ring, and the grass was green in a way that hurt, green like the color had been invented that morning. A boy was running through it. Dark-haired, maybe eight years old, sleeves rolled, mud to his knees, laughing with his whole body the way children laugh when no one has taught them yet that kings don't.

"Come *catch* me!" the boy shouted—to whom? The memory shivered, and Dain saw a woman's hands, quick and clever, reaching for him, and heard a voice say, warm and amused: *"Slow down, little majesty. Crowns are heavy. Don't practice carrying one before you must."*

And the boy—Aldric, Dain realized, it was *Aldric*, the boy who became the king in the portraits, except no portrait had ever shown him *like this*, alight, ordinary, *loved*—the boy stopped and grew solemn and said:

*"When I'm crowned, will I still be me?"*

And the woman's hands stilled. And after a moment she said, *"Yes. Whatever they tell you, whatever they put on you—you stay you. Promise me."*

*"I promise."*

The memory ended. Dain stood in the Lantern Hall with a hundred nobles murmuring around him and Lord Cassel being consoled with wine, and discovered that his eyes were wet and that eleven years of the Wall had developed a fault line straight down the middle.

Because he remembered—he hadn't known he remembered, it had been buried so deep under oaths and drills and not-noticing—he remembered standing post, years ago, young and new, outside a sealed door in the oldest wing, and hearing through the stone a sound he had reported to no one because there was no one to report it to. A sound like a man weeping. Or singing. Or both.

*The king remembers being kind,* someone had told him once. He couldn't recall who. He couldn't recall much of anything anymore about why he'd taken the oath, only that he'd meant it, wholly, at nineteen, kneeling before an empty throne, and that meaning it had been the last time he'd ever been certain of anything.

They had taken that from him. Slowly, politely, with promotions and pensions. They had taken the boy in the garden from the whole kingdom, and hung a stranger's jaw in his place on every wall.

Dain closed his fist around the locket.

Across the hall, the terrace doors stood open on the night. The woman in green was gone.

* * *

He found her three streets later, which surprised them both.

She came out of a doorway with a knife already in her hand, saw it was him, and didn't lower it. "Captain. Following ladies home is frowned upon."

"You left this." He held up the locket.

Something crossed her face—alarm, calculation, and then a strange kind of grief. "I left it on purpose," she said slowly. "For whoever found it. Consider it payment for not arresting me."

"It's not mine to take."

"No?" Her eyes narrowed. "Then why are your knuckles white around it?"

Dain looked down at his own hand as if it belonged to a stranger. He thought about the boy in the garden. He thought about the sealed door, and the sound behind it, and the eleven years of not-asking. He thought about the oath, and how a leash looks exactly like a rope of honor right up until you feel it pull.

"My name is Dain Ferros," he said. "Captain of the Royal Guard. And I am either about to commit the greatest treason in the history of Virelay, or the first honest thing I've done in a decade. I haven't decided which."

The knife lowered an inch. "Those are usually the same thing," she said. "Sable Pemberton."

"Sable." He tested the name. "What's in the locket, Sable?"

She studied him for a long moment—the Wall, the wet eyes, the white knuckles—and whatever she was looking for, she seemed to find it. "A piece of a king," she said. "The real one. And if I'm right, he's still alive, behind the Ninth Gate, and the Regent is eating him alive one moon at a time. So." She sheathed the knife and lifted her chin. "Are you coming, Captain? Or are you going to stand there holding your leash?"

Somewhere across the rooftops, the Summoning bell rang again—nine strokes, pause, nine strokes—and Dain Ferros, who had spent eleven years learning not to notice, finally noticed which direction the sound was pulling him.

He followed her into the dark.
