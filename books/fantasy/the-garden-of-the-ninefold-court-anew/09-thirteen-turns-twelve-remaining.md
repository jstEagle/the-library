## 9. Thirteen Turns, Twelve Remaining

Wren Calloway had once believed that exile would be the death of him. He had imagined it as a slow wasting—years of cheap ink and cheaper wine in some border town where nobody cared about the sky. He had not imagined that exile would instead involve sharing a rented room above a Hollowmere tannery with a memory-thief who snored like a collapsing roof beam, while he tried to save the world with a stolen star chart and a stub of charcoal.

The universe, he had learned, had a sense of humor. It was simply not a kind one.

He worked by candlelight because the tallow was all they could afford, and because Sable had pointed out—with the particular gentleness she reserved for telling people things they already knew—that lamplight through shutters attracted attention, and attention, these days, came wearing nine-pointed sigils and carrying fire.

So: candlelight, charcoal, and the chart.

The chart was his own work, confiscated when the Court expelled him, and recovered from the saddlebag of a dead rider on the road south of Hollowmere. Wren tried not to think about whose blood had paid for its return. He thought about it anyway, most nights. Mag's face floated up at inconvenient moments—the ferryman laughing, the ferryman burning—and Wren had decided that this was not sentimentality but bookkeeping. Debts should be remembered. That was rather the whole point of astronomy, if you squinted at it sideways: the sky was just a ledger that never forgave and never forgot.

He spread the chart across the floorboards, weighted the corners with four mismatched cups, and knelt over it until his knees ached.

"Thirteen," he murmured. "It has to be thirteen. Everything about them is thirteen."

Above him, through the ceiling, Sable turned over in her sleep. The roof-beam snore resumed.

Wren counted the lunar turns backward from the night the stars went out. That was the anchor point—he was certain of it, certain the way you are certain of your own heartbeat. The starless night had not been an accident or an omen. It had been a *harvest*. The first cut. The regent—Vessia, radiant and wrong-eyed, according to every frightened whisper that crossed the valley—had opened the crown's account that night and begun making deposits.

One turn per constellation. That was the rhythm hidden in the old rite fragments he'd memorized as a boy, back when he'd thought the Court's liturgies were poetry rather than arithmetic. *The crown shall drink the sky in sips, that it never choke.* Thirteen new moons. Thirteen constellations. And on the thirteenth—

On the thirteenth, the binding completed, and whatever wore King Aldric's body became permanent. Eternal. A king who could not die, could not be unmade, could not even, Wren suspected, be *changed*—frozen forever behind a crown of harvested light, with Vessia's hand resting on the clasp.

He did the subtraction again, because he was the sort of man who checked his sums three times and then dreamed about checking them a fourth.

The first new moon had been the starless night. Since then: one full turn of the moon. Waxing, waning, dark again. Which meant the second harvest was due in—

Wren counted on his fingers, felt foolish, counted on the chart instead.

Eleven days.

"Eleven days," he said aloud, to the candle, to the chart, to the sleeping thief overhead. "We have eleven days to walk into the capital of a kingdom that wants us dead, find a king who may no longer exist as a person, and convince him—or the thing behind his eyes—to let us commit treason against his own head."

The candle flame flickered, which Wren chose to interpret as agreement.

* * *

Sable came down the stairs near dawn, hair escaping its braid in every direction, and stood over his chart with her arms crossed and her eyes still half-shut.

"You didn't sleep."

"I slept beautifully," Wren lied. "For hours. Deeply. Like a felled ox."

"You're kneeling on the same cup you were kneeling on last night. There's a cup-shaped bruise forming on your knee. I can see it from here."

"That's a birthmark."

"It wasn't there yesterday."

"A very fast birthmark." He sat back on his heels and rubbed the offending knee. "I've finished the rite. Or I've finished understanding it, which is worse. Do you want the good news or the bad news?"

Sable poured herself the dregs of last night's tea, drank it cold without flinching, and said, "You have one piece of news and you've dressed half of it up as good. Give me both."

"The good news is that the binding isn't finished. The crown is incomplete. Eleven constellations remain unharvested, which means eleven chances for the rite to fail. It is not inevitable. Nothing about it is inevitable."

"And the bad news?"

"The bad news is everything else." Wren pulled the chart toward himself and tapped the western edge, where he'd inked the capital's position. "Each new moon, the crown devours a constellation—not metaphorically. The stars go out, one grouping at a time, and their light goes *into* the crown. I watched the first harvest happen. We all did. The whole sky, gone in a single night—that was the Pleiades, the Seven Sisters, and the Crown of the North taken together. The opening payment. The regent wanted to make an impression."

"She succeeded," Sable said dryly. "I screamed."

"Many people screamed. It was, from a certain professional standpoint, an excellent piece of theater." He caught her look and raised a hand. "Not a compliment. An observation. Vessia understands spectacle. She took the entire sky at once so that no one would ever wonder whether the next disappearance was natural. Everyone will remember the first night. Nobody will notice the eleventh star going missing over some farmer's field."

"So the rest will be quiet."

"Quiet, local, deniable. A constellation here, a constellation there. By the time ordinary people understand what's happening, the thirteenth turn will have passed, and the crown will be eternal, and arguing with eternity is a famously losing proposition." Wren traced the moon-phases he'd drawn down the chart's margin—thirteen small circles, each darker than the last. "Here. This is our ledger. First turn, spent. Second turn—" he touched the second circle, "—eleven days from now. If we do nothing, we lose another constellation and the crown grows stronger. Each harvest makes the binding harder to crack. The rite feeds on accumulated light. By the twelfth turn, I estimate the crown would take a god's hammer to break. By the thirteenth, nothing breaks it at all. Not ever."

Sable set down her cold tea. When she spoke, her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of someone handling something fragile. "And the sacrifice the rite demands. To unbind him. You said the old texts mention a price."

"They do." Wren hesitated. He was, by nature and profession, a man who believed in saying exactly what the numbers said. But he had watched Sable Pemberton for two weeks now, and he had noticed the locket she never let out of arm's reach, and the way her thumb found it whenever she thought no one was looking—a small, constant motion, like touching a wound to check it hadn't reopened. "The texts say the seal cracks only for 'a willing surrender of what is treasured most.' Deliberately vague. Liturgy always is. Priests love a phrase that means whatever the congregation fears it means."

"What do you think it means?"

"I think it means different things to different people, and the crown doesn't care which. It takes the truest answer." He met her eyes. "Whatever you treasure most. That's the currency. And before you ask—no, I don't know how it verifies sincerity. I suspect it doesn't need to. I suspect you'll know, and it'll know, and there won't be any lying to it."

Sable was quiet for a long moment. Her thumb moved toward the locket, stopped, returned to her side. "Then we'd better make sure it's not me standing in front of that crown," she said lightly. Too lightly. "I'm a thief, Wren. My most treasured possession changes weekly depending on market rates."

"Of course," Wren said, and let her have the lie, because he was learning that kindness, sometimes, looked exactly like bad scholarship.

* * *

They spent the morning doing sums, because hope, Wren had concluded years ago, was best measured arithmetically. Feelings inflated and deflated like weather. Numbers held still and let you build on them.

"Eleven days," Sable said, pacing the length of the room, which took four steps. "Virelay is how far?"

"Nine days on foot. Six if we ride, and we can't afford horses, and horse thieves get hanged, and I've seen you climb a fence and I wouldn't trust you with a horse."

"That fence was slippery."

"That fence was *horizontal*." He pulled out the map. "Six days riding, five and a half if we push and ruin the horses. That leaves perhaps two days inside the city to find the king, learn the truth of the binding, and act. Two days, in a palace we've never entered, guarded by soldiers sworn to the woman performing the rite."

"So we don't enter as ourselves."

"We don't enter at all, ideally. There are ways under Virelay. Old ways. The palace was built on top of something much older—I found references in the Court archives before my expulsion. Cisterns, foundations, sealed galleries. The Ninefold Court didn't build their capital; they built it *over* something, the way you'd build over a grave you didn't want to look at."

Sable stopped pacing. "A garden," she said quietly.

Wren looked up. "What?"

"Nothing. Something my mother left me. A garden, under the palace." She touched the locket this time, openly, and Wren pretended not to see. "If there are ways under, I can find them. Finding ways into places is most of what I do. The other part is getting back out, which we can worry about later, in the traditional manner."

"In the traditional manner being—"

"Screaming, mostly."

Despite everything, Wren laughed. It startled him, the sound coming out of his chest after weeks of ash and arithmetic. He covered it by coughing and pointing at the chart. "There's one more problem. The largest one. We keep speaking as though finding the king solves something. But consider what we're actually proposing. The crown has been eating him for—how long? Years? Decades? Every harvest takes light, yes, but the binding also takes *him*. His memories first, I'd wager. Memory is the densest form of light there is; any memory-thief could tell you that. By now the king may be—" he searched for a gentle word and failed, "—hollow. A shape wearing a crown. We may reach him and find there's no one left to save."

Sable was silent. Then she said, "Mag told me something, before he died. He said, 'The king remembers being kind.'" She shook her head slowly. "I thought it was a dying man's nonsense. But if the crown eats memories—if it's eaten almost everything—then maybe that's what's left. Maybe *kindness* is the last thing it can't digest."

Wren wrote it down. He wrote everything down; it was the only prayer he knew. *The king remembers being kind.* Five words, in charcoal, at the bottom of a stolen star chart, beneath thirteen darkening moons.

"If that's true," he said, "then the arithmetic changes. Because a hollow king is a locked door. But a king who remembers being kind—that's a door with a handle. All we need is to remind him of the rest."

* * *

They left at dusk, when the light made everyone equally anonymous. Wren carried the chart rolled tight against his spine, wrapped in oilcloth, and felt it there like a second backbone. Sable carried the locket and, presumably, several items she had acquired without discussing their acquisition.

At the edge of town, where the road bent toward the low hills and the long walk south, Sable stopped and looked back at Hollowmere. The valley lay in evening shadow, scarred where the riders' fires had been, the boundary stones visible as pale teeth along the ridgeline. The wards were dead. The stones were just stones now. Anyone could leave. Anyone could enter. The prison had become a place, which was either freedom or exposure depending entirely on the day.

"Eleven days," she said.

"Ten and a half, if we walk through the night tonight."

"Then stop talking and walk, astronomer."

They walked. Behind them the first stars were coming out—fewer than there should have been, thinner, as though the sky itself were being skimmed a layer at a time—but Wren counted them anyway, named them anyway, kept his ledger anyway. Hope measured in arithmetic. Eleven constellations remained. Ten and a half days remained. One king, somewhere beneath a palace, remembering how to be kind.

It was not much. But it was more than nothing, and Wren Calloway had built a career on noticing exactly the difference between those two numbers.

Halfway up the first hill, Sable suddenly stopped and swore.

"What?"

"The riders," she said. "The ones hunting me. They asked after 'the daughter of Lys.' They knew my mother's name, Wren. Before I even knew what my mother had left me—they knew." She turned to face him, and in the thin starlight her expression was grim and sharp and awake. "Which means Vessia knows about the locket. She's known all along. She isn't searching for me because I stole something."

She started walking again, faster.

"She's searching for me because I'm *carrying* something. And she wants it back before the twelfth turn."

Wren quickened his pace to match hers, and did the arithmetic again, and wished—for the first time in his exiled life—that he were the sort of man who could believe in anything other than numbers.
