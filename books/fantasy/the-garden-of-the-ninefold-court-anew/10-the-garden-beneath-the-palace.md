## 10. The Garden Beneath the Palace

The passage Dain led them through had not been opened in a hundred years, and it smelled like it.

"Charming," Wren said, sneezing for the fourth time in as many minutes. His star-charts—recovered copies, the originals still locked somewhere in Vessia's archives—were rolled tight against his back, and he held them now like a man holding a child above floodwater. "I spent six years mapping the heavens, and I have never once encountered a smell like this."

"That would be the century," Dain said. He walked ahead with a lantern held low, his armor left behind in favor of a servant's grey livery that made him look less like a captain and more like a very large, very apologetic butler. "The old service tunnels run under the whole palace. The Court sealed the western wing after the king was... after. Nobody comes down here. The servants think it's haunted."

"Is it?" I asked.

Dain considered this with the seriousness of a man who had sworn an oath to consider things seriously. "Not yet."

We had entered the palace at dusk through a coal chute, which struck me as fitting. I had spent my life taking things from people who never noticed they were gone; now three of us were stealing our way toward a king, and the whole city above us glittered with stolen light. Through grates in the tunnel ceiling I caught glimpses of it: lamps burning white and steady, fed on harvested stars, lighting streets where children sold their grandmothers' wedding days for bread. Every lamp we passed made my teeth ache.

Wren kept up a running commentary, mostly to keep his own fear company. "Left here. No—sorry—my left or your left? This is why astronomers don't navigate. We deal exclusively in fixed points, and nothing down here is fixed." He bumped his head on a low arch. "Ow. That arch is not fixed either."

"You could be quiet," Dain suggested.

"I could," Wren agreed, and managed it for nearly eleven seconds.

I let them fill the dark with noise. It was easier than thinking about what waited at the end of it. For nine days since Hollowmere burned, I had carried the locket against my sternum like a second heart, and every mile closer to Virelay it had grown heavier—not in weight, in *pull*. Mag's last words came back to me at bad moments, usually around dawn: *The king remembers being kind.* A dying ferryman's riddle. I had turned it over so many times the words had gone smooth as river stones.

The tunnel changed.

It happened gradually enough that at first I thought it was only the lantern. The stone underfoot stopped being damp. Then it stopped being stone at all in places—soft loam, threaded with pale roots that crossed our path like the letters of a word none of us could read. The air warmed. It smelled, impossibly, of green things: cut grass, wet earth, roses past their prime but trying.

Wren stopped sneezing. "That's not right," he said softly, which from Wren was practically a hymn of awe. "There shouldn't be—" He knelt and pressed two fingers into the soil, then held them to the lantern light. "This is alive. This is *growing*." He looked up at us, and for once he had no commentary at all.

Dain had gone very still. "I've served in this palace eleven years," he said. "I've walked over this spot ten thousand times. There's nothing on any map."

"There wouldn't be," I said. My mouth had gone dry. I knew this place. Not from maps—from memory. From a boy's memory, hidden in a dead woman's locket: a garden with nine hedges woven into courts, a fountain shaped like a sleeping swan, sunlight that fell in a particular slanted gold because it came through glass older than the palace around it. *Aldric's garden.* The one place in his gilded childhood where he had been allowed to be only a boy. "It's not on any map because it isn't supposed to exist anymore. They sealed it when they crowned him."

"Then why," Dain said slowly, "does it smell like June?"

Ahead of us, the tunnel ended at a door.

It was not an impressive door—that was what unsettled me most. It was a garden door, oak gone silver with age, with an iron latch worn smooth by hands. Someone had chained it shut, wrapped the chain three times, and sealed each link with wax stamped by the nine-pointed sigil. But the door itself stood slightly ajar within its chains, the way a sleeping person's mouth falls open, and through the gap spilled light that no lantern makes—light with a faint gold-green warmth to it, like the last hour of a summer afternoon that refuses, out of stubbornness, to end.

Nobody moved for a moment.

"Well," Wren said finally, "the wards on it are dead. Whatever sealed this has been failing for years. Decades, maybe." He glanced at me. "Sable?"

I reached out and touched the chain. The wax seals crumbled at my fingertips like old bread. Underneath, the iron was cold—but the cold stopped exactly at the door's edge, the way winter stops at a windowpane.

"It's been waiting," I said. I didn't know how I knew. I knew the way you know a tune you heard in childhood. "It's been waiting for someone to come in."

Dain drew a breath that wanted to be an objection and settled for being a sigh. "My oath is to protect the Crown," he said. "Eleven years I've told myself that means the throne, the regent, the court. But an oath is a strange thing. You can forget what it means and still feel it pull." He set his hand on the latch. "The king remembers being kind. Mag said that? Then let's go see if it's true."

He lifted the latch. The door swung inward without a sound, and the summer-light poured over us like water.

* * *

The garden was dying beautifully.

That was my first thought, and it was wrong, and it took me the length of the first hedge-row to understand how wrong. At first glance everything looked lush—the ninefold courts of hedgerow all present, the swan fountain still trickling, roses climbing trellises that should have rotted a century ago. But the colors were wrong. Too vivid. Roses red as fresh wounds, grass green as stained glass, sky—there was no sky, only the ancient glass ceiling, but light came through it anyway, golden and sourceless—and the light itself was too rich, like cream poured over everything until the whole garden drowned in sweetness.

And everywhere, things were fading even as they bloomed. A rose would open fully, hold for a heartbeat, then go grey at the edges and drop. Grass sprouted, shimmered, wilted, sprouted again. The fountain's water ran clear, then clouded, then cleared, over and over, like something being remembered and forgotten in cycles.

"It's a loop," Wren whispered. He had his charts out, though there was nothing in them about gardens; he consulted them anyway, the way frightened men consult anything. "No—it's worse than a loop. It's being *fed*. Something is pouring life into this place and the place is burning through it as fast as it arrives." He looked at me, and his face had gone the color of his charts. "Sable. What burns life like that?"

I didn't answer, because I had seen him.

At the center of the ninth court, where Aldric's memory said a sundial should stand, there was a chair. An ordinary chair, wooden, the kind a gardener might keep by a tool shed. And in the chair sat a man.

He was younger than I expected and older than anyone alive. Both at once. His hair was black shot through with white in patterns like frost ferns, and his hands—resting open on his knees, palms up, in the posture of a man who has given up holding anything—were the worst. The skin of his hands was translucent. I could see light moving underneath it, slow and tidal, the way you see a candle through a curtain. Where the light passed, his flesh brightened briefly, almost warmly, and then dimmed again, a little more each time.

His eyes were open. They were the color of a winter afternoon, pale and far away, and they were looking at the roses without seeing them—or seeing, perhaps, some other garden entirely, some other century.

"Aldric," I said.

Nothing. The name fell into the space between us and did not echo.

"He can't hear," Dain said hoarsely. He had removed his cap—he'd kept the wretched thing on through coal chutes and sewers out of some stubborn idea of disguise—and now he held it against his chest like a schoolboy at a funeral. "Your Majesty," he tried, louder, and the title cracked in the middle. Eleven years guarding a throne, and I watched him understand in real time that the man he'd been guarding was this: a hollowed figure in a garden chair, being eaten by his own crown.

Because he wore it. Of course he wore it. That was the horror I hadn't let myself picture. The Ninefold Crown sat on his head—nine points of dark metal, thin as needles, delicate as insect legs—and where each point touched his scalp, the skin had gone smooth and silvered, like scar tissue, like the metal had been there so long the flesh had grown around it in acceptance. Or surrender.

The crown was drinking him. Slowly. Elegantly. With the patience of a thing that had done this to eight kings before him.

"Oh," Wren said. Just that. All his arithmetic, all his constellations and bindings and turns of the moon, reduced to one small syllable of grief.

I walked forward. I don't remember deciding to. My feet decided, and the rest of me followed the way you follow someone through a house you grew up in.

Up close, the details were worse and better at once. Worse: the fine tremor in his translucent fingers, the way his chest rose and fell in the rhythm of the garden's loop—bright, fade, bright, fade—as if he breathed in time with the roses being born and dying around him. Better: his face. Even hollowed, even half-consumed, there was something in it that the memory-locket had shown me in a boy of nine—a stubbornness in the jaw, a kindness in the set of the mouth that suffering had bent but not broken. The tyrant of Hollowmere's nightmares. The prisoner-king whose ward-prison had caged me my whole life. I had imagined a monster wearing a man's shape.

What sat in the chair was a man wearing a monster's crown.

"I stole memories for twenty years," I said to him quietly. "Hundreds of them. First kisses and deathbeds and the smell of somebody's mother's kitchen. I told myself a rule to survive it: never keep what you steal. Take it, sell it, walk away clean." My hand went to the locket at my throat. "You're the only thing I ever broke that rule for. My mother hid your childhood inside me before I could walk, and I didn't even know I was carrying contraband royalty until the stars went out."

His eyes moved.

It was small—I might have imagined it—but the faraway focus shifted, drifted, and settled. On me. On the locket in my hand. His lips parted, and what came out was barely a voice at all, more breath shaped into words, frayed at every edge:

"...Lys."

The name went through me like a blade finding the gap in armor. "You know her name."

"She..." His brow furrowed, and the effort of it cost him visibly—the garden around us flickered, a whole row of roses greying and reviving in a single shudder. "She promised. She said she'd... hide it well. Hide *me* well. The part of me that was..." His gaze drifted to the crown, and something like fear crossed the ruin of his face. "Before."

"The garden," Wren breathed behind me. He had gone pale in the sourceless light. "It's not being fed by accident. Look—the loop, the blooming and dying—it's *him*. The crown takes his self, his memories, his—his *person*, and what's left leaks out, and the garden drinks it. This place is alive because he's being emptied into it." He laughed, a terrible small laugh. "They sealed the garden to hide the evidence. The king's childhood died the day they crowned him, so they buried the childhood and kept the king. And the garden refused to die, because he keeps—"

"—remembering it," I finished.

The king's eyes had closed. When they opened again they were clearer, focused, and for a moment—just a moment—I saw the boy from the locket looking out of the ruined face: a child in a hidden garden, deciding to trust someone.

"You're the daughter of Lys," he said. Stronger now. Each word seemed to cost him a rose; around the ninth court, blooms were dropping like snow. "She had your laugh. I never met you. But she used to sit where you're standing and tell me about the baby she was going to give the whole world to, if the world would just leave her alone long enough." A ghost of a smile. "I remember being kind. Did she send you? Is that what she hid in you—did she hide *me* in you?"

"Yes," I said, and my voice betrayed me, cracking on the single syllable. I pulled the chain over my head. The locket lay in my palm, warm despite everything, warm as it had always been. "There's a piece of you in here. A coronation day. It felt like a funeral. Your mother's hand on your shoulder. You were nine."

"Ten," he whispered. "I was ten. Everyone says nine. It matters to me that it was ten." And then his eyes filled—with actual tears, in that drained face, as if the garden had wrung the last of his humanity up to the surface just in time to show me. "Please. I haven't been *ten* in a hundred years. The crown takes the years in order. It started with the newest and it's working backward. It has my reign, my crowning, my father, my—" His breath hitched. "It will reach the garden soon. It will reach the boy in the garden soon, and then there will be nothing left of me that ever wanted to be kind, and whatever is sitting in this chair when the thirteenth turn comes will wear my face forever."

Behind me, Dain sank to one knee. Not ceremony—collapse. A captain's legs simply refusing further service. "Majesty," he said, into the loam. "I guarded your door for eleven years and I never knew there was a door."

Wren was scribbling on the back of a chart, muttering. "Twelve days. Twelve days to the twelfth new moon, and the crown needs the twelfth turn to finish—it's close, look at his hands, it's *nearly done*—"

Aldric's translucent fingers twitched toward me. Toward the locket.

"Play it," he said. "Whatever she gave you. However she hid it. Please. Before it takes the rest." The winter-afternoon eyes held mine, and in them was the last unguarded thing in the Ninefold Court: hope, small and starving, of the kind that has learned to expect nothing and asks anyway.

"I have to warn you," I said, kneeling so we were eye to eye, king and thief. "Memory-craft hurts. What's broken in you will feel every crack."

"Sable Pemberton," he said, and smiled the smile from the locket, the one that had survived a hundred years of being eaten, "everything in me is already broken. Do your worst. It will be the kindest thing anyone has done in this palace in a century."

I opened the locket.
