## 11. Aldric Speaks in Fragments

The garden knew him before he knew himself.

That was the first thing Aldric understood, in the long grey centuries of his dissolution: the roses remembered his name when he could not. They leaned toward him in the dark beneath the palace, blooming out of season and out of reason, fed on whatever thin warmth still leaked from the place where his heart used to be. He had named them once. All nine hundred of them. He had named them after days of the week, after his mother's laughter, after every servant who had ever been kind to him, and when he ran out of names he had started over, because a king who cannot remember kindness has nothing left to rule.

He was sitting against the fountain — dry these hundred years, its basin full of fallen petals like snow that refused to melt — when he heard footsteps.

Footsteps were not unusual. Vessia came sometimes, radiant and wrong-eyed, to check on her investment. She would stand at the garden gate and look at him the way a jeweler looks at a stone she is still cutting, and she would say, "How are we today, Your Majesty?" in a voice like honey poured over iron, and he would try to answer, and mostly fail, because words came to him now the way birds come to a winter window: one at a time, startled, half-frozen.

But these footsteps were wrong. There were three sets of them, and they did not walk like courtiers. Courtiers walked as though the floor might report on them. These walked like people who expected the floor to fight back.

And there was something else. Something small and bright and *familiar*, carried in one of their pockets like a coal wrapped in linen.

Aldric lifted his head. It took a while. It always took a while.

"Hello," he said. His voice came out rusted. He tried again. "You're... standing in my garden."

The woman in the lead went very still. She had quick dark eyes and the wary posture of someone who had been chased by better-armed people than herself and won anyway. Behind her stood a tall man clutching a leather tube of star-charts as though it were an infant, and a broad-shouldered soldier whose hand kept drifting to a sword hilt and then away, drifting and returning, like a moth at a lamp.

"You can talk," the woman said. Not a question. An accusation, almost.

"I can talk," Aldric agreed. "I can't always promise it will be *worth* listening to. The last coherent sentence I managed was approximately forty years ago, and I believe it was about soup."

The tall astronomer made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

"We've come to help you," the woman said. She crouched down to his level, which no one had done in living memory — or in dead memory either, for that matter. "My name is Sable Pemberton. My mother was Lys."

*Lys.*

The name went through him like a bell struck in an empty cathedral. He felt it in his teeth. In the crown — oh, he always felt things in the crown first, the way you feel weather in an old wound — the metal tightened, greedy, reaching for the sound of her name the way it reached for everything, hoarding, devouring, never satisfied.

"Lys," he repeated. "Lys who ran through the west meadow with her shoes off. Lys who said a king should have to carry his own firewood so he'd know what fire cost." He blinked. His eyes, he knew, were hollow as lanterns with the candles gone; he could feel the strangers flinching from them and did not blame them. "She left. I told her to leave. It was the only kingly thing I ever did."

"She died in Hollowmere," Sable said quietly. "She left me something. She said — she said if the stars ever went out, I should bring it to you."

Her hand went into her coat and came out with a locket.

Aldric's breath stopped. He had not known he was still breathing. It seemed important to resume immediately.

* * *

Here is what it is like, being eaten by your own crown:

Imagine a house where someone comes every night and takes one thing. Not the grand things, at first — not the portraits, not the silver. A spoon. A book. The good chair by the window. You notice, you grieve a little, but the house still stands. Then they begin taking walls. Then floors. Then the memory of what the house looked like, so that even your grief goes homeless.

The crown had taken his childhood first — no, that wasn't true, and he prided himself, insofar as pride remained to him, on precision. It had taken his *access* to his childhood. The memories were still in there, sealed in the metal with all the others, every king who had ever worn it, centuries of them stacked like bodies in a mass grave. Sometimes at night he could hear them murmuring. Nine kings' worth of voices, and underneath them, faintest of all, his own.

What remained to him floated loose: fragments. The smell of cut grass. The weight of a coronation cloak that felt less like honor than like a lid. His mother's hands. Soup. For some reason, soup had survived intact, in lavish detail, and he clung to it the way drowning men cling to driftwood, because it was *his*, because the crown had somehow never thought to want it.

He watched Sable open the locket with fingers that trembled only slightly, and he understood, dimly and then all at once, what she held.

"That's—" His voice cracked. "That's the garden. That's *this* garden. Before."

Inside the locket, light moved. Memory-light, the kind memory-thieves worked with, gold shot through with green. And in it, impossibly, a boy of eleven or twelve knelt in the dirt of this very garden, planting something, his sleeves rolled up, his crown set aside on a bench because crowns are terrible for gardening, and a young woman with mud on her cheek was laughing at something he'd said—

"That's you," Sable whispered. "That's you and—"

"My mother," Aldric said. "Queen Isolde. She said a garden was the only honest thing a palace could grow." He laughed, or made a sound adjacent to laughing, and it hurt, everything hurt, but it was the good hurt, the hurt of a joint remembering how to bend. "I planted a plum tree that day. It died within the month. I planted another. Also died. I planted forty of them over six years and every single one died, and my mother said that persistence was the royal virtue nobody put on the coins."

"Forty?" said the astronomer — Wren, the woman had called him, though perhaps not aloud; names drifted to Aldric these days like petals on wind.

"Forty-one," Aldric corrected. "The last one lived. It's behind you. Don't look too closely at it; it's been alive far longer than any plum tree has any right to be, and I suspect it's feeding on me, and frankly I don't mind. Everything else feeds on me. At least the tree makes plums." He paused. "It made plums. Once. They were terrible. Best thing I ever ate."

Sable was looking at him with an expression he recognized, distantly, from a hundred years ago: the expression of someone meeting a legend and finding a person inside it, and not knowing whether to be relieved or heartbroken.

"They told us you were a tyrant," she said. "In Hollowmere. They told us the valley was built to keep you *in*."

"The valley was built to keep me in," Aldric agreed. "Vessia is very thorough about her stories. But no. The valley was built to keep me *quiet*. There's a difference. A tyrant does things. I haven't done anything in a century except sit here and be digested." He gestured at himself — the hollow eyes, the translucent skin, the way the light of the memory-locket passed through his hand and cast his shadow on the wall behind him, which was more solid than he was. "I'm not the prison's warden, Miss Pemberton. I'm the prisoner. And the meal."

* * *

They played the locket.

Wren had some theory about it — resonance, attunement, the way stolen memory seeks its origin like water seeking sea — but Aldric stopped listening after the first sentence, because the light spilled out across the dead fountain and the garden *woke*.

There was no other word for it. The roses straightened. The moss greened. The dry fountain coughed, sputtered, and produced a single arc of water, clear and cold and impossible, and in the light of the opened memory Aldric saw the garden as it had been — as it *was*, somewhere, in the place the crown could not fully reach — and he heard his own young voice saying something foolish and kind, and his mother answering, and the sound went through the hollow places in him like water into cracked earth.

He wept. He hadn't known he could. The tears were real enough to fall, at least, though they fell strangely, slowly, as if reluctant to leave him, as if even his grief had grown thrifty.

When the memory finished its circuit and folded itself back into the locket, Aldric found he could think. Not clearly — thinking, for him, was now like walking through a house in the dark, bruising yourself on furniture you owned — but *connectedly*. Sentences held their shape. Causes led to effects. It was the finest he had felt in sixty years, and he knew, with the clarity the memory had lent him, exactly why: the locket was a splinter of himself, and near it, the crown's grip loosened. The metal at his brow had actually cooled. He could feel it sulking.

"How long," Sable asked, "has Vessia had you like this?"

"Since the beginning." He settled back against the fountain, and the words came easier now, tumbling like children let out of school. "You should understand — I loved her once. Or I loved who I believed she was. She was my father's regent-counselor, brilliant, tireless, the only person at court who never flattered me. When my father died and the crown came to me at nineteen, she was the one who said, 'It will be heavy, and I will help you carry it.' And she did help. That's the horror of it, Miss Pemberton. She never lied to me. She simply arranged the truth so carefully that lying became unnecessary."

He touched the crown. He could not remove it — he had tried, in the early years, until his fingernails broke and bled and healed and broke again — but he could touch it, and touching it now felt like touching a snake you know is asleep but might not stay that way.

"The crown is old. Older than the Court. It was forged — no. Let me be precise, since precision may be all I have left to offer you. It was *completed* by Vessia. The original smiths made twelve bindings, twelve seals, one for each of the twelve constellations that watch over the Ninefold Court. Twelve was meant to be enough. Twelve was always enough. But Vessia found the smiths' notes, the forbidden arithmetic, the thirteenth binding — the one that doesn't bind a king to his crown, but binds the crown to *forever*. Eternal. Unremovable. Undying. A king who cannot be deposed, cannot die, cannot be *outlasted*."

"And each seal needs a constellation," Wren said softly. He had gone pale. Astronomers, Aldric thought, probably paled beautifully; it was practically the uniform. "Each new moon, the crown devours one. We counted eleven remaining."

"Twelve turns to finish the rite," Aldric confirmed. "And when the thirteenth seal closes, I will stop being a man who wears a crown and become a crown that occasionally remembers wearing a man. Vessia will pour what's left of me into the metal and rule forever through a thing that used to have my face." He smiled, and knew it must be a terrible smile. "She'll be very good at it. She's had practice."

"Why not just kill you?" Dain spoke for the first time — the soldier, the one whose hand kept arguing with his sword. His voice was rough, guilt-ridden. "If the crown needs you alive—"

"Because the crown *is* me, Captain. Now. We've grown together, like the tree and the garden. Kill me and the crown falls dormant for a generation until some poor child touches it, and Vessia starts over. No — she needs me alive until the last seal closes, and then she needs me *empty*." He looked at Sable, and made himself say the rest plainly, because she had crossed a kingdom to hear it and deserved plainness. "Which brings us to the only door out of this room."

Sable's chin lifted. "Tell me."

"The twelve bindings were forged in fear — fear of death, fear of deposition, fear of the mob. Fear makes excellent locks. But the thirteenth binding, the eternal one, has a flaw the smiths built in deliberately, because they were wiser than Vessia and crueler to themselves. An eternity bought with stolen things can be *refunded*. One willing sacrifice — freely given, treasured beyond price, surrendered without coercion — cracks the first seal. And a cracked seal, in a rite of thirteen, is a broken rite. The whole architecture fails. Thirteen is a number that will not abide damage."

"A sacrifice of what?" Sable asked. Her voice was steady. Her hands were not.

"A memory," Aldric said gently. "Not a stolen one. Not one taken from you. One you *kept*. The crown eats what we refuse to give; it can only be wounded by what we choose to lose. That is the whole secret of it, and Vessia has spent a century making sure no one in her Court ever chooses to lose anything." He smiled ruefully. "We are, all of us in Virelay, hoarders now. Wonder turned to currency, memory turned to coin. Nobody gives anything away. It's her true fortress, you know. Not the guards. The *economy of holding on*."

The garden was quiet. Even the impossible fountain had stilled, as if listening.

Sable looked down at the locket in her hands, and Aldric saw — because he had spent a hundred years learning to read faces, having lost nearly everything else worth reading — the exact moment she thought of something she loved, and the exact moment she understood what he was asking, and the exact moment she decided not to say it aloud.

"It doesn't have to be tonight," he said quickly. "It doesn't have to be announced. It only has to be *willing*, and it only has to be yours." He leaned forward, and the crown pulsed hot against his brow, angry, sensing the shape of the conversation. "Miss Pemberton — Sable. Whatever it costs you, I want you to know what it buys. Not a throne. I'm done with thrones; I intend to abdicate so fast it will make historians dizzy. What it buys is a sky. Every constellation the crown has swallowed, returned. Every star Vessia harvested, given back. And one tired gardener, allowed at last to die on schedule like everybody else." He spread his ruined, translucent hands. "That's the entire offer. I apologize for its modesty. I used to have kingdoms to give away. Someone ate them."

For the first time, Sable Pemberton laughed — a short, wet, startled sound, the laugh of a person who has been carrying grief like a load of wet wool and has just discovered it can be set down, briefly, to rest her arms.

"You're not at all what the stories said," she told him.

"No one ever is," Aldric said. "That's rather the point of stories. They're memories with the inconvenient parts trimmed off. Speaking of which—" he nodded at the locket, warm in her palm, "—keep that close. As long as it's near me, I can think. And I suspect you'll want me thinking, because—"

The garden gate rang.

Not knocked. Not opened. *Rang* — a clear, cold chime, silver and absolute, the sound of authority announcing itself, and every rose in the garden turned toward it as one, and the plum tree shuddered, and the crown on Aldric's brow went from hot to *triumphant*.

From beyond the hedge, sweet as poisoned wine, came a voice he had known for a hundred years:

"Your Majesty," called Regent Vessia, radiant and wrong-eyed, her footsteps unhurried on the gravel path. "And guests. How *lovely*. Do come out — all of you. I've brought such generous terms, and I should hate for the gardener to see what happens to trespassers who make me repeat myself."
