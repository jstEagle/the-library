## 12. Vessia's Generosity

The invitation arrived at dawn, which was the first insult. Nobody in Virelay did anything honest before noon, and nobody summoned a memory-thief to anything but a cell.

It came on cream paper, sealed with wax the color of dried blood, and it was delivered by a page boy so clean and solemn he looked like he'd been ironed. He stood in the doorway of Wren's garret — all three of them crammed into it now, Dain snoring on the floor pallet like a landslide waiting to happen — and held out the envelope as though it might bite him.

"Her Radiance the Regent requests the pleasure of Lady Pemberton's company," he recited.

"I'm not a lady," Sable said.

"Her Radiance requests the pleasure of *Lady* Pemberton's company," the boy repeated, with the weary precision of someone who had been told to say exactly that or lose his supper.

Wren took the letter from her hands before she could, unfolded it, and read it twice. His eyebrows climbed in that way they did when the arithmetic was bad. "She knows where we're staying."

"She's known where we're staying since the night we arrived," Sable said. "She let us walk in the front door of her city. You don't leave the front door open unless you want something to come inside."

Dain sat up, hair sticking out sideways like a startled hedge. "What does she want?"

"To talk." Wren handed the letter over. "Alone. No guards. She promises safe passage both ways, on her honor."

The three of them looked at one another for a long moment.

"On whose honor?" Dain asked finally. "Because I've taken an oath to that crown, and I can tell you what its honor is worth."

"It's a trap," Wren said.

"Of course it's a trap," Sable said. "But it's a trap with tea, probably. And I've been wondering what she looks like up close." She turned the letter over. At the bottom, beneath the Regent's flowing signature, someone had added a smaller line in different ink: *Bring the locket if you like. Or don't. I am not particular about how conversations begin.*

"She's not particular," Sable repeated softly. "That's worse than threats. Threats mean you've frightened someone."

* * *

Vessia received her in the Lantern Hall at midday, though the Hall had no windows and needed none. The ceiling was a lattice of captured starlight — thousands of glass globes strung on silver wire, each one holding a pinprick of stolen sky — and they burned with a light that had no warmth in it at all. It fell on everything evenly, mercilessly, the way interrogation rooms are lit.

Sable counted the globes out of habit. Four hundred and six visible from the floor. Each one a star somebody had loved once, naming it, navigating by it, wishing on it. Now it hung here, doing decorative work.

"You count things," said a voice like honey poured over a blade. "I'm told that's common among your profession. A way of keeping yourself outside the memory while you're inside it."

Regent Vessia stood at the far end of the hall beside a table set for two, and Sable understood immediately why the poets had run out of words. She was beautiful the way a harvest moon is beautiful: complete, luminous, and utterly indifferent to what grew beneath her. Her left eye was wrong — not blind, nothing so simple. It moved a half-second behind the right, like a reflection that hadn't agreed to be part of her.

"Your Radiance." Sable bowed the precise amount owed to a regent and not a hair more. "You wanted a thief. I hope I haven't disappointed."

"On the contrary." Vessia gestured to the table. Two chairs. A pot of tea breathing steam. Small cakes arranged in a fan, each one dusted with sugar fine as frost. "Sit. Eat. The cakes aren't poisoned; I had the taster try four."

"That's very generous."

"I'm told I'm a generous woman." Vessia poured the tea herself, which was either intimacy or theater, and Sable suspected the Regent had never once in her life confused the two. "Sugar?"

"No, thank you."

"You take it bitter. Of course you do. Lys did too."

The name landed in Sable's chest like a dropped coin. She kept her hands still around the cup. That was the first rule of the craft: when someone throws a memory at you uninvited, you do not reach for it where they can see.

"You knew my mother."

"I knew *of* her." Vessia settled into her own chair and folded her hands, and the wrong eye drifted, settled, drifted again. "A court memory-keeper, once. Before she ran. Before she hid herself in your little valley full of wards and lies and very good cheese, I'm told. Did you know her? Truly?"

"I knew a woman who made bread and lied about where she'd learned to read."

"Mm." Vessia smiled, and it was almost kind. "Then you know exactly as much about her as I do. Isn't that funny? We're both orphans of the same silence." She sipped her tea. "But I didn't bring you here to trade griefs. I brought you here because I'm going to make you rich."

"There it is," Sable said. "I was starting to worry."

"Four thousand crowns." Vessia said it the way other people said *good morning*. "Pardon for every crime committed in my city, named or unnamed. A house in the Lantern District — a real one, with windows. And passage anywhere in the nine provinces, first class, forever, under any name you choose. In exchange: the locket. The fragments of Aldric's childhood. Everything your mother smuggled out between her teeth."

"And if I say no?"

"Then we have a less pleasant conversation later, and I'll have wasted these cakes." Vessia nudged the plate an inch toward her. "Truly, they're excellent. Almond."

Sable ate one. It seemed important, somehow, to be the sort of person who ate the cake. It was excellent. She hated it.

* * *

"Now," Vessia said, setting down her cup, "before you answer, I owe you honesty. It's rare currency in this palace; I like to spend it where it will cause the most confusion." She rose, walked to the center of the hall, and lifted one hand toward the lattice of star-globes overhead. "May I show you something? It won't hurt. That's not a promise people make me often, so I make it carefully."

Sable should have said no. Every instinct she owned, every rule her mother had ever taught her, said *never let a stranger into your head, never mind a stranger with half a second's delay in her eyes.* But there was another voice too, quieter, older — the voice that had spent twenty years wondering what her mother had actually seen, what she'd been running from, why she'd taught her daughter to steal memories and then refused to share her own.

And Sable had always been weak in exactly that one place.

"Show me," she said.

The hall went dark. Not dim — *dark*, the globes guttering out one by one until only a single point of light remained above them, small and white and trembling. A star. A real one, or the memory of one.

And then the vision opened like a wound.

Sable saw a kingdom with its king free. She saw it in pieces, fast and vivid, the way nightmares are stitched: Aldric crowned again and whole, standing on the palace balcony, and below him the city — the city *changing*. She saw the Lantern District's lamps go dark as the stars were given back, and the poor who sold their memories to eat suddenly having nothing left to sell that anyone wanted, because the hunger for wonder had gone home to the sky. She saw the guilds turn on each other for the scraps. She saw provinces that had never known a living king remember old grudges with old borders, and armies moving like ants when the stone is lifted. She saw fires. She saw a mob — faces she almost recognized, faces from her own trade — dragging tax collectors through the street. She saw Hollowmere, her valley, its wards gone, its people looking up at a sky full of returning stars and understanding, all at once, that they had lived their whole lives inside a lie, and watching some of them break under the weight of it.

She saw a child crying because the storybook sky had come back and taken away the only world she'd ever known.

"This is what freedom costs," Vessia said quietly, somewhere close. Her voice came from everywhere, the way voices do in memories. "Not for kings. Kings are fine. Kings always land on their feet; it's the first thing crowns teach them. This is what it costs for everyone else. The baker. The ferryman. The girl who sells her sixth birthday so her brother can eat. You've met them. You've *been* them. I keep the stars caged because caged stars light lamps, and lamps feed cities, and cities full of fed people do not burn."

The vision shifted. Now Sable saw the alternative: the crown completed, thirteenth binding sealed, the king eternal — and eternal meant *fixed*, meant frozen, meant a kingdom under a king who could never change, never err, never die, and therefore never be argued with. She saw centuries of it, smooth and orderly and airless, like a garden pressed under glass.

"And this is peace," Vessia said. "I won't pretend it's lovely. Peace rarely is. But nobody burns in it. Nobody's brother starves. The boy in the crown sleeps, and dreams nothing, and hurts no one, and the world goes on being ordinary, which is the kindest thing a world can be."

The darkness held. Somewhere in it, Sable became aware that her face was wet.

"The locket," Vessia said gently. "Your mother died carrying contraband. A child's memories. A garden. A coronation that felt like a funeral — yes, I know what's in it; I helped decide what would need smuggling. She gave her life for a boy who is, by now, mostly crown. And you would spend yours — Dain Ferros's, Wren Calloway's, Mag's, if you count the dead, and thieves always count the dead — to finish what she started. For what? So a hollow man can be a king again? So the sky can go back to being beautiful and useless?"

The light returned slowly, globe by globe, until the hall was bright and ordinary and smelled of almond cakes.

Sable wiped her face with the heel of her hand. She was distantly amazed to find she wasn't trembling.

"You're good," she said. "You're really good. Better than me, and I say that professionally."

"Thank you."

"But here's what you got wrong." Sable stood. Her legs felt strange, like they belonged to someone braver. "You showed me a burning city and called it the cost of freedom. But I've lived in the valley you built, Your Radiance. I've seen what your peace costs. It costs a ferryman who feeds the wards once a year and pretends he doesn't know what he's feeding. It costs a hundred years of people not asking questions because asking questions gets the wards hungry. You didn't show me chaos. You showed me *change*, and you dressed it in fire because fire frightens people faster than truth does."

Vessia's smile didn't move, but something behind it did. The wrong eye stopped drifting and fixed on Sable, steady as a needle finding north.

"Careful," the Regent said pleasantly.

"Oh, I'm always careful. It's my entire personality." Sable picked up her cloak. "Here's my counteroffer. Keep your four thousand crowns. Keep your pardon — I'll earn my crimes honestly, the way I always have. And keep the king asleep a little longer, if you like. Because I've seen his memories, Your Radiance, the real ones, the ones your lattice can't fake. And I'll tell you what I found there, for free, since you were so generous with your honesty today."

She paused at the door.

"He remembers being kind. And kindness, Your Radiance, is the one thing in this palace you can't buy back once you've spent it."

* * *

The page boy walked her out through corridors that all bent slightly the wrong way, and said nothing, and looked at her once with an expression she couldn't quite read — pity, maybe, or envy, or the look of someone who has carried messages for the Regent a long time and remembers what happened to the last person who said no.

Outside, the afternoon was gray and the lamps were already lit, four hundred and six stars' worth of stolen light burning over streets full of people who had forgotten what they were looking at.

Wren and Dain were waiting in the garret, and they read her face before she got the door shut.

"She offered you everything," Wren said.

"She offered me everything," Sable agreed. She sat down heavily on the crate that served as a chair. "That's the problem. It was almost enough. For about ten minutes in there, I believed her. The visions — Wren, they weren't tricks. They were true. All of it could happen. Cities could burn. People I know could starve."

"Visions of the future are always true," Wren said grimly. "That's what makes them useful as weapons. They're true *somewhere*, in *some* version, and the listener supplies the rest." He rubbed his eyes. "Did she say anything about the moon? About timing?"

"No. She didn't need to. She wanted me scared, not informed." Sable looked down at her hands. "There was one other thing. When I left — the page boy. He looked at me like—" She stopped. Started again. "Like he knew something I didn't. Like he was sorry."

Dain went very still. "Describe him."

"Young. Clean. Red livery, silver collar—"

"Silver collar means household staff, inner circle." Dain was on his feet. "Sable, the Regent doesn't send inner-circle pages to deliver invitations. She sends them to deliver *messages after* the invitation. What did he say? Exactly?"

"Nothing. He just—" Sable frowned, reaching back through the morning, through the tea and the visions and the almond cake, to a small solemn face in a doorway. And then she heard it, the sentence she'd filed away without listening, the way you file away a servant's murmur, background noise, nothing:

*"Her Radiance hopes you enjoyed the cakes. There will be others."*

Others. Other cakes. Other tasters.

Other guests.

"Wren," Sable said slowly, "who else knows we're in the city? Who else did we trust with where we sleep?"

Wren's face went pale in the lamplight, and she watched him arrive at the same name she did — the name of the one person in Virelay who had been kind to them, helpful to them, *generous* to them, and had asked for nothing in return.

The lamp nearest the window flickered, as if something far above had just swallowed another star.
