## 13. The Price of a Face

The safehouse above the chandler's shop on Tallow Row had one window, and through it Sable could see the moon—fat and silver and counting down. Twelve nights until it turned new. Eleven, if you were pessimistic about travel time to the palace, which Wren was, professionally.

She sat at the scarred table with the locket-memory open in her palm, its little brass case warm as a held hand, and she watched her mother's face for the hundredth time that week.

Not watched. That was the wrong word. You watched a stranger. You watched weather. What Sable did was something older and less dignified: she looked at Lys Pemberton's face the way a starving woman looks at bread she has decided not to eat yet.

*There,* said the memory, in her mother's voice, in her mother's garden, in the last spring before the wards took everything. *There, little thief. If you're going to steal from people, at least be kind about it. Take only what they can spare.*

Her mother's face turned toward her across twenty years and a dead valley, and it smiled, and Sable's chest did the thing it always did—the folding, the quiet collapse, like a house settling after everyone has gone to bed.

"You're going to have to give it up," Wren said from the doorway. He had a mug of chicory in each hand and the tact of a man who had been exiled for telling the truth and had learned nothing whatsoever from the experience.

"I know."

"No, I mean I've done the arithmetic." He set a mug by her elbow. "The rite requires a memory freely surrendered, treasured beyond all others, given whole. Not a copy. The crown doesn't take copies—it takes the root. And your mother's face is the only memory you've ever kept. Twenty years in the trade, and you've kept exactly one thing."

"Thank you, Wren. I hadn't noticed."

"You're welcome." He sat down across from her, all elbows and earnestness, his star-charts spread out beside his cup like a man who brought his work everywhere the way other men brought dogs. "I'm saying it because you keep looking at the locket like it might negotiate. It won't. Memory isn't a contract. There's no clause where you give up ninety percent and keep visiting rights."

"I know how extraction works, Wren. I invented half of it."

"Yes, but you've never been on this end of the needle." He tapped the table. "You've taken hundreds of memories. Have you ever once felt what it's like to lose one?"

Sable opened her mouth to say something cutting and found, to her considerable annoyance, that her eyes were full.

Wren had the grace to pretend not to notice, which was the kindest lie an astronomer had ever told.

* * *

That afternoon she did what she always did when a job frightened her: she rehearsed it.

Memory-work was mostly rehearsal. You walked the client through the leaving of a thing before you ever touched the needle, because grief that arrives unannounced breaks people, and grief that is expected merely bends them. She had talked a dying matriarch through surrendering her wedding day. She had talked a soldier through giving up the face of the man he'd killed. She was very good at this. People said so. People paid her handsomely to be good at this.

So she sat on the narrow bed in the safehouse, locket in her lap, and began the litany she used with clients.

*Name the memory.* My mother's face. Her name was Lys. She smelled of rosemary and lamp-oil. She had a laugh that started in her shoulders.

*Describe what you'll lose.* Everything. The way her left eyebrow went up higher than the right when she caught me lying. The crease between her brows that deepened every year of my childhood like a road being worn into a hillside. The exact gray of her eyes—not blue, not green, the color of the Hollowmere river in March, which is to say the color of patience.

*Say goodbye to it.*

And there the litany stopped, the way a cart stops when the axle snaps.

Sable tried again. *Goodbye, Mama.*

Nothing came out. Her mouth made the shapes and her throat refused them passage, and after a while she stopped making the shapes and just sat there, breathing, holding a brass locket like a talisman against a god.

Dain found her like that an hour later. He'd come up the stairs with the particular heavy tread of a man trying to announce himself, which was considerate of him, and he stood in the doorway filling most of it, arms crossed, jaw doing the thing where it worked sideways before he spoke.

"You haven't eaten," he said.

"I'm working."

"That's not work. That's sitting."

"It's rehearsal. It's the hardest part of any extraction, and I'm doing it on myself, which nobody has ever done, because everybody knows better." She heard her own voice go sharp and reined it in. "I'm sorry. Yes. I haven't eaten."

He went down and came back with bread and hard cheese and a jar of pickled onions he'd charmed off the chandler below, and they ate at the table without talking much, and it was almost bearable, the evening, right up until Dain said:

"You could not do it."

Sable looked up.

"The rite needs a willing sacrifice. Willing means willing. Nobody can make you." He said it plainly, the way he said everything, a man built out of plain statements. "We find another way. There's always another way. I spent ten years believing there wasn't, and then I met you lot, and now I believe in other ways."

"There isn't another way, Dain. Aldric was clear. The first seal cracks only for a treasured memory, freely given. Vessia designed it that way on purpose. She wanted the price to be unbearable, because she assumed no one would ever pay it." Sable pushed an onion around her plate. "She assumed correctly, so far."

"So far," Dain repeated, and there was something in how he said it—a soldier's stubbornness, the belief that battles turn on the last hour—that Sable held onto later, in the dark, like a rail in a corridor.

* * *

She tried the rehearsal again at midnight, and this time she got further.

She got as far as lifting the needle.

Wren had adapted one of her own extraction rigs—he was insufferably pleased about it—for self-administration. A mirror arrangement, a stabilizing clamp, a hair-fine filament of glass. "You'll feel a coldness behind the eyes," he'd said, "and then a sort of opening, like a door in a house you didn't know you had."

Sable sat before the little mirror with the filament poised at her temple, and in the mirror she could see herself, and behind her reflected self, faintly, the window, and beyond the window the moon, white and patient as a creditor.

*Freely given,* she reminded herself. *That's the whole trick of it. You can't flinch. Flinching spoils the gift.*

She thought about Aldric's face when he'd played the locket. How a man who had been eaten down to scaffolding had stood up straight for eleven minutes. How he'd said *my mother planted that rose* about a bush that had died two centuries ago, and meant it, and wept, and how his weeping had sounded like a man being handed back a piece of himself, one stitch at a time.

She thought about the stars. All those small fires going out over Hollowmere, one constellation at a time, and how the valley children wouldn't know the names soon—the Shepherd, the Sisters, the Long Chariot—and how Wren had recited them once, all of them, like a man reading a casualty list.

She thought about duty. Duty was a word Dain used comfortably, a load-bearing word, and she had borrowed it these past weeks the way she borrowed everything, on approval.

And then she thought about her mother's face, actually thought about it, deliberately, the way you press a bruise to check whether it still hurts.

It hurt.

It hurt more than the stars. More than the king. More than the whole shining doomed machinery of crowns and bindings and lunar arithmetic. Because the stars belonged to everyone, and the king belonged to history, and duty belonged to whoever claimed it loudest—but her mother's face belonged to her. Only her. It was the single object in all the world of which Sable Pemberton was the sole owner, and she had guarded it for twenty years through every famine and every temptation, through Vessia's gold and the Guild's debts and the long gray years when keeping it was the only proof that she had ever been loved by anyone at all.

She lowered the filament.

She put it down on the table with exaggerated care, the way you put down a knife after deciding not to use it, and she looked at her reflection in the mirror, and her reflection looked back with an expression she recognized from clients on the worst night of their lives: the face of someone who has just discovered the exact dimensions of their own cowardice.

"Oh," she said quietly, to herself. "Oh, you can't do it."

The reflection didn't argue.

* * *

In the morning she was foul company, and she knew it, and she did it anyway.

"You didn't sleep," Wren observed, over the chicory.

"I slept fine."

"You have the specific pallor of someone who lay awake composing arguments." He squinted at her. "Also there's a filament on the floor by the bed, unbroken, which suggests deployment was aborted. Also you're wearing yesterday's shirt inside out."

"Wren."

"Yes?"

"Stop being observant for five minutes. Just five. Consider it a professional courtesy between liars."

He considered this. "Four minutes," he said, and drank his chicory, and gave her four minutes of genuine silence, which from Wren Calloway was practically a sacrament.

When the four minutes were up, he set down his cup and said, gently, in the voice he probably used on students before the disgrace: "Can I tell you something true? About astronomy?"

"If you must."

"Stars don't burn because it's easy. They burn because they're collapsing. Every star is matter falling into itself forever and refusing to finish the fall. The light is what refusal looks like." He turned his cup slowly on the table. "I used to think that was just physics. Now I think it might be the only honest description of courage I've ever heard. It's not the absence of wanting to stop. It's the light you make while refusing to."

"That's very pretty, Wren."

"It's also a roundabout way of saying I saw the filament on the floor, and I don't think less of you, and neither will Aldric, and neither will anyone who matters. Refusal to finish the fall is allowed more than once. Stars do it constantly. It's rather their defining feature."

Sable stared at him. "Did you practice that speech?"

"Every night since the twelfth turn. In front of a mirror. I got it down to under a minute." He shrugged, abashed and unashamed at once. "I'm not brave the way Dain's brave. I prepare."

* * *

She went down to the chandler's shop that afternoon, ostensibly to buy candles, actually because the old woman who ran it, Mistress Odile, had a face like a walnut and a manner like a warm stove, and sometimes Sable needed to stand near warmth that asked nothing of her.

Odile wrapped six tallow candles in brown paper and said, apropos of nothing, "My husband's been dead thirty-one years."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. But I'll tell you what I learned." She tied the string with a click of her knuckles. "For the first year, I kept his coat on the hook by the door. Couldn't move it. Felt like moving it would be moving him. Then one winter we were short of coal, and I lined the cat's basket with it, and you know what happened?" She looked up, bright-eyed and merciless. "Nothing happened. He stayed dead. The cat stayed warm. And I found I loved him just as much with the coat in the cat's basket as on the hook. More, maybe. It was doing some good in the world, finally, instead of hanging there being grieved at."

"Mistress Odile," Sable said, "are you trying to tell me something?"

"I'm an old woman on Tallow Row. I don't tell people things. I wrap candles." She pushed the parcel across. "But if I were telling someone something, I'd say: love isn't the keeping. Love is what the keeping was *for*. Any fool can hoard a treasure. The question is whether you ever let it buy anything worth having."

Sable paid her, and took the candles, and stood outside the shop in the gray light with the parcel under her arm, feeling as though she'd been pickpocketed by a grandmother.

Because here was the terrible arithmetic of it, laid out plain at last:

If she kept the memory, Aldric died by inches under the crown, and the sky went dark forever, and every child born after would never learn the names of the constellations, and Vessia's eternal court would grind on like a millstone with nothing left to grind. And her mother's face would sit in her head like the coat on the hook—grieved at, useless, beloved, and alone.

And if she gave it up—

If she gave it up, she would walk out of the palace knowing that somewhere in the world there had been a woman with a crooked eyebrow and river-colored eyes who had loved her, and she would never see her again, not even in the dark behind her eyelids. She would know it the way you know a country you've read about. Secondhand. On faith.

Twenty years of thieving, and the crown had found the one theft she couldn't survive committing against herself.

* * *

Evening came down over Virelay, and the harvested-star lamps flickered on along Tallow Row, pale and secondhand-bright, and Sable climbed back up to the safehouse and found Dain cleaning his boots by the window and Wren hunched over his charts muttering figures, and the room was small and lamplit and full of people she had somehow, without noticing, stopped thinking of as assets.

They looked up when she came in.

"I couldn't do it," she said. No preamble. She was done with preambles; they cost extra. "Last night. I had the needle at my temple and I put it down. I tried three times today to rehearse the goodbye and I couldn't get past naming her eyebrow." She sat down heavily. "So either one of you finds me another way in the next nine days, or you find someone else to carry the locket, because I am not reliable. I'm the weakest link in this whole ridiculous chain, and I need you both to know it."

Silence. Then Dain said, "Noted," in the tone of a man logging a report, and added, "You're also the only one of us who can perform the rite, so we'll manage."

"And I," said Wren, "have been running figures since dawn, and I want to show you something, and you're going to hate it."

"Hate what?"

He turned his chart around. On it, in his cramped astronomer's hand, was a diagram of the crown—or rather, of what the crown contained. Thirteen seals. Twelve kings' worth of hoarded memory, centuries deep. And at the center, marked in red ink, the thirteenth binding, incomplete.

"Vessia built the crown to hold memories," he said. "Every king's whole life, pressed flat and stored. Which means—" he tapped the diagram, tap tap tap, a man unable to leave a point alone— "the crown already contains faces. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Kings' mothers, kings' lovers, kings' children. Including—" and here he looked up, and his eyes behind the smudged lenses were careful and kind— "including, quite possibly, records of the Court from the era when your mother lived. Before the exile. If Lys Pemberton ever traveled to Virelay, if she was ever seen by any scrying-glass or court record or bound memory in the royal archive—"

"Then her face might exist somewhere other than my head," Sable finished. Her voice came out strange. Flat. The voice of a woman standing at the edge of a high place, testing the air.

"A copy," Wren said. "A shadow of it. Not the same. Never the same. But—" he spread his hands— "you wouldn't be walking into the dark empty-handed. You'd be trading the original for a sketch. And some people can live on sketches. Poets do it constantly."

Dain was frowning. "You're telling her to give it up."

"I'm telling her the dark has furniture in it," Wren said. "That's all. I'm an astronomer. My entire profession is telling people the dark has furniture."

Sable looked at the chart. At the thirteen circles. At the red mark at the center like a wound drawn in ink.

Nine days.

Outside, the moon was rising over the rooftops of Virelay, one night fatter than it should have been, one night closer to new, and somewhere beneath the palace a hollow-eyed man was waiting to find out whether anyone loved anything enough to pay for him.

Sable picked up the locket. It was warm. It was always warm. She closed her hand around it and felt her pulse knock against the brass, once, twice, patient as tide.

"All right," she said. "Rehearsal resumes tonight. Both of you stay. If I put the needle down again, I want witnesses, so I can't pretend it didn't happen."

Dain pulled his chair closer to the table. Wren trimmed the lamp. And Sable Pemberton, the best memory-thief in two valleys, sat down to practice the only theft that had ever frightened her—

and did not hear the footsteps on the stairs below, soft and deliberate, climbing.
