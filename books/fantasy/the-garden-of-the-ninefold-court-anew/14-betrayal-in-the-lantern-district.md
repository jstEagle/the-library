## 14. Betrayal in the Lantern District

Dain Ferros had spent eleven years learning to read a room by its silences, and the silence coming up the stairs of Sable's safehouse was wrong in three distinct ways.

First: it was too even. A drunk climbing stairs staggers. A patrol climbs in rhythm. This was neither—this was someone ascending with the particular care of a person who knew exactly which step creaked and had made peace with it years ago.

Second: there were two sets of feet, not one, and they moved in counterpoint, which meant training.

Third—and this was the one that turned his stomach to lead—the footsteps did not hesitate at the third-floor landing where any honest visitor would have knocked. They simply continued, unhurried, toward the door of the only room in the Lantern District that mattered.

"Company," Dain said quietly.

Sable looked up from the locket, needle poised above her own temple like a woman deciding whether to prick her finger on a spinning wheel. Wren's head came around with the quick birdlike motion Dain had privately named *the astronomer's twitch*.

"How many?" Sable asked.

"Two. Trained. They know the building." He was already moving, snuffing the lamp's brighter wick, leaving only the low blue flame he'd insisted on since their first week here. "They know it *well*."

The color left Sable's face in a way Dain had learned to hate watching, because it happened rarely and always for good reason. "Who else knows this address?"

Three names hung in the air between them, unspoken. The network of debts and favors Sable had spent weeks weaving through Virelay's underworld had exactly three threads that reached this high: a fence named Odo Quill who moved stolen memories in barrels of pickled eels; a night-soil man called Tansy who heard everything in the city because no one ever stopped talking over a full bucket; and—

"Oh," said Wren softly. "Oh, no."

"You don't know it's him," Sable said.

"I don't know it isn't," Wren said. "That's rather my point."

Dain didn't ask. There wasn't time, and besides, he could do the arithmetic himself. Three contacts. Two of them owed Sable money. One of them *was* owed money, a great deal of it, by a man who had recently discovered that loyalty purchased at discount does not hold its value in a market panic.

The footsteps reached the landing.

* * *

Later—in the cell, in the dark, in the long hours when his hands would not stop shaking and he let them shake because fighting it took strength he needed elsewhere—Dain would replay those ninety seconds the way he replayed every failure of his career, frame by frame, looking for the hinge.

Here is what the frames showed:

Sable went out the window first, locket clenched in her teeth like a pirate's knife, because she was Sable and dignity was a currency she'd never once been tempted to hoard. Wren followed, clutching his satchel of star-charts to his chest with both arms, whispering "careful, careful, careful" to the papers as though they were infants.

And Dain stayed at the top of the stairs, because someone had to make the retreat look like an accident instead of an escape, and because eleven years of guarding other people had taught him exactly one trick worth passing on: the best shield is the one nobody notices until it's already taken the blow.

The door opened on a man Dain recognized from a hundred galas and a hundred reports and had never once suspected. Master Corvin Hale, memory-broker, collector of rare recollections, the gentle-voiced gentleman whose shop in the Amber Row sold nostalgia to the rich the way other shops sold perfume. The man who had taught Sable half her craft, back before either of them had reputations. The man whose debts Sable had quietly forgiven, twice, because you don't call in what your teacher owes you—you just carry it.

Behind him stood two of the Regent's quiet men, the ones who wore gray instead of black because black announced itself.

"Dain Ferros," Corvin said, and his voice was exactly as warm as it had been at every gala, and that warmth was the worst thing in the room. "Captain. Or should I say former Captain? I confess I've lost track of your titles lately."

"Corvin." Dain kept his voice level. "Walk away. Whatever she's paying you, I'll—"

"She isn't paying me anything, dear boy. That's the tragedy of it." Corvin stepped inside, brushing dust from his sleeve with two fingers, examining the room with the connoisseur's eye he brought to everything. "She forgave my debts. Did you know that? Twice. And I sat here in my shop thinking: what a remarkable young woman. What a waste. Because I could see the shape of what was coming—I read the Court's ledgers the way your friend reads his stars—and I thought, better she be found now, gently, than later, badly."

"So you sold her."

"I *appraised* her," Corvin said, with genuine hurt. "There's a difference. Everything has a price, Captain. Even mercy. Especially mercy—it's the most expensive thing there is, because nobody ever budgets for it." He sighed, and for one moment the mask slipped and Dain saw underneath it something tired and old and almost sorry. "The Regent offered me my shop, my licenses, and my memories of this entire month, wiped clean. Do you understand? I won't even have to live with it. That's what power looks like these days. It doesn't threaten you. It *spares* you."

From the alley below came the sound Dain had been dreading: a whistle, sharp and doubled. The quiet men on the roof.

Corvin glanced toward the window with professional regret. "Ah. Well. I did tell them the window, but they never listen." He looked back at Dain, and now the sorrow was fully visible, unhidden, which somehow made it worse. "You could still walk away, you know. Stand aside. Tell them the thief fled alone. You're a guardsman; lying is practically liturgy."

Dain thought about his oath. Not the words of it—he'd forgotten the exact words years ago, the way everyone forgets the exact words of things said too often—but the weight of it, the shape it had pressed into him across eleven years of standing in doorways while important people lied behind him.

He'd broken that oath already, of course. The night he'd caught Sable at the gala and let her go, he'd broken it so thoroughly that there was nothing left to protect by standing aside now except himself.

"No," Dain said.

Corvin closed his eyes briefly, like a man wincing at a false note. "I was afraid you'd say that. You always were the honorable one. It's why I liked you." He stepped back into the hallway, and the gray men came forward, and Dain Ferros did the last thing he would ever do as anyone's captain:

He held the stairwell.

* * *

He got Wren thirty seconds. Maybe forty.

He knew this because he counted them—not consciously, but the way a drowning man counts waves—each exchange of blows marked against the sounds from the alley below: Sable's boots on cobblestone, Wren's breathless cursing, the fading percussion of flight. Thirty seconds of parry and grapple on a narrow stairwell, using the terrain the way he'd drilled a hundred recruits to use it: high ground, tight quarters, darkness. The gray men were good. They were also working in a space built for one broad-shouldered fool with nothing left to lose, and Dain made that space cost them.

A chair broke over somebody's shoulder. He honestly couldn't have said whose.

Then a boot caught the back of his knee—the low sweep, textbook, he'd have complimented the technique under other circumstances—and the stairwell tilted, and the world became elbows and banister and the taste of blood, and then the floor, and then hands on his arms, practiced and impersonal, folding him into irons with the brisk efficiency of men wrapping a parcel.

Through the window, as they hauled him upright, he saw the alley: empty. Sable and Wren were gone, swallowed into the warren of the Lantern District where even the Regent's quiet men moved slowly and hated every step.

Good, he thought, and then the thought turned over and showed its underside: *gone where?* Without his knowledge of the palace passages—without the map that lived in his head because he'd never trusted paper—what did they have? A thief, an astronomer, and eleven days until the twelfth new moon.

Eleven days. He'd done the arithmetic himself, sitting at that table while Wren chalked lunar phases on the wall like a schoolmaster possessed. Each new moon, the crown devoured a constellation. Eleven remained. After the twelfth turn, the thirteenth binding would close, and Aldric—or the thing wearing him—would be beyond reach forever, eternal and empty, a king-shaped hole in the world wearing a crown of harvested light.

Eleven days. And now the plan lay in pieces on a stairwell in the Lantern District, and the pieces were being inventoried by the enemy.

Because that was the true cost, Dain realized, as they marched him down into the street. Not the bruises. Not the cell that was certainly waiting. It was this: Corvin hadn't just sold their address. Somewhere in the gray men's report, written in tidy bureaucratic script, would be everything the room contained. The lamp trimmed low. The needle. The locket, warm brass, handled nightly. And Wren's satchel—no. Wren still had the satchel. But Corvin had seen the table, the chalk marks on the wall, the lunar arithmetic. He'd appraised it all, as he'd said. He'd priced it.

The Regent Vessia would know, by morning, exactly how many nights she had left to worry.

* * *

The cell they gave him was better than he expected, which frightened him more than a bad one would have.

It was in the palace itself—not the dungeons below, with their damp and their rats and their honest misery, but a comfortable room three floors up, with a real bed, a real window (barred, but a window), and a tray of food that included fresh bread and a small dish of honeyed pears. His favorite. Someone had checked.

"They're being kind," said the turnkey, a round-faced young guard Dain vaguely remembered training, apologetic as a man delivering bad weather. "Regent's orders. You're to want for nothing."

"That's worse," Dain said.

"I know," the young guard said, with feeling, and locked the door as gently as if it might bruise.

Dain ate the pears anyway. Pride was a luxury; he'd need his strength for whatever came next, and refusing honeyed pears out of spite was the sort of gesture that felt noble for ten minutes and stupid for ten years. Then he sat on the bed and began, methodically, to take stock.

What had they lost? Him—though whether he was a loss or merely a liability depended on how well he could hold his tongue, and he intended to find out. Time—two days, perhaps three, between the raid and whatever Vessia did with the knowledge Corvin had sold her. The element of surprise, entirely. The safehouse, certainly; Sable would have burned it on principle within the hour.

What remained? Sable, who was the best memory-thief in two valleys and had survived worse nights than this one. Wren, who was a liar and a coward and the finest mathematician Dain had ever met, and who had once told him, drunk on two cups of plum wine, that cowardice was just courage that had learned to count. The locket. The garden beneath the palace—which only Dain knew how to reach, through passages that existed on no chart, which meant that somewhere in her radiant skull, Vessia had a problem she didn't yet know she had.

*They need me to get in,* he thought. *But they can't reach me to ask.*

And then, because despair is patient and finds you in the quiet hours, the other thought arrived, the one he'd been holding at arm's length since the stairwell:

*Or they don't need you at all. Or they find another way. Or Sable decides a captured guard is a loose end, and loose ends get cut.*

He lay back on the too-comfortable bed and stared at the ceiling, where the shadow of the bars made a lattice against the plaster, and he thought about Mag the ferryman dying in a burning valley so that strangers could run, his last words carried out on smoke: *the king remembers being kind.* Dain had never met the king. He had served the crown for eleven years without once seeing the head it sat upon, and some traitorous part of him had always assumed there was nothing under it—that the throne was a costume with no actor, a story told to keep the lamps lit.

But Mag had died believing otherwise. And Sable carried a boy named Aldric in a brass locket, warm as a heartbeat. And Wren had counted the stars down like a man counting coins he couldn't afford to spend.

Somewhere out there in the dark city, the twelfth new moon was approaching, patient as tide, and the sky above Virelay was emptier every night. Dain could see a sliver of it through his barred window—a patch of black where, in his childhood, the Hunter had strode with his belt of seven bright stars.

Now there was nothing. Just the hole where wonder used to be.

He was still staring at that hole when the lock turned.

The young turnkey again—but different now, wrong now, eyes fixed carefully on the middle distance, the studied blankness of a man repeating memorized words. Behind him, in the corridor, lamplight fell across the hem of a gray cloak.

"Message from the Regent," the boy recited, toneless. "She says: tell the Captain that the astronomer's charts are already in her hands. Recovered intact from a fence on Amber Row. She says—" and here the boy faltered, swallowing, clearly wishing to be anywhere else, "—she says to tell him the Thief of Hollowmere will come for her people eventually. They always do. And when she does, the Regent will be waiting, and the Captain may choose in that moment whose side his oath serves. She says there is no hurry. She says the moon is doing her counting for her."

The door closed. The footsteps faded.

Dain sat very still in the comfortable cell with the taste of honeyed pears going sour in his mouth, and did the only arithmetic left to him.

Charts seized. Contact turned. Guard captured. Eleven days.

And somewhere in the Lantern District, a woman with a warm brass locket clenched in her teeth was running blind through the dark, toward a palace she could no longer reach, carrying the only hope in the world—

and the Regent had just invited her to bring it closer.
