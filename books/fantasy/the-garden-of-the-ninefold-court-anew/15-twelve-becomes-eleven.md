## 15. Twelve Becomes Eleven

Dain Ferros had spent eleven years learning the exact weight of his armor—every plate, every strap, every buckle—and in the three days since they had locked him in a cell beneath the Lantern District garrison, he had learned something new: that shame weighed more than all of it.

They had taken the armor, of course. And the sword, and the boots with the good heels, and the little tin whistle he'd carried since his first posting because a girl in Thistle Row had given it to him and told him to blow it if he ever needed rescuing. He had never blown it. He suspected she'd been joking anyway. But they had left him his undershirt and his dignity, and then, over the course of three days, they had gone to work on the dignity too.

The cell was not cruel, precisely. That was the Regent's way. Cruelty made martyrs; comfort made collaborators. So there was a cot with a blanket that smelled faintly of lavender, and a window high on the wall through which he could see a sliver of sky, and meals delivered by a guard named Pell who was either genuinely kind or genuinely bored, and Dain had decided after two conversations that these were not mutually exclusive.

"Porridge," Pell announced this morning, sliding the bowl through the slot. "Again."

"You spoil me."

"I do. Cook wanted to give you the burnt batch but I told him you're technically still a captain until they hang you or worse, and captains get unburnt porridge. It's in some regulation somewhere. Probably."

"There's no such regulation," Dain said, taking the bowl.

"No," Pell agreed cheerfully. "But you looked like you needed to believe there was." He lingered at the slot, lowering his voice. "It's happening tonight. The twelfth turn. They're doing it from the Spire steps so everyone can watch. The whole city's been ordered to attend."

"Ordered to attend a funeral," Dain said, "and dress for a festival."

"That's about the size of it." Pell glanced down the corridor, then back. "Captain—Ferros—I shouldn't say this. But my sister lives in the Weft. She says people are already gathering in the streets. Not celebrating. Just... standing. Looking up."

"Looking up at what?"

Pell didn't answer. He didn't have to. Both of them knew what was up there now: a sky with holes in it. The Crown had eaten twelve constellations across twelve new moons, and each one had left a wound of pure black where stars used to be. The astronomers' charts—the ones Wren Calloway had spent his life drawing, before the Court burned him for drawing them honestly—hung in Dain's memory like a map of a country being erased.

Eleven turns remained. Or ten, depending on how you counted the one coming tonight.

He ate the porridge. It was, as promised, unburnt. Small mercies; the Court dealt in nothing larger anymore.

* * *

They brought him up to a viewing room rather than the steps themselves—a courtesy, or a cruelty, he wasn't sure which. A long chamber in the garrison's upper floor with tall windows facing the Spire, where the crowd had gathered in their tens of thousands, pressed shoulder to shoulder in the great square below, their lanterns lit despite the early hour because the sky itself had become unreliable and people clung to small flames the way drowning men cling to driftwood.

Dain stood between two guards who would not meet his eyes. He was in irons, in his undershirt, watching the execution of a piece of heaven.

Regent Vessia stood on the Spire's highest balcony, radiant as always. That was the word people used—radiant—and it was meant as praise, though Dain had learned in the past weeks what the radiance actually was: harvested starlight, worn like a gown, drawn from the same hoard the crown was feeding on. She wore white and silver, and her left eye caught the lamplight wrong, the way glass catches it, and when she raised her arms the crowd hushed so completely that Dain could hear the fountain in the square, splashing its indifferent water.

"Citizens of Virelay!" Her voice carried without effort, poured into every corner of the square by means Dain preferred not to think about. "Tonight we complete another turning of the Great Work! Tonight the crown takes unto itself the light of the Spear-Bearer, and our king is made safer, and our city is made brighter, and our future is made certain!"

A cheer went up—but Dain, who had stood in enough crowds to read them the way other men read letters, heard what was underneath it. The cheer came from the front rows, from the Court's people, from those paid in lamp-oil and privilege. From further back it thinned. And from the rooftops and the dark mouths of side streets, from the places where the poor of the Weft and the Tanneries had climbed to see, there came no cheer at all. Only silence, and the sound of many thousands of people not breathing.

Below the balcony, on a dais of black marble, sat the crown upon its cushion—or rather, the thing that held the crown: a construct of silver and glass, shaped like an open hand reaching upward, that the Court called the Cradle. Within it, the crown turned slowly of its own accord, and as it turned, Dain saw the light inside it shift and gather, the way a throat shifts before swallowing.

Above, in the patch of sky directly over the Spire, the stars of the Spear-Bearer began to move.

That was the part no decree had prepared anyone for. The stars did not simply go out. They *moved*—dragged across the black in slow, luminous streams, like embers pulled by a current, streaming down toward the crown in threads of silver fire. The whole constellation, forty-one stars by Wren's charts, peeling loose from heaven and pouring into a piece of jewelry while a woman in white smiled and called it progress.

Someone in the crowd screamed. Then someone else. It was not a scream of awe. Dain knew the difference. He had heard awe at coronations and victories, and this was the other thing—the sound people make when the world shows them it does not care about them.

The last star of the Spear-Bearer slid into the crown, and where the constellation had been there was now nothing: a hole in the night, smooth and absolute, darker than any darkness Dain had ever seen. It did not look empty. It looked *removed*. As if someone had cut the shape out of the sky with scissors and taken the piece away.

Vessia spread her arms. "Behold! Twelve constellations bound, and our beloved king preserved forever!"

And the crowd broke.

It did not break all at once. It broke the way ice breaks on a thawing river—one crack here, a groan there, and then all at once, catastrophically. A bottle arced out of the crowd and shattered against the Spire's lower wall. A voice—Dain never learned whose—began to shout, "Give them BACK," and others took it up, Give them back, give them back, give them back, until the words were not words anymore but a single animal roar rolling off the buildings. People surged against the barrier lines. A barrier line went down. Somewhere a lantern fell and something caught fire—not a building yet, just a barricade, just a symbol, but symbols burn hot in Virelay.

The guards beside Dain shifted. One of them said, quietly, to no one in particular, "Gods. Look at them."

"I am looking," Dain said.

"They're saying—" The young guard swallowed. "They're saying the king should be let go. That the crown's killing him. That's madness, isn't it? That's sedition talk."

"Is it?" Dain asked, and could not keep the bitterness out of his voice, and the young guard looked at him then—really looked, at the irons and the undershirt and the eleven years of service hanging off him like wet cloth—and said nothing more.

From the balcony, Vessia's smile did not waver. She lifted one hand, and the Court's own guard moved forward in perfect ranks, and the square became a grinding crush of bodies and torchlight and screaming. Dain watched it with his hands curled into fists inside the irons until his knuckles went white, and he thought: *I swore an oath to protect these people.* He thought: *I let her take me instead of stopping her, and I called it strategy.* He thought, most honestly and most painfully: *Wren Calloway predicted this night to the hour, and I helped arrest him for it.*

They took him back downstairs. The roaring followed him all the way, fading slowly, like a tide going out over stones.

* * *

She came at midnight, which was theatrical of her, and Dain found himself almost grateful for it. If Vessia was going to ruin your life, at least she did it with a sense of occasion.

The cell door opened without a key turning—he never did learn how her locks worked, and suspected the locks themselves were only a courtesy—and she entered alone, which was also theatrical, and also a message: *I am not afraid of you. You are nothing.*

"Captain Ferros." She looked around the cell with mild distaste, as though inspecting a stable. "You've lost weight. Prison doesn't suit you."

"Prison rarely does. That's generally considered its main feature."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile. Vessia did not smile; she permitted her face to suggest the possibility of one. "I've come with an offer. I'll be brief, because you're not worth a long conversation, and because there's a riot outside that requires my attention."

"How is it going?"

"Poorly," she said, with the honesty that always unsettled him most. "Which brings me to you. Tomorrow, the Council will convene an emergency session. There will be questions about loyalty. About oaths. About a certain captain who released a thief carrying contraband royal memories, and then compounded his treason by leading said thief beneath the palace itself." She tilted her head. "Your friends escaped, incidentally. All three. I thought you'd want to know they're alive. I find hope makes prisoners more reasonable."

"They're not my friends," Dain said. "They're my oath."

"Ah. Yes. The oath." She drifted closer, and the lamplight did its wrong-glass trick in her left eye. "Here is my offer, Captain, and it is generous, because I am feeling generous tonight. I can make you forget."

Dain went very still.

"Not kill you—death creates questions, and questions create sympathy, and sympathy creates riots, as we've seen. No. I offer something cleaner. I will lift from your mind the moment of your betrayal. The corridor beneath the palace. The thief in the dark. The fragment of the boy-king's memory that woke something in you that should have stayed asleep." She spoke softly now, almost tenderly, a surgeon describing an incision. "In its place, a faithful memory. You pursued the thief. You lost her. You have regretted it ever since. Your honor remains intact. Your rank is restored. Your pension, even. And tomorrow, when the Council asks, you will stand before them clean, and testify against the memory-thieves, and help me find them before the twelfth turn completes."

"The twelfth turn," Dain said carefully, "isn't completed?"

Her pause was very small, but he had spent years reading faces in throne rooms, and he caught it. "The rite proceeds," she said. "On schedule."

"On schedule," Dain repeated. "Then why does the whole city say the sky's falling faster than your charts allow? Why did your own astronomers petition you last month and get exiled for it?" He leaned forward as far as the chains allowed. "It's slipping, isn't it. The binding. Something's pulling at it from the inside, and you need a traitor's testimony to plug the hole, and you came to *me*."

For a moment—just a moment—the radiance around her guttered, like a lamp in a draft. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped its warmth entirely, and what was underneath was older and colder and much more tired. "You mistake curiosity for leverage, Captain. It's a common failing of honest men."

"And you mistake fear for loyalty. Also common. In dishonest ones."

Silence. Outside, distantly, the riot still murmured—shouts, a bell, the crackle of something burning. Dain realized he was listening to it the way a man listens to rain in a drought. *They're still out there. They're still angry. Angry is alive.*

"So," Vessia said at last. "You refuse."

"I refuse."

"You understand what happens next. The alternative to my generosity is not martyrdom. It's obscurity. You will rot in this cell until the Work is finished, and when it is finished, no one will remember you at all—including, quite possibly, you. The crown eats memories, Captain. It has eaten kings entire. Do you imagine I'll hesitate over one disgraced guard?"

"No," Dain said. "I don't imagine that at all. I think you're terrified, and terrified people hesitate over nothing."

He expected anger. What he got was worse: she studied him for a long moment with those mismatched eyes, and then nodded slowly, almost respectfully, the way one soldier acknowledges another across a hopeless field.

"The girl," she said. "Sable Pemberton. She carries a memory of Aldric's childhood. Did you know that? A locket full of a boy in a garden. That's what turned you—that's what's turning half the city, if my informants are right. A dead woman's keepsake." She moved toward the door, then paused. "When I find her, Captain—and I will find her—I'm going to open that locket and pour it into the crown myself. And the last thing Aldric ever was, the boy who liked gardens, will be gone forever. Think about that in the dark. Think about whether your refusal bought anything worth the price."

The door closed behind her. The lock, this time, audibly turned.

Dain sat on the cot in the lavender-smelling dark, and he did think about it. He thought about a boy in a garden, though he'd only glimpsed the memory once, secondhand, through Sable's stolen fragment—a flash of green leaves, a child's laugh, sunlight through a trellis—and how that single glimpse had undone eleven years of certainty in the space of a heartbeat. He thought about Mag the ferryman, whom he'd never met, but whose death Sable had spoken of in the tunnels with a flatness that frightened him more than tears would have. *The king remembers being kind.* An old man had died for that sentence.

And he thought about the riot, still murmuring beyond the walls, and about Pell's sister standing in the streets of the Weft, looking up at the holes in heaven, and about the fact that fear, properly shared, has another name.

His tin whistle was gone—confiscated with everything else. But he found, when he searched the cot's thin mattress with his fingers, that the guards had missed something: a bent nail, rusted, no longer than his thumb. Useless for picking a lock built by whatever craft built Vessia's doors. Perfectly useless.

He held it anyway, in the dark, and tapped it softly against the iron frame of the cot. Tap. Tap. Tap. Not a signal. Nobody could hear it. Just a rhythm, to keep time with, while above him the city burned and the sky emptied and somewhere in the dark a memory-thief was running out of moons.

Tap. Tap. Tap.

On the third day of his imprisonment, Captain Dain Ferros began—very quietly, with a rusted nail and no audience whatsoever—to count down.
