## 16. The Memory Thieves' Guild Rises

There is no such thing as the Memory Thieves' Guild.

That is the first thing you learn in Virelay's underworld, usually from someone smiling at you with too many teeth while they pick your pocket. There are only loose associations, mutual acquaintances, and the unspoken agreement that everyone who makes their living off other people's remembering will eventually owe someone else a favor they'd rather not pay.

Sable Pemberton had spent eleven years collecting those favors the way other people collected buttons: carefully, patiently, and with an eye toward the day she'd need every single one of them at once.

That day was today.

She stood on the roof of the Lantern District's oldest tenement—the one with the crooked chimney that leaned like a drunk telling a secret—and looked out over a city that had gone quiet in the wrong way. Not peaceful-quiet. Held-breath quiet. Three days since Dain had been dragged away in chains. Two days since Wren's star-charts had been seized from the safehouse along with everything else they'd built. One day since the crown had eaten another constellation, and the sky above Virelay had grown visibly, horribly emptier, like a book with pages torn out.

The Lantern District still burned its lamps, of course. Captured starlight didn't care about grief; it just glowed, obedient and pale, lighting streets where half the people below couldn't remember why they were sad anymore because they'd sold the memory of it for bread.

"They're not coming," said Quill, who was leaning against the chimney pretending he wasn't nervous, which was how Quill did everything—pretending. He was a small man made mostly of elbows and excuses, a document-forger whose forgeries were so good that three separate nobles had discovered, to their horror, that they owned country estates that had never existed. "Sable. They're not coming. You sent word to forty people. This is Virelay. Half of them sold your message to the Guard before you finished writing it."

"Then I'll know which half," Sable said. "That's useful too."

"You're insane."

"Everyone keeps saying that like it's news."

Below her, the meeting place filled.

* * *

It had started, as most dangerous things do, with a debt nobody wanted to acknowledge.

Old Maren ran the bathhouse on Candlemaker's Row, and twenty years ago—before Sable had rules, before she'd learned never to keep what she stole—a young thief had come to her bleeding and half-drowned with a stolen memory burning a hole in her head. A memory of a fire. Of a child screaming behind a door. The thief who'd extracted it from a guilt-ridden nobleman hadn't been able to sell it fast enough, and it had lodged in her mind like a splinter, playing itself over and over until Sable couldn't sleep, couldn't eat, couldn't be anything but the sound of that door.

Maren had taken her in. Fed her. Sat with her through three nights of it. And when Sable finally managed to give the memory away—to a grieving woman who needed to understand that her brother's death hadn't been her fault, which was a story for another time—Maren had asked for nothing.

"I'm calling it in," Sable told her now, in the steam-soft back room of the bathhouse. "All of it."

Maren was seventy if she was a day, with forearms like dock rope and eyes that had seen every kind of nakedness there was, including the kind people wore with their clothes on. She looked at Sable for a long moment.

"The king," she said. "This is about the king."

"This is about the sky." Sable leaned forward. "You've seen it, Maren. You've seen what's missing. And you know what happens next, because you've watched this city eat itself for sixty years. When the last star goes into that crown, Vessia won't need us anymore. The poor sell memories to eat? After the Thirteenth Binding, she'll take them. Why buy what you can harvest?"

Maren turned her hands over in the steam, palms up, as if weighing something invisible.

"I knew your mother," she said.

Sable went very still. "Everyone says that lately. Nobody remembers her face either."

"No. But I remember what she taught me, once, when we were both younger and stupider." Maren's voice dropped. "She said: *a memory kept only by one person dies with them. A memory shared is a memory armed.*" She looked up. "How many people can you gather?"

* * *

Forty messages went out. Sable had expected perhaps a dozen responses, and had braced herself for betrayal from at least half.

By nightfall, the roof of the crooked-chimney tenement held sixty-three people, and more were climbing the stairs.

They came in ones and twos, hooded and wary, the way all of Virelay's invisible people moved—like people who had spent their whole lives practicing not being noticed. The cutpurses came first, naturally, because cutpurses hear everything. Then the smugglers who ran memory-vials down the river in hollowed-out cheese wheels. Then the dream-brokers of the Shambles, who dealt in sleep-stolen recollections and were widely considered the least trustworthy people in a district where trustworthiness was measured in negative numbers.

Quill stood at the edge of the gathering, counting under his breath, his elbows somehow expressing disbelief.

"That's the whole Kettle Street crew," he whispered. "And the Ferret twins, and—gods' teeth, is that *Harrow*? Harrow hasn't left his shop since the Regent's men took his thumb. And—" His voice cracked slightly. "Sable. That's the Widow Anselm. She's ninety. She sold memories during the *old* king's reign."

"She's here, isn't she?"

"She brought her own chair."

Sable stepped forward onto the water tower's platform, and sixty-three conversations died at once—not because she was intimidating, though she could manage that, but because everyone present understood instinctively what it meant when a memory-thief asked to speak. You listened. Information was the only currency that never devalued.

"I owe most of you something," she began. "A favor, a secret, a name, a second chance. Some of you owe me, and I'm telling you now: tonight, I'm spending it. All of it. Every coin in every account." She let that settle. "I'm not going to dress this up. Three days ago the Guard took Captain Dain Ferros, who turned his coat for us because he remembered what oaths were actually for. They have Wren Calloway's charts—the complete map of the Binding rite, thirteen turns, and we're running out of both. In nine nights, the crown finishes eating the sky, and whatever sits on the throne after that won't be a regent anymore. It'll be something eternal. Something that never has to listen to any of us again."

Someone in the crowd laughed—a short, bitter sound. "And you want us to storm the palace. Us. What are we going to steal, the front door?"

"No," Sable said. "We're going to steal something much harder."

She reached into her coat and drew out the locket. Even now, even dimmed, it caught the lamplight and held it, and a ripple went through the crowd—because half of them recognized the craft, and the other half recognized the *age* of it.

"My mother hid a piece of the king inside this. His childhood. His self. The part the crown hasn't digested yet." Her voice steadied. "Vessia has spent thirty years teaching this city that memories are currency. That remembering is a thing you sell when you're desperate and buy when you're guilty. She built an economy out of forgetting." Sable lifted the locket higher. "So here's my proposition. Let's crash the market."

* * *

It did not go smoothly. Nothing worth doing ever does.

The argument lasted two hours, and it was magnificent. The smugglers wanted payment upfront. The dream-brokers wanted assurance they wouldn't be hanged. The Ferret twins—who were not twins, and possibly not named Ferret—wanted to know, with genuine philosophical urgency, whether stealing a king back from a crown counted as theft or rescue, because it affected the rate.

"We're not doing it for coin," Sable said, for the fourth time.

"Then what?" demanded Harrow, holding up the stump of his missing thumb as evidence. "Glory? I've met glory. Glory gets you arrested."

"No," said a voice from the back.

The crowd parted, and the Widow Anselm came forward, small and straight-backed, carrying her own chair, which she set down with the deliberate care of a woman who had buried three husbands and outlived two governments. Ninety years old. Memory-seller since before the valley wards fell. Her eyes were milky at the edges, but what was left of her sight fixed on Sable with terrible precision.

"I remember the stars," the old woman said. "All of them. Before the Court started harvesting, before the lamps, before any of it. I used to sell star-memories to children who'd never seen a clear night—little vials of summer skies, and they'd hold them to their ears like seashells." She smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile. "Do you know what I've realized, girl, in ninety years of selling people their own pasts? Vessia thinks she owns remembering because she taxes it. But a memory isn't real until somebody *chooses* to keep it. That's the whole trick. That's always been the whole trick."

She turned to face the crowd, and her voice, thin as it was, carried.

"I've sold everything I ever extracted. Every happy wedding, every dying confession, every child's first snow. Gone, all of it—I kept nothing, same rule as any of us. Except one." She tapped her own temple. "The night sky. Whole and entire, the way it was. I've been saving it for sixty years without knowing why." Her chin lifted. "I'm spending it on this. Who's with me?"

For a moment, nothing.

Then Harrow sighed the sigh of a man who had already lost the argument with himself, and said, "Well. Someone has to keep you old fools alive." And he sat down heavily and began, right there on the rooftop, to sketch the palace's service tunnels from memory—all forty years of it, including the sections that officially didn't exist.

After that, it came like a flood.

* * *

They worked through the night, and it was the strangest night of Sable's life, which was saying something given recent events.

Quill forged passes so beautiful they nearly wept—sixteen sets of guard credentials, each one perfect down to the ink-smudge patterns of specific clerks. The smugglers mapped every river gate and which watchmen took bribes and which took bribes *and reported it anyway*, which was crucial information. The dream-brokers, in a gesture nobody had asked for and everybody appreciated, agreed to spend the next week slipping restful dreams into the barracks of the palace guard—not to disable them, the Widow Anselm insisted, but because tired men make worse decisions, and the plan required the guards to make several very bad ones.

And the cutpurses—sixty of them, organized for the first time in the history of organized crime—went out into the city with a single instruction:

*Steal back the memories.*

Every pawnshop stockpile. Every confiscated vault. Every noble's private collection of purchased joys. For one night, Virelay's thieves did not steal gold or silver or jewels. They stole *remembering*—vials and lockets and ribbon-bound thought-glass, thousands of them—and carried them to the bathhouse on Candlemaker's Row, where Old Maren stood in the steam with her sleeves rolled up, cataloguing each one and whispering, over and over, like a prayer or a promise:

"A memory shared is a memory armed."

By dawn, the bathhouse cellar held the largest collection of stolen human experience ever assembled in one room. Weddings. Funerals. First steps and last words. A baker's memory of his daughter's laugh. A soldier's memory of coming home. All of it, hoarded by the powerful, priced beyond the reach of the people it belonged to.

Sable stood in the middle of it, surrounded by the accumulated weight of a city's heart, and felt something crack open in her chest that wasn't grief for once.

Quill found her there, sitting on a crate with the locket in her hands.

"It's not enough," she said quietly. "All this. It's a army of the forgotten, and Vessia has a crown that eats constellations. We have nine nights, no charts, and Dain is in the Ninth Gate cells, and I don't even know if he's—"

"Sable." Quill crouched beside her. He was holding a folded scrap of paper, and his elbows, for once, were perfectly still. "A boy brought this to the bathhouse an hour ago. Wouldn't say who sent him. Paid the entry fee with a captain's insignia."

She unfolded it.

The handwriting was cramped and hurried, written in the dark by a man with injured hands, but she would have known that stubborn, upright script anywhere—it looked exactly like the man who'd written it, all duty and no flourish.

*Still breathing. Gate cells, lower level, third door. They questioned me about you. I told them nothing worth having. Listen carefully: the charts weren't the only thing they took. There's a twelfth-turn rite chamber beneath the garden—you saw it, the sealed door. Vessia moves the Binding there on the new moon. And Pemberton—*

The letter stopped. Started again, smaller:

*The king asked about you. Through the walls. He doesn't remember much, but he remembers your mother's voice, and he says—his words—tell the thief her mother sang off-key and he loved her for it.*

Sable read it three times. Then she folded it very carefully, the way you fold something that has become suddenly, unbearably precious, and put it against her heart next to the locket.

"Quill," she said, standing, "how many of us are there now?"

He grinned, all elbows at last put to good use. "As of this morning? A hundred and twelve sworn, plus whoever the Ferret twins are blackmailing. And Sable—the whole Kettle Street crew wants to know one thing before we move."

"What's that?"

"What do we call ourselves? Can't storm a palace as 'no such thing as the Memory Thieves' Guild.' Bad for morale."

Sable looked around the cellar—at the vials glowing softly in the dark like a captured sky, at the invisible people of Virelay standing visible in the lamplight, choosing, for the first time, to be seen.

Above them, through the grate, the last few stars of a wounded constellation were rising over the rooftops. Nine nights left.

"Tell them," she said, "we're the people who remember."

Outside, somewhere across the sleeping city, a bell began to toll—and it was not the hour bell. It was the alarm from the Ninth Gate.

Dain had done something. Or Vessia had. Either way, the clock had just started early.
