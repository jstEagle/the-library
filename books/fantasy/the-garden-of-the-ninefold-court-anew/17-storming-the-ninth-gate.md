## 17. Storming the Ninth Gate

**Sable**

The pre-dawn dark was Sable's oldest friend and she had never asked it for anything until tonight.

"Again," Wren whispered beside her, hunched over his salvaged charts in the shadow of a tanner's shed two streets from the palace wall. His fingers were shaking. She pretended not to notice. "The Ninth Gate opens at the fourth bell for the ash-carts. It stays open eleven minutes. Eleven minutes, and then it's iron and wards and men with pikes."

"And we've got how many people?" Sable asked, though she knew. She liked hearing him say it. It made the arithmetic real.

"Forty-one." He said it the way a man reads his own death warrant aloud to check for typos. "Two memory-thieves, nine smugglers, a guild of pickpockets who call themselves *the Kindly Cousins*, one retired ferryman's widow with a crossbow—"

"Mag's widow?"

"Mag's widow," Wren confirmed. "She says Mag would have hated missing this."

Sable looked out at the ragged line of them crouched in the tanner's yard: lamplighters and cutpurses, a woman who sold other people's childhoods by the ounce, a boy of maybe fifteen who had already lost two fingers to a guildmaster's temper and grinned like he'd won them in a card game. The invisible people of Virelay, come to be seen.

*Victory purchased in blood,* she thought, and hated how easily the phrase came, how much blood the purchase might require.

"You're doing the face again," Wren said quietly.

"What face?"

"The one where you're counting everyone and deciding which ones you can afford to lose." He didn't look at her; he kept his eyes on the gate. "Stop it. They chose to be here. That's the whole point of them."

"I know what the whole point of them is. I invented the whole point of them."

"Yes, but I'm better at pep talks. Observe." He turned to the assembled thieves and smugglers and raised his voice to a whisper that carried anyway, because Wren had never quite learned volume. "In approximately four hundred heartbeats, the Ninth Gate will open for the ash-carts. We go through it in the dark, quiet as debts. Nobody is a hero before the fifth bell. Heroes before the fifth bell are corpses, and corpses make terrible distractions. Any questions?"

A hand went up. It belonged to the boy with two fingers.

"Yeah. What if I want to be a hero *after* the fifth bell?"

Wren considered this with the gravity of a man consulting an orrery. "Then I suppose that's between you and the bells."

Somewhere down the street, a dog barked twice and stopped—the signal. The ash-carts were coming.

Sable pulled her hood up. In her pocket, wrapped in silk, sat the locket-memory with Aldric's childhood inside it, warm as a held hand. Somewhere above them, behind those walls, a man was being eaten alive by his own crown, and the twelfth new moon was six nights away.

"Right," she said. "Quiet as debts."

They moved.

**Dain**

Dain Ferros had spent eleven years learning exactly how heavy a cell door could be, and he had never once imagined he'd be grateful for the knowledge.

The cells beneath the Lantern District were old—older than the Court, older than the wards, sunk into bedrock by some forgotten king who'd feared his own subjects. Which meant the locks were old too, and old locks had opinions, and Dain had spent three days pressing his thumb into every seam and hinge until he knew this one the way he knew his own sword hilt.

When the first explosion came—a muffled *whump* from somewhere above, followed by shouting and the smell of burning lamp-oil—he was ready.

He hit the lock joint with his shoulder. Once. Twice. On the third blow something gave with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and the door swung open onto darkness and freedom and, immediately, a guard named Pell who took one look at him and dropped his pike.

"Captain Ferros," Pell breathed. "You're—you're supposed to be—"

"In a cell? Yes. I've been meaning to complain about the accommodations." Dain picked up the fallen pike and weighed it. Not his sword, but a sword-shaped object, which in a pinch was nearly the same thing. "Pell. You stood honor watch at my mother's funeral. You cried. I saw you."

"I had something in my eye—"

"You cried beautifully, Pell. Now. Are you going to raise the alarm, or are you going to have something else in your eye and let me walk out of here?"

There was a long moment in which Pell's entire career passed visibly across his face, reviewed itself, and found itself wanting.

"There's something in my eye," Pell said thickly, and turned to face the wall with the tremendous dignity of a man pretending not to see a treason happening directly behind him.

Dain clapped him on the shoulder as he passed. "Tell Vessia's people the cell door was rotten. It'll be true enough by morning."

The stairwell up was chaos in the making. Above him, the palace garrison was waking in stages—first confusion, then alarm bells, then the particular organized bellowing that meant officers had arrived and were making everything worse. Dain climbed toward it with his stolen pike and his stolen freedom and his oath, which was the only thing he owned outright anymore, burning in his chest like a coal someone had finally blown on.

He had broken his oath once, letting a thief slip through his fingers at a gala. It had been the only honest act of his career. Tonight he intended to finish the job properly.

At the top of the stairs, two guards barred the door against the riot noise outside. They turned at his footsteps.

"Captain—" the younger one started.

"Don't," Dain advised. "Don't say my name, don't think about it, just look very hard at that wall for the next minute. There seems to be a lot of that going around."

The younger guard looked at the wall. The older one hesitated—and then, slowly, deliberately, unhooked the ring of keys from his belt and set them on a barrel between them, and also looked at the wall.

Dain took the keys. "Ferros," he said, because they deserved to know whose treason they were abetting. "If anyone asks."

"Nobody's asking anything, Captain," the older guard said to the wall. "Terrible night for asking questions."

Dain pushed through the door into the burning, bell-ringing dark of the palace grounds, and ran toward the garden he wasn't supposed to remember existed.

**Wren**

Wren Calloway had been exiled from the Court Observatory for being right, and if there was one thing he had sworn on the cold ashes of his career, it was that he would get his star-charts back.

They were kept in the Chart-House, a squat stone building at the edge of the observatory grounds where the Court stored inconvenient astronomy alongside confiscated telescopes and the tax records of dead nobles. Two guards at the door. A ward-seal on the cabinet. And inside, under glass, twelve years of his life: every measurement of the sky's slow starvation, every calculation of the crown's hunger, every night he had sat alone counting stars that were no longer there while his colleagues laughed themselves into promotions.

"Twelve years," he muttered, flattening himself against the Chart-House wall while Sable picked the lock. "Do you know what I did with twelve years? I counted. Do you know what counting gets you in this city? Exile. Poverty. A strong recommendation from the Royal Astronomer that I pursue 'a quieter relationship with the truth.'"

"You're monologuing," Sable said through gritted teeth, working the picks. "You only monologue when you're scared."

"I am not scared, I am *furious*, and fury requires footnotes." The lock clicked. He eased the door open. "Besides, you steal memories for a living. You of all people should understand wanting your work back."

"I keep my work in my head. It's lighter."

Inside, the Chart-House smelled of dust and lamp oil and the particular institutional despair of rooms where important things go to be ignored. Wren went straight past the tax records—past the confiscated telescopes, past a stuffed owl of unclear provenance—to the glass-fronted cabinet in the corner, and there they were. His charts. Rolled, tied, labeled in his own cramped hand: *Declinations, Year One of the Fading. Star-Counts, Autumn. THE CROWN'S APPETITE — DO NOT LET THEM FILE THIS.*

"They filed it," he said, stricken. "Look. Someone has written a filing number on it. In *red ink*. Like it's a *recipe*."

"Wren."

"It's the most important astronomical document in a century and it's filed between a complaint about gutter maintenance and someone's recipe for candied violets!"

"*Wren.* Guards."

There were, indeed, guards—two of them, drawn by the fires, coming around the corner of the observatory at a jog. Wren did the arithmetic he was best at: two guards, one astronomer, one thief, forty paces, and a window.

"Window," he said.

"Window," Sable agreed.

What followed was not, Wren would later insist, a retreat. It was a *tactical repositioning with momentum*. He gathered his charts to his chest like a rescued infant, Sable put her shoulder through the shutters, and they went out into the night in a shower of glass and dignity. They landed in a rosebush. The rosebush had opinions. So did the guards, who arrived at the empty window a full breath too late and were thereafter delayed considerably by a crossbow bolt from Mag's widow, who had taken up a position on the observatory roof for reasons she declined to explain and nobody dared question.

"My charts," Wren gasped from the roses, holding them aloft, thorn-scratched and triumphant. "I have my charts."

"You have thorns in your scalp."

"I have *my charts* and *thorns in my scalp*, and only one of those matters." He struggled upright, and stopped.

Above them, over the burning skyline of the palace, the sky was doing something it had not done in months. The stars were gone, yes—but the darkness itself seemed thinner tonight, restless, like a skin stretched over something that wanted out. And at its center, faint as breath on glass, the new moon hung invisible, six nights from its twelfth turn.

"Sable," he said quietly. "It's time. Whatever we're going to do—we do it now, or we do it never."

**Sable**

They found Dain at the garden door, fighting.

Not winning, exactly—there were six of the Regent's household guard and one of him, and his pike had broken sometime in the last minute so that he was now fighting with half a pike, which is to say a stick, which is to say badly. But he was fighting the way Sable imagined he did everything: thoroughly, joylessly, and with a running commentary of formal objections.

"—that was a *coward's stroke* and I'll thank you to—*oof*—to conduct yourself with some—*some* *decency*—"

Sable put a knife-hilt behind the nearest guard's ear, and the Kindly Cousins materialized out of the shadows the way they did everything—silently, professionally, and with an air of mild apology. What followed was brief. The guards, finding themselves outnumbered by pickpockets, made the tactical decision available to sensible men everywhere and ran.

"Dain Ferros," Sable said, hauling him up by the arm. "You look terrible."

"I've been in a cell for a week. This is me *rested*." He spat blood, grinned, and then his eyes found hers and the grin fell away into something rawer. "The garden. Is it true? Is he really—"

"He's really. Half-eaten and still standing. Come on."

They went through the door together—thief, astronomer, guard, and forty ghosts of the underworld at their backs—and down the hidden stair into the sealed dark where a boy named Aldric had once planted a garden, and where a king was waiting to learn whether anyone had come to save him or merely to watch him die.

The passage breathed cool air up at them, green-smelling, impossibly alive. Behind them, the palace burned and rang with bells. Ahead, at the bottom of the stair, something glowed softly—not lamplight, not starlight, but the pale luminescence of leaves remembering the sun.

And standing at the foot of the steps, alone, hands folded, radiant and wrong-eyed, waiting for them as though she had sent the invitations herself—

was the Regent Vessia.

"Well," she said pleasantly. "How generous of you all to come."
