## 18. The Crown Remembers Everything

The Regent's chamber at the top of the Ninth Gate was not what Sable had expected.

She had expected a throne room, all black marble and menace, the kind of place where villains rehearsed their monologues in front of mirrors. Instead she found something closer to a grandmother's parlor—if the grandmother collected starlight the way misers collected coins. Lamps burned along every wall with captured constellations inside them, and Vessia sat among them in a plain chair, doing needlework.

Needlework.

"You're bleeding on my carpet," the Regent said without looking up. "The one from the southern looms. It took nine weavers four years. Do sit on the chair instead."

Sable did not sit. Her side was indeed bleeding—someone in the corridor below had gotten lucky with a pike—and Wren hovered at her elbow making the small anxious noises he made when he wanted to say *you should bandage that* and knew she'd bite him. Dain stood at her other shoulder, sword drawn, looking like a man who had broken his oath once already today and was prepared to make a hobby of it.

"Where is he?" Sable asked. "The king."

"Below." Vessia set down her embroidery—a half-finished garden, of all things—and folded her hands. "In the garden. Where he has always been. You've seen it, haven't you? The one under the palace. I know you have; my guards are useless and my captain is a traitor, so let's not pretend otherwise."

"You know why we've come," Dain said.

"I know what you *think* you've come for." Vessia rose, and here at last was the wrongness Sable remembered from the gala—the way light bent toward her slightly, hungrily, as if her skin were drinking it. Up close, without the crowd and the music, Sable could see the truth beneath the radiance: the woman was hollowed out like a gourd. Something else was shining through her. "You think I'm keeping him prisoner. You think the crown is a cage. Sit down, child, and let me show you what it actually is."

"I'm not your child."

"No," Vessia agreed, and for a moment—just a moment—her voice lost its courtly polish and became something older and wearier. "No, you're hers. You have her stubborn chin. Sit anyway. Your side is bleeding."

* * *

They sat. It seemed foolish not to. Even Dain lowered his sword an inch, which from Dain was practically a treaty.

Vessia lifted the crown from its stand beside her chair.

Sable had imagined it a hundred times since the locket-memory first showed her Aldric's coronation: a thing of black iron, perhaps, or thorned gold, visibly wicked. It was neither. It was delicate—a circlet of woven silver, thin as wire, set with thirteen stones that were not stones. They moved. If she looked directly at them they seemed to be stars, and if she looked away she could feel them looking back.

"Do you know what memory-thieves never steal?" Vessia asked. "It's the question I'd expect you to ask, so I'll save us time. They steal moments. A face. A wedding. A last afternoon. But no thief can take the *shape* of a life—the whole long weight of being one person, decade upon decade. That's too large to hold in a locket. Too large to hold anywhere."

"Except there," Wren said quietly. His eyes were fixed on the circlet with the expression he usually reserved for particularly beautiful mathematics. "Thirteen stones. One per binding. Each stone holds an entire reign."

"Your astronomer is quick," Vessia said. "Yes. Every king who ever wore it. Their whole selves, wound in tight as thread on a spool. Do you understand what I'm telling you, girl? The crown doesn't devour its kings out of malice. It *keeps* them. Every one. Aldric's grandfather is in there, entire. His great-grandmother, who held the northern passes alone through a seven-year winter. The first king, who planted the original garden with his own hands because he believed rulers should know how things grow." Her hollow eyes glittered. "Twelve lifetimes of wisdom, judgment, mercy, and grief—all of it preserved against the day it would be needed. And you want to shatter it."

"It's killing him," Sable said. "I've *seen* him. He's being eaten alive."

"He is being *carried*," Vessia snapped, and the lamps flared. "He was a boy who never wanted any of this! Weak, kind, weeping over dead birds in the garden! The binding gives him twelve lifetimes of strength to bear a burden he could never carry alone!"

"And what do you get?" Sable asked softly.

The flare died. For a long moment the only sound was the faint hum of the captive stars, and then Vessia laughed—not cruelly, which was somehow worse.

"What does anyone get? To continue. The thirteenth binding makes it eternal. Makes *him* eternal. No more succession, no more wars over the throne, no more watching everything you build crumble because some future fool inherits it. Permanence, girl. Order. The stars go into the crown and the crown goes on forever, and everyone in this miserable kingdom finally stops having to be afraid of tomorrow." She leaned forward. "I have watched three generations be born and buried waiting for someone to fix the world. I am *tired* of waiting."

* * *

It was almost convincing. That was the horror of it. Sable had spent her life listening to people justify themselves across her worktable—*I only sold the memory because the debts, I only took hers because she was dying anyway*—and Vessia's justification was the grandest and saddest of them all. She almost admired it.

Then Vessia turned the crown in her hands, and the stones turned too, and one of them looked at Sable.

Not metaphorically. One of the thirteen lights swiveled in its setting like an eye finding a familiar face, and warmth poured out of it, and suddenly—

—suddenly Sable was eight years old in a kitchen that smelled of cinnamon, and her mother was laughing at something, turning from the stove with flour on her cheek, and the light came through the window at exactly the angle it always did, gold and patient, and her mother's face was—

There.

Whole. Real. Not the locket's fragments, not the edges-of-a-dream impression she'd guarded for fifteen years like a coal in cupped hands, but the *face itself*, every line of it, the crooked tooth, the scar through one eyebrow she'd gotten falling out of the pear tree, the way her eyes went crescent-shaped when she laughed—

Sable made a sound she didn't know she could make. She was on her feet. She didn't remember standing.

"There she is," Vessia murmured. "Lys's daughter. The crown remembers your mother too, you know. Not her—she was never its—but it remembers *him* remembering her. The boy Aldric loved her, did you know that? Before the coronation. Before everything. Your mother carried his childhood out of this palace in her own head to keep it safe, and the crown has been holding the shape of her ever since, waiting." She extended the circlet, gentle as an offering of bread. "Every stolen thing can be returned, memory-thief. That is the one true law of your craft, isn't it? Here. Take her face back. Take all of it. Walk away, and the twelfth binding completes peacefully, and the king endures, and somewhere in the forever that follows, your mother's laugh is kept safe in silver instead of rotting in a grave. What could be wrong with that?"

"Sable." Wren's voice, very quiet. "Don't."

"Dain." Also quiet. "Her hand."

But Sable wasn't listening. Sable was looking at her mother's face.

* * *

Here is the thing about temptation that no one warns you: it doesn't feel like evil. It feels like relief. It feels like setting down a weight you forgot you were carrying until someone offers to carry it for you.

Fifteen years. Fifteen years of guarding a single memory the way Mag guarded his ferry—jealously, superstitiously, knowing it was the only thing in Hollowmere that was truly hers. Fifteen years of waking some mornings unable to summon it, panicking, checking it like a miser counting coins in the dark. Fifteen years of the rule she told clients—*never keep what you steal*—and breaking it, once, secretly, and hating herself for the crack in her own professionalism even as she treasured the crack more than anything alive.

And here it was, offered back to her, complete and gleaming, in exchange for walking away. In exchange for letting a man she'd met twice—half-eaten, weeping over dead birds—be bound forever so that order could triumph and her mother's smile could live in a jewel.

It would be so easy. She could feel how easy. Her hand was already rising.

"Sable." Wren again, closer now. "Whatever you're seeing—it's real, I believe that. That's what makes it monstrous. But listen to me. Listen." He caught her wrist, gently, the way he handled his charts. "You told me once that the worst thing a thief can do is convince herself the theft is a kindness. You said it about the matriarch's son. Remember?"

She remembered. The grieving son who bought his mother's happiest hour and played it over and over until he couldn't tell which memories were his own anymore.

"The crown is doing that," Wren said. "To a whole kingdom. To a whole *forever*. And it's doing it to you right now, and it's very good at it, because it has had twelve lifetimes of practice at being persuasive." His voice cracked slightly. "Also, if you take that deal I will be extremely cross with you, and you know how I sulk."

Despite everything—despite the face, despite the flour on her cheek, despite the light through the window—something in Sable's chest loosened by a single degree. "You're a terrible orator, Wren Calloway."

"I know. I lead with astronomy and end with sulking. It's a flaw."

Dain hadn't spoken. When she glanced at him, he was staring at the crown with an expression she'd seen on him exactly once before—in the gala's shadowed corridor, when Aldric's memory had brushed against whatever oath still lived in him under the leash. Now he knelt. Slowly, deliberately, in front of the Regent of the Ninefold Court, he laid his sword on the carpet between them.

"I gave my oath to the crown of the Ninefold Court," he said. "To the *Court*. Not to a piece of jewelry wearing its kings as trophies." He looked up at Vessia, and there was no shame in his face anymore—only the clean, hard peace of a man who had finally stopped lying to himself. "You keep saying the crown preserves them. Ma'am—I served in the palace guard for eleven years. I walked past the sealed garden every day. If those twelve lives in there are so wise and so merciful, why did none of them ever cry out? Why is the king below us hollow-eyed and starving while his ancestors' wisdom sits in silver doing *nothing*?"

Vessia's composure flickered. Just a hairline crack. "They sleep."

"They're *kept*," Dain said. "There's a difference, and you know it. Keeping isn't love. My grandmother kept songbirds in cages her whole life and called it protecting them from cats. The birds never sang once."

Silence. The captive stars hummed. And in that silence, Sable looked one last time at her mother's face—flour on her cheek, crescent eyes, the pear-tree scar—and understood, with a grief so vast it was almost calm, what she had to do.

Because here was the truth Vessia's offer had shown her without meaning to: the face in the crown was perfect, and that was exactly what was wrong with it. Her mother had never been perfect. She'd been tired sometimes, and short-tempered on market days, and wrong about Sable's father, and frightened at the end. The crown offered a portrait. Sable had owned the woman. And a portrait, however lovely, however eternal, was just another kind of cage—the same cage, in fact, the same shining lie, that Vessia was building around a king and a kingdom and the sky itself.

"You can't give her back to me," Sable said. Her voice didn't shake. She was almost proud of that. "She's not yours to give. She never was. You're offering me a copy of a memory of a man remembering her, polished up pretty, and you're calling it my mother." She stepped back from the crown, and the warmth of it reached for her, and she let it reach, and she did not take it. "My mother taught me my craft. First lesson, first day: *the difference between a thief and a keeper is that a keeper knows when to open her hand.*"

She turned to Wren. "How long until the twelfth new moon?"

His breath caught. "Two days. Perhaps less—the charts put it at dawn after next."

"Then we're done talking." Sable looked at Vessia—at the hollow, radiant, exhausted woman behind the borrowed light—and felt, unexpectedly, a flicker of pity. Whatever the regent had been before the crown got its hooks in her ambitions, she had once been someone's daughter too. "Keep your forever. We're going to go open a hand."

Vessia rose. The lamps guttered. And when she spoke again, all the weary grandmother was gone, and what remained was cold and vast and very old.

"Then you should know," she said, "that the rite begins tonight. Not at the new moon. Tonight—because you've cost me two guards, one captain, and my patience, and I will not wait for dawn after next to finish what a century started. By the time you find your way down to the garden, girl, the twelfth binding will already be underway." She smiled, and it was the loneliest, most terrible thing Sable had ever seen. "Run."

Behind them, all along the walls, the captured stars began to go out—one by one by one—as somewhere far below, in a garden fed on a dying man, the crown opened another eye.
