## 19. The Twelfth Turn

The moon died at midnight, and Sable felt it go the way she felt a memory leave a client's head: a small, cold click behind her eyes, like a lock turning in an empty room.

She stood on the roof of the Lantern District's oldest counting-house, Wren beside her with his charts spread across the slates and weighted down with candlesticks he'd stolen from three different temples, because Wren Calloway believed that blasphemy was a form of organization. Below them, Virelay held its breath. The lamps fed on harvested starlight burned low, as they always did on new-moon nights now, and the city's poor had learned to stay indoors when the sky went hungry.

"Twelve constellations left," Wren said, though she hadn't asked. He said it every night, the way other men said their prayers. "The Crown will take the thirteenth tonight if we fail. The River Serpent. It's Aldric's birth-sign." His voice caught on the arithmetic of it. "Vessia has a sense of theater."

"Then we'll disappoint her," Sable said. "It's the one thing I've never been able to do for anyone."

Wren snorted—a small, startled sound, half laugh. In the weeks since the betrayal in the Lantern District, since Dain had been dragged away in chains and her charts had gone up in Vessia's braziers, Sable had watched the astronomer fold himself smaller and smaller, a man made of apologies. But tonight something in him had straightened. Tonight he looked like a man who had counted nothing his whole life and finally arrived at a number worth saying aloud.

"The Ninth Gate opens in an hour," he said. "The Guild is in position. Mag's cousin's people have the harbor watch bribed, the ferry-guild owes you two favors and a funeral debt, and the lamplighters—" He shook his head, wonder creeping through the exhaustion. "The lamplighters, Sable. Every lamp in the palace quarter goes dark at the same moment. I still can't believe you got the lamplighters."

"I didn't get them," Sable said. "I gave them back something. A widow's memory of her husband's voice. A boy's memory of his mother singing. You'd be amazed what people will do when you stop charging them for their own lives."

She touched the locket at her throat. Inside it, folded into silver like a letter into an envelope, lay the fragments of a boy named Aldric: a garden, a coronation that felt like a funeral, a king remembering being kind. And beneath those, kept separate and secret for thirty years, the one thing she had never sold, never traded, never even looked at when she was alone—her mother's face.

*Never keep what you steal.* That had been her rule, her whole life, the wall she built between herself and the hunger of the work. She had broken it exactly once, and not by stealing. By keeping.

"You're doing the thing," Wren said quietly.

"What thing?"

"The thing where your face goes very still and very professional and I can tell you're somewhere else entirely." He gathered his charts with careful hands. "My mother did that too. Right before she told me my father wasn't coming back from the war. Stillness isn't peace, Sable. It's just grief wearing its good coat."

Sable looked out over the darkening city. Somewhere below, nine hundred thieves and smugglers and memory-sellers—the invisible people, the ones who dealt in what others wanted to forget or couldn't bear to lose—were moving toward the palace with knives and crowbars and, in the case of old Tessic the smuggler, an alarming quantity of fireworks. Somewhere in that palace, in a cell or a garden or a throne room, Dain Ferros was either alive or he wasn't, and she would know which soon enough.

And somewhere deeper than all of that, under the palace itself, a man was being eaten alive by his own crown, and the only key to his cage was a dead woman's face.

"Wren," she said. "If this doesn't work—if the rite needs more than I can give—"

"It won't."

"But if it does—"

"Then I'll remember her for you." He said it simply, without ceremony, the way he said everything that mattered. "The shape of her. The way she laughed. Whatever fragments you've shown me. I'll carry them until you want them back or until you don't. That's what memory-thieves never understand about the rest of us—we've been carrying other people's losses our whole lives. We're practiced."

Sable found, to her surprise, that she was smiling. "You're going to make someone a very annoying husband someday."

"I'm going to make *astronomy* a very annoying discipline someday," Wren said. "Husbands are a later project."

Below them, across the city, every lamp in the palace quarter went dark at once.

* * *

The palace of Virelay had been built to be seen, which meant it had never been built to be entered. Its architects had assumed no enemy would ever come from below—from the forgotten passages, the sealed gardens, the bones of the older court that lay beneath the newer one like a body under a shroud.

Dain had known the way. Dain had drawn it for her in charcoal on the back of a stolen menu, in the last hour before the Lantern District went wrong, pressing the paper into her hands with both of his as though he could transfer his knowledge through his skin. *The garden remembers him,* he'd said. *Go to the garden.*

She went alone. That had been the plan—not heroics, but arithmetic. Wren's charts said the crown's binding weakened at the moment of each new moon, a hairline crack in thirteen seals, and only at that moment, and only for as long as the moon stayed dead. The Guild's assault on the Ninth Gate was noise, beautiful necessary noise, to hold Vessia's guards while one memory-thief slipped down through the palace's forgotten throat and did the one thing no army could do.

The passages smelled of stone and old water and, faintly, impossibly, of roses.

She knew that smell. She had carried it inside a locket for most of her life without knowing what it belonged to. It grew stronger as she descended, past doors bricked shut generations ago, past murals of kings whose faces had been chiseled away by some pious hand, until the passage opened and she stood at the edge of the garden beneath the palace.

It was alive. That was the horror and the miracle of it, and she had no time for either. Roses climbed trellises that should have rotted centuries ago. A fountain trickled though its pipes must be dust. And in the center, beneath a tree that bore white flowers out of season and out of reason, sat the king.

He was worse than she remembered. The crown sat on his brow like a spider on a trapped fly, its nine points sunk into his skin, and where they sank, his flesh had gone smooth and featureless, like wax near a flame. His hands lay open in his lap. His eyes were open too, fixed on the fountain, and Sable understood with a lurch that he was watching the water because it moved, because it was the only thing in his world that changed.

"Aldric," she said.

Nothing. Not a flicker.

She knelt in front of him, close enough to see that his breathing was shallow and slow, a man rationing himself. "Aldric. My name is Sable Pemberton. My mother was Lys, and she hid pieces of you inside me before I could walk, and I have carried you through Hollowmere and across the Nine Roads and into this rotten shining city, and I have walked a very long way to be annoyed with you, so the least you can do is look at me."

His eyes moved. Slowly, painfully, like hinges in a door long unopened. They found her face.

"Lys," he said. The word came out rusted. "Lys had a daughter?"

"She had me." Sable's throat tightened. "She didn't talk about you much. Just the garden. Just the boy who planted roses because his nurse told him kings shouldn't kneel in dirt, and he decided that was exactly why he should."

Something moved behind his eyes—pain, or recognition, or the ghost of a smile. "That was treasonous advice," he whispered. "Nurse Maren. She was hanged for less."

"She was remembered for better. I promise you that."

Above them, muffled by fathoms of stone, the assault on the Ninth Gate began. The sound reached the garden as a heartbeat—booms, shouts, the deep groan of old gates—and Sable saw the crown *react*. The nine points pulsed with light, drinking, and Aldric cried out, a small terrible sound, and his back arched.

"It feeds on fear," he gasped. "On violence. On—all of it. Everything they feel down there, it—it drinks—"

"Then we finish this before it finishes drinking." Sable pulled the locket over her head. Her hands were steady. She noticed that they were steady and thought, distantly, *so this is what courage feels like—it feels like nothing at all.* "Aldric. Listen to me. The crown has thirteen seals. Twelve are whole. The rite that breaks the first seal needs a memory given willingly—given away forever, by choice, by sacrifice. Your memories are already eaten. But mine aren't."

She opened the locket. Inside, two things glimmered: the contraband fragments of his childhood, and beneath them, wrapped around them like a second skin, the warm gold weight of her mother's face.

"I don't know what she looked like," Sable said. Her voice did not shake, and she hated it for that, and forgave it, and hated it again. "I was four. She hid this in me so well that I can't even find the edges of it anymore. For thirty years it's been the one thing I wouldn't sell. People offered me fortunes. People offered me *kingdoms*, near enough. And I told them all the same thing: never keep what you steal. Except I kept this. Because it was hers. Because it was the only proof she ever loved me that I could hold in my hands."

"Sable." The king's ruined voice cracked on her name. "There are other ways—"

"There aren't. You know there aren't. You've had centuries to look." She smiled at him, and it was almost easy, because his face—what remained of it—was so full of helpless grief that it made her feel, absurdly, protected. "You know what's funny? I spent my whole life taking memories from people who begged me to. *Take it, take it, I can't live with it.* And I always refused to take the ones they loved. I thought that made me honest." She closed her hand around the locket. "Turns out I was just saving up."

The booms above were closer now. The garden trembled; rose petals fell like snow. And Sable felt it—the crack in the night, the hairline fracture in the world that Wren's charts had promised. The new moon's dead moment, when the seals grew thin.

She reached into herself, into the place where memory-thieves go, the quiet room behind the eyes where stolen things wait in their wrappings. There it was. Her mother's face. Warm as bread, bright as a window at dusk. She could almost see it now, rising to meet her—cheekbones, a mouth, eyes she suddenly realized were the same color as her own, and the realization broke her heart so cleanly she barely felt it cut.

*I'll remember everything else,* she told the face silently. *Your hands. Your voice. The song about the ferryman. How you smelled after rain. I'll keep all of it alive. I'm only giving them your face, Mama. I'm giving it to a king who forgot how to be one.*

*I'm giving it because you taught me that some things are worth more given than kept.*

She let it go.

There was no thunderclap. That was the strange part. Thirty years of hoarded love left her not with a roar but with a *click*—the same small cold click she felt when a memory left a client's head, the sound of a lock turning in an empty room. And then the space where her mother's face had been was simply space, clean and swept and terribly, perfectly empty, and Sable Pemberton knelt in a buried garden and could not have described the woman who bore her if her life depended on it.

But the locket blazed.

Light poured from the silver like water from a broken jug—the fragments of Aldric's childhood unfurling, and threaded through them now, braided into them by her sacrifice, her mother's face, given freely, arriving where it was needed. The memory struck the crown like a bell being struck.

The first seal *cracked*.

The sound was enormous and intimate at once, a sound like a century of held breath released. One of the nine points of the crown went dark, its light guttering out, and Aldric screamed—but it was a scream of return, of blood rushing into a numb limb, of a man feeling his own face for the first time in a hundred years. The waxy smoothness along his jaw softened. His hands curled, uncurled.

And far above them, beyond stone and soil and the burning gates of the Ninth, the rite faltered.

Sable felt that too, though she couldn't have said how. A great gathering pressure in the sky—the River Serpent constellation being reeled in, thirteen years of harvested starlight converging on a single point—and then a *stutter*, a hesitation, a machine missing a gear. Somewhere in the palace above, Vessia's perfect ceremony stumbled. Somewhere in the dark, twelve remaining constellations hung on, and the thirteenth slipped loose and scattered back across the sky like coins from a torn purse.

Aldric sagged against the tree, breathing hard, and when he lifted his head his eyes were clear. Clearer than any king's eyes had a right to be.

"You gave away her face," he said hoarsely.

"I gave away her face."

"What did she look like?"

And here was the wound of it, the true and unbearable wound: Sable reached for the answer and found nothing. No cheekbones. No mouth. No color of eyes, though she knew—they had matched her own, she knew it the way you know a thing you no longer possess. She began, helplessly, to laugh, and the laughter turned halfway through into something else, and Aldric reached out and took her hand with fingers that were becoming human again, and held it while she grieved a face she could no longer picture, for a woman she would now remember only in pieces: hands, voice, rain-smell, song.

"That's the trick of it," she said at last, wiping her eyes with her free wrist. "You don't get to choose what survives. She taught me that too, apparently. I just never listened."

"Twelve seals remain," Aldric said. His voice was stronger with every word, and underneath it Sable could hear something waking—something that had been patient for a century. "One is broken, but the crown knows. It will fight now. And Vessia—"

Above them, the sounds of battle changed. The booms stopped. The shouting stopped. And in the ringing silence that followed, a new sound began: bells. Every bell in Virelay, ringing at once—not in alarm, Sable realized, but in *summons*, the old signal, the one from the histories, the one that called the Court to assemble before its regent.

Vessia was not retreating. Vessia was gathering everything she had left.

Aldric rose. It cost him visibly—he swayed, caught the tree, stood anyway—and when he turned to her, one point of his crown was dark and eight still burned, but his spine was straight, and Sable thought: *there he is. The boy who knelt in dirt.*

"They'll have taken the Gate," he said. "They'll have taken your friends, or lost them, or both. And she will come for you now, memory-thief, with everything the crown can offer—everything it has *eaten*." His grip on her hand tightened. "When she offers you your mother's face back—and she will, it's in there, everything is in there—you need to decide, right now, what your answer will be."

Sable looked at her empty hands. At the locket, dimming now, its work done. At the man the crown had not yet finished eating.

"My answer," she said, "is that I've been poor my whole life, Your Majesty, but I've never once been cheap."

Somewhere above, the bells rang on, and the stairs at the far end of the garden filled with torchlight, and footsteps began to descend.
