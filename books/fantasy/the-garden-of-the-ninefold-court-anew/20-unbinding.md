## 20. Unbinding

Aldric had forgotten what it felt like to be a person.

Not the fact of it—he remembered facts the way a drowned man remembers the shape of air—but the feeling. The weight of a body that belonged to him. The strange, unremarkable luxury of thinking a thought and having it remain his own at the end of it.

He was lying on cold marble in the ruined heart of the Ninth Gate, and there was a girl kneeling beside him with tears on her face and an absence where something precious used to be. He could see it the way he could now see everything: the crown's grip had loosened, one seal cracked, and through the crack poured not light but *her*—a memory given away so completely that it left a wound shaped like love.

"You gave it up," he said. His voice was a ruin. He laughed anyway, because if he did not laugh he would weep, and he had done eleven centuries of weeping already and found it tedious. "You absolute fool. Do you know how rare that is? Willing sacrifice? Kings have conquered continents to avoid it."

"Is that gratitude?" Sable Pemberton wiped her face with the back of her hand, leaving a smear of soot across her cheek like a war paint applied by someone who had never seen a war. "Because it sounds like an insult wearing gratitude's coat."

"It's both. I'm a king. We're trained for it."

Behind her, the astronomer Wren Calloway was crouched over his recovered star-charts, muttering arithmetic at the ceiling as though the ceiling owed him money. And Dain Ferros—still in his broken half-armor, still bleeding from a wound he refused to acknowledge with anything so undignified as sitting down—stood at the shattered doors with a sword in each hand, watching the corridor.

"They're coming," Dain said. "All of them. She's pulling every guard in the palace toward us."

"Good," Aldric said.

Sable looked at him sharply. "Good? Aldric, you can barely sit up. One seal is cracked. Eleven to go, and Vessia is—"

"Vessia is coming *here*." He pushed himself upright, and the world swam, and he let it swim, because swimming meant moving, and moving meant he was still in his own body rather than a passenger in it. "She has spent a century feeding this crown from a safe distance. She harvests stars like a woman milking a goat she's afraid of. But the rite falters now. Her thirteenth binding is broken mid-stitch, and she cannot finish it unless she comes and finishes it *in person*, over my body, before the twelfth turn completes." He smiled, and felt his own smile move his own mouth, and nearly wept at that instead. "So let her come. I have been waiting nine hundred years to say what I am about to say to her face."

"And what's that?" Wren asked, without looking up from his charts.

"I haven't decided yet. I want it to be devastating. Give me a moment."

* * *

She came the way winter comes—not quickly, but inevitably, and with a certainty that made resistance feel quaint.

The guards parted for her and then fled past her, which told Aldric everything about how much even her own soldiers wished to be elsewhere. Vessia walked into the ruined garden-hall alone, and she was radiant, and the radiance was wrong in the way that a painted sunrise is wrong: beautiful, and flat, and lit from no source at all.

Nine hundred years ago she had been his aunt. He tried to remember her laugh. The crown, even cracked, kept that memory locked in its vaults, and so all he had was the present: a tall woman in white and gold, with eyes like coins—shining, round, stamped with someone else's face.

"Aldric." She said his name gently, the way you speak to a horse you intend to put down. "Look at you. Sitting up. Speaking. She cracked a seal with one small memory. Imagine what we could do together, with proper material." Her gaze slid to Sable, and softened into something almost kind, which was the most frightening thing in the room. "I offered you your mother's face, child. I offer again. It is in here—" she touched her own breastbone, where the crown's reflection glimmered beneath her skin, "—whole. Untouched. I keep everything. That is my curse and my gift."

"Don't answer her," Aldric said quietly. "It's what she does. She makes the price sound like a gift and the gift sound like a debt."

"And you would know about debts," Vessia said, turning back to him, and now the gentleness fell away like a dropped cloak. "You were going to *die*, Aldric. Did they tell you that part? The crown was eating you alive and the Court stood around wringing its hands, and I *saved* you. I bound the devouring. I slowed it to a sip. Nine hundred years of sip. Every constellation I took bought you another decade of breath. You are welcome."

"You harvested the sky," Wren burst out, unable to contain himself, holding up a chart like a prosecutor's exhibit. "Do you know what you did? Children in the Lantern District used to navigate by the Weaver's Loom. Sailors. Poets. There are songs that no longer make sense! 'Bright as the Seven Sisters' means nothing now, it's just words, you've unmade *metaphor*—"

"Wren," Sable murmured.

"I'm sorry, it's been building for some time."

"The metaphors will return," Aldric said, and stood.

It cost him. Standing cost him the way a mountain costs a climber. But he stood, and the crown beneath his skin flared and shrieked—a soundless shriek, felt in the teeth—and Vessia took one step backward before catching herself.

"You can't renounce it," she said. "No one can. It was forged to be eternal. That was the entire point."

"No," Aldric said. "It was forged to bind *me*. Eternity was your addition. And eternity, dear aunt, has a flaw you never noticed, because you never loved anything enough to test it." He began to walk toward her, one step, then another, each one an argument his body won against the crown. "A chain holds only while the prisoner wants what's outside it. I have wanted freedom for nine centuries, and it held, because wanting isn't willing. Wanting is just weather. But tonight a memory-thief knelt beside me and *gave away the best thing she owned*, freely, gladly, to free a stranger—and the crown cracked. Because willing sacrifice is the one key it was never built to resist. Its maker never imagined anyone would pay that price. You didn't imagine it. You counted on no one ever loving anything more than themselves."

"That is not a flaw," Vessia said. "That is simply how everything works."

"Yes," Aldric agreed. "And that is why you lose."

He stopped three paces from her. Close enough to see that under the coin-light of her eyes there was something tired, something old, something that might once, in another life, have been a frightened young woman trying to keep her nephew alive by any means available. He chose to see it. Not to forgive it—forgiveness could wait, or not arrive at all—but because relinquishing power, he was learning, begins with relinquishing the comfort of a simple enemy.

"I'm not going to destroy it," he said.

Vessia blinked. Behind him, he heard Sable go very still, and Wren make a small strangled noise of scholarly outrage.

"Aldric—" Sable started.

"Hear me first. I've had nine hundred years to think, and most of that time I thought about breaking it. Shattering the crown. Ending the line. And every version of that ending ends the same way: with centuries of kings' memories—every coronation, every mistake, every kindness, every name of every servant who ever mended a royal sleeve—burned to nothing. The crown ate them too, you understand. It wasn't only eating me. It holds them all. My father. His mother. The gardener-king, Osric, who planted the garden above us. All of them, hoarded in gold." He turned his head, found Sable's eyes. "You taught me something without meaning to. You spend your life telling people: what is taken can be returned. Never keep what you steal. So I won't destroy the theft. I'll *return* it."

"To whom?" Vessia asked, and for the first time her voice held something other than certainty. "They're dead."

"To the sky," said Wren Calloway, very softly, understanding arriving on his face like dawn. "Oh. Oh, you brilliant, impossible man. The binding drew down starlight—the crown is *made* of harvested light and hoarded memory, they're the same substance, they always were—" He was scrabbling at his charts. "If you release the bindings voluntarily—if the crown isn't shattered but *opened*—the memories don't die, they disperse, they go back to—"

"To the dark between the stars," Aldric finished. "Which is not empty. It never was. It's where light waits." He smiled at Vessia. "You've been mining a treasury, aunt, and calling the mine shaft a kingdom. I'm simply giving back what was borrowed."

"And you?" Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the word. "What happens to you?"

"Ah." He considered the question honestly, because she deserved one honest thing. "I don't entirely know. Some of me may go with it. A king is mostly crown, by the end; there may be very little Aldric left to walk away." He shrugged, and the shrug hurt, and he treasured the hurt. "But that is rather the point. I would rather be a small true man than an eternal false one. I have been the second thing for nine hundred years. The wages are terrible."

"You'll undo the Court," Vessia whispered. "Everything I built. Everything I *protected*—"

"You protected nothing," Sable said, rising to stand at Aldric's shoulder, and her voice was quiet and absolutely level. "You fed a valley full of people to a lie for a hundred years. You made children sell their birthdays so their mothers could eat. I know. I bought some of those birthdays myself." She lifted her chin. "Whatever happens next, it happens in daylight. That's all any of us ever wanted."

For a long moment, Vessia looked at the three of them—the thief, the astronomer, the guard hovering wounded and loyal at the door—and Aldric watched her search for the lever, the fear, the greed, the angle, and find nothing to grip. Power, he thought, is helpless in front of people who aren't reaching for it.

Then she did the last thing he expected.

She stepped aside.

"Open it, then," she said, hollow. "And when the sky stays empty, when your grand dispersal turns out to be nothing but darkness—remember that I offered you eternity, and you chose a girl with soot on her face."

"Soot is very fashionable this season," Sable said.

* * *

It took an hour to prepare, and it was, absurdly, the happiest hour of Aldric's long life.

Wren directed operations from atop a fallen column, charts pinned under stones, arguing geometry with anyone who came within earshot. Dain Ferros guarded the doors and pretended not to listen, and failed, and eventually asked a question about stellar mechanics so earnest and so basic that Wren nearly fell off his column with joy. Sable sat beside Aldric and talked to him—not about crowns or rites, but about Hollowmere, about Mag the ferryman who had died so that strangers might live, about the taste of river water in a valley that hated magic and somehow, in spite of everything, had been home.

"Tell me about her," Aldric said. "Your mother. You can't remember her face. Tell me the rest."

"She hummed when she cooked. Off-key, always the same wrong note, like she'd signed a contract with it." Sable's mouth trembled into a smile. "She taught me that a stolen memory weighs exactly as much as the keeping of it. She said thieves who forget that end up carrying houses." She paused. "I think she knew. About you. About what she was hiding in me. I think she spent my whole childhood deciding whether to tell me, and ran out of time."

"Then we'll carry it together," Aldric said. "The not-telling. It's lighter shared."

When the preparations were done, he knelt at the center of the ruined garden, where a single tree grew through the broken marble—alive, impossibly, fed on his own fading self—and pressed both hands to the crown's presence inside him.

He did not tear it open. That was the old way, the king's way, the way of seizing. Instead he did the harder thing. He opened his hands, inside, where it mattered, and *let go*.

The first seal dissolved like sugar in warm water.

The second followed, and the third, and then the crown understood what he intended and fought him with everything it had—with his father's voice, with the weight of duty, with visions of the Court crumbling and the borders burning—and Aldric held on to the letting-go the way a drowning man holds a rope, except the rope was made of surrender, which should not work, and worked.

Light came out of him.

Not the stolen coin-light of Vessia. This was older, warmer, embarrassed almost, the light of a thousand years of human moments remembering themselves: a queen laughing at her own joke; a boy-king planting an apple tree upside down to see if it would grow downward; a servant's lullaby sung in a stairwell; rain on a coronation everyone pretended to enjoy. The memories rose through the broken roof of the Ninth Gate and streamed upward into the starless dark, and the dark—

The dark *answered*.

One point of light. Then three. Then a scatter, a spill, a slow deliberate handful of stars returning to the sky above the palace, each one perhaps a kindness, perhaps a grief, perhaps a name, given back to the night that had waited so patiently to hold it.

Dain Ferros made a sound like a man being forgiven. Wren sat down hard on his column, chart forgotten, mouth open, counting and refusing to count. Sable gripped Aldric's hand, and hers was shaking, and he found that his was not, and that this too was new.

Vessia stood apart, and the borrowed radiance drained out of her as the crown emptied—not all at once, but steadily, like tide going out. The coin-light left her eyes. The gold dimmed in her hair. What remained was a tall, thin, elderly woman in expensive clothes, blinking in ordinary lamplight, smaller than she had been a moment ago, mortal as bread. She looked at her own hands as though checking whether they belonged to her.

"Well," she said at last, in a voice with cracks in it. "I suppose I shall have to learn to sleep."

"You'll have help," Aldric said. "Or a trial. Or both. The Court will decide—not me. I'm finished deciding things for other people." He got to his feet, unsteadily, and found that he was lighter than he had been in nine hundred years, and also less, and that both of these were gifts. "Though I'd advise the tribunal to start with the valley of Hollowmere. There are reparations owed there. I intend to deliver the first one personally."

Sable looked at him sideways. "You're coming to Hollowmere."

"If I'm invited."

"I'll consult the ferry schedule," she said, which from Sable Pemberton, he was learning, was roughly equivalent to a sonnet.

Above them, through the torn roof, the sky went on quietly filling—one star, then another, patient as spring—and Aldric tipped his head back and watched, and felt the last of the crown's weight lift off something deep in his chest, and thought: *so this is what it is to be a man at dusk, owning nothing, owing everything.*

Then Sable caught his arm. "Aldric. Look east."

On the horizon, low and red and impossibly welcome, the sun was coming up—its first unhindered dawn in a century, gilding the ruins, gilding her soot-smudged face, gilding the small mortal figure of Vessia standing alone among the columns. And riding toward the Ninth Gate along the morning road, banners furled, faces upturned, came the people of Virelay, thousands of them, drawn out of their houses by a sky that had begun, overnight, to remember itself.

"They'll want to see you," Sable said. "The king who gave it all back."

Aldric looked at the crowd, and at the girl beside him, and at the long road east that led, eventually, to a valley called Hollowmere.

"What they need," he said, "is breakfast. Kings are terrible at breakfast. Come on—I know a baker near the Lantern District who owes me nine hundred years of back payment."
