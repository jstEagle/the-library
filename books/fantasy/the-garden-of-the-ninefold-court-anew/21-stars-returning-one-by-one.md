## 21. Stars Returning, One by One

The first star came back on a Tuesday.

Sable knew it was a Tuesday because Wren had made her memorize the calendar of the sky's recovery, and because Dain had complained that Tuesdays were unlucky ever since the Lantern District betrayal, which had happened, inconveniently for his theory, on a Thursday. The star appeared low over the western hills of Virelay, small and stubborn as a coin at the bottom of a well, and the whole city stopped to look at it.

Bakers stood in their doorways with flour still on their hands. Memory-sellers closed their stalls mid-haggle. A child in the street below Sable's window pointed and shouted something Sable couldn't hear through the glass, but she could guess: *there, there, there.*

She sat on the windowsill of the room Wren had rented them in the Tinker's Quarter—rented honestly, with money, which Wren insisted was a novelty he intended to enjoy—and watched the light arrive like a guest who'd been delayed by weather.

"One," she said to the empty room.

By the end of the week there were four. By the end of the month, thirty-one, and Wren had taken to standing on the roof every night with his charts pinned under weights, ticking off constellations as they reassembled themselves out of nothing, out of the crown's hoarded centuries, out of Aldric's renunciation. He cried the night the River Serpent came back whole. He claimed it was the cold.

"You're allowed to cry about stars," Sable told him.

"I'm an astronomer," Wren said with dignity, wiping his nose on a chart he would later regret ruining. "We're famously stoic."

* * *

Aldric abdicated on the first morning the dawn sky held more than a hundred stars.

He did it in the Hall of Ninefold Light, before the assembled remnant of the Court—the ones who hadn't fled, the ones who had, the ones who came back when word spread that the regent was mortal now, small and gray-eyed and awaiting trial like any other criminal. Vessia sat in the dock they'd built for her, because the Court's old judgment seat had been melted down along with most of its regalia, and she listened to the whole proceeding with her hands folded, wearing a plain wool dress that made her look, for the first time, like exactly what she was: a woman who had once been very good at one very terrible idea.

Aldric spoke without notes. Sable stood near the back, between Dain and a delegation of memory-thieves who kept elbowing each other and whispering that they could see the king's actual face from here, no ward-glass, no distance, imagine.

"Crowns remember everything," Aldric said. "That was always their purpose. To hold what a person cannot hold alone. But I have learned—" and here his voice caught, just slightly, and Sable thought of a garden beneath a palace, fed on a man's fading self, alive because he was dying—"that some things should be allowed to be forgotten. Or given away. Or simply *carried*, badly, by the people who own them. I am not fit to hold nine centuries. I was barely fit to hold myself." He set the circlet of office—it was a plain thing now, iron and river-stone, the crown itself having dissolved into starlight over the palace lawn while half the city watched and the other half slept through the second miracle of the season—on the table before the council delegates. "I return the Court to you. All of you. Not to one. Never again to one."

The hall was silent long enough that Sable heard a pigeon somewhere in the rafters rearranging itself.

Then an old woman in the council delegation—a ferry-warden from the eastern districts, Sable later learned, someone who had spent forty years counting barges and settling disputes about mooring fees—stood up and said, "Well. About time somebody said it out loud," and the hall broke into noise, and that was that. History, Sable reflected, was mostly logistics with better lighting.

Afterward, in the corridor outside, Aldric found her.

He looked better than he had in the garden. That was the strange arithmetic of it: he had lost nearly everything—the crown, the throne, the borrowed centuries—and gained back the ordinary weight of a face. There were lines around his eyes now that hadn't been there when he was bound, lines he'd earned in the weeks since, sitting through councils, signing reforms, learning the names of people whose names had never mattered to a king before.

"Sable Pemberton," he said. "Memory-thief. Liberator of kings. Terrible at accepting thanks."

"I've accepted your thanks twice."

"You've accepted my *gratitude*. What you won't accept is anything else." He smiled, and it was a good smile, unguarded, the smile of the boy in the locket-memory who had once planted a garden because he wanted something in the world that grew instead of ruled. "There's a place beside me. In whatever comes next. Not a throne—I'm done with thrones—but a chair. A chair with your name on it, if chairs can have names. People will listen to you. They already do. The thieves' guild has been telling anyone who'll stand still how you called in every debt in the city in three days."

"The guild is exaggerating."

"The guild says you threatened a smuggler baron with the memory of his own wedding."

"He *sold* that memory to pay a debt. I merely suggested he might want it back." She shrugged. "It was a professional courtesy."

"Sable." His voice gentled. "Come stand beside me. Whatever we build—councils, schools, a court that serves instead of devours—you helped make it possible. You gave up the most precious thing you had."

And there it was, the hollow where her mother's face used to live. She carried it everywhere now, that absence. It had a shape, the way a missing tooth does; her tongue kept finding it. She remembered everything else—her mother's hands, her laugh, the smell of the tinctures she brewed, the lessons, all the lessons, *never keep what you steal, except once, and look what keeping cost*—but the face itself was gone, given willingly under a penultimate new moon, cracked into the first seal of the crown like a key turning the wrong way on purpose.

"I didn't give it up so I could be paid in chairs," Sable said.

"No," Aldric agreed. "You didn't." He studied her for a moment, and then, being the man Mag's last words had promised—*the king remembers being kind*—he let it go. "The offer doesn't expire. Chairs are patient."

"Chairs," Sable said, "are also stationary. Which is the problem."

* * *

Hollowmere was rebuilding the way wounds rebuild: unevenly, with scabbing and argument.

Sable went back in the spring, when the burned farms had put up their new timber frames and the valley smelled like sawdust and thawing earth. The border stones still stood, but they were quiet now—not humming, not feeding, not holding anything in or out. Wren had examined them for two full days and pronounced them "honest masonry," which Sable chose to take as a compliment.

They buried Mag properly this time. The riders' pyre had been hasty, desperate, done under arrow-fire while the valley burned; now there was a stone by the ferry crossing, and Dain had carved it himself, badly, with a soldier's lettering: *MAG. HE FED THE WARDS NOTHING BUT LIES AT THE END, AND IT WAS ENOUGH.* Wren had objected that the epitaph was confusing. Dain had said Mag would have loved it. Sable had sided with Dain, and Wren had sulked about it for a week, and that, too, felt like rebuilding.

The valley did not know what to make of her. Some of them remembered the riders asking after the daughter of Lys. Some of them remembered the fires. A few—the children, mostly—had heard the stories coming back up the road from Virelay, garbled and glorious, about the woman who stole memories and broke a crown and made the stars come home.

"You could correct them," Dain said, watching a pack of those children trail her down the market lane at a respectful distance, whispering.

"I could."

"You won't."

"It's a good story," Sable said. "Why ruin it? Besides—" she nodded at the half-built inn, the new ferry rope, the surveyor's stakes marking where the schoolhouse would go, "—they need it. Hope is the one thing I never had to steal from anybody. Turns out it grows wild here."

Dain grunted. He had come with her without being asked, which was very like him; he had resigned his commission in a letter of four sentences, the last of which read, *I find I prefer oaths I choose to oaths I was given,* and then he had packed one bag and appeared at the city gate the morning they left, as though he'd always intended to be there and was mildly annoyed nobody had told him the departure time.

"What will you do here?" she asked him. "Guard a valley with no wards and no enemies?"

"Rebuild the guard," Dain said, as if it were obvious. "Somebody has to teach these farm boys which end of a spear to hold. And somebody has to keep an eye on you."

"I don't need—"

"Nobody needs guarding until suddenly they do," Dain said. "I learned that from watching a king eat himself. Ask me sometime. It's a riveting lecture." He paused, then added, quieter, in the tone he used for things that cost him: "Also, Hollowmere fed me when I was nothing but a deserter with a bruised conscience. I owe the valley. Ferroses pay their debts."

* * *

Wren stayed in Virelay longer than the rest of them. There were charts to redraw—the old charts were useless now, monuments to a stolen sky—and a new observatory to argue into existence, and a council subcommittee on celestial restoration that he described as "eleven people who believe stars are a budgetary matter." He wrote letters. Long ones. Sable answered them shorter.

*The Pleiades returned Tuesday,* one letter began. *All seven. I checked twice, then a third time, then Dain would say I checked a fourth time but Dain exaggerates. The city held a festival. Someone sold star-shaped pastries. I bought nine and regretted nothing. How is the schoolhouse? Have you taught any children to extract a memory yet, and if so, have you taught them the rule?*

Sable wrote back: *Twelve students. Ages eight to fourteen. Mostly orphans of the burning, a couple of runaways from Virelay who followed the story up the road. Yes, I taught them the rule. First lesson, every time: never keep what you steal. Second lesson: some things aren't stealing if they're given. Third lesson: know the difference, or don't do the work at all.*

*And the fourth lesson?* Wren wrote.

*Fourth lesson is mine,* she replied. *I'm still learning it.*

Because here was the truth she was slowest to learn: the absence did not close. She had thought—she had *hoped*, in the secret ledger where she kept her hopes, the same ledger where she'd once recorded *one memory kept, against all rules*—that giving the face away freely would be different from losing it. That sacrifice would transmute into peace. And sometimes it did. On good days the hollow felt like a gift she'd chosen, a door she'd opened on purpose, and she could stand in front of her students and tell them about her mother without her chest seizing.

On bad days she woke reaching for a face she could no longer see, and lay in the gray light doing the arithmetic of grief: *I traded her face for a king's freedom, for the stars, for all of it, and I would do it again, and I miss her, and both of these are true, and neither one cancels the other.*

Mag's granddaughter, a sharp-eyed girl of ten named Tansy who had appointed herself Sable's unofficial apprentice, caught her on one of the bad days. Sable was sitting on the ferry landing with her boots off, feet in the cold river, staring at nothing.

Tansy sat down beside her without asking. She was good at that. She'd inherited Mag's talent for silence.

"My gran says you gave something away," Tansy said eventually. "Something big. Nobody knows what."

"Your gran talks too much."

"She says everybody gives something away eventually. She says the trick isn't keeping it. The trick is deciding what it's *for*." Tansy swung her legs. "Did it work? Whatever it was for?"

Sable looked at the river. At the far hills, where the border stones stood honest and quiet. At the sky, which even in daylight seemed thicker now, richer, as though the blue itself had been fed.

"Yeah," she said. "It worked."

"That's the whole answer?"

"That's the whole answer." Then, because Tansy was watching her with Mag's patience and Mag's eyes, Sable added, "Mostly. It works mostly. Some days it works less."

Tansy considered this with the gravity of a judge. "Gran says that's called being a person."

"Your gran," Sable said, "should write a book."

* * *

Aldric came to Hollowmere in autumn, traveling without escort, wearing a coat that any merchant in Virelay might have owned. He arrived at the schoolhouse on a market day, and the children swarmed him before Sable could intervene—they'd heard the stories too, improved versions in which Sable featured larger and the king featured as a sort of rescued dragon—and he bore it with the startled delight of a man who had spent nine centuries being bowed to and had discovered, late, that being climbed on was better.

"You look well," Sable said, when the children had been bribed away with the promise of a memory-craft demonstration.

"I look tired," Aldric corrected. "Councils are worse than crowns. At least a crown only ate *me*." He turned, taking in the schoolhouse—the new roof, the practice-vials on their shelves, the wall where the students' first careful extractions hung in labeled vials like a garden of bottled afternoons. "You built this."

"We built this. Half the guild sent supplies. Wren sends books. Dain drills the older ones in self-defense on Thursdays, which he insists are unlucky, which is why he chose them."

"And you?" Aldric asked. "Are you well?"

It was the question everyone asked and nobody wanted answered honestly. She considered lying to him—he was, after all, a king, or had been, and kings were owed courtesy—but he had renounced that too, and besides, he had seen her at her worst, in a garden beneath a palace, holding a locket and shaking.

"Some days it works less," she said.

He nodded slowly. "I know the arithmetic. I spent a century subtracting myself and calling it duty." He reached into his coat and produced a letter, sealed with plain wax. "This is why I came. Not the council business—though there is council business, there is always council business, the council breeds business like wet wood breeds mushrooms. This."

She took it. Her name was on the front in his handwriting: *Sable Pemberton, who keeps one thing and gives away everything else.*

"Aldric—"

"It's not a chair," he said quickly. "I learned. It's not an offer, either, or a summons, or anything with weight attached. Read it or don't. Burn it if you like. But I wanted you to have it while the ink was fresh, because—" and here the ex-king of the Ninefold Court, the man whose childhood she had carried in a locket across a burning valley, looked suddenly and completely uncertain— "because I remember what it was to have something precious kept for me by someone else. My mother did it. Kept memories for me, in case the crown took everything. It's the only reason any of me survived to be freed." He stepped back. "So. Consider it a mother's trick, passed down the line. From Lys, to you, to—well. To whoever needs it next."

He left the next morning, before dawn, because kings-turned-councilmen apparently rose early out of habit even when there was nothing to reign over. Sable stood in the schoolhouse doorway and watched him walk down the ferry road until the mist took him, and then she broke the seal.

The letter was short. She read it twice. Then she sat down on the schoolhouse step, in the cold gold light of an autumn morning with a fat handful of stars still visible above the western ridge, and laughed until she cried, and cried until she laughed, and finally read it a third time, aloud, to the empty yard:

*Sable —*

*I have no right to ask, and I am asking anyway. The council has voted to fund a network of memory-craft schools — ethical ones, yours as the model — and they want someone to oversee the founding of the next. There is one planned for Virelay, in the Lantern District, where the poor sold their remembering to eat. Those memories are archived in the palace vaults. Every one of them. The council lacks the courage to open the vaults. You lack many things, Sable, but I have never once seen you lack courage.*

*Come teach us how to give people back what was taken from them.*

*Come — and stay as long as you like, and leave whenever you choose, and sit in whatever chair pleases you, or stand, or pace, or steal my pen, which I expect you will anyway.*

*There is no deadline. There is only an open door, and a man who remembers being kind, trying to be it on purpose this time.*

*— A.*

*P.S. Wren says the Lyre completes itself next month. He wants everyone he loves in Virelay to see it. I told him I'd pass that along. Consider it passed along.*

Sable folded the letter carefully and put it in her pocket, next to the shape of the hollow that no longer ached quite so much on good days.

From inside the schoolhouse, twelve voices were arguing about whether a memory of rain could legally be extracted from someone who'd only ever *heard* about rain, and Dain was bellowing that Thursdays were for spearwork, and Tansy was winning the argument, whichever argument it was, because Tansy always won.

Sable stood up. She brushed off her skirt. She looked west, where the last few morning stars were dissolving into a sky that filled a little more every day, one by one, patient as spring.

"Well," she said to no one, to the valley, to a mother whose face she could not picture but whose lessons she remembered perfectly. "Looks like we're going to need a bigger school."

She went inside to tell the children they'd be learning a new lesson soon: how to give back.
