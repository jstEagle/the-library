## 22. The Garden of the Ninefold Court Anew

The roses had opinions.

Sable had learned this over the course of a year spent coaxing the garden back from its long enchantment: that the white ones by the eastern wall bloomed stubborn and late, as if they distrusted the sun on principle; that the blue ones near the fountain—blue, an impossibility she'd stopped questioning around midwinter—opened only for children's laughter and closed like offended aristocrats at the first raised voice. And that the old climbing rose over the ruined arch, the one Aldric remembered his mother planting before he could remember anything else, flowered exactly once a year, on no schedule any gardener could name, and always on the morning after Sable dreamed of her mother.

She was checking for buds when the first students arrived.

They came through the Ninth Gate now, which stood open. That still caught in her throat sometimes—the gate itself, iron and nine-pointed, unbarred, with a bored guard named Tamsin who spent most of her shift teaching sparrows to take crumbs from her boot. A year ago the gate had been sealed behind wards fed on fear. Now it was a door children pushed open because they were late for lessons.

"Madam Underhill!" The shout came from Pell, who was eight and built entirely of elbows and urgency. He skidded through the rose bed without apology, scattering two indignant white blooms' worth of petals. "Madam Underhill, Marisol did it again."

"Did what again?"

"She *took* my memory." He said it the way other children said *she hit me*, with the particular outrage of the wronged. "The one about my dog. She just *looked* at me funny and then I couldn't remember what color he was!"

Sable crouched to his level, brushing soil from her knees. Behind him, Marisol stood at the garden's edge with the studied blankness of a child who knows precisely what she has done and is calculating whether confession or defiance will cost less.

"Marisol," Sable called. "Come here, love."

Marisol came, dragging her feet through the mint—which, Sable noted, would smell magnificent for days now, so perhaps some good would come of this.

"Did you take Pell's memory?"

"He *dared* me," Marisol said. "He said memory-craft wasn't real, it was just stories, so I showed him."

"And how did it feel? Taking it?"

Marisol's chin came up, but her eyes wavered. This was the part that mattered. Not the technique—technique could be taught, though Sable taught it slowly, with more warnings than lessons—but the *feeling*. What it cost. What it left in you afterward, that faint cold smudge where someone else's joy had been.

"It felt..." Marisol frowned, searching. "Warm. But then it felt like I'd eaten something that wasn't mine."

"There it is," Sable said. "That second feeling. You hold onto that one. The first feeling is why people become thieves. The second is why they should stop." She held out her hand. "Now give it back. Gently. Memories bruise."

Marisol cupped her hands around something invisible, breathed out, and pressed her palms to Pell's temples. His face went through a small storm of expressions—confusion, recognition, delight.

"BROWN!" he shouted. "He was brown! With one ear that—" He stopped, looked at Marisol, and thrust out a grubby hand. "Sorry I dared you. Do you want to see where he's buried? Under the plum tree at home."

And just like that, war became friendship, as wars between eight-year-olds do, and they ran off together toward the plum-tree end of the garden, leaving Sable kneeling in the mint with dirt on her hands and something loose and warm in her chest.

A year ago she had made her living doing exactly what Marisol had done, except without giving anything back, and with considerably better technique, and with the cold smudge worn so deep into her she'd mistaken it for her own shape.

*Never keep what you steal.* Her rule. Her one rule, and she'd broken it exactly once, keeping her mother's face folded away against her heart like contraband—and in the end she'd given even that away, willingly, under a penultimate new moon, to crack the first seal of a crown that had been eating a king alive.

She could not recall her mother's face.

She had made her peace with it the way you make peace with weather—not gladly, but finally. What remained was everything else: the sound of Lys Pemberton humming while she worked, off-key and unbothered; the way she'd said *check your exits twice, entrances once*; the weight of her hand on Sable's head, heavy as a blessing. The face was gone. The love had stayed. It turned out the love was the part that had ever mattered, though it had taken her twenty-some years and the loss of every star in the sky to learn it.

She stood, dusted her knees, and went to see whether the blue roses had forgiven yesterday's argument between two students over whose turn it was with the telescope Wren had donated.

* * *

Wren Calloway arrived at midmorning, as he did three days a week, carrying his charts under one arm and an expression of aggrieved wonder under his hat, which was the same expression he'd worn since the stars began coming back.

"Seventeen new ones," he announced, dropping onto the garden bench hard enough to make it complain. "Counted them last night. Seventeen. The Serpent's whole tail is back, and half the Lyre, and there's a star in the Hunter's left knee that I have *searched for my entire professional life* and it simply wasn't there, and now it is, and I intend to be insufferable about it."

"You're already insufferable about it."

"I'm being *restrained* about it. You should hear me at the Observatory. Dain threatened to confiscate my charts last week." He flipped one open across his knees—a great inked wheel of constellations, holes punched where stars had gone out, and lately, small gold dots penciled in the margins where they'd returned. "Do you know what it's like, being right about the end of the world and then having to help un-end it? No one warns you about the paperwork."

"How is Dain?" Sable asked, though she'd seen him two days ago. She asked anyway, because Wren liked answering.

"Gloriously miserable. They gave him a title—*Warden of the Restored Watch*, something absurd—and he hates it, which means it fits. He spends his days retraining the guard to guard things instead of obeying things. Half of them quit. The other half are better men than they were." Wren tapped the chart. "He asked me to tell you the council vote went through. The garden charter. It's official. This place is yours—ours—in perpetuity, whatever perpetuity means to a council that can't agree on bread prices."

Sable looked out over the garden: the fountain where the water ran clear now instead of starlit and strange; the beds where students' names were carved into slate markers; the far corner where Mag's ferry lantern hung from a hook on the old wall, kept lit day and night, because some debts you pay in oil and attention rather than grief.

"The Ninefold School of Remembering," she said, trying the words again. They still fit strangely, like boots borrowed from a better woman.

"Terrible name," Wren agreed comfortably. "I voted for the Calloway Institute of Celestial Reckoning."

"You voted for that?"

"I did, and I was outvoted seven to one, which is democracy's whole flaw." He rolled up his chart and squinted at her. "You've got the look."

"What look?"

"The look you get when a letter's come and you haven't opened it yet. Your left eyebrow does a thing."

"My eyebrow does nothing."

"Your eyebrow has been doing things since the day I met you. Where is it? The letter?"

Sable hesitated, then drew it from her apron pocket. It had come at dawn with the milk cart, sealed with plain wax—no crown, no sigil, just a pressed violet in the seal, because Aldric had never once used the royal seal since abdicating, as if he considered it someone else's signature now.

It was the fourth letter this year. She had answered all of them. She had opened none of them immediately, because opening them meant sitting very still for a while afterward, and the garden required a mobile headmistress.

"From the ex-king," Wren said knowingly. "How is the ex-king?"

"Tending orchards somewhere in the north. Teaching farmers' children their letters, allegedly. Allegedly also fixing their roofs, which no former king should know how to do, which suggests either a humble heart or a suspicious past."

"Mm. And does the humble roofer know that the council keeps a chair empty at every session? Officially 'in reserve.' Unofficially everyone glances at it like it might bite."

"He knows. I've told him. He says a throne is a chair that eats its sitter, and he's done being eaten."

"Sensible man." Wren stood, brushing off his coat, and paused. "For what it's worth—and I'm an astronomer, so my worth is mostly arithmetic—I don't think he writes those letters for the council's benefit."

He left her alone with the letter and the roses, which was, Sable suspected, exactly the sort of exit Wren practiced in front of mirrors.

* * *

She opened it at noon, sitting on the fountain's rim with her boots in the runnel where the overflow ran, because her mother had taught her that important things should be read slightly uncomfortably, so you didn't get too comfortable with them.

*Sable,*

*The northern orchards are in blossom, which the farmers tell me happens every year and which I find outrageous every year, as if the world were showing off. I have fixed four roofs. I have been bitten by a goat. I mention the goat because you once told me you judge correspondents by their honesty about livestock.*

*The council sent again. They are kind, and persistent, and I have declined again, politely, with the goat bite as evidence that I am unfit for ceremonial duties. But I find myself thinking less about thrones than about gardens—specifically yours, specifically whether the arch-rose bloomed, and specifically whether you have yet told your students the story of the night the stars went out, or whether you are saving it, as you save everything, until the telling will do the most good.*

*I have been remembering lately—not the crown's hoard, which is gone, dissolved, thank every star that came home, but my own. Small things. My mother's hands in the soil of that garden. The sound the gate made before anyone thought to bar it. I lost nearly all of it, and got some of it back, and learned the difference between a memory that is kept and a memory that is carried. You taught me that distinction, though I suspect you learned it the hard way, from a woman who hummed off-key.*

*Here is the thing I have been circling for eleven months, the way I used to circle decisions I was afraid of, back when my decisions fed a crown:*

*I am not writing to offer you a throne. I know what you'd say. I've heard you say it, at length, with citations.*

*I am writing to ask if the school takes apprentices too old for lessons. There is a valley east of here rebuilding itself, and I am told the ferry needs a new keeper, and I am told the keeper's cottage has room for two, and I am told—by a disgraced astronomer who cannot keep a secret, which is his single greatest virtue—that the headmistress of the Ninefold School sometimes stands at her window on clear nights, counting stars, and looks east.*

*Come count them with me. Or don't, and I'll keep writing letters, and I'll keep fixing roofs, and I'll keep being bitten by goats, and we'll both grow old in separate gardens full of things we planted. That would be a fine life too. But I think—you taught me to check my exits twice, so I have checked this one—it would be a finer one together.*

*A.*

P.S. The goat's name is Duchess. She has no respect for rank, which is another point in her favor.

Sable read it twice. Then she sat very still, as she'd known she would, while the fountain murmured and the blue roses opened one by one along the south wall, and somewhere in the garden Pell and Marisol shrieked with laughter over a dog long dead and a plum tree in bloom.

Her mother's face she could not summon. She tried, gently, the way you press a bruise to check its healing—and found, as always, the soft blank space, the missing pane. But beneath it, everywhere, everything else: *check your exits twice.* *Never keep what you steal.* *What you give away is not lost, little thief; it's only moved to where it can do some good.*

She laughed aloud, alone, at the fountain, because her mother had apparently known about this moment too—or would have claimed to, with that crooked smile Sable could no longer see but could, somehow, still feel the shape of.

She went inside, took a fresh sheet of paper, and wrote:

*Aldric,*

*The rose bloomed this morning. It always blooms the morning after I dream of her, which means either the garden remembers or she does, and I've stopped needing to know which.*

*The school takes apprentices of all ages. Ours are mostly eight, ill-behaved, and learning faster than I did at twice their years. One of them stole a dog's color yesterday and gave it back before lunch, which is further than I managed in a decade.*

*As for the valley: the ferry lantern is lit. The cottage roof leaks in the northeast corner, and I am told—by a disgraced astronomer who cannot keep a secret—that there is a former king in the north who fixes roofs and lies about goats.*

*Check your exits twice. Mine, it turns out, have always pointed east.*

*Come at the turn of the season. The children will want to meet the man from the stories, and I warn you now: Marisol will try to steal a memory from you, and Pell will dare her to, and you will let them both, because you are exactly that kind of fool, and it is the finest thing about you.*

*S.*

P.S. Bring the goat.

She sealed it with plain wax and a pressed violet, walked it to the Ninth Gate herself, and handed it to Tamsin, who took it with the gravity of a knight accepting a quest and immediately went back to feeding the sparrows.

Then Sable Underhill turned back up the path, through the mint and the roses and the noise of children learning to give back what they took, and began planning the autumn term—two chairs at the head table, one of them for a man who'd need reminding that a schoolroom outranked a throne, and both of them, at last, exactly where they belonged.

Above the eastern wall, early, stubborn, the evening's first star came out.

Sable counted it, smiled, and went in to teach.
