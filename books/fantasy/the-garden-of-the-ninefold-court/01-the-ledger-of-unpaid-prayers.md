## 1. The Ledger of Unpaid Prayers

There are three rules to keeping the ledgers of the Ninefold Court's memory-guild, and I break all of them before breakfast.

Rule one: never read what you catalogue. Rule two: never wonder about your own file. Rule three: never, under any circumstances, fall in love with a debt.

I was breaking rule one when I found the prayers.

The audit was routine — quarter-end reconciliation, the guild's way of making sure every stolen memory bought, sold, or pawned in the last ninety days had been properly entered, priced, and taxed. My desk sits in the counting room beneath the Sable Exchange, where the light comes down through grates in the street above and lands on the ledgers like it's checking up on them. Nine hundred volumes. Forty years of other people's pasts, filed by season and sin.

I had reached volume six hundred and twelve — autumn, twenty years back — when the handwriting changed.

Not mine. Not anyone's, really. It was the handwriting of someone who hadn't yet decided who to be: letters leaning left as if trying to escape the page, loops too big for their words, an *i* dotted with a little circle instead of a point because some child somewhere had been told that was how you showed you were paying attention.

*Please let Mama stop crying at night,* the entry began. Then, in the margin, in the clerk's dry hand: *Received. One (1) memory of mother weeping, extracted, condition good. Credited.*

*Received.* Answered. A prayer to a dead god, logged like an invoice.

I turned the page. Another prayer. *Please let me forget the river.* Received. One (1) memory of drowning, extracted, condition fragile. And another, and another — eleven of them across three volumes, each addressed to gods whose names had been struck from the rolls before I was born, each one answered by the guild's machinery as if the dead gods had simply forwarded their mail.

And every single one signed with that same childish hand. The circled *i*. The leftward lean.

My hand. My childhood handwriting, which I have never seen, because I have no childhood to compare it to.

Here is what the guild tells its keepers: you don't need a past to do this work. You need arithmetic, discretion, and a horror of sentiment. They took my memories when I was small — or rather, they tell me they did, though even that is secondhand, a note in my own personnel file reading *collateral, see founding instruments* — and they gave me a name, a wage, and a cot behind the counting room. Ines Okonkwo. Keeper of Ledgers. A woman made entirely of present tense.

I sat there with volume six hundred and twelve open on my knee and felt something I can only describe as being read. Not reading. Being read. As if the book had looked up from itself and recognized me, the way you recognize a face across a market crowd — not the features but the gait, the particular way a person carries their own weight.

The books know me better than I know myself. That is not a figure of speech. It is the entire problem.

* *

Tomas Ferreira came at the hour when the exchange empties and the lamps are trimmed, which is when desperate people come, having spent the whole day rehearsing and losing their nerve and rehearsing again.

He was a cooper by trade, broad through the shoulders, with the kind of hands that look wrong holding anything smaller than a barrel stave. He set those hands flat on my counter like he was steadying himself against weather.

"I want to unsell something," he said.

"Memories aren't returnable," I said. "It's in your contract. Clause nine."

"Not a memory." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "My soul."

I should have laughed. The guild doesn't traffic in souls; souls are below our floor, so to speak — the province of the court beneath the mountain, which buys them the way we buy pasts, in lots, at discount, from people who need coin more than eternity. We keep records of those transactions only because the court pays us a filing fee, and the court always pays its fees, which is more than I can say for the living.

"Tomas," I said carefully, "if you sold your soul to the court, no one in this building can help you. Least of all me."

"You can read the books." He leaned across the counter, and up close his eyes were wet and furious and terrified, the eyes of a man who has done the arithmetic of his own remaining time. "My daughter's sick. Was sick. Three years ago, winter fever, the physicians wanted payment I didn't have, and a man came — gray coat, gray voice — and offered me a price. Said the court beneath the mountain would hold my soul as surety until I repaid. Said it was just paper. Just a formality." His jaw worked. "She lived. She's eleven now. And yesterday a letter came under my door with no stamp and no signature, just a date. The dark moon. Twenty-two days from now."

I pulled the court's subsidiary ledger — the one we keep out of professional courtesy, bound in gray leather that never warms in your hands — and ran his name.

It was all there. Tomas Ferreira, cooper, of Copperlane Ward. Soul, one (1), sold under instrument 4471, value assessed at two hundred crowns against a loan of two hundred crowns, interest compounding quarterly. And there, in the column where clerks note satisfaction or default, the entry that ended everything:

*Balance outstanding: 214 crowns, 6 shillings. Collection due: dark moon.*

"It's valid," I said, and hated how steady my voice came out. "Fully executed. Witnessed. There's no defect I can find. If there were a defect — a missing witness, a misdated seal, anything — the contract would be void, and the court, bound by its own bookkeeping, would have to release you. But it's clean. Whoever drew it up knew their work."

He stared at me. "Then forge a defect."

"That's—"

"I've seen what you do here." He wasn't begging anymore; he was accusing. "You move other people's yesterdays around like furniture. You're telling me one line in one book is going to take me down the mountain in twenty-two days, and you won't move it?"

I told him the truth, which is the least useful thing a ledger-keeper can offer: that forging the court's records was a hanging offense in three jurisdictions and a soul-forfeit everywhere else; that the court's books were kept by something that had been doing arithmetic since before arithmetic had a name; that no forgery of mine would survive its reading.

He left without another word. At the door he stopped, and didn't turn around, and said, "You found my contract fast enough. Like you already knew where it was."

I hadn't noticed that. Now I'll never unnotice it.

* *

That night I went back to volume six hundred and twelve, and then to the others, and then — breaking rule two with both hands — to the restricted shelf, where the founding instruments live under lock and dust.

The guild's charter is a strange document. Everyone knows the story they tell apprentices: a wise founder, a generous endowment, a guild built to protect the city's memories from predation. What the charter actually says is that the founder borrowed heavily from the court beneath the mountain to build the vaults, and put up collateral. Not gold. Collateral of a personal nature. The clause reads: *the debtor's line, in perpetuity, firstborn of firstborn, their pasts held in surety against the principal.*

Firstborn of firstborn. I am the founder's great-great-granddaughter. Nobody ever says so. Nobody has ever said so to me. But the personnel note — *collateral, see founding instruments* — suddenly had teeth, and the teeth were shaped exactly like my own mouth.

Eleven prayers, answered. Eleven entries in the guild's own books, signed by a child who prayed to dead gods because the living ones weren't taking calls. Each prayer granted by extraction — the grief removed, the fear removed, the memory of the river removed — and each one credited, paid into an account I have never seen, against a debt I was never told I carried.

They didn't steal my childhood. I sold it. Or rather, I paid with it, installment by installment, tear by tear, a child settling her family's account the only currency she had. The books know me better than I know myself because the books *are* me — the part of me the mountain holds.

Twenty-one days now until the dark moon. Twenty-one days until they come for Tomas Ferreira's soul, and I sit here with ink on my fingers and a forged defect in my head already half-drawn, knowing it will fail, knowing the thing beneath the mountain reads handwriting the way priests read scripture.

But here is what I know that the god does not: I learned my letters twice. Once as a child, praying to the dead. And once as a keeper, trained to write in a hand so neutral no one could trace it. Two hands. Two women. And the court has only ever met one of us.

Tomorrow I go down through the market-tunnels. Tomorrow I find out what the mountain owes me.

Tonight, I practice my childhood signature until it stops shaking.
