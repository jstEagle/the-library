## 2. What the Mountain Owes

The market-tunnels beneath Salt Row open at three in the morning, which is the only honest hour in this city, because at three in the morning even the liars are too tired to lie.

I came down the stairs behind the tallow-chandler's with a satchel of memories I had no right to sell and a speech rehearsed for the ferryman of the deep galleries. The speech was good. It had a lie in it shaped like a truth, which is the best kind. But the tunnels have their own bookkeeping, and they took one look at me and adjusted my account.

"You're the ledger-keeper," said the woman at the first toll-gate. She was mending a net of gray thread, and each knot she tied made a small sound like a word being forgotten. "We can smell the ink on you. You people never come down unless someone's soul is due."

"Passage," I said. "To the court beneath the mountain."

"Everything costs." She held out her hand, palm up, patient as arithmetic.

I'd prepared for this. In my satchel, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp, were six memories I'd lifted from the guild's unclaimed shelf — the ones nobody ever comes back for. A fisherman's last summer. A wedding that ended better than it began. The smell of a mother's kitchen belonging to a man who had sold everything else about her and kept only that. Small things. Orphaned things. The guild calls them *residuals*; I call them what they are, which is grief in storage.

I gave her the wedding. She held it to her ear like a shell, listened to whatever weddings sound like when they're being remembered by no one, and nodded once. The gate opened.

Three more gates. Three more memories. By the fourth toll I was carrying a satchel of air and a headache like a debt collector's knock, and the tunnels had gone from brick to something older — stone that sweated, walls veined with salt that glittered when my lamp passed over it, as if the mountain itself were trying to remember light.

At the fifth gate there was no keeper. There was only a door of black wood, and on the door, in handwriting I knew better than my own face, a single line:

**PAID IN FULL — PENDING**

"That's not how accounting works," I told the door.

The door agreed to disagree by opening.

* * *

The court beneath the mountain is not what the stories say. The stories say gold, say thrones, say a god on a seat of bone counting souls like coins. The truth is worse and kinder: it is an office. Vast, yes, and lit by veins of luminous salt, but it is shelves, and ledgers, and clerks — pale, patient figures who move through the aisles filing the pasts of the living. It is a library where every book is a person's yesterdays, and every book has been purchased, which means every book belongs here and not to the person who lived it.

I hate that I understood it immediately. I hate that some part of me felt *at home*.

A clerk led me down an aisle of contracts older than the guild, older than the city, their vellum gone soft as skin. At the end of the aisle stood a figure made entirely of the wrong kind of quiet — tall, robed in gray that moved like tide, its face a smooth expanse of salt crystal that caught my lamplight and held it, refracted, so that I seemed to stand inside my own reflection.

Vessel-of-Salt. The god who bought the mountain's debts.

"Ledger-keeper of the Ninefold Court," it said. Its voice was the sound of water finding its level. "You've come about the Okonkwo clause."

"I've come about Tomas Ferreiro. His soul-sale matures at dark moon. Four days."

"We know his name. We wrote it ourselves, in the manner of writing." The god tilted its head, and somewhere in the shelves above us, a thousand pages turned at once. "But you know why you came. Shall we do the accounting?"

It opened a ledger on a lectern between us — black binding, salt-crusted, and on the first page, in ink gone brown with age, a contract I had read a hundred times in the guild archive without ever reading it. The founding bargain. The one that gave the Ninefold Court its charter, its authority, its right to buy and sell the pasts of the living.

Except now, standing under the mountain, I saw the line I had never seen before. Collateral. Security against default. Not gold. Not land.

*A child's memory. Complete. To be held until claimed.*

Dated ninety-one years ago. Signed by the guild's founder, Aldric Vane, whose portrait hangs in our entrance hall looking wise and mildly disappointed in everyone.

And beside the signature, in a child's careful hand — the hand I had seen on those impossible prayer-entries just two nights ago — a name.

Mine.

"You," I said. My voice did something embarrassing. "That's not possible. I wasn't born ninety-one years ago."

"No," the god agreed, with the gentleness of a tide going out. "You were born once, long ago, in a body that failed. And again. And again. You are the interest, ledger-keeper. Compounded across lifetimes. Each time you are born, your childhood ripens, and each time, we collect it — per the terms. Your founder needed our capital to build his guild. He offered the one thing he could keep offering forever: the memory of a girl who writes prayers to dead gods, because she cannot stop believing they might answer."

The floor did not move. The mountain did not groan. Everything stayed exactly where it was, which is how I knew it was true. Catastrophes make noise. Truth just sits there, balanced, like a column that finally adds up.

All those years of feeling like a house with one room bricked over. All those nights auditing other people's pasts, pressing my palm flat against the ledger pages as if the ink might seep up into me. I hadn't been poor in memory by accident or misfortune. I had been *collateral*. An entry. A line item renewed across generations, and I had spent my whole life keeping the books of my own foreclosure.

"Why show me?" I asked. "Why now?"

"Because the contract nears its end. The founder's line is extinct; the guild will fail within a decade; and when it fails, all collateral reverts." The god's salt face caught my lamplight and scattered it. "Or is redeemed. There are two ways a debt closes, Ines Okonkwo. Default, or payment."

"And Tomas Ferreiro?"

"Is due at dark moon. His soul was pledged against a debt he could not pay, and the debt has come due, and we are not cruel but we are *thorough*." The god folded its hands, and the gesture was so human it hurt. "However. We find ourselves — interested. You wrote prayers to us, child. Ninety-one years of them, in every life, addressed to gods who were already dead, and every one landed here, and every one we answered, because someone had to. Do you understand what that makes you? Not our property. Our *correspondent*."

Somewhere in the shelves, softly, a page turned toward us, listening.

"So here is the trade, offered freely, no trick in the terms — check my arithmetic, you're qualified. Walk away tonight, and at dark moon we collect the Ferreiro soul, and the guild fails, and the collateral reverts to you entire. Every stolen year. Your mother's voice. Whatever was done to you at seven, and whatever was done before that, back through every life, all of it returned, paid in full, yours." A pause, tidal. "Or stay. Trade the claim. Give us your past — freely, finally, as a gift instead of a seizure — and the Ferreiro boy walks out whole, and the guild limps on, and you remain exactly what you have always been: a woman with a hole where her childhood should be, keeping everyone else's."

The tunnel air tasted of salt and old paper. Somewhere far above, I imagined Tomas asleep, dreaming whatever a man dreams four days before his soul gets called in — probably nothing. Probably he slept fine. That was somehow the worst part.

I thought about my mother's voice. I don't have it; I want you to understand that. I have the *fact* of it, the way you have the fact of a country you've never visited. And it was being offered to me. All of it. The whole bricked-over room thrown open, every year handed back, the ache of belonging answered at last — I would know why I flinch at bells, why I can't sleep under open windows, why the name *Ines* feels like a coat borrowed from someone taller.

And I would get it by letting a man be unmade. By choosing my yesterdays over his tomorrows. By doing, in the end, exactly what the guild does — putting a price on a soul and paying it.

"I need to think," I said.

"Of course." The god inclined its head. "You have until dark moon. But Ines—" and the lamplight bent strangely in the salt of its face, and for one moment I would have sworn the thing looked almost sorry— "be careful what you audit. Some accounts, once opened, must be closed by hand."

Behind me, the black door began, very quietly, to swing shut.

* * *

Interlude — recorded in the margin of the mountain's master ledger, in handwriting the clerks pretend not to recognize:

She counted her steps on the way out. Forty-one gates, forty-one small surrenders, and she apologized to the toll-keepers, every one, as though the memories were guests she was delivering late. This is the trouble with correspondents. They write to you for ninety-one years and then they arrive in person, and you discover that what you loved was the letters.

The Ferreiro soul screams nicely when it's drawn. Most do. We do not enjoy it. Enjoyment is for gods with smaller ledgers.

But here is the entry I did not post, and will not: when I named the terms, she looked at the shelf where her own childhood sleeps — third aisle, salt-lit, the spine worn soft from my clerks rereading it — and she did not look at it the way owners look. She looked at it the way a librarian looks at a book that has been misfiled for a century. Not *mine*, her face said. *Wrongly shelved.*

There is a difference. I have kept accounts since before the mountain had a name, and I am telling you: there is a difference.

Dark moon in four days. Let us see which of us the books betray.
