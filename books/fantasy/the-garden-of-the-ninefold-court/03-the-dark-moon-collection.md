## 3. The Dark Moon Collection

The dark moon rose over the mountain like a hole cut in the sky, and I climbed toward it with two forged contracts in my coat and a lie in every pocket.

Let me be honest about the forgery, since honesty is the one currency I've never been able to counterfeit. I had spent three days practicing. Three days hunched over Tomas's contract copy in my rented room above the tallow-chandler's, reproducing the court's hand — that beautiful, terrible script where every letter leans forward as if eager to be paid. The Ninefold Court keeps its books the way a spider keeps a web: everything recorded, nothing forgiven. And a ledger, I told myself, is only ink. Ink can be moved. I have moved ink my whole life. It is the one prayer I know how to answer.

The trouble with stealing from a god's accounting is not the lock. The trouble is that gods do not audit their books because they expect theft; they audit them because they are lonely, and reading entries is the closest thing they have to conversation.

I knew this. I went anyway.

* * *

The collection chamber sat at the mountain's heart, and it was less a room than a throat. Salt crusted the walls in white weals. Nine lamps burned without oil, each flame a different color of owing: the green of unpaid interest, the red of principal, the grey-black of debts so old no one remembered what they'd bought. In the center stood the Ledger itself — not a book, exactly, but a slab of slate grown through with pages, breathing slowly, its spine a vein of quartz that pulsed like something sleeping.

Vessel-of-Salt was not there yet. Gods arrive on schedule; it is the one courtesy they keep.

I worked fast. My hands were steadier than they had any right to be, which frightened me more than shaking would have. I opened the Ledger to the collection rolls and found Tomas Okonji's entry glowing faintly, the way all due debts glow — a soft amber, like a lamp left burning for someone who isn't coming home.

*Tomas Okonji. One soul, whole and unencumbered, sold by the bearer for passage out of grief. Due: dark moon.*

Beside it, in an older hand, an annotation I hadn't been able to forget since I read it in the guild archives:

*Collateral held against founding debt: the childhood of the ledger-keeper's line. One memory-set, ages four to eleven. Redeemable at any time by settlement in full.*

My own past, filed between a fisherman's drowned boat and a widow's seven years of luck. Catalogued. Shelved. Collecting dust instead of interest, because no one had ever wanted it back enough to pay.

I set to work. This was the craft of it, the part I loved even now, even here: the quiet violence of making a page say something it never said. I wrote the discharge first — *Received in full settlement, this dark moon, the soul of Tomas Okonji, per prior arrangement* — matching the court's forward-leaning letters stroke by stroke. Then the second entry, the dangerous one, the one that made my chest hurt to write:

*Collateral released without condition to the bearer. The childhood of Ines Okonkwo, ages four to eleven, returned.*

Both contracts voided in a single night. Both of us walking out free. I had told myself, descending, that I would choose Tomas if forced. But I am a ledger-keeper, and ledger-keepers are greedy in a very specific way: we want the books to balance. All of them. Even ours.

I signed the discharges with the guild's countersignature seal, pressed the pages flat, and closed the Ledger.

And then, because I am also a fool with three days of practice behind her and no sense of when to stop, I checked my work.

That was my mistake. Or it was my salvation. On the dark moon, I still don't know which.

* * *

The salt began to move before the god arrived — rising off the walls in slow spirals, gathering into a shape like a woman made of tide-line and bone-white crystal, wearing the memory of a face the way a coat wears out at the elbows. Vessel-of-Salt did not walk so much as arrive incrementally, the way dawn does.

"Ledger-keeper," the god said. Its voice was the sound of a wave deciding whether to return. "You are early. Collections are not until the hour of the ebb."

"I came to reconcile the accounts," I said. "Early reconciliation is a courtesy."

"It is a confession." The god drifted past me toward the Ledger, and I felt the temperature of the room change — felt my forged entries cooling under its attention like footprints in snow. "But you knew that. You are a bookkeeper. You know that no one reconciles accounts at midnight unless the books are wrong."

I said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't a second forgery.

The god opened the Ledger. Read. And for one long, salt-hung moment, the forgery held. I watched the amber glow around Tomas's name gutter and dim. I watched the collateral clause loosen its grip on my childhood like a fist remembering how to open. It was working. It was actually working, and somewhere above us Tomas was sleeping his last night as a debtor, and I had done it, I had beaten a god with good penmanship—

Then Vessel-of-Salt went still.

Not angry-still. Reading-still. The stillness of someone who has found a familiar word in a stranger's mouth.

"This hand," the god said softly. "I know this hand."

"The guild's countersignature—"

"No." The god turned, and its face — that worn, borrowed face — looked at me with something worse than suspicion. Recognition. "Not the guild's. Yours. These loops, this pressure, the way your *e*'s close like doors being shut gently. You wrote prayers here, little keeper. Hundreds of them. Ages four to eleven." A pause, tidal and vast. "You did not sell me your memories, child. You *wrote* to me. Every night for seven years. And I answered every one, and filed every answer, and you have just tried to forge a discharge in the handwriting of my most faithful correspondent."

The floor did not move, but I felt the mountain tilt. Because I remembered — not the memories themselves, those were still locked away in the salt and slate — but the shape of the absence. The way certain words felt warm in my mouth without reason. The way I had always written my *e*'s closed, and never asked myself why, or who had taught me that a closed door could still be gentle.

"You answered them," I whispered. "The prayers. They were landing in my ledgers for years — prayers to dead gods, marked paid in full. I thought it was an error."

"There are no errors," said Vessel-of-Salt. "There is only bookkeeping. You prayed; I kept the account. That is what I am. Not a thief of children. An archivist of the desperate." The god drifted closer, and the salt-light made its borrowed face almost kind. "So here is what your forgery has purchased you, ledger-keeper: not a charge of theft. A question. Why does a child who wrote to me willingly grow into a woman who breaks in to steal herself back?"

Above us — below us, inside the stone itself — a sound began. Low at first, then rising: a voice drawn thin and long, like wire pulled through a die. Tomas. The collection hour had come, and whatever my forged discharge had loosened, the mountain's older machinery was still turning. His soul was being drawn up through the rock, screaming in a register I felt in my teeth, in my ledger-callused fingers, in the place behind my ribs where I kept the ache of a childhood I couldn't remember.

"He is due," the god said, regretful as rain. "Your forgery is discovered. The original contract stands. Unless—"

"Unless what?"

"Unless you tell me why." The god's crystal face tilted. "Give me the answer I have waited twenty years to collect. What happened to the child who wrote to me? Who sold her? And why — this is the part I cannot reconcile, Ines Okonkwo — why did she stop writing?"

Tomas screamed. The mountain drank. And I stood in the throat of the Ninefold Court holding the only thing I had ever truly owned — my ignorance — and understood at last that my handwriting had betrayed me twice: once when it gave me away, and once, long ago, when it stopped.

"I don't remember," I said. "That's the answer. Someone took the years I would have needed to answer you, and I have spent my whole life keeping other people's pasts because mine was filed away in a god's basement."

The salt went very quiet.

Then Vessel-of-Salt reached out one tide-line finger and touched the open Ledger — touched my forged entries, my stolen penmanship, my closed and gentle *e*'s — and said:

"Then let us read the file together. And you will learn, before this night ends, who sold you — and what they made you promise in exchange."

The screaming above us stopped. Not ended — paused. Held, like a breath, like a debt deferred.

The god turned the page.
