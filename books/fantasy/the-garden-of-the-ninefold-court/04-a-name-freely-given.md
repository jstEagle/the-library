## 4. A Name Freely Given

The god's offer hung in the air of the counting-room like the last coin of a long night, spinning and refusing to land.

"Your childhood," Vessel-of-Salt said again, patient as erosion. "Every year of it. The smell of your mother's kitchen. The name she called you when you scraped your knee. All of it, whole and warm, returned to you — if you walk away now. Leave the ledger open. Let the collection proceed."

Below us, through a hundred fathoms of living stone, Tomas was screaming.

I want to be honest here, since honesty is the one currency I've never been able to counterfeit: for three full heartbeats, I considered it. Three heartbeats in which I saw a small girl with ink-stained fingers learning to write her letters at a table that smelled of pepper soup, and I wanted her back so badly my teeth ached. Three heartbeats of pure, unbankable greed.

Then the screaming changed pitch, and I remembered whose soul was being drawn up through the mountain to pay for my nostalgia.

"No," I said.

The god tilted its head. It wore the shape of an old clerk tonight — it had been wearing shapes all evening, testing which one would make me flinch least — but beneath the borrowed face its voice stayed the same: dry, mineral, patient. "No? You are the first debtor in nine hundred years to refuse a refund."

"I'm not refusing a refund. I'm declining a trade." I set my hand flat on the oldest ledger in the Ninefold Court — the founding book, the one chained to its lectern by custom rather than iron, because no one in nine centuries had dared touch it otherwise. "You taught me the rules yourself, or rather, the child who wrote your prayers did. A debt entered in good faith can only be voided by the book that holds it. And this—" I tapped the cover, "—is the book that holds everything. Including me."

The god went very still. Gods are bad at stillness; they're used to time doing their waiting for them. "That page is not yours to tear."

"It isn't yours either." My voice didn't shake. I'd have paid good money for it to shake, actually — shaking would have meant I felt something other than arithmetic. "It belongs to whoever holds the account. And per your own entry, dated the winter the guild was founded, the account is held *in trust* — by the keeper of the ledgers. Which is me. Has been me since the last keeper died without an heir. You checked the succession yourself when you tried to collect on my childhood. Don't look surprised. You wrote the clause."

For a moment the mountain itself seemed to hold its breath. Somewhere far above, the dark moon pressed against the stone like a thumb on a bruise.

"You would erase every inherited debt," the god said slowly. "Nine hundred years of them. The collateral. The children. Yourself."

"And Tomas. And every soul sold beneath this mountain by people who never read what they signed." I picked up the paper knife from the god's own desk — it had offered me tea earlier, in the shape of a courteous magistrate, because apparently even gods believe hospitality softens a mark. "You know what the funny thing is? I spent six years cataloguing stolen pasts, telling myself I couldn't afford to buy mine back. Six years of envy priced by the page. And the whole time, the power to end the entire market was sitting in my job description. I just had to read the footnotes."

"The footnotes," the god repeated, "were written by me."

"Which is why nobody reads them. Bad marketing."

I turned to the founding page. The ink had gone brown as old blood, but the words stood sharp: *Let all debts contracted under this seal bind the signatory and their heirs, unto the closing of the account.* Beneath it, in handwriting I finally recognized as my own — small, careful, a child's hand practicing its letters on the most dangerous paper in the world — a single line:

*I give this freely.*

I had written prayers to this god when I was six years old. Willing ones. Happy ones. That was the horror at the bottom of everything: not that my past had been stolen, but that I had *sold* it, gladly, before I was old enough to know what a soul cost. Every prayer in my ledger, every impossible answered entry, bore my signature because I had signed myself over before I could spell *collateral*.

But *freely given* cuts both ways. That was the loophole the child left me, whether she knew it or not. A gift freely given can be refused. And a refusal, entered in the book, closes the account.

"Tomas," I said aloud, so the record would show intent. "Your debt is voided. Everyone's is. As keeper of this ledger, in trust, I close the account of the Ninefold Court, effective—"

The mountain screamed first.

Not the god — the mountain. Stone groaned and flexed around us, and somewhere below, the machinery of collection — nine hundred years of gears and contracts and hungry patience — seized against its own terms. The god lunged, and it moved fast for something wearing a clerk's body, fast enough that I only got the knife to the page because I had already torn halfway through during our conversation, because I am, above all things, a thorough auditor.

Paper ripped.

The sound was very small for what it did. It sounded like a page turning. It sounded like a door opening after a long illness.

The light in the counting-room went strange — not dark, but *unpriced*, every object suddenly worth exactly nothing and therefore exactly itself. The god stopped moving. When it spoke, the mineral patience had cracked, and underneath was something almost like relief.

"The account is closed," it said. "You understand what you've done. The court must release him. The court must release *all* of them."

"That was the idea, yes."

"You will get nothing. No memories. No name from your mother's mouth. Nothing restored."

"I know." And here is the part I'll defend to any tribunal, divine or otherwise: I smiled. "That's what makes it mine. Everything I ever get from now on, I get the honest way. Earned, or given, or not at all."

The god looked at me for a long moment. Then it did something no client, no collector, no keeper had ever reported: it bowed. Shallow, grudging, real.

"The books will remember you," it said.

"Good. That's the only afterlife I believe in."

* * *

Tomas weighed nothing coming up the mountain stairs. Grief does that — empties a person out until they're mostly echo. He wept the whole climb, and I let him, because I'd done my crying privately, into the dark, where the accounting stays clean.

Above ground, the dark moon was setting over the city, thin as a paring of rind. The guild hall was lit — every lamp, every window — because word travels fast when nine hundred years of debts die in a single night. Half the memory-brokers in the district were standing on my steps looking like men whose ledgers had caught fire, which, in a sense, they had.

Tomas stopped at the bottom of the stairs. He was a hollowed-out man, forty years sold, and he looked at me with the terrible naked gratitude of someone who owes a debt no book can hold.

"They told me your name once," he said. "In the contracts. The name you were born with. I could give it back to you—"

"Don't." Gentle, but firm. "That name was purchased. It wouldn't fit anymore anyway. Clothes bought for a child." I shrugged. "Besides, names are cheap when they're inherited. I'd rather have one that costs something."

He thought about that. Then he said, "What do I call you?"

And here's the thing I hadn't planned, the entry no ledger anticipated: he *asked*. Not claimed, not recorded, not collected. Asked — which means whatever I answered next would be the first thing anyone had ever given me freely, including me.

"Ines," I said. "Just Ines. Keeper."

"Ines." He tried it out, weighing it like a coin he suspected was gold. "Ines Okonkwo. That's not from the contracts. Where's that from?"

"Nowhere yet," I said. "You just made it up. Congratulations — it's the only name in this city with a clean provenance."

He laughed — wet, cracked, alive — and the sound went up the stairs ahead of us, into the lit hall, into the remade guild where the brokers were waiting to learn what a world without inherited debts looked like. Behind us, the mountain settled into silence, holding nothing but stone.

I walked up the steps owning neither my past nor my future, carrying a name freely given and a ledger with one blank page left.

I already know what goes on it. But that's tomorrow's audit.
