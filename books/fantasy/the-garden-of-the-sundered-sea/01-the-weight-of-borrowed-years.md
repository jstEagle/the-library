## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

The fog came off the Sundered Sea the way grief comes to a widow: slowly at first, then all at once, until you couldn't remember what the world looked like without it.

Lena Reyes had lived inside that fog for twelve years, and he had made his peace with it the way a man makes peace with an old coat—by never taking it off and pretending it was a choice.

He was on Lantern Row before the lamps were lit, which was the only honest hour Vhal Maren ever offered. The city looked almost kind in the grey half-light: gulls wheeling over the fish-market like scraps of paper thrown by a careless god, the tide-flats breathing their long salt breath beneath the pilings, and the first brave shutter clattering open somewhere above him with a sound like applause for no one.

Maren's Widow was already awake, of course. Widows always were.

She kept a stall of dried kelp and candied ginger at the mouth of Rope Walk, and every third morning she bought a charm from him, and every third morning she pretended she hadn't been waiting.

"Back again," she said, not looking up from her bundles, in the tone of a woman who has rehearsed indifference so thoroughly it has become a kind of affection.

"You say that like it's a complaint."

"It is a complaint." She straightened, wiped her hands on her apron, and gave him the look—the one all his customers eventually gave him, equal parts suspicion and hope, as though he were a physician who also happened to be a pickpocket. "You have it?"

"I have it." He patted the inner pocket of his coat, where the little glass vial sat warm against his chest. "And I'll thank you not to ask how I got it, same as always."

"Same as always," she agreed. "Which is why I keep buying."

That was the arrangement, and it was a good one, and good arrangements were rarer in Vhal Maren than dry socks.

Here is what Lena sold, for those who cared to know—and almost nobody did, which was precisely the point: memories. Not his own. Never his own. Other people's, skimmed and bottled, the way a bee takes nectar without troubling the flower overmuch. A sailor's last morning ashore. A mother's memory of her daughter's first steps. An afternoon, forty years gone, when a man named Aldous Maren—who had mended nets for a living and argued with his brother for a hobby—had stood on the seawall with his new wife and watched the fog lift off the water, and for one unguarded hour had believed, absolutely, that his life was going to be a good one.

The widow bought that hour back in pieces. She had been buying it for three years.

"This is the last of it," Lena said, holding up the vial so the pale light caught the silver suspended inside, turning slow as milk in tea. "Aldous's happiest hour. What's left of it. I've stretched it as far as stretching goes."

"How much?"

"Same as ever."

"Don't be noble, boy. You've got rent."

"I've got rent," he admitted, "but I also have a reputation, and my reputation is that I'm cheap. Guard it jealously."

She pressed coins into his palm—too many, as always—and he pushed two back, as always, and they performed this small dishonest dance with the ease of people who had done it a hundred times, because they had.

"You could charge more," she said. "Half the quarter would pay double for what you sell. Half the quarter can't sleep for remembering things wrong."

"I don't want half the quarter."

"No?" She squinted at him through the fog. "What do you want, then? Every man wants something. Even the ones who pretend to want nothing. Especially those."

It was a fair question. It deserved better than the answer he gave her, which was a shrug and a smile he'd practiced until it fit his face like a well-worn glove.

"A quiet life," he said. "And maybe some dry socks."

* * *

His shop—if you were generous, and the tax assessor occasionally was—sat above the tide-flats at the end of a walkway that groaned like an old man climbing stairs. It had one room, one window, one door with three locks (two of them decorative; the real security was that nobody remembered the shop existed), and a smell of brine and beeswax that Lena had stopped noticing years ago and would have missed desperately if it vanished.

He lit the lamp, hung his coat on its hook, and counted the widow's coins into the chipped blue jar on the shelf. Then he took inventory, because ritual was the spine of survival.

Eleven vials on the rack. He turned each one to the light.

A dockhand's memory of his father teaching him to whistle—a thin, worn thing, nearly spent. A baker's recollection of rain on a hot oven door, which sounded like nothing and sold beautifully to people who missed their mothers. A young man's single perfect wave, ridden at dawn off a coast that no longer allowed surfing, or possibly no longer existed; provenance unclear, buyer beware.

Small things. Harmless things. That was the whole architecture of his life, built on three rules he'd carved into himself so deep he sometimes suspected they predated his memory:

*Steal small.* Never take more than a thimbleful. Never take anything anyone would fight to keep. Grief, joy, love—those stayed where they belonged. He dealt in the margins. The second-best day. The pleasant hour nobody would miss until it was offered back to them, and even then only in sips.

*Sell cheap.* Rich men attracted attention, and attention in Vhal Maren came wearing grey coats and carrying ledgers. A memory-thief who charged coppers was a curiosity. One who charged gold was a criminal, and the Wardens drew a very bright line between the two—or claimed to, anyway, right up until the day they didn't.

*Never be remembered.* This was the rule above rules. His clients paid in coin and forgot his face within the week—he wove forgetting into every transaction, a little larceny tucked inside the larceny, a thief's tithe. The widow thought of him as *the charm-boy*. The dockhands called him *that fellow*. Somewhere in this city there might be four people who knew his name, and he could name three of them, and the fourth worried him on principle.

Twelve years of that. Twelve years of being a rumor with a shop.

He put the kettle on the little iron stove and listened to the tide come in beneath the floorboards, that vast patient respiration, and told himself—as he told himself every evening—that this was enough. A warm room. Work he was good at. Nobody hunting him, nobody waiting for him, nobody weeping over him.

Enough. It was a word you had to say out loud sometimes or it evaporated.

The kettle sang. He poured tea over yesterday's leaves, because today's leaves cost money, and carried the cup to the window.

Below, the flats lay exposed by the ebb, acres of grey-brown mud stitched with tidal pools that held pieces of sky. Mudlarks worked the shallows with their long rakes, hunting for whatever the sea surrendered—old coins, old bones, older secrets. Vhal Maren had been built on top of something; everyone said so, nobody said what. There had been a city here before, or a town, or a name on a map that some clerk had drawn a careful line through, and now the mud kept all of it, and the mud did not gossip.

Lena drank his weak tea and felt the particular loneliness of a man who is surrounded by people who cannot quite recall meeting him.

It wasn't bitterness. He'd checked, over the years, thoroughly, the way you probe a tooth. It was more like... standing outside a lit window on a cold night. You could see the fire. You could see the people laughing. You understood, intellectually, that doors opened. You simply couldn't locate the handle from where you stood.

He had customers who loved him, in the way you love a well. They came to him hollow and left full, and they never wondered about the well itself.

"Enough," he said aloud, to the empty room, and the empty room, being polite, said nothing back.

* * *

Three evenings later, business found him instead of the other way around, which should have been his first warning. Business never found him. He found it, gently, quietly, the way you find mushrooms.

The knock came at dusk, during the dead season—that flat grey week between Stormtide's end and the market reopening, when the whole city seemed to hold its breath and count its coins. Lena was re-corking vials by lamplight when someone knocked, twice, soft as a bird landing.

Nobody knocked. Clients came to the market stall on Rope Walk, Tuesdays and Fridays. His home address was known to exactly no one living.

He blew out the lamp—habit, reflex, the instincts of a man whose survival depended on noticing that the rules had just changed—and crossed the floorboards on the balls of his feet.

Through the door's warped panel he could see a shape. Small. Child-sized.

He opened the door on the chain.

A boy stood on the walkway. Eight years old, perhaps nine. Barefoot on planks slick with sea-spray, in the season when barefoot children lost toes. His clothes were wrong—not poor, *wrong*, cut in a style Lena had never seen, dyed a grey-green like the underside of a wave. And he was looking up at Lena with an expression no child wore in Vhal Maren: entirely unafraid. Curious, almost. As if Lena were the strange thing, which, statistically speaking, was absurd.

"Lost?" Lena asked. "There's a Warden post two streets down. They'll feed you and find your people. Go there. Quickly, before curfew."

The boy tilted his head and answered.

Not in Common. Not in Low Trade, the dockside pidgin. Not in any tongue Lena had heard in twelve years of listening to a city talk in its sleep.

The syllables came out round and ringing, old as sea-stone, and they did something impossible: they reached past the boy, past the door, into the shop behind Lena, and every vial on his rack began to hum.

Not break. *Hum.* Eleven small glass voices singing one chord, low and aching, like a tuning fork struck against a grave.

Lena's hand went cold around the chain.

Then the boy spoke again—one word this time, two syllables, soft, aimed directly at him like a key sliding toward a lock—

—and something behind Lena's ribs cracked. Not pain, exactly. More the feeling of a door in a house you've lived in all your life swinging open onto a room you have never seen, though the dust on its threshold says you walked through it every day, once.

He knew that word. He didn't know how he knew it. It sat in him like a fishhook sitting in a lakebed, and something vast and forgotten circled below, worrying the line.

"Where did you get that name?" His voice came out wrong. Rusty. Frightened.

The boy just looked at him, patient as the tide, and out in the dark beyond the walkway, far across the flats, the fog was gathering itself together in a way fog had no business doing—thickening, arranging, *paying attention*.

Somewhere in the city, a bell rang the first hour of curfew.

Lena unlatched the chain.
