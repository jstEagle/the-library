## 2. A Knock in the Dead Season

The door swung inward and the fog came in with the boy, curling around his ankles like a dog that had followed him home.

Lena had expected many things to knock on his door during Stormtide curfew. Warden patrols checking shutters. A drowning sailor. Once, memorably, a seal — a large, opinionated seal that had taken offense at his doormat and refused to leave for two days. He had not expected an eight-year-old child, barefoot on salt-slick planks, dry as a Sunday sermon despite the weather that could strip paint from a hull.

"You'd better come in," Lena said, because the alternative was leaving him on the walkway, and whatever else Lena Reyes was — thief, coward, keeper of other people's happiest hours — he was not the man who left a child standing in a storm that ate children.

The boy stepped over the threshold without hesitation, which was itself strange. Everyone hesitated at Lena's door. It was a small shop above the tide-flats, wedged between a rope-maker's storehouse and a chandlery that smelled permanently of tallow and regret. The sign out front read *CHARMS & CURIO — NO REFUNDS*, which was honest advertising, since half of what he sold wasn't charms at all but memories wearing charm's clothing: a widow's late husband laughing at his own terrible joke; a fisherman's last good morning before his knees went; a girl's first kiss, sold twice by accident and once on purpose. Small things. Harmless things. Things nobody would miss and nobody would kill for.

That was the whole architecture of his life. Steal small. Sell cheap. Never be remembered. Twelve years of it, and he had gotten so good at being forgettable that sometimes he forgot himself, which he had always assumed was the point.

The boy stood in the middle of the shop floor and dripped nothing.

"Sit down before you fall down," Lena said, dragging a stool from behind the counter. "There's bread. There's yesterday's bread, which is today's bread if you don't ask questions."

The boy sat. He did not take the bread. He looked around the shop with the unhurried attention of someone taking inventory — the shelves of glass vials, each holding a sliver of colored light like a firefly under arrest; the rack of ribbon-woven keepsakes; the locked cabinet in the back that held the things Lena never sold, including three memories he couldn't account for and one he was afraid of.

Then the boy looked at the vials, and the vials looked back.

Lena felt it happen. All forty-one of them, at once — a low harmonic hum, like wineglasses singing when a cart rattles past. The light inside each one leaned toward the boy the way candle flames lean toward a draft. One of them — the widow Marta's husband, the laughing-at-his-own-joke one — flared so bright it threw shadows, and then it *cracked*, a fine hairline fracture across the glass, weeping a single drop of golden light that fell to the floorboards and vanished.

"Stop that," Lena said.

The boy stopped looking at the vials. The humming faded. But the crack stayed, and something in Lena's chest hummed too now, a sympathetic vibration behind his sternum, like his ribs were also glassware and someone had run a wet finger around the rim.

He crouched to be eye-level, which put him close enough to see the boy properly for the first time. Dark hair cut raggedly, as if by someone who valued speed over artistry. Eyes the gray-green of shallow water over sand. Salt-crusted feet, uncut by the barnacled walkway, which should have been impossible. And around his neck, on a cord, a small wooden token — a circle with a line through it, worn smooth by thumbs.

"That's a pretty trinket," Lena said. "Where are your people?"

The boy opened his mouth and said a word.

It was not Common. It was not anything Lena had a shelf for. It moved through the shop like weather — the remaining vials shivered in their racks, the ribbons stirred though the windows were shuttered, and somewhere below, out on the flats, the tide made a sound like a held breath being released. The word was old. Not old like Grandmother Quill's stories, old like *before*. It tasted of cedar smoke and cold stars and a green country that Lena's tongue insisted it had never seen while every other part of him leaned hard against that insistence, straining toward it like a horse smelling home.

And behind his sternum, that hum *cracked* too.

What came through the crack was not a memory. Lena knew memories the way a locksmith knows locks, and this was no lock he'd ever picked. It was more like a room in a house he'd lived in all his life without knowing it existed — a door in a wall he'd painted over a dozen times, suddenly swinging open an inch, exhaling air that smelled of pine resin and rain on warm stone. In that inch of open door: a woman's voice, singing. A hand, smaller than his own, gripping his. Green hills. A word — the same word, or its sibling — spoken softly, the way you say a name you love.

Then the door slammed, and Lena was on his knees on his own floorboards with blood in his mouth where he'd bitten his tongue, and the boy was watching him with those tide-flat eyes, patient as the sea deciding whether to come in.

"What," Lena managed, "was that."

The boy said nothing. Of course he said nothing. Children who appear out of Stormtide fog speaking languages that predate the maps rarely explain themselves; it's practically a rule.

"All right." Lena hauled himself up by the counter, spat pink into the spittoon, and tried to reassemble his dignity, which lay about the shop in pieces like the widow Marta's cracked charm. "Let's establish some facts. Fact one: it's curfew, and anyone found abroad gets a warning, a fine, or a Warden's boot depending on their mood and your teeth. Fact two: you walked here barefoot through weather that kills grown men, and you're dry. Fact three—" he gestured weakly at the vials, still trembling faintly in their racks like nervous birds, "—you did *something* to my stock, and I'd very much like to know what, because I have to sell those, and 'slightly haunted' is not a feature I can charge extra for."

The boy considered him. Then, slowly, deliberately, he reached up and touched the wooden token at his throat — the circle with the line through it — and pointed at Lena.

"No," Lena said. "No, no. Whatever you think I am, I'm not that. I'm a shopkeeper. I'm barely a shopkeeper. I'm the least magical man in a city where magic gets you a bonfire with your name on it. I sell *feelings*, friend. Secondhand ones. At discount."

The boy kept pointing.

Outside, the fog pressed against the windows. It had been pressing for a while now, Lena realized — since the boy arrived, maybe longer — thick and attentive, the way fog got when something was moving inside it. Wardens used fog-lanterns on patrol. Wardens also used the fog itself, sometimes, when they wanted to listen. He crossed the room and drew the heavy storm-shutters closed, latching them, and the shop went dim and amber with lamplight, and somehow that was worse, because now the only thing between him and the listening dark was an inch of salt-bleached wood.

When he turned back, the boy had finally taken the bread. He ate the way children eat when they've been hungry long enough to stop showing it — carefully, completely, wasting nothing, watching Lena the whole time as if eating might be a trick.

"There's more," Lena said, and meant the bread, mostly.

* * *

They passed the night in a state of armed truce. Lena worked — there was always work, a charm to decant, a ribbon to weave, the delicate business of bottling a memory so it didn't sour — and the boy watched him do it with the fixed interest of a cat observing a suspicious cupboard. Twice, Lena caught himself humming. Both times it was the same tune, a slow rising-falling thing he did not know, and both times the boy's head came up sharply, eyes wide, and the vials sang their thin glass note, and Lena shut his mouth so fast he nearly bit his tongue again.

"I don't know that song," he told the boy, and then, more quietly, to himself: "I don't know that song."

This was, he was beginning to understand, going to become a refrain.

Because here was the trouble, and it sat in him growing heavier by the hour: Lena Reyes had no history. Oh, he had a *present* — twelve years of it, meticulously constructed, a life built like a smuggler's false bottom, all surface and no depth. But underneath? Nothing. No parents he could picture. No hometown. His first memory began mid-escape, age fourteen, running down an alley in a city he hadn't known the name of with a stolen memory-purse hot in his hand, and everything before that was a smooth gray wall, seamless as poured concrete, and he had spent twelve years not thinking about it, which is a full-time occupation when you get good at it.

And tonight, a child had knocked on that wall. Had said one word in a dead language, and the wall had *cracked*, and through the crack had come the smell of pine resin and the sound of a woman singing, and a hand gripping his, and —

A sister.

The thought arrived whole, unbidden, and Lena set down his tweezers very carefully because his hands were not steady.

*I had a sister.*

Did he? Did he *know* that, or had the boy put it there? Was it a memory or a wound? He searched himself the way he'd search a stolen purse — quick fingers, professional detachment — and found the shape of her everywhere and the substance of her nowhere. An absence with her outline. A silence shaped exactly like a name.

The boy was watching him again. Of course he was.

"Do you know me?" Lena asked. His voice came out small. "Before tonight. Have you seen me before?"

The boy nodded once, slow, the way you confirm something sad.

Lena waited for more. None came. He thought about the token — circle, line — and reached for it, and the boy let him lift it, and under Lena's thumb the wood was warm as skin, and the moment he touched it the whole shop resonated like a struck bell, every vial flaring, the lamp guttering, and behind his sternum that hidden door strained against its hinges, and through the gap he heard — almost heard — surf on a shore that no chart admitted to, and voices, hundreds of voices, saying one word together like a prayer or a password:

The word. That same word. The boy's first word, the one that had cracked him open.

Lena dropped the token like it had bitten him.

"It's a name," he whispered. "It's not a word, it's a *name*. Yours?" The boy shook his head. Touched the token. Touched Lena's chest, lightly, with two fingers, right over the sternum, right over the humming door.

"Not mine," Lena said slowly. "*Mine.*"

The boy smiled. It was the worst thing yet — not eerie, not uncanny, just a child's smile, relieved and tired and trusting, the smile of someone who has walked a very long way through very bad weather to find a specific door, and has found it, and the door has a crack in it, and a crack is enough. A crack lets in light. A crack lets in air.

A crack is how a door begins to open.

Above the tide-flats, the second bell rang the deep hour, and out in the dark, the fog shifted its weight, and far off — too far to be real, too near to ignore — a lantern flared green. Warden-green. Patrol lanterns were white. Lena knew that the way he knew the weight of a memory-purse: without knowing how he knew.

He blew out the lamp, took the boy's hand — small, dry, warm, gripping back with that same fierce certainty as the hand in the crack of the hidden door — and stood in his own dark shop, listening to the fog breathe.

"Whatever you are," he murmured, "you're mine now. Gods help us both."

Somewhere below the floorboards, beneath the tide-flats, beneath the drowned foundations of Vhal Maren itself, something vast and patient turned over in its sleep — and began, gently, to remember him back.
