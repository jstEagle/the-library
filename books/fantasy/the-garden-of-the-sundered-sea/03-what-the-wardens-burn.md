## 3. What the Wardens Burn

The interrogation room in the Wardenhouse smelled of wet wool and old ink, which Ilsa Corvane had long ago decided was the truest smell of the law. Not blood, not smoke—though there was plenty of both in a Warden's week—but wool and ink. Paperwork and damp uniforms. The machinery of order, grinding along.

The man across the table from her was called Fenwick Ostrow, and he sold charms.

Not deep magic. Not the forbidden kind. The harmless kind—the kind that made a loaf rise on a cold morning or helped a shy man ask a girl to dance without his voice cracking like green wood. Charms woven from purchased memories, secondhand joys, the emotional equivalent of hand-me-down coats. Legal, provided the seller held a license, paid his tithe to the Bureau of Permitted Enchantments, and kept a client list.

Fenwick's list sat between them on the table, and it was, in Ilsa's professional opinion, a disaster.

"Mr. Ostrow," she said, "this entry is just the word *Marta*."

"Marta's a good customer," Fenwick said. He was a round man with the defeated posture of someone who had been summoned before authority many times and had learned that dignity was simply not one of the things authority permitted. "She buys a charm every Frostwane. Her husband used to make her laugh. Now he doesn't, on account of being dead, so she buys the memory of him making her laugh instead. Once a year. It's very sweet, actually, if you think about it—"

"I don't need it to be sweet. I need it to be legible." Ilsa turned the page. "*Bram, twice, paid in eels.* What does 'paid in eels' mean?"

"It means Bram paid me in eels."

"Did you declare the eels?"

"To whom?"

"The Bureau of Permitted Enchantments has a schedule for barter transactions."

"Ilse—" He caught himself. "Sergeant. Nobody declares eels. You can't tax an eel. An eel is a *slippery*—"

"Everything is taxable if you write down how many of them there are." She said it flatly, but something in her chest twinged, because she had once believed the opposite with her whole heart: that some things were too small, too human, too slippery for the ledger. Twelve years in the Wardens had cured her of that. Or rather, twelve years had taught her that the cure worked, and she hated the cure, and she took it daily anyway.

She flipped another page. And stopped.

There. Third column, near the bottom, in Fenwick's cramped hand: a name she didn't recognize, no surname, just *the tide-flats man*, and beside it, in what looked like a later addition, squeezed into the margin as though Fenwick had written it reluctantly and small, hoping it would go unnoticed:

*asked about old things. Paid double. Declined.*

Ilsa kept her face still. This was a skill she had practiced until it was less a skill than a feature of her anatomy, like a jawbone. "This last one. The tide-flats man. Tell me about him."

Fenwick shrugged, which was a mistake, because shrugging at Warden-Sergeant Corvane was widely known among Vhal Maren's minor criminals to be a mistake, and Fenwick knew this, and did it anyway, because fear makes fools of us all. "He bought a charm. A small one. Nothing—"

"What charm?"

"A—a hearth-blessing. Warmth memory. Very standard."

"And then?"

"Then he asked—" Fenwick's hands did a little anxious dance on the tabletop. "He asked if I could get hold of anything *older*. Deep stock, he said. Pre-Imperial. And I told him, Sergeant, I swear on my mother's grave, I told him we don't traffic in that. That's capital work. That's burning work. And he thanked me very politely and left, and I wrote it down because I'm a *law-abiding*—"

"You wrote it down because you're a coward," Ilsa said, without heat, "and I mean that as a compliment. Cowards keep excellent records. It's why the city still stands."

Fenwick blinked at her, uncertain whether he'd been insulted, praised, or both, which was, again, the correct response to most things a Warden said.

She copied the entry into her own notebook, word for word, including the eels, because thoroughness was a discipline and disciplines were all she had left that she trusted. Then she closed the ledger.

"One more thing, Mr. Ostrow. Your license renewal is due next month. Your paperwork is a disgrace. Fix it, or the next conversation you have with the Wardens will be considerably shorter and considerably warmer."

"Warm?"

"We'll be standing closer to a fire," she said, and stood, and watched him understand.

* * *

Procedure required the shop.

That was the part nobody outside the Wardens understood, Ilsa thought, as she walked the rain-slicked streets toward Candlemakers' Row with six wardens at her back and a writ in her coat pocket. People thought the burnings were rage. They thought some officer lost his temper, some torch slipped. They liked to believe it was cruelty, because cruelty could be hated cleanly, and hating cleanly was comfortable.

It wasn't cruelty. It was procedure. That was so much worse, and Ilsa knew it, and carried the knowledge the way other people carried bad knees.

Ostrow's shop was the third on the Row, narrow and crooked, its windows fogged with the breath of a hundred little bottled memories. Inside, shelves ran floor to ceiling, each jar labeled in Fenwick's terrible handwriting: *first snows*, *a dog's greeting*, *wedding morning (damp)*. Harmless. Sweet, even. If you thought about it.

Which was exactly the problem. Charm-work was legal precisely because it seemed harmless. But the same craft that bottled a wedding morning could, in deeper hands, bottle a kingdom. The edicts were absolute: any practitioner suspected of trafficking in pre-Imperial memory forfeited stock, license, and premises. The premises burned. The practitioner, if cooperative, walked away with his life and a fine and the permanent smell of ash in his clothes.

"Clear the street," Ilsa said. "Two of you inside. Inventory first. We do this properly."

Her corporal, a young man named Brannic with the earnest face of a farm dog, hesitated at the threshold. "Sergeant. Some of these are people's *memories*. Bought fair. If we burn them—"

"They're already sold, Corporal. The sellers spent them. What's in the jars is copies." She heard how easily the sentence came out of her mouth, smooth as oiled hinges, and despised the smoothness. Copies. As if joy were bread, divisible, replaceable. As if the widow Marta's yearly purchase weren't the only hour of her marriage she had left to visit.

But the law said burn, and the law said burn for reasons she had once recited to recruits with genuine conviction: because unchecked, memory-craft metastasized; because the Sundering had begun, according to the oldest records anyone was permitted to read, with someone selling something they shouldn't; because a city that had survived by forgetting must never permit remembering to become commerce.

"Inventory," she repeated.

They inventoried. It took forty minutes. Brannic read labels aloud in a low voice, and Ilsa found herself cataloguing them against her will—*a mother's lullaby, half-remembered*; *the smell of a house you grew up in*; *being forgiven*—and somewhere around the two hundredth jar she realized her eyes were stinging and blamed the smoke that hadn't started yet.

Then it started.

They burned it from the doorway, torches laid along the dry floorboards, and the fire went up through the shelves with a sound like applause. Bottled memories popped as they cooked, releasing little ghosts of sensation into the street: a burst of laughter that belonged to no one present, a wash of summer warmth on a rainy day, once—briefly, unbearably—the scent of fresh bread. Passersby stopped and breathed it in without knowing what they were breathing, and their faces softened, and Ilsa watched them soften and felt like a thief.

"Sergeant?" Brannic, beside her, quiet. "You all right?"

"Fine, Corporal."

"You look—"

"I said fine." She adjusted her collar. "Log the destruction. Full manifest. Fenwick Ostrow cooperated; note that. He keeps his life and his fine."

"Yes, Sergeant." Brannic paused. "It's just—I keep thinking about the woman who buys the laughing-husband charm. Every Frostwane, the ledger said. Where's she supposed to go now?"

Isla looked at the fire. It was very beautiful, in the way fires always were, orange and honest and warm, doing exactly what it was told.

"Nowhere," she said. "That's rather the point of us."

Brannic had the grace not to answer. He was young enough that she could see the question still alive behind his eyes, and she gave him perhaps a year before the question died quietly of administrative causes, the way hers had. Or the way hers was *supposed* to have.

Because here was the thing she did not say aloud, not to Brannic, not to herself in the mirror, not to the god she no longer believed in: the question wasn't dead in her. It was only sleeping. And lately it had started turning over in its sleep, muttering, keeping her awake.

*Deep stock,* the tide-flats man had asked for. *Pre-Imperial.*

There was no such trade. There couldn't be. Anything older than the Empire had been unwritten—names struck from maps, languages struck from tongues, records burned in the great Purification so thoroughly that even the ashes had been swept. Ilsa herself had been taught the history of that era as a single sentence: *Before the Empire, nothing worth recording occurred.* She had been nineteen and had raised her hand and asked what the Sundering was, and her instructor had looked at her with something that was almost pity and said, *Sergeant-track students don't need that page. It isn't in your copy.*

She had accepted that. She had built a career on accepting that.

And now some barefoot ghost of a customer was asking charm-sellers about *old things*, paying double, and the word in the margin of a coward's ledger had snagged in her mind like a fishhook.

* * *

Back at the Wardenhouse, past midnight, she pulled the rumor file.

Every Wardenhouse kept one: a thin folder of nonsense, the reports too strange to prosecute and too persistent to destroy. Ilsa had added to it maybe twice in twelve years. Tonight she added a third.

*Charm-seller interrogated re: unlicensed inquiry. Subject sought "deep stock," "pre-Imperial," per seller's testimony. No transaction. Subject description: none. Seller useless. Eels involved.*

She sat back and stared at the page, and let herself do the thing she almost never let herself do, which was think sideways.

Pre-Imperial. Old Tongue. She'd heard the phrase *Old Tongue* exactly once in her career, three years ago, from a dying smuggler whose confession she'd been transcribing. He'd babbled it between prayers—*they speak the Old Tongue under the water, Sergeant, they never stopped*—and she'd written it off as fever. Dying men said all sorts of things. Dying men said the tide sang and the lighthouse walked and the Magistrate ate dreams for breakfast.

But dying men also sometimes told the truth, and the truth was the one thing the Wardens were structurally designed to misfile.

She opened the drawer beneath the rumor files, the locked one, the one whose key High Warden Dreth wore on a chain around his neck and she wore, unofficially, because Dreth forgot things and she did not. Inside: confiscated materials pending review. Mostly junk. One item, though, tagged three months ago from a raid on a smuggler's skiff: a scrap of vellum, water-stained, bearing writing no examiner could identify.

Ilsa had looked at it once, three months ago, and felt something she hadn't had words for. She looked at it now and had words for exactly one of the feelings: recognition. The letters were wrong—not corrupted, not illiterate, *wrong* the way a song is wrong when sung in a language you didn't know you knew. Curved where Imperial script stood straight. Flowing where Imperial script marched.

Pre-Imperial, the examiners had finally concluded, and then, embarrassed by their own conclusion, had appended: *probably a forgery.*

Probably.

She spread the scrap under her lamp. Below the flowing script, half washed away, someone had drawn a map fragment—or the ghost of one. A coastline. And where a harbor should be, where any map of Vhal Maren put the tide-flats, the ink had been scraped away. Deliberately. By someone who wanted that stretch of shore forgotten even on a document already dedicated to forgetting.

The tide-flats.

The same direction the tide-flats man had come from. The same district the smuggler, three years drowned now, had muttered about with his last breath. Three threads, and Ilsa Corvane had spent twelve years learning to pull threads, and her hands were moving before her doubt could catch up.

She copied the scrap. She noted the discrepancy in the map. She wrote, in her precise, damning, dutiful hand: *Recommend canvass of tide-flats district, focus on unlicensed charm-trade.*

Then she sat with the report unsigned for a long moment, lamp guttering, rain ticking at the window like a patient visitor.

Here was the arithmetic she couldn't make come out right. If there was nothing in the tide-flats—if the Old Tongue was fever-talk and the scrap a forgery—then the canvass would cost a day and find nothing, and she could file it and sleep.

And if there was something—

If there was something, then everything she had burned, every shop, every jar, every widow's borrowed hour of laughter, had been the law protecting itself from a truth it had murdered centuries ago and was terrified of meeting again. And she would be the one who found it. And finding it would be the worst thing she ever did, or the best, and either way there would be no filing it, no forgetting it, no clean hatred afterward.

Duty, her instructors had said, is a wall. You stand on it. You don't look over.

Ilsa signed the report. Then, alone in the lamplight, she folded the copy of the scrap into her breast pocket, over her heart, where a different woman might have kept a letter from someone loved—and told herself it was evidence protocol.

Outside, the rain moved in off the sea toward the tide-flats, falling evenly on the lawful city and the erased one alike, and Warden-Sergeant Ilsa Corvane blew out her lamp and went to prepare for a search she already suspected would change everything, in a district that officially contained nothing worth finding.
