## 4. The Language Before Kingdoms

The boy slept in the back room of my shop for two days before I worked up the courage to do anything about it.

Not that I hadn't tried to do something about it. On the first morning I'd stood over his cot with a bowl of barley porridge and rehearsed six different ways to say *good morning* in a voice that wouldn't frighten him, and by the time I'd settled on the fourth one he was awake and watching me with those dark, unblinking eyes, and every word I'd prepared evaporated like spit on a stove-iron.

He'd taken the porridge. He'd eaten it with a small wooden spoon he produced from somewhere on his person — children always have spoons; it's one of nature's great mysteries — and when he finished he'd looked at me and said a word. A soft word, round at both ends, like a river stone worn smooth by centuries of water.

I did not know what it meant. That was the problem. That was the whole problem, wrapped up in one smooth little stone of a word.

Because here is what you need to understand about me: I have made a life out of knowing things. Not big things. Small things. The taste of a bride's wedding morning, lifted neat from her mind while she haggled over eels and sold back to her as a charm against forgetting she'd ever been happy. The smell of a fisherman's first catch, bottled for his widow. Little thefts, harmless thefts, the kind that leave no wound and no witness. Twelve years of them. And in twelve years of handling other people's memories — sifting through them, weighing them, threading them into charms — I had learned every language spoken in Vhal Maren and half the languages spoken in the ports beyond it. Trade-cant, high Imperial, the gutter dialects of three drowned harbors, even the hand-signals the oyster divers used under the water.

The boy's language was none of these.

Worse than that: it wasn't *adjacent* to any of these. Every tongue I knew shared bones with some other tongue I knew. His had no bones at all. When he spoke, the syllables went places my ear had never been invited, and my stolen charms — the little inventory of other people's happiness hanging in their glass vials along my workbench — resonated. Actually resonated, humming faintly, like struck glass. Like they recognized something in his voice that I didn't.

Charms don't recognize things. Charms are thread and salt-glass and a whisper of borrowed time. They are the least sentient objects in the world, which is precisely why I deal in them.

And yet.

So on the third morning, after the boy ate his porridge and said his smooth stone of a word at me again — patiently, the way you'd speak to someone very slow or very deaf — I wrapped him in my second-best coat, told him we were going for a walk, and took him to see Mother Quill.

*

Mother Quill lived above a chandler's shop on Rope-Walk Street, in rooms that smelled of tallow and old paper and, underneath it all, faintly of lavender, because even archivists are allowed one vanity. She was blind as a cellar and had been for forty years, which meant she read with her fingers and remembered with a ferocity that frightened grown scholars, and she charged for her knowledge in exactly the currency you'd expect.

"Sit down, Lena Reyes," she said before I'd finished knocking, "and stop hovering in my doorway like a man deciding whether to confess."

"How do you know it's me?"

"You breathe like a guilty man. Guilty men hold their breath between sentences." She turned her face toward me. Her eyes were the pale gray of weathered driftwood, and they saw nothing, and somehow they saw everything. "And you've brought someone. Small. Light footsteps. A child?"

"A boy," I said. "Maybe eight."

"Mm." She folded her hands. "And you've come to me about the way he talks."

My stomach performed a small, cold somersault. "How could you possibly—"

"Because you're standing there holding your breath again, and because you only ever bring me problems you can't sell. Sit. Both of you. There are biscuits in the tin if the boy has manners, and biscuits anyway if he doesn't."

The boy sat. The boy took a biscuit. The boy said his smooth stone word, and this time I heard Mother Quill hear it — heard the small sharp intake of her breath, the way her hands went still on the table.

"Say that again," she said quietly.

He said it again. And then, because children are generous creatures once fed, he said more of it — a whole bright run of it, rising and falling, his hands moving a little, his face open as a window. It sounded, I thought wildly, like water deciding to be speech. Like something older than speech wearing speech's clothes.

Mother Quill listened the way other people pray.

When he finished, she was silent for so long I started to worry. "Mother Quill—"

"Don't." She raised one finger. "Don't say anything yet. Let an old woman be certain before she ruins your week." She rose, slow and precise, and crossed to the wall where her books lived — hundreds of them, each one braille-stamped or knot-coded or simply memorized, spines worn soft as cloth. Her fingers walked the shelves like a woman greeting old friends, hesitated, moved on, hesitated again.

Then she pulled down a slim volume bound in gray leather gone greenish with age, and carried it back to the table, and laid her palms flat upon it.

"Lena," she said. "What do you know about the Sundering?"

"What everyone knows," I said. "A hundred and fifty years ago the sea came up the streets of some city, and the Empire wrote it all down very carefully, and then wrote it all out again. There's a monument on Customs Hill. Nobody remembers what it commemorates. The plaque's been blank longer than anyone can explain."

"The plaque is blank," Mother Quill agreed, "because the name that was carved on it was *removed*. Not worn. Removed. Chiseled off by imperial stonemasons who were paid by the letter." Her hands pressed flatter on the book. "There was a kingdom, child. Before the Empire consolidated the coast, before Vhal Maren was Vhal Maren, there was a kingdom on these waters. Rich. Old. Its ships traded with places the Empire hasn't discovered yet. And its people spoke a language that predates every tongue now legal on this coast."

The room felt suddenly colder. The boy watched us, biscuit forgotten, eyes moving between our faces as though he understood every word.

"They called it Callowmere," Mother Quill said.

The word landed in my chest like a dropped anchor. Not because I knew it. Because some part of me — some part below knowing, below memory, in the wet dark where things sink — *shivered* at it. The charms on my workbench were miles away and I swear I felt them hum.

"After the Sundering," she went on, "the Empire did not conquer Callowmere. Conquest leaves records, treaties, grudges — messy things, living things. No. The Empire did something cleaner. It *unwrote* them. Maps redrawn. Archives burned. Families reassigned new histories, new names, new griefs. Any soul caught speaking the old tongue was tried for sedition, and sedition on the coast means fire. Within a generation, Callowmere existed nowhere but—" she tapped the gray book beneath her palms, "—in volumes like this one, hidden by fools like me, in a script nobody alive can read."

"I thought you couldn't read it either," I said.

"I can't read it. But I can recognize it. Forty years ago a smuggler sold me this book thinking it was a cookbook." For the first time, something almost like humor crossed her seamed face, and vanished just as fast. "It is not a cookbook. And the language the boy is speaking, Lena Reyes, is its cousin. Its *living* cousin. Pre-Imperial. Older than the Empire's oldest charter. A dead language walking around my sitting room eating my biscuits."

She reached across the table and found the boy's hand. He let her take it. She turned it over gently, ran her thumb across his palm, and frowned.

"Calluses," she murmured. "Rope-work. Oar-work. On a child of eight." She released his hand and turned her driftwood eyes toward me, though of course they rested on my shoulder, or perhaps on nothing at all. "Where did you find him?"

"My door. Stormtide night. Curfew. Barefoot in the snow-melt, no fear in him at all, like the cold was a rumor he hadn't heard yet."

"No parents? No one looking for him?"

"No one's posted notices. No one's asked."

"And you took him in." It wasn't quite a question. "You, who have spent twelve years perfecting the art of being unnoticed, took in the single most noticeable child on this coast."

"He was cold," I said, which was true, and insufficient, and we both knew it.

Mother Quill leaned back. For a long moment the only sound was the chandler downstairs wrapping candles for some early customer, paper rustling like distant surf.

"Here is what happens next," she said at last, and her voice had changed — lower, flatter, the voice she used for prices and other hard truths. "You will tell no one. You will not write it down. You will not think the kingdom's name in public, because there are Wardens whose entire trade is listening for thoughts that show on faces, and yours, my dear thief, shows on yours like weather. If a Warden hears that word from your mouth, they will not arrest you. Arrest implies a trial, and trials imply records, and records imply the thing exists. They will simply burn your shop with you in it and report a chimney fire."

"That's cheerful."

"I'm not finished. The boy—" and here she paused, and her stillness became total, the stillness of a heron over water, "—the boy is not merely speaking a forbidden language. He speaks it *natively*. Do you understand what that means? Dead languages don't have native speakers. Languages die because their last speakers die. Unless—" her hands closed slowly around the gray book, "—unless the last speakers didn't die. Unless they went somewhere the Empire couldn't follow. Somewhere maps don't reach. Somewhere behind—"

She stopped herself. I watched her decide, visibly, deliberately, not to finish the sentence.

"Behind what?" I asked.

"Nothing," said Mother Quill, which was the least convincing pronunciation of *nothing* in the history of the coast. "An old woman's speculation. Forget it."

"Mother Quill. In forty years you have never once told me to forget anything. Forgetting is my trade, not yours."

For a moment I thought she would laugh. What came out instead was closer to grief.

"Then here is what I remember, since you insist," she said softly. "There is a story the oldest archives whisper about, before the burning, in fragments too broken to prosecute anyone over. A door. A door at the edge of the world, or under it, or *between* — the fragments disagree, and geography is a flexible discipline at the best of times. A door that was sealed during the Sundering. Sealed from one side. And sealed, the fragments say, not with chains or wards or any craft the Empire ever mastered, but with a *memory*. A memory given up willingly, by someone of the blood, as the lock's keystone." Her head tilted. "They erased everything else, Lena. Kingdom, language, names, graves. But they left the door itself alone. Do you know why? Because a locked door is only dangerous if people believe there's something behind it. Erase the something, and the door becomes a wall. Erase the wall, and it becomes nothing at all."

The boy chose that moment to speak.

One word. The smooth stone word he'd been giving me every morning, patient as tide. He said it to Mother Quill, and then — this was the part that stopped my heart and restarted it wrong — he pointed, small finger steady, directly at me.

Mother Quill's breath caught. Her sightless eyes widened, and though they could not see, I had the terrible certainty that in that moment she perceived me more clearly than anyone ever had.

"Do you know what he said?" I whispered.

"Yes," she said. "It's a name. An old name, a name-shape from the pre-Imperial root-tongue. It would translate roughly as—" her lips moved, testing, tasting, "—*the one who holds*. Or perhaps *the keeper*. Old tongues are imprecise about keeping and holding; they treat them as the same act." She turned her face fully toward me. "Lena Reyes. Why is a native speaker of a drowned and unwritten kingdom calling *you* the keeper?"

"I don't know," I said.

And that was the truth, and it was also the most frightening sentence I had ever spoken, because I checked — I did what I always do, reached inward for the ledger of myself, the tidy inventory of a life: shop, trade, rules, twelve years of small safe thefts — and I found that the ledger had pages missing. Not torn. *Missing*, clean as a magistrate's redaction. My childhood was a smear. My parents were a rumor I'd never bothered to investigate, because why would I? My first memory began at fourteen, mid-flight, lungs full of salt air, running from something I could not name toward a city I had never seen.

I had always assumed that was poverty. Poverty eats childhoods; everyone knows that.

Standing in Mother Quill's lavender-and-tallow sitting room, with a dead language ringing in my ears and a child pointing at me and calling me *keeper*, I understood for the first time that my past had not been eaten.

It had been *edited*.

"Lena," Mother Quill said sharply. "You've gone white as gull-bone. What is it?"

"Nothing," I said.

It was my turn to pronounce it badly.

I got the boy home before curfew, wrapped in the coat, walking fast through lanes that seemed to watch me pass. Behind us, Mother Quill stood in her doorway — I felt her there the way you feel sun through cloud — and just before the corner took her from view, she called out one last thing, quiet enough that only I could have heard it:

"The book, Lena. If you ever need the rest of the story, come back for the book. And whatever you do — *don't try to remember*."

But I was already remembering.

That night, with Tam asleep in the back room and the charms humming on their shelf like a choir holding one long note, I sat at my workbench and stared at the place in myself where fourteen years of my own life should have been, and for the first time in twelve years of careful, invisible, forgettable living —

I tried to open it.

Something answered. Far down, behind the smear, behind the salt-dark, something that had been waiting a very long time turned over in its sleep — and knocked.
