## 5. Twelve Years of Small Thefts

The boy slept in the corner behind the counter, curled around a coil of rope like it was a mother he'd invented. He had been asleep for six hours and had not once made a sound. Children, in Lena's experience, were never silent. Even sleeping children breathed like small storms, sighed, whimpered after dreams. This one slept the way deep water sleeps.

Lena sat at his workbench with a half-woven charm between his fingers and did no work at all.

*Where did you come from?*

It was a question he kept circling back to, the way his tongue returned to a cracked tooth. The boy had appeared during Stormtide curfew, barefoot on his doorstep, dry despite the flood-waters, unafraid despite everything a child should fear in Vhal Maren after dark. And he had spoken that word—that impossible, resonant word that had made every charm in Lena's shop hum like struck glass.

*Lena.* His own name, but older. Rounder. Spoken in a language Lena's hands knew and his mind did not.

He set down the charm. It was a simple one, commissioned by the cooper's wife: her father's laugh, preserved in amber thread, to be worn on days when grief sat heavy. Honest work. Small work. *Steal small, sell cheap, never be remembered.* Twelve years of rules, and the boy had broken all three of them in a single knock.

Because here was the thing Lena could not stop turning over: when the boy spoke, something in Lena had *answered*. Not recognition exactly. Recognition would have been a door opening. This had been a door that should have been there—a doorway-shaped absence, cold air moving through a wall where no wall should be.

He knew what edited memory felt like. He handled other people's memories daily; he knew their weight, their grain, the way a stolen hour sat differently in the mind than a lived one. And he knew—professionally, intimately—the seam left behind when someone cut a piece out.

He had just never thought to look for a seam in himself.

* * *

Twelve years ago, give or take a season, a boy of fourteen had come stumbling out of the marsh road into Vhal Maren with no name anyone could pronounce and no story anyone could check.

He remembered it. That was the strange part—he remembered it *vividly*, and the vividness itself was wrong, like a painting too detailed for its size. Rain. A cart track gone to mud. His feet bleeding inside shoes two sizes too large. The first lights of the city rising out of the fog like a row of drowned lanterns, and the thought, clear as a bell:

*Nobody knows me here. Nobody will ever know me here.*

And then—nothing. Not nothing, precisely. Then the ordinary record of a life resumed: hunger, theft, an old fence named Doss who caught him lifting a memory-charm off his stall and, instead of turning him in, laughed until he wheezed and said, "You've got the touch, marsh-rat. Bad luck for you. Good luck for me."

But before the rain and the cart track? Nothing. No parents. No hometown. No face leaning over a childhood bed. Every person Lena had ever met carried some before—the smell of a mother's kitchen, a father's rough hand, a village, a river, a reason they'd left. Lena's history began mid-stride, mid-escape, at fourteen years old, on a muddy road in the rain, with a thought about anonymity that no fourteen-year-old thinks.

He had told himself stories over the years. Orphan of the border wars. Fleeing a debt-bond. Fleeing worse. The stories were useful because they were unfalsifiable, and he had worn them smooth as river stones from handling.

Tonight, with a mute child breathing silently in the corner of his shop, the stones came apart in his hands.

* * *

He got up quietly—he was always quiet; quiet was a trade skill—and went to the strongbox under the floorboards where he kept the things he didn't sell. Not much. A lock of hair he'd bought off a grieving sailor because the grief in it was beautiful. A charm containing thirty seconds of a festival he'd never attended, which he took out sometimes on bad nights just to feel belonging secondhand. And the oldest thing he owned: his own first memory, or rather, the memory of arriving, which he had once tried to weave into a charm just to see what it looked like from outside.

He'd failed. Of course he'd failed. You can't steal from yourself—or so he'd believed at nineteen, with the arrogance of nineteen.

He held the arrival-memory now in his mind's grip the way he'd hold a suspect charm, feeling for the flaw. And there—it was subtle, it was *beautifully* done, but there. The rain fell in the memory, yes, but it fell without touching him. The mud sucked at his shoes, but he felt no cold. The pain in his feet was reported rather than felt, a note pinned to the scene: *your feet hurt here,* like a stage direction in a play performed by someone who had never bled.

Real memories were wet. Real memories soaked you. This one had been dried, pressed, and mounted—and mounted by a master, because the seams were finer than anything Lena could have cut himself even now, after twelve years of practice.

Someone had edited him.

The thought arrived with vertigo, the floor tilting gently beneath his chair. Someone had taken a boy and sanded him smooth, leaving only the parts that pointed forward. And they'd done it well enough that he'd lived twelve years without noticing—which raised the obvious, terrible follow-up question, the one that made his hands go cold on the edge of the workbench:

Who in Vhal Maren worked at that level?

Answer: nobody he knew. Nobody Doss knew, and Doss knew everyone. Which meant either a stranger of extraordinary skill had passed through a marsh road fourteen years ago and paused to edit a random orphan—

Or the boy on the road had done it to himself.

* * *

Lena laughed. It came out wrong, a single dry bark that made the sleeping boy stir.

Self-editing was supposed to be impossible. Every thief learned it in their first year, usually the hard way: memory resisted its owner. You could lift another person's grief because grief wanted, on some level, to be set down. But your own past held onto you like a drowning man holds a plank. There were legends, of course—thieves' tavern tales about the great ones who'd cut out their own crimes, their own griefs, whole decades—but Doss had dismissed those stories with the contempt of a professional. "Any man tells you he stole his own memory," Doss used to say, "check his pockets. What he actually did was lose it, and now he's charging people for the loss."

And yet.

Lena looked at his hands. Thief's hands, scarred, steady. They had known things lately that he couldn't account for. Last night, smuggling the boy through the flooded undercity, his feet had found the safe path through black water without his eyes or his judgment—left at the sunken arch, three steps past the drowned shrine, up where the ceiling dipped. He'd assumed it was instinct. But instinct doesn't know the undercity. Instinct doesn't know which of Old Maren's streets still stand beneath the water and which collapsed forty years ago.

Somebody had taught him. Somebody had walked him through those streets, hand on his shoulder, saying *remember this, remember this, remember this*—

Or he had taught himself, and then taken the lesson away, leaving only the muscle of it behind.

"Why?" he asked the boy, softly, uselessly. The boy slept on. "Why would I do that? What was worth cutting out?"

The shop answered with tide-sounds, the old building settling against the flats, water worrying the pilings below. On the shelf above him, his unsold charms hung in their neat rows, each one a small stolen warmth, each one humming very faintly since the boy's arrival—out of tune, all of them, like instruments waiting for a conductor.

He thought about the word the boy had spoken. *His* name, in that older rounder tongue. Not Reyes—Reyes was a name he'd taken at sixteen off a dead smuggler's papers, chosen because it sounded like nothing, belonged to nothing. But the word the boy spoke had landed somewhere underneath Reyes, somewhere underneath even *Lena*, in a room of himself he'd never had the key to.

And the charms had sung when the boy said it. All of them. As if the word were a tuning fork and every stolen memory in the shop recognized its home key.

Which meant the language wasn't just old. It meant the language was *his*—or had been, before somebody (before *he*) scraped it out of him and left only the shape of the hole.

* * *

Morning came gray and salt-fogged, the way mornings came in Vhal Maren nine months of the year. Lena opened the shutters a hand's width, checked the street out of habit—empty except for gulls arguing over a crab—and put water on to heat.

The boy woke the way he did everything: silently, completely, eyes open and watching before any part of him moved. Lena had seen cats wake like that. He'd seen wardens wake like that.

"Porridge," Lena said, holding up the pot. "You'll hate it. Everyone hates my porridge. I make it with seawater because fresh water costs."

The boy watched him with those grave dark eyes and then did something new: he lifted one small hand and pointed—not at the pot, not at the window, but at Lena's chest. At the place where a man might wear a charm on a cord. Then he touched his own lips, and made a sound—not a word, more the ghost of one, a syllable bitten in half. The same syllable that had rung through the shop two nights ago. *Le-.*

"You keep trying to tell me my name," Lena said. "I know. I heard you." He crouched, bringing himself level with the boy, and found that this close, the humming of the charms was almost painful, a chord held too long. "Here's my problem. Everything I know about myself fits in one hand. Arrived at fourteen. Rain. Mud road. That's it, that's the collection. And you—" he tapped the boy's chest gently, "—you walk up to my door speaking a language that makes my merchandise sing, and you look at me like we've met."

The boy's expression didn't change. But his eyes did—something moved behind them, deep down, grief or patience or both, the look of someone watching a slow person understand a fast thing.

"And here's the part I haven't said out loud," Lena went on, because apparently he was the kind of madman who confessed to children over porridge. "I think I did it to myself. Cut it all out. And I think—" his voice caught on the absurdity and the horror tangled together in it, "—I think whatever I took out of my head, I took it out for a *reason*. People don't perform surgery on their own souls for fun. They do it to survive. Which means whatever I forgot, it was dangerous enough that forgetting was the safer option."

He stood. The porridge was boiling over, because of course it was; he rescued it with the automatic competence of twelve years of feeding himself alone.

The boy ate the porridge and, contradicting Lena's prediction entirely, seemed to like it. He ate the way he slept, without sound, without waste. When he finished, he set the bowl down with strange formality, folded his hands, and looked at Lena with the unmistakable air of someone about to conduct business.

Then he slid off the stool, crossed to the shelf of charms—all of them still faintly singing—and pointed at the oldest one Lena owned. Not the arrival-memory; that one lived in his head. This was a physical thing, bought in his second year in the city from a dying smuggler for three coppers: a knot of sea-gray cord, worthless-looking, that the smuggler had sworn contained "a word from before the maps changed." Lena had never been able to crack it. Some charms were woven too tight by dying hands, sealed with intent stronger than craft. He'd kept it because it was pretty and because he was sentimental about unsolvable things.

The boy picked it up. Held it in both palms, the way you'd hold a bird.

And the humming in the shop stopped. All of it, everywhere, every charm going silent at once—as if the whole room had drawn a breath.

Then the knot in the boy's hands loosened. By itself. Cord that had held for sixty years unwinding like a flower opening, and from it rose a voice, thin and dry as paper, a dead smuggler's last purchase speaking across the years in that same round, old tongue—one word, twice.

The first time, Lena didn't understand it.

The second time, he did—not with his mind, which had been emptied, but with something deeper that hadn't. The word meant *home*. And underneath *home*, folded inside it like a letter inside an envelope, was a second meaning the Common tongue had no shelf for:

*the place that was taken out of the world.*

The cord fell slack. The boy looked up at Lena, and for the first time since arriving on his doorstep, the child's composure cracked—his chin trembled, his eyes flooded, and he said, in a whisper of ruined Common, the first full sentence Lena had ever heard from him:

"She said you would forget. She said to find you anyway."

The fire popped. The fog pressed at the shutters. And Lena Reyes, who had built twelve careful years on the foundation of knowing exactly how little he knew, felt the last of that foundation go soft as tide-flat mud beneath him.

"Who," he said. His voice came out steadier than he deserved. "*Who* said?"

The boy wiped his face with his sleeve, an ordinary gesture, heartbreakingly ordinary, and pointed again at Lena's chest. Then, slowly, he raised his pointing finger and drew a shape in the air—a shape Lena's hands knew, a shape every charm-weaver in Vhal Maren knew, though none dared speak it.

The shape of a door.

Lena looked at the dead smuggler's unravelled cord, at the boy, at the silent shelves full of other people's happiness. Mother Quill would know. If anyone alive could untangle a language before kingdoms and a word that meant *home-theft*, it was the blind archivist who charged in memories and forgave nothing.

He fetched his coat.

"We're going to see a woman about a hole in the world," he told the boy. "Try not to sing."
