## 6. The Warden at the Door

Ilsa Corvane had walked a great many streets in her eleven years as a Warden of Vhal Maren, and she had developed a private taxonomy for all of them. There were obedience streets, where the shutters opened the moment her boots hit the cobbles and closed again the moment they passed—streets that performed compliance like a troupe of trained dogs. There were fear streets, where nothing opened at all and you could feel eyes watching through the gaps in the boards, counting your steps, waiting for you to leave. And there were the tide-flats.

The tide-flats were something else entirely.

Here the houses leaned on each other like old drunks propping one another upright at closing time, their foundations half-swallowed by the silt that crept a little further inland every year. The salt fog came in off the drowned harbor in slow grey tongues and licked at everything—wood, iron, memory. The people here did not open their shutters when she passed, but neither did they close them. They simply went on with whatever they had been doing, mending nets, gutting fish, arguing about the price of eels, and let her walk through their lives like weather. Unwelcome. Unremarkable. Rain, essentially.

She found it restful, in a way she would never have admitted to Dreth, and damning if she had.

"Morning, Sergeant," called a woman who was scaling mackerel on a doorstep, not looking up. "You're early."

"I'm on time, Mistress Vell."

"That's what I said." The knife flashed; another silver skin of scales fell away. "There's tea on if you want it. Won't poison you. Poison's expensive."

It was, Ilsa reflected, the closest thing to hospitality the flats had ever offered her, and it was almost certainly an insult. She took the tea anyway. It was terrible—oversteeped, salt-tinged, brewed in a pot that had seen more years than either of them—but she drank it standing in the fish-gut smell because refusing would have been procedure, and this morning she was not entirely sure she believed in procedure.

"You're hunting the charm-seller," Vell said. Not a question.

"I'm canvassing."

"Mm." Vell scraped her blade clean on the doorstep. "The one up on Pilcrow Stair. Reyes. Lena Reyes." She said the name the way you'd say the name of a man who owed you money and was very good at never being home. "Buys sad things off people. Sells them back prettier."

"Sells them *what* back?"

"Charms." Vell shrugged with one shoulder, the other busy with the mackerel. "Little woven things. My sister sold him an hour once—her wedding morning, before the drinking started. Got three coppers and a charm that makes moths leave wool alone." A pause. "It works, mind. Moths hate that charm. Never understood it, but there it is."

Ilsa wrote the address in her ledger without comment. *Pilcrow Stair.* The tide-flats kept their addresses loosely, the way a careless man keeps promises, but the Wardens' maps were thorough enough. Shop above the tide-flats, shuttered most days, open at odd hours, no registered trade license, no client list filed with the Bureau of Licensed Memorywork—which was, strictly speaking, three separate crimes before the man had even stolen anything.

Three coppers for a wedding morning. She turned that over as she walked on. It was robbery, obviously. It was also, she suspected, exactly the price a widow could pay, which meant he'd priced it to sell rather than priced it to bleed. That was the trouble with the small thieves. They made theft look like charity, and charity was harder to prosecute.

* * *

Pilcrow Stair smelled of brine and lamp oil and, faintly, of something sweeter underneath—dried lavender, maybe, or the ghost of it. The shop sat where the stair gave out into a boardwalk over the mud, a narrow building with a crooked sign that read, in paint faded nearly to apology: **CHARMS & SMALL MERCIES**.

Ilsa stood outside it for a long moment, adjusting her collar, settling her weight, doing all the small physical theater of authority that she had learned young and performed so often it had worn smooth as sea glass. Then she knocked.

No answer. She knocked again, harder.

"One moment," said a voice from within, unhurried, "I'm burning the evidence."

She had her baton halfway drawn before the door opened and a man looked out at her with an expression of such mild, genuine apology that she felt briefly like the intruder. He was perhaps thirty-five, dark-haired going grey early at the temples, with the kind of face that suggested it had spent its life being forgotten by people who had just met it. His sleeves were rolled up. There was flour—or ash, or dust of some kind—on one forearm.

"Warden-Sergeant," he said. "You'll be Corvane. You've the look of the Corvanes. All jaw and conscience."

"I don't have a family," Ilsa said.

"No," he agreed pleasantly, "you don't. Come in, then, orphan of the law. Mind the third board; it bites."

The shop inside was smaller than the outside promised, which Ilsa had learned was common near the water—the sea took a room here and there and nobody argued. Shelves lined every wall, crowded with little objects: knotted cords, glass beads the color of old bruises, tin medallions stamped with symbols she didn't recognize and suspected didn't exist anywhere else. Everything was labeled in a small, careful hand. *Against nightmares (mild). Against rain (moderate). Against mothers-in-law (variable results).*

"Against mothers-in-law," Ilsa read aloud.

"It's my bestseller," the man said. "Grief is free. Mothers-in-law are forever."

"And you are Reyes."

"Lena Reyes, licensed to nothing, proprietor of everything you see, none of which is illegal until you decide it is." He spread his hands. "Ask me anything. I've nothing to hide that I know of, and several things I don't."

That was, Ilsa thought, either the confession of an innocent man or the most polished performance of one she'd ever heard, and the truly irritating part was that she couldn't tell which, and neither, she suspected, could he.

"You sell memories," she said. "Unlicensed."

"I sell charms. What's *in* the charms is between the charm and its buyer." He poured tea from a dented pot without asking—everyone in the flats seemed constitutionally incapable of letting a guest stand unteacupped—and handed her a cup chipped at the rim. "The Bureau requires licenses for memorywork above a certain weight. I keep under it. Diligently. Religiously. I once turned away a man weeping to remember his dead son because the hour was too heavy to move legally, and I have thought about that man every day since, and I still turned him away, and that, Warden-Sergeant, is what a law-abiding man looks like. Ugly, isn't it?"

Ilsa drank the tea. It was better than Vell's, which annoyed her.

"A rumor's reached the Bureau," she said. "Deep magic. Old Tongue. Something moving through the flats during Stormtide curfew."

Something changed in his face. It was very slight—a stillness behind the eyes, like a pond going glassy—and if she hadn't spent eleven years learning to read faces she'd have missed it entirely. Then it smoothed over again, and he was smiling, warm and easy, and offering her a biscuit.

"Old Tongue," he repeated. "In Vhal Maren? Warden, the last thing spoken in Old Tongue in this city was a curse, and it was aimed at the Empire, and everyone who heard it was hanged. Nobody here speaks it. Half of them can't read. The other half pretend they can't, which is wiser."

"So you know what Old Tongue is."

"I know what *everything* is. It's my trade. Knowing things is the cheap part; keeping them is where the cost comes in." He set down his cup. "Sergeant. May I speak frankly, officer to citizen?"

"You may speak. Frankness is your own affair."

"Then frankly: you don't believe I'm your rumor. You think I'm a small fish and you've been sent to net a whale, and you're walking the flats knocking on doors hoping the whale answers one." He tilted his head. "Am I wrong?"

Ilsa said nothing, which he correctly interpreted as *no.*

"But you knocked anyway," he went on, "because orders are orders, and because—" and here he paused, and looked at her with an expression she couldn't parse at all, something almost gentle, "—because it's a long walk otherwise, and nobody in this whole city asks how yours has been."

"The state of my walk is irrelevant to my duty."

"Of course it is." He said it kindly, which was worse than saying it cruelly. "Sit down anyway. The chair's bad but it's honest about it."

She did not sit. She looked at the shelves instead, working, cataloguing, being a Warden—and then her eye caught on one object, low on the third shelf, apart from the others, unlabeled.

It was a small cord, grey-blue, braided in a pattern she almost recognized. Her mother had braided things. Rope, hair, bread dough, the fraying hem of Ilsa's first cloak—her hands had always needed to be making knots, and Ilsa had forgotten that, had forgotten it so thoroughly that remembering it now felt less like recollection and more like falling.

"How much for that one?" she heard herself ask, and hated herself for asking.

"That one?" He glanced at it, then at her, and something moved across his face too fast to name. "That one's not for sale."

"Everything's for sale. It's a shop."

"Not that one. That one found me." He lifted it down anyway, held it in his palm, and looked at her for a long moment with those forgettable eyes. "Tell you what. Take it. Gift. No charge, no ledger entry, no crime committed. Consider it a souvenir of your canvass."

"Giving gifts to a Warden is bribery."

"It's only bribery if it changes your behavior." He pressed it into her hand, and his fingers were warm, and quick, gone before she could decide whether to refuse. "If it changes your behavior, arrest me. Fair terms."

She should have refused. She knew she should have refused. There was a version of Ilsa Corvane, the version from five years ago, who would have refused, who would have written *attempted inducement* in her ledger and made the small thief sweat for it. But the cord was already in her pocket, light as a moth's wing, and her hand had not trembled, and some traitorous part of her mind was already noting that the braid was done in her mother's knot, the one with the doubled turn at the end, the one she had not thought of in nineteen years.

"What's in it?" she asked quietly.

He considered her. Outside, the fog pressed against the windows like something listening.

"Grief," he said finally. "A small grief. Yours, I'd guess, though I couldn't say whose shape it wears—it wasn't mine to begin with. Someone sold me an hour once and never came back for it, and I wove what was left into cord, and it's been sitting on that shelf waiting for someone who flinched when they saw it." He shrugged. "You flinched."

"I didn't flinch."

"You asked the price of the one unlabeled thing in the shop. That's a flinch, Sergeant. I've built a life on noticing flinches." He smiled, and it was a tired smile, and underneath the tiredness was something that looked unbearably like loneliness wearing company as a coat. "Anyway. If it's not yours, throw it in the harbor. The harbor's used to discarded things. It collects them."

* * *

She left without arresting him. She told herself it was because there was nothing to arrest him *for*—no license violation provable, no deep magic visible, no client list forthcoming despite two hours of questions that he answered with the tireless, cheerful evasiveness of a man who had been interrogated before and had decided to enjoy it this time. Twice he made her laugh. Once, deliberately, she was sure. Once by accident, which unsettled her more.

At the door he said, "Come back sometime. Not on duty. The tea's better when nobody's taking notes."

"Nobody takes notes off duty," Ilsa said.

"Exactly," he said, and closed the door gently, like a man shutting a book he intended to finish later.

* * *

She walked the rest of the flats because the report required it, and because walking was easier than thinking. By the seawall she stopped, took out the grey-blue cord, and held it in her palm.

She should throw it in the harbor. Procedure was clear on gifts. Dreth would call it a test of her discipline, and Dreth would be right, and knowing Dreth was right had never once stopped the sea from coming in.

She didn't throw it in the harbor.

Instead she stood at the rail while the fog thickened toward evening, and she let the charm do whatever charms do, and what it did was this: it gave her back forty seconds of a kitchen. Steam on a window. Hands braiding—her mother's hands, younger than Ilsa remembered any hands being, turning a doubled knot at the end of a cord, humming something tuneless and half-remembered. Forty seconds. Less than a minute of a woman who had been erased from Ilsa's own record by her own oath, sworn at nineteen, renewed every year since, the oath that said *a Warden holds nothing, owes nothing, remembers nothing that isn't the law's.*

Forty seconds, and then it folded away again, and the fog was just fog, and the seawall was just a seawall, and Ilsa Corvane stood alone on it holding a piece of string like the last person on earth.

"Well," she said aloud, to no one, in the flat professional voice she used for damage assessments. "That's a problem."

Because here was the arithmetic she could not make balance: the charm was stolen goods. Its contents were contraband memory. Its seller was, by every measure she possessed, guilty of something—and she had liked him. Not trusted him; she was too well-trained for trust. Liked. Found him funny. Found him *lonely*, in the specific way she recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting—not because you study it, but because you've signed with it ten thousand times.

And beneath all of it, quieter than the rest, a fact she had not yet said to herself in words: he had known. Standing in that shop full of labeled shelves, he had looked at her—one Warden among hundreds, interchangeable, forgettable, designed to be forgotten—and he had known which shelf to reach for. He had known her grief before she did. Which meant he had *looked*. Which meant that somewhere in that shabby little shop above the tide-flats, a man who claimed to steal nothing had seen all the way down to the bottom of Ilsa Corvane, and had put the sight of it gently, carefully, into a length of braided cord, and waited.

Wardens were not seen. That was the whole architecture of her. Seen things got remembered, and remembered things got loved or hated, and both were liabilities. She had built herself out of unseen the way the Empire had built Callowmere out of unwritten, and she had never once, until today, wondered whether the two constructions might be related.

She wrote her report that night in the barracks, by lamplight, in her precise hand. *Canvassed forty-one premises. No evidence of deep magic. Subject Reyes cooperative, verbose, likely harmless. Recommend continued observation.*

She looked at the word *harmless* for a long time.

Then she added, in smaller letters, as though the ink itself might not notice what it was carrying: *Recommend observation be conducted personally.*

Below the desk drawer where she kept her oath-scrolls, there was a shallow false bottom her predecessor had left, and in it, that night, the grey-blue cord went—folded once, knot outward, the doubled turn her mother's hands had taught a knot to make.

Somewhere across the city, in a shuttered shop above the mud, a lamp was still burning. Ilsa did not know that. But the tide knew, and the tide kept better records than the Bureau ever would, and tomorrow it would bring the fog in again, and with it, sooner or later, the fire.
