## 7. Salt and Ash

The fire came at low tide, which was the Bureau's way of being merciful without ever admitting it.

Lena woke to the smell of pitch and burning kelp, and for one groggy heartbeat he thought it was simply the flats doing what the flats did—some fish-oil renderery catching light, some drunk lamp-keeper's mistake. Then he heard the bells. Not the tide-bells, which rang in slow triplets all night like patient old women counting waves, but the Warden bells: two sharp strikes, a pause, two more. *Inspection in force.* Every child in Vhal Maren learned that rhythm before they learned their letters.

He was down the ladder before his boots were fully on.

From the shop's seaward window he could see it: three lantern-flares bobbing along the Boardwalk, and behind them the orange bloom of something already well alight. Old Sefa's charm-stall, by the shape of it. Sefa sold love-knots and seasickness wards and had never in forty years stolen anything larger than an unattended pastry. It didn't matter. When the Wardens came to the flats, they came with a list of addresses and a can of pitch, and the list was mostly ceremonial.

"Tam." He kept his voice level, the way you keep a full cup level while walking a slick deck. "Up. Now. Don't ask."

The boy was already awake—he was always already awake, sitting cross-legged in the corner where Lena had made him a nest of sailcloth and an old fur coat, watching the door with those dark, unblinking eyes. He looked at Lena. He looked at the window, at the orange light painting itself across the fog. And then he did the thing that frightened Lena more than any Warden ever had: he nodded, calm as a man agreeing to breakfast, and began folding the fur coat with neat, deliberate care, as though the coat were coming with them and would need to be presentable.

"Leave the coat," Lena said.

Tam looked at him. Kept folding.

"Fine. Bring the coat."

* * *

The rules of surviving a raid were simple, and Lena had written them himself years ago in the front of his own mind, in letters so familiar he no longer remembered writing them:

*One. Never run from a Warden; walk like a man late for work.*
*Two. Never hide in your own walls; every wall has ears with badges.*
*Three. Go down. The Wardens burn up.*

Down meant the undercity—the drowned streets beneath the drowned streets, the first Vhal Maren, swallowed by the sea two hundred years before the second one was built on top of its roof-beams. At dead low tide, the upper galleries were passable if you didn't mind water to the knee and things moving in it. At high tide they were a grave. The Wardens knew this, which was why they never went down, which was why everyone who mattered went down.

Lena knew the way. That was the trouble. He knew it the way he knew how to pick a lock or read a mark's tells or fold a memory into a ribbon of blue-grey cord—with a certainty that lived in his hands and not in his history. He could stand at the mouth of the Drowned Arcade and tell you that the third archway flooded fastest but the fifth had a false floor over a dry crawl-space, and when Tam looked at him with the unspoken question—*how do you know?*—Lena had nothing to offer but the truth, which was worse than a lie:

"I don't know. I just know."

The boy considered this. Then, in that voice like wind through a door left open somewhere very far away, he said a word in the Old Tongue. Lena felt it land in his chest like a hook finding meat. He'd stopped flinching at it, mostly. Mostly.

"Yeah," he said roughly. "Me too."

* * *

They went out through the shop's back, down the oyster-shell stairs, into the alley that smelled of brine and tar. Above them, the sky was going the color of a bruise deciding whether to be purple or yellow. Somewhere to the east, Sefa's stall was collapsing inward with a sound like a sigh, and Lena made himself not look.

The flats district emptied ahead of the Wardens the way water empties ahead of a plow. Families stood in doorways holding bundles they'd packed years ago and never unpacked, because packing them properly would mean admitting the night might come. An old woman was sweeping her step. *Sweeping her step,* Lena thought, with a tenderness that hurt. As though ash cared about clean stone. As though procedure could be swept away.

A hand caught his sleeve in the shadow of the net-mender's arch.

It was Doss. Old Doss, fence, fixer, and the closest thing to a friend that a man with Lena's rules allowed himself—which is to say, a man he paid promptly and insulted affectionately, and who paid him back in kind. Seventy if he was a day, with a beard like unravelled rope and a wheeze that had been promising to kill him for a decade and always lost its nerve.

"You're awake, then," Doss rasped. "Good. Means I don't have to feel guilty about what I'm about to say."

"Don't say it."

"They're working the Boardwalk end first, then the Netmenders' Row, then they'll sweep toward the shell-market. You want to be gone before the sweep." His grip tightened. "And boy—I'll say the other thing too, since you won't let me not. There's a Warden-Sergeant asking after you by *description*. Not by name. By face. Grey coat, young, doesn't smile. She's got your sketch."

Lena's stomach performed a slow, professional drop. "Since when do Wardens sketch?"

"Since somebody upstairs decided you were worth ink." Doss released his sleeve and, with the air of a man closing a ledger, pressed something into Lena's palm: a small tin whistle, dented, ugly. "Ferryman's call. Bottom of the Arcade stairs, there's a man named Pell who owes me more than money. Blow that, he takes you down the long way. Don't blow it unless you mean it, because Pell doesn't do refunds."

"Doss—"

"Go." The old man smiled, and there was something in the smile that Lena would spend the rest of the night taking apart, piece by piece, like a lock he couldn't stop picking. "I'm seventy years old and I owe the tide a death anyway. Let me choose the terms."

"I'll come back," Lena said, "and we'll drink, and you'll cheat me at cards, and I'll let you."

"See that you do." Doss turned away, pulling his patched coat around him, walking—not running, *walking*, rule one—toward the Boardwalk, toward the lanterns, toward the men with the pitch cans. A fence's best trick was being seen where he wasn't wanted, being memorable in the wrong direction, being a wall that bullets found interesting.

Lena watched him go for exactly as long as it took Tam to tug his hand.

Then he went down.

* * *

The Drowned Arcade breathed.

That was the only word for it. The whole undercity inhaled and exhaled with the tide, a slow tidal respiration through ten thousand broken windows, and when you stood in it you could feel the city thinking. Lena had been down here—when? He hunted for the memory the way you hunt for a dropped coin in dark grass, and came up with nothing but grass. But his feet knew. His feet turned left at the shrine of the unnamed god, right at the stairwell choked with mussels, and stopped dead at the lip of the flooded gallery where the water lay black and still as poured glass.

"Third arch floods fast," he murmured. "Fifth has a false floor."

Tam peered into the dark, then at Lena, then produced from inside the fur coat—because of course the coat had come—a stub of candle and, with great ceremony, lit it.

"Of course you have a candle," Lena said. "Why wouldn't you have a candle."

They went by the fifth arch. The false floor was exactly where his hands said it would be, a slab of rotted decking laid over a crawl-space that ran dry and narrow between the bones of two collapsed buildings. Lena went first, elbows and knees, breathing the smell of old salt and older wood, and tried very hard not to think about the fact that he knew the crawl-space was clear because he had cleared it himself, once, with a borrowed crowbar, on a night he could not remember, for reasons he could not remember, in a life that appeared to have been conducted entirely without his attendance.

There was a moment, halfway through, when the candle-glow behind him caught Tam's face close to his own, and the boy spoke—softly, urgently, a whole sentence of the Old Tongue, the first time he'd strung more than three words together—and every charm in Lena's pockets resonated at once. Not painfully, this time. Like tuning forks finding each other. Like a chord.

He understood none of it. He understood all of it. The two feelings sat in his chest together like strangers forced to share a bench, and neither would leave.

"I know," he whispered back, lying gently. "I know. Almost there."

* * *

They came up through the shell-market an hour later, into grey dawn and the smell of smoke drifting wrong-way on the wind, and that was when Lena understood that the raid hadn't passed them by. It had *turned*.

The Wardens were working outward from the Boardwalk in a spiral, methodical as a drain emptying, and the spiral's next arm was the market. Already there were lanterns at the far end, and the particular disciplined clatter of armed men who have done this many times and expect to do it many more. Between Lena and the open mudflats beyond lay four hundred yards of stalls and awnings and nowhere to hide.

"Pell," Lena breathed. "We need Pell. The Ferryman's stairs are—"

The whistle was in his hand. He raised it—

And then he saw Doss.

The old man was standing in the mouth of the Boardwalk lane with his arms folded, talking to three Wardens with the expansive, infuriating bonhomie of a man who has never once hurried anywhere. Even at this distance Lena could read the performance: the shrug, the laugh, the pointing finger sending them confidently toward the *wrong* quarter. Buying minutes. Selling misdirection at his usual extortionate rates.

One of the Wardens wasn't listening anymore. He was looking past Doss, at the ground, at something small and bright that had fallen from the old man's pocket when he'd gestured. Something that glinted. Something that looked, even at forty yards, even through fog, exactly like a length of blue-grey memory-cord tied in a knot that only two people in Vhal Maren knew how to tie.

Doss followed the Warden's gaze down. Lena saw him see it. Saw the shoulders square. Saw the old man look up—not at the Wardens, but out, across the market, toward the shadows where he knew, because he had always known everything, that Lena was standing.

He winked.

Then he said something to the Wardens that Lena couldn't hear and would spend years inventing versions of, and put his hand inside his coat, and the world became noise and orange light and Tam's fingers digging into his wrist, and Lena was running, dragging the boy through the stalls, the whistle crushed forgotten in his fist, while behind them the Wardens shouted and the fog took everything and gave back nothing.

* * *

Pell's ferry was a flat-bottomed skiff that poled the flooded galleries like a hearse poling through a cathedral. The Ferryman himself was a slab of a man with river-silt eyes who asked no questions, which was fortunate, because Lena had no answers. He sat in the stern with Tam against his side, the boy's candle finally guttered, the fur coat pulled over them both, and watched the drowned city slide past overhead—roof-beams like whale ribs, a chandelier furred white with barnacles still hanging in someone's forgotten parlor, a child's doll floating by with one painted eye open.

"He winked at me," Lena said. It came out before he could stop it. "The stupid old fraud. He *winked*."

Pell poled. The water whispered against the hull.

"He had the cord in his pocket," Lena went on, because the words had a leak in them now and were coming out whether he wanted them or not. "My cord. Why would he—why would he carry it, why would he *bring it*, he knew the risk, he taught me half my rules, he—" His voice did something humiliating. He let it. There was no one down here to perform for except a mute child and a man who sold silence by the yard.

Tam's small hand found his. The boy looked up at him with those dark eyes and said one word in the Old Tongue, soft, and for once Lena didn't need the translation. Some words predate kingdoms. This one meant *stay*. Or possibly *grieve*. In a language old enough, he suspected, they were the same word.

The skiff slid on through the dark.

*Invisibility has a body count,* Lena thought, and the thought had teeth. Twelve years he had stayed small. Twelve years of harmless charms and cheap prices and never being remembered, and he had told himself the smallness was safety, was virtue even, was a kind of kindness to the world—*I take nothing, I break nothing, I am no one.* And now Sefa's stall was ash and Doss was a story the tide would keep and a Warden-Sergeant carried his face around in her pocket like a coin she hadn't decided how to spend.

The smallness had never been safety. It had just been a slower fire.

"Where are we going?" he asked aloud, and realized he was asking the boy, not the Ferryman.

Tam considered the question with the gravity of a small emperor. Then he raised one hand and pointed—not down any gallery, not toward any exit Lena knew—but deeper, toward the oldest part of the undercity, where even Pell's pole hesitated, where the drowned streets gave way to something older than drowning.

Toward, though neither of them could have said how they knew it, the blind archivist who kept a library at the bottom of the world.

Lena looked at the pointing finger. Looked at Pell. Pell shrugged the shrug of a man whose fee had been paid.

"Well," Lena said, to the dark, to the boy, to the ghost of an old man's wink. "You heard him."

The skiff turned, and the city exhaled, and somewhere far above, the fog rolled in off the sea carrying the smell of ash—and under the ash, faint and stubborn, the smell of rain coming.
