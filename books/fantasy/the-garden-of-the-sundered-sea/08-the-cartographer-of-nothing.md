## 8. The Cartographer of Nothing

Mother Quill's shop did not appear on any map of Vhal Maren, which was fitting, because neither did its owner.

Lena had found it years ago by accident—a wrong turn in the Sump during a rainstorm, a door that seemed to have been waiting for him. It sat wedged between a chandler's and a building that had burned down so long ago the chandler had built his wall over the gap, so that Quill's door opened onto solid brick and had done so, apparently without complaint, for forty years.

He knocked twice, then once more—the rhythm of someone asking a favor rather than making a purchase—and heard the shuffling he'd come to know: the drag of slippers, the tap of a cane finding furniture, the small grunt of a woman who resented every step but refused to be carried anywhere.

"Late," said Mother Quill through the door. "You're late, Reyes."

"The tide was against me."

"The tide is always against you. That's what I like about you." The bolts went back one, two, three. "Come in before the fog does. It gets into everything. Last month it got into my inkwell and I wrote a letter to my sister that read like a eulogy for a fish."

The interior was exactly as he remembered, which was to say it looked like a library had been drowned, rescued, and never told anyone about the experience. Books stood in towers on every surface, some stacked spine-out, some piled flat with heavier volumes on top as though the lighter ones might otherwise float away. Maps hung from the ceiling on strings, curling gently in the damp air like kelp. A single lamp burned under a green glass shade, turning everything the color of deep water.

And there, sitting cross-legged on a table where no child should sit, surrounded by an archipelago of open books, was Tam.

The boy looked up when Lena entered. He didn't smile—he rarely smiled—but something in his shoulders came down half an inch, the way a sail comes down when the wind finally eases. He said something in Old Tongue, soft and quick, and pointed at Lena, then at himself, then at the books around him, as if to say: *I've been reading.*

"You let him touch your books," Lena said. "You don't let *me* touch your books."

"Your hands are greasy with charm-work. His are clean." Mother Quill lowered herself into her chair by the lamp, cane hooked over the armrest, and fixed him with the pale, unblinking stare of a woman who had spent eighty years looking at things other people couldn't see. "Also, he asked permission. In writing. Well—in charcoal. On my good table. But the sentiment was correct."

She tapped the table. Sure enough, faint gray letters marked the wood—Old Tongue characters, careful and round, followed by what Lena could only assume was an apology.

"What did he write?"

"He asked if the books were lonely." Quill snorted. "Cheeky little sprat. I told him all books are lonely; that's why they're written." Her expression shifted, and the humor drained out of it like tide out of a channel. "Sit down, Reyes. We need to talk about what your boy speaks."

Lena sat. Tam watched them both, still as driftwood.

"I brought you the word lists you asked for," Lena said, producing a folded packet from inside his coat. "Everything he's said that I could remember well enough to write down. And the—" he hesitated— "the resonance notes. What happened to my charms when he talked."

"I read them twice." She laid her hand flat on the tallest stack of books beside her, the way another woman might lay a hand on a grave. "Do you know what I did before I went blind, boy?"

"You've never told me."

"No. Because it wasn't interesting until now." She turned her face toward him, and the lamplight caught the milky film of her eyes. "I was a cartographer. Not the kind that draws coastlines for merchants. The kind that draws *everything else*. The old roads. The buried rivers. The places the Empire decided weren't there anymore." She smiled thinly. "They called me the Cartographer of Nothing, because nothing was my specialty. I mapped erasures."

Lena felt the cold come up through the floorboards. "Quill—"

"Let me finish. You'll want to interrupt. Everyone interrupts at this part, and then they don't sleep for a week." She drew a breath. "Every kingdom has wars it wins and wars it loses. The Empire has something rarer. It has a war it refuses to admit happened. Two hundred years ago, give or take a generation, there was a country across the Sundering Sea. Green hills. White harbors. A language older than Imperial, older than the stones the Imperial roads were laid on. Its name was Callowmere."

The word landed in the room like a dropped glass.

Tam's head came up sharply. He said something—one word, urgent—and pressed his hand flat against his own chest.

"Yes," Quill said gently, to the boy, in his own tongue—or an approximation of it, worn and accented, like a song remembered from childhood. Then, to Lena: "That word means 'home.' Or it used to. Now it means nothing, because we made it mean nothing."

"How do you erase a whole country?" Lena asked. His voice came out smaller than he intended.

"Not with armies. Armies leave ruins, and ruins leave questions, and questions leave *memories*, and memories are the one thing the Empire cannot abide being loose in the world." Quill's fingers found a leather cord around her neck and drew out a pendant—a flat disc of dark stone, scratched all over with tiny marks. "With a pact. With a Door. There were nine cartographers who worked the Callowmere project, and eight of us recanted or died. I recanted. I gave up my maps, took a bargain that cost me my eyes—*sight* draws memory, boy, sight is how memory hooks itself into the world—and I kept one thing they never thought to confiscate." She turned the disc over. Scratched into its face, in lines fine as spider silk, was a coastline. "A map of a place that officially does not exist. Drawn from memory. My last act of treason, and I've worn it against my heart for sixty years waiting for someone to ask me about it."

Lena's mouth was dry. "Someone's asking now."

"So they are." She set the disc on the table between them, and Tam leaned over it immediately, tracing the coastline with one finger, naming the bays under his breath in that language that made the air feel thicker. "Here's what the pact was, Reyes, and listen carefully, because this is the part that will keep you up. Callowmere wasn't destroyed. It couldn't be—there were too many people, too much life, and the magic of that land ran too deep to kill. So instead of burning it, the Empire *unwrote* it. Struck it from every record. Salted every mention of it in every memory-charm in circulation. Paid off, threatened, or blinded every soul who knew its name. And behind all that forgetting, they sealed the people themselves away—behind a Door. The Sundered Door. The last way in or out of a country that no longer exists."

"A door to where?"

"To Callowmere. Which is nowhere. Which is everywhere the Empire says it isn't." She waved a hand impatiently. "Don't try to picture it. Geographers have broken their minds trying. The point is this: such a seal doesn't hold on hinges and iron. It holds on a bargain. A pact, struck between the last queen of Callowmere and the first Magistrate. And the lock of that pact—the keystone, the thing that keeps the Door shut—is not a key or a word or a stone." Her blind eyes found him with terrible precision. "It's a memory. The memory of a blood-heir. The royal line's own remembering of their kingdom, taken and bound into the Door, so that the kingdom stays shut as long as the heir stays forgetful."

The lamp flickered. Outside, somewhere down the flooded lane, a boat horn moaned, long and low, like something grieving.

"And if the heir remembers?" Lena asked.

"Then the lock turns. The Door opens. Everything the Empire buried for two centuries walks back into the world." Quill's voice dropped. "Which is why the Wardens burn districts over rumors of Old Tongue. Why speaking that name aloud is treason of the highest order. Why a barefoot child who talks in his sleep in the language of Callowmere is, right now, the most dangerous creature in Vhal Maren."

Lena looked at Tam. The boy was still touching the map, following the coastline, and his lips moved silently, reading it like a letter from someone he loved.

"He's just a child," Lena said.

"He is a child of the blood. Descendant of the heir whose memory locks the Door. The blood remembers even when the records don't—that's how blood works, boy, it's the oldest memory there is. It's why he speaks a dead tongue like it's his mother's milk. It's why your charms scream when he talks." Quill reached out and gripped his wrist, her hand dry and strong as root-wood. "And it's why they will not stop hunting him. Not for justice. Not for punishment. For *maintenance*. The Empire doesn't fear the boy. It fears the arithmetic. One living descendant means the line isn't finished, means the seal has a loose thread, means someday, somehow, the Door might—"

She stopped.

Lena realized she had stopped because he had gone rigid. Because his own hand had risen, without his permission, to press against his sternum—the same gesture Tam made when he said *home*.

"Reyes?" Quill said slowly. "What is it?"

"I don't know." His voice sounded far away to him. "When you said *heir*. When you said the blood remembers. Something—" He laughed, and it came out wrong, hollow. "Something in me moved. Like a floorboard under a rug. Like there's a room in me I've never opened because I never noticed the door."

For a long moment the only sound was the lamp hissing and Tam's soft voice, counting bays on a drowned coast.

"Twelve years you've been in my city," Quill said quietly. "And in twelve years you've never once told me where you came from. I never asked. Thieves and blind women have a professional understanding about unasked questions." Her grip tightened. "But I'm asking now. Where are you from, Lena Reyes? Who were your parents? What is your earliest memory?"

And that was the horror of it, laid out plain between the book-towers and the hanging maps: he didn't know. He had never known. His life began at fourteen, mid-flight, lungs full of smoke, running through streets he couldn't name toward a harbor he couldn't describe, carrying nothing. Before that—nothing. No mother's face. No hometown. No reason to run. Just the running, and then Vhal Maren, and then twelve years of stealing small and selling cheap and never being remembered.

"I can't answer," he whispered. "There's nothing to answer *with*."

Tam had stopped tracing the map. He was looking at Lena now with those calm, unchildlike eyes, and slowly, deliberately, he raised his hand and touched his own chest again. *Home.* Then he reached across the table and touched Lena's hand.

One word. Spoken softly, in the language before kingdoms. The word that had cracked something open the first night on his doorstep, and cracked it wider now:

His name. Not *Lena*. The other one. The true one, in Old Tongue, the one that meant something in a country that didn't exist—

—and the floorboard under the rug of his mind groaned, and somewhere behind his eyes a door he had never seen strained against its hinges, and Mother Quill said, very quietly, in the voice of a woman watching a map redraw itself:

"Oh," she breathed. "*Oh, you fool. You beautiful, doomed fool.* They didn't just take your childhood, boy. Think. *Think.* A blood-heir's memory is the lock. Someone stripped your past to keep you safe or to keep you silent—and if the blood runs true, then what they took, what you've been missing all these years—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to.

The keystone wasn't lost. It wasn't hidden in some vault beneath the Magistrate's tower.

It was walking around Vhal Maren in a charm-thief's coat, selling widow's charms for coppers, keeping his head down, keeping his rules—*steal small, sell cheap, never be remembered*—never knowing that the most important memory in the world had been locked inside him all along, and that he himself was the Door.

Tam squeezed his hand and said it again, gentle as tide.

Outside, far off across the flats, a bell began to ring—the Wardens' signal, three strokes, pause, three strokes. Not a drill. A search.

Quill's blind eyes widened. "They're sweeping the Sump," she said. "Get the boy out. *Now.*"
