## 9. Ledgers of the Living

The summons came at dawn, which in Ilsa Corvane's experience was never a good sign. Summons that arrived at civilized hours were about paperwork. Summons that arrived before the gulls had finished arguing over the harbor refuse were about fire.

High Warden Dreth's office occupied the top floor of the Wardenhouse, a room designed by someone who believed that intimidation could be achieved through architecture. The ceiling was too high. The windows were too narrow. The desk was a slab of black ironwood that had probably cost more than the street it overlooked, and behind it sat Dreth himself, gray as slate and twice as heavy, reading a report with the expression of a man who found the alphabet personally offensive.

"Sergeant Corvane." He did not look up. "Sit."

Ilsa sat. She had learned long ago that Dreth's silences were weapons, and that the only defense was to let them ring against you without flinching. She folded her hands and waited, counting the seconds out of habit. At forty-one he looked up.

"Your canvass of the tide-flats." He tapped the report. "You logged forty-one interviews."

"Forty-two. One fisherman declined to speak until he'd finished his breakfast. I waited."

"Generous of you." His eyes were the color of dishwater left standing. "And your assessment?"

"No confirmed deep magic. One unlicensed charm-seller operating above the flats—Reyes, Lena. Minor infractions. Unregistered premises, sale of low-grade charms without guild stamping. Nothing that warrants—" she chose the word carefully, "—escalation."

Dreth set the report down and laced his fingers together on the black wood. "I'm going to tell you something, Sergeant, because you're one of the few officers in this house who can hear it without weeping or bragging. Three nights ago, a listening-post on the seawall recorded resonance consistent with pre-Imperial speech patterns. In the flats district."

Ilsa kept her face still. It took effort. *Pre-Imperial.* The word sat in her chest like swallowed ice.

"That's impossible," she said. "That language hasn't been spoken since—"

"Since nothing." Dreth's voice didn't rise, but the temperature of the room dropped several degrees. "It has never been spoken. There is no such language. There is no such *anything*. That is the entire point of the edicts, Sergeant, and if you have forgotten them, I will have them read aloud to you at length, as they were read aloud to me when I was young enough to think history was something that happened to other people."

"Yes, High Warden."

"The resonance is real. Whatever produced it is real. And it is in your district." He slid a second document across the desk. It bore the seal of the Magistrate's own office, black wax stamped with the closed eye—the sigil of things officially unseen. "By order of the Magistrate's Bench: total sanitization of the tide-flats quarter. Every structure. Every resident relocated to processing. Every charm-seller's stock confiscated and burned under supervision."

Ilsa read the order twice. The words were clean, bureaucratic, bloodless—which was how such orders always were. Nobody wrote *burn the homes of four hundred people* anymore. They wrote *sanitization* and let the ink do the cruelty for them.

"Sanitization," she said. "Of the whole quarter."

"You have a question, Sergeant?"

"I have several, High Warden. I'm deciding which ones won't insult us both."

Something flickered at the corner of Dreth's mouth—not quite a smile, but the ghost of respect that passed between two people who understood each other's trade. "Ask the one you actually want answered."

"Why now?" Ilsa said. "The edicts have held for twelve years. If this resonance appeared three nights ago, why not investigate quietly first? A purge this size will cause panic. Panic causes rumors. Rumors are worse than the thing itself."

"Because quiet investigation is how the last incident began," Dreth said. "Twelve years ago. You weren't in the service yet. There are files you'll never see, and in those files there is a lesson, and the lesson is that some doors are better burned than knocked upon." He stood, which ended conversations the way a slammed book ends an argument. "The purge begins in three days. You'll lead the eastern sweep. Confiscated materials come to me personally. Not to the incinerator detail. To me."

"To you, High Warden?"

"There are things in a charm-seller's stock worth reading before burning," Dreth said, "and things that must never be read at all. I need someone with the discipline to know the difference." He looked at her a long moment. "Can I count on your discipline, Sergeant Corvane?"

It was, she recognized dimly, a trap dressed as a compliment. She gave him the only answer the uniform allowed.

"You can, High Warden."

* * *

The confiscation took two days, and Ilsa hated every hour of it in ways she couldn't put into her reports.

Not because it was hard. Because it was easy. That was the obscenity of it. The wardens moved through the flats like a tide of gray uniforms, and the people of the quarter—fishwives, net-menders, oyster-shuckers, the charm-sellers who operated out of market stalls with their guild papers displayed like talismans—stood aside and watched their livelihoods carried off in crates. No one resisted. Resistance was a capital act, and everyone in Vhal Maren knew the arithmetic of survival.

A woman selling luck-knots watched Ilsa's squad load her entire inventory and said only, "Will I be paid for it?"

"An inventory receipt," Ilsa told her. "Redeemable after review, if items are cleared."

"And if they're not cleared?"

"Then they were contraband, and possession of contraband carries its own penalties."

The woman nodded slowly, the way people nod when the law explains itself and they wish it hadn't been so honest. "My mother wove those knots," she said. "Each one's got an hour of her life in it. She sold her good years so I could eat." She handed over the last crate herself. "Tell whoever reads them that they're holding my mother's hands. Be gentle."

Ilsa said she would. She wasn't sure it was a promise she could keep.

By the second evening, the confiscated stock filled the evidence vault beneath the Wardenhouse: six hundred crates of charms, knots, woven blessings, bottled hours, and the small desperate merchandise of people who traded pieces of their lives to survive. The incineration detail was scheduled for morning. Dreth had ordered the vault sealed overnight, with himself and one trusted officer granted access.

The officer was Ilsa.

She told herself it was an honor. She told herself it was a test she intended to pass. She spent the evening hours cataloguing crates by lamplight, logging each item with the mechanical precision that had gotten her promoted twice and liked by no one, and it was nearly midnight when she opened Crate 114 and found the charm that ruined her.

It was small. A woven band, sea-gray thread shot through with a single strand of pale blue, the kind of cheap work sold from a stall for two coppers. But the weave was wrong—or rather, right—in a way she recognized now, having spent weeks studying confiscated craft. This was skilled work disguised as clumsy work. This was a master hiding as an amateur.

The maker's tag read: *L. Reyes, tide-flats.*

Her jaw tightened. Reyes. The charm-seller above the flats, the one with the careful smile and the eyes that saw too much, the one whose verbal fencing had left her unsettled for days afterward in a manner she refused to examine. He'd given her a gift, that day—a little charm pressed into her hand with a joke about wardens needing all the luck they could get. She'd pocketed it without thinking, meaning to log it as evidence and never quite doing so. It was in her coat pocket right now, in fact, which was technically a violation of about four regulations, and she'd been telling herself she'd turn it in any day now.

This was a different charm. But the same hands had made it.

Protocol said: log it, shelve it, burn it with the rest. Protocol said a great many things, and Ilsa had built twelve years of service on the foundation of following them even when they chafed, especially when they chafed, because the moment you started deciding which laws deserved obedience was the moment you became the thing the laws existed to prevent.

She logged it. Crate 114, item 9, classification: memory-charm, minor grade, origin: L. Reyes, disposition: pending.

Then she picked it up.

Later—much later, in the years she would spend reassembling herself—she would try to explain what happened next, and fail. The closest she ever came was this: a memory-charm doesn't show you a memory. It lends you one. For a few breaths, you remember what someone else remembered, with their love still attached, and there is no armor against that, because armor is worn on the outside and memories go directly to the place where you keep yourself.

The charm opened like an eye.

*A kitchen. Small, poor, clean. Rain on a window that rattles in its frame. A woman stands at a stove with her back half-turned, singing—badly, cheerfully, a song with no tune to speak of—and a girl of perhaps six sits on the floor winding yarn around her own fists, tangling it hopelessly, and the woman turns and laughs and says,*

*"Ilsa, my heart, you've murdered that poor wool, bring it here and we'll commit the crime together."*

*The girl holds up the tangled mess, solemn as a magistrate. The woman takes it, kisses the top of her head, and begins unpicking the knots with quick clever fingers, and the whole world is rain and wool and warmth and the smell of bread that has not yet finished baking but will, it will, because mothers keep their promises about bread—*

The charm went dark in Ilsa's hand.

She stood in the evidence vault for a long time. The lamp hissed. Somewhere above, the Wardenhouse settled and groaned like an old ship, and Ilsa Corvane, Warden-Sergeant, eleven years decorated, author of two hundred flawless reports, discovered that she was crying.

She hadn't cried since her oath. That was rather the point of the oath. When she'd sworn into the Wardens at nineteen, the induction rite had included the Severance—a formal, voluntary surrender of personal memories deemed compromising to impartial judgment. Family faces. Childhood attachments. The soft things that made officers hesitate. She had walked into that stone room carrying her grief for her dead mother like a stone in her chest, and she had walked out lighter, cleaner, efficient. She had always believed the ache she sometimes felt at odd moments—an emptiness shaped exactly like something lost—was just the ordinary melancholy of an orphan grown into a soldier.

But the charm had been woven from *her own* memory. Stolen or salvaged—she didn't know which, didn't want to know—from some corner of her past before the Severance. Which meant somewhere in the city, in a shuttered shop above the tide-flats, Lena Reyes was holding a piece of her mother's face in his two clever thief's hands, and he had *given it back to her*, wrapped in a joke about luck, and said nothing.

He had known. God help her, he had known what she'd lost, known what the Severance had taken, and instead of selling it—instead of the cruelest blackmail imaginable—he had pressed it into her palm like a gift between friends.

Ilsa sat down slowly on an evidence crate, in violation of approximately five regulations, and held the little woven band the way you hold something that might break, though of course it was she who was broken.

The fracture, when it came, was quiet. Fractures usually are. It was not a dramatic renunciation, no tearing of the uniform, no shouted defiance. It was simply this: for the first time in eleven years, Warden-Sergeant Ilsa Corvane looked at the law of Vhal Maren—the edicts, the purges, the closed eye stamped in black wax—and saw it clearly, from the outside, as a thing with a shape. And the shape was a fist. And the fist was closing around a quarter full of fishwives and net-menders and a woman who had asked her, politely, to be gentle with her mother's hands.

*Some doors are better burned than knocked upon,* Dreth had said.

Whose door? she wondered. Burned for whom?

She became aware, distantly, that she was still holding Reyes's charm, and that her other hand had drifted to her coat pocket, where the gifted one lay. Two pieces of her stolen childhood, both smuggled back to her by a criminal. She should arrest him. The thought arrived on schedule, dutiful and hollow, like a clerk announcing a meeting no one attends.

Instead, she did something else. She took out the gifted charm, and beside it she laid the one from Crate 114, and then—with the deliberation of a woman signing her own treason—she amended the ledger. Item 9, Crate 114: *damaged in handling. Non-viable. Disposition: destroyed by examining officer.*

The lie sat on the page in her neat, award-winning handwriting, and it was the most honest thing she had written in eleven years.

She tucked both charms inside her coat, over her heart, where the oath was supposed to live.

Above her, boots crossed the vault stairs—the night watch making rounds. Ilsa rose, straightened her uniform, banked the lamp, and climbed up into the gray pre-dawn of a city preparing to burn four hundred homes over a language that officially did not exist. Three days. Dreth's timetable gave her three days.

Somewhere out there, in a shuttered shop above the tide-flats, a charm-thief who traded in other people's yesterdays was sitting on the secret that had brought the Wardens down on the quarter—and he didn't know it yet. Or maybe he did. Maybe that careful smile hid a man counting the same hours she was.

Either way, Ilsa Corvane realized as she stepped out into the salt wind, she had a decision to make, and for the first time in her career, the law and her conscience had walked into different rooms.

She found she already knew which door she meant to knock on.
