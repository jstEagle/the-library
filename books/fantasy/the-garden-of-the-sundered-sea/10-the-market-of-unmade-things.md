## 10. The Market of Unmade Things

There was a rule Lena had kept for twelve years, and it had a shape the way all good rules do—simple on the surface, complicated underneath, like an iceberg or a mother-in-law.

*Steal small.*

He had broken it exactly once before, and that time had cost him three days of vomiting and a week of forgetting his own favorite breakfast. He had never learned what he'd stolen then; the memory had been too big to carry whole, so he'd sold it in slivers and let the buyers' faces tell him what it held. One old man had wept into his tea. One had laughed until he choked. Lena had decided after that that large thefts were like lightning: impressive, occasionally useful, and best admired from a great distance.

But escape required coin, and coin required either patience or sin, and Stormtide had eaten most of his patience along with half his roof.

So on the third night of the storm season, wearing a coat that made him look like a minor customs official—which was the most invisible thing a man could be in Vhal Maren—Lena Reyes went to rob Ossian Vell.

* * *

Vell's house sat above the harbor like a fat gull on a pilings, all white stone and green shutters, with lanterns burning at every window because Vell believed darkness invited thieves. He was half right. Darkness invited Lena. It was the light he liked.

The party inside was audible from the street: fiddles, laughter, the particular braying laugh of men who had never once been told no by anyone who mattered. Harbor magnates were a special breed of corruption in Vhal Maren—they didn't steal from the poor directly, which would have been honest work. They stole sideways, through tariffs and tolls and "dredging fees," skimming the city's pockets so gently the city never felt the hand leave.

Tam had eaten nothing but dried eel and stolen bread for six days. Mother Quill's archive needed bribing to keep open. And the fence who might have moved their remaining charms out of the flats district wanted payment up front now that Wardens walked the mudflats in pairs.

Coin, then. And there was one purse in Vhal Maren deep enough to matter.

Lena paid the door-servant two coppers to be suddenly, urgently ill, took his tray of mulled wine, and walked into Ossian Vell's grand salon as if he belonged there, which—given that he was carrying wine and wearing authority—he very nearly did.

* * *

The trick with memory-theft, for those lucky enough never to have experienced it from either end, is that memories are not stored where people think they are. Amateurs imagine them filed away like letters in a drawer. In truth they live everywhere—in the hands, in the gut, in the particular way a man holds his glass when he's thinking about money. To steal a memory you have to find its body first.

Vell's body was easy to read. He stood near the fire with a brandy he wasn't drinking, watching his guests the way a spider watches a web—not with hunger, exactly, but with accounting. A decade of harbor ledgers lived in that stance. A decade of bribed inspectors and drowned cargo and widows whose husbands' boats had been "lost" to storms that existed only in Vell's books.

Lena drifted past him with the tray, murmured the customary nothing about the weather, and let his fingers brush the back of Vell's wrist.

It was like putting his hand into a river of grease and gold.

*Steal small,* screamed twelve years of habit. *Take the hour of the last bribe. Take the name of one inspector. Take something you can sell twice without anyone noticing the weight is gone.*

But small wouldn't buy Tam across whatever sea lay beyond the Sundered Door. Small wouldn't bribe Quill's archivists or buy passage on a ship whose captain could be trusted not to sell them to the Wardens by morning. The Door—if Tam's word meant anything, if the boy's Old Tongue meant anything—was real, and real doors require real keys, and keys in Vhal Maren cost coin.

Lena breathed out, and pulled.

* * *

Here is what a decade feels like when it comes out of a man:

It does not come gently. It comes like pulling a net full of live fish—all thrash and silver flash, fragments surfacing whether you want them or not. Lena braced against a pillar, tray steady, face pleasant, while ten years of Ossian Vell poured through him in a torrent of other people's misery.

A customs inspector named Brall, bought for four hundred crowns, then drowned in the outer channel when his price went up. The smell of the ink on the falsified manifests. A wife who knew—her face surfaced and sank, surfaced and sank, a woman with clever eyes who chose her comfort over her conscience and hated herself in the specific way of the comfortable. Storms invented on paper. Sailors' names crossed out. A strongbox beneath the floorboards of a counting-house, and beneath the strongbox—

Lena's breath caught.

Beneath the strongbox, in the deepest vault of Vell's memory, there was a thing Vell himself did not understand. He had bought it years ago from a dying diver—a map, or the ghost of one, inked on skin that had spent too long underwater. The diver had sworn he'd seen it with his own eyes: a door at the bottom of the drowned lighthouse off Gullwrack Point, standing open on black water, standing closed on nothing at all, depending on the tide, depending on the year, depending on whether the viewer's grandmother had come from a village that officially did not exist.

Vell had kept the map because he kept everything. He had never once looked at it properly. It lived in his memory the way a splinter lives in a thumb—present, ignored, occasionally throbbing.

And now it lived in Lena's.

* * *

He finished the theft in the space between two songs. When he stepped back, Vell was still holding his brandy, still wearing his faint accounting smile—but behind his eyes there was a new smoothness, a stretch of years gone blank as fresh snow. He would notice within days. He would blame drink, then age, then his wife, then his enemies. He would never, ever suspect the wine servant, because wine servants were furniture, and furniture doesn't take anything you'd miss.

That was the theory, anyway. That was always the theory.

Lena set down the tray, bowed to nobody in particular, and left the way he'd come, out into rain that tasted like salt and consequence.

* * *

The walk home took longer than it should have, because a decade of stolen memory is heavy in a way that has nothing to do with pounds. By the time he climbed the stairs to his shop above the tide-flats, his temples were pounding and the world had developed a faint double edge, like a print slightly misregistered.

Tam was awake, of course. The boy slept the way cats sleep—lightly, and only as a formality. He was sitting cross-legged on the floor amid Lena's stock of unsold charms, holding one up to each ear like seashells.

"You're back," Tam said, in his own tongue, which Lena still couldn't speak and yet somehow, increasingly, understood—the words arriving in his head pre-translated, like letters opened by someone else first. "You smell wrong."

"That's the smell of a rich man's parlor. Candles and hypocrisy."

"You're bleeding."

"I'm—" Lena touched his nose. His fingers came away red. Ah. So that was the cost this time. He fetched the cloth and pressed it to his face and sat down heavily on his stool, and told himself the nosebleed was the price, just the nosebleed, nothing more.

Except that wasn't quite true either, and he knew it, because even as he sat there, things were happening in his head that he hadn't authorized. The stolen decade was settling into him like silt into a harbor bed, and it was rearranging the landscape around itself. He looked at his hands and thought, unbidden, *Brall's hands shook at the end.* He looked at the rain on the window and thought, *the night the Merrow's Pride went down, the rain sounded exactly like this, and forty-one men died of a lie I can now recite from memory.*

He was carrying other people's sins now. That was the trade nobody warned you about: you didn't just take the memory, you took the weight of having done it. Somewhere in his skull, Ossian Vell's guilt had taken up residence, unpaid rent and all.

"Lena." Tam had put down the charms. The boy's dark eyes were fixed on him with that terrible child-clarity, the kind that sees straight through coats and trays and jokes. "You took too much."

"How would you know?"

"Because your shadow is louder than you are."

Lena laughed, and then stopped laughing, because Tam wasn't joking. Children who spoke dead languages didn't joke about shadows. "I got what we need," he said instead. "Coin, soon. Passage, maybe. And something else." He closed his eyes, and there it was, patient as a pearl: the diver's map, the drowned lighthouse off Gullwrack Point, the door beneath it that was sometimes open and sometimes nothing at all. "I know where the Door is, Tam. Or where it was. Under the old light at Gullwrack."

Tam went very still. Then, slowly, he smiled—and it was the first smile Lena had seen from him that wasn't borrowed from some adult's face, the first one that belonged entirely to a boy. He said something long and liquid in the Old Tongue, and though the translation arrived late and imperfect, one word came through clean as a bell:

*Home.*

"Yes," Lena said quietly. "Home."

* * *

He should have slept. Instead he sat up with a candle and did the arithmetic of what he'd done, because Lena Reyes had survived twelve years by keeping honest books with himself, even when the entries hurt.

Entry one: he had stolen ten years from a living man. Not a charm's worth, not an hour—a decade. Vell would feel that absence the way an amputee feels weather. Some part of Lena, the part that had sold a widow her husband's happiest hour and charged her fairly, whispered that Vell deserved it, that men who drowned sailors for margin had no right to their yesterdays. But another part—the older part, the tired part—knew that "deserved" was a word thieves used the way drunkards used railings. For balance. Not because it held.

Entry two: the theft had been loud. Small thefts whispered; this one had roared. If any Warden-sensitive happened to be working the harbor tonight—if any of them kept instruments tuned to the deep magic, as the rumors said—Ilsa Corvane among them—then somewhere across the sleeping city, a needle had just jumped.

Entry three, and this was the one he wrote smallest, in the back of the ledger where he kept the truths he didn't want to reread: the map in his head had shown him the lighthouse, yes. But it had shown him something else too, something the diver had seen and Vell had never understood, and now Lena understood it less, because understanding it required believing it.

The diver had sworn that when he looked at the Door, the Door looked back. And the face in it—young, frightened, unmistakable—

Had been his own.

Lena pinched out the candle and sat in the dark with the rain coming down and a decade of a stranger's sins coiled in his chest, and told himself it was coincidence. Faces in visions were tricks of fear and salt-spray. There were a hundred explanations.

Outside, far off toward the harbor, a bell began to ring—the Wardens' signal bell, two short strokes and a long. Not alarm. Not yet. Just attention. The city, turning over in its sleep, beginning to wonder what had woken it.

Beside him, Tam stirred and murmured in his sleep, one soft syllable of the Old Tongue that Lena's bones understood before his mind did:

*Soon.*
