## 11. Hunted Through the Tide-Flats

### Ilsa

The tide-flats at dawn were the color of an apology—grey, wet, and offered too late to be believed.

Ilsa Corvane crouched at the edge of the last cobbled street and watched the mud breathe. That was the only word for it. The flats inhaled with the retreating water, exposing channels and sandbars and the ribs of boats that had been "lost" so many decades ago that their owners' grandchildren now paid taxes on other ships. Everything out here was a record of something the city preferred not to discuss. She had always found that fitting.

Two days since Dreth had handed her the order: *Find the charm-thief. Find the child. Report by week's end.* Two days of following a trail that any competent tracker could have followed and any *honest* tracker would have lost on purpose.

She was not sure which kind she was anymore.

The mud held prints. Small ones, barefoot, going out. Larger ones, boot-heeled, careful, going out after—and here was the thing that made her jaw tighten—the larger prints were *good*. Deliberately broken gait, weight rolled to the outside edge, a half-step backward inserted every dozen paces like a sentence with a lie folded into it. Lena Reyes knew he was followed. He was good at this.

He was not good enough, because he kept stopping.

She could see him now, three hundred yards out where the flats went silver, a thin silhouette crouched beside a tide-pool, and beside him the small shape of the boy, pointing at something in the water with the absolute authority of an eight-year-old who has located a crab. The boy's voice carried across the flat water, high and bright, in that language that made Ilsa's oath-scar itch behind her ear.

*Old Tongue.* The tongue of a kingdom that did not exist. Speaking it was treason. Listening to it, apparently, was becoming a habit.

"Come on, Sergeant," she murmured to herself. "Do your job."

Her boots disagreed. Her boots, in fact, had been disagreeing for two days, carrying her along the trail at a pace she could only describe as *reluctantly thorough*. She had searched three buildings she could have raided. She had interviewed six witnesses she could have arrested. She had stood outside a shuttered shop above the tide-flats for the better part of an hour, telling herself she was establishing surveillance, while actually watching lamplight move behind the shutters and thinking about a man who had given her a charm woven from her own forgotten grief without charging her a single coin.

Wardens paid for memories. It was policy. He'd given hers away free, and that, more than anything, was what kept her up at night. Thieves wanted things. What did you call a thief who gave?

The boy splashed. Lena laughed—Ilsa heard it carry across the mud, startled and unguarded, a sound like a lock opening—and something in her chest performed an act of insubordination so complete that she decided, right there, ankle-deep in civic ambiguity, that she was going to have to have a conversation with him.

Not an arrest. A conversation.

She told herself this was tactics. She was almost convincing.

### Lena

Lena heard the Warden coming the way you hear weather: not by sound but by the way everything else went quiet.

Tam felt it too. The boy stopped mid-sentence about the crab—which had, in Lena's professional opinion, been winning—and turned toward the landward mudflats with his head cocked, listening with whatever sense children from erased kingdoms used instead of ears.

"Warden," Tam said. One of his Common words. He'd been collecting them like coins off a dead man's eyes: *Warden. Run. Bread. No.* An efficient vocabulary. Lena wished he'd learn *maybe* or *let's discuss our options*, but children were pragmatists.

"I know." Lena rose slowly, hands visible, heart doing something undignified against his ribs. Twelve years of rules, and rule one had been *never let a Warden get within speaking distance*. Rule two had been *there is no rule two because rule one is the whole religion*.

And yet here he was, standing in open mud at dawn, waiting for her to arrive, because some traitorous part of him had recognized her footfall pattern two nights ago through a wall and had spent the interval not running.

"You could go," Tam said, tugging his sleeve. The boy pointed back toward the drowned undercity, toward escape, offering it with the simple generosity of someone who had already lost a kingdom and considered flight a reasonable default.

"We could," Lena agreed. "But she'd follow. And she follows better than I run." He glanced down at the boy. "Also, I owe her a conversation."

Tam looked at him with the expression of a child who has just learned adults are even stupider than advertised.

Ilsa Corvane came across the flats like a blade being drawn slowly—not fast, not threatening, just inevitable. She stopped ten paces out, close enough to talk, far enough to lunge, and Lena noted with grim admiration that she'd chosen ground where the mud would slow any runner equally. Fair. Professional. He was beginning to suspect fairness was the cruelest thing about her.

"Sergeant Corvane," he said. "You've been following me for two days."

"You've known for two days."

"The backward step every twelve paces is very distinctive. Most people fake a limp. You fake *doubt*. It's unsettlingly convincing."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—annoyance or approval, hard to say, possibly both sharing custody. "You're aware I'm supposed to arrest you."

"I'm aware you've had two days to arrest me and instead you've given me a guided tour of your surveillance techniques. If this is interrogation, it's the gentlest I've ever experienced, and I once had a tax assessor apologize to me mid-audit."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"That." She gestured at all of him. "The charm. The patter. You use it on everyone. Widows, fences, Wardens. You make people feel *seen* so they forget to look at you."

The words landed harder than they should have, mostly because they were true, and partly because she said them with the weary precision of someone describing her own reflection. Lena let the smile drop. It felt strange, his face without it. Unfurnished.

"All right," he said quietly. "No patter. Ask me what you came to ask."

For a long moment the only sound was the flats breathing and Tam, mercifully, interrogating the crab again.

"Why didn't you run?" she asked finally. "You had the undercity. You had routes I haven't mapped. You had—" she nodded at the boy "—a head start and a reason. Why stand here?"

Because, he almost said, *you're the first person in twelve years who looks at me like I'm a person and not a transaction, and I am so catastrophically lonely that a Warden's disapproval feels like company.* Because when she'd stood in his shop doorway, something in his chest had recognized something in hers, two locked rooms with matching doors.

What he said was: "Because you burned a shop on Cordwainer Street last month. Procedure, they'll call it. And you signed the report with your left hand shaking so badly the ink smeared." Her face went still as slate. "I know what duty costs you, Sergeant. I pay a version of the same bill. So no, I didn't run. Running is for people with nothing to say."

"I could arrest you right now."

"You could."

"And the boy."

"He'd go with you quietly. He's brave like that. It's awful to watch."

Her hand wasn't near her irons. He noticed that. He noticed also that she kept glancing—at the horizon, at the mud, at the pale smear of dawn—cataloguing sightlines, and that every sightline she catalogued was one that *concealed* rather than revealed. She was mapping his escape route. For him. With the same meticulous competence she'd use to catch him.

"Tide comes in at midmorning," she said abruptly. "It cuts the flats into islands. Anyone caught out there drowns in four feet of water because the mud holds your legs like a debt collector." She was looking past him now, out toward the far channel where the ruined bones of the old seawall broke the water. "There's a fisherman's hut on the second sandbar. Collapsed roof, but the chimney stands. Dry inside. My grandmother used it." A pause, and then, quieter, with the air of a woman stepping off a cliff and pretending it was a staircase: "I lost the trail here. Mud's treacherous. Prints wash out. I'll report that I picked it up again heading north, toward Gullmarket. That buys you until week's end. After that, Dreth sends someone who doesn't stop to talk."

Lena stared at her. Twelve years of reading faces for a living, and he couldn't find the trick in this one. There wasn't one. That was the trick.

"Why?" he asked. Just that. Stripped of charm, stripped of wit, because anything else would have insulted them both.

Ilsa Corvane looked at the boy—barefoot, mud-splattered, holding a crab with the tenderness of a small priest—and then at Lena, and whatever she saw made her exhale like a woman setting down something heavy.

"Because I burned Cordwainer Street," she said, "and I can still smell it. Because my orders are to find a child and I've met the child, and he offered me half his bread yesterday when he thought I wasn't looking, and I *took it*, and I ate it, and I have committed treason over bread and I'm not even sorry." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word, and she hated it, he could tell, hated the fracture in her own masonry. "Because somewhere between your shop door and this mudflat I stopped being able to pretend the law and the right thing are the same animal, and I would rather be lost out here than found back there."

The tide whispered in. Somewhere a curlew cried its one opinion.

"Okay," Lena said softly.

"*Okay?*" She almost laughed, sharp and disbelieving. "I just confessed to treason over baked goods and your answer is *okay*?"

"What would you prefer? A speech? I'm told I give lovely speeches but they're mostly inventory." He held out his hand—then thought better of it, then did it anyway. "Thank you, Ilsa."

Her name in his mouth did something to her face that she'd deny under torture. She didn't take his hand. She looked at it, and then at him, and then she took it—briefly, firmly, a soldier's grip, except that neither of them let go quite as fast as soldiers do.

"Reyes," she said. "If we're doing this, there are rules. You don't contact me unless it's life or death. You don't tell me where you're hiding. You don't make me any more charms." A beat. "I still have the one you gave me. I haven't decided whether to destroy it."

"Have you looked at it again?"

"That's none of your—"

"You have."

"I will arrest you *so fast*—"

"Mother Quill says the Door is real," Lena said, and watched the words hit her like cold water. "The Sundered Door. Under the old lighthouse. The boy is the key—or his blood is—and everything the Wardens burn, everyone Dreth purges, it's all to keep a door shut on a kingdom full of people who are still *in there*, Ilsa. Still alive. Still singing in a language your oath-scar itches at." He stepped closer, mud sucking at his boots. "I'm going to open it. I don't fully know how yet. But when I do, I'd rather not be arrested at the threshold by someone whose grandmother fished off the second sandbar."

Ilsa was silent for a long time. Out on the flats, the light was turning gold, gilding the wreck-ribs and the breathing mud, making the whole drowned world look briefly, treacherously beautiful.

"Dreth reviews my reports at week's end," she said at last. "After that, I'm either a traitor or I'm yours. I haven't decided which frightens me more." She turned to go, then paused, back straight, voice carefully level. "The hut on the sandbar. Take the boy. Tide's patient but it isn't kind."

Then she walked away across the mud, bootprints precise, doubt folded into every twelfth step—but this time, Lena noticed, the false steps pointed *toward* the city, not away from it. Building her alibi in reverse. Lying in mud before anyone asked her to.

Tam appeared at his elbow, crabless, solemn.

"She lied," the boy observed, in Common, with deep satisfaction, as though lying were a skill he approved of in adults.

"Yes."

"For us?"

Lena watched the grey-green figure shrink toward the shore, and felt something in his chest that hadn't moved in twelve years shift its weight.

"For us," he said. "God help us both."

### Ilsa

Dreth's office smelled of ink and cold iron, the way justice smelled when it was administered by men who never got mud on their boots.

"Well, Sergeant?" High Warden Dreth didn't look up from his ledger. He rarely did. Ledgers were the only witnesses he trusted; they never trembled, never wept, never offered anyone bread.

"Lost the trail on the flats," Ilsa said. Each word placed like a brick in a wall she intended to live behind. "Tide took the prints. I picked up a sighting in Gullmarket—a charm-seller matching Reyes's description, moving north. I've requested warrants for the Gullmarket stalls."

"Mm." Dreth turned a page. "The child?"

"No sign. Locals claim no child's been seen. Either they're lying or the flats swallowed him." She paused, and added the detail that would make the lie load-bearing: "There were wader tracks near the old seawall. Someone's been out to the ruins. Smugglers, most likely. I recommend a patrol."

"Approved." Dreth wrote something. Then, without warning, he looked up, and his eyes were the color of ledger paper. "You've been out there a long time, Corvane. Two days for one charm-thief."

"The flats are difficult terrain, sir."

"They are." He returned to his ledger. "Difficult terrain makes for difficult loyalties. Dismissed."

Ilsa saluted, turned, and walked out with her spine straight and her heart hammering like a fist on a locked door. In the corridor, young Warden Pell—whom she'd once spared a flogging for a kindness he never learned she knew about—fell into step beside her and said nothing at all, which was its own kind of testimony.

At the end of the corridor, alone in the grey light, Ilsa put her hand in her pocket and closed it around the little charm. She still hadn't destroyed it. She suspected now she never would.

Week's end, she thought. Then the net closes, and I'm either the net or the fish.

Out beyond the window, far off across the water, the tide-flats gleamed gold, and she allowed herself—for exactly three seconds—to imagine a door opening.
