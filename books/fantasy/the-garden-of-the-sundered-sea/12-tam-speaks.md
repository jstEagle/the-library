## 12. Tam Speaks

The storm had been sitting on Vhal Maren for three days like an unwelcome relative—present, loud, and refusing to leave—and by the third evening Lena had run out of ways to keep a traumatized child entertained in a two-room shop above the tide-flats.

He had tried everything. Cards (Tam arranged them into spirals and stared at them as though reading futures). Charms (Tam held each one to his ear like a seashell and shook his head solemnly, as if to say *no, this one is lying*). Cooking (Tam had watched Lena burn the fish with an expression of profound, silent judgment that made Lena feel he was being graded by a very small, very disappointed professor).

"You know," Lena said, scraping the ruined fish into the fire, "in my professional opinion, you're the worst houseguest I've ever had. And I once sheltered a smuggler who slept standing up and bit people."

Tam looked up from his card-spiral and smiled. It was the first smile Lena had seen from him since the raid on the flats—the night old Ferro had died holding the Warden's attention so a thief and a mute boy could vanish into the flooded undercity. Since then Tam had been quiet even by his standards: not mute now, exactly, but sealed, like a jar turned down tight.

Lena understood sealed. He was, professionally speaking, the most sealed man in Vhal Maren.

So he didn't push. He fed the boy, dried his socks by the stove, taught him a cheating shuffle that Tam mastered in four attempts while it had taken Lena a week at whatever age he'd learned it—which, come to think of it, was another thing he couldn't remember learning. The list of those things was getting long. He kept it in a notebook under a floorboard, along with everything else about himself that didn't add up.

It was nearly midnight when the storm finally broke—not ended, but broke open for a moment, the way storms do, offering a pocket of unnatural stillness. The rain stopped all at once. The wind dropped. Even the sea seemed to hold its breath beneath the floorboards.

And in that silence, Tam spoke.

Not Old Tongue. Common.

"Her eyes were yours."

Lena's hands went still over the cards.

The boy's voice was thin from disuse, rusty at the hinges, but steady. He wasn't looking at Lena. He was looking at the middle distance, at something far away and long ago, the way people look when they're not remembering so much as being remembered through.

"The night the door closed," Tam said. "I was very small. Smaller than this." He held a hand at his own chest height, then lower. "But I remember her face. Everyone remembers her face. She came to the door last of all."

"Tam—" Lena began, and didn't know what he was going to say after that, so he said nothing, which was perhaps the wisest sentence of his career.

"She stood where the door was going to be," the boy went on, "and she put her hand flat against the air, and the air became stone, and the stone became a door. There were soldiers behind us. Not your soldiers. *Their* soldiers, the ones with the white fire, the ones who unmake things. They were coming up the hill and everyone was running and screaming, and she just... stood there. Facing both ways. Us behind her, them ahead."

His small hands moved as he spoke, unconsciously shaping it: a hand pressed flat, a door swinging shut.

"My grandmother said later—we said it for years after, before the forgetting got thick—that she could have gone through. The door stayed open for her. It waited. That's what they tell children, that the door loved its keeper and would have held itself open until the end of the world if she'd asked." Tam's voice wavered, steadied. "She didn't ask."

"Why?" Lena asked. His mouth was dry. "Why wouldn't she—"

"Because of what was following us." Tam finally turned and looked at him, and his eyes were too old for his face, the way eyes get when they've been carried a long way by smaller and smaller bodies. "You don't know yet. Nobody knows anymore, because she took the knowing with her when she locked it. But there was something behind the white-fire soldiers. Something the soldiers were also running from. And she chose. She chose to seal us in with it rather than let it out into your world."

He said it simply, without drama, the way children report terrible things they don't fully understand: *the dog died, the house burned, the monster followed us.*

"And then," Tam said, "she spoke the locking words, and the door closed, and the last thing anyone saw of the outside was her face. Her eyes." He touched his own eyelids, gently, as if handling something borrowed. "They were your eyes."

* * *

Lena did not remember crossing the room. He found himself at the window with the shutter half-open, looking out at nothing, at the black sheet of rain resumed, at the drowned lights of the harbor district smeared across wet air. His heart was doing something arrhythmic and embarrassing.

"That's not possible," he said. It came out steadier than he felt, which was the one useful trick his voice knew. "I'm not from Callowmere. I'm nobody. I'm a charm-seller from—I'm from—"

From where?

The question opened underneath him like a trapdoor, the way it always did, and this time he fell all the way down it.

No parents. No hometown. No childhood. A first memory that began mid-escape at fourteen, running through streets he couldn't name with a satchel of stolen charms he couldn't remember stealing, pursued by Wardens whose faces he'd never been able to recall no matter how hard he tried—as if someone had taken a cloth to the whole first act of his life and wiped it down to bare boards.

Twelve years he'd lived with that gap. He'd built rules around it, walls, a whole architecture of careful smallness: *steal small, sell cheap, never be remembered.* He'd told himself the missing years were a mercy. That whoever had cut them out had done him a kindness, or at least a service.

Now a child of eight was telling him the keeper of the Sundered Door had his eyes.

"There's a woman," Lena said slowly, turning back from the window. "In your telling. Your grandmother's telling. The one who closed the door. Does she have a name? In the stories?"

Tam nodded. He opened his mouth and produced a word in Old Tongue, soft and round, and then, laboriously, translated it the way Mother Quill had taught him during their visits: "Maerin."

The name landed in Lena's chest like a coin dropped down a well. He waited for the splash. He waited for the memory to rise up and meet it.

Nothing rose. Just depth. Just dark water going down and down.

"I don't know that name," he whispered.

"No," Tam agreed, with the terrible gentleness children deploy when they are being kinder than the situation deserves. "But you cried when I said it. So some part of you does."

Lena touched his own cheek. Wet. When had that happened?

He sat down heavily on the stool by the cold stove, and Tam—matter-of-fact, unhurried, the way of children and cats—climbed onto the bench beside him and leaned against his arm, and together they watched the storm do its work against the shutters.

"Tell me the rest," Lena said finally. "All of it. Every fragment. Don't fix it, don't make it tidy. Fragments are how I live."

So Tam talked, in his halting, newly-learned Common, piecing the Sundering night together from things he'd been told and things he'd half-seen and things that had soaked into him the way dye soaks through cloth. The white-fire soldiers had come at dusk. The evacuation had been chaos—Callowmere hadn't fallen in battle; it had been *withdrawn*, pulled back street by street toward the Door like a tide going out. And at the center of it, always, the woman with the tired eyes and the bloodline, Maerin, directing the retreat with a voice that carried over armies.

"She wasn't a soldier," Tam said. "She was a mapmaker. That's why they chose her. The Door only opens for the blood, but it only *holds* for someone who can read the lock. And the lock is a memory. One specific memory. The most important one the blood-heir owns."

"What memory?" Lena asked.

Tam shrugged, small shoulders rising and falling. "Nobody knows. That's the whole point of a lock made of memory—it hides inside the person keeping it. My grandmother used to say: 'The key is not a thing you carry. It is a thing you *are*. Lose yourself, and you lose the way home.'" He paused. "She said it sadly. Like she was quoting someone who'd learned it the hard way."

Lena's notebook suddenly felt very heavy under the floorboard, twelve years of gaps and anomalies, and he thought: *I should get it. I should write this down.* He did not move. Some part of him understood, with a thief's instinct for weight, that he was already carrying more than the notebook could hold.

"Here's what I keep catching on," he said aloud, because saying things helped, even when no one answered, even when the answer was the kind you dreaded. "Say your story is true. Say the keeper was my sister. Fine. Terrible, but fine. Then explain this part to me: how does a blood-heir of Callowmere end up in Vhal Maren with no past, selling charms to widows? If Maerin stayed behind the Door—if she sealed herself in with her people—then I'm either behind that Door too, or I'm..." He gestured vaguely at himself, at the whole improbable fact of his existence. "...spurious."

Tam considered this with the gravity of a magistrate.

"Maybe you got out," he said. "Before the closing. Maybe she sent you."

"Then I'd remember Callowmere."

"Maybe you weren't supposed to remember." Tam said it simply, and it went through Lena like a fishhook. "My grandmother said the Door takes a price from its keeper. Not just the lock-memory. Everything near it. Everything that could be used to find it again. Maybe—" the boy frowned, searching for the words, this language still stiff as new boots— "maybe your sister didn't just lock the Door. Maybe she locked *you*. To keep you safe outside it. To make sure no one could ever torture the way home out of you."

The silence that followed was long enough to have weather of its own.

Because if that was true—if Maerin had carved her brother hollow to protect him—then every rule Lena had ever lived by, every careful small theft, every year of deliberate invisibility, wasn't cowardice or caution or a thief's wisdom.

It was obedience. He had spent twelve years faithfully executing his sister's last instruction, without ever knowing he'd received one.

*Steal small. Sell cheap. Never be remembered.*

Never be remembered. Because remembering him meant tracing him backward, past the wiped years, to a kingdom that officially did not exist and a Door that must never be found.

"Oh," Lena said. It was a very small word for the size of the thing landing on him.

Tam patted his arm twice, the universal gesture of *there, there*, and Lena laughed—a cracked, wet, astonished sound—and then the laugh turned into something else and he put his face in his hands and let it happen, because there was no one to see and the boy already knew him better than anyone alive.

* * *

Later—much later, past the smallest hour, with Tam asleep in the back room curled around the card-spiral he'd insisted on preserving—Lena sat at the table with the notebook open and a candle guttering between him and the truth.

He wrote:

*The keeper of the Door was Maerin. My sister. I have a sister. Had. Has. The grammar of sisters is beyond me tonight.*

*I am Callowmerean. I am the blood. Which means—*

He stopped. The candle flame leaned, straightened, leaned again, as if it too were flinching.

Which means the lock is in me.

That was the arithmetic he'd been avoiding since Tam said *her eyes were yours.* The Door required a blood-heir's memory as its keystone. Maerin had sealed it with hers. But Maerin was behind the Door now, sealed in with her people, unreachable. And a lock, any lock, any lock worth the name, needed its key on the *outside*.

Or hidden inside the nearest living blood.

Himself. His blank, wiped, carefully emptied self. Fourteen years old and scrubbed clean, dumped in Vhal Maren with nothing—not because someone had robbed him, but because the robbery *was* the hiding place. You want to hide a keystone? Bury it in a man who doesn't know he's carrying it, in a city that punishes magic, among people trained never to look twice at anything.

He had spent twelve years perfecting his own invisibility. He'd thought it was survival.

It was the lock maintaining itself.

Lena set down the pen. His hand was shaking, and not from the cold.

Because here was the shape of it, the full terrible geometry: the Door could not be opened without the keystone. The keystone was his erased childhood. And his erased childhood contained—had to contain—the memory Maerin had used to lock it. The one specific memory. The most important one the blood-heir owned.

To free Callowmere, he would have to remember everything.

Including, at the bottom of it all, whatever it was his sister had chosen to die behind a door for. Including whatever had made her stay. Including, possibly, the reason she'd sent him away instead of letting him stay with her—and whether that reason was love, or whether it was that she'd needed one heir outside the Door, one key in the world, and had chosen him the way you choose a tool.

He didn't believe that last one. He refused to believe it. But refusal, he'd learned, was not the same as knowing, and knowing was waiting for him at the bottom of his own head like a body in deep water.

There was a knock at the shutter.

Lena froze. Past curfew. Past sense. Three slow knocks, then two fast—the rhythm old Ferro used, except Ferro was dead, had died on the mudflats buying them their escape, and dead men did not climb stairs in storms.

The knock came again. Three slow. Two fast.

Lena blew out the candle, took the knife from under the floorboard—he kept a knife the way other men kept opinions, close and rarely examined—and crossed the dark room on the balls of his feet, twelve years of practice moving a body quietly through a world that mustn't notice it.

He lifted the shutter an inch.

Rain. Wind. The drowned lights of the harbor. And pinned under the door, sodden and streaming, a single sheet of paper—a page torn from a Warden's ledger, the ink bleeding but legible, written in a sharp, disciplined hand he recognized from the canvass of the flats, from the woman who had stood in his shop and fenced with him and left with a charm she shouldn't have accepted:

*They know. Dreth has the flats scheduled for purge at dawn. The lighthouse. Go to the lighthouse. Trust no one else. —I.C.*

Below it, smaller, almost an afterthought, almost human:

*P.S. The charm worked. I hate that it worked. Come find out why, if you survive the morning.*
