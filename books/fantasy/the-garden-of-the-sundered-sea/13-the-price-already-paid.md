## 13. The Price Already Paid

Lena did not sleep that night. He sat at his workbench with a single candle burning and Tam's blanket-wrapped form breathing softly in the back room, and he tried to do the one thing no memory-thief should ever attempt: steal from himself.

It was not as simple as it sounded. A thief's craft, properly done, was a matter of edges—finding the seam where one memory ended and another began, slipping the knife of attention in, and lifting the whole cloth away intact. But you could not pick your own pocket. Every time Lena reached inward for the years before his fourteenth winter, his hands came back empty, and worse than empty: *smooth*. There was a place in him where memories ought to have been, and it had been sanded flat like driftwood, worn down by twelve years of tides until it felt almost natural.

Almost.

He held his hands over the candle and looked at them. Thief's hands. Clever hands. Hands that knew forty ways to open a lock and could weave a charm from raw grief without trembling—and where, he asked them now, *where did you learn?*

The hands said nothing. Hands were terrible conversationalists.

"Right," he told them anyway, because talking to oneself was either madness or method and after tonight he'd stopped caring which. "Let's review the evidence, shall we?"

He laid it out on the bench like stolen goods, which, in a sense, it was.

Item the first: a boy who spoke Old Tongue, who had walked through curfew and storm to find one specific door in all of drowned Vhal Maren, and who had called Lena by a name Lena had never heard anyone say aloud—a name that had made every charm in the shop ring like struck glass.

Item the second: Mother Quill's blind eyes going wide, her voice dropping to the register people used for plague ships. *Callowmere.* The kingdom that wasn't. The Sundered Door. The pact that required a blood-heir's memory as its lock.

Item the third, and this was the one that kept catching on his ribs like a fishhook: the gaps. No parents. No hometown. No first day of anything. His earliest memory began mid-stride, fourteen years old, running down an alley in a city whose name he'd never learned because everyone in Vhal Maren already knew where they were, and why would anyone ask?

Nobody asks a man where he's from if he acts like the question is rude.

He had built a whole life on that rudeness. Twelve years of small thefts and smaller charms, of being forgettable on purpose, of selling widows their husbands' happiest hours for three coppers and telling himself it was kindness when it was also camouflage. He had thought he was hiding from the Wardens.

He might have been hiding from himself.

* * *

Tam woke near dawn, the way children do, all at once and without apology. He padded out barefoot into the shop's main room, where Lena had fallen asleep sitting up with his cheek on the ledger of clients he kept in code, and stood regarding him with those grave dark eyes.

"You didn't sleep," Tam said. His Common had been arriving in pieces all week, like furniture delivered one leg at a time, but he was getting frighteningly good at observation.

"I slept beautifully," Lena lied. "I'm doing it right now. It's very advanced."

Tam climbed onto the stool across the workbench and folded his small hands on the wood in an attitude of such perfect seriousness that Lena had to bite down on a laugh. Where had an eight-year-old learned to look like a magistrate? From the same place he'd learned Old Tongue, presumably. From a kingdom that officially did not exist.

"Ask me," Lena said, and the humor went out of him like a snuffed wick. "What you saw. That night. Ask me properly, and I'll answer properly."

Tam considered him for a long moment. Then, in careful Common, with the accent of somewhere very far away and very long ago:

"The woman at the door. She had your face."

"My sister," Lena said. The word came out steady, which surprised him. "Maerin."

"You know her name." Not an accusation. Worse—an observation.

"I know it the way I know the tide comes in. I don't remember learning it. I just—" He spread his hands. "It's there. Like a stone at the bottom of a well. You can't see it but you can hear it when you drop something past it."

"What else is at the bottom?"

And there it was. The question. Eight years old and already asking better questions than High Warden Dreth ever would.

"I don't know," Lena said honestly. "That's the problem, little one. I think everything else is at the bottom. And I think someone threw it all down there on purpose."

* * *

Mother Quill's archive smelled of dust, lamp oil, and the particular melancholy of paper that nobody had been allowed to read for generations. She sat in her chair by the window she could not see out of, hands folded, listening the way blind people listen—with her whole body turned toward you like a flower toward light.

"Say it again," she said. "The name the boy gave you."

"Lena Reyes." He hesitated. "But that's not what he said. What he said was—" and here he made the sound again, the Old Tongue word that had cracked something open in his chest, that had set his charms ringing. "That."

Mother Quill was quiet for so long that Lena checked whether she was still breathing.

"That is not a name," she said finally. "That is a title. In Callowmere's tongue—the tongue we are both committing treason by discussing, so lower your voice, child, though I suppose neither of us has much left to lose—it means *the hand that holds the lock*."

"The lock."

"The Sundered Door does not have a keyhole, as far as any fragment records. It has a *keeper*. Someone who carries the lock inside themselves. The pact was woven so that the Door could only be opened by the blood-heir who sealed it—or by whatever holds that heir's memory of the sealing." Her sightless eyes found him with unnerving accuracy. "You told me last week that your history begins at fourteen. Mid-escape, you said. Running."

"Yes."

"And you've spent twelve years stealing other people's memories. Small ones. Harmless ones. You told me that too, and I believed you, because you have an honest face and dishonest hands." She smiled faintly at her own joke, then let it fall away. "Lena. Who taught you the craft?"

"No one. I just—knew it. I woke up knowing how to take a memory apart without bruising it, like I'd been born holding the tools."

"Mm." She turned her face toward the window, toward the gray light and the sound of gulls arguing over the tide-flats. "There are two kinds of forgetting, in my experience. The kind that happens *to* you, and the kind you do *to yourself*. The first leaves ragged edges. Torn pages. You can always tell where the wound was. But the second kind—" she tapped her own temple, "—the second kind is surgical. Clean margins. A story with the first chapter removed so neatly you'd swear the book always began on page two."

Lena's mouth had gone dry. "I've seen work like that. Once. Early on. A man who paid me to check whether his wife had been charmed, because she'd forgotten their wedding entirely, and I remember thinking whoever did it was a master, because there wasn't a seam anywhere—"

He stopped.

Twelve years ago. A frightened boy of fourteen with no past and an impossible skill set, waking up in a city full of Wardens, knowing exactly how to make himself unmemorable. Knowing exactly how to survive interrogation.

Knowing, because he had already survived one.

"Oh," Lena said. It was a very small word for the size of the thing falling on him.

"There it is," Mother Quill said gently.

"I did it to myself." He heard his own voice from a great distance. "Before Vhal Maren. Before—I stole my own childhood to get through questioning. To get through *something*. And if the Door's lock is the heir's memory of the sealing, and Maerin was the heir, and I was—" his breath caught, "—if I took her memory too, or mine of her, or mine of *everything*, then I didn't run from Callowmere carrying nothing. I ran carrying the lock. I've been walking around for twelve years as a walking keyhole and I didn't—"

He put his head down on the ledger and laughed, or something adjacent to laughing, the way a man drowns adjacently to swimming.

Mother Quill let him. She was good at letting people break quietly; you could tell she'd had practice.

When he surfaced, she said, "Do you understand what this means?"

"That I'm an idiot?"

"That you're both things at once, dear. That's rather the point of locks." She leaned forward. "The Door cannot be opened by force. The Wardens have known that for two hundred years—it's why they burn instead of breach. It cannot be opened by the boy alone; he carries the blood, but not the memory. It can only be opened by whatever holds the keystone. And the keystone, if my reading of the fragments is right, is not hidden in some vault beneath the lighthouse."

She reached across and laid her papery hand over his thief's hands.

"It's hidden in you. In the exact place you're most afraid to look."

* * *

He walked home the long way, along the seawall, because the short way passed the Warden garrison and because he needed the wind. The tide was out, exposing the mudflats in all their pocked, gleaming ugliness, and somewhere out there under the gray water and grayer mud lay a lighthouse that geography itself had agreed to forget.

A jailer, he thought. All this time, he'd thought of himself as a thief, a survivor, a small honest crook in a large dishonest city. But a jailer doesn't have to be cruel to be a jailer. A jailer just has to hold the key and refuse to use it. And he hadn't even had the dignity of refusing—he'd lost the key inside his own pocket and spent twelve years patting himself down and finding nothing.

Maerin had sealed a whole kingdom behind a door to save it—or to save the world from it; he still couldn't untangle which, and suspected the truth was both—and she had done it knowing, surely knowing, that her little brother was the fragile part of the mechanism. Had she planned it? Had she pressed his own stolen childhood into his hands like a bundle wrapped against the cold and said *run, don't look back, don't even remember what you're not looking back at*?

Or had he done it to himself, in terror, in a cell, with Wardens' questions falling on him like hammers—and thereby, unknowingly, done exactly what the pact required? Carried the lock out through the burning streets because the lock *had* to leave, had to be scattered somewhere no Warden would ever think to search?

Inside a forgettable man in a forgettable shop above the tide-flats.

He laughed again, alone on the seawall, and this time it came out sounding like what it was.

The horror wasn't that he was the key. Keys were useful. The horror was the arithmetic of it: to open the Door, he would have to give back everything he'd stolen from himself. Every year. Every face. And he had no idea what was in there. Twelve years of small thefts had taught him what people's memories weighed—a happy hour, a mother's face, a decade of a corrupt man's greed—and he knew that some memories were not survivable twice. Whatever was at the bottom of his well had made a fourteen-year-old boy choose oblivion over keeping it. That boy had been braver than him, maybe. Or the thing in the well had been heavier than anything he could imagine.

And Tam was waiting at home. Tam, who had crossed a drowned country and a hostile city to find the hand that holds the lock, and who deserved better than a keeper who flinched.

Lena turned back toward the flats. The sun was coming up behind the cloud-bank, turning the mud to beaten pewter, and far off, where the water met the sky in a line too straight to be natural, he thought—for the first time, and only for a heartbeat—that he could see the shape of something tall and thin standing in the sea. Watching. Waiting for him to stop being afraid.

He wasn't ready. He might never be ready.

But tomorrow, he decided, he would go to the old harbor district and do the hardest theft of his life—not from a stranger, not from a widow, not from a corrupt magnate, but from the locked room inside his own skull. He would need help. He would need money, and maps, and a fence he trusted, and possibly a miracle.

And, though he did not yet know it, he would need a Warden.

But that realization was still two tides away, and for now, Lena Reyes—thief, coward, jailer, key—walked home through the waking city to make breakfast for a king's descendant, whistling a tune he did not recognize and could not stop humming.
