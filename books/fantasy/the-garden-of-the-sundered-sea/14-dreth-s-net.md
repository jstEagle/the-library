## 14. Dreth's Net

Ilsa Corvane had lied to High Warden Dreth exactly once in eleven years of service, and she had spent the four days since that lie rehearsing the many ways it would kill her.

The lie itself had been small, as lies went. A sentence and a half in her morning report: *Trail lost at the mudflats; subject likely fled the city by the north causeway; recommend reduced presence pending further intelligence.* She had written it in her neatest hand, the one she used for reports that would be read aloud at tribunal, because a lie told carelessly was just a confession with extra steps.

What she had not accounted for was that Dreth read everything twice.

She knew this now, standing before his desk in the Hall of Wardens while rain needled the high windows, because he had laid her report out flat between them like a corpse on a slab, and beside it lay three other documents: a tide-chart, a fishmonger's deposition, and a charcoal rubbing of a boot print taken from the flats at dawn.

"Your report says you lost him at the third channel marker," Dreth said. He was not a large man. He had never needed to be. He had the stillness of deep water, and deep water drowned more people than storms did. "The fishmonger says a woman matching your description stood at the third channel marker for the better part of an hour, talking to no one, and then walked back toward the city *away* from the pursuit route."

"The fishmonger," Ilsa said, "smokes seaweed and once testified that the moon was following him."

"He did. And we discounted it." Dreth turned the boot-print rubbing toward her. "This is a Warden-issue tread. Size nine. You wear a nine, Sergeant."

"Half the garrison wears nines."

"Half the garrison was not on the flats at dawn." He folded his hands. "Ilsa. Sit down."

She sat. It felt like stepping off a ledge in slow motion.

"I have known you since you were nineteen years old and threw a smuggler off the Saltspire bridge for touching a child," Dreth said. "You have never lost a trail in your life. You once tracked a memory-thief through a carnival of ten thousand people by the smell of burnt sugar alone. So when your report tells me you lost this man—a man you interviewed personally, a man who gave you a charm, which I note you did not log—my first thought is not that you failed." He leaned forward. "My first thought is that you succeeded at something else entirely."

The rain filled the silence. Ilsa kept her face the way she kept her quarters: bare, orderly, nothing on display.

"Say what you're going to say, sir."

"What I'm going to say," Dreth said, "is nothing. Not yet." And here he did something worse than accusation: he smiled. "Because a net works best when the fish doesn't feel the water close."

* * *

She understood the shape of it within the day, because Dreth wanted her to understand. That was the cruelty of it—or perhaps the courtesy; with Dreth the two had always shared a border.

He assigned her to lead the next sweep of the tide-flats herself. Full authority. Six wardens, two hounds bred to scent charm-residue, and orders to bring in "the thief Reyes and any accomplices, alive if practical."

Alive if practical. In eleven years she had never heard Dreth use the word *practical*. He said *possible*, or he said *required*, or he said nothing at all and people were carried out on boards.

"You'll report directly to me," he told her. "Every hour. And Ilsa—" the smile again, brief as a knife-flash, "—do try to lose him properly this time. Somewhere I can watch."

So she led the sweep. And she led it badly, on purpose, angling her wardens away from the shuttered shop above the flats, calling searches in streets she knew were empty, wasting an hour on a warehouse full of nothing but salt-rotted rope. The hounds whined at her heels, confused. The six wardens followed their sergeant because that was what wardens did, and five of them believed every word she said.

The sixth was Probationary Warden Sef Calloway, twenty-two years old, freckled, earnest, and—she remembered with a lurch that arrived about halfway through the second wasted hour—the boy she'd pulled out of the burning print-shop on Candle Row last winter when protocol said let the evidence burn and the boy with it. She had gone in anyway. She had taken a week's suspension for it. He had brought her honey-bread every day of that week like she was convalescing instead of punished.

Sef was watching her. Not the streets. Her.

By the fourth hour she could feel his attention the way you feel weather change. By the fifth, when she ordered a search of a drainage culvert that connected to absolutely nowhere relevant, he opened his mouth, and she cut across him before sound could escape.

"Calloway. Take Hound Two and check the seawall stairs."

"But the hounds flagged residue back at—"

"*Seawall stairs.*"

He went. But he went looking back over his shoulder, and Ilsa stood in the grey drizzle among the tide-pools and understood that the net Dreth had built did not need her confession. It only needed witnesses. And the worst witness of all was the one who admired her.

* * *

Dreth sprang it that evening, in the briefing hall, with the whole garrison assembled—which was itself the tell, because evening briefings were for captains, not sergeants, and everyone knew it, and everyone came anyway, drawn like gulls to a fish-cart.

"Sergeant Corvane," Dreth said from the front, and his voice carried the way it always did, without effort, without heat, the voice of a man reading a shipping manifest. "Eleven days ago you reported contact with a suspect in the tide-flats district. Eleven days ago you also received, per your own testimony, a gift from that suspect. An unregistered memory-charm. Which you did not surrender. Which you did not log. Which our records show you wore against regulation for six days before it was confiscated from your quarters during a routine integrity inspection."

Routine. The word landed in the hall like a dropped plate. Everyone knew there had been no routine inspection. Everyone understood, in the particular silent language of soldiers, that they were watching a hanging with the paperwork pre-signed.

Ilsa stood at attention and said nothing, because everything was true, and truth was the one weapon Dreth never left within reach.

"During the same inspection," Dreth continued, "we recovered your field reports. Cross-referenced against harbor watch logs, tide-tables, and civilian depositions, they establish a pattern of deliberate misdirection consistent with aiding a fugitive classified under Edict Nine as a carrier of deep magic." He paused. "Sergeant. Is there anything you wish to say?"

There was. There was a great deal. There was the fact that the man was a thief of harmless charms and a keeper of a child; that the edicts themselves were a wound the city kept pretending wasn't bleeding; that she had knelt in the ash of a burned bookshop four months ago and felt something in her chest crack along a line she hadn't known was there.

But she looked out at the assembled faces—Sef Calloway's stricken freckled face among them—and she understood that whatever she said would be measured, recorded, and used to measure others. If she confessed, they would ask who else had helped. If she named Lena, they would stop asking.

"No, sir," she said. "Nothing I wish to say."

"Then hear what is offered." Dreth came around the front of the lectern, and for the first time all evening his voice warmed, which was how she knew the trap had teeth on both sides. "The Magistrate is prepared to be merciful. Surrender the fugitive Reyes and the child. Testify to the location of their refuge. Do this, and the charges are set aside. Full restoration of rank. No mark on your record." He stopped an arm's length from her. "You would be a hero of the purge, Ilsa. They'd write songs about the warden who caught the key."

The hall was so quiet she could hear the rain.

And Ilsa Corvane, who had given her whole adult life to an institution because it was the only family that had ever claimed her, who had believed—had needed to believe—that the law was a wall between the fire and the people, looked at High Warden Dreth and finally saw him plainly: a man guarding a door that should never have been locked, with a torch in each hand.

She thought of a charm pressed into her palm on a cold morning, woven from something she'd lost and never known she'd lost until it sang. She thought of her mother's face, arriving like sunrise after a decade of night.

"No," she said.

Dreth waited. The hall waited.

"No," she said again, louder, because some things deserved an echo. "I won't give you the man or the child. And I'll tell you why, since you've asked me to speak. Because I've burned shops for you. I've burned *homes*. I told myself it was procedure, and procedure was a kind of virtue, and virtue was a kind of armor, and I wore it for eleven years until a thief handed me my own heart back and I found out what you'd had me using it for." She turned to face the room, to face all of them, because this part was not for Dreth. "The law we serve erased a kingdom. Not defeated it. *Erased* it—out of the maps, out of the ledgers, out of the mouths of anyone who remembered. That's not law. That's just a bigger theft than any charm-seller ever managed, and I am done holding the bag."

The silence afterward had texture. She felt it settle on her shoulders like snow.

Dreth exhaled through his nose, almost gently. "Warden-Sergeant Ilsa Corvane. Under the authority of the Magistrate's Edict Nine, you are stripped of rank and declared traitor to Vhal Maren. Sentence: the pyre, at dawn." He turned away already, dismissing her the way one dismisses weather. "Take her to the cells."

They came for her from both sides. She let them. There were forty wardens in the hall and she was tired down to the marrow, and besides—some small stubborn ember in her noted—dawn was a long way off, and she had once watched a thief walk out of a locked room with a stolen decade, and she had learned from the experience that locks were mostly suggestions wearing uniforms.

* * *

The cell was dry, which she supposed was the institution's idea of mercy. She sat on the bunk with her back to the wall and took inventory, because that was what you did: no boots, no belt, no knife, one blanket, one candle, one guard on the door.

The guard was Sef Calloway.

Of course it was. Dreth was thorough, and cruel in the thorough way, and putting the disappointed admirer on the traitor's door was exactly the sort of test he'd design. Either Sef would prove loyal, or Sef would prove soft, and either result told Dreth something useful.

For three hours neither of them spoke. The candle burned down. Rain kept up its patient argument with the roof.

Then Sef said, to the door, not to her: "You went into the fire for me."

"Yes."

"They say you're a traitor."

"They're right."

His head came around. She'd expected anger. What she saw instead was worse—young, honest confusion, the face of a man watching a load-bearing wall come down. "You were the best of us. You were the one they *taught us with*. If you're a traitor, then what—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "Is it true? About the kingdom? That it was real?"

"It was real," Ilsa said. "That's the whole crime, Sef. It was real, and they made it a rumor, and rumors are easy to burn."

He was quiet a long time. The candle guttered, recovered, guttered again.

"When I was fourteen," he said finally, "the Wardens took my father in for questioning about unlicensed charms. He came back in nine days. Mostly." His jaw worked. "I joined up because I thought if the rules were held by good people, the rules couldn't be used wrong. That was stupid, wasn't it."

"It was the same thing I thought," Ilsa said. "We were all stupid together. It's called an institution."

Something happened to his mouth that was almost a laugh and mostly wasn't.

Then he stood, and unbarred the door, and stepped aside, and looked very hard at the far wall of the corridor as though it contained the most fascinating masonry in the world.

"I'm going to count to sixty," he said. "Slowly. I count slowly. Always have. Ask anyone."

Ilsa rose. Her legs ached; her ribs ached where they'd cuffed her; none of it mattered. At the threshold she stopped, because there was one thing she would not steal, even now.

"Sef. Look at me."

He did. His eyes were wet and furious and certain.

"When they ask, you fought," she said. "You fought, and I overpowered you, and you're carrying a bruise to prove it, so—" she hit him. A short, honest right cross, pulled to maybe half strength, enough to raise a splendid bloom across his cheekbone by morning. He rocked back, blinked, and then—gods bless him, gods keep him—he grinned through the blood in his lip.

"Sixty seconds," he said, and turned to the wall, and began counting aloud, slow as syrup. "*One.*"

She ran.

* * *

Later—much later, soaked to the bone, breath tearing, crouched in the flooded dark beneath the old customs house where the city's drainage forgot itself the way geography sometimes did—Ilsa allowed herself thirty seconds of shaking. Fear, fury, grief for the uniform she could feel evaporating off her body like warmth. All of it, thirty seconds, timed against her pulse, and then done.

Everything she had served was hunting her now. Everything except the thing she'd chosen instead.

She had no rank, no name worth speaking, no coin, no plan beyond the next hundred yards of dark. What she had was a direction: the tide-flats, the shuttered shop, a thief who owed her nothing and a child who spoke the language of drowned kingdoms. And somewhere past them, at the end of every road this story had left, a lighthouse that wasn't there.

She wiped her face, found the current, and started swimming.

Behind her, faint through stone and water, she could just make out Sef Calloway's voice, still counting, faithful as a bell:

*"Fifty-eight. Fifty-nine. Sixty."*

And then the alarm bells of Vhal Maren began to ring.
