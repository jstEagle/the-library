## 15. The Lighthouse That Isn't There

The magnate's memories had a smell to them. That was the first thing Lena had learned about stolen recollections: they carried the scent of the life they'd been pried from. This one smelled of brandy and ledger-ink and, underneath it all, salt — a deep, old salt, like water that had been sitting in a dark place for a very long time.

He turned the memory over in his mind as they walked, careful not to press too hard on it. Stolen memories were like eggs. Squeeze them and you got a mess.

*The lighthouse,* the magnate had thought, in the smug interior voice of a man who believed no one would ever steal his head. *Everyone thinks it burned in the Sundering. Everyone is wrong. It didn't burn. It was never allowed to exist. There's a reason my grandfather's charts show open water where every sailor swears he's seen a tower.*

Lena had taken that memory along with the decade of bribes and smuggling routes and one genuinely appalling incident involving a harbor official's wife, and he had spent three days since turning it over like a coin he suspected was counterfeit.

"You're doing the face again," Ilsa said.

They were walking the sea-road north out of Vhal Maren, Tam riding on Lena's shoulders with his heels drumming gently against Lena's chest in a rhythm that had become, over the past weeks, as familiar as his own heartbeat. Which was saying something, given how little of his own heartbeat's history Lena could actually vouch for.

"What face?"

"The one where you look like a man arguing with someone who isn't there."

"He isn't there," Lena said. "That's rather the point of stealing someone's memories. You get the house but not the tenant." He shifted Tam's weight. "I'm trying to find the lighthouse in what I took from Corvane Hask. And the trouble is—"

"That there isn't one."

"That there isn't one," Lena agreed. "Hask remembered looking for it. Three expeditions. He stood on this very road, at this very stretch—" Lena gestured at the gray water rolling against gray shingle, featureless as an unwritten page "—and saw nothing. Because there was nothing to see. The thing about a place that's been unwritten, Mother Quill says, is that it doesn't hide. Hiding implies it's still on the map somewhere, just behind a rock. No. It's been *removed*. The world has closed ranks around the hole where it used to be and agreed, unanimously, not to mention it."

Tam said something in Old Tongue. It was a long sentence, and Lena caught only the shape of it — a rising, singing shape, like a gull's cry bent into grammar.

"I don't know, little fish," Lena said. "I hope so."

"What did he say?" Ilsa asked. She walked with her Warden's coat left open, which was as close to naked rebellion as she could manage while still wearing it. She'd cut the insignia off the collar herself, with her service knife, and Lena had watched her do it and said nothing, because some things you witnessed and kept.

"He said—" Lena frowned, feeling for it. The Old Tongue still came to him in fragments, rising up through the gaps in himself like bubbles through deep water. "Something about the tower being shy. Or... asleep? He says it sleeps until someone calls its name."

"Can we call its name?"

"We don't know its name."

"I know its name," Tam said.

Both adults stopped walking. It was the longest sentence Tam had yet produced in Common, and he delivered it with the mild patience of a child who has been waiting some time for the grown-ups to catch up.

Ilsa crouched to his level. "You know the lighthouse's name?"

Tam shook his head, then nodded, then made a gesture that seemed to encompass both answers and dismiss the distinction between them. Then he pointed north, along the shore, and said a word in Old Tongue that went through Lena like a fishhook.

Because Lena knew that word. Not from Tam. From *himself* — from somewhere below the twelve years of small thefts and careful anonymity, down in the flooded cellar where his childhood was supposed to be. The word meant something like *the finger that points home*, or possibly *the last candle*. Old Tongue did that. One word, six meanings, all of them true.

"Right," Lena said, when he could speak again. "Well. That settles it. We're going north."

* * *

They walked through the morning and into the afternoon, and the coast changed around them the way a dream changes — gradually, then all at once.

It began with the birds. Gulls followed the sea-road for miles out of Vhal Maren, wheeling and complaining, and then, quite suddenly, there were none. Not fewer. None. The silence they left behind was enormous, a silence with weight and texture, and Tam pressed his small hands over Lena's ears as if to hold the quiet in.

Then the shingle changed color. South of them it had been the ordinary slate-gray of any northern beach. Here it was pale, almost white, shot through with veins of something greenish that might have been glass and might have been bone. Lena crouched and picked up a piece. It was warm. Everything else on that wind-scoured shore was cold, and this fragment of unknown material sat in his palm like a sleeping bird.

"Don't pocket it," Ilsa said quietly.

"I wasn't going to pocket it."

"You were absolutely going to pocket it."

"I was *considering* pocketing it," Lena admitted, and set it down. "There's a difference. A professional distinction."

But he understood her caution. Things here were not things. Mother Quill had warned them — in her roundabout, blind-woman way, tapping her stick on the floor of her archive while she spoke — that the edges of an unwritten place were soft. *Reality forgets itself out there, boy. Step carefully, or you'll step into a gap and come out the other side of a day that never happened.*

The fog came next. It rose off the water without warning, not drifting in but simply *arriving*, as though the sea had exhaled, and it was the wrong color for sea-fog. Vhal Maren's fogs were gray-green, smelling of weed and tar. This fog was white-gold, faintly luminous, and it smelled — Lena stopped dead in the shingle and breathed it in, and something in his chest cracked open like ice on a spring river.

It smelled like bread baking. Like woodsmoke and rain on hot stone and, beneath everything, a flower he had no name for and would swear he had never met and yet knew instantly, helplessly, the way you know your own mother's voice.

"Lena?" Ilsa's hand found his arm.

"It's all right." His voice came out strange. "It's — I know this. I don't know how I know this, but I know it. It's the smell of—" He groped for it. "Home. Some version of home. Before."

Tam tugged his hair and pointed north again, insistent now, and Lena walked on with his heart hammering and his eyes stinging and the taste of a word on his tongue that he refused, for the moment, to say aloud.

* * *

They climbed a headland as the light began to fail — though *light* was doing generous work; the gold fog gave everything a twilight glow regardless of the hour, as if the sun here had been politely asked to wait outside. From the top of the headland, the magnate's stolen memory finally agreed to cooperate.

*Stand here,* Hask had thought, on the third and last of his expeditions, furious and cold and certain he was being mocked by the universe. *Look north. There is nothing. There is nothing and it is the loudest nothing I have ever seen.*

Lena stood where Hask had stood. He looked north.

And he saw the nothing.

It was hard to explain, even to himself. The bay below the headland was ordinary in every particular — water, shingle, a scatter of rocks — except that his eyes kept *sliding off* one particular patch of it, the way a tongue keeps returning to a missing tooth. Perhaps two hundred paces of shoreline that simply declined to be looked at. When he tried to fix his gaze on it, his attention skated away to the rocks on either side, to the horizon, to a fascinating piece of driftwood, anywhere else. When he forced it back, the patch of shore seemed almost offended, rippling at the edges like heat-haze.

"There," he said. "Do you see it? Or rather — do you see the not-it?"

Isla stared. Her brow furrowed. "I see water."

"Look *beside* it. Look at the edge of it. Don't look at the middle — the middle fights back."

She tried. He watched her struggle with it, watched her Warden's discipline grapple with the simple animal refusal of her own eyes, and then Tam solved the problem the way children solve most problems: by ignoring the rules entirely. The boy slid off Lena's shoulders, ran down the headland path toward the impossible stretch of shore, and shouted a word into the golden fog.

The word was the name. Lena felt it land — felt it strike the invisible place like a bell struck in a cathedral, felt the whole bay *ring* with it.

And the lighthouse appeared.

Not dramatically. That was the terrible, wonderful thing about it. It didn't rise from the waves or blaze into being. It was simply *there*, the way a chair is there when you turn around, as if it had been there all along and the fault had been entirely yours for not seeing it. It stood on a low black reef at the mouth of the bay, half-drowned, its base wrapped in weed and its tower leaning — not broken, but leaning, the way an old man leans on a gate. Its stone was the same pale green-veined stuff as the shingle fragments. Its lamp room was dark. Its door, at the top of a stair that the tide was patiently unmaking, stood open.

No. Not open. *Waiting.* There was a difference, and Lena felt the difference in his teeth.

"Oh," Ilsa said softly. All her Warden training, all her cynicism, all the iron she'd spent twelve years forging herself out of — it all came apart in the face of one leaning tower, and what was left underneath was just a woman seeing something beautiful and impossible. "Oh, it's *lovely*."

"It's a trap," Lena said automatically, and then winced. "Sorry. Twelve years of habit. Everything lovely in Vhal Maren is a trap. It takes a moment to adjust."

"Is it?" she asked seriously. "A trap?"

Lena considered. The magnate's memory offered nothing more — Hask had never gotten this far, had never had a child to shout the name for him. What Lena had instead was that smell, bread and smoke and the nameless flower, and the hook-shaped ache behind his sternum, and Tam's hand slipping trustingly into his.

"No," he said. "I don't think it's a trap. I think it's a door, and doors are honest in a way people aren't. A door only ever does one of two things. It opens, or it stays shut. It doesn't pretend."

"And which will this one do?"

Lena looked at the dark lamp room, and at the boy beside him, and at the woman who had burned her own coat-sleeves off rather than burn another shop, and heard himself say, with a calm that surprised him:

"I think it's going to ask me for something. And I think I've known that since the day Tam knocked on my door, and I think that's why I've been so tired lately." He smiled, and it cost him something, and he paid it anyway. "Come on. Tide's coming in. Doors hate to be kept waiting."

They went down together — the thief, the traitor, and the child who spoke the language before kingdoms — across shingle that glowed faintly under their feet like a path remembering footsteps, toward a lighthouse that wasn't there, which had begun, very softly, in a voice like surf and like singing, to hum.

And in the humming, Lena heard — unmistakably, impossibly — a woman's voice. Young. Warm. Calling a name across water.

Calling *his*.
