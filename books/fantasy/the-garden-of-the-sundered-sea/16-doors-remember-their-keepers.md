## 16. Doors Remember Their Keepers

The lighthouse did not exist, and it was the largest thing Lena had ever seen.

That was the sort of sentence that would have made Mother Quill purse her lips and say something dry about grammar and metaphysics, but standing before it — if *before* was a word that applied to a structure whose base was somewhere beneath the water and whose top was somewhere above the sky, with no honest middle in between — Lena found he no longer cared about precision. The lighthouse rose out of the drowned shingle like a memory rising through sleep: not built so much as *remembered into being*, its stones the grey of old salt, its lamp-room burning with a light that cast no shadows because there was nothing left down here for it to shadow.

The humming filled everything. It came up through his boots, through his teeth, through the scar on his palm he'd never been able to explain. And threaded through it, over and over, patient as tide:

*Len. Len. Len.*

"Tam," Lena said, and his voice cracked in the middle like a dropped plate. "Do you hear—"

But Tam was already walking.

The boy moved up the glowing shingle with the certainty of someone going home, bare feet finding the sure path between the rocks as though he'd walked it every day of a life he'd never lived here. Behind him Ilsa had gone very still, one hand on her knife out of pure habit, though they both knew steel would be about as useful against this place as a polite letter.

"It's a door," she said flatly. "It's actually a door."

"It's a lighthouse," Lena said.

"It's both. That's the worst thing I've ever heard." She looked up at the impossible height of it, and something in her jaw tightened — the look she got when procedure failed her and she had to improvise, which Lena had learned to find endearing at exactly the wrong moments. "Wardens hate doors that are also lighthouses. There's no form for it."

"There's no form for any of this."

"No," Ilsa said quietly. "There isn't." And then, because she was Ilsa, and because terror and tenderness lived in her side by side like tenants who'd long since stopped speaking to each other: "Whatever happens in there — you come back out. That's an order."

"You can't order me. You're a traitor now."

"I'm a traitor *sergeant*. It comes with seniority."

He laughed — a small, cracked, astonished sound, the kind of laugh you make when the alternative is weeping — and then Tam turned back and held out his hand, and there was no more room for anything but following.

* * *

Inside, the lighthouse was a stairway.

Of course it was. It wound upward through walls of grey stone veined with lines of faint gold, and the gold moved — slowly, slowly, like sap, like writing, like the last strokes of a hand finishing a very long letter. Lena touched the wall once and snatched his fingers back, because the stone had been *warm*, blood-warm, and under his palm for half a heartbeat he'd felt something that was almost a pulse.

Tam climbed without hesitation. Ilsa climbed like a soldier, checking corners that were only ever more stair. And Lena climbed like a man ascending his own gallows, which was, he was beginning to understand, not far from the truth.

Because the humming had words now. Or rather, it had always had words, and he was finally letting himself hear them, and they were in Old Tongue, and he understood them — understood them the way you understand your own name, without translation, without effort, with a rightness that felt like a knife sliding home.

*Come and be unmade, little brother. Come and give back what you took.*

"Maerin," he whispered.

The stairs glowed brighter.

They climbed for what might have been an hour or a year. Time went strange in the gold-veined dark; hunger came and went without settling; twice Lena caught himself mid-step unable to remember whether he'd been climbing up or down, and both times Tam's small hand found his and pulled him onward without comment, the way you steady someone who is drunk, or grieving, or both.

And then the stairs ended, and there was the Door.

It stood where the lamp should have been — vast, taller than the tower had any right to contain, made of a wood so old it had gone the colour of storm-clouds, banded in iron that was not iron and carved so deeply that the carvings seemed less decoration than wounds. Waves and ships and stars and running figures, all around its edges. And at its center, set into the heartwood like a keystone set into an arch:

A hollow. Man-shaped. Child-shaped, really — the outline of a boy of perhaps fourteen, arms slightly out, head bowed, as though the Door had taken a casting of him at the moment of his greatest failure and kept it all these years the way you keep a coat someone died in.

Lena knew the shape. He knew it the way Tam knew the path across the shingle.

"That's me," he said. His voice sounded very far away. "That's — I stood there. I *stood there*, and I—"

The Door hummed. Not surf now. Not singing. Something older and sadder, the sound a hinge makes when it has waited too long.

*KEystone,* it said, in a voice that was Maerin's and the sea's and his own, all three braided together. *Come home.*

"Lena." Ilsa's hand closed on his shoulder — firm, real, callused, alive. "What does it want?"

"It wants what I stole." He was shaking. He could hear himself shaking, feel his teeth click. "Twelve years ago I sold charms and told myself I'd started from nothing. No parents, no hometown, a first memory that begins mid-escape. I thought someone edited me. Some Warden interrogator, some enemy." He laughed again, and this time there was nothing in it at all. "Nobody edited me, Ilsa. I did. Fourteen years old, and I cut my own childhood out of my head with magic I didn't know I had, and I ran, and I didn't even have the decency to remember what I was running *from*."

"And the Door?"

"The lock needs a key. The key is the memories. All of them. Every year I erased." He looked at the hollow, at the shape of the boy he had been, waiting for him with the patience of stone. "It wants me to put them back. To become, for one moment, the person who sealed this place — so the seal can be undone by the same hand that made it."

Ilsa was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had the careful flatness she used when the truth was too large for inflection.

"And after? If you pour twelve years back in — do you get to keep them?"

"I don't know."

"If you open it — do you get to keep *anything*?"

"I don't know that either."

She turned him around. She had that look — the one from the seawall, from the mudflats at dawn, from every moment she'd chosen him over the law and hated herself a little and done it anyway. But this time there was no self-hatred in it at all. Only a terrible, level clarity.

"Then listen to me," she said. "I spent my whole life believing the record mattered. That what's written down is what's true, and what's erased is what's safe. I burned shops over that belief." Her thumb brushed his cheekbone, rough and gentle. "You are not a ledger, Lena Reyes. Whatever you put back in there, whatever it takes out of you — you'll still be the man who smuggled a child through the undercity. That happened. It's written in *me*. And I don't erase."

For a moment he couldn't speak at all.

Then Tam tugged his sleeve and pointed at the hollow, and said — in Common, halting and careful, the first full sentence the boy had ever given him:

"She waits for you. She has waited so long. She is not angry." A pause, and then, with the devastating simplicity of children: "She is only sad that you were alone."

* * *

Lena stepped forward and pressed his hands against the hollow.

The Door drank him in.

It began gently, the way all his thefts began — a warmth behind the eyes, a loosening, a sense of something heavy being lifted off a shelf. But this was no theft. This was restitution, and restitution runs backward along the same road, and so the memories did not arrive as recollections. They arrived as *returns*. He was fourteen again, and it was happening to him for the first time, and the last time, and every time in between.

Rain. A night of rain, and torchlight on wet cobbles, and a city screaming in a language that was being murdered syllable by syllable. Callowmere burning — not falling, *unwriting*, the edges of buildings going soft and grey as the edicts took hold, names dissolving off signboards, gravestones smoothing themselves blank. He was fourteen and he was holding his sister's hand and she was dragging him through streets that were forgetting themselves faster than they could run through them.

*"Len. Look at me. Look at me."*

Maerin. Nineteen and already carrying the weight of a whole kingdom's ending on her shoulders, because their mother had died passing it to her and their father had died refusing it and someone had to stand at the Door and someone had to live, and Maerin had decided — in the way of eldest sisters since the invention of eldest sisters — that it would be both, and she would simply not survive the arrangement.

*"The Wardens are coming. The pact needs a keeper's memory as the lock — you know this, Mama taught us, you were listening even when you pretended to sleep. Someone has to take the memories away and keep them safe inside a living mind, or the Door opens for anyone, and Len—"*

Her voice broke, just once, and then became the voice he would spend twelve years hearing in dreams he didn't know were memories.

*"Someone has to carry them out. And it can't be me. If I go, there's no one to turn the lock. So it has to be you, and you have to take the memories WITH you, all of them, mine and Mama's and Papa's and the whole of Callowmere, locked inside your own head where no edict can reach — do you understand? You won't remember why. You won't remember me. You'll hate yourself for running, and I am so sorry, my love, I am so sorry, but it's the only way, and you were always the best of us at getting away with things—"*

*"No," young Lena said. "No, Maerin, I'm not leaving you, I won't—"*

And then — cowardice. He could see it now with perfect, merciless clarity, the way you can only see your own worst moment once you've paid enough years for the view. He hadn't argued long. He hadn't fought. Torchlight had flared at the end of the street and his legs had simply *decided*, the way young legs do, and he had run, and Maerin had pressed her palms to his temples as he went and poured a kingdom into him like water into a cracked jar, and the last thing his remembering mind ever saw of her was her face in the rain, smiling, saying —

*"Go be small, little brother. Go be invisible. Grow old somewhere safe and never know what you're worth."*

Lena came back to himself on his knees at the foot of the Door, weeping without sound, Ilsa's arms around him, Tam's small forehead pressed to his shoulder.

"I ran," he said. "Ilsa, I ran, and she let me believe — she made sure I'd never even know enough to come back—"

"She saved your life," Ilsa said. "And she lied to do it. Both things are true. Sisters are like that." Her voice wobbled, just barely, on the last word, and he understood that she was thinking of a charm he'd once slipped into her pocket, woven from a grief she'd sworn an oath to forget. "Believe me. I've read the literature."

A wet, hiccupping laugh escaped Tam — the boy's first laugh in Common — and something in the great grey Door shifted, softened, the carvings seeming to breathe.

*Now you remember,* it hummed. *Now you may choose.*

Lena stood. His legs shook. Twelve years of stolen childhood sat in him like a flood behind a gate, and beyond the gate, he could feel the rest — Maerin's bargain, the terms of the pact, the price the Door would ask — waiting to be known.

He put his hand on the keystone one last time.

"Maerin," he said softly, in Old Tongue, in the language before kingdoms. "I'm home. Tell me what it costs."

The Door opened its memory to him — and what he saw made the blood leave his face, and made Ilsa reach for her knife at nothing, and made Tam whisper a single word that meant, in a tongue no map remembered:

*Dearly.*
