## 17. The Siege of Memory

Ilsa Corvane had held a stair once before.

She'd been nineteen, newly sworn, and the stair had been in the Warden barracks at Cindral Row—a drill exercise, three flights of stone, wooden staves, an instructor shouting about choke points and angles of retreat. She had held it for four minutes against six colleagues and been commended for form.

The stair she stood on now was black basalt slicked with sea-spray, barely wide enough for two men abreast, climbing out of the drowned lighthouse's broken crown toward the grey morning sky. There were no instructors here. No wooden staves. Behind her, down through the hatch and into the dark below, Lena Reyes was doing something with a door that Ilsa did not fully understand and could not afford to doubt.

Ahead of her, coming up the causeway through the burning fog, were forty-one wardens.

She counted them out of habit. It was a habit she was beginning to hate.

"Forty-one," she murmured to herself, rolling her shoulders. "I've had worse odds."

She hadn't. She wanted to be honest about that, even alone, even now. The worst odds she'd ever faced before this week had been eleven smugglers in a warehouse on Gull Street, and she'd had backup then, and the smugglers had mostly surrendered because she'd bought them all drinks afterward and they'd decided they liked her.

Nobody was going to buy these wardens drinks.

* * *

The anti-magic engines came first.

They were Dreth's new toys—copper-banded carriages on iron wheels, each one cradling a lattice of cold-iron rods that hummed like a struck tuning fork heard through water. Ilsa had seen one demonstrated at the Citadel two years ago. A warden had cast a simple light-charm inside its radius and the charm had died mid-sparkle, and the warden had wept without knowing why, because magic wasn't just something you did; it was something you *were*, and having it torn out of you, even briefly, felt like being reminded that someday you would die.

Three engines ground their way up the causeway now, and the air around them went flat and tasteless. Ilsa felt it reach her like a hand pressing on her chest—the small warm knot of borrowed charm still tucked in her breast pocket, the one Lena had given her, going silent and cold.

*Hold on,* she told it silently. *I'm working.*

The vanguard reached the base of the stairs. She let them get ten steps up before she moved.

The first man got her boot heel across the jaw. The second caught her elbow in the throat as he bent over his falling colleague. The third was smart enough to draw his baton, and she took it away from him and introduced him to the concept from the other end. He sat down hard on step fourteen and looked up at her with an expression she recognized, because she'd worn it herself a hundred times: the bewildered hurt of a person who had followed the rules their whole life and could not understand why the rules had turned on them.

"I'm sorry," she said, and meant it, and hit him again anyway.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about treason: it is remarkably similar to duty.

Same posture. Same discipline. Same careful attention to angles and footing and the conservation of breath. The only difference is which side of the line you're standing on when the orders come down, and Ilsa had spent the last three days discovering that the line was drawn in sand on a beach at low tide.

Dreth's trap had been elegant. She gave him that. Falsify a report, he'd said, or bring me Reyes' head—and when she'd done neither, when she'd come back with nothing but salt spray on her boots and a lie dying in her mouth, he'd simply smiled and shown her the report she *hadn't* filed, already written in her hand, already dated. Forgery by anticipation. He'd known what she would choose before she chose it, and he'd built the noose to fit.

She should have been in a cell. She was not in a cell because Warden Tammas Vey—young, earnest, a boy whose brother she'd once kept off the gallows for poaching—had looked at her shackles, looked at the corridor, and developed a sudden and profound deafness coupled with a malfunctioning key-ring.

"You were never here," she'd told him.

"Never who?" he'd said, and she'd thought: *that boy is going to be a sergeant someday, and a good one, and I hope to every god listening that he never has to make the choice I just made.*

So now she was here. On a stair. Alone. Defending a lighthouse that officially did not exist, guarding a door that officially had never existed, protecting a memory-thief and a mute child from the institution she had given twenty years of her life to.

Her mother would have had opinions. Her mother, whose face lived in a charm in her pocket, erased by her own oath twelve years ago, restored to her for one shattering hour in Dreth's evidence room before she'd had to give it back—or pretend she had. She'd kept it. That was treason too, probably. At this point the list was getting long enough that she'd stopped keeping track.

*Ilsa Corvane,* she imagined her mother saying, in the voice that had once made entire dinner parties sit up straighter, *you always did take after me.*

She grinned into the smoke, and the grin frightened the fourth warden so badly he fell back down six steps.

* * *

They tried fire next.

This was procedure. She knew it was procedure; she'd read the manual. Structural denial of a fortified position, Article Nine, subsection on non-magical threats. The lighthouse was stone and would burn slowly, but the stairs were timber-reinforced, and if they burned the stairs they cut off retreat and advance alike.

The problem with procedure, Ilsa had learned this week, was that it assumed everyone involved was a piece on a board rather than a person standing in the rain.

She tore a burning plank loose and threw it back down into the men holding the torches. It was not a dignified maneuver. It was, in fact, the sort of thing she'd have reprimanded a subordinate for—improvised, undisciplined, statistically likely to set your own position alight. But the statistics didn't account for a woman who had spent two decades learning exactly how much punishment a body could absorb and had recently concluded that the answer was *more than mine needs to survive this morning.*

Something cracked in her left forearm when she blocked a halberd shaft. She noted it the way you note weather: unpleasant, relevant later. She switched the baton to her right hand.

Below, the second anti-magic engine crested the causeway rocks, and its hum deepened, and she felt the dead zone wash over her like cold water. Every trick she'd ever learned from Lena's world—all the little charms, the luck-weaves, the reflex-sharpeners she'd never admitted to using—went quiet.

Fine. She'd never needed them anyway. That had always been the joke between her and the other wardens, back when there were jokes: *Corvane fights like she's got no magic at all.* They'd meant it as an insult. She'd taken it as training.

The engine crew made the mistake of stopping at the base of the stairs to reposition, which put them directly beneath her, which put them within range of a falling woman with a baton and a grudge.

She dropped onto the engine carriage itself, and the crew—three technicians and a squad-leader—looked up at her with the pure astonishment of men encountering a problem not covered anywhere in the manual.

"Morning," she said, and disabled the engine with two precise strikes to the regulator lattice, and then disabled the crew with somewhat less precision, and rode the carriage's momentum back up onto her stair like the world's angriest delivery.

Somewhere below, someone shouted, "It's Corvane! It's actually—it's Sergeant Corvane!"

And then, worse, quieter, from someone else: "Why is it Corvane?"

That question hurt more than the arm. She let it hurt. Pain was fuel today; she'd decided that around dawn.

* * *

The third wave came with pikes and shields and proper formation, and it nearly worked.

She gave ground. Step by step, trading space for time, letting them think the stair was won. They pressed up eagerly, forty men funneling into a corridor built for two, and Ilsa Corvane—who had once lectured a room full of cadets on the tactical folly of committing reserves to a narrow frontage—watched them do exactly that, and waited until the column was packed tight as herring in a barrel.

Then she pulled the pin on the last thing in her pockets: a charm Lena had pressed into her hand two nights ago, muttering that it was "a distraction, technically legal, don't ask how I know."

It was a sound. One enormous, rolling, catastrophic sound—the recorded memory of a shipwreck bell, amplified past all decency, the kind of noise that bypasses the ears entirely and grabs the spine by the collar.

Forty men flinched at once. In a narrow stairwell, a flinch is a chain reaction. The front rank stumbled backward into the second, the second into the third, and somewhere near the bottom somebody tripped over the anti-magic engine carriage and went through the railing into the shallows with a splash that Ilsa would remember fondly for the rest of her life, however long that turned out to be.

She laughed. Actually laughed, standing in the smoke with a broken arm and blood in her eye, laughing like a madwoman on her stair while forty trained wardens untangled themselves at the bottom like kittens in a sack.

Because underneath everything—the fear, the grief, the treason—there was this: she was good at this. She had spent twenty years becoming very, very good at exactly this, and for twenty years the skill had been pointed at people like Lena Reyes, like Mother Quill, like every small desperate soul who'd ever sold a charm to buy bread. And now, for one morning, for whatever it cost her, the skill was pointed the right way.

Duty inverted was still duty. It turned out the direction of the pointing was the whole moral universe, and she'd simply been facing the wrong wall.

* * *

They withdrew to regroup. She used the minutes badly—leaning on the railing, splinting her forearm with a strip torn from a fallen warden's cloak, breathing through the nausea.

Through the hatch behind her, faintly, she could feel it: a vibration in the stones, rhythmic, patient. Something happening below. Something opening, or being opened. Lena's work.

"Hold," she whispered—not to the wardens, not to the stair. To him, to the child, to whatever was stirring under the lighthouse. *Hold. I'm buying you minutes. Spend them well.*

A shadow fell across the causeway.

High Warden Dreth came up the last stretch of rock on foot, unhurried, his white cloak immaculate despite the smoke, two honor-guard wardens trailing him at a distance that suggested even they found the proximity uncomfortable. He stopped at the base of the stairs and looked up at her the way a man looks at a ledger entry that refuses to balance.

"Sergeant Corvane." His voice carried easily over the water. "You are aware that everything you are doing this morning constitutes high treason."

"I'm aware of a great many things this morning, High Warden," she called back. "It's been an educational week."

"There is a version of this where you stand aside. Where you are remembered as confused rather than condemned. I am offering it to you now, once."

Ilsa considered it. Honestly considered it—the way you test a rotten plank, putting weight on it briefly to confirm what you already know.

"No," she said.

"You would throw away your oath. Your name. Everything."

"My oath," she said, "was to protect the people of Vhal Maren. All of them. Even the ones your edicts say don't exist. Especially those." She shifted her weight off her bad arm. "You wrote the erasure orders, Dreth. You tell me—which of us broke the oath first?"

For a moment—just a moment—something flickered behind his eyes. Doubt, maybe. Or the memory of a younger Dreth, the one who'd sworn the same oath she had, before the paperwork ate him alive.

Then he raised his hand, and the horns sounded, and the fourth wave began—and this one came with fire-pots and grappling lines and no intention of climbing politely.

Ilsa spat blood onto the stones, settled her baton in her good hand, and smiled at the oncoming tide of white cloaks.

"Come on, then," she said. "The stair's held worse than you."

It hadn't. But behind her, deep beneath the lighthouse, something vast and old began—very softly—to open.
