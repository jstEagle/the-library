## 18. Maerin's Choice

The memories came back the way a tide comes back—not all at once, but in long, patient pulls, each wave reaching a little further up the sand than the last.

Lena sat on the cold stone floor of the lighthouse's drowned chapel, Tam asleep against his side with his small mouth open and one hand fisted in Lena's sleeve, and let the tide take him.

He remembered salt marshes that smelled of honey instead of rot. He remembered a city called Vess Callow—*the bright harbor*, though no map had ever been permitted to say so—and its bells, which were tuned to different keys so that the whole city rang chords when the wind blew. He remembered his mother grinding pigment for the festival lanterns and complaining that her apprentice had the artistic sensibility of a herring. He remembered Maerin.

Maerin, two years older, tall where he was wiry, patient where he was quick. Maerin who could make the old words sit up and behave, who had been chosen by the Door-wardens before she was twelve because her blood sang to the threshold stones. Maerin who used to hold him upside down by the ankles over the harbor rail until he laughed so hard he hiccupped, and who always, *always*, caught him.

He remembered the last night of Callowmere.

Not the way he'd remembered it before—not as fragments, not as a shapeless terror he'd spent twelve years stepping around like a hole in the street. Now it came whole, and whole was worse.

The Wardens had come across the water in black ships, and behind them had come something else, something the ships were built to contain rather than carry. The Sundering hadn't been an invasion. It had been a quarantine. The kingdom of Callowmere had opened a door it should never have found—a door at the bottom of the world, older than kingdoms, older than the language Tam spoke—and what had come through it wore the faces of the dead and spoke in your own voice and wanted, with a patience like stone wanting to be a mountain, everything.

The Door-wardens had fought it for nine days. On the ninth day, Maerin had stood before the Sundered Door with blood on her face that might have been hers and might not have been, and she had made the bargain.

Lena heard her voice now as clearly as if she were sitting beside him in the dark, and he had to press his fist against his mouth to keep from answering her.

*"The Door can be sealed,"* she said, to the thing on the other side, to the wardens, to the world. *"But a seal needs a keystone. Not blood—I know what you're thinking, and no, we're done with blood. Memory. The Door will hold as long as the memory of how it was opened stays locked away. Take my years. Take my knowing."*

And the Door had asked her, in the voice doors have—which is no voice at all, only a pressure behind the eyes, a question shaped like falling: *And who will remember them? Who will remember Callowmere, if its heir forgets?*

*"No one,"* Maerin had said. *"That is the price. Let them be unwritten. Let the maps go blank and the records burn and every tongue that spoke their name grow silent. I would rather my people be forgotten and alive than remembered and devoured."*

She had looked back once—only once—at the crowd being herded toward the threshold, hundreds of pale faces in the lantern-light, and among them a fourteen-year-old boy with their mother's pigment stains still on his fingers.

*"Run, little fish,"* she'd said. Or she'd tried to say it. What actually happened was worse and stranger: she had caught Lena's eye across the chapel, and she had smiled—the same smile she gave him when she held him over the harbor rail, the smile that meant *I've got you, I'll always catch you*—and then she had turned to the Door and begun to speak the unbinding, and the world had come apart like wet paper.

Lena had run. That was the part the restored memory made unavoidable. He had run, because Maerin told him to, because everyone was running, because he was fourteen and terrified and trusted his sister absolutely. He had run through streets that were forgetting themselves as he passed through them, houses going gray and doorless, signs going blank, and he had felt the unwriting chase him like a tide chasing a crab, and just before it caught him—

Just before it caught him, he had done the only clever, cowardly, desperate thing a thief's son could do. He had stolen himself. Reached into his own mind with the crude gift every Callow child carried and ripped out everything the Wardens' truth-finders could use: the kingdom, the language, the Door, Maerin's face. He had stuffed those years into a charm—a crude, desperate, fourteen-year-old's charm—and thrown it into the sea, or lost it, or buried it somewhere even he couldn't find, and he had walked out onto the mudflats of a city that officially did not exist, carrying nothing but a first memory that began mid-stride.

*I sealed the Door,* Lena thought, in the dark of the chapel, with the child breathing against his side. *Me. A boy with paint on his hands. I am the reason they've been locked in the dark for twelve years.*

Because that was the horror under the horror, the thing he kept circling and circling like a gull over a drowned fish. Maerin's seal had needed a keystone, and the keystone was the memory of the opening—and Maerin had given her own years gladly, but the Door had taken *his* too, or rather his theft had tangled with hers, two locks braided into one, and the result was a seal stronger than any warden could have dreamed. Strong enough that no Warden-Sergeant, no High Warden, no fleet of black ships could ever break it.

Strong enough that nothing inside could ever get out either.

* * *

"You're doing the face again."

Lena blinked. Tam was awake, sitting up, watching him with the grave, unhurried attention of a cat deciding whether the thing on the floor was dead or merely resting.

"What face?"

"The one where you look like you swallowed a bell and it's still ringing." Tam said it in Common now, mostly—his Common was still odd, rounded at the edges, with Old Tongue surfacing in it like stones in a shallow stream—but he said it with the particular relish of a child who has recently discovered sarcasm and intends to get full use of it before the world ends.

"I don't swallow bells."

"You do. Ilsa says so too."

"Ilsa says a lot of things." Lena pulled his coat tighter around them both. The chapel was cold; the sea pressed against the walls with the patience of something that had all the time in the world, which, to be fair, it did. "How much did you hear?"

"Enough." Tam's flippancy folded away, the way it always did, revealing the old soul underneath—the remnant-souls of Callowmere lived in him somehow, Lena understood that now, a whole drowned nation crowded into one small boy's silence. "You remembered her. Your sister."

"Yes."

"And now you understand why the Door won't open for us."

Lena looked at him. Eight years old, barefoot, unafraid, carrying more ghosts than a graveyard. "Tell me what you know," Lena said gently. "All of it. You've been speaking in pieces since the flats. I want the whole story, from you, in your own words."

Tam hugged his knees. For a while there was only the sea, pressing and pressing.

"The Door isn't a wall," he said at last. "That's what the teachers said, before. It's a *choice*. Every time someone comes to it, the Door asks again. *Which do you want?* Freedom for the people behind it, or safety for the world outside it. You can't have both, because the thing on the other side of the Door is attached to us—it rides our dreams, our names, our remembering. Open the Door wide and it comes through with us. Keep the Door shut and we stay safe and buried forever." He picked at a loose thread on his sleeve. "Your sister chose the world. She was right to. Everyone says she was right to."

"Do you think she was right?"

Tam was quiet for a long moment. Then, in a smaller voice: "I think I'd like to see the sky without bars on it. Even for one day." He looked up. "But the Door won't ask me. It doesn't ask children. It asks keepers. And the keeper is you."

* * *

Ilsa found them an hour later, coming down the spiral stair with a lamp in one hand and her sword still unbuckled at her hip—she'd been standing watch at the seawall stairs, and the shadows under her eyes said the watch had not been quiet.

"Dreth knows where we are," she said, without preamble. She had stopped pretending, around the time she'd lied to her own report, that softening news was a service she owed anyone. "Two hours, maybe three. He's brought the anti-magic engines. And fire-priests." Her jaw tightened. "He means to burn the lighthouse down to the waterline and salt whatever's left."

"Then we have two hours to solve a problem that's stumped a kingdom for twelve years," Lena said. "Wonderful. I've had tighter deadlines."

"This isn't a joke, Lena."

"No," he agreed. "It really isn't." He stood, and his knees cracked, and he thought: *twelve years of crouching in doorways, and this is where it ends, in a drowned chapel arguing theology with a child.* "Sit down, Ilsa. Both of you. Because I finally understand the bargain, and I need to say it out loud to people who'll tell me if I'm mad."

They sat. Tam curled against Ilsa's side without asking, the way children do when they've decided someone is furniture that loves them, and Ilsa went very still and then very carefully put her arm around him, as if handling something explosive and precious, which, Lena reflected, she was.

"The Door asks a question every time it's faced," Lena said. "Freedom for Callowmere, or safety for the world. Maerin answered it one way. She paid with her memory—her years, her knowing—and the Door took mine too, because I stole them at the wrong moment and braided my lock into hers. That's why the seal has held. Two keystones. Hers given, mine taken." He paced the length of the chapel, three steps and turn, three steps and turn, an old habit from a life he was only now re-inhabiting. "But here's what I didn't understand until tonight. The question isn't fixed. It's a *bargain*, and bargains can be renegotiated—if you have standing. Maerin had standing because she was the heir. I have standing because I'm the other half of the lock."

"So open it," Ilsa said. "Unseal it. Let them out."

"It's not—" He stopped pacing. Looked at her. Loved her, in the sudden helpless way he'd been loving her since the tide-flats, since the fencing and the charm and the lie she'd told Dreth with her career in her teeth. "Ilsa. If I simply unlock it, the Door opens both ways. Everything Maerin feared walks out with them. The face-wearers. The patient things. Twelve years they've been pressed against the other side of that seal, listening to us, learning us. Maerin knew that. That's why she chose as she did. There was no third option."

"There's always a third option," Ilsa said. "You taught me that. You, the man whose entire philosophy is *steal small, sell cheap, never be remembered*. You found a third option in every room in Vhal Maren."

"Not in this room." He knelt in front of Tam. "In this room, there's only the price. Listen, both of you, because I need to hear myself say it. The keystone isn't a memory. It's *all* of them. That's what Maerin understood, and what I refused to. The Door doesn't want the memory of the opening. It wants the keeper. A seal is only as strong as what you're willing to lose to maintain it. Maerin gave her years and it wasn't quite enough—so the Door took mine as well, and together, the two of us, brother and sister, we made a lock strong enough to hold back the end of the world." His voice cracked, and he let it. "To open the Door safely—to free them without freeing *it*—someone has to stand in the doorway and be the new seal. Alive. Willing. Paying the whole price, all at once, forever. Their entire remembered self, poured into the lock, so the Door can sort the difference: let the souls through, keep the hunger back."

The lamp flame leaned in the draft. Somewhere above them, the ruined lighthouse groaned against the sea like an old man shifting in his sleep.

"That's what she did," Tam whispered. "At the end. That's what the woman with your eyes did. She stood in the doorway."

"She stood in the doorway," Lena agreed, "and she became the lock, and she has been standing there for twelve years, alone, in the dark, holding a door shut with her whole self so that a frightened boy could run free." He laughed, and it came out broken. "And I ran. She said *run, little fish*, and I ran, and I stole my own past so I wouldn't have to feel it, and I built a cozy little life selling widows happy hours in jars, and meanwhile my sister has been holding a door open with her body for twelve years."

"Lena." Ilsa's voice was low and steady, the voice she'd used on the seawall stairs. "What are you saying?"

"I'm saying the Door will accept a substitution. One keeper for another. Maerin goes free—she's earned rest, she's *owed* rest—and a new keeper takes her place." He met Ilsa's eyes, and made himself not flinch. "Someone with standing. Someone whose memory is already half the lock. Someone who owes her."

"No," Ilsa said.

"You haven't heard the—"

"*No.*" She was on her feet, and Tam scrambled back, and the lamp flame bent hard. "Whatever you're about to propose, the answer is no. Find another way."

"There is no other way. I've spent the whole night looking for one. Believe me, I'm a thief; finding the cheaper exit is my entire religion. But some doors only cost what they cost." He spread his hands. "Twelve years ago I paid a coward's price. I cut out my own past and ran, and it worked, and I told myself for twelve years that surviving was enough. It wasn't. It was never enough. This is the bill coming due, Ilsa. And the terrible, wonderful thing—the thing I need you to hear—is that I *want* to pay it. Not because I'm noble. God knows I'm not. But because Maerin caught me my whole childhood, every single time, and this is the one time I can catch her back."

Tam had gone white. "If you become the lock," he said slowly, working it through, "then you forget everything. Not like before. Before, you could get it back—it was stolen, and stolen things can be stolen again. This is different. The Door *keeps* it. Forever. You'd walk out of here empty. You wouldn't know your name. You wouldn't know—" his voice wobbled— "you wouldn't know *us*."

"I know," Lena said softly.

"And you'd do it anyway."

"I'd do it anyway."

Ilsa crossed the chapel in four strides and grabbed the front of his coat, and for one wild moment Lena thought she was going to hit him, which would have been fair, and then instead she kissed him, fierce and furious, which was also fair, and better.

When she pulled back, her eyes were wet and blazing. "You don't get to decide this alone."

"I'm not. I decided it with you and a child and my sister's ghost. That's a bigger committee than I've ever had."

"Lena—"

"Ilsa." He covered her fists with his hands, still knotted in his coat. "There are maybe two hundred souls behind that door. Children like Tam. Grandmothers like Mother Quill's grandmother. People who have been *unwritten*, Ilsa—struck from every record, erased so thoroughly that their own descendants don't know they exist. I spent twelve years being invisible by choice. They've spent twelve years being invisible by decree. I know exactly what that costs." He smiled, and found, to his surprise, that the smile was steady. "Besides. Think of it professionally. It's the biggest theft of my career. I'm stealing two hundred people out of a locked vault, and the only thing I'm leaving behind is the fence's cut."

Above them, faint through the ruined roof, came a sound that was not the sea and not thunder: the deep, rhythmic thrum of anti-magic engines spinning up, and beneath it, thin and terrible, the chanting of fire-priests.

Ilsa closed her eyes. When she opened them, the grief was still there, but banked, controlled, filed away in the place she kept things she'd deal with after the emergency—the place, Lena suspected, where she kept her mother's face.

"How long do we have?" she asked.

"Minutes," Lena said. "Maybe less."

"Then you'd better tell me exactly what you need me to do."

Lena turned to the far wall of the chapel, where the salt-stained stone bore, invisible to anyone who did not carry Callowmere's tongue or blood, the faint outline of a door.

He took Tam's hand. He took Ilsa's.

"Well," he said. "First, we knock."
