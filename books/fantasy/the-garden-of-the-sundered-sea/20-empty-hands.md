## 20. Empty Hands

The Door did not slam shut. That was the terrible part, Ilsa thought later. It closed the way a book closes when a reader falls asleep—gently, without malice, as if the whole history of Callowmere were merely a story someone had finished.

One moment it was there: a seam of light in the drowned stone beneath the lighthouse, tall as a cathedral arch, humming with a language that predated kingdoms. The remnant souls of a nation were passing through it in ones and twos and threes—pale figures, translucent at the edges, some of them children, one of them an old woman who paused to press her hand against the wet rock as though saying goodbye to a country that no longer had edges to say goodbye to. Tam went through last of all, or nearly last. He turned at the threshold, looked back at Lena with those too-old eyes, and said something in Old Tongue that Ilsa would never be able to translate but understood anyway.

*Thank you for remembering us so we could forget.*

Then he stepped through, and the light folded inward like a hand closing, and the Sundered Door was gone—not broken, not sealed, simply *finished*, the way a debt is finished—and the silence underneath the lighthouse was the loudest thing Ilsa Corvane had ever heard.

And Lena stood where he had stood through all of it, arms at his sides, hands open and empty, staring at nothing.

"Lena?" she said.

He didn't turn.

She crossed the flooded chamber—water to her knees now, cold as a grudge—and came around in front of him. His eyes were the worst part. Not blank exactly. Blank would have been restful. They were *searching*, moving from her face to the ruined archway to the water to his own hands, quick and frightened, a man waking in a strange bed trying to assemble a life out of furniture.

"Where—" he began, and stopped, and tried again. "Where is this? Who are you?"

The words went into her like a blade going into water: no resistance, just depth.

"Ilsa," she said. "My name is Ilsa. You're Lena. We're under the old lighthouse, and the Door is closed, and everyone you sent through it is safe."

"Did I send someone through it?"

"You sent a whole kingdom through it."

He looked at her the way people look at mad prophets—with pity, mostly, and a little fear. "I don't know what that means," he said. "I don't know what any of this means. I woke up standing in water." A pause. His voice cracked on the next part, quietly, the way ice cracks. "I can't remember anything before this. There's nothing. There's just—me, and now, and you."

Above them, the lighthouse groaned.

It had been groaning for some time, actually, ever since Dreth's anti-magic engines had chewed through its foundations, and Ilsa had been ignoring it with the professional discipline of a woman who had decided she would deal with collapsing buildings after she dealt with collapsing men. But the groan deepened now into something structural, something final, and a crack ran up the inner wall like lightning deciding to stay.

"We need to go," she said.

"Go where?"

"Up. Out. Anywhere that isn't here." She took his wrist. He flinched—of course he flinched; she was a stranger grabbing him in a flooding ruin—but he didn't pull away, and that small trust, given by a man who had no reason to trust anything, undid her more thoroughly than the Door ever could.

"The stairs are gone," he said, looking at the seawall stairwell where she'd held off six wardens and two engines with a truncheon and a bad attitude. "How do we get out?"

"There's a maintenance shaft. Thief's route." She almost smiled. "You showed me. Apparently you've known it since you were fourteen."

"I've known nothing since I was fourteen," he said. "I've known nothing since I was *born*."

"Well," said Ilsa, hauling him toward the shaft, "you're going to have to take my word for a great many things, then, and I should warn you: I'm a terrible liar."

* * *

The shaft was exactly as narrow as he'd described it in the memory she'd watched him unlock—a memory that had been his, once, and was now ash along with everything else. She went first because she knew the way and because she didn't want him to see her face while she climbed. Grief, she had learned, was not improved by witnesses.

Behind her, Lena climbed in silence for a long time. Then:

"You said your name was Ilsa."

"Yes."

"That's a Warden's name."

Her heart did something complicated. "It is a name," she said. "I used to be a Warden."

"*Used* to be?"

"I resigned. Approximately forty minutes ago. By refusing an order, striking a superior officer, and being branded a traitor in front of my entire command." She wedged her shoulder through a junction and pushed upward. "It was a very efficient resignation. I've always been good at endings."

"Why would you do that?"

She considered several answers. *Because I love you* was true and useless—he'd have had no idea what to do with it, and neither, frankly, did she. *Because the law was wrong* was also true, but abstract, and this man had just paid every memory he owned for a kingdom of ghosts; he deserved better than abstractions.

"Because someone had to carry you out of here," she said finally, "and I volunteered before anyone else could."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I'm giving today. Take it up with management tomorrow."

"What management?"

"Nobody. There's no management anymore. That's rather the point."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh, if laughter could be wrung out of terror and amnesia and salt water. "You're very strange," he informed her.

"So I'm told," said Ilsa, who had been told it by drill sergeants, by High Warden Dreth, by her mother (once, long ago, in a voice she could no longer fully picture), and by Lena himself, twelve years' worth of times, none of which he remembered and all of which she did.

That was the shape of it, then. That was the bargain she'd made without signing anything. She would remember for both of them. Every joke, every argument, every night on the seawall when he'd shown her how charms caught moonlight and she'd pretended not to be charmed herself—all of it lived in her head now, a single tenant in a house built for two. And he would look at her, every morning, with the polite wariness of a man meeting a stranger who claimed to know him, and she would have to be kind about it, endlessly, patiently kind, and never once ask him to be anything back.

Love without reciprocity, freely given. Her mother would have called it foolishness. Her oath would have called it treason. Ilsa called it Tuesday.

The shaft ended at a grate above the tide-line. She shouldered it open and hauled them both out onto the shingle beach beneath the cliffs, and behind them the lighthouse—the lighthouse that wasn't there, that geography itself had agreed to forget—leaned, sighed, and came down in a long slow avalanche of white stone and older darkness. The spray reached them even at fifty yards. When it cleared, there was nothing left but a reef and a rumor.

Lena stood watching the place where his life had been.

"All of it," he said softly. "Everything I was. It's just *gone*."

"Not gone," Ilsa said. "Spent. There's a difference. Money that's spent bought something."

"Did it? Buy something?"

She looked at the reef. Somewhere beyond it, past the horizon, in a country that was learning again how to exist, an eight-year-old boy was leading his people home.

"Yes," she said. "It bought the best thing I've ever seen anyone buy."

* * *

Getting him off the flats was a problem she solved the way she solved most problems now: illegally, quickly, and with a borrowed coat.

Dreth's wardens were still crawling the district, though their hearts weren't in it—Ilsa had seen the moment at the Door, had seen Dreth stand witness to a nation walking home and visibly fail to raise her hand against it, had watched something in the old woman's face break and re-form into a different shape. Dreth hadn't ordered them stopped. She'd ordered them *withdrawn*, which in Warden grammar meant the same thing and cost less. But withdrawn wasn't gone, and a traitor sergeant escorting a barefoot amnesiac through a cordon was still a hanging offense, so Ilsa stole a fisherman's oilskin off a drying line, wrapped Lena in it, and walked him inland through the gull-cries and the morning fog like a woman taking her drunk brother home from a wedding.

He kept stopping to stare at things.

"That's a charm-seller's stall," he said at one point, halting before a shuttered kiosk. "I know what that is. I know what they sell. How do I know that?"

"Because you sold them. For twelve years. Small charms, cheap prices, never remembered."

He frowned at the stall, then at his own hands, turning them over as though the knowledge might be written on the palms. "It feels like remembering someone else's dream," he said. "I can see the shop. Little shop above the tide-flats. Shuttered windows. There's a kettle I liked." His frown deepened. "Why do I remember a *kettle*?"

"Because you were fond of it. You named it."

"I named a kettle."

"You named it Gerald."

A pause. Then, despite everything—despite the hollowed-out look and the shaking hands and the kingdom-sized grief he couldn't even locate inside himself—Lena laughed. One short, startled bark of it, immediately followed by an expression of profound suspicion, as though his own face had betrayed him.

"Why did I name a kettle Gerald?"

"Because you said everything in your life should have a name, so that losing things would hurt properly." Ilsa kept walking. "You were very firm about it. I thought it was nonsense. I've reconsidered."

He hurried to catch up, oilskin flapping. "You keep doing that. Saying things like you're quoting me. Like there's a whole—archive of me somewhere, and you've read it, and I haven't."

"Something like that."

"And you're just going to carry it around? All of it? My whole life, in your head, and I don't get any of it back?"

Ilsa stopped. The fog moved between them, patient as tide.

"No," she said. "You don't get it back. That was the price, and the price is paid, and I won't insult it by pretending otherwise." She pulled the collar of the borrowed oilskin straight where the wind had ruffled it—an excuse to touch something, any small thing. "But I'll tell you pieces. Whenever you want. Whenever it doesn't hurt too much. And when it hurts too much, I'll stop, and we'll make tea, and I'll introduce you to Gerald's successor, because I saved the kettle. It's in my quarters. Or—it was. I'm not welcome in my quarters anymore."

"Because you're a traitor."

"Because I'm a traitor," she confirmed cheerfully. "First one in my family. Mother will be devastated. Well. Mother would be devastated, if I could remember her face, which I can't, thanks to my own oath, so perhaps we're even, you and I. Two empty houses." She heard herself and winced. "That was supposed to be comforting."

"It wasn't."

"I know. I'm out of practice at comfort. I spent fifteen years practicing procedure instead."

They walked on. The fog thinned toward the flats, and the sun came up the way it always did over Vhal Maren—apologetically, through salt haze, gilding the rooftops of a city that had spent a century pretending it had never done anything wrong.

At the edge of the district, where the causeway rose toward the shuttered shops and the tide-flats beyond, Lena stopped again. This time he didn't stare at a stall or a kettle. He stared at a house. A narrow, salt-stained house with a crooked chimney and a window box full of dead flowers, sitting alone above the mudflats like a comma in a sentence nobody had finished.

"I live there," he said. It wasn't a question. His hand had come up to touch the doorframe without his permission, the way a tongue finds a missing tooth. "Don't I. I live there. I can feel it. It feels like—" He struggled. "Like the kettle felt. Like something loved."

"You lived there twelve years," Ilsa said. "Alone. On purpose. You had rules. Steal small, sell cheap, never be remembered." She looked at the crooked chimney, at the dead flowers he'd never gotten around to replacing because hope was a security risk. "You broke every rule you had, in the end. For a boy who knocked on your door in a storm."

"Was it worth it?"

The question hung there. Behind them, faintly, the city was waking: cart wheels on cobbles, a fishwife arguing with the dawn, the ordinary music of a place that would never know how close it had come to burning. Ahead of them, the little house waited with its dead flowers and its ghost of a kettle.

Ilsa thought about the Door closing like a book. About Tam's face at the threshold. About a woman on a seawall holding a staircase alone, and why she'd done it, and what she'd do again, twice as hard, given half a chance.

"I think," she said carefully, "that it was worth it to everyone except the man who paid. And I think that man deserves a warm room, a new kettle—we are *not* naming it Gerald, that name is retired with honors—and someone to sit with him while he figures out who he's going to be next." She met his eyes, those searching, frightened, brand-new eyes. "I'm volunteering. Again. I seem to keep doing that around you."

He studied her for a long moment. Strangers do that—weigh each other, looking for the trick. Whatever he found in her face, it must have been enough, because something in his shoulders let go, a tension he hadn't known he was carrying, the first knot of a thousand coming loose.

"Okay," he said simply. "Okay. I don't know you. I don't know me. I don't know what a Sundered Door is or why my chest hurts when I look at the sea." He took a breath. "But you carried me out of a falling building, and you saved my kettle, and you lie terribly, which means everything you've told me is probably true. So." He stuck out his hand, formal as a treaty. "Hello. I'm Lena. Apparently."

Ilsa looked at the offered hand.

Twelve years of memories pressed against the inside of her skull—every one of them his, none of them hers to give back—and in all of that hoard there was not a single moment as strange, or as painful, or as strangely, painfully hopeful as this one: a man with empty hands introducing himself to the only person alive who knew what those hands had held.

She shook it. His grip was cold and certain.

"Hello, Lena," she said. "I'm Ilsa. I'm going to tell you a story. It's long, and most of it's about you, and some of it's even flattering."

She steered him toward the door of the little house above the tide-flats, and behind them the sun finished rising over Vhal Maren, and somewhere far out past the reef, in a country that existed again, a boy named Tam was teaching his people the words for *home*.
