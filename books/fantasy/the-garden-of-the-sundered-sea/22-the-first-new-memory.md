## 22. The First New Memory

The cottage above the tide-flats had a name, though Lena did not know it yet.

He knew other things instead. He knew that the third stair from the bottom creaked if you stepped on its left side and sang if you stepped on its right, and that he preferred it when it sang. He knew that the kettle went on the hook by the window, not the one by the stove, because the hook by the stove was where the cat — there was no cat, but there had been talk of a cat, an ongoing negotiation — would knock it down. He knew that the woman who lived here with him was called Ilsa, and that she took her tea bitter enough to strip barnacles, and that when she laughed, which was rare and therefore precious, like ambergris or honest tax collectors, she covered her mouth with the back of her hand as though laughter were something she'd been caught stealing.

He knew all this the way you know a tide chart: accurately, usefully, and without any feeling of having earned it.

The rest of him was a room with the furniture gone. He could see the pale rectangles on the walls where pictures had hung. He could feel the shape of absences the way his tongue found the gap of a missing tooth. Sometimes at night he lay awake cataloguing them — *I had a trade once; I know I did, my hands remember knots* — and sometimes the cataloguing turned into drowning, and then Ilsa would appear in the doorway with two cups of tea, saying nothing, sitting on the edge of the bed until the water receded.

"You don't have to do that," he told her once. "Sit there. I'm sure you have important warden things."

"I'm not a warden anymore."

"Former warden things."

"They mostly involve paperwork," she said. "You're a significant improvement."

It was spring, or what Vhal Maren generously called spring: the rain turned from hostile to merely resentful, and the tide-flats put out their first green shoots through the salt crust, small stubborn things that looked like they'd read about plants in a book and were doing their best. Lena had taken to walking down among them in the mornings, Ilsa a few paces behind him — she always kept a few paces behind, close enough to catch him, far enough that he didn't feel caught — while the gulls argued overhead about jurisdiction.

That was where he was, one morning in the fourth month, when the memory came drifting in on the tide.

* * *

He had learned, in these months, that memories behaved like flotsam. The city had drowned twice over, once in water and once in law, and both drownings left debris. When the erasure edicts were repealed — quietly, the way embarrassing laws are always repealed, between market days and with no announcement anyone official would admit to — old things had begun surfacing. A street name here. A lighthouse-shaped hole in the harbor charts there. And memories, actual memories, loose ones, washing up against people who had never asked for them.

Mother Quill called it "the thaw." She was blind as ever and sharper than ever, and she had taken it upon herself to re-teach Vhal Maren its own name, one scandalized citizen at a time.

"The edicts didn't destroy anything," she'd explained, when Lena and Ilsa had visited her in the autumn. "Erasure is just storage nobody's allowed to open. You cut a lock off a cellar, everything inside comes tumbling up the stairs eventually. The city is going to be finding itself in odd corners for years. Some of it will be beautiful. Some of it will be very awkward dinner conversation."

Lena understood this better than most, because some of the debris washed up on him specifically. Fragments drifted into him the way sand drifts into an empty shell — not *his*, exactly, but shaped for him, fitting him. A smell of bread baked over driftwood. A word in a language he didn't speak but somehow knew the weight of. Once, horribly, a complete memory of falling off a pier at age six, which he experienced with such vivid terror that Ilsa had to hold him upright on the seawall until it passed, and which turned out, when they investigated, to belong to a fisherman named Odo who had fallen off that same pier at that same age and had spent forty years wondering why he couldn't remember it.

"He wants it back," Ilsa reported, after speaking to Odo. "He says it's the only interesting thing that ever happened to him."

"Tell him I apologize. Tell him I'll return it."

"Tell him yourself, coward."

So Lena had walked to Odo's boat, and taken the old man's hands, and given the fall back to him, and watched his face light up like a boy's. "That's the stuff," Odo breathed. "That's *exactly* the stuff. Cold water, right on the crown of the head. Forty years I've been drinking to forget worse things than that." He'd pressed a smoked fish on Lena that neither of them needed, and Lena had walked home lighter, in the strange double way: lighter by one memory, heavier by one fish.

He was careful with the strays. That was the rule he'd made for himself, the first rule of his new life, invented fresh because he had no old rules left. *Don't keep what isn't yours.* It felt familiar, that rule. It felt like something he might once have known so well he'd built a whole life on top of it. Ilsa said nothing when he told her this, but she got a particular look, the look of someone watching a man rediscover a room he'd furnished himself.

Which is why, on that morning in the fourth month, standing in the green shoots with the gulls yelling, when the memory came drifting toward him across the flats like smoke looking for a chimney — he tried to turn away from it.

He failed.

* * *

It arrived the way they all arrived, as a pressure behind the eyes, a warmth at the back of the skull, a sense of something pressing at a door —

*a door*

— and then it was simply there, whole and sudden, the way a wave arrives: not gradually at all.

A seawall. Grey stone, wet with spray, under a sky the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom. Wind hard enough to lean on. And a woman standing on the wall itself, feet planted on the narrow capstone, arms braced wide against a door behind her — a door that stood impossibly in open air, tall and dark and older than stone has any right to be — holding it open with her body. Not struggling. Holding it the way you hold a gate for someone you love, patiently, past the point of comfort, because the person coming through matters more than your aching shoulders.

Her hair whipped sideways in the wind. Her face was set in the expression of someone who has already decided and is merely waiting for the world to catch up. And she was looking — directly, deliberately, across all that wind and ruin — at someone. At the place where the memory's owner stood. At him.

She smiled.

Not a big smile. A tired one, crooked, the kind you give when you want the last thing someone sees of you to be easy to carry.

And she said — her voice half-stolen by the wind, but he caught it, he caught every syllable and knew even as he caught them that he would keep them longer than anything else he owned —

"Well done, little brother. Now *run*."

Then the memory ended, the way waves end, all at once and leaving foam.

* * *

Lena came back to himself kneeling in the salt-grass, which was undignified, and crying, which was confusing, because he did not know the woman, and you cannot weep for strangers.

Except apparently you could, because he was doing it.

Ilsa reached him in four strides — she'd been four strides away the whole time, she was always four strides away, he suspected she could sleep at that distance — and crouched beside him in the mud.

"What did you get?" she asked. Not *are you all right*. She'd learned that question was useless; the answer was always the same complicated no. "What came in?"

"A woman," he managed. "On a seawall. Holding a door open. With her body, Ilsa, she was just — *standing* there, holding it, like it weighed nothing, like *she* weighed nothing—" His breath hitched. "She looked at me. Whoever owned this memory, she looked right at him, and she smiled, and she said—" He stopped. Saying it again felt like spending money he didn't have.

But Ilsa had gone very still, in the way she went still, total stillness, a stillness with weapons in it.

"What did she say?"

"*Well done, little brother. Now run.*"

The silence lasted long enough for three gulls to resolve a territorial dispute and for the tide to make a decision about a crab. Then Ilsa sat down in the mud next to him, entirely ruining trousers she would later claim not to care about, and took his hand.

"Lena," she said carefully. "What color was her hair?"

"Dark. Almost black. Why—"

"And her eyes? Did you see her eyes?"

He closed his own, and the memory gave it up willingly, generously, the way it had given everything. "Grey," he said. "Sea-grey. Like—" He opened his eyes. Ilsa was watching him with an expression he had no chart for.

"Like yours," she finished quietly. "They're like yours. I've seen them before, Lena. In the lighthouse. On the far side of the Door, when it opened. There were hundreds of faces in that light, and hers was the closest to the threshold, and she was the last one through." Her thumb moved over his knuckles, slow. "I think that was Maerin. I think that was your sister. And I think that memory isn't a stray at all."

"It isn't?"

"No." She almost smiled, that hand-over-mouth smile, except this time she didn't cover it. "I think it's a letter. I think somebody carried it a very long way and addressed it to you."

* * *

They walked home slowly, because Lena's legs had opinions and none of them were cooperative, and because Ilsa had learned that after a drift-memory the best medicine was smallness: small steps, small talk, the kettle on the singing stair-side of things.

At the cottage door she paused. "Do you want to give it back?" she asked. "If it belongs to someone else — Odo's fall, the baker's bread smell — you've always handed those back. I can help you find the owner, if—"

"No," Lena said.

It came out fast and certain, faster than anything he'd said in months, and it surprised them both. He stood there on the threshold with the wind coming off the flats and the memory warm behind his eyes like a coal wrapped in cloth.

"She told me to run," he said. "Whoever she was — whoever she *is* — she used her last look at her brother to tell him to run, and then she waited, Ilsa, she held that door until everyone was through, and the first thing she made sure he'd carry out of there wasn't fear. It was permission. It was *well done*." His voice cracked and he let it. "Everyone else gets their memories back. This one, I'm keeping. If she ever comes looking for it, I'll return it with both hands and an apology. But until then—" He touched his own temple, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping child. "Until then it stays."

Ilsa looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, the nod she used for verdicts.

"The first thing you've chosen to keep," she said. "In four months. Do you know what that makes it?"

"Stolen?"

"Mm. Also: yours." She pushed the door open onto the smell of yesterday's bread and the particular golden light the cottage did at late afternoon, as if the sun had decided this one small building was worth showing off to. "Everything else you know, you recovered. That one you *picked*. That's different, Lena. That's the difference between driftwood and a keel."

He frowned. "Is that a nautical metaphor? Because I feel like I should know nautical metaphors. I feel like I *used* to know nautical metaphors."

"You used to know everything," Ilsa said, hanging her coat on its peg. "Now you get to choose. Frankly, I think you're doing better this time. Last time you chose to forget your entire childhood. This time you're choosing to keep one good moment. That's progress by any ledger I've ever seen."

He laughed — and stopped, because the laugh had come easily, unarmored, from somewhere with no furniture but plenty of light, and he wanted to mark the spot.

Inside, the afternoon arranged itself around them the way it always did: tea, the negotiation about the cat (Ilsa had located a kitten at the fish market with one bent ear and the confidence of an admiral; Lena had held out for two weeks and was losing), the small domestic machinery of two people building a life out of parts. But Lena kept returning, all evening, to the coal behind his eyes. The seawall. The wind. The crooked smile.

*Little brother.*

He had a sister. He had *had* a sister. Somewhere beyond a door that no longer existed, she was alive, and she had sent him a message across the impossible distance, and the message was not grief. It was pride. It was *go*.

Before bed, he stood at the window looking out over the dark flats, where the green shoots were coming up through the salt in their thousands, absurd, unstoppable, plants that had read about hope in a book and were doing their best anyway.

"One more thing," Ilsa said from the doorway. She was holding something — a folded paper, worn soft at the creases, as if it had been unfolded and refolded many times. "I've been waiting for the right night. And I think — if you're keeping things now — maybe tonight's the night you start keeping this too."

She crossed the room and put it in his hands.

It was a story. His story — the true one, the whole one, written out in Ilsa's blunt, disciplined handwriting, the handwriting of someone who had once filled out reports and had decided, at last, to fill out something worth reading. *Lena Reyes,* it began. *Born in Callowmere, in a house above the water, to parents who loved him and a sister who loved him more...*

He looked up. "You wrote me?"

"You were taking too long to write yourself," Ilsa said. "Someone had to keep the records. It's what I'm for." She settled into the chair across from him, tea in hand, bitter enough to strip barnacles. "Start anywhere. Or — start at the beginning. I hear it's a popular choice."

Lena smoothed the pages on his knees. Behind his eyes, a woman on a seawall smiled her crooked smile, and somewhere past the edge of every map, a kingdom was learning its own name again, and outside the window the flats were quietly, stubbornly turning green.

He began to read.

And for the first time since the Door had closed behind him, the empty rooms inside began, one small chosen piece at a time, to fill with light.
