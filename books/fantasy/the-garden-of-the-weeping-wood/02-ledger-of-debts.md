## 2. Ledger of Debts

The summons came the way all bad news came in Corvale: on good paper.

Wren knew paper the way a fishwife knew fish. She could feel its provenance in her fingertips—the cheap grey stock of market receipts, the waxy brown of debt-notices, the soft rag paper of private letters. The summons that arrived at her stall on Tallowday morning was cream-colored, heavy, edged in the Scriptorium's double rule of black. It was the kind of paper that did not get sent twice.

*Wren Thornbury, scribe of the third bench, is required to present herself before the Office of Ledgers within two days.*

She read it standing behind her counter, between a woman haggling over candle-charms and a boy counting out coppers for a blessing against toothache. She read it with the particular stillness of someone who has learned to keep her face like a locked room. Seven years in Corvale taught you that. Seven years of selling small protections under the counter, of keeping your voice low and your sleeves long and your name spelled three different ways on three different documents—taught you that the city was a machine for noticing people, and survival was the art of being dull.

She folded the summons along its crease and slid it beneath the ledger she kept open as theater—a book of legitimate transactions, ink prices, copywork fees. Then she smiled at the boy, took his coppers, and sold him a charm against toothache that was, by every law of the Sundered City, nothing but knotted red thread and a whispered word.

The word had never mattered before. That was the whole architecture of her smallness: the charms were supposed to be placebo, comfort sold to people who needed comfort more than magic. A knot tied with intention. A breath spoken over wax. Harmless. *Harmless*, she had told herself seven hundred times, because harmless things did not draw the eye of the Inquisitors' Office, and the Inquisitors' Office drew eyes the way wounds drew flies.

But yesterday a charm had worked. Not the way charms worked when you wanted them to—not the slow self-deception of a mother believing her child slept better because of a knot in his window-curtain. It had worked *impossibly*. The luck-charm Wren had sold to Sera Voss, the laundress with the sick daughter and the landlord circling like a gull over offal, had done something no thread and no whisper should do. Sera had come back to the stall weeping, clutching Wren's wrist with both hands, saying the fever had broken overnight, saying the landlord's men had come and gone without a word, saying *it worked, it worked, bless whoever made it*—and Wren had felt, in the moment of that grip, something move beneath her skin like water under ice. Something old. Something that had been waiting so patiently she had almost forgotten it was there.

She had gone home and burned salt in her hearth and not slept.

Now this summons, on good paper, two days after a miracle.

Correlation was not causation. Wren repeated this to herself like a rosary through the whole walk up Bellows Street, past the chandlers and the pawnshops and the shrine-shells where gods used to live and now only pigeons lived, their niches swept clean by city ordinance, their little altars legally converted to flower-boxes. Correlation was not causation. Summons were routine. Debt-review happened every quarter. There was no reason—no *reason*—to think anyone had connected a laundress's broken fever to a scribe's knotted thread.

But her hands were cold anyway, and she put them in her pockets, and she climbed the Scriptorium steps like a woman walking into deep water.

* * *

The Scriptorium smelled the way it always smelled: tallow, dust, iron-gall ink, and fear pressed flat under discipline. It was a beautiful building, which was part of the cruelty. Vaulted ceilings painted with the Sundering itself—gods falling like comets, mortals raising walls of law against the sky. Every apprentice who copied a line beneath those ceilings learned early what the paintings meant: *this is why we write everything down. This is why nothing is trusted to memory or mercy again.*

Wren had spent six years of her life on the third bench, copying ledgers, transcribing testimony, learning the beautiful brutal grammar of the city's accounting. Her debt had been born here too—or rather, recorded here. Seven years ago, Magister Halev had stood beside her while an assessor read out the sum: the cost of her apprenticeship, her keep, her tools, compounded quarterly, a number designed never to be paid. Debt-bound scribes were not slaves, exactly. They were simply not free. Their names lived in a ledger, and their labor belonged to whoever held the page.

Her holder had been the Scriptorium itself, these seven years. Until today.

They put her in a small room with a small window and a clerk named Pell who had the face of a man who enjoyed his work in the way that was worst to witness: quietly, thoroughly, without malice. Pell unrolled the ledger page across the table and weighted its corners with brass.

"Quarterly review," he said. "Routine."

"Of course," Wren said.

"Except." Pell smoothed the page once with his palm, a gesture like a man petting a dog he intended to sell. "Your debt has changed hands."

Wren looked at the page. Her own name sat there in her own handwriting from seven years ago—younger ink, steadier hand—and beside it, in the column where the Scriptorium's seal had always sat, was a new mark. A stranger's seal. She didn't recognize the sigil: some merchant house, some moneylender, some name she had never heard spoken in any room she'd ever been allowed into. Beneath it, the transfer date. Four days ago.

Four days ago was two days before Sera Voss's charm worked.

"Who bought it?" Wren asked, and was proud of how level her voice came out.

Pell consulted his notes as though the answer were boring. "The instrument of purchase doesn't disclose the principal. That's standard now, since the Ledger Reforms. You'll address correspondence to the seal." He rolled the page closed. "Your obligations don't change. Your labor remains assigned to the Scriptorium. But your person—" he paused, enjoying the word, letting it sit in the air like a fly in amber, "—your person is now collateral to a party unknown."

*A party unknown.* Wren turned the phrase over later, walking home, and hated how precisely it fit. That was what she had been trying not to be for seven years: known. Known was the first step toward noticed, and noticed was the first step toward the pyre-wagons, and everyone in Corvale knew what the pyre-wagons were for, even if no one said it above a whisper. Magic was outlawed. Practitioners were not imprisoned; imprisonment implied you might come back. They were *corrected*. The word had a bureaucratic gentleness that made it worse.

She asked Pell one more question—whether the transfer required her signature, whether she had any recourse—and Pell smiled the smile of a man closing a drawer. "Debt isn't a contract, scribe. It's a condition. Conditions aren't signed. They're inherited."

* * *

Magister Halev found her at dusk, in the copying hall, at the bench she still technically occupied. He came quietly, the way he always did—an old man moving like a rumor of himself, gray-robed, half-lame, carrying his authority like a weight he'd learned to distribute invisibly. For six years he had been the closest thing Wren had to a protector, which in Corvale meant the closest thing to a jailer who occasionally pretended to be kind.

He sat down beside her without asking, which was itself a message. Masters did not sit at apprentices' benches.

"You received the summons," he said. Not a question.

"And answered it."

"I know. I read the transfer myself." His eyes went to the ledger in front of her, then away, quickly, like a man avoiding a mirror. "Wren. Listen to me. Whatever you think is happening—stop selling the charms."

"They're nothing," she said. "Thread and breath."

"Nothing has a signature." Halev's voice dropped lower, until it was nearly subvocal, a frequency only the two of them occupied amid forty scratching pens. "I've seen the Inquisitors work. I helped build the procedures they use—I was young, I thought I was building safety. A working charm leaves a trace in the pattern of the city. Fever breaks where fevers shouldn't. Luck pools where luck shouldn't. They don't need to catch you holding the thread. They only need to notice the shape of the impossible and ask who profits from it."

"Sera Voss's daughter had a fever. Fevers break."

"Yes," Halev said. "And landlords change their minds. And dice turn kind. And carts miss the people they were aimed at. All of it happens, constantly, naturally, and none of it matters, because the Inquisitors don't investigate causes. They investigate *coincidences*. Enough coincidences around one address, one name, one stall, and the coincidence becomes a confession." He laid one spotted hand flat on the bench, very gently, over her knotted threads. "You have been careful for seven years. Be careful for seventy. Sell them nothing but comfort. Say the words softer. Or say no words at all."

Wren wanted to argue. She wanted to say: *the words are nothing, the words have always been nothing, you taught me they were nothing.* But she had felt the thing under the ice yesterday, turning over, waking, and she could not make her mouth form the lie.

"Halev," she said instead. "My debt was bought by a stranger four days ago. Before the fever broke. Who buys a scribe's debt?"

Something crossed the old man's face—too fast to name, too slow to miss. Grief, maybe. Or guilt wearing grief's coat.

"Money moves in the dark in this city," he said. "That's all I know. That's all I can tell you." And then he rose, slowly, wincing at his hip, and left her with forty pens scratching and the vaulted ceiling full of falling gods overhead, and the feeling—cold, certain, unprovable—that the man who had protected her for seven years had just declined to protect her from something he understood completely.

* * *

Her rooms above the chandler's shop on Ropewalk Lane were one room and a hearth, and she loved them the way you love a hiding place: fiercely, and with the knowledge that love does not make a thing safe.

That night she did not sleep. She sat at her counter—the same counter where she mixed wax and tied knots and spoke her soft harmless words—and she inventoried her life the way Pell inventoried debts. One room. One trade. One name in one ledger, held now by a seal she couldn't see the face behind. One mentor whose silence had a new texture. One miracle, loose in the world, wearing her signature.

The walls felt closer than they had yesterday. That was the thing about claustrophobia—it wasn't the room that shrank. It was the certainty that the room had always been a cell, and you had mistaken it for shelter.

Below her window, Ropewalk Lane went about its business. A drunk singing. A watchman's boots. Somewhere a baby crying in the rhythm of colic, that endless exhausted loop. Wren listened to the crying the way she listened to everything now—with the terrible new attention of someone who has felt power stir and cannot decide whether to be its keeper or its prey.

And then, near midnight, footsteps on her stairs.

Not the watchman's measured tread. Not the drunk. Soft steps, hesitant, the steps of someone climbing toward a door they weren't sure they had the right to knock on. Wren blew out her candle on instinct and stood with her back to the wall, heart going hard, listening.

The knock, when it came, was gentle. Three taps. Paused. Two more.

She knew before she opened it who it would be. The lane below had shown her: a thin man, hooded, carrying nothing. And she knew him from the market—from weeks of passing her stall, never buying, always watching, always leaving before she could speak to him. Tam. The cooper's brother. The man whose wife had died in winter and whose hands had shaken ever since, and who had stood at her counter one afternoon and whispered, so quietly she'd almost missed it, a prayer.

Not a request for a charm. A prayer. The old kind. The kind that had nowhere to go anymore, since the shrines were flower-boxes and the gods were outlawed and the only ears left in Corvale belonged to clerks and creditors. He had prayed over her counter—to whatever might be listening—for his hands to stop shaking. For one night of sleep. For his dead wife to stop appearing at the foot of his bed every time he closed his eyes.

She had sold him a sleep-charm out of pity and told herself it meant nothing.

Now he stood at her door at midnight, hood back, face hollowed, and he said the thing she would carry up out of this chapter of her life like a coal in cupped hands:

"It answered."

Wren did not move. "What answered?"

"The prayer." His voice was steady in the way of a man who has passed through shaking and out the other side into something worse. "I whispered it over your counter, over the wax, while you were tying the knot. I didn't mean anything by it. It was just—old habit. My grandmother prayed like that. You say it to the thing nearest, and in this city the thing nearest is usually a counter." He laughed, briefly, horribly. "Last night I slept. First time since she died. And my wife came to me—not at the foot of the bed, not the accusing ghost, not the dream. She came in the dream and she *spoke*, and she said the wood outside the city remembers us. She said the trees are weeping because the prayers are pooling with nowhere to go. She said—" his voice caught, and he steadied it with visible effort, "—she said someone has to go and listen."

Wren stood in her dark room with her back against the wall of her careful life, and she felt the ice crack all the way through.

Because she had heard it too. That afternoon, alone at her counter, mixing wax, she had heard—faintly, underneath the traffic noise, underneath the sound of her own thoughts—a sound like water moving through roots. Like something vast and patient drawing breath. She had assumed it was the pipes. Old buildings made old noises. Everyone knew that. Everyone in Corvale knew a hundred ways to explain away a miracle, because explaining away miracles was the civic religion, the litany that kept the pyre-wagons rolling past your door.

But Tam had heard the answer *aloud*. Out loud, in the dark, in his sleeping and his waking. An answer, returned, to a prayer whispered over a scribe's counter in a city where prayer was legally a form of talking to yourself.

The walls of her life had been closing all day—summons, transfer, warning, silence—and now, at midnight, one of those walls had a door in it. And through the door: the ruined shrine district, the empty niches, the swept altars, the place where the pigeons lived where gods used to. *Go and listen,* the dead woman said. *Someone has to go.*

Tam stood wringing his steady, newly quiet hands, waiting for her to tell him he was mad. Waiting for the comfortable words. *Grief-dreams. Sleep deprivation. The mind making shapes out of missing.* She had those words ready. She had used them on herself for seven years.

Instead she heard herself say: "Which shrine? Which wood?"

Tam blinked, as if he'd expected anything but that. "The old grove past the North Gate. The Weeping Wood. The one the ordinance calls a timber reserve." He swallowed. "You know it?"

Everyone knew it. The petrified trees at the city's edge that wept sap like tears, that the city had fenced and posted and forbidden, that children dared each other to touch and adults pretended not to see. Wren had walked past its fence a hundred times and felt nothing but the ordinary dread of a law-abiding woman near a boundary stone.

But tonight, hearing Tam speak, she felt the fence differently. She felt it as a lid.

"I know it," she said.

"Then you understand why I came to you." Tam's eyes, hollow and sleepless no longer, fixed on her with a desperate clarity. "You're the one the prayer touched. It was your counter. Your wax. Your hands tying the knot. If the answer came through anything in this city, it came through—" he gestured helplessly at her, at the room, at the whole small architecture of her hidden life, "—through whatever you are. Whatever's been sitting under your skin all these years, quiet, harmless, waiting."

*Whatever you are.* Wren had spent seven years making sure no one asked that question, including herself. And now a grieving cooper stood in her doorway at midnight asking it, sent by a dead woman, and the honest answer was rising in her throat like floodwater: *I don't know. I don't know what I am. I only know that something woke up, and it knows my name.*

She looked past him, down the stairwell, into the dark of Ropewalk Lane, where the watchman's lantern cast its slow circle. Somewhere out there was a Scriptorium ledger with a stranger's seal beside her name. Somewhere out there was an Inquisitor's office where coincidences became confessions. Somewhere out there was a fence around a grove of weeping trees, and behind the fence, if the dead were telling the truth, something ancient had begun, very quietly, to listen.

The walls were closing in. She could feel it in her teeth, in the shrinking dimensions of her safe small life, in the way the summons on good paper and the stranger's seal and Halev's frightened silence all pointed at her like compass needles finding north.

But a door had opened. And doors, in Wren's experience, did not open in cells unless someone on the other side wanted out.

"Tomorrow," she said to Tam, and her voice sounded strange to her own ears—steadier than she felt, older than she'd ever let it be. "Before dawn. Before the market. Before the clerks wake up and start their counting. Meet me at the North Gate."

Tam nodded, dazed with gratitude, and descended the stairs into the lantern-dark, and Wren sat back down at her counter and did not sleep at all.

She sat instead with her palms flat on the wood, feeling for it—that sound beneath the sounds, the water through roots, the vast patient breathing. And near the hour before dawn, when the city's noise thinned to nothing and even the watchman's circle paused at the far corner of the lane, she felt it clearly for the first time. Not heard. *Felt.* A pressure against her palms, gentle and immense, like the touch of something on the other side of a door, laying its hand flat against hers.

It knew her. However it had learned her name—through the wax, through the knots, through seven years of small harmless words spoken over small harmless objects—it knew her, and it was waiting, and the fence around the Weeping Wood was not a boundary but a threshold, and somewhere beyond it, in the ruined shrine district where the pigeons nested in the empty niches of outlawed gods, something had opened its eyes.

Wren took her cloak from its hook. She took her grandmother's ring from the box beneath the floorboard—the one relic of the family she never spoke about, the one inheritance she had never dared examine too closely. She took the summons on good paper and left it on the counter, face up, where the morning light would find it, because a part of her understood already that she would not be here when the clerks came looking.

At the window, the sky over the North Gate was not yet light. But it was not quite dark either. It was the color of held breath.

She blew out her candle, and the dark came in like water, and beneath the water, faint but unmistakable, the wood was weeping.
