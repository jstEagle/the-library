## 3. What the Gods Remember

The prayer had been answered aloud. That was the fact Wren carried through the dark streets of Corvale like a coal cupped in both hands, too hot to hold and impossible to put down.

She had whispered it over her counter at midnight—*show me where*—the way she had whispered a hundred small petitions in seven years of careful living, the way one talks to a locked door out of habit, not hope. And the door had opened. A voice had come up through the floorboards, through the mortar, through the old bones of the city itself, and it had said: *Ashes. Where the ash was laid.*

Now she walked north, past the tanneries with their sleeping vats, past the wall where the proclamations were nailed and re-nailed until the wood beneath was a scab of paper and rusted tack, past the last of the lamp-posts beyond which the city simply stopped pretending to govern itself. The sky had gone from held breath to a thin, bruised grey. Her breath made ghosts in front of her face. Beneath her sleeve, the debt-brand sat against her wrist like a second pulse—the Scriptorium's sigil inked into her skin at fourteen years old, the mark that said *this life belongs to a ledger*. She had spent seven years learning every way there was to be small. Tonight smallness felt like a coat that no longer fit, buttons straining, seams splitting at the shoulders.

She should have gone east instead. East was the river road, east was the ferry, east was anywhere that wasn't where a voice in the floorboards told her to go. Every sensible instinct she owned lined up in a row and pointed away from the North Gate, and she walked past all of them, because the voice had used her grandmother's word for the dead. Not *ashes*. Not the Inquisitors' word, the clean administrative word for what remained after a pyre. The old word. The word from the lullaby her grandmother had sung before the singing stopped: *where the ash was laid, the roots remember.*

Wren had thought, for seven years, that she was the only person left alive who knew that song.

* * *

The ash-field lay beyond the North Gate like a wound the city had decided to stop looking at. Once it had been a district—she knew this the way everyone in Corvale knew it, in the vague way one knows things officially unwritten. Before the Sundering there had been temples here, a whole quarter of them, and after the Godswar the Office had burned them and plowed the stone into the earth and seeded the ground with quicklime so that nothing would grow, and then the city had grown anyway, sprawling around the dead place like flesh around a splinter, and now it was just a field of grey dust and half-buried foundations that children were warned away from and went to anyway.

She picked her way down the broken steps into the sunken street. The air changed as she descended—not colder, but *older*, the way the air in the Scriptorium's deepest archive was older, thick with the smell of time doing its slow work. Dust hung motionless. Her boots left prints in ash fine as flour, and each print filled back in behind her, softly, as though the ground were breathing closed.

The shrine was exactly where the voice had said it would be, which was nowhere, which was everywhere—which was a doorway she had passed a thousand times without seeing, set into the side of a buried wall, half its lintel fallen, the rest furred with grey lichen. It was small. Smaller than a market stall. The kind of shrine a lamplighter might have kept, or a midwife, or a scribe. Carved above the empty niche where an idol had once stood was a single line of script in the old hand, the hand her grandmother had taught her to read on winter nights with the shutters barred:

*WE HEAR. WE ANSWER. WE ARE ANSWERABLE.*

Wren stood in front of it for a long time. The words did something to her chest that she didn't have a name for. They sounded like a promise. They also sounded, now that she read them slowly, like terms. Like the opening clause of a contract—and she knew contracts; she had copied ten thousand of them; she knew that every promise ever written was also a leash, and the only question was who held the other end.

"You read the old hand."

The voice came from inside the shrine's shadow, and Wren's whole body went to iron. Her hand found the little knife she carried for cutting quills—a scribe's knife, useless, but hers—and she turned toward the darkness under the broken lintel.

A beggar sat there. That was the first thing, and for a moment the only thing: an old man in a coat of patched grey, thin as a bundle of sticks, hands folded in his lap like a man who had been waiting so long that waiting had become his occupation. His face was unremarkable in the way that faces of the very poor are unremarkable—the city taught them invisibility early and thoroughly. But his eyes—

His eyes were wrong. Not cruel-wrong, not mad-wrong. *Deep*-wrong. Looking into them was like looking down a well and realizing, halfway, that the water at the bottom was looking back up at you.

"I'm sorry," Wren said, which was what you said to beggars, and she hated herself for the reflex even as she said it. "I didn't mean to disturb—I was just leaving—"

"No," he said mildly. "You weren't."

And the terrible thing, the thing that made her stop with one foot already turned, was that he was right. She hadn't been leaving. She had been standing in front of a carved sentence feeling her whole careful life tilt on its axis, and some part of her had known, since the voice spoke through her floorboards, that she would end up here, in this exact spot, having this exact conversation.

"Who are you?" she asked.

"A question." He smiled, and the smile rearranged his whole face into something briefly, startlingly young. "Good. Questions are how it starts. Statements are how it ends—that's how it ended last time, you know. Everyone making statements. Nobody asking anything." He unfolded one hand from his lap and gestured at the space beside him against the wall. "Sit, if you like. Or stand. I've waited eleven hundred years; I can wait while you decide."

Eleven hundred years. The number landed in her stomach like a dropped coin. The Godswar had ended nine hundred years ago. The Sundering, the ban, the burning of this quarter—all of it inside nine hundred. Which meant either the beggar was mad, or he was counting from something older, something before the histories began, and either answer was worse than the question.

"What did you say your name was?" she asked, and did not sit.

"I didn't." He tilted his head. "But you may call me Ashen-Vey. It isn't my name—it's my *sentence*, if you like, the shape my exile took when they poured me out of heaven. Names are for those who are kept somewhere. I am kept nowhere, so I go by what I am made of." He looked down at his own thin hands. "Ash. And the wish not to be."

The dust in the shrine stirred. Not wind—there was no wind down here—but a slow turning, as if the air itself had leaned in to listen. Wren felt the hair rise on her arms, and beneath the fear, unwelcome and unmistakable, she felt the other thing: the stirring in her blood, the same warmth that had flared in the market when the luck-charm worked, the same current that had answered her prayer through the floorboards. It rose in her like sap. It recognized him.

"You," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "It was you. Last night. The prayer—I prayed over my counter, and something answered—"

"I answered." He said it simply, without apology. "I have been answering prayers for eleven hundred years, child. Into silence. Into nothing. It is the one work left to a god with no door into the world—to hear, and to be unable to help. Do you know what that is? To hear every prayer in a city and be to them exactly what the Inquisitors say I am? Nothing?" His eyes came up, and the well-water look of them was suddenly unbearable. "Then three nights ago, someone prayed, and the prayer *landed*. It struck the sealed door like a knock. And I put my ear to the other side, and I followed the sound, and do you know whose blood knocked?"

The field above seemed to press down. The dust hung. Wren became aware of her own heartbeat, loud and mortal in the hush.

"Mine," she said.

"Yours." He nodded slowly. "Blood opens doors. It always has—it's why the covenant was written in it, once. Your charm in the market, the one that worked when it should have been a trinket. Your whisper over the counter that reached me through six feet of stone and law. You are leaking, Wren Thornbury. Whatever has been dammed up in you for seven years—" he paused, and corrected himself, gently, terribly, "—for twenty-four years, since the day they branded you—is finding the cracks."

Her name. She hadn't given him her name. She took a step backward and her heel came down in the ash and the ash swallowed the sound, and the knife was in her hand now without her remembering drawing it, a ridiculous little crescent of scribe's steel pointed at a beggar in a ruin.

"How do you know my name."

"Because I knew your grandmother." He said it without triumph, without drama, the way one reports weather. "Maren Thornbury. Who came to this shrine every seventh day for thirty years. Who sang the lullaby to you before the singing stopped. Who wrote the letter that got you sold into debt, and wept while the ink dried, and signed it anyway, because the alternative was watching the Office take you the way they took her daughter." He watched Wren's face as he said it, and whatever he saw there made him lower his voice. "I heard her pray too. For years. *Keep her small. Keep her hidden. Let her live.* I could not answer. But I remembered. We remember, we gods. It is nearly all we do."

The knife was shaking. Wren looked at it—at her own white knuckles, at the debt-brand showing below her cuff where her sleeve had slipped—and the vertigo hit her all at once, the full weight of it, the ground going soft under everything she had built her life on. Seven years of smallness. Seven years of harmless charms and careful ledgers and never saying her family's name aloud, and it had all been *arranged*. Not fate, not caution, not her own good sense. A dead woman's plan, executed by a god in a beggar's coat, and she had lived inside it like a fish lives in water, calling the water her own idea.

"My grandmother," Wren said slowly, "was a hedge-charmer. A village wise-woman. She grew herbs and told fortunes at the harvest fair and—"

"Maren Thornbury was the heir of the Weeping Wood." Ashen-Vey said it quietly, and the quiet was worse than shouting. "As her mother was before her, back and back, to the first sealing. When the gods and mortals met in the grove at the world's edge and swore the covenant—*we hear, we answer, we are answerable*—it was a Thornbury who held the mortal pen. The covenant runs in that blood like a river runs in a valley. You can dam it. You cannot remove it." He spread his ruined hands. "They burned the grove's gates. They outlawed the names. They killed or scattered every heir they could find. Maren hid you in plain sight—in the city, in a ledger, under a brand—because no one looks for a god's heir among the debt-bound. And it worked. For twenty-four years, it worked." His eyes lifted to hers. "Until three nights ago, when the heir of the Weeping Wood sold a luck-charm to a frightened mother, and it *worked*, and the whole sleeping covenant turned over in its grave."

"The covenant broke," Wren said. "Everyone knows it broke. That's why the ban exists. The gods broke faith and started the Godswar, and the mortals won, and—"

"And the victors write the histories." Something moved across the beggar's face—grief, or its fossil. "Child, I was there. I argued on your side of it, if it matters, and it cost me everything, and I will tell you all of it, every shameful piece, when you're ready to hear it. But not tonight, and not as a beggar's tale. Tonight I will tell you only what you need to walk out of this place alive." He leaned forward, and the shadows leaned with him, and the dust turned in slow spirals around the little shrine like a congregation settling. "The covenant can be reforged. The grove still stands at the city's edge—petrified, weeping, but standing, because a covenant is not a building; it is a *promise*, and promises do not die, they only wait. And a reforged covenant needs two hands to seal it. A god's hand—" he raised his own, ash-grey, "—and the heir's. Only the heir's. There is no other door. There has never been another door."

The words went through her like cold water finding every crack in a wall. *Only the heir's.* She thought of the summons lying face-up on her counter in the morning light. She thought of Magister Halev's warning, the fear under his kindness. She thought of the mother in the market weeping over a working charm, and of the voice in the floorboards, and of the lullaby, and of her grandmother's ring sitting heavy in her pocket right now, warm as a live thing.

"I'm a scribe," she said. "I copy other people's words for four coppers a page. I owe a debt I'll die owing. I am nobody's heir. I'm a *ledger entry*."

"Yes," Ashen-Vey agreed, and his voice was gentle as a hand on a frightened animal. "That is what they wrote about you. And here is what I know, Wren Thornbury, last heir of the Weeping Wood: the blood does not read ledgers. The blood remembers. It has been remembering in you your whole life—every time a charm came out stronger than it should, every time you knew rain an hour early, every time you looked at the petrified trees past the North Gate and felt homesick for a place you'd never been." He let that sit. Then, softly: "You felt it just now, walking down these steps. Don't lie to me. I've heard eleven hundred years of lies; I know their smell. You felt the wood weeping, and you felt it call you *home*."

She wanted to deny it. She opened her mouth to deny it. And what came out instead, in a whisper, was the truth:

"It never stopped. All these years. I thought I was mad."

"You were *haunted*," he said. "There is a difference, and the difference is that hauntings end."

For a long moment neither of them spoke. Above the sunken street, dawn was arriving in Corvale—the bells of the morning watch, faint and tinny, the first carts on the gate road, the ordinary machinery of a city that believed its gods were dead. Down here the dust turned and turned. Wren looked at the carved words above the empty niche—*we hear, we answer, we are answerable*—and understood, finally, with a scribe's precision, that she was standing inside the oldest contract in the world, and that contracts, once witnessed, could not be unsigned. Only renegotiated. And she had spent her whole life learning exactly what words could bind.

"If I say yes," she said. "If I even listen. What happens to the life I have?"

"That life," said Ashen-Vey, "is already ending. The Office traced your charm. They will trace the prayer. By tomorrow night there will be men with iron brands and fire-pikes walking this quarter, and they will find the shrine, and they will find whoever is near it." He rose, unfolding joint by joint, and up close, in the greying light, the beggar's body seemed less like a disguise than like a punishment—a god poured into the smallest vessel available, wearing poverty the way other men wore chains. "So the question is not whether your old life survives, heir. The question is what you carry out of it."

And that was when Wren heard it—faint, above, at the top of the broken steps: boot-heels on stone. Many of them. Moving in the disciplined cadence she had heard only once before, at fourteen, on the day of her branding, and had never forgotten.

Iron brands. Fire-pikes. Already walking.

Ashen-Vey's eyes flicked upward, and then to her, and the well-deep calm of them did not change at all.

"They're early," he said. "Decide faster."
