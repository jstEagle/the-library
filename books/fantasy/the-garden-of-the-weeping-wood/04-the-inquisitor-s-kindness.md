## 4. The Inquisitor's Kindness

The rain came down on Corvale like a penance, steady and gray, and Inquisitor Dain Marrec walked through it without a hood because he had long ago decided that discomfort was a form of honesty. A man who sheltered himself from the weather would, in time, shelter himself from other things—the sight of the debt-brands at the fish stalls, the sound of children coughing in the Bellows Street tenements, the small cruelties that accumulated in a city like silt in a harbor. He refused to be sheltered. It was, he told his juniors, the whole discipline of the Office: to see clearly what you were sworn to prevent.

He was forty-one years old, broad through the shoulders in the way of men who had once carried things for a living—he had been a stevedore's son on the Saltquay before the examinations took him—and he wore the slate-gray coat of the Inquisitors' Office with its single silver eye stitched at the collar. The eye did not blink. That was the point of it.

The charm lay on his desk when he returned to the Office on Candlemaker's Row, wrapped in oilcloth, still faintly warm.

That was the thing that troubled him. Not that it worked—working charms surfaced two or three times a year, usually the desperate improvisations of hedge-witches or the last flickers of some inherited gift burning out of a bloodline—but that it *worked well*. Cleanly. Without the scorched smell of strained magic, without the backlash that left untrained casters with nosebleeds and trembling hands. He had seen a hundred confiscated charms in eleven years of service, and they were almost all ugly things: knotted twine smeared with tallow, coins drilled and threaded on wire, prayers scratched backward into wax. This was different. This was a simple disc of river-clay, fired pale, stamped with a sigil so small and so precise it might have been cut by a jeweler—a sigil Marrec did not recognize, which was itself remarkable, because he had spent eleven years learning to recognize them all.

And it was warm. Hours after its making. Hours after its working.

"Tell me again," he said to Junior Inquisitor Sella Voss—no relation to anyone worth noting, she always said, too quickly—who had taken the report from the night watch.

"The mother bought it in the Lamplight Market, sir. Thursday evening. Stall near the fountain, the row of scribes and charm-sellers under the awnings." Sella recited it flatly, the way reports were meant to be recited, but her eyes kept drifting to the clay disc as though it might do something. "Seller described as female, mid-twenties, slight build, dark hair. Paid the stall fee, packed up before full dark. The buyer's boy had the fever—lungfever, the physiker said, the bad kind. Mother pinned the charm inside his shirt Friday morning. By Friday night the fever broke."

"Fever breaks," Marrec said. "Children survive lungfever."

"Not this fast, sir. And not—" Sella hesitated. She was young, twenty-three, earnest in a way the Office had not yet ground smooth. "Not with the fever pattern the physiker described. He wrote it up himself, unprompted. Said he'd never seen a crisis turn like that. Said it was as if the fever *decided* to leave."

Marrec turned the charm over in his palm. Warmth seeped into his skin like a small animal's heartbeat. Somewhere beneath his sternum, in the place where he kept the things he did not say aloud, something stirred—not fear exactly, and not longing either, but the uncomfortable proximity of both.

Because he remembered. That was the trouble with Dain Marrec; he remembered everything. He remembered his sister Maren, aged six, burning with lungfever in a room above the Saltquay cooperage while their father spent three days' wages on a charm from a woman who smelled of woodsmoke—and how the charm had done nothing, nothing at all, and Maren had died on the fourth morning, and the woman with the woodsmoke smell had been taken by the Office two weeks later and burned in the Plaza of Judgment, and their father had spat at the pyre, and Marrec, aged nine, had not known whether the spit was for the witch or for the god who hadn't answered.

He had spent his whole life deciding it was for both.

"Find her," he said.

* * *

It took four days, and Marrec spent all of them in the Lamplight Market, which suited him better than the Office's upper rooms ever could. He liked markets. They were honest in aggregate even when dishonest in particular. He bought chestnuts he didn't eat and eels he didn't want and let the vendors talk, because vendors talked, and somewhere in the stream of complaint about fees and weather and the new weighmaster's crooked scales, the shape of the charm-seller emerged.

A scribe, they said. Kept to herself. Sold little paper blessings folded into triangles—traveling mercies, mending charms, the kind of harmless nonsense the Office tolerated the way a householder tolerates mice. Seven years at the same corner, maybe longer. Never greedy. Sometimes didn't charge at all if the need looked bad enough. There was something about the debt, someone said—she wore her sleeve long even in summer, and once a collector had come round and she'd gone white as tallow and handed over nearly a week's earnings.

"She's careful," said an old woman selling candied ginger, in the tone of one delivering a verdict. "Carefuler than any of them. Whatever you're wanting her for, Inquisitor, you'll find she's never given cause. Not once."

"No one gives cause," Marrec said quietly. "That's rather the nature of the law."

The old woman looked at him with the flat appraisal of the market, which weighs everything and forgives little. "That's what my grandmother said about the tithe-men," she said, and went back to her ginger.

He traced the stall fee through the Market Registry—a clerk's labor, dull and exacting, and Marrec was a good clerk, which was a thing people forgot about good inquisitors. The fee had been paid every week for seven years, in exact coin, never late, never early enough to draw notice. The name attached to the license was Thornbury. Wren Thornbury. Debt-bound, indenture held by the Corvale Scriptorium, term of service: life, absent redemption.

Life. He read the entry twice. Most debts ran five years, ten. A lifetime scribal indenture on a girl who sold paper blessings for coppers—it sat wrong in him, the way a badly set bone sits wrong. He copied the registry number into his notebook anyway, because wrongness was not evidence, and evidence was the only currency the Office honored.

On the fourth evening, the rain returned, and with it a boy from the night watch, breathless, holding out a scrap of paper.

"Begging pardon, sir. Sella sent me. We pulled the Scriptorium's work-ledgers—quietly, like you said. Outwork assignments, piecework, the commissions farmed to debt-bound scribes." The boy swallowed. "There's a commission record, sir. Three years back. A private order for a hundred blessing-seals, clay-fired, custom sigil. Paid through an intermediary. The sigil drawing is appended to the entry."

Marrec took the paper. The sketch was crude, a clerk's copy of a clerk's copy, but the line of it went through him like cold water.

The same sigil. His unknown sigil, the jeweler-precise mark on the warm clay disc, rendered here in a ledger three years old, ordered in quantity, paid for by no one in particular.

"Who placed the order?"

"That's the thing, sir. The intermediary's seal is a merchant mark—Vessin & Sons, importers. But Vessin & Sons dissolved six years ago. And the authorizing signature on the Scriptorium side—" the boy checked his notes, though Marrec suspected he knew it by heart, having read it four times on the walk over— "is Magister Edran Halev. Master of the Bindery. Twenty-two years' service."

Halev. Marrec knew the name the way one knows the names of load-bearing walls. Halev had testified before the Conclave twice on the question of residual magic in debt-bound populations. Halev had drafted half the Office's current classification standards for harmless charms. Halev was, in the polite phrase used in the Office's dining hall, *sound*.

And Halev had commissioned a hundred seals bearing a sigil that appeared on a charm that had cured lungfever in a night.

Marrec stood a long time at his window, watching rain silver the lamps of Candlemaker's Row. Below, a mother hurried past with a child bundled against her shoulder, and he caught himself watching them the way he watched everything now—with the terrible double vision of his office, seeing at once the living thing and the threat it might conceal.

Here was the shape of it, and he could not pretend otherwise: someone had seeded the city's charm-trade with a working sigil years ago, patiently, in quantities, waiting. Whether the scribe Thornbury was the hand or only the glove, whether she knew what she carried or was herself carried—those were questions. But the ban was the ban. It had ended the Godswar. It had ended the burning of villages whose only crime was a prayer answered too loudly, the lords who drank power from their tenants' faith, the gods themselves squabbling over mortal devotion until mortals were merely the field the war was fought on. Forty thousand dead in the Sundering Year alone. His own grandfather had been a soldier in the last of it and had come home without a soul to speak with, and had sat by the fire silent for thirty years until death took the rest of him.

The ban was not cruelty. The ban was the dam wall, and Marrec had spent his life being one stone in it, and he had made his peace with being a stone. Stones did not get to choose which flood they held back.

But.

But the warmth in the clay. But the physiker's note, *as if the fever decided to leave*. But a mother in the Lamplight Market weeping over a living child, and somewhere in Marrec's chest the nine-year-old son of a cooper, who had prayed every night for a year after Maren died—not to any god in particular, just *please*, into the dark—and had received silence, and had been told the silence was mercy, the silence was safety, the silence was the price of peace.

He thought: if the gods are waking, and this is how they wake—in a fever breaking, in a poor woman's child breathing easy—then what precisely am I defending the city from?

He thought: that is not a question an inquisitor is permitted to ask.

He closed the notebook over the registry number and the ledger page and Magister Halev's name, and he felt the truth of his profession settle onto him the way the wet coat settled onto his shoulders: that duty was a kind of devotion, that he had given his life to it the way other men gave theirs to gods, and that devotion asked everything and explained nothing, and that tomorrow he would draft the warrant.

Sella appeared at the door, rain-damp, holding a second report. Her face had the careful blankness of someone carrying news they wished they did not have.

"Sir. The night watch checked the shrine on Pilgrim's Stair—the ruined one, the old ash-shrine off the processional way. Someone's been tending it. Fresh offerings. And there are traces, sir. Residue in the altar cracks. Working residue, recent, and—" she hesitated— "it matches the signature on the charm. Same hand. Same... warmth."

Marrec looked at the rain. Somewhere out in it, a scribe with a hidden brand and a stranger's sigil in her inventory was walking home through the city he had sworn to keep safe, and he found that he was already sharpening toward her, the way a blade sharpens toward whatever it is given, and hating, quietly and without effect, the excellence of his own edge.

"Warrant for the shrine," he said. "Tonight. And put the name Thornbury in front of a judge by week's end."

He picked up his pen. It was a good pen; he had owned it eleven years. It began, as it always began, with the words *In the name of the peace of Corvale*, and his hand did not shake, because he would not permit it to, because a stone does not shake, because the wall must hold, must hold, must hold—

—and beneath the words, unwritten, unsaid, in the place where a nine-year-old still whispered *please* into the dark, the first hairline crack opened in the stone, thin as a sigil drawn in clay, and waited.
