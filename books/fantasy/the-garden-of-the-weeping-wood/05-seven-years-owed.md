## 5. Seven Years Owed

The bells of the Inquisitors' Office began to ring before Wren reached the second stair, and she knew the sound for what it was—not the hour, not the fire alarm, but the *raiding peal*, the triple-strike pattern every child in Corvale learned to flinch at before they learned to read.

She was three streets from the ruined shrine when the first screams came.

Behind her, the quarter of Lantern Row lit with torchlight. She could hear the boots—dozens of them, the coordinated hammer of a sweep in progress—and beneath that, a sound that would follow her for the rest of her life: the crackle of a warding-fire catching on old wood, and the voices of people praying as they ran. Praying *aloud*. In the open. Because a god had answered one desperate woman's prayer over a scribe's counter, and now the whole city was remembering what prayer was, and the Office was remembering why it had made them forget.

Wren pressed herself into the recessed doorway of a shuttered dyehouse and pulled her hood low. Her hands, she noticed distantly, were shaking. Not from fear. From the residue. The shrine had been *alive* when she'd touched it—she could still feel it under her skin, the groaning of old stone remembering what it was to be holy, Ashen-Vey's voice like smoke in her ear: *You opened the way back. Your blood, scribe. Your blood is the key that was lost.*

And then the Inquisitors had come, and Ashen-Vey had put a beggar's hand against her sternum and said, *run*, and the wall behind her had simply—opened. A door where no door was. She'd fallen through into an alley that smelled of tanneries and had run without stopping since, her lungs full of knives.

*They will have your name from the shrine's stones within the hour,* the god had said. *And from your name, your debt. And from your debt, your address. The ledgers of Corvale are more thorough than its gods ever were.*

The bells stopped. In the sudden quiet, she heard the thing she'd been dreading: a crier's voice, hoarse with official urgency, moving up the cross-street.

*"By order of the Inquisitors' Office! Wren Thornbury, debt-bound scribe, is sought for the crime of unlicensed working! Any who harbor her share her guilt! Reward of fifty crowns against any debt—"*

Fifty crowns against any debt. Wren closed her eyes. Half the city owed more than that. Half the city would sell her for half of that.

She should run for the gates. She should run for the river, the coal barges, anywhere. Instead her feet carried her—because seven years of habit is stronger than seven minutes of terror—toward the Scriptorium. Toward Magister Halev, who had taught her to bind a ward so fine it looked like neat penmanship, who had found her at fourteen with no family and no name worth having and said, *I can keep you small enough to survive*, who had been the only constant in a life measured in careful increments.

The Scriptorium's night door was in the shadow of the bell tower, and Halev answered her knock before the second rap fell, as if he'd been standing on the other side of it for hours.

He looked, for the first time since she'd known him, *old*. His silver hair was unbound, his collar open, and behind him the scriptorium's long tables were bare—every ledger, every quill, every scrap of vellum gone. He'd burned nothing. He'd *hidden* everything.

"Wren." He said her name the way a man says the name of something he has already begun mourning. "You shouldn't have come here."

"They're burning Lantern Row." Her voice came out flat, strange. "The shrine. Ashen-Vey—the beggar, the god, whatever he is—he told me they'd trace me. I needed—"

"I know what you needed." He stepped back to let her in, and closed the door, and threw the bolt, and for a moment they simply stood in the empty room where she had spent half her life bent over a desk, and the silence was so complete she could hear the tallow candle gutting.

"Magister." She turned to face him. "Tell me the Office doesn't know it was me."

He didn't answer. And in that non-answer, in the way his eyes went to the floor, Wren felt something shift in the architecture of her life—a load-bearing wall, quietly removed.

"Magister. *Halev.*"

"They came at dusk," he said. "Before the raid. Two of them, and a clerk. They had a warrant of inquiry, which is not a warrant of arrest—it's the gentler instrument, the one they use when they want cooperation rather than confession." He laughed, a small terrible sound. "They were *cooperative* with me, Wren. They sat at my own table and drank my wine and asked me, with great courtesy, whether I had any knowledge of a debt-bound scribe dealing in unlicensed charms."

"And you told them—"

"I told them I had no knowledge of it." He looked up. "And then the senior one—Marrec, his name was Marrec—he put a paper on the table. A ledger page. My own hand, Wren. The record of your indenture. And he said, very kindly, that the Office had noticed an irregularity in it. That the debt had been *originated* under circumstances the Office found curious. That if I wished to avoid an audit of the Scriptorium's full accounts—every charm, every ward, every small mercy I have sold out of this room for twenty years—then I might assist them in locating the originator."

The candle guttered. Wren's mouth had gone dry.

"Originator," she repeated. "The debt was originated—Halev, you told me my parents owed it. You told me their estate was seized and I was bound against the remainder. You told me that when I was fourteen years old, weeping on the floor of this room."

"Yes."

"*Yes?* That's all you have to say? Yes?"

"No." He drew a breath, and Wren watched him do the thing he did best: compose himself, line by line, like a page being ruled. "Sit down, child."

"Don't call me that."

"Then sit down, Wren, because what I have to tell you will take longer than your legs will hold you."

She did not sit. So he told her standing, both of them standing, like adversaries, like the last two people in a burning house.

"There was no debt," he said. "Not at first. Your grandmother came to me the winter you were thirteen. Maerlin Thornbury. She was—she was a woman out of the old stories, and I say that as a man who has read all of them. She came to my door with a token of the Weeping Wood in her hand and she told me what her blood was. What *your* blood is. And she told me the Sundering had not ended the way the histories say, and that one day the grove would call its heir home, and that when it did, the Office would come for that heir with fire."

"So she hid me—"

"She *hid you*. She brought you to me because I was the only scrivener in Corvale who still knew the old binding-scripts, the ones from before the ban, and she asked me to do the one thing the ban made easiest: to make a person disappear into paper." His voice cracked, and he mastered it. "A debt-bound scribe is invisible, Wren. Debt-bondsmen can't hold property, can't marry, can't testify, can't inherit. No one looks at them. No one *records* them beyond the ledger line. She asked me to bind you so thoroughly into smallness that not even the Office's genealogists could find the Thornbury line. And I did. I wrote you into debt against a fiction, and I sold the fiction to a holding-house, and I have spent seven years quietly buying your paper back, piece by piece, so that someday I could burn it and set you free."

Wren's hand went to her sleeve, to the debt-brand beneath it, the scar that read her as property. Seven years. Seven years of ink-stained fingers and counted coins and the smallness she had believed was her own failure, her own caution—and it had been *written*. Designed. A cage her grandmother had commissioned out of love and Halev had built out of—out of what?

"You let me believe I was nothing," she whispered. "You let me believe I'd *inherited* nothing. That my parents died owing money. I mourned people who didn't exist, Halev. I mourned a debt."

"You mourned a story. Yes. And I let you, because the story was armor." He stepped toward her, hands out, the old teacher's gesture, and she stepped back and saw him register it, saw the wound of it land. "The Office came tonight, Wren. Marrec is not a cruel man, which is worse—he's a *thorough* one. They gave me until dawn to produce you or produce your location. And if I produced neither, they would audit the Scriptorium, and the audit would find the old binding-scripts, and the old binding-scripts would lead them not only to you but to the *script itself*—the pre-ban covenant-script your grandmother trusted me with. Do you understand? It's not your life I was trading. It's the only surviving copy of the terms."

The room tilted. Wren caught the edge of a desk.

"You sold my location," she said. "Just now. Tonight."

"I bought you until dawn," he corrected, and his voice was steady and his eyes were not. "The eastern gate watch changes at the fourth bell and the new watch-captain owes me. I told them you'd gone to ground in the Tanner's Warren, which is a maze they'll spend three hours searching badly. I bought you the night, child. I have spent seven years buying you nights. That is *all I have ever done*—"

"You sold me *first*." The words came out of her like something tearing. "Seven years ago. You sold me into bondage first, and I thanked you for it. I *thanked* you, Halev. I brought you tea. I defended you to the other scriveners. Every night I knelt and thanked the saints my mentor was a good man, and the truth is that my whole life is a document you forged and I have been living inside your handwriting—"

"Yes," he said simply. "And it kept you alive."

"And now you've sold me *again*."

"I bought you *time*—"

"Time for *whom*?" She was shouting now, and did not care; the bells had already told the whole city. "Time for you? Time for your scripts, your ledgers, your twenty years of small mercies? You always told me the difference between us was that you understood the price of things. Well, Magister, I finally understand the price of *me*. Fifty crowns against any debt. That's what I cost. That's what I've always cost. You just kept the receipt."

He flinched as if she'd struck him. And then, horribly, he nodded—slow, weary, a man conceding a point in a debate he'd been having with himself for decades.

"Perhaps," he said. "Perhaps that's true. Go east, Wren. The Tanner's Warren, the fourth bell, the watch change. And take the—" he turned, rifled in the one drawer he had not emptied, and held out a folded packet of vellum, sealed with plain tallow, "—take this and never bring it back here. It's the covenant-script. Your grandmother's terms. I was supposed to give it to you when you were ready, and I kept deciding you would never be ready, because as long as you weren't ready you were still *mine to protect*."

She took it. Their fingers did not touch.

"And Halev," she said, at the door, not turning around, "when they come back at dawn—what will you tell them?"

The silence stretched so long she almost looked back.

"The truth," he said at last. "It seems to be the only thing left in this room that I haven't sold."

She went out into the dark, and she did not let herself cry until the Scriptorium was out of sight, and even then only for the length of one alley, one breath, one hand pressed against wet brick—because grief, she had learned at fourteen, was a debt too, and she could not afford the interest tonight.

* * *

Ashen-Vey was waiting for her at the mouth of the Tanner's Warren, sitting on an overturned dye-vat as if he'd been placed there by a stagehand, and the sight of him should have frightened her and instead made something in her chest unclench by a single degree.

"You're late," he said. "I had time to count the rats. There are more than the ledgers claim."

"The ledgers claim nothing about rats. Ledgers only count what someone paid to have counted." She pulled the hood back. "They know my name. They'll know my face by morning. There's a crier offering fifty crowns."

"Then we should be flattered. When the gods walked openly, a working miracle bought a war. Now it buys fifty crowns and a crier with a sore throat." He slid off the vat, and even in the beggar's body, even stooped, there was something in the way he moved that made the shadows lean toward him. "The watch changes at the fourth bell. Your mentor's arithmetic holds, if that's any comfort."

"It isn't." She looked at him—really looked, the ruined shrine still burning behind her eyes. "You knew. About the debt. About the binding. You said *your blood is the key*, but you knew it was bound and buried. How long have you been watching me, Ashen-Vey?"

The god was quiet for a moment. In the dark of the warren-mouth, his eyes caught light that had no source.

"Since the first charm worked," he said. "Seven years of smallness, and then one luck-charm that *took*. I followed the crack in the wall, scribe. That's all any of this is. I heard something break, and I came to see what was on the other side."

"And the debt? The ledgers? You said the Office would trace me through my debt. You knew the debt was real enough to trace."

"I know paper," he said, and something old and tired moved under the words. "I was a god of oaths before I was a god of ash. I know what it is to be written into a binding you did not choose. I looked at you in that market and I knew the brand on your arm the way a drowning man knows water." He tilted his head. "Does it matter, how much I knew?"

"It matters whether you're using me."

"Of course I'm using you." He said it without cruelty, the way one admits to breathing. "You are the heir and I am the exile and we are each the other's only door. That is not a betrayal, Wren Thornbury. That is a *bargain*. The difference between a bargain and a betrayal is whether the terms are spoken aloud. So let me speak them: I need the covenant reforged, and only you can reforge it. You need the debt ended and the Office outrun, and only I can do the second and possibly the first. Those are the terms. Hate them if you like, but do not pretend you were offered gentler ones by anyone else tonight."

She thought of Halev, buying her nights for seven years and calling it protection. She thought of her grandmother, writing her into a cage of smallness and calling it safety. Everyone she had ever trusted had held a pen over her life.

At least this one was showing her the page.

"Fine," she said. "The terms stand. But I set one of my own. You don't lie to me again. Not by omission, not by *arrangement of truth*. If I'm to be your heir, I'll be it with my eyes open. Swear it."

Something flickered across the god's ruined face—surprise, or its ancient equivalent.

"You would take an oath from a god," he said. "After everything your histories say about gods and oaths."

"The histories are forged. I've recently learned that about a great many things." She held out her hand. "Swear it, or find another heir."

And Ashen-Vey, last of the exiled, took her hand in his beggar's fingers and swore—quietly, in a language that made the dye-vats sweat and the rats go still, in a language she somehow understood without knowing how—and the oath settled over them both like a second debt, and she found she did not entirely mind.

"East," he said. "Through the warrens, under the Bellows Street, down where the city keeps the things it doesn't want to count. There are people below, Wren. People who never stopped praying, even into the silence. And one of them has been waiting thirty years for a Thornbury."

They went down together, past the fourth bell, past the last lit window, into the dark beneath Corvale—Wren with her grandmother's terms against her ribs and her mentor's betrayal in her mouth like copper, and behind her, far above, the Scriptorium's candle burning down toward dawn, toward the Inquisitors' knock, toward the truth Halev had promised them at last.

She did not look back.

But she swore, in the dark, in her own plain human words, that when this was over, someone would read every ledger in Corvale aloud—and that no one, ever again, would learn what they cost from the inside of a binding they never chose.
