## 6. Beneath the Bellows Street

The dark beneath Corvale had a smell, and the smell was old.

Wren had lived in the city seven years and had never once thought of it as having an underside. She had thought of it the way everyone did: as streets stacked on streets, roofs on roofs, the Scriptorium's spire rising like a needle through the middle of everything. But now she was learning that the city was more like a tree—what showed above ground was only half of it, and the other half clung to the dark and drank from hidden water.

Ashen-Vey walked ahead of her with a beggar's shuffle that was, she suspected, entirely optional. He moved through the undercity's first layers—the drainage galleries, the abandoned cisterns, the bricked-up mouths of wells older than the Sundering—with the ease of someone returning to a house he'd been locked out of for a long time. Twice he stopped at blank walls and pressed his palm flat against the stone, eyes closed, listening.

"What are you hearing?" Wren asked the second time.

"Prayer," he said. "Old prayer. It soaks into stone the way rain soaks into chalk. This whole city is saturated with it." He opened his eyes, and in the gloom they caught light that wasn't there. "They prayed for a generation, Wren Thornbury. They prayed knowing no one answered. Do you understand what that is? That isn't faith. Faith expects a return. That is *loyalty*."

He said it with something like hunger, and Wren found herself walking a little faster, as if she could outpace the sound of it.

They descended past the fourth bell—that was how Ashen-Vey measured depth, by the bells whose ringing reached down here as only a memory of a memory—and then past the last lit window, which belonged, absurdly, to nothing at all, a frame set into raw stone with candlelight behind it and no room attached. Someone had built it as a joke, or a shrine, or both. In the undercity, Wren was beginning to suspect, the distinction barely held.

Then the passage narrowed, turned twice, and opened without warning into warmth.

She smelled it before she saw it: tallow smoke, wet wool, bread. Real bread, baked recently, somewhere close. After two days of stolen sleep in cistern corners and meals of hardtack bought from a smuggler who hadn't asked questions because Ashen-Vey had paid him in coins that were too old to be legal tender but too beautiful to refuse, the smell hit her like a blow.

Ahead, a wall of rough-hewn blocks blocked the tunnel end to end. It looked like any dead-end in any forgotten gallery—except that a hairline seam ran down its center, and at the base of the seam, someone had carved a small mark: a circle, crossed by a single vertical line, worn nearly smooth by touch.

Thousands of fingers had touched that mark. Wren could see the polish of it.

"They'll hear us before we knock," Ashen-Vey said quietly. "So don't knock. Stand where they can see you, and let them look."

"Look at what?"

"At what you're carrying." His voice gentled in a way that frightened her more than any threat had. "At what you *are*."

He stepped back into shadow, folding himself into the dark like a beggar folding into a doorway, and Wren understood that this part she would do alone.

She stood before the sealed door with her heart going like a trapped bird and pressed her own finger to the circle-and-line. The stone was warm. Not sun-warm, not body-warm—warm the way a charm was warm when it worked, when something on the other side of the world leaned close to listen.

The seam split. Light spilled out, gold and thick with smoke, and a voice said, in the tone of someone asking a question they already knew the answer to:

"Name yourself."

And Wren, who had spent seven years giving false names to customs men and careful names to customers and no name at all to anyone who might write it down, heard herself say:

"Wren. Wren Thornbury."

The silence that followed was total. Even the tallow flames seemed to hold still.

Then hands—many hands—pulled her through.

* * *

The room beyond the door was not large, and it should have been suffocating, and it was not. It had been a cistern once; the vaulted ceiling still dripped mineral tears in slow, patient intervals, each drop catching the light of dozens of candles set in niches along the walls. Benches made from ship's planking lined the floor. At the far end stood an altar that was really just a table—a table with a cloth, and on the cloth a bowl of river water, a dish of salt, and a single green branch, real and living, which should have been impossible this far underground and this far from spring.

There were perhaps forty people there. Dockworkers still in their gear. A laundress with red-knuckled hands. Two women in fine coats gone shabby at the cuffs, the kind of poverty that had once been wealth. A boy of maybe twelve, asleep against his mother's side, his cheek smudged with ash. They had been mid-service when the door opened—she could see the hymnal, hand-copied, chained to the table like the Scriptorium chained its ledgers—and now every face was turned toward her.

Not with fear. That was the thing she couldn't make sense of at first. Not with fear, and not with the hungry curiosity of a crowd. With something closer to recognition, though they had never seen her before in their lives.

An old woman came forward from the altar. She was small and straight-backed, with white hair braided tight against her skull and eyes the color of strong tea. She wore a scribe's ink-stain on her right middle finger—old, deep-set, the stain of decades—and Wren felt a jolt of kinship so sharp it was almost pain.

"You gave your name," the old woman said. Her voice was low and even, a voice used to being heard without being raised. "Your true name. To a sealed door. In the Bellows Street warrens. You must be either very desperate or very sure."

"I'm not sure of anything," Wren said. "I'm extremely desperate."

A ripple went through the congregation—not laughter exactly, but the place where laughter lives, the shape of it, held carefully. The old woman's mouth twitched.

"Honesty," she said. "That's rarer than faith, down here." She studied Wren's face the way Halev had once taught her to study a manuscript—looking past the surface text to the hand beneath it. "You flinched when you said it. Your name. Why?"

Because the last time I said my full name aloud, a man I loved sold me. Because names are debts. Because I've spent seven years teaching myself to be no one—

"I was taught to keep it small," Wren said.

"By whom?"

"A scribe. Magister Halev, of the Scriptorium."

Something moved through the room at that name—not hatred, though some faces hardened, but a kind of grim confirmation, like weather arriving that had long been promised.

"And did he teach you letters?" the old woman asked.

"He taught me everything. Reading, cipher, the law of contracts, the proper boiling of ink—" Her voice caught. "He taught me how to hide."

"Yes," the old woman said softly. "He would have. It was the only way he knew to keep anything alive." She extended her hand, palm up. "I am Elder Sef. And you are standing in the last congregation of the Old Covenant in the city of Corvale, child, and we have been praying to silence for thirty-one years. So before anything else happens, I am going to ask you one question, and I want you to answer it slowly, because forty people in this room have risked their lives on the answer."

She drew a breath.

"The charm-seller of Tanner's Row. The one whose luck-charms *worked*. Whose working brought Inquisitors into the shrines and the poorhouses and the Scriptorium itself. Is that you?"

Every eye in the room was on her. Wren thought about lying. It was her oldest skill, her safest craft, the smallness she had built brick by brick for seven years.

But these people had kept a living branch under the earth for a generation. They had earned better than her lies.

"It's me," she said. "I didn't mean it to work. I never meant any of it to work. It was supposed to be harmless."

Elder Sef closed her hand around Wren's and gripped it hard, hard enough to hurt, hard enough that Wren understood the grip was the point.

"Child," she said, "nothing holy has ever been harmless."

* * *

They fed her first. That was the law of the place, Sef explained—no business, no questions, no revelations until a stranger had eaten bread and salt at the table. So Wren sat on a ship's plank bench between the laundress (whose name was Maret) and one of the shabby-genteel women (who had been a magistrate's wife, once, and who now copied prayers by hand because all printing of them was contraband), and ate dark bread and salted fish and drank wine so sour it made her eyes water, and felt something in her chest unclench for the first time in two days.

Around her, the congregation resumed its service as if interrupted services were routine, which she gathered they were. They sang, very quietly, a hymn with no words she recognized—melodies passed mouth to mouth for thirty years, never written down until the magistrate's wife began copying them, and even her copies were kept in three pieces in three houses. They prayed aloud, each in turn, and here was the thing that undid Wren, sitting with her sour wine: they prayed *expecting nothing*. No amen chased after their words like a hand reaching for payment. They spoke their griefs and gratitudes into the air the way you'd speak them to a grave, and the speaking itself was the whole of the act.

"We stopped praying for answers a long time ago," Maret murmured, seeing her expression. "Answers get people burned. We pray to remember we're allowed to want things."

"That's the saddest sentence I've ever heard."

Maret shrugged, red knuckles flexing. "It's also the bravest. Sad and brave travel together down here, scribe. You'll get used to the company."

When the service ended, the boy who'd been asleep woke and stared at Wren with open, unembarrassed curiosity, and asked his mother, in the carrying whisper of children everywhere, "Is that her? The one the gods woke up for?"

His mother hushed him, but the question sat in the air, and no one took it back.

Elder Sef beckoned Wren to the altar table then, and dismissed the others to their benches, and the two of them stood alone among the candles while the congregation pretended very badly not to watch.

"Now," Sef said. "Tell me what happened at the shrine. All of it. Leave nothing out because it seems impossible—I promise you, child, in this room impossibility lost its power to frighten us decades ago."

So Wren told it. The ruined shrine behind the tanneries. The beggar with light in his eyes. The words Ashen-Vey had spoken—*your blood opened the way back*—and the claim that had followed her out of that place and into every hour since: heir of the Weeping Wood, last of the line that had sealed the covenant between gods and mortals, daughter of a family erased from every ledger in Corvale except, apparently, the ones written in blood.

Sef listened without moving. When Wren finished, the old woman was silent for a long moment, and then she laughed—a short, disbelieving, almost angry laugh.

"Thirty-one years," she said. "Thirty-one years I have led this congregation, and I have told them the covenant would wake again when the heir returned, and do you know what I expected? Nothing. I expected to die saying it. It was a story to keep the candles lit." She pressed both hands flat on the table. "Thornbury. Say it again."

"Wren Thornbury."

"Do you know what that name was, before the Sundering?"

"No. Nobody does. It's not in any record. Believe me, I've looked. Looking is the one thing I'm good at."

"It's not in any record," Sef agreed, "because the records were burned, and the burning was ordered by the same office that wrote the history your Magister Halev taught you. But it was in other places. Places that don't burn so easily." She tapped her own temple. "Mouths. And—" she paused, and her tea-dark eyes went distant— "one other place. Wait here."

She crossed to the back of the chamber, to a niche behind the altar, and knelt, and did something with her hands that Wren couldn't see. When she rose and turned, she was holding a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord gone soft with age, and she carried it the way you carry a sleeping child, or a wound.

She set it on the table between them.

"This was given into my keeping by the elder before me, who received it from the elder before her, going back to the night of the Sundering itself. The instruction that came with it was simple, and I have repeated it to myself for thirty-one years like a prayer: *when a Thornbury comes through the door, give it to her, and tell her the grove remembers.*"

Wren's mouth had gone dry. "How did you know a Thornbury would come?"

"We didn't know. We *hoped*, and hoping is the discipline of the hopeless." Sef began untying the cord with fingers that trembled slightly, whether from age or from the weight of the moment Wren couldn't tell. "But we kept the door marked. We kept the branch alive. We taught our children the name, and the name of the name, and the reason for both. That is what a congregation *is*, child. It is a memory that refuses to die politely."

The oilcloth fell open.

Inside, on a bed of cloth gone brown with age, lay a token.

It was small—a disc of pale wood, no bigger than a coin, strung on a leather thong worn black with handling. But the wood was wrong for wood, or right for a wood Wren had never seen: grained like water frozen mid-flow, faintly warm, and at its center, carved so fine it could only be read by touch, was the circle-and-line from the sealed door. The mark of the covenant. And around the edge, in script older than any alphabet the Scriptorium taught, ran a ring of tiny characters that Wren's fingers, more than her eyes, understood.

When she touched them, the chamber went quiet.

Not silent—quiet, the way a forest goes quiet, the way a room goes quiet when someone important enters. The candle flames leaned toward her, all together, all at once, like grass bending under wind. The drip of mineral water from the vaulted ceiling slowed, hung, resumed. And beneath her fingertips the token warmed, and warmed, and Wren felt—distinctly, unmistakably, the way you feel a hand take yours in the dark—something answer.

*Welcome home,* said the feeling, which was not a voice, or was a voice older than voices. *We kept your seat.*

Wren snatched her hand back. The candles righted themselves. Somewhere in the congregation, the boy gasped, and his mother pulled him close, and no one scolded him, because every adult in the room had seen it too.

"What—" Wren's voice cracked. She started over. "What is it?"

"That," Elder Sef said, "is the covenant-token of Maera Thornbury, who stood at the heart-tree of the Weeping Wood on the last night of the old world, and spoke the terms that bound gods and mortals both. It was cut from the heart-tree's living wood the day the covenant was sealed. There were three such tokens. Two were destroyed in the Godswar." She lifted the token from its wrappings and held it out across the table, and her hand was steady now, steady as a scribe's rule. "This one survived because your grandmother gave it away. To us. To the small faithful, the ones the histories never mention. She said someone from her line would come looking for it someday, and that when they did, they should know they weren't coming to strangers."

Wren didn't take it. She couldn't. Her hands wouldn't move.

"Why me?" she whispered. "I sell luck-charms out of a market stall. I owe a debt I can't pay to an office that owns my signature. Three days ago the largest thing I'd ever done was forge a customs seal. I'm not—I'm nobody. I'm *small*. I made myself small on purpose. That was the whole point of me."

"Yes," Sef said. "And do you know what the covenant was, child? Before it was terms and seals and heart-trees? It was a promise made by small people. Farmers. Fishwives. Scribes. People who had watched gods walk over them like weather and decided, together, in their plain human voices, *enough*. The covenant was never grand. That was its genius, and the reason it broke—because the grand ones on both sides forgot what it was made of." She took Wren's limp hand and pressed the token into it, and folded Wren's fingers closed over the warm wood. "Smallness was never your weakness, Wren Thornbury. It was your inheritance. You are exactly the right size for this."

The token beat against her palm like a second heart.

Behind her, from the shadows near the tunnel mouth where he had waited all this time, silent and patient as furniture, Ashen-Vey stepped forward into the candlelight—and the entire congregation flinched as one, forty bodies drawing breath at once, because whatever beggar's shape he wore, they *felt* him now, the way you feel a storm front through a shut window.

Elder Sef did not flinch. She looked at him for a long, long moment, and her tea-dark eyes were not warm.

"So," she said. "The exile comes home too. Tell me, fallen one—does he come as her servant, her teacher, or her leash? Because we buried enough of our people over the centuries to learn the difference, and I will know which it is before I let him stand one step closer to that child."

Ashen-Vey smiled his terrible, gentle smile, and bowed.

"Elder," he said, "I come as all three. And only one of those answers is a lie."

The candles guttered. Somewhere far above, muffled by thirty feet of stone, Corvale's dawn bells began to ring—and beneath their peal, thinner but unmistakable, carried down through the warrens like a smell of smoke, came another sound.

Boots. Many boots. Moving through the upper tunnels, in step, in order, closing on the Bellows Street entrances from three directions at once.

Maret was suddenly at Wren's elbow, her red-knuckled hand cold as riverwater as she gripped Wren's arm.

"Inquisitors," she breathed. "They've never come this deep. Never, in thirty-one years." Her eyes went to the token in Wren's fist, then to the old woman, then to the dark of the tunnel. "Elder—they followed *her*."
