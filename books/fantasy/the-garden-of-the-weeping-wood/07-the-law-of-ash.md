## 7. The Law of Ash

The Scriptorium smelled the way Marrec imagined old men's regrets would smell: dust and lamp oil and the faint sourness of ink gone bad in its pots. He had been in a hundred rooms like it over eleven years with the Inquisitors' Office—records halls, archive vaults, the cold annexes where confessions were taken down in triplicate—but this one made his skin prickle in a way he could not immediately name. It took him until he was halfway across the reading floor to understand why.

There were no prayers here. No petitions pinned to doorposts, no small chalked invocations on lintels, none of the furtive little devotions that survived everywhere in Corvale like moss growing in the cracks of a wall the city kept promising to scrub clean. The Scriptorium was a building devoted to words, and it had scrubbed itself of the sacred entirely. Even the ledgers were secular: debts, debts, debts, column after column of human obligation, tallied with a precision no god had ever demanded.

"Magister Halev will see you in the second scriptorium," said the under-clerk, a young man with ink-stained cuffs and eyes that did not quite meet Marrec's. "He asked me to say—" the clerk hesitated, swallowed— "he asked me to say he expected you yesterday."

"Then we are both behind schedule," Marrec said, and followed him down the corridor.

* * *

Halev was not what Marrec had expected. He had built a picture on the walk over from the Office: the corrupt mentor, the kind of man who fattened himself on the desperation of debt-bound children, soft-bodied and evasive, sweating under questioning. The man who rose from his chair to greet him was instead thin as a lath, silver-haired, with a scholar's stoop and hands so ink-dark at the fingertips they looked stained to the bone. His eyes were clear. That was the worst part. Guilty men usually had shifty eyes; Halev looked at Marrec the way one scholar looks at another across a disputed text—with weary, level attention.

"Inquisitor Marrec." He gestured to the chair opposite. It was already pulled out, waiting. "I wondered how long the Office would take to find me."

"You knew I would come."

"I have known for seven years that someone would come eventually." Halev sat back down, folding his ruined hands on the desk between them. "I hoped it would be later. I hoped, frankly, that it would never happen. But hoping is a poor strategy. May I offer you tea? It is terrible tea. The Office requisitions the good leaves for itself, I assume."

Marrec did not sit. "You'll understand if I decline."

"I would think less of you if you accepted." Halev's mouth twitched—not quite a smile, but the ghost of where a smile had once lived. "Ask your questions, Inquisitor. I will answer what I can. You should know in advance that there are things I cannot answer, and things I will not, and that the difference between those two categories may matter more than either of us yet understands."

"The charm," Marrec said. "Sold three days ago in the Lamplighters' Market by a scribe named Wren Thornbury. A luck-charm. It worked."

"Yes," Halev said simply. "I heard. Word travels through the Scriptorium faster than through the streets. Debt-bound girls gossip worse than fishwives, and better than most historians."

"It worked," Marrec repeated, "which means either the girl committed high heresy with considerable skill, or something answered her that has not answered anyone in Corvale since the Sundering. Which is it?"

Halev was quiet for a long moment. Outside the narrow window, a cart rattled past on Bellows Street, and somewhere a child was singing a skipping rhyme—one of the old ones, Marrec noticed, the kind with a god's name worn smooth out of it, like a coin rubbed blank by too many thumbs.

"Do you know what I did before I became a Magister of Debts?" Halev asked at last. "Before I spent thirty years tallying what people owe?"

"You were a historian," Marrec said. "It's in your file. Ecclesiastical history, pre-Sundering period. You left the University under circumstances the file does not specify."

"Circumstances the file does not specify," Halev echoed softly. "Yes. That is one way to put it. I was a historian of the Godswar, Inquisitor. Do you know what that means? It means I read the primary sources. Not the Concordance. Not the Authorized Account that every schoolchild recites. The actual documents. Letters written during the war. Temple ledgers from before the temples burned. The covenant terms themselves—or what remained of them." He leaned forward. "And I can tell you, with a historian's certainty and an old man's weariness, that the account on which the entire ban rests—the account that justifies every pyre, every branding, every debt-brand like the one you're hunting—is a forgery."

The word landed in the room and seemed to change its weight. Marrec found, to his own surprise, that he had sat down without deciding to.

"That is a serious accusation."

"It is a serious fact." Halev rose, unhurried, and crossed to a shelf against the far wall. Marrec's hand drifted toward the cudgel at his belt—an old habit, though he had drawn it perhaps six times in eleven years—and Halev noticed, and paused, and said gently, "Inquisitor. If I intended to harm you, I have had seven years to arrange it, and I would not choose a method requiring me to reach a shelf first." He drew out a slim volume wrapped in oilcloth, and beneath it, a flat wooden box, and set both on the desk. "The forgery was commissioned. That is the part that took me years to accept. I kept looking for error—a copyist's slip, a dating mistake, some innocent explanation. There is none. The Authorized Account of the Godswar was written forty years after the war ended, by order of the first Conclave, and it was written backward from the conclusion they had already decided upon. They needed the gods to be the aggressors. They needed the war to be unambiguous. Because if it were ambiguous—if mortals bore any share of the blame—then the ban was not justice. It was amputation. And amputations require a surgeon's confidence, not a judge's honesty."

"What actually happened?" Marrec heard himself ask, and hated the hunger in his own voice.

Halev opened the box. Inside, on cloth gone brown with age, lay pages—loose leaves, brittle, covered in a hand so old the ink had gone the color of dried blood. "The gods broke faith first," Halev said. "That much of the Authorized Account is true. But what the Account omits—what it was commissioned to omit—is that the covenant contained a mortal veto. A term granting humanity the right to refuse any divine demand. The gods ignored it. Repeatedly. And when mortals invoked it at the council of Vaelmoor, the gods' answer was fire." He touched one page with a fingertip, feather-light. "But here is what the Conclave could not allow to be known: the veto was never formally dissolved. The Sundering broke the *door*, Inquisitor—the grove, the heart-tree, the channel between worlds. It did not break the *terms*. The covenant still stands, in law older than any law this city has written. Dormant. Waiting for an heir to invoke it."

"An heir," Marrec said. The word came out flat. "You mean Thornbury."

"I mean that Wren Thornbury's grandmother stood witness at Vaelmoor. I mean that blood remembers even when records burn." Halev closed the box. "And I mean that I sold that girl into debt seven years ago to keep her small, and hidden, and alive—and that every day since, I have told myself it was protection, and every day the telling has gotten harder to believe."

* * *

They talked until the lamp burned low. Or rather, Halev talked, and Marrec listened, and the listening was the hardest work he had ever done, because everything the old man said fit. That was the obscenity of it. Eleven years of service had taught Marrec the shape of lies—they creaked, they needed propping, they avoided corners. This did not creak. The forged Account explained the gaps he had always been taught not to see: why the pre-war temple records had all perished in conveniently identical fires; why the Conclave's first act after the Sundering was not reconstruction but the burning of the University's ecclesiastical collection; why the ban's penalties escalated not when magic grew dangerous but whenever anyone asked questions about origins. He had arrested a printer for that, once. A mild, myopic man who had published a pamphlet speculating about the Godswar's first causes. Marrec had felt uneasy about it even then, and had buried the unease under procedure, the way one buries anything.

"You must report this," Halev said, when the lamp finally guttered. "I want to be clear that I expect you to. I am not showing you these pages to convert you. I am showing them to you because the Office will find them regardless—you are not the only investigator working this thread, and mine is not the only name in their files—and because when they come, I would like there to have been at least one honest man who saw the evidence before it burned."

"Burned."

"They will destroy it, Inquisitor. Of course they will. What else can they do? The ban is not a policy; it is the foundation the whole city stands on. You do not repair a foundation by letting everyone look at the crack." Halev's voice was not bitter. That frightened Marrec more than bitterness would have. "You will be ordered to seal this room. Then you will be ordered to inventory the contents. Then the inventory will disappear into the Office's deep archive, and the contents will follow it, and in five years no one will remember there was ever a question. I have watched it happen twice before. Once to a colleague. Once to me."

"And you stayed anyway. Thirty years tallying debts in the house of the lie."

"I stayed," Halev said, "because the girl was here. Because a debt-brand is the best hiding place in Corvale—no one looks twice at the owned. And because someone had to be close enough to warn her when the walls started closing." He met Marrec's eyes. "Which they are doing now. So I will give you the warning I gave her, and you may make of it what your conscience allows: stop looking for Wren Thornbury. Every hour you spend on her trail shortens hers. There are men in the Office—I will not name them, you know their faces—who have already decided what she is, and what they have decided requires no trial."

* * *

The order came the next morning, and it came exactly as Halev had predicted, which was somehow worse than if it had surprised him.

Sealed by authority of the First Chair. All materials in the second scriptorium of the Scriptorium, Magister Halev's chambers, to be catalogued and transferred to Office custody. Transfer to be supervised personally by Inquisitor Dain Marrec. Disposition of materials: destruction by fire, certificate of burning to be countersigned.

*Destruction by fire.* Four words in a clerk's neat hand, and beneath them the countersignature line waiting for his name.

Marrec stood in the second scriptorium with the inventory list in his hand and the crates stacked around him—forty-one items, each numbered in his own writing, each a document that had survived a war and a cover-up and thirty years of an old man's guarded keeping, and every one of them now condemned by a signature he had not yet given but would give, because he was Dain Marrec, and in eleven years he had never once refused a lawful order, and the order was lawful, and that was precisely the problem, wasn't it, that the whole machinery of ash ran on lawful orders signed by decent men.

He thought of the mother in the Lamplighters' Market, the one whose daughter's fever had broken the night after she bought the luck-charm. He had spoken to her himself, gently, taking her statement. She had not defended the charm. She had only said, over and over, *it worked, Inquisitor, it worked,* with the bewildered joy of a woman who had prayed into silence for fifteen years and heard, at last, a footstep on the stair. He had written in his report: *subject displays no understanding of the danger.* He had meant it as mercy. Reading it back now, it sounded like something else. Like a man describing a bird as displaying no understanding of the cage.

He worked through the morning. Catalogued, crated, sealed. His hands were steady. He noted that his hands were steady and found no comfort in it.

Item thirty-seven was a single loose leaf, separated from the others—Halev had set it apart deliberately, Marrec realized, tucked inside a commentary on contract law where no censor would think to look. One page. The mortal veto, in the old hand, the clause underlined in a faded iron-gall ink: *...and no demand of the divine shall bind the mortal signatories without their freely given consent, this term being coequal with all others and dissolvable by none.*

Coequal with all others. Dissolvable by none.

Marrec stood holding it for a long time. Through the window he could see the smoke of the city rising straight into a windless sky, ten thousand hearths, ten thousand small lives, all of them standing on the foundation Halev had described—all of them safe, or sheltered, or smothered, depending on which word you chose, and he had chosen *safe* for eleven years because choosing otherwise required believing that everything he had built his life upon was mortar over a crack.

He was a good man. He had always believed that, and belief, he understood now, was its own kind of debt: you paid it down daily in small compliance, and you told yourself the balance was virtue.

He copied the page. It took him twenty minutes, working in his own hand, translating the old script faithfully, and then he folded the copy into the lining of his coat, where it lay against his chest like a coal that would not cool. The original went into crate thirty-seven with the rest. He sealed the crates. He signed the transfer order. He carried the certificate of burning back to the Office and countersigned it, and watched the pyre in the Office yard from his window that evening, and told himself—as the smoke went up straight into the darkening sky, joining the smoke of ten thousand honest hearths—that he had done his duty, and that duty was all he had, and that it was enough.

It was not enough. He had known it was not enough since the moment his pen finished copying the clause and he read it back and felt, for the first time in eleven years, the specific vertigo of a man standing on a floor that has begun, very quietly, to tilt.

That night he sat at his desk with a blank sheet of paper and wrote four lines, unsigned, in a hand not his own:

*The grove wakes. The heir lives. The history is false. Find me.*

He did not know yet whom he was writing to. He only knew that somewhere in Corvale a debt-bound girl was running, and that the men hunting her had already decided her ending, and that he held in his coat lining the one page in the world that might change it.

By dawn, the note was gone from his desk, passed along channels he would deny knowing, moving through the undercity like a spark down a fuse.
