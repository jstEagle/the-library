## 8. Road Through the Weeping Wood

They left Corvale before dawn, when the watchfires burned low and the lamplighters were still making their rounds with their long brass poles, snuffing the night's flames one by one. Wren kept to the shadowed side of Bellows Street with her hood up and her satchel clutched against her ribs, and Ashen-Vey walked beside her in his beggar's body—stooped, gray-bearded, unremarkable—though she could no longer quite see him that way. When he moved now, she caught glimpses of something else beneath the skin: a suggestion of vastness, like the shape of a mountain seen through fog.

"You keep looking at me," he said without turning his head.

"I'm trying to decide if I'm walking toward my destiny or my execution."

"Both, possibly." He smiled, and it was a terrible thing, that smile—it rearranged his whole face into something ancient and amused. "The grove will decide. I only know the road."

The road wound north and east out of the city gates, past the tanneries whose stink clung to the air for a mile, then through fields gone fallow since the Sundering. No one farmed this land anymore. The Inquisitors' Office had declared it blighted ground—a fiction, Wren understood now, but a useful one, keeping the curious away from what grew at the city's edge.

What grew there was the Weeping Wood.

She had seen it from the walls as a child, a dark smudge on the northern horizon that her grandmother had pointed to once, only once, and said: *That is where we made our promise, little wren. Remember it, even if you forget everything else.* She had forgotten nearly everything else. She had not forgotten that.

Now, seven years of careful smallness behind her, hunted by an Office that had already raided the shrine beneath Candlemaker's Row, she was walking toward the smudge on the horizon with an exiled god at her elbow, and the debt-brand under her sleeve had begun to itch in a way that had nothing to do with ink or iron.

* * *

By midmorning they reached the boundary stones.

They stood in a ragged line across the fields—waist-high pillars of black granite, each carved with a word in the old covenant-script that Wren could not read but somehow *understood*, the way you understand a mother's tone before her words. She stopped at the first stone and laid her palm against it, and the stone was warm.

"Stones shouldn't be warm," she said. "It's autumn."

"The grove remembers being loved," Ashen-Vey said. "Memory generates heat. You'll learn that."

She looked at him sharply. "You said the grove would test me. You didn't say how."

"I don't know how." For once, he sounded entirely honest, and it frightened her more than any lie might have. "I was cast down before the petrification. Whatever waits in there has been waiting longer than my exile. It may not remember me kindly."

"You're afraid," she said, wonder creeping into her voice despite herself.

"I am *cautious*," he corrected, with dignity. "There is a difference. Fear is for creatures who can be destroyed. I can only be diminished. But I have been diminished enough for one eternity, thank you."

Beyond the stones, the land changed. Grass gave way to a fine gray ash that had never been ash—the soil itself had turned to powder over the centuries, ground down by grief or by time, Wren couldn't tell which. And rising out of that pale ground stood the trees.

They were not trees anymore. They had been caught mid-motion, mid-*feeling*, when the Sundering froze them: trunks twisted like bodies in pain, branches flung upward in supplication or downward in despair. And from every trunk, from every frozen crevice of bark, sap wept in slow amber tears that fell to the roots and hardened there, so that each tree stood in a pool of its own solidified grief, glittering dully in the morning light.

Wren had expected desolation. What she felt instead was *recognition*—a deep, bodily knowing, as though she had walked these paths before, as though some ancestor's memory lived in her feet and knew the way between the trees.

"This way," she heard herself say, and Ashen-Vey raised his eyebrows but followed.

* * *

The visions began at the third turning.

One moment she was walking; the next, the gray world dissolved like sugar in water, and she stood in the same grove—but alive. Green canopy overhead, gold light falling through leaves, and the air so thick with presence that she could hardly breathe. Every tree hummed. Every blade of grass was attentive.

And there, at the heart of the grove, stood a woman with Wren's own dark hair and stubborn chin, kneeling before a tree so vast its trunk was a wall, so tall its crown vanished into sky. Her grandmother—but younger than any memory of her, younger than Wren herself, hands pressed flat against the living bark.

Around them stood others. Mortals in rough wool, farmers and smiths and midwives, faces worn with war. And facing them, arrayed in forms too bright to look upon directly, the gods.

Wren watched, unable to move, unable to speak, as her grandmother rose and spoke words in the old tongue—words Wren did not know and yet heard clearly, the meaning arriving directly in her blood:

*We give you worship freely given, and you give us your protection freely given. Neither shall compel the other. Neither shall deceive the other. This is the covenant: consent, witness, truth.*

A god stepped forward—one she somehow knew was Ashen-Vey, though he wore a body of storm-light and his voice was thunder given grammar—and spoke his half of the terms. And the mortals spoke theirs. And the great tree's sap ran silver instead of amber, and the covenant was sealed, and the crowd wept and sang, and Wren's young grandmother laughed with joy—

Then the vision *tore*.

The green world ripped like cloth, and beneath it Wren saw another scene, older, darker. Gods standing over a burning village—not punishing it, not cleansing it, simply *passing through*, indifferent as weather, while mortals screamed below. A god taking a mortal woman because he wanted her, and her family's prayers going up like smoke and changing nothing. Prayers offered in desperation, answered with silence—or worse, answered with *amusement*. She saw a child pray for rain and receive drought. She saw a town pray for mercy and receive a god's boredom.

*The gods broke faith first,* the vision said, without words. *Before the Godswar. Before the ban. Before any of it. They took the consent you gave and treated it as ownership.*

Wren came back to herself gasping, on her knees in the gray ash, Amber tears dripping somewhere nearby. Ashen-Vey crouched beside her, his hand hovering near her shoulder but not touching.

"What did you see?" he asked quietly.

She looked up at him—at the beggar's face, the patient eyes—and the question underneath the question was loud in her chest: *Did you see what I saw? Do you know what they did? What YOU did?*

"The covenant," she managed. "My grandmother sealing it. And then—" She stopped. Steadied. "Something else. Something about the gods breaking faith first."

Something flickered across his face—too fast to name, gone before she could catch it. "Visions are not history," he said carefully. "They are arguments. The grove is making a case, Wren. Be careful whose advocate you let it become."

"And which side are you arguing?"

He held out his hand and helped her up, and his grip was warm and steady and utterly unreadable. "I'm arguing for you," he said. "That should be enough."

It wasn't. But she let him pull her to her feet anyway, because the alternative was standing alone in a forest of petrified grief, and she was not yet brave enough for that.

* * *

They walked deeper. The trees grew larger, older, their weeping more profuse, until the pools of hardened sap merged into amber sheets that covered the ground like frozen lakes. Wren's footsteps left prints in the thin ash that dusted everything, and behind her, she noticed, the prints filled slowly with tiny beads of fresh sap, as though the wood were trying to heal the wound of her passing.

"It knows me," she whispered.

"It has been waiting for you," Ashen-Vey said. "Seven generations of Thornburys carried the heir's blood thinner and thinner. Yours is the last full measure. The grove has been holding its breath since your grandmother died."

At the fifth turning, the second vision took her.

This time she did not kneel; the vision simply replaced the world, seamless and total. She stood in the grove again, alive and green, but the light was wrong—bruised, storm-colored. Her grandmother was older now, gray-streaked, standing before the heart-tree with her arms spread wide as if to shield it. And around her, the gods were *arguing*.

She heard fragments. *They have forgotten us—worship is fading—we are owed—* And another voice, hers, sharp with fury: *Owed? They gave freely! That was the covenant! You do not collect a gift!*

And then a god's voice, vast and cold: *The covenant binds us to their need. Their need grows tedious. We will take what sustains us.*

*You swore consent—*

*Consent was given. It does not expire, but neither does it constrain forever. We are gods, woman. We do not ask twice.*

And the heart-tree's sap ran from silver back to amber, tear by tear, as the gods turned from the grove and went out into the world to take what they had decided was theirs—and mortals, who had once prayed freely, learned to pray in terror, and then learned to stop praying at all, and the Godswar began not as a war of faith but as a war of *breach*.

The vision released her harder this time. She stumbled against a trunk and the bark bit her palm, and when she looked down, her blood welled bright against the gray wood—and the tree *shuddered*. A long, slow tremor ran up through its petrified limbs, and from high above, a single branch cracked and fell, landing in the ash at her feet. It was covered in buds. Tiny, impossible, green buds, on a tree dead for two hundred years.

Ashen-Vey stared at the branch. Then at her hand. Then at her.

"Do you understand what you are?" he asked softly.

"A scribe," she said. "A debt-bound charm-seller who is in very deep trouble."

"Wren." He took her bleeding hand gently, turned it over, examined the cut with an attention that felt almost devotional. "I have watched empires rise and fall. I have watched mountains wear down to sand. I have waited two hundred years in a beggar's body for someone whose blood could make dead wood dream of spring." His thumb moved across her palm, and the cut closed—not healed exactly, but *answered*, the skin knitting as though the wound had been a prayer and he the answer. "Do not make yourself small. Not here. Not ever again."

She pulled her hand back, heart hammering—not from fear, or not only from fear. From the seduction of it. That was the danger she could feel now, clear as cold water: how easy it would be to believe him. How easy it would be to let this ancient, patient, wounded creature tell her who she was, and to sink into that identity like a warm bath, and never ask whether he wanted her *whole* or merely *useful*.

"My grandmother's visions showed me the gods breaking faith first," she said. "Were you among them?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere above, sap dripped onto stone with the regularity of a heartbeat.

"I argued for mercy," he said at last. "When the others chose taking. I argued, and I was cast down for it, and I have crawled through your city's gutters for two centuries watching mortals suffer under a ban built on the wreckage of a broken promise." He met her eyes. "That is true. All of it is true. And it is also not all of the truth, and you deserve to know that much honesty from me, at least."

"What's the rest?"

"Not here." He glanced up at the towering trees, and for the first time since she'd known him, he looked genuinely uneasy. "The grove is listening. And there are things I will say to you, Wren Thornbury, that I will not say in front of witnesses—even wooden ones."

* * *

They reached the heart-tree at dusk.

It dwarfed every other tree in the grove, a petrified colossus wider than a chapel, its trunk seamed with channels where the sap had wept for two centuries until the tears had carved grooves like the lines of a face. And it did look like a face—grief-stricken, eyes closed, mouth open in a soundless cry that had been crying since the day the covenant died.

Wren approached it the way she imagined one approaches a sleeping bear, or a grave, or a god. Slowly. With her palms open.

When she laid both hands against the bark, the warmth of it nearly made her weep. Beneath the stone-cold surface, faint as a pulse in a wrist, something still beat.

"Grandmother," she whispered. "I found it. I'm sorry it took so long."

The vision that came this time was small. Quiet. Just her grandmother, old again, sitting on a bench beneath the heart-tree in the last green summer, holding a small brass token stamped with a wren in flight. She looked directly at Wren—directly at *her*, across two hundred years, as though she could see her—and she said:

*They will tell you the covenant broke because mortals rebelled. They will tell you the gods grew cruel because mortals grew faithless. Both lies serve the same master, little wren: the lie that power answers to no one. Remember the veto. The terms named a mortal veto. Find the original terms. Promise me.*

Then the vision folded shut like a book, and Wren was alone with the tree and the dusk and the man-shaped exile standing behind her.

"The terms," she repeated aloud, testing the weight of the words. "The original covenant terms. Named a mortal veto."

Ashen-Vey had gone very still. Stillness, she was learning, was what he did when the truth got close.

"Where are they?" she asked, turning to face him. "Who holds them?"

"That," said Ashen-Vey, "is a longer story. And we should not be standing in the open when I tell it." He nodded northward, toward the darkening horizon beyond the grove. "Because we are not alone, Wren. We haven't been since the boundary stones. Someone else has come to the Weeping Wood tonight—and they did not come to weep."

Far off, down the winding road they had traveled, a pinpoint of torchlight flickered among the petrified trees. Then another. Then a dozen, moving with the disciplined spacing of trained soldiers.

Inquisitors.

And in her pocket, the covenant-token her grandmother had shown her in the vision weighed suddenly heavy as a sin—as though it had always been there, waiting, and she had only just now thought to reach for it.
