## 9. Terms of the Broken Covenant

Gods do not sleep. This was the first mercy of exile and the cruelest of them all: that Ashen-Vey lay awake through every night of the girl's dreaming, listening to her breath slow and quicken, feeling the small heat of her mortal body radiate through the thin blanket like a hearth he was forbidden to touch.

They had made camp in the hollow beneath the aqueduct where the smugglers' path bent toward the Weeping Wood. Wren slept curled against her satchel, the covenant-token clutched in one fist even now, even unconscious, as though she feared someone might take it from her in the dark. She had held it that way since the elder on Bellows Street pressed it into her palm. *Thornbury*, the old woman had said, and wept, and something in Wren had changed shape—something Ashen-Vey had been waiting seven years to see.

He sat with his back against the aqueduct's stone leg, wearing the beggar's body he had worn for a century, and he watched her, and he told himself the watching was tenderness.

It was not entirely a lie.

* * *

He had argued for mercy. That much was true, and it was the only coin he had left to spend honestly, so he spent it carefully, rationed across their days of travel, letting her believe she was prying secrets out of him one at a time when in truth he had chosen each secret before she asked.

The Godswar had lasted eleven years. Mortals remembered it as a storm; the histories called it a Sundering, as though the sky had simply cracked of its own accord, as though no hands had held the hammer. Ashen-Vey remembered it as a council chamber. He remembered standing among his kin—the bright ones, the thunderous ones, Vaelith with her crown of drowned stars, Orren-Kai whose voice could unmake walls—and he remembered saying, in the old tongue that no living throat could pronounce anymore:

*They are not ours to burn.*

Eleven years of war, and near its end, when the gods stood victorious over the smoking fields of the mortal kingdoms, he had said it. *We have taken enough. Let what remains live. Bind us if you must—bind ourselves, by our own hand, and let the covenant hold.*

Vaelith had laughed at him. That was the part he never told Wren, the part that curdled the memory even now: not that they refused mercy, but that they found it funny. A god pleading for insects. Orren-Kai had put a hand on his shoulder, almost gentle, and said, *Little ash, you love them too much to rule them,* and the word *love* had been an insult in his mouth, a disease, a leash.

So they had broken faith instead. The covenant had named terms—terms written in the heart-tree's sap by a mortal woman with Thornbury blood, terms that gave mortals a veto over divine will, a voice, a door that swung both ways—and the gods had signed and then ignored every clause that constrained them. They took worship without consent. They answered prayers selectively, cruelly, making examples of doubters. And when the mortals' greatest seers finally read the covenant aloud and demanded the gods honor it, the gods burned the seers and called it justice.

The Sundering was not mortals casting gods down. It was mortals, desperate, sealing the door against things that would not keep their word.

Ashen-Vey had been cast down afterward—not for the war, but for what he did next. When his kin fled beyond the veil, he stayed. He hid in a beggar's stolen flesh and waited a hundred years for a door to reopen, because he believed—he still believed—that the covenant could be reforged rightly this time, with him inside it rather than outside. With him holding the pen.

Or holding the hand that held the pen.

* * *

Wren woke before dawn, as debt-bound people do, her body keeping the hours of a market stall even here in the wild dark. She sat up, pushed hair from her face, and looked at him sitting motionless against the stone, and her expression did the thing it always did in the first moments of waking: softened, then armored. He had learned to count the seconds between those two faces. Four, usually. Sometimes five when she dreamed well.

"You don't sleep," she said. Not a question. She'd stopped asking questions about him somewhere outside the city and started making observations instead, which was either trust or strategy, and he genuinely could not tell which, and the not-knowing was the closest thing to joy he had felt in a century.

"I watch," he said. "It's nearly the same thing."

"That's a horrible thing to say."

"It's an honest thing to say. You'll find I'm mostly honest in ways that horrify." He rose, joints cracking in the borrowed body—another small cruelty of exile, that he had chosen a form that ached. "There's water in the cistern channel. Drink before we walk. The grove tests the thirsty first."

She went to the water, and he watched her cup her hands, and he arranged his face into the mild benevolence she had come to expect, and underneath the arrangement he thought: *I need your body to hold my power, and you do not know yet, and every hour you do not know is a theft.*

* * *

He told her the first truth on the road, because truths given freely buy more than truths pried loose, and because he had learned over a hundred years of beggary that the fastest way into a mortal's heart is to let them rescue you a little.

"The day they cast me down," he said, walking with his hands clasped behind his back like a scholar on a garden path, "was not a battle. There were no fires. That's what no one remembers about falling—it's quiet. Vaelith came to me where I hid in the temple of a dead river-god, and she didn't raise her hand. She just spoke my name backward."

"Backward?"

"A name spoken forward is a thing that holds together. Spoken backward, it comes apart. I felt myself come apart. Everything I was—my domains, my works, the prayers I'd ever answered—all of it poured out of me like water through fingers. What was left was—" he gestured at himself, the gray skin, the stoop, the eyes gone the color of wood ash, "—this. A vessel with nothing in it. Exile isn't being sent away, Wren. It's being emptied and then locked out of the room where they keep the filling."

She walked beside him in silence for a long while. The road climbed through gorse and old field-stone, and ahead, past the last farms, the land went strange and pale where the Weeping Wood began—trees visible even from here, white as bone, their branches hung with hardened ribbons of sap that caught the morning light like glass.

"And the mercy?" she said finally. "You said you argued for it. Argued for *us*."

"I did." He let the weariness into his voice, genuine weariness, a century deep. "Near the end of the war. When the fields were burning and your grandmothers' grandmothers were dying in ditches. I stood in front of the assembled host and I said: enough. They are not ours to burn. And they laughed at me, little scribe. Not angrily. *Amusedly.* Do you understand what that does to you, to be laughed at by your own kind for loving something? I have been empty for a hundred years, but that laughter emptied me first."

He heard her breath change. There it was. Pity was such an honest door; mortals opened it themselves, from the inside, and then were surprised to find you standing in the room.

"And the others," she said. "The ones who laughed. Where are they now?"

"Beyond the veil. Waiting. Hating." He turned to look at her fully, and he let her see the ash-colored eyes, let her see how old they were. "And they are coming back, Wren. Your blood opened the way. Every day the door stands open a little wider, and when it is wide enough, they will pour through, and they remember the veto your grandmother wrote, and they intend to make certain no mortal ever reads it again."

* * *

What he did not tell her, on the road, in the shadow of the pale trees, was the rest.

That a god returned through a mortal door arrives diminished—a candle flame carried in a wind. That the vessel must be more than a door. That it must be an *anchor*, a mortal life wrapped around divine fire the way a lantern wraps around flame, holding it steady, feeding it, containing it. That the heir of the Weeping Wood was not merely the key to the covenant but the intended wick of it.

That when the reforging came, and the power of the old covenant flooded back into the world, it would need somewhere to live—and he had spent a hundred years ensuring that somewhere would be a girl with Thornbury blood whom he would find, guide, befriend, and lead to the heart-tree with her eyes full of trust.

He did not tell her that he had chosen her before she was born. That the prayer he had answered in her shop—the first prayer any god had answered in seven years—had not been an accident of proximity but the opening move of a game begun generations ago. That her grandmother's token, her debt, her loneliness, her careful smallness: all of it was soil he had helped till.

He did not tell her because she was not ready. That was what he told himself. *She is not ready.* The words had a comfortable weight, and he set them down inside himself each time the other thought stirred—the truer one, the uglier one, the one shaped like *she would refuse*.

Because she might. That was the unbearable arithmetic of it. He had watched her for seven years through a beggar's eyes, from the corners of markets, from the steps of the Scriptorium. He had seen her sell luck-charms she barely believed in to people who needed them, and pocket only what the debt required, and give the rest away in small unrecorded kindnesses. He had seen her hide her brand beneath her sleeve not out of shame but out of a fierce, private courtesy—as though her suffering were a mess she refused to track onto other people's floors. He had seen her whisper a stranger's prayer over her counter after closing, unpaid, unwitnessed, because no one else would say it.

A vessel like that. An anchor like that. There had never been another. There would never be another. And if he told her the whole of the terms—if he said *I need your body, your life, your self, to hold what I am*—she would look at him with those four-seconds-softening eyes, and she would do the arithmetic herself, and she would know that everything tender between them had been installed like scaffolding around a building meant to bear a weight she'd never agreed to carry.

Love as leverage. His kin would have approved. That was the worst of it. Every manipulation he performed upon her, he performed with tools forged in the council chamber where they had laughed at him. He had become fluent in their language while hating it, and fluency does not care what you hate.

* * *

They reached the edge of the Wood at dusk.

Up close, the trees were worse than pale—they were *grieving*. The petrified trunks gleamed with fresh sap that welled from a thousand seams and ran down the bark in slow ropes, hardening mid-fall, so that each tree wore centuries of tears frozen on its face. The air smelled of resin and something older underneath, something like incense burned in a closed room for a very long time. No birds. No wind moved the branches, and still, faintly, impossibly, they dripped.

Wren stopped at the treeline and put her hand flat against the nearest trunk, and the sap welled up between her fingers, warm, and she snatched her hand back and stared at it, and then—because she was who she was—she touched the tree again, deliberately, and held it there.

"It knows me," she whispered.

"It has been waiting for you," Ashen-Vey said, "since before your grandmother was born."

And that was true too. That was the maddening architecture of his honesty: almost everything he told her was true. Only the frame was false. Only the reason he stood beside her, only the patient hunger behind his gentleness, only the fact that when he looked at her he saw not a person but a *doorway*, and loved the doorway, and could no longer separate the two loves even from himself.

She turned to him in the failing light, sap shining on her palm, and her face had gone soft all the way through—no armor at all, five seconds and counting—and she said, "Whatever the grove shows me in there. Whatever my grandmother did, or didn't do. Will you tell me the truth? All of it? Even the parts that make you look bad?"

The question hung between them like the suspended sap.

"Yes," said Ashen-Vey, who had once argued for mercy and been laughed at, and who heard, in that moment, very faintly, very far away, his own name beginning to laugh.

Behind them, down the dark road toward Corvale, a glow was rising—not dawn, not lamplight. Torchlight, many torches, moving in formation. And threaded through the wind that had finally begun to blow came a sound that made the beggar's stolen body go rigid: the measured, carrying cadence of an Inquisitor's proclamation, read aloud to the countryside, naming crimes, naming names.

Naming, at length and with terrible precision, a debt-bound scribe called Wren Thornbury.
