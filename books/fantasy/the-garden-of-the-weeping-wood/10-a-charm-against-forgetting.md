## 10. A Charm Against Forgetting

The road back into Corvale smelled of rain and tallow, and Wren walked it like a stranger wearing her own face.

Three days in the undercity had changed the way she saw everything. The bellows-street warrens still echoed behind her eyes—the candlelit congregation singing hymns with their mouths barely open, the elder Marta pressing her grandmother's covenant-token into her palm with hands that shook from age and something older than age. *Thornbury,* the old woman had said, and the name had gone through Wren like a nail through wet wood. She had carried that name carefully for seven years, folded small and hidden beneath her sleeve along with her debt-brand, and now strangers were saying it aloud in the dark as if it were a key.

She came up through the tannery gate at dusk, hood drawn, and the city received her without ceremony. Corvale did not care who you were. Corvale cared what you owed. That was the bargain she had lived inside since she was nineteen years old: be small, be useful, be invisible. Sell charms that did nothing. Charge little. Keep your head down and your ledger clean.

But the ledger was not clean anymore. Her debt had been bought by a stranger's seal. Inquisitor Marrec was pulling threads somewhere in the Scriptorium's records. And a beggar with ash-grey eyes had told her she was the last heir of the Weeping Wood, and the terrifying thing—the thing that kept her awake on Marta's floor with the token warm against her sternum—was that she had believed him. Not because he was convincing. Because some door inside her had already been open, creaking on its hinges, waiting years for someone to say its name.

*Gather them,* Ashen-Vey had said, when she'd told him where she meant to go first. He had worn his beggar's body slumped against a wall, watching her with that patient, terrible attention. *The faithful under Bellows Street. They have prayed to silence for a generation. Silence is a kind of soil, little scribe. Things grow in it.*

*And if I'm caught?*

*Then you will learn what your grandmother learned.* He had smiled, and it had not been comforting. *That faith is cheap until it costs.*

Now, moving through the evening market toward the mouth of Bellows Street, Wren touched the token through her shirt. It was a small disc of heart-tree sapwood, amber-pale, shot through with veins of gold that might have been sap or might have been light. Her grandmother had held this. Her grandmother had stood in the grove when gods and mortals still spoke face to face, and had helped seal whatever covenant the Sundering had broken—and the histories said the mortals broke it, but Wren no longer trusted the histories. She was a scribe. She knew how easily ink lied.

Bellows Street was quiet in the way that made her stomach drop before her mind caught up.

She knew every rhythm of this district. She had sold charms here for seven years; she knew which doorways leaked lamplight, which stairwells harbored lovers and which harbored knives, what hour the fishwives quarreled and what hour the watch made its rounds. And the rhythm was wrong. No lamplight leaked from the tannery stairwell. No smell of supper from the widow Osk's window. The street held its breath, and streets only held their breath around soldiers.

She found the first mark of them at the warrens' entrance: a boot-print in the tallow-drippings, military-issue, deep-heeled. Then a smear of candle wax, still soft. Then—scratched hastily into the doorpost of the smuggler's false-front shop, in the old pre-Sundering script that the congregation used for their hymnals—a single word.

*Fled.*

Wren stood in the dark doorway and felt the word land in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. Fled. Or taken. There were two ways to read it, and only one of them let her keep breathing.

She should run. Every instinct honed across seven careful years screamed it—run back to Ashen-Vey, run to the grove, run anywhere the Inquisitors' writ grew thin. But underneath the fear, another voice had been growing louder since the night a prayer over her counter had been answered aloud, since the charm had worked, since the old power had stirred in her blood like a sleeper turning over. That voice said: *they prayed to silence for a generation, and the silence finally answered, and the answer is you, and you are standing in a doorway doing arithmetic with your own safety while they are in chains.*

She went down into the warrens anyway.

* * *

They had not even bothered to hide what they'd done. That was the worst of it. The Inquisitors' Office had grown bold—or desperate—and desperation in an office built on fear looks exactly like boldness from the outside.

The congregation had been dragged out through the tannery yard and held there in the rain, twenty-three souls, kneeling in the mud with their hands bound behind them. Wren saw them from the shadow of the drying-sheds: Marta, the elder, her white hair plastered to her skull, refusing the shoulder of the soldier trying to help her kneel. The cooper Tam and his wife, whose hymn-voice Wren had heard shaking the low ceiling three nights ago. A boy of perhaps twelve, weeping without sound, the way children learn to weep in a city where magic was outlawed after the Sundering.

And before them, reading charges by lantern-light, stood an Inquisitor Wren did not know—young, precise, his grey coat dry above the mud while everyone else soaked. His voice carried the flat clarity of a man reciting words he had not written.

"—by order of the Office of Inquiry, gathered worship of the banned powers, possession of unlicensed texts, conspiracy to—" He paused. Turned a page. The rain ticked against the lantern's glass. "Conspiracy to awaken."

The word fell into the yard and lay there.

*Awaken.* As if the gods were sleeping dogs and these kneeling, mud-soaked people were conspirators slipping meat under the fence. As if prayer were contraband. Wren pressed herself against the shed wall, rain running cold down her neck, and understood with sudden terrible clarity that this was what the ban had always been—not law protecting the city from divine fire, but law protecting certain men from having to share the world.

A wagon stood at the yard's gate. Not a prisoner wagon. A pyre-wagon, stacked with split pine and pitch-barrels, and the sight of it did something to Wren that seven years of hiding had never done.

It did not make her afraid.

It made her angry.

The anger rose in her like water finding a crack—no, like sap rising, like the slow gold light she had seen threading through her grandmother's token. She thought of Halev, her mentor, who had sold her into debt to protect her and then sold her location to buy time, and how both betrayals had been made of love and cowardice in equal measure, and how the system that shaped him had never once asked him to be brave. She thought of the mother in the market, weeping over a luck-charm that worked. She thought of the boy kneeling in the mud, weeping without sound because sound was dangerous.

*No more,* said something in her, in a voice that was hers and also much older than hers. *No more hiding. No more smallness. No more arithmetic.*

The Inquisitor raised his hand for the wagon to come forward.

Wren stepped out of the shadows.

Later—much later, in the years when children would ask her about this night and she would give them different answers each time because the truth refused to sit still in memory—she would try to describe what happened next, and fail. Here is the closest she ever came:

*I didn't cast anything. Casting implies intention, shape, a craftsman's hand. What I did was stop holding the door shut.*

The token burned against her sternum like a second heart. The rain stopped falling—not drifted, not thinned, *stopped*, each drop hanging in the air around the yard like beads on black string, and in the lantern-light every suspended drop lit up gold, so that the whole muddy yard hung inside a sphere of falling stars. The soldiers cried out. The horses at the gate screamed and fought their harness. The Inquisitor's page of charges lifted from his hand and turned slowly in the air, ink bleeding upward off the vellum like smoke returning to a fire.

And Wren Thornbury, debt-bound scribe, last heir of the Weeping Wood, walked through the hanging rain with her hood back and her brand bare—she had torn her sleeve back without deciding to, as if her own arm had voted—and spoke words she did not know she knew, in the old tongue of the grove, and the bindings on twenty-three pairs of wrists came apart like knots in rope left too long in water.

Marta looked up at her from the mud. The old woman's face was streaming—rain, tears, impossible to tell—and she did not look surprised. She looked like someone greeting a letter long delayed.

"The Thornbury girl," Marta whispered. "The garden sent its gardener."

"I don't know what I'm doing," Wren said, and her voice cracked, and the hanging rain trembled with it, every golden drop shivering. "I don't—I need everyone to get up. Can you get up? We have to go, all of you, now—"

The Inquisitor moved. To his credit—he was young, and young men in grey coats are not trained for this—he drew no steel. He drew instead a slim iron rod from his coat, a nullifier, standard issue, the tool that ended the Godswar's last battles, and leveled it at her with hands that were not quite steady.

"In the name of the Office," he said, "stand down."

And Wren, who had spent seven years being small, who had spent seven years selling harmless charms and keeping her ledger clean and folding her name up small enough to hide beneath her sleeve, looked at him and said, in a voice that was not loud but reached every corner of the yard as if the yard itself were listening:

"No."

The nullifier sparked once against the field of hanging gold and guttered out like a candle in a gale. The Inquisitor stared at the dead iron in his hand, and then at her, and Wren saw something pass across his young face that she would think about for a long time afterward—not hatred, not fear exactly, but recognition. The look of a man seeing, for the first time, that the wall he guards has a door in it.

Then the world remembered how to fall. The rain came down all at once, a cold blessing, and the yard erupted into motion—soldiers shouting, prisoners stumbling upright, Tam hauling Marta to her feet, the silent boy grabbing Wren's hand with fingers slick with mud and terror and hope.

"Through the tannery," Marta gasped. "There's a gate the watch doesn't know. Child, can you hold whatever that was?"

"No," Wren said honestly. "But I can do it again if I have to."

It was a lie, and everyone present probably knew it, and it worked anyway. They went through the tannery in a flood of bodies and rain, past the lime-pits breathing their chemical fog, out through a gate hidden behind a wall of hides, into the labyrinth of the undercity where the Inquisitors' writ ran thin and the smugglers' tolls ran thick. Behind them, the pyre-wagon sat unlit in the yard, and one soldier—only one, a woman with rain running down her face—did not raise the alarm. She stood very still and watched them go, and then she looked down at her own hands as if checking whether they belonged to her.

* * *

By morning, Corvale was burning in the only way that mattered.

Not with fire—with talk. Talk moves faster than any flame and leaves deeper scars. By the time the fishwives opened their stalls, the story had already changed hands forty times and grown a new limb with each telling: a scribe-girl in Bellows Street who stopped the rain in midair. Who unbound twenty-three prisoners with a word. Whose skin glowed gold beneath the lantern-light—or whose eyes were silver, depending on the teller—or who was escorted by a grey-eyed beggar whom no blade could touch, depending on how much wine the teller had drunk.

In the tannery district, a chandler's apprentice swore on his mother's grave that he'd seen it himself. In the Scriptorium, copyists whispered over their desks until Magister Halev—grey-faced, hollow-eyed, dying by inches of the price he'd paid for his bargains—told them all to get back to work in a voice that shook. In the Inquisitors' Office, a report crossed a desk marked with the highest priority seal the Office possessed, and somewhere in that building a lamp burned all night as men who had spent thirty years insisting the gods were dead confronted the possibility that dead things do not stop rain in midair.

And in the market, in the stalls, in the queues at the wells and the bread-lines, ordinary people looked at one another differently. A woman touched the luck-charm at her throat and wondered. A man who had muttered a prayer over his sick daughter for a year, quietly, shamefully, the way one commits a small crime—went home and knelt by her bed and prayed *aloud*, and the room felt different afterward, and he could not say why, and he did it again the next night.

Rumor spread like fire through dry grass, and like fire it could not be called back, and like fire it gave light and took fuel.

Wren heard fragments of it all through the day as the congregation scattered into safe houses across the undercity—Tam's cousins, a shrine-keeper's widow, a smuggler who owed Marta a life-debt and grumbled about it while making up beds. She sat by a window in a safe house above the Chandler's Walk, exhausted down to the marrow, her hands still trembling faintly, and listened to the city say her miracle back to her in a dozen distorted tongues.

"They're calling you the Rain-Maiden," said the silent boy, who was not silent anymore, whose name was Pip and who had attached himself to Wren's side with the immovable devotion of a burr. He was eating bread with the focus of the truly starving. "Or the Gold-Blooded. One man says you're the ghost of the Sundering come back for revenge."

"I'm a scribe," Wren said faintly.

"You stopped the rain."

"I panicked in several directions simultaneously."

Pip grinned around his bread, and despite everything—despite the pyre-wagon, despite the young Inquisitor's dead nullifier, despite the fact that she had torn away seven years of careful invisibility in the space of ten minutes in a muddy yard—despite all of it, Wren laughed. It came out ragged and half-hysterical, but it was a laugh, and Marta, dozing in her chair by the hearth, smiled without opening her eyes.

The exhilaration lasted until full dark. Then the terror arrived to collect its customary tax.

She sat alone at the window while the house slept, turning her grandmother's token over in her fingers, watching the gold veins catch the lamplight. Somewhere out there, the Inquisitors' Office was deciding what to do about her. Somewhere out there, Ashen-Vey waited at the edge of the ruined shrine, patient as rot, and she could no longer fully separate his tenderness from his purposes. Somewhere out there, Halev was dying, and she had not forgiven him yet, and did not know if she could.

And somewhere out there, the pyre-wagons were being loaded again. She could feel it the way she now felt the old power—in her teeth, in the soles of her feet, in the scar-tissue of the brand beneath her sleeve. The Office would not send one Inquisitor and one wagon next time. They would send the thing they had been saving for thirty years, the solution they had built into the city's foundations: if the gods wake, burn the grove. Burn the Weeping Wood, and burn whatever heir stands in it.

"They'll come for the grove," she said aloud to the empty room, testing the truth of it in her mouth. "They'll burn it rather than let us reach it."

The token pulsed once against her palm—warm, unmistakable, deliberate—and outside the window, far off toward the city's eastern edge where the petrified trees stood weeping their slow sap-tears into the dark, lightning flickered though there were no clouds, and no thunder followed.

Something in the grove had heard her.

And something in the city had heard her too: boots on the stairs below, many boots, moving fast—but the knock, when it came, was not the Office's heavy fist. It was three soft raps, a pause, two more. The smuggler's signal. Wren opened the door on a breathless courier girl, rain-soaked, eyes wide with the particular terror of someone carrying news too large for her body.

"You're the Thornbury woman." It wasn't a question. "You have to come. There's a man dying in the Scriptorium infirmary—an old scribe—and he's paid everything he has to get a message to you, and he says—" the girl swallowed— "he says to tell you he's kept the original terms. All these years. He says you'll know what that means, and he says the Inquisitors are marching for the grove at dawn."

Wren closed her hand around the token until the gold veins bit into her palm.

Halev.

Of course. Of course it was Halev—dying, bargaining, loving and betraying to the last, and keeping, all this time, the one document that could undo everything.

She reached for her cloak.

"No more hiding," she said—to Pip, to the courier, to the trembling house, to herself—and went down into the waking dark of Corvale, toward the Scriptorium, toward the dawn, toward the grove.
