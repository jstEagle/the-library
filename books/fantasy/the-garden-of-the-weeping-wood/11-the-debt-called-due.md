## 11. The Debt Called Due

The message came by way of a child with soot on her knuckles and a coin pressed into her palm too large for any errand she'd have named. *The Magister asks for you. The Magister is dying.* Wren read the scrap of paper twice, as though repetition might change the words, might make them say anything other than what they said.

*Come before the debt is called due. Come before I am called due.*

Halev's handwriting had always been beautiful—the kind of beauty that came from a lifetime of being paid to make things legible. Even now, even in what was clearly a scrawl dashed off between spasms of pain, there was craft in it. Wren hated that she noticed. She hated more that her chest tightened.

She should not go. Every instinct honed over seven years of careful smallness told her not to go. Halev had sold her location to the Inquisitors—had traded her, again, the way he'd traded her at nineteen when he'd signed her into debt-bond and called it protection. Ashen-Vey had warned her, in his patient, terrible way: *The man who sold you once will sell you twice. It is not malice. It is arithmetic. Men like him always believe the sum justifies itself.*

But Ashen-Vey was not here. Ashen-Vey was somewhere beneath the city, or above it, or threaded through it like smoke through a keyhole, and Wren was standing in a rented room on Bellows Street holding a dying man's handwriting, feeling the old pull she had never managed to cut out of herself: *he raised me, he taught me my letters, he taught me which prayers were safe.*

She went.

* * *

The Scriptorium at night was a body without a heartbeat. Wren remembered it differently from her childhood—remembered lamplight and the rustle of vellum, Magister Halev's voice rolling through the copying hall like a tide, correcting a novice's hand with something close to tenderness. Now the lamps were unlit. The desks stood empty, their inkwells capped, their quills laid neatly across their rests like sleeping birds. Debt-bonds hung in their thousands from the archive walls, each one a life reduced to a ledger line, and in the dark they looked like what they were: a forest of paper ghosts.

She found him in his private scriptorium, the small room behind the great one where he had taught her to cut a nib without wasting the feather. He was propped in his chair beneath a single candle, and the sight of him stopped her in the doorway with her hand still on the latch.

Magister Edran Halev had been a mountain of a man once—not tall, but dense, immovable, a man whose disapproval could hold a door shut. What sat in the chair now was a ruin wearing his robes. His skin had gone the grey of old ash, stretched over cheekbones that jutted like shipwreck timbers. His hands lay in his lap, and Wren saw with a lurch that the debt-brand on his own wrist—the mark every bonded scribe carried, the mark she had spent seven years hiding under her sleeve—had spread. It crawled up his forearm in black filigree now, past the elbow, threading toward his shoulder like frost climbing a windowpane, and where it touched skin the skin had died.

*The price of his bargains,* she thought. *Paid in full, at last, in the only currency he ever truly had.*

"You came." His voice was a whisper with gravel in it. "I told them you wouldn't. I won the wager."

"There were wagers?"

"There are always wagers." He tried to smile; it cost him visibly. "Sit down, child. No—don't sit. Stand there where I can see you. I've spent seven years looking at your ledger entries. Let me look at the actual article."

"I'm not an article."

"No," he said softly. "You never were. That was rather the whole of my sin, wasn't it? Treating you as one anyway."

Wren stayed by the door. The candle guttered, steadied. Somewhere in the great dark hall beyond, a loose shutter knocked against its frame, patient as a clock.

"Ashen-Vey says you sold me to the Inquisitors," she said. "My location. After the shrine raid. Say it isn't true, and I'll—" She didn't know what she'd do. That was the worst of it. Seven years of grievance and she still didn't know what she'd do if he denied it.

"It's true." He said it simply, without flinching, and somehow that was worse than any denial. "I gave Marrec's runners the Bellows Street contact. Not your name—I kept your name out of it as long as I could—but enough. They were closing on the congregation, Wren. Forty souls in those warrens, and the Office had a thread, and the thread led through me. I gave them a smaller truth to buy time for a larger lie of safety. It's what I do. It's all I've ever done." His breath caught, held, released. "It's why I'm dying in this chair instead of on a pyre in the plaza. The bargains come due. The brand collects."

"Then you'll forgive me if I don't weep over the interest."

"Gods, no." And there, briefly, was the old Halev, the dry flash of him that had made novices both love and dread him. "Weep for yourself, if you must weep. Weep for the forty in chains. Don't waste it on me. I bought nothing worth the tears."

"Why?" The word came out of her harder than she meant, seven years of it compressed into a single syllable. "Why did you sign me over in the first place? You told me it was to protect me. You let me believe—I spent *seven years* believing I was paying for my mother's debts, that this was arithmetic, that someone, somewhere, had done the sums honestly—"

"Your mother owed nothing." He closed his eyes. "No one owed anything. There was no debt, Wren. There never was a debt."

The room went very quiet. The candle flame stood perfectly still, as if listening.

"The Sundering laws required that anyone with god-touched blood be registered and bound," Halev said, each word dragged up from somewhere deep. "Registered, branded, and placed under a bonded master who would answer for their magic with their own freedom. Your grandmother understood what that meant. She'd seen what the Office did with registered children. So she hid you, and she hid your line, and when the census came to Thornbury's Row, I stood in the doorway and I lied. I said the household was clean. And then the audit came, and the lie would not hold, and so I did the only thing left that the law would accept." He opened his eyes, and they were wet and steady. "I invented a debt. I signed you into bond against a sum that never existed, because a bonded scribe with a fictional creditor was invisible in a way a registered god-heir would never be. The debt was the shield. I was the shield. And I told myself, for twenty-six years, that this made it righteous."

Wren's legs would not hold her rage and her grief at once, so she leaned against the doorframe and let them take turns.

"You let me scrub floors," she said. "You let me copy other people's contracts until my fingers bled. You watched me hide my wrist in the market like a leper hides a lesion. For a fiction."

"For your life." His voice cracked on the second word. "Yes. And I would do it again, and that is precisely what damns me—that I can stand here at the end of everything and tell you honestly that I would forge the ledger again tomorrow. Mercy that costs the merciful nothing is cheap, child. Mine cost *you*, and I signed the bill myself, and I have hated the arithmetic every day since, and I did it anyway. That is not a defense. I want you to understand that it is not a defense. It is only the truth, and you deserve the truth more than anyone I have ever wronged."

"And then," Wren said, and her voice was shaking now, "when the shield stopped working—when the gods woke up and my blood started answering—you sold the *shield*. You sold me twice."

"I bought you forty hours." He lifted one ruined hand, and the black filigree shifted on it like something alive. "Marrec moved on the warrens forty hours later than he would have otherwise. Ask the elder. Ask any soul who got out through the flood-tunnels. I am not asking you to call it loyalty. I'm telling you it was the same sin as always—a smaller truth sacrificed to a larger lie—and this time the larger lie was *you living long enough to finish what your grandmother started*."

"What did she start?"

Something changed in his face. Carefully, painfully, he reached beneath the ledger-box that served as his desk's weight, and drew out a packet wrapped in oilcloth, tied with cord gone brittle as straw.

"She started this."

* * *

He unwrapped it with hands that trembled, and Wren saw at once that the oilcloth was not for dust but for secrecy—layered, waxed, sealed with a sigil she half-recognized from her grandmother's few surviving letters. Inside lay pages. Old ones. Older than the Scriptorium's oldest archive, older than the ban, the vellum gone the color of weak tea and soft as cloth at the edges.

"I was the Scriptorium's master restorer before I was its master," Halev said. "Thirty years ago, cataloguing the deep vaults, I found these misfiled inside a binding of tax rolls. Someone had hidden them there during the Sundering itself—hidden them so well that everyone who knew of them died before they could be burned." He wet his lips. "They are the covenant terms, Wren. The original instrument. Not the version in the Office histories. The actual words the gods and the mortals spoke over the heart-tree, in the hand of the scribe who witnessed the sealing."

Wren took the pages the way you take a sleeping animal—carefully, palms open, hardly breathing.

The script was archaic but legible, and she had been trained by the best forger-honest-copyist in Corvale to read hands. She read aloud, slowly, because some part of her needed to hear the words in air:

"*...and the gods shall hear the prayers of mortals, and answer according to their nature, save where mortals speak against it. This is the Veto of the Small: that no power of heaven shall act upon the world, nor claim vessel, nor take toll, nor bind any mortal soul to divine purpose, where a mortal in witness says: this we refuse.*"

Her voice failed on the last clause. She read it again silently. *This we refuse.* Three words. Three words that had existed for a thousand years and been buried under tax rolls while the Sundering burned and the Inquisitors built their law on ashes.

"The histories say the covenant bound mortals to serve," she whispered. "That the Godswar came because mortals grew proud and refused their obligations—"

"The histories were written by the victors of a war nobody won." Halev's breath was shortening; she could hear it, a rattle like paper. "Read the next page. Read what the gods agreed to and then ignored. Read the account of the first veto spoken and struck down. Read the marginalia—the witnessing scribe's own hand, recording how the god-host laughed at the mortal terms within a generation. Read it and understand what your grandmother understood: that the Sundering was not mortals breaking faith with heaven. It was heaven breaking faith with mortals, and mortals burning the bridge rather than live as slaves to creditors who never honored a single clause."

Wren turned the pages with hands that were not quite steady. Visions of the grove rose in her—the test, the visions of her grandmother sealing the covenant, and of the gods breaking faith *first*. Ashen-Vey had shown her fragments. But this was ink. This was witness. This was a dead scribe's testimony, preserved by accident and cowardice and one restorer's stubborn conscience.

"Why give me this now?" she asked. "Why not thirty years ago? Why not when you signed me over?"

"Because thirty years ago you were a child, and this would have gotten us both pyres, and I was a coward who dressed his cowardice as prudence." He met her eyes. "And because I hoped, for a long time, that the pages would stay lost and the world would stay safe and small and godless, and you would grow old copying contracts and never need to know. I chose your ignorance over your inheritance. Over and over, year after year, I chose it. That is the shape of my whole life, Wren. Every kindness I did you was a decision about what you were allowed to know."

The candle had burned down to a stub. The black filigree had crept past his shoulder now; she could see it moving, faintly, at the collar of his robe, like ink diffusing through water. Whatever bargain-price was collecting him, it was collecting quickly.

"Halev." She used his name bare, without the magister, because titles were for people you hadn't buried yet. "Ashen-Vey wants me to reforge the covenant. He says I'm the heir. He says the grove is the door. And he has never once lied to me outright, which I have learned is more dangerous than lying." She looked down at the pages in her hands. "If I bring these terms to the heart-tree—if I invoke this veto—what happens?"

"I don't know." He said it without shame. "No one knows. That is the point of it. The veto was never tested, because the gods never permitted it to be tested. You would be doing a thing that has never been done, armed with three words and whatever you are." A ghost of the old classroom smile. "Which, if my judgment of students holds, may be sufficient."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then you die in a burned grove having refused, in witness, to be a vessel. Which is more than most mortals get." His breathing hitched. "There is one thing more. One thing I have to ask, and you are free to refuse, and I will deserve the refusing."

"Ask."

"Forgive me." The words seemed to cost him more than the walking corpse of his body already had. "Not absolution—I know the difference, I've spent my life exploiting people who don't. Absolution is a transaction; forgiveness is a gift. I'm not owed the gift. I'm asking for it anyway, because I am dying with your name on my ledger and I cannot bear to leave the entry open."

Wren stood very still. The shutter knocked. The candle wept its last wax.

Everything in her wanted to say *no*. Everything in her wanted to make him die with it unresolved, to carry the weight of her seven years down into whatever dark collected men who forged ledgers on children. And beneath that wanting, quieter, was the thing she had learned in the market seven years ago selling luck-charms to desperate mothers: that mercy given only when it is deserved is not mercy at all, merely commerce.

"I forgive *you*," she said at last, and watched his face break. "Edran Halev, the man, the teacher, the coward, the liar who lied to save me—I forgive you. I carry none of it forward. But the system—" Her voice hardened. "The system that made a man choose between forging a child's life and surrendering her to the pyre, the system that priced mercy in debt-bonds and called the price justice, the system that let you spend twenty-six years telling yourself a fiction was protection—that system I do not forgive. That system I intend to *amend*. With these." She lifted the pages. "Your bargains bought me forty hours and a dead scribe's testimony. I'll spend both."

"That," Halev whispered, and the smile that crossed the ruin of his face was the truest thing she had seen in it all night, "is the best use anyone has ever made of my sins."

The filigree reached his throat. He did not seem afraid. He looked, instead, like a man setting down a load he had carried so long he had forgotten it was heavy, and his last breath came out almost peacefully, almost like a sigh at the end of a long day's copying, and the candle went out as if in courtesy.

* * *

Wren walked out of the Scriptorium through halls that smelled of cold ink and old paper, carrying the covenant terms against her chest like a second heartbeat. Behind her, the debt-bonds hung in their thousands in the dark, waiting for morning clerks who would find their master dead at his desk and a blank space where the master-restorer's final entry should have been.

In the street, the night was raw and starless, and Ashen-Vey was leaning against a wall as though he had been there all along, which perhaps he had. His beggar's face was unreadable in the dark.

"He's dead," Wren said.

"I know. I felt the bargain close." The god tilted his head. "And the pages? Did the old forger give you truth at the end, or one last lie to carry?"

"He gave me the original terms." She pulled the oilcloth packet from her coat and held it where he could see it, watching his face with new, cold attention. "Including the Veto of the Small. The clause the gods never honored. The clause that says a mortal in witness may refuse divine power outright."

For the first time since she had known him, Ashen-Vey went entirely, perfectly still. Not the stillness of surprise. The stillness of a man recalculating.

"Well," he said at last, softly. "Isn't that interesting."

"Yes," Wren said, tucking the pages away, meeting his ancient eyes without flinching. "It is. And before we go another step toward that grove, exile, you're going to tell me exactly what you thought would happen when I learned I could refuse you."

Somewhere far off, a bell began to toll—not the hour, but the alarm. The Inquisitors' Office had found the empty scriptorium, or the missing pages, or the trail of a dead man's bargains. The hunt was opening its eye again.

Ashen-Vey smiled, slow and strange, and said, "Walk with me, heir. We have until dawn, and I find I am suddenly very motivated to be honest."
