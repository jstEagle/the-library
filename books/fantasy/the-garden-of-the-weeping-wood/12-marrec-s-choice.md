## 12. Marrec's Choice

The order came at dawn, written in the Inquisitors' Office's finest hand, on paper so white it looked like snow that had never touched the ground.

*By authority of the High Consistory: the prisoners taken in the Bellows Street raid, having been found guilty of unlawful devotion, are to be executed publicly in Sable Plaza, that the city may see and be warned. The executions are to be carried out by your own hand, Inquisitor Marrec, as senior officer of record. So ordered. So sealed.*

Dain Marrec read it three times while his tea went cold beside him, and each reading made the words no kinder.

Eleven prisoners. He had seen their names on the manifest the night of the arrest. A cooper. Two laundresses. An old man who had once repaired clocks for the Office itself. A girl of perhaps sixteen whose only crime, so far as the record showed, was being found in a room where other people prayed. Eleven names, and beneath them, in the margin of the order, a note in the Provost's slanted script: *Make it clean, Dain. No speeches from the scaffold. The city is nervous enough.*

He sat for a long time with the paper flat on his desk, his thumb resting on the wax seal, feeling the ridges of it like something alive. Outside his window, Corvale was waking. Bakers' boys ran their rounds. A water-cart groaned up Bellows Street. Somewhere a child was crying about something small and survivable, and somewhere else a mother was telling her it would be all right, and both of them believed it, because they had to.

Seven years he had worn the gray coat. Seven years of warrants and ledgers and long interviews with frightened people, and in all that time he had told himself the same story every night before sleep: *the ban keeps the peace; the peace keeps the living; I am the wall between the fire and the city.* He had believed it the way you believe in load-bearing walls—without looking too closely, because the whole house rested on it.

But walls, he had learned this season, can be built over graves.

He thought of Halev in his cell, the old scribe's hands shaking as he'd described the forged account of the Godswar—not invented, not quite, but bent, curated, its worst pages kept and its best ones burned. *They needed the gods monstrous,* Halev had said, *because monstrous things may be outlawed. Complicated things must be lived with.* And Marrec had taken the page—the true page, the one he'd been ordered to destroy—and hidden it inside his commission scroll, because he could not burn it and could not yet use it, and so it had lived against his heart for eleven days like a second conscience.

Now the Office was asking him to kill eleven people whose deepest crime was hope.

He dressed slowly. He polished his boots. He did these small familiar things the way a man holds onto a rope in deep water, and all the while some quiet voice in him—the one he had spent seven years not listening to—kept saying: *you knew this day would come. Every wall falls or is climbed. You only get to choose which side you're standing on when it does.*

* * *

Sable Plaza at midmorning was full almost past holding.

They had come out of curiosity, most of them—that was what the Provost's people would say afterward, anyway—but Marrec stood on the scaffold steps and looked out at the faces and saw something else. He saw the laundress's neighbors from Tanner's Row, standing together with their arms folded. He saw market women who had bought charms from a scribe named Wren Thornbury and had, until recently, told no one. He saw a cluster of young clerks from the Scriptorium, pale and silent, and an old woman holding a candle unlit in her fist, as if lighting it were still a crime but carrying it were not.

And threaded through the crowd, gray-coated and watchful, forty Inquisitors who were not there to keep the peace. They were there to watch *him*. Everyone understood the arithmetic of the morning. Eleven deaths, one officer's obedience, and a city taught its lesson in a single hour.

The prisoners were brought up in chains. The cooper walked steadily. The clock-mender looked around the plaza as though memorizing it. The girl was crying without sound, which was somehow worse than screaming, and Marrec made himself look at her, made himself hold her face in his memory the way he held the forged page against his chest—as evidence, as debt, as the thing he would answer for either way.

The Provost stood at the edge of the scaffold with his hands clasped behind him, wearing the expression of a man attending a wedding he had personally arranged.

Marrec unrolled the execution warrant. His voice, when it came, carried farther than he expected, the way voices do in plazas built for proclamations.

"Dain Marrec, Inquisitor of the Third Chair," he read, "by authority of the High Consistory—" and then he stopped.

The plaza went very quiet. Forty gray coats shifted. The Provost's smile thinned by a degree.

Marrec looked down at the warrant, at the beautiful white paper, at the seal that had authorized the burning of a history and now the burning of eleven lives, and he found that the words would not come out of his mouth a second time. It was the strangest sensation of his life: not hesitation, not fear, but a simple mechanical refusal, as though his throat had been built for other purposes.

"No," he said instead.

It rang across the plaza. A thousand people heard it.

"The law I swore to," he said, louder, "was meant to stand between Corvale and the Godswar. I have spent seven years believing it did. I was wrong." He reached into his coat, and forty hands moved toward weapons, and he drew out—not a blade, but the commission scroll, and broke its seal, and let the true page fall open in the wind. "This is the account of the Godswar your Office burned. This is what the ban was built on. Read it. Copy it. Shout it in the streets if you must. Truth is not heresy."

"Marrec." The Provost's voice was soft and terrible. "You are ill. Step down."

"I am not ill." He took the warrant next, the white paper, the order with eleven names on it, and held it high. "I am awake. And I will not do this."

He had brought no flint. He had planned nothing beyond the refusal itself—he understood that much about himself now; whatever else he was, he was not a man built for conspiracy. But the old woman with the unlit candle pushed through the crowd, held up her candle, and lit it from the brazier at the scaffold's foot, and Marrec took the flame from her with a nod that felt like the first honest transaction of his life, and touched it to the corner of the warrant.

The paper caught. He held it as it burned, letting the heat climb toward his fingers, letting the plaza watch eleven names turn to ash and rise. Then he fed the commission scroll after it—the forged history curling black at its edges—and for one moment the smoke smelled, impossibly, faintly of sap and green wood, as though something old in the stones of Corvale had leaned close to breathe it in.

The crowd did not cheer. That was the remarkable thing. They simply *witnessed*, thousands of eyes recording, and Marrec understood that this too was testimony, that he had just made every person in this plaza into a copy of the page he'd burned.

Then the gray coats closed in, and the moment ended.

They took his coat, his badge, his boots even, as though shame were stored in leather. As they marched him down the scaffold steps past the Provost, the older man leaned close and said, quietly, "I gave you a clean death to administer, Dain. You've made it dirty for everyone."

"No," Marrec said. "I've made it visible. There's a difference."

They put him in a cell in the Office's own undercroft, which he noted, with a tired sort of humor, was directly below the archive where the original forgery had been authored. The irony was probably intentional. The Provost struck him as a man who enjoyed ironies the way other men enjoyed wine.

* * *

Three days passed. They fed him. They did not question him, which frightened him more than questioning would have. On the fourth day, a young clerk named Ossel—someone he had trained himself, two winters ago, in the proper handling of evidence chains—came down with a tray of bread and water and a face like a man carrying a live coal.

"Inquisitor," the boy whispered, setting down the tray. "I shouldn't be here."

"Then be quick."

Ossel glanced at the door, then said, rapid and low: "The believers weren't executed. The Provost commuted the sentences—publicly, after what you did. Says mercy looks better than martyrdom. But that's not—" The boy swallowed. "That's not why I came. There's a requisition order crossed my desk yesterday. Pyre-wagons. Oil by the barrelload. And a survey team sent out to the Weeping Wood."

Marrec went cold. "A survey for what?"

"For burning, sir. All of it. The whole grove." Ossel's voice dropped further. "There's a theory going round the upper chairs. That the awakening—it's coming *from* the Wood. That the covenant-door is there, and if the door burns, whatever's waking gets locked out forever. They're calling it the Final Measure. The Provost signed it himself."

"When?"

"Nine days. Maybe ten. They're building the wagons first. They want it done properly." The boy's mouth twisted. "*Cleanly.*"

Marrec gripped the bars. Nine days. He thought of Wren Thornbury—of the charm-ledger trail that had started all of this, of the woman the whole city was whispering about now, the heir, the scribe, the one the believers called the last of the Thornburys. He had hunted her signature through the ledgers once with righteous patience. Now he would have given his remaining years to put a message in her hands.

"Listen to me," he said. "Do you know where she is? The Thornbury woman?"

"No one does. She comes and goes like weather. But—" Ossel hesitated. "There's talk. In the low taverns. That she has friends among the smugglers under Bellows Street. And that messages left at the shrine of Saint Vessa on Ashlane find their way to strange places."

"Ashlane," Marrec repeated. "Saint Vessa."

"Yes, sir." The boy straightened, and for a moment he looked less like a clerk and more like what Marrec had hoped he might become. "Why are you doing this, sir? Telling me. Trusting me."

Marrec looked at him—at the boy he'd trained, at the future arriving early—and said, "Because someone once chose to trust me with the truth, and it ruined my life, and it was the best thing that ever happened to it. Go."

After Ossel left, Marrec sat on the cell floor with his back to the stone and tore a strip from the hem of his shirt. He had no ink. He used the ash from the candle stub Ossel had smuggled in with the tray—small mercies, small conspiracies—and wrote in a hand gone careful and cramped:

*To Wren Thornbury, heir of the Weeping Wood, from Dain Marrec, prisoner of the Office he served:*

*I burned my commission rather than execute the faithful. I tell you this not for absolution—I hunted you, and would have caged you, and called it duty. I tell you because I have read the orders they think I cannot see. In nine days, maybe ten, the Inquisitors' Office will bring pyre-wagons to the Weeping Wood and burn it to the root. They believe the grove is the door the gods wake through, and they mean to weld it shut with fire. If they succeed, everything dies in that grove—every prayer, every name, every chance of any covenant, old or new. You have nine days. Perhaps fewer. I am sorry it is so little, and sorrier still that it comes from a man who was part of the machine that built the pyre. Use what I was. Forgive what I was. Or neither. Only be quick.*

*—M.*

He folded the strip small, and on the fifth day, when Ossel came again with bread, Marrec pressed it into the boy's hand along with the last coin sewn into his collar—a bribe, an inheritance, a prayer.

"Saint Vessa's, on Ashlane," he said. "Left of the altar, under the loose tile. Tell no one. Trust no one. And Ossel—" he caught the boy's eye, "—whatever happens to the Wood, whatever happens to me: you saw nothing today except a prisoner eating his bread."

The boy nodded once, and was gone, and Marrec lay back on the stone floor and stared up at the ceiling, where a hairline crack ran from corner to corner like a river on a map of some country he had never visited.

Nine days.

He had spent seven years enforcing a law built on ash and lies, and it had taken him one morning and one match to end it, and now he lay in the dark beneath the archive of the lie itself, waiting to learn whether a message scrawled in candle-soot could outrun a wagonload of oil.

Somewhere above him, the city went about its day. Somewhere beyond the walls, a petrified grove wept its slow sap-tears into the wind, not yet knowing what was coming for it. And somewhere between those two places, if the gods had any mercy left—and Marrec, lying in the dark, found that despite everything, despite the forged histories and the pyre-wagons and the eleven names he had refused to cross out, he still believed they might—a debt-bound scribe would open a scrap of cloth and read it, and run.

He closed his eyes. For the first time in seven years, he prayed.

He did not know to whom. He suspected that no longer mattered.
