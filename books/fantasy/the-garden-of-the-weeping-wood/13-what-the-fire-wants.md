## 13. What the Fire Wants

Ashen-Vey had worn many bodies across the long centuries of his exile, and he had learned this much about them: they betrayed their owners. The beggar's flesh he occupied now ached at the joints, grew hungry on schedule, and flushed with heat when he lied—which was inconvenient, because lying was most of what he did.

He was lying now, and the body knew it.

They stood in the high gallery of an abandoned granary above Bellows Street, where the smugglers kept their stores, and through the broken slats of the roof Wren could see the distant smudge of the Weeping Wood against the evening sky—dark, patient, weeping its slow amber tears into the roots of the world. She did not know he could see it too, even here, even through borrowed eyes. A god's attention traveled along older roads than light.

"The Inquisitors will burn it," she said. Her voice was steady in the way that cost her something; he had learned to hear the price beneath her steadiness, the way a musician hears the effort in a held note. "Marrec's message was clear. Pyre-wagons, oil, the whole grove. They mean to end the awakening by killing the door."

"Then let them."

She turned. In the grainy half-light her face went still—not the stillness of calm but the stillness of a creature deciding whether the thing it has just heard is a threat. He catalogued the small movements: her thumb finding the covenant-token at her throat, her weight shifting to the balls of her feet. Seven years of hiding had taught her to read rooms. He had taught her, without meaning to, to read him. That had been a miscalculation. He was beginning to understand how many of those he had made.

"Explain," she said.

*Let them burn it.* He had rehearsed the argument for three days, shaping it in the silence while she slept, testing each phrase for the texture of honesty. Because the truth would not serve him. The truth was that fire would unmake the covenant's last anchor, and what remained of his kind would pour back into the world like water through a cracked dam—unbound, ungoverned, and hungry after a thousand years of thirst. The truth was that he wanted it. The truth was that his famous mercy, the mercy that had gotten him cast down, had never been mercy at all.

So instead he said: "The grove is dying anyway. You have seen it. The heart-tree is black to the cambium. What the Inquisitors burn tomorrow, rot would take in a decade. But if the trees go *by fire*—" he let his voice drop into the register he used for prayers, the one that made mortal spines remember they had once been temples "—fire is not an ending, Wren. Fire is a door opened violently. The covenant is anchored in living wood. Kill the wood, and the terms die with it. No terms. No veto. No chains."

"No chains," she repeated.

"For us." He stepped closer, and the beggar's body did what bodies do—it leaned toward warmth it did not feel, performing a tenderness he could deploy like a coin. "For me. Do you know what it is to be bound? To have every step of your existence circumscribed by an agreement you were not permitted to refuse? I argued for your ancestors' lives when my kin wanted ash. For that mercy they flayed my name from the roll of heaven and dropped me into meat." He spread the beggar's scarred hands. "I am not asking you to love what I am. I am asking you to finish what your grandmother started and then abandoned. She wrote terms the gods never agreed to. Burn the grove, and no one agrees to anything ever again. Everyone is free."

The word *free* did its work—he watched it land, watched it find the bruise. Freedom. He had chosen it deliberately. A debt-bound scribe did not hear that word often, and when she heard it, she heard it with her whole body.

But Wren Thornbury had spent seven years selling luck-charms to desperate people, and somewhere in those seven years she had learned the trick that made her dangerous in a way no god had anticipated: she listened for what people needed, and then she asked about it.

"And the city?" she said quietly. "When your kin come back unbound? What happens to Corvale?"

He had an answer prepared for this too. It was a good answer, layered and reasonable, about balance and old grievances cooling over time, about how vengeance was a mortal invention really, gods were above—

"They would be vengeful," he said, and stopped.

Because the body betrayed him. Heat climbed the beggar's neck, traitorous blood rising in a face that was not his face but answered to him nonetheless, and Wren saw it. He watched her see it. He watched the understanding move through her like cold water through a channel—slow, then all at once.

"You want them angry," she said.

"Wren—"

"You *need* them angry." Her voice did not rise. That was the worst of it; she did not give him the comfort of a shout to defend against. "An unbound god who comes home grateful might negotiate. Might listen. Might look at the world and decide it was worth gentleness. But an unbound god who comes home furious—" She pressed her hand flat against her sternum, against the token, as if steadying something in herself. "Furious gods don't listen. Furious gods don't need heirs or anchors or scribes. They just take. And you'd be standing there beside them, free, having arranged the whole thing so that no one could ever hold you to a promise again."

The granary was very quiet. Somewhere below, the smugglers moved among their barrels, and the smell of grain dust hung in the dark like the memory of plenty.

"That is not what I—" he began.

"When the Godswar came," she said, "you argued for mercy."

Every word she spoke now was a stone placed with a mason's care. He felt each one settle into a wall between them.

"You told me that yourself. At the shrine, the first night. You said you stood before the host and pleaded for the mortals, and they cast you down for it. I built everything on that. Every choice. Every mile." She laughed once, short and terrible, a sound with no joy in it anywhere. "And it was true. You *did* argue for mercy. You just never told me why."

The body's heart was hammering. He could have stilled it—he had that much command over his meat—but some stubborn instinct made him let it race, made him wear the panic honestly, because a part of him still believed, in the way that drowning men believe in branches, that if she could only see his sincerity she would forgive the architecture underneath it.

"Tell me why," she said.

He looked at her—at the thin shoulders, the ink-stained fingers, the brand-scar hidden under a sleeve she no longer bothered to hide it under—and he understood that the moment had arrived that every long game arrives at eventually: the moment when the player must choose which losses to count.

"I argued for mercy," he said, "because mercy leaves debts."

She did not flinch. He had hoped—I say *hoped*, though hope is a word I must use carefully, being uncertain even now whether what moved in me then deserved the name—that she would flinch, would cry out, would give him a wound to bind and thereby a reason to stay. She did not. She simply waited, the way creditors wait, the way the grove itself had waited a thousand years with its sap running down its bark like grief that had forgotten how to stop.

"Spare the mortals," he went on, because there was no longer any sense in stopping, "and they owe you. Spare them, and when the doors reopen—as doors always reopen, Wren, wood rots, seals crack, blood remembers—they will open the way for the one who showed kindness. Not for Veyra of the Nine Thunders. Not for Ossuin who eats coastlines. For me. The merciful one. The one they'd *want* to let back in." He heard his own voice flatten into honesty at last, and it was almost a relief, the way confession is a relief, the way a fever breaking is a relief. "My mercy was a key I forged in advance. You were the lock. I found you the way a man finds a door he built the blueprint for a thousand years ago."

"And the burning?" Her eyes were bright and dry. Dryness, in mortals, he had learned, was worse than weeping. Weeping spends the grief; dryness hoards it. "If I let the grove burn. If your kin come back unbound and furious. Where does the merciful one stand then?"

"Beside them," he said. "Unbound. Owed nothing, owing nothing. Free."

"*Free,*" she said again, and this time the word came out of her like a blade coming out of a wound. "You keep using that word as if it were a gift. Ashen-Vey. Listen to me. I have been owned since I was nineteen years old. I know exactly what freedom costs when someone else sets the price. And you are asking me to set fire to the only law that ever bound your kind, so that your kind can walk back into a world full of people exactly like I was—debt-bound, powerless, praying into silence—and call that liberation." She shook her head slowly. "You're not offering me freedom. You're offering *yourself* freedom and dressing it in my language."

The terrible thing—the thing I have carried longest—is that she was right, and I knew it, and some part of me had known it from the first night at the ruined shrine when I watched her kneel in the ash and speak to the dark with more courtesy than the dark deserved. I had chosen her for her bloodline. I had kept her for her usefulness. And I had—this is the part I still cannot fully account for, even now, even wearing this quiet mortal shape with its honest aches—I had *loved* her, in whatever way a thing like me loves, which is to say possessively, which is to say as a miser loves gold, which is to say not at all in the way that matters.

"Ashen-Vey," she said, softer now, and the softness was crueler than the blade had been. "In the Godswar. When you argued for mercy. Was there a single moment—a single breath—when you argued because it was *right*?"

I searched. That is the honest truth of it: I searched, there in the granary dark, through ten centuries of memory, through the smoke and screaming of the Godswar, through the council of the host where I had stood with my hands open and my voice like silver and pled for the lives of a species I considered livestock. I searched for one unmixed motive, one clean thread of compassion running through the strategy, and I found—there, at the very bottom, small and buried as a seed under winter—something. A hesitation. A moment when the screaming below had sounded like music played badly, and something in me had ached toward it, wanting it done *well*. Wanting the world to be beautiful rather than merely won.

Perhaps that was compassion. Perhaps it was only aesthetics. I genuinely do not know. It may be that no god has ever known the difference, and that this ignorance is the reason covenants exist at all.

"There was a moment," I said finally. "One. I cannot swear it was pure. I no longer trust myself to know what pure looks like. That is what exile does, Wren. It takes the instrument you measured yourself with and melts it down."

She nodded slowly, and I watched her do the thing she did better than anyone I had ever known, mortal or divine: she took the truth, all of it, the ugly architecture and the buried seed both, and she weighed it without letting either side tip the scale by shouting.

"Then here is what happens now," she said. "I don't let the grove burn. I don't hand your kin a world without terms. But I'm not casting you out either—you've been cast out enough, and besides, I may still need you, and you may still be useful, and I'm done pretending otherwise." She lifted her chin. "But you will tell me the rest. All of it. What an anchor actually is. What it costs the vessel. Every term of every plan you've made around me, spoken aloud, tonight, before we march. Because I have been sold twice in my life, Ashen-Vey—once by Halev, once by history—and I will not be sold a third time by something that calls itself my friend."

*Friend.* The word went through me like a splinter going into a palm—small, deep, and impossible to ignore.

"And if I refuse?" I asked. "If I keep my counsel?"

She smiled, and it was the smile she wore in the market when a buyer tried to haggle her below cost—patient, weary, absolutely immovable.

"Then I'll walk to the grove alone," she said, "and I'll reforge the covenant without you, and I'll write the terms myself, and believe me when I tell you that I have learned from watching you exactly how binding a well-drafted contract can be. You taught me that. Everything you taught me, I'm going to use."

For a long moment neither of us moved. Below us the smugglers sang a work-song in low voices, something about rope and river, and the sound rose through the floorboards like the pulse of a patient animal.

Then, from the street outside, came the tolling of a bell—not the market bell, not the hour bell, but the Office bell, the one rung only for assembly, and beneath it the drum of many boots, and a smell threading up through the broken roof that I knew from the Godswar and had hoped never to smell again in any world:

Oil. Torch-oil, in quantity, moving toward the western gate.

They were not waiting for dawn. The pyre-wagons were rolling *now*.

Wren was already moving, snatching up her cloak, her face lit orange by the first far glow—and I stood in the dark behind her, a god in a beggar's skin, holding my one unmixed moment like a coal cupped in bare hands, knowing that everything I had planned for a thousand years now came down to what I did in the next hour: help her save the grove and be bound forever by whatever she wrote—or stand aside and watch it burn, and be free, and be nothing.

"Wren," I called after her, and she turned in the doorway, firelight on her face. "Wait for me."

Whether I meant it as mercy or strategy, even I could no longer say.
