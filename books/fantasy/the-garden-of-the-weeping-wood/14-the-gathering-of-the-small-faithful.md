## 14. The Gathering of the Small Faithful

The rumor reached Wren before the dawn did.

She woke in the loft above the dyer's shop on Bellows Street with the taste of smoke still in her mouth—dream-smoke, the memory of a grove burning that had not yet burned—and found Sefa crouched at the top of the ladder, holding a lamp she had not lit. She didn't need to. Her hands glowed faintly, the way they had since the night Wren tore open the Inquisitors' wagons and let the congregation spill out into the street like water from a broken jar.

"They're coming," Sefa said. "The pyre-wagons. Left the Bastion an hour ago. Old Tam's cousin saw them loading pitch and iron at the Saltgate."

Wren sat up. Her body ached in places she hadn't known a body could ache—the price of magic spent too fast, spent *loudly*, in front of half the Fishmarket. Seven years of smallness, and then one night of being enormous, and now there was no going back down the well.

"How long to the grove?"

"By the wagon road? They'll reach it by midday tomorrow." Sefa's glow guttered as her fear rose. "Wren, they're not taking prisoners this time. The wagons are for the trees."

*For the trees.* As if you could arrest a grove. As if you could hang it.

Wren dressed in the dark, fingers finding the laces by habit, and felt the covenant-token warm against her sternum where it hung on its cord—her grandmother's token, a sliver of heart-wood carved with a knot no scribe had ever been able to transcribe. It had been warming more often lately. She chose to believe it was encouragement.

"We need everyone," she said. "Everyone who prayed and was answered. Everyone who ever bought a charm off me and never asked where it came from. Everyone the city forgot it owed."

Sefa looked at her the way people had been looking at her all week—not quite afraid, not quite believing, hungry in a way that frightened Wren more than any Inquisitor could.

"And if they don't come?"

Wren thought of Halev dying in his chair with his ledgers spread around him like fallen leaves, buying her time with the last coin of his ruined life. She thought of Marrec in his cell, having burned his commission in front of the whole plaza, choosing conscience over the only law he'd ever loved. She thought of the mother from the market, seven years ago now, weeping over a luck-charm that should not have worked.

"Then I walk to the grove alone," Wren said, "and I stand between the fire and the heart-tree, and I find out what my grandmother's blood is actually worth." She pulled her coat on. "But I don't think I'll be alone. I've sold four hundred luck-charms in this city, Sefa. Luck has to land somewhere."

* * *

It landed in the tannery yard off Ropewalk, where the smell of lime pits kept casual passersby away and the walls were high enough to hide a crowd from anything but the sky.

Wren had expected dozens. She had dared, in her most reckless hour before sleep, to hope for a hundred.

There were three hundred people in the yard when she climbed onto the rendering platform at dawn, and more coming through the gate in twos and threes, hoods up, faces turned away from the street. She stood among them and could not speak for a moment, because she knew them. That was the thing that undid her. She *knew* them.

There was the fishwife whose son's fever had broken the night Wren's charm hung over his cot—a charm Wren had made as a fake, a nothing, a bit of braided string and beeswax, until the old power moved through it anyway. There was the lamplighter who'd bought a charm against forgetting his dead wife's face, and had come back twice to say it worked, and Wren had told him both times that he was imagining it. There were dockhands and laundresses, a midwife with her bag still on her shoulder as though she'd come straight from a birth, two of the freed believers from Bellows Street standing shoulder to shoulder like bookends, and—Gods, *there*—a squad of soldiers out of uniform, six of them, their officer a broad woman with a sergeant's knots of grey in her hair who looked at Wren with the flat patience of someone who had already decided and did not intend to be argued with.

"Captain Verel," the woman said, when Wren's eyes snagged on the sword under her cloak. "Was. The Office doesn't know yet that I'm not. My whole file will know by noon, so I'd rather be doing something with my treason than hiding it."

"You'll be arrested," Wren said. "Or worse."

"I've marched for Corvale eleven years," Verel said. "Corvale is those people behind you, not the men loading pitch onto wagons. I know which one I swore to." She shrugged, a soldier's shrug, economical. "Besides. Someone has to teach your weavers how to hold a line."

Wren looked out at them all—the overlooked, the unrecorded, the ones whose names appeared nowhere in the Scriptorium's ledgers except as debts and taxes and death-notices. Ashen-Vey stood at the edge of the platform in his beggar's shape, arms folded, watching her with an expression she had learned to distrust precisely because it looked so much like tenderness.

"You see?" he said softly. "I told you. The small faithful. They were always the covenant's true ink; the gods only ever signed it."

"Save your poetry," Wren murmured. "I'm still angry with you."

"I know. It becomes you."

She turned to the crowd, and her voice, when it came, was smaller than she wanted it to be, and truer for that.

"I'm not going to give you a speech about glory," she said. The yard went quiet the way snow quiets a street. "I've never fought anything bigger than a debt-collector, and neither have most of you. Tomorrow morning the Inquisitors' wagons reach the Weeping Wood, and they're going to burn it—burn it the way they burned the shrines, burn it the way they burned the histories, because a living grove remembers things the Office needs forgotten. I'm going to stand in front of that fire. I don't know if I can stop it. I know I can't stop it alone."

She held up her hands, palms out, the way she'd seen Ashen-Vey do at the ruined shrine, and hated herself a little for borrowing the gesture, and did it anyway.

"Every one of you has a reason to be here or you wouldn't have come through that gate. Somebody's prayer got answered. Somebody's charm worked. Somebody saw the congregation freed in the Fishmarket and thought, *finally, finally.* I can't promise you victory. I can't even promise you tomorrow. What I can promise—" and here her voice cracked, and she let it, because these people had heard enough polished lies from better mouths than hers, "—what I can promise is that no one who stands in that grove will stand there unseen. Not by me. Not by what's waking under the roots. Whatever happens, we will have *witnessed each other*, and that is the one thing the Sundering never managed to take."

The silence held for three heartbeats. Then the midwife began to sing—an old work-song, a counting song, the kind women sang over wash-buckets—and the yard took it up raggedly, then together, three hundred voices that had spent a generation praying in whispers learning, all at once, the sound of their own chorus.

Ashen-Vey closed his eyes. When he opened them again, for just a moment, the beggar's face was gone and something vast and grieving and glad looked out from underneath, and Wren understood that whatever else he was, whatever lies he'd told, this—hearing them sing—was not one of the lies.

* * *

They left by the Corpse Gate at dusk, because it was the gate least watched and the name least likely to make anyone superstitious enough to look twice, which Wren found grimly funny. Three hundred and forty-one people, counted by Captain Verel with a sergeant's efficiency, moving out along the river road in loose columns disguised—as much as three hundred people can be disguised—as a funeral procession. Which, Wren reflected, was not entirely a lie either. Something was being buried tomorrow. She intended to make sure it wasn't the grove.

Verel organized them the way water organizes itself downhill: naturally, inevitably, without asking anyone's permission. The dockhands went to the front because they were used to hauling and could drag the wagons' wheels into mud if it came to that. The laundresses and fishwives carried the salt and the oil-cloths, because salt throws sparks wrong and wet cloth beats out flame, and every one of them had put out a kitchen fire at least once. The midwife took charge of the wounded-to-be with the brisk calm of a woman who had delivered babies in plague years. Even the children who had come—because of course children had come; the overlooked bring their children everywhere, having nowhere to leave them—were given jobs. Carrying water. Holding lanterns. Being the reason.

"Why are you really doing this?" Wren asked Verel, somewhere past the second milestone, walking beside the captain while the column breathed and shuffled around them like a sleeping animal.

Verel was quiet long enough that Wren thought she wouldn't answer.

"When I was nineteen," she said at last, "I stood post at the Sundering Day parade. You know the one. Every year, the Office reads the history of the Godswar from the steps of the Bastion, and we stand there in formation while they tell us how the mad gods burned the world and how the wise Inquisitors saved us, and every year I believed it harder, because believing it was easier than wondering why the reading never changed and never deepened and never once named a single god by name." She spat, neatly, off the road. "Last year I stood that post knowing what I know now—that the account is forged, that a mortal veto was struck from the terms, that everything I marched for was built on a page somebody wrote in a comfortable room. And I stood there anyway, because I hadn't decided yet." She glanced at Wren. "I've decided."

"The page Marrec kept," Wren said. "Do you think it's true?"

"I think," Verel said, "that when powerful men forge documents, it's because the truth was inconvenient. And I think a woman who walks into a fire to save a grove of trees is either the greatest liar in Corvale or exactly what she says she is, and liars don't usually cry when fishwives sing."

Wren hadn't known she'd cried. She wiped her face with her sleeve, the sleeve that had hidden a debt-brand for seven years, and found that the brand had faded to almost nothing—a ghost of a ghost, since the night her record was read aloud in the plaza and annulled. She was, by the law's own reckoning, nobody's property anymore. By the law's reckoning she barely existed. *The Reckoning of Names*, they were calling it, in the taverns and the temples-that-weren't-temples-yet. A debt-bound scribe had walked into the records and come out the other side as a free woman with no history at all.

It should have frightened her more than it did. Instead it felt like being handed a blank first page.

* * *

They smelled the grove before they saw it—or rather, they smelled what was coming to the grove. Pitch and hot iron, riding the night wind down from the north road, where torchlight crawled like a slow luminous worm: the pyre-wagons, making cold camp, saving the last miles for morning.

Wren stood on the low rise above the river with Ashen-Vey and looked at that light, and felt the token against her chest pulse like a second heartbeat.

"They'll have outriders," Ashen-Vey said. "And they'll have brought the null-staves. If they take you alive, they won't kill you, little heir. They'll do worse. They'll write you."

"Write me?"

"Into a ledger. Into a cell. Into a story where you are the mad witch who burned the granaries and poisoned the wells. The Office's oldest weapon isn't fire." His beggar's mouth curved. "It's the archive. I watched them do it to my siblings, one name at a time, until the world forgot we'd ever had names at all. Fire is only how they finish the sentence."

Wren looked at him—at the exile wearing a beggar like a coat, at the god who had argued for mercy and been cast down for it, at the ally who needed her as anchor and vessel and door, and who had, in the grove's visions, shown her truths he would rather have kept folded away. She did not trust him. She had stopped pretending otherwise somewhere on the road, and strangely, the honesty of not trusting had made them easier together, the way a knot holds best when you stop trying to hide where it's tied.

"If we win tomorrow," she said, "you'll tell me the rest of it. All of it. What you want me for. What freedom costs a god. Everything."

"Yes," he said simply, and she believed him, which was the most dangerous thing that had happened all week.

Below them, the column had halted in the shelter of a willow brake, and the small faithful were doing what ordinary people do when the battle waits for morning: sharing bread, mending straps, teaching each other songs, falling asleep against each other's shoulders without shame. The lamplighter walked the line lighting no lamps, just talking quietly to anyone too anxious to sleep. The midwife checked blisters. Two of Verel's deserters drilled a dozen weavers in the dark with broom-handles, gently, laughing at their own mistakes.

Nobody had ordered any of it. Nobody had promised any of them anything. They had simply chosen—each one alone, in kitchens and barracks and cellars, in the private country of the conscience—and their separate choices had converged here, on a cold road, facing a fire.

Belonging, Wren thought, watching them. She had spent seven years believing it was something you found, like a coin in the gutter, luck falling on whoever happened to be standing there. But it wasn't. It was something you built, plank by plank, out of bread shared and names remembered and promises kept badly and then kept again better. Her grandmother had built it once, in a grove, with gods. The gods had let it rot. So mortals would build it again—with or without them.

The token pulsed against her breastbone, and far off across the dark fields, the Weeping Wood answered: a faint silver shimmer along the petrified canopy, sap-light rising in the dead branches like breath returning to a body. The grove knew she was coming. Perhaps it had always known.

"Wren." Sefa's voice, urgent, from down the slope. "Rider coming up the river road. Alone. No colors. He's calling your name."

Wren's hand went to the token. Ashen-Vey's eyes narrowed to embers.

The rider reined in at the foot of the rise, and the lantern light found a face she knew—hollowed, bruised, unmistakable. A man who had burned his commission in front of the whole city and traded a gilded career for a cell.

"Inquisitor Marrec," Wren said slowly.

"Not anymore." Dain Marrec swung down from the saddle and stood swaying with exhaustion, holding out a folded paper like a man offering proof of a miracle. "They moved the burning up. The wagons you're watching are the decoy, Wren. The real company rides tonight, ahead of you, through the marsh path—and they're not carrying pitch." His voice cracked on the words. "They're carrying the fire-pots from the Bastion vaults. The ones made to burn things that can't die."
