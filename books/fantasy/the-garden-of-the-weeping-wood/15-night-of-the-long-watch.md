## 15. Night of the Long Watch

The cell beneath the Hall of Judgment had no window, but someone—Marrec himself, Wren guessed—had wedged a sliver of polished tin between two stones at eye level, angled to catch what little light leaked under the door. It threw a pale coin of brightness on the far wall. He sat beneath it like a man sitting beneath a real sun.

Wren stood in the doorway a long moment before he noticed her. The guard who had let her in—a broad woman with a deserter's stillness and a believer's eyes—had said only, *You have until the watch changes.* That gave her perhaps an hour.

"You came," Marrec said. He did not rise. His wrists were bare where the manacles had been struck off—the Office had not bothered to replace them for a man awaiting execution—and he rubbed one wrist absently, as if the weight of them lingered there anyway. "I half hoped you would. I half hoped you wouldn't."

"Which half won?"

He smiled, and it was a tired, honest thing. "The half that's been arguing with you in my head for a week."

She crossed the small room and sat on the floor across from him, because there was no second chair and because she wanted, tonight, to be lower than no one. The stone was cold through her skirts. Up close she could see what the rumors had already told her: he had lost weight since the plaza, since the morning he burned his commission in front of three hundred witnesses and let the ash drift over the Inquisitors' boots. His uniform shirt hung loose at the collar. But his eyes were the same—clear, deliberate, the eyes of a man who read every ledger twice.

"They'll kill you tomorrow," she said. "Or the day after. They're deciding whether it's better to do it quietly or make an example."

"Quietly," he said. "They've already decided. I heard the warden complaining about the ink requisition." He said it without bitterness, the way a man reports weather. "A public execution gives the faithful a martyr. They'd rather I just stopped existing."

"And you're telling me this calmly."

"I've had time to practice." He leaned back against the wall. The coin of light slid across his face. "Wren. Why did you come? Not to grieve me in advance—I can see you're not here for that. You came to argue."

"I came to understand," she said. "Because tomorrow my people march into fire, and some of them will die holding a line for me, and I need to know that the man who spent seven years hunting girls like me wasn't—" She stopped. Started again. "I need to know you'd have done it differently. If you could."

"That's not understanding. That's absolution." He shook his head slowly. "And I won't give it to you, because you didn't come here to be lied to either."

* * *

The silence between them filled with the sounds of the building above: boots, doors, the distant groan of the great ledgers being carried from shelf to shelf as clerks worked through the night. Somewhere overhead, the Office was rewriting itself in real time—purging names, sealing records, preparing for a world in which one of its own had chosen truth over law.

Marrec spoke first. "Do you know why I became an Inquisitor?"

"Because you believed the ban kept people safe."

"Because when I was nine years old, my street burned." He said it flatly, a fact worn smooth by repetition. "Not the Godswar—that was over, decades past. A hedge-witch's working gone wrong. A love charm, they said afterward. Eleven houses. My mother pulled me out of a window by my hair. I watched the woman who cast it stand in the crowd and weep, and I believed even then that she hadn't meant it. And I learned that meaning well is worth nothing if power has no leash." He looked at her directly. "That's the law, Wren. Not cruelty. A leash. Every leash chokes something. We chose what to choke."

"And the people your leashes choked?" Her voice came out quieter than she intended. "The congregation in the Bellows Street warrens. Grandmothers praying over cradles. Children with their grandmothers' tokens sewn into their coats. You knew the ban fell hardest on the poor—we both know the rich buy their charms from Scriptorium-certified scribes at forty times the price and call it medicine."

"Yes."

"*Yes?*"

"Yes." He did not flinch. "I arrested a woman in Ashfall Ward once for a hearth-blessing. Old, half-blind, alone. Her granddaughter had died that winter and she was asking—someone—for help carrying it. I took her in. I wrote the report myself. And I wrote in it that she was a danger to the city, knowing it was a lie, because the form required a lie and the form required my name." His hands were steady in his lap. "I have thought about her every day for six years. So don't tell me what the ban costs. I have paid attention longer than you've been angry about it."

"Then why refuse now?" Wren leaned forward. "What changed? The forged history? You must have suspected—"

"The forged history changed nothing about the leash. It changed who was holding it." He met her eyes. "For twenty years I believed the gods broke faith first. That the Sundering was mercy—that mortals ended the war by ending the covenant, that the ban was the scar tissue of survival. When I read the original terms, and saw that the mortal veto existed all along, and that the Office buried it—" He exhaled. "A leash held by liars isn't order. It's just a different kind of madness, wearing uniforms. I could serve a hard law honestly. I cannot serve a false one at all."

"So you burn your commission in a plaza and hand them your head."

"I hand them a question," he said. "It's the only weapon I've ever had that works. Make them answer, publicly, why a man who enforced their law perfectly for twenty years deserves to die for reading it too closely." He almost smiled. "Whether they answer before or after the axe falls is out of my hands."

Wren found she was gripping her own knees hard enough to ache. "Your answer will be written in my friends' blood tomorrow. They're firing the grove, Marrec. Pyre-wagons are already loaded. Whatever question you've planted, they intend to burn the ground it grows in."

"I know. I sent you word so you'd know." He paused. "There's more. Listen carefully, because I don't know how much of this reaches you otherwise. The firing isn't the plan—it's the *pretext*. The Office believes the grove's death will sever whatever door the gods are using. Burn the root, kill the tree, end the awakening. But Commander Ilvane has a second column. Two hundred picked men, not pyre-wagons—they go in *after* the fire, to the heart-tree, with cold-iron axes and a rite I found in the sealed archives. It doesn't just kill the grove. It salts the covenant. Nothing rooted there will ever grow again—not tree, not god, not heir."

The cold went through her like well water. "An heir. They know about me."

"They've known since the raid on the shrine. Halev's sale bought them your name, and your name bought them the bloodline." He said it gently, which made it worse. "You were never going to walk into that grove unnoticed, Wren. The only question left is whether you walk in knowing what waits at the center."

* * *

She stayed until the watch changed. They talked of smaller things then—he asked after the freed believers, whether the young man Tam with the burned hands had found work among her followers (he had; he was learning to bind wounds), and she asked whether he regretted it, and he said, "Ask me tomorrow. Tonight the answer keeps changing," and they both laughed, briefly, at the terrible honesty of it.

At the door she turned back. "If there's anything—"

"There isn't." He was already settling against the wall again, beneath his coin of borrowed light. "Go weigh what matters. I'm a settled account." Then, as she stepped through: "Wren. The veto. Use it *before* they reach the heart-tree. A covenant renegotiated in front of an army is worth ten argued over ashes."

The door closed behind her, and she walked up out of the earth into the last night of the old world.

* * *

Ashen-Vey was waiting on the roof of the tannery where her people slept, and she knew before she reached him that he had felt the shape of her evening in the way gods feel weather. He stood at the roof's edge with his beggar's shoulders squared against the wind, watching the distant glow of the Inquisitors' campfires beyond the walls—so many of them, strung along the dark like a second, hostile constellation.

"You saw Marrec," he said.

"You felt it."

"I feel everything you carry now. It's the anchor, remember." He turned. In the starlight his borrowed face was ordinary—a smuggler's face, a nobody's face—but the eyes underneath were old as grief. "So. Before the wagons roll. There's a road out."

"Ashen-Vey—"

"Hear it." His voice was low and very even, the voice he used when the thing beneath it mattered most. "South gate, third hour. The ferryman at the Corpse Docks owes me a favor older than this city. He takes us downriver past the salt marshes to a coast no Office map has ever charted. There are islands there, Wren. Old ones. Places where the covenant's reach thinned to nothing even before the Sundering. On those islands, a god can be only what he is—no host required, no anchor, no mortal vessel slowly hollowed out to hold what she was never built to hold." He stepped closer. "You'd live. You'd sleep whole nights. You'd grow old as yourself, not as a door."

"And the grove burns behind us."

"The grove is already dying. It died the day the covenant broke—you've seen it, stone trees weeping sap for a century. What happens tomorrow happens whether you stand in front of it or a hundred leagues away." His hands rose, hovered near her shoulders, did not land. "I am offering you the only honest thing I have ever offered anyone. Not power. Not victory. *Distance.* Survival without spectacle. I have watched heirs walk into fires for four thousand years, and I am telling you: the fire does not care how right you are."

Wren looked past him, at the campfires, and let herself actually consider it. That was the strange mercy of the night—there was still time to be a coward, and no one would ever know but the two of them and the river. She thought of sleeping whole nights. Of growing old as herself. Of a coast with no maps.

Then she thought of Tam's burned hands learning bandages. Of the elder Sefa pressing her grandmother's token into her palm in the dark of the warrens, saying *it waited for you*. Of Marrec beneath his coin of light, handing her a question instead of begging for his life. Of the heart-tree, black and waiting, holding every prayer ever unanswered inside its rings.

"No," she said.

"Wren—"

"No." She said it more gently the second time. "You're right that the fire doesn't care. That's exactly why it has to be me. Because if everyone who understands the cost walks away from it, the cost gets paid by people who never got to choose." She reached up and closed his hovering hands in hers. They were warm. Beggar-warm, human-warm, and she wondered—not for the first time—how much of his tenderness was strategy, and decided, not for the first time, that she would keep choosing to believe in it until it became true or destroyed her. "Come with me instead. Not as my god. As my witness. If I'm going to stand in front of an army and dictate terms to heaven, I want one soul there who remembers every word."

Something moved through his face—too fast to name, too slow to miss. "You ask a god to take notes."

"I ask a liar to stop lying," she said. "Start with the smallest things. It's good practice."

He laughed—a short, startled sound, real as a sneeze—and bowed his head over their joined hands like a man accepting a sentence. Or a gift. With him, she was learning, the two wore the same shape.

* * *

Dawn came gray and smelling of smoke that had not yet been lit.

Wren stood on the ridge above the Weeping Wood while her people gathered below her in the half-light—freed believers and deserters, market women with luck-charms knotted into their aprons, soldiers who had walked away from the pyre-wagons in the night, Tam with his bandaged hands and Sefa with her cane and four hundred others who had no business facing two hundred picked men and a wall of fire except that they had chosen to. Below, the petrified trees caught the first light and wept their slow amber tears, and beyond them, at the tree line, the Inquisitors' torches were moving into position like embers rolling uphill.

Ashen-Vey stood at her shoulder. Marrec's warning lay in her chest like a swallowed stone: *two hundred men, cold iron, a rite to salt the earth.*

She unbound her hair, took out her grandmother's token, and began to walk down toward the burning.
