## 16. The Burning of the Grove

The smoke reached them before the fire did.

Wren stood at the tree line with the dawn grey behind her and the Weeping Wood rising black and patient ahead, its petrified branches silvered with sap that had wept for a hundred years without anyone to wipe it away. She had imagined this moment so many times in the last three days—marching, arguing, praying—that she had thought herself prepared for it. But nothing in her imagining had included the smell. Burning stone did not smell like burning wood; it smelled like the inside of a kiln, like something ancient being made into something useful, and beneath it, faintly sweet, the caramel stink of sap catching flame.

"They fired the western edge first," said Tam Vossler, one of the deserters, squinting through the morning haze. He was a broad man with a soldier's flat way of speaking, and he had not stopped cleaning his sword since midnight, though it had been clean for hours. "Wind's out of the west. They want it to burn toward the heart-tree."

"Of course they do," Wren said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "You don't leave a wound half-cauterized."

Behind her, along the road and spilling into the ditches, stood what remained of her army of the overlooked. Three hundred souls, perhaps fewer now after the night's desertions—freed believers from the Bellows Street congregation still bruised from their cells, soldiers who had walked away from their commissions with only what they carried, market women who had bought her luck-charms for coppers and never asked where the luck came from. Old Maren was there, who had once paid Wren two bits for a charm against her husband's cough and had sworn ever after that it worked. The cooper's daughter. Two boys from the tannery rows who couldn't have been more than fifteen.

They had come because she had asked. That was the part that sat in Wren's chest like a swallowed stone. Not because a god commanded it, not because a law required it—because a debt-bound scribe with ash on her sleeves had stood in the smuggler warrens and said *the grove is going to burn and I mean to stand in it*, and three hundred people had decided that was worth dying over.

She could hear the Inquisitors' pyre-wagons even now, groaning somewhere inside the smoke, oil sloshing in their great iron tanks.

"Wren." Ashen-Vey stood at her shoulder, wearing his beggar's body like a coat that no longer fit him. Since the night she had seen him clearly—since she understood that his mercy had always been strategy—he had changed. Or rather, he had stopped pretending, and what remained was stranger and more honest: a god stripped of leverage, walking beside the woman he had tried to use, because there was nowhere else for either of them to go. "There is another way."

"There isn't."

"The heart-tree will call to you whether you stand in Corvale or at the wood's edge. Distance means nothing to what you carry. Let the Office have their bonfire. Let them think they've won. And when the last of the grove is ash—"

"—I walk into an empty field and speak to a dead tree." Wren shook her head. "No. I've read enough ledgers to know how this works, Vey. A covenant is sealed in witness. If I renew it over ashes, alone, with no one to see—" She looked back at the ragged line of believers, at Maren's weathered face, at the tannery boys trying not to look afraid. "Then it's just my word against theirs. Again. For another hundred years. It has to happen here, while the grove is still alive enough to answer. While there are eyes to see it done."

Ashen-Vey was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped into something almost human. "People are going to die today, Wren Thornbury."

"I know."

"And you will carry every one of them."

"I know that too." She turned to face him fully. His eyes were the color of banked coals, and for once there was no calculation in them—or if there was, it was buried so deep that even he had lost track of it. "You told me once that during the Godswar you argued for mercy, and they cast you down for it. I believed you meant it as confession. Maybe it was. But here's what I know about mercy, Vey: it isn't mercy if you spend other people to buy it. So today I'm not spending them. I'm asking. And every soul behind me gets to choose."

* * *

They chose.

The Inquisitors' line came out of the western smoke near midmorning—two hundred men in ash-grey cloaks, pikes leveled, behind the pyre-wagons with their hoses of burning oil. At their head rode a figure Wren recognized from Marrec's warning sent by a trembling novice: High Inquisitor Serane Callow, whose sermons on the Godswar were required reading in every schoolroom, whose voice could make a mob feel righteous.

Callow did not parley. She did not offer terms or demand surrender. She simply raised one gloved hand, and the wagons opened their throats, and fire came down the slope toward the tree line like a river deciding to flow uphill.

"Hold the ditch!" Tam bellowed, and the line of the overlooked braced itself in the dry drainage cut before the trees, shields scavenged from barracks stores, farm scythes, kitchen knives wrapped in leather.

What followed was not a battle. Battles have shape, movement, meaning. This was a wall of people standing between a fire and a forest, and the fire did not care about courage. The first volley of burning oil arced over the ditch and landed among the trees, and the Weeping Wood began to scream.

Wren felt it before she heard it—a sound below hearing, a vibration in her teeth, in the scar of her debt-brand, in the marrow of her. The grove was not silent anymore. A hundred years of petrified patience were ending all at once, and the trees were waking into pain.

*Hold,* something said, in a voice like sap moving under bark. *Hold, little heir. We can be reborn from anything but abandonment.*

The second volley fell short of the trees and into the ditch.

Wren would remember the rest of that hour in fragments for the rest of her life, each one lit too brightly, like pages held too close to a candle. Tam Vossler raising his shield over the tannery boys and taking the burning oil across his back, standing anyway, standing until his legs went, still standing somehow, propped between two believers who would not let him fall. Old Maren singing—the harvest hymn, the one every child in Corvale learned without knowing it was a prayer—and her voice cracking and continuing anyway. The cooper's daughter dragging a wounded Inquisitor out of the ditch because he was someone's son and she could not watch him burn, and the man weeping, and not fighting her.

And everywhere, everywhere, the fire climbing the white trunks, and the sap running down the bark like tears, and catching, so that the whole grove seemed to weep flame.

"Go," Tam rasped at her, when she knelt beside him with her hands pressed uselessly to the ruin of his shoulders. His teeth were gritted; his eyes were clear. "You're the heir. Go to the tree. We're holding the line, aren't we? Tell me we're holding the line."

"You're holding it," Wren said, and her voice broke on it.

"Then go, girl. Go write us down in whatever book you're writing." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man who had cleaned his sword all night knowing exactly why. "Make it a good entry."

* * *

She ran into the burning grove.

The heat hit her like a hand. Smoke tore at her eyes and throat, and the light was wrong—not orange but white, the white of a forge, of a page consumed. Petrified branches crashed down around her, shedding centuries in burning shards. She ran with her sleeve over her mouth and the covenant-token clutched in her fist, her grandmother's token, warm as a living thing, and she ran toward the heart-tree because there was nowhere else in the world left to run.

Halfway there, the grove caught her.

Not the fire—the grove. The dying magic, flooding free as the trees burned, poured into her like water finding a breach in a dam. She staggered, fell to her knees on ground hot as a griddle, and the prayers came.

Every prayer ever unanswered in the Weeping Wood. That was what the grove had been holding all these years, she understood then—not magic, not power, but *listening*. A hundred years of prayers whispered to silence, stored in sap and stone because there was nowhere else for them to go, and now the vessels were burning and everything they held was pouring into the last vessel left: her.

A mother praying over a fevered child in the year of the Sundering. *Please. Please, I'll give anything.* A farmer praying for rain until his voice gave out. A girl praying not to be sold, praying it over and over in words so small they barely qualified as words. Soldiers praying. Thieves praying. Ten thousand voices, fifty thousand, a century of the unheard, and they came into Wren all at once and she screamed, or she thought she screamed, though she could not hear herself over them.

This was what the gods had walked away from. This was what the ban had been built on top of, like a road built over graves. Not wickedness. Not the Godswar's forged history. Just this: the accumulated weight of everyone who had ever needed something and been answered with law instead of love.

Grief went through her like a blade and came out the other side as fuel. She understood that transformation now, in the white heart of the smoke—understood it the way the grove understood it, the way her grandmother must have understood it when she set her name against heaven. Grief was not the opposite of strength. Grief was what strength was made of, when it was honest.

Wren rose to her feet.

She walked the last hundred yards to the heart-tree through a corridor of falling fire, and the prayers carried her—she let them, she *used* them, every unanswered plea becoming a step, becoming breath, becoming the fierce unbearable forward motion of a woman who refused to let any of it have been for nothing.

The heart-tree stood at the grove's center, vast beyond sense, its trunk wide as a chapel, already crowned in flame—but at its base, impossibly, a seam of living green still glowed where the roots went down into the dark. The old door. The last door.

Wren pressed her palm and her grandmother's token against the bark, into the weeping sap, into the heat, and spoke—not a prayer, not anymore. Terms.

"I am Wren Thornbury," she said, "last heir of the Weeping Wood, and I am calling the covenant due. Hear me, gods of the broken word. The door is open. Come and answer for yourselves—or lose it forever."

The fire roared. The seam of green flared bright as a struck match.

And out of the burning dark behind her, wings of ash and light began to descend.
