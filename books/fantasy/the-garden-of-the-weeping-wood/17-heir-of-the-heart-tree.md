## 17. Heir of the Heart-Tree

The heart-tree had burned for three days before the fire finally let it go.

Wren stood in the ash-field where the Weeping Wood had been, and the silence was worse than the screaming had been. All through the night the trees had wept as they died—sap boiling from a thousand wounds, hissing into steam, running down blackened trunks like the grove itself was grieving out loud. Now there was only smoke hanging low over the ground, and the smell of it, sweet and terrible, like incense made from something that had once been alive.

Her hands were burned. She could not remember when that had happened. Somewhere between the third line of believers and the moment the fire broke—somewhere between Sera's scream and the soldier who had thrown himself across a child and not gotten up again—the pain had stopped registering as hers. It sat on her skin now like a borrowed coat.

Behind her, the survivors knelt in the ash. Forty-one of them, maybe forty-three; she couldn't make her eyes count straight. They had come with pitchforks and kitchen knives and one rusted pike from a war nobody remembered, and they had held the line against Inquisitor flame-canters long enough for her to walk into the burning grove, and most of them were dead, and she was not, and she did not yet know what to do with the arithmetic of that.

*Walk,* Ashen-Vey had said, when the flames rose up like a wall taller than the city walls. *You are the door. Fire cannot close what you open.*

So she had walked. And the fire had parted—not gently, not kindly, but the way water parts around a stone—and she had walked through a cathedral of light and heat and dying prayer, and at the center of it all the heart-tree had stood, vast and silver-barked even as it burned, and it had looked at her.

She was sure of that now, standing in its ashes. Trees do not have faces. But the heart-tree had looked at her, and what it had shown her in that last moment—before it collapsed inward like a sigh—was every prayer ever spoken beneath its branches and never answered. Thousands of years of them. A mother begging rain. A boy begging his father back from the Godswar. A girl begging to be seen. They had poured into Wren like floodwater into a cracked vessel, and she had carried them out of the fire because someone had to, because no one else was left to carry them.

They were still in her. She could feel them pressing against the inside of her ribs, patient and enormous, like a congregation waiting for a service to begin.

"Wren."

Ashen-Vey's voice, behind her. She did not turn. He came around into her sight anyway, moving the way he always moved, like smoke deciding to be a man. The beggar's body he wore was scorched at the hem, ash-gray to the elbow, but his eyes were the same as they had been in the ruined shrine: old gold, banked fires, watching everything.

"They're coming," he said.

"I know." She could feel them too—a pressure on the horizon of things, like weather. "How many?"

"All of them. Every god who was cast down or fled or sealed behind the Sundering. Every one who has felt the door crack open." His voice was very quiet. "You should understand what is about to stand in front of you, Wren. What you hold in you—the prayers, the grove's last strength—it is the key. But keys can be taken."

"By them?"

"By me," he said, and there it was again, that terrible honesty of his, offered always at the moment it cost him most. "I told you once I argued for mercy during the Godswar. That was true. I have not lied to you, Wren Thornbury. But I have let you believe things about me that were comfortable, and I am done letting you. If you fall, if you fail, if you die with that power unclaimed—I will take it. I will take it and I will use it, and I will tell myself afterward that it was mercy. Do you hear me? I need you to know exactly what stands beside you before they arrive."

Wren looked at him for a long moment. The smoke drifted between them.

"You're telling me this now," she said, "because you want me to refuse you. You want me to bind you before they get here, so that when they offer you your old seat among them, you'll have an excuse to stay."

Something flickered across the god's face—wonder, maybe, or grief. "You see too much for a scribe."

"I was a debt-bound scribe for seven years. I learned to read what people owed each other. It's the same skill." She flexed her burned fingers. "I'm not binding you, Ashen-Vey. Not yet. I want you to watch what I do, and then I want you to choose—with everything known—in front of witnesses. That's the whole point of what's coming. So stand beside me and be witnessed."

He bowed his head. For a heartbeat he looked less like a god wearing a beggar than like a beggar wearing a god, and she thought: *that's the true shape of him, maybe. Both at once. Neither.*

Then the horizon answered.

It began as a sound below hearing—a vibration in the ash, in her teeth, in the dead ground itself. Then the air at the edge of the burned grove thickened, the way water thickens before it freezes, and they came through it.

Not walking. *Arriving.* One moment the field was empty; the next it was full of them, ranks upon ranks of presences wearing shapes borrowed from mortal memory—a woman crowned in wheat, a tall figure whose face was a doorway, something vast and jointed that wore the idea of a storm like a cloak. There were hundreds. Some were beautiful beyond bearing. Some were beautiful the way a landslide is beautiful. Between them walked lesser powers, spirits and saints and half-forgotten household gods, and above them all hung a pressure like the sky leaning down.

The god-host of the Sundering, returned to the world at last.

And at their head, a figure Wren knew. Not from memory—from the visions the grove had given her. Veyra-of-the-Roots, first among the old covenant, the one who had set her seal to the terms her grandmother had countersigned two hundred years ago. She wore a body like carved birchwood, and her eyes were green as the wood had been before it petrified, and when she spoke, her voice arrived inside Wren's skull without troubling the air.

*Little heir. You opened the door. Step aside, and be honored.*

"No," Wren said.

The word fell into the ash-field and lay there, small and enormous.

Around the kneeling survivors, heads lifted. Marrec was somewhere behind her—she'd seen him in the fighting, bare-handed, dragging wounded out of the flame-line—but she didn't look for him. She kept her eyes on Veyra.

*You misunderstand,* the goddess said, and her voice was gentle, which was somehow worse. *We do not come to conquer. We come home. The covenant bound us out; the covenant can bring us in. Its heir stands before us. Speak the words of welcome, Wren Thornbury, daughter of the Wood, and all of this—* the host rippled, a wave of glory passing down its ranks *—is yours to command.*

"There it is," Wren said. Her voice shook, and she let it. Let them hear that she was afraid. Let them hear that she was afraid and speaking anyway. "That's the second time someone has offered me command of something enormous in exchange for just signing my name. The first time, I was nineteen, and Magister Halev put a debt-contract in front of me and told me it was standard terms. I signed. It took me seven years to learn what I'd actually agreed to." She took a breath. The prayers in her chest stirred, thousands of them, listening. "So before anyone signs anything today, we're going to read the original document. All of it. Out loud."

*There is nothing to read,* Veyra said. *The covenant is broken. Burned with your grove.*

"The covenant was *written*," Wren said. "Written by my grandmother, Maren Thornbury, Witness-scribe of the Weeping Wood, in the year the Godswar ended. And I have read it. Every clause. Including the ones you've spent two hundred years hoping no mortal would find." She reached into her coat and drew out the pages Halev had pressed into her hands as he died—water-stained, fire-edged, but whole. The original terms. "Clause nine, Veyra. Shall I read it? Or do you remember it?"

The god-host went still. Not quiet—still, the way a forest goes still when a predator moves through it.

*That clause was set aside by common accord—*

"It was set aside by *your* accord," Wren said. "*Yours.* Clause nine is the mortal veto. Any term of the covenant may be refused by the mortal signatory or her heirs, and no divine power may compel assent. Your own law, older than the Sundering, older than the ban, written in your own hand—" she turned the page toward the host, though she doubted they needed to see it "—and you never honored it. Not once. When mortals said *no more war*, you made war. When they said *answer our prayers*, you grew silent and called it teaching us self-reliance. And when the covenant strained past breaking, you blamed *us*. You let the Sundering happen. You let the Inquisitors build a hundred years of law on top of your broken promise, and you stayed comfortably outside it all, waiting for some poor debt-bound girl to burn a hole in the wall for you."

*You presume—*

"I *presume*?" The prayers in her chest rose with her voice, and the ash-field trembled. She felt them move through her—every unanswered prayer the grove had ever held—and when she spoke again, it was not entirely her own voice. It was a mother begging rain. It was a boy begging his father back. It was ten thousand voices saying *we asked, and you did not come.* "You want to talk about presumption? You stood outside a locked door for two centuries while people starved on the other side of it, praying to *you*, and now the door is open and your first word is *welcome me*?"

The pressure of the host bore down on her like deep water. Beside her, she felt Ashen-Vey go taut—not afraid for himself, she realized. Afraid for her. Ready, if it came to it, to spend whatever he was to buy her a moment.

She put her hand back, briefly, against his sleeve. *Stand witness,* she didn't say. He heard it anyway.

"Here is how it will be," Wren said, and the shaking had gone out of her voice now, and what remained was the flat, precise cadence of a scribe reading terms aloud—which was, after all, the only authority she had ever actually owned. "The covenant is not broken. It is *in default*. There's a difference, and every god here knows contract-law older than this continent, so don't pretend otherwise. Default means the injured party sets new terms. I am the injured party. I am the heir. I am"—she spread her burned hands, and the ash on them caught the gray light—"the last door you have. Because look around you. Look at what's left of your way back into the world. One burned tree. One tired scribe. Forty-odd witnesses kneeling in the ash who watched you do *nothing* while this grove burned."

She turned slowly, letting her gaze pass down the ranks of the host, and the host—she saw it clearly, and marveled—even the host shifted under being looked at.

"So here are your choices," she said. "Hear my terms, negotiate them in good faith, in front of mortal witnesses, bound by clause nine and by every law of covenant you yourselves wrote—or turn away now, and the door closes. Not burns. *Closes.* I will take every prayer in me, every spark of this grove, and I will seal it so thoroughly that your names become stories, and the stories become nothing, and mortals learn to answer each other. You will be free, and irrelevant, forever." She paused. "Some of you might prefer that. Think carefully. But understand: those are the options. Welcome is not on the table. Worship is not on the table. *Terms* are on the table."

Silence. The vast, ringing silence of several hundred immortal beings recalculating.

Then, from far back in the host, a ripple of laughter—low, incredulous, delighted. A small god, a household thing shaped like a fat cat with human hands, nudged its neighbor and whispered something that traveled down the ranks like wind through wheat. Wren caught fragments of it. *A scribe. She's a scribe. She's out-claused us. Two thousand years of divinity and we've been out-claused by a debt-girl.*

Veyra-of-the-Roots regarded her for a long, long moment. The birchwood face gave away nothing, but the green eyes had changed—narrowed, appraising, and beneath the appraisal something that Wren recognized, because she had seen it in Magister Halev's face once, and in the moneylender's, and in her own reflection on bad nights:

Respect. The grudging kind. The kind extended to an equal.

*You know,* the goddess said at last, *when Maren Thornbury wrote clause nine, I argued against it. I told her mortals would use it to make themselves tyrants.* A pause. *She told me that if they did, it would be the first tyranny in history the powerful hadn't written themselves. I have thought about that argument for two hundred years.*

"And?" Wren said.

*And,* Veyra said, *you are standing in the ashes of your inheritance, burned, outnumbered, holding a dead woman's paperwork, and you have not flinched. Perhaps she was right about your line. Perhaps she was right about all of you.* The goddess turned her head, and her voice rang out across the host, formal now, ritual-old: *The heir of the Weeping Wood invokes the mortal veto. By the law older than law, the host must hear terms or withdraw. Who among you disputes it?*

No one disputed it. Wren watched the silence travel the ranks and understood, with a strange lurch, that she had won—not the war, not even the negotiation, but the *standing*. They had to listen now. Whatever happened next, they had to stand in the ash and listen to a mortal read terms aloud, and that alone was more than any human being had wrung from the divine since the world was young.

Ashen-Vey exhaled beside her, slow and careful, like a man setting down something breakable.

"Well fought," he murmured.

"Don't celebrate yet," Wren said. "Now comes the hard part." She looked down at the pages in her hand, then at the kneeling witnesses, then at the host of heaven arrayed against a field of ash—and she felt, rising in her chest beneath the weight of ten thousand prayers, something that was almost, absurdly, joy.

"Now," she said, "we write the new covenant. And gods help you all—you're going to do it *my* way."

She uncapped the ink-stained stub of pencil she had carried through fire, and turned to a clean page.

* * *

The first term took until dusk.

Wren had expected fury. She had expected bargaining, threats, the whole ancient machinery of divine pride grinding against her clause by clause. What she had not expected was how *hungry* they were. Gods who had spent two centuries outside the world wanted back in so badly that they argued like merchants at closing time—over thresholds, over consent, over the exact wording of *witness*. The wheat-crowned woman wept openly when Wren proposed that no prayer go unheard, and demanded, as her price for agreeing, the right to personally hear every prayer for rain in the western valleys. The storm-shaped thing wanted jurisdiction over sailors' oaths. The cat-god wanted, explicitly and in writing, offerings of cream, and saw no shame in it whatsoever.

It was Veyra who fought her, and Veyra who mattered.

*Consent,* the goddess said, over and over, as the light failed and the witnesses built small fires in the ash. *You ask that magic answer only by consent. But mortals pray in desperation, little heir. Desperation consents to anything. A drowning man consents to anything. Your consent-clause will become a new chain, and the desperate will forge it themselves.*

"I know," Wren said quietly. "That's why the terms include Witnesses. Mortal scribes, trained and sworn, present whenever a covenant-power answers a prayer—to record what was asked, what was granted, and what it cost. Nothing divine happens in the dark anymore. Not for us. Not for you." She met the green eyes. "You think I haven't thought about desperation? I sold luck-charms to desperate mothers for seven years. I know exactly what desperation will sign. That's why the signature isn't enough. That's why there's a witness. My grandmother understood that. It took me twenty-six years and one burned forest to catch up to her."

Veyra was silent for a long time. Around the ash-field, the small fires crackled, and the god-host waited, and the mortal witnesses—Marrec among them, his face lit orange and unreadable—waited with them.

*Terms accepted in principle,* the goddess said finally. *Detail tomorrow. And little heir—one matter remains before this host sleeps.* Her gaze slid sideways, deliberate, to where Ashen-Vey stood at Wren's shoulder, silent through all the long bargaining. *Your ally. The exile. Under the old law, a god who returns to the world must declare his standing before the host: free, bound, or anchored. He has watched you claim the veto. He has watched you wield it. And he has not declared.*

Every head among the host turned. Every mortal witness turned too.

Ashen-Vey stepped forward into the firelight, and Wren saw that his hands, for the first time since she had known him, were not quite steady.

"Yes," he said. "I have something to declare."
