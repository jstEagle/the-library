## 20. Sap and Seedling

The first green thing Wren saw that spring was a weed.

She had come to the grove before dawn, as she did most mornings now, when the ash lay soft and grey as unmarked paper and the city behind her was still only a rumor of bells. She had learned, in the months since the burning, to walk the Weeping Wood without weeping herself—mostly. The charred trunks still stood in their hundreds like a congregation struck mid-prayer, black fingers against a paling sky. But at the base of the smallest of them, right where the root flared into the soil, a single blade of something ordinary and stubborn had pushed up through the crust of ash, bent under its own weight, and opened two leaves to the light.

Wren crouched over it for a long time.

It was nothing. A weed. In another year it would be a nuisance she'd pull from between flagstones. But her hands shook as she reached toward it, and she let them shake, because there was no one here to see, and because seven years of hiding had taught her that some tremors deserved their hour.

"You're early," said a voice behind her, dry as winter grass. "Even for you."

Ashen-Vey came down the burned avenue between the trees wearing the beggar's body he had never quite shed—the stooped shoulders, the patched coat, the face like a crumpled letter someone had tried to smooth and given up on. He moved carefully among the dead trunks now. He had told her once, in one of his rare unguarded hours, that walking this grove felt to him like walking through his own funeral, attended by everyone he had ever failed.

"It's growing," Wren said. She didn't look up. "Through the ash."

He lowered himself beside her with the groaning care of an old man, though he was neither old nor, strictly speaking, a man anymore—or rather, he was both, and less, and more; the covenant had seen to that. He studied the weed the way scholars study scripture.

"Ragwort," he said. "Mortals call it a weed. Bees call it breakfast." A pause. "The gods called such things beneath notice, once. We were wrong about a great many categories."

"We were," Wren agreed, and heard him take the correction without flinching. That was new too. That was spring.

* * *

The heart-tree stood at the center of the grove where it had always stood, but it was no longer a tree so much as a monument to having been one—a vast trunk hollowed by fire, its crown gone, its great limbs fallen and half-buried in their own ash. Yet it had not died. Wren had known it within days of the flames dying: a warmth in the char, a pulse slow as seasons. When she laid her palm against the burnt bark, she felt it thinking. Not words. Not yet. More the way a sleeper turns toward a voice calling from far off.

Today she had brought the token.

Her grandmother's covenant-token had traveled farther than Wren had, in its way—it had passed from the elder of the Bellows Street congregation into her hands in a cellar smelling of tallow and fear, and it had been in her pocket when she walked into the fire, and it had come out of the fire with her, which no one could explain and no one tried very hard to. It was a small disc of pale wood, older than any living tree in Corvale, carved with the interlocking rings that had once meant *witness*—the sign of the scribes who had recorded every prayer made under the old covenant, so that no god could claim ignorance and no mortal could claim silence.

Wren knelt at the base of the heart-tree and dug with her hands. The ash gave way to soil that was warm and dark and smelled, impossibly, of rain that hadn't fallen yet.

"Do you want to say anything?" Ashen-Vey asked. "Gods used to require speeches. I'm told mortals prefer them too."

"She's not listening," Wren said. "She's been listening for forty years. To everything. To all of it." She set the token into the earth, carved rings facing upward, and began to draw the soil back over it. "I'm not talking *to* her. I'm just—letting her hear the answer."

The answer being: it grew anyway. They burned it and it grew anyway.

She pressed the last of the soil down with both palms, the way she'd seen gardeners do in the market district, firm but not cruel, and sat back on her heels. The morning light came through the broken canopy in long slanted columns, catching the drifting ash so that the whole grove seemed full of slow white bees.

"Your grandmother," Ashen-Vey said quietly, "would have hated what they did to this place."

"Yes."

"And loved what's happening to it now."

"Yes." Wren wiped her hands on her skirt, leaving grey streaks she didn't mind. "That's the whole inheritance, isn't it? Grief and shoots. You don't get one without the other."

* * *

The Scriptorium had changed hands three times since the Reckoning of Names, and each change had left its sediment. The ledgers of debt were gone—burned publicly, by Marrec's own hand, in the plaza where he had once burned his commission—and in their place stood shelves of blank books bound in undyed leather, waiting.

Wren had been offered a desk. She had refused a desk. Desks, in her experience, were where careful lives went to be filed.

Instead she had claimed the old reading room on the ground floor, the one with the tall windows and the worn threshold, and she had filled it with simple things: a long table, benches, ink, paper by the ream, and a bell at the door that anyone might ring. Above the door, in letters she had painted herself, slightly crooked, slightly proud of being crooked:

**THE ORDER OF WITNESS — EVERY PRAYER RECORDED. NO PRAYER ANSWERED UNASKED.**

The second clause had taken her a week to settle on. The gods, bounded now by witness, could not act without a mortal's spoken consent—that was the law she had written into the covenant with the whole grove burning around her and Ashen-Vey's hand in hers. But laws, she had learned from watching Marrec, are only as honest as their doorways. If recording a prayer became a summons, people would stop praying. So the Witness-scribes wrote prayers down and did nothing else. The answering belonged to the gods and the consenting, together, or not at all.

The first month, almost no one came. Corvale remembered seven years of quiet and a lifetime of fear; fear outlasts most architecture. The second month, an old woman came in off Bellows Street, stood in the doorway for a long while, and finally said, "My husband's been dead eleven years and I still talk to him. Can you write that down?"

Wren had written it down. Every word. The old woman had read it over twice, wept a little, thanked her stiffly, and left without asking for anything more.

By the third month there was a line some mornings.

* * *

"Prayer of Tam Broadstairs, cooper, of Tanner's Row," Wren recited as she wrote, her pen moving in the quick clean hand of a woman who had spent seven years making small letters survive scrutiny. "Taken down the fourteenth day of Thawmonth, in the first year of the Witness. He asks—" she glanced up at the broad-shouldered man fidgeting on the bench across the table, hat crushed in his fists— "he asks that his daughter's fever break. He does not ask the gods to do it. He asks them to sit with her, if sitting is allowed, so she won't be frightened in the night. And he offers in return—" a pause, and the man mumbled, and Wren smiled— "he offers in return that he'll stop swearing at the rain."

"That's a fair trade," rumbled a voice from the corner, where Ashen-Vey sat in his beggar's shape doing the work he had assigned himself: listening. Under the new terms he held no power worth the name—he had chosen binding beside her over freedom without anchor, and Wren still turned that choice over in her hands some nights like a coin she suspected of being counterfeit and hoped wasn't—but he knew things. Old things. Which saints had been real, which omens were weather, which prayers had worked in the centuries before the Sundering and why.

"The rain doesn't care," Ashen-Vey told the cooper gravely. "But your daughter might notice the difference in you. Fevered children watch their fathers closer than fathers think."

Tam Broadstairs went out lighter than he'd come in, clutching a copy of his own prayer like a talisman—which, Wren reflected, was exactly what it was, and always had been. Paper didn't heal fevers. But a father who had said the frightening thing aloud, in a room where it was written down and witnessed and real, was a different father by nightfall.

When the door closed behind him, Ashen-Vey said, "You're good at this."

"I'm a scribe."

"You're the first person in a thousand years to build a doorway instead of a wall." He said it without flourish, which from him was the highest praise he owned. "Your grandmother built a covenant. You're building the habit of it. Habits outlive covenants. I've watched several die of neglect."

Wren blotted the page and set it in the growing stack. Each prayer was copied twice—one copy stayed in the reading room, open to anyone who wished to read what their neighbors carried; the other went to the grove, to be buried at the roots of the seedlings rising through the ash. Feed the trees with words, the elder of Bellows Street had suggested before she died in the winter, gently, in her sleep, having outlived at last the silence she'd prayed into for a generation. Trees remember. That was the whole of the old magic, Wren was beginning to understand. Not power. Memory, kept faithfully, tended like a garden.

* * *

Marrec came on a Thursday, in plain clothes, which fooled no one; he had the posture of a man who had spent his life standing at attention and had only recently been permitted to stop.

"The Office sends its regards," he said, taking the bench across from her like a man sitting down to confession. "And a request. There's a dispute in the Weavers' Quarter. Two households, one blessing—each claims the god of the loom promised the same good fortune to both. They've come to shouting. One of them has a knife."

"What does the Office want me to do about it?"

"Nothing. That's the point." His mouth twitched—not quite a smile, but the scar tissue of one. "Under the old law, we'd have arrested whoever prayed loudest. Under no law at all, they'd have settled it with the knife. The Office wants to know if the Order of Witness will hear the grievance. Both sides. Write it all down. Let them see their own words next to each other." He paused. "They want to know if the new thing works before they admit the old thing didn't."

Wren looked at him for a moment. Once, this man had traced her charm-work through the Scriptorium ledgers with kindness in his eyes and shackles in his hands. Once, she had been a name in his book. Now he sat in her reading room asking permission, and neither of them found it strange, and that strangeness-not-strange was perhaps the truest measure of how far the world had turned.

"Send them tomorrow," she said. "Both households. Separately first, then together. And send bread. People say truer things when they're fed."

"I'll note it in my report," Marrec said solemnly, "'the Order requests bread,' and watch the Archivist try to decide whether that's doctrine." He rose, then hesitated at the door. "Wren. The page I kept. The forged history. It's going into the public archive next month. Read aloud, annually, forever. I wanted you to hear it from me before you hear it from a herald."

"I know," she said softly. "Halev's name is in the margins. His hand corrected the forgery, thirty years ago, and was made to bury the correction." She smoothed the page in front of her. "I forgave the man, Marrec. I'm still learning to forgive the machine that taught him burying was love."

Marrec nodded slowly, the nod of a man adding a line to a ledger only he could see, and went out into the noise of the street.

* * *

That evening she walked back out to the grove with the day's copies of prayers bundled in oilcloth against her chest, as she did every evening, and found the ragwort had become six ragworts, and the six had become a scatter of green freckling the black earth all down the eastern row of dead trees. Further in, near the heart-tree, the first actual seedlings had come up—slender birch-whips, pale as candles, absurdly brave.

She buried the prayers one by one at the roots, pressing each folded page into the warm dark soil, saying nothing, because the trees already knew how to listen and she was learning, at last, how to stop performing grief and simply carry it.

At the heart-tree she stopped. The token she had planted in the thaw was no longer visible; the earth had closed over it, and from the exact spot, a shoot had risen that was not birch. Its leaves were a deep ringing green she had never seen in any market-garden, and when the evening wind moved through the ruined grove, the shoot's leaves wept a single bead of clear sap each, bright as glass, falling onto the ash.

Sap, in a seedling weeks old. Tears, in a tree that hadn't learned yet to be a tree.

Wren knelt and touched the wet leaf, and the warmth of the heart-tree pulsed up through her palm, slow and enormous, and this time—this time—it was not a sleeper turning. It was a voice, faint as a word remembered from childhood, forming itself out of the grove's long attention:

*Who writes for us now?*

Wren looked at the little weeping tree, at the hundreds of black pillars standing vigil around it, at the city lights kindling beyond the grove's edge where a reading room waited with its crooked painted sign and its bell for anyone to ring.

"Anyone who asks," she said aloud. "That's the terms. Anyone who asks, and everyone who listens."

The sap-bead fell. Somewhere in the dark soil beneath her knees, her grandmother's token turned its carved rings upward like an eye opening—and far off in the city, faint on the wind, Wren heard the bell above her own door begin to ring, and keep ringing, though the reading room had been locked and empty for an hour.

Someone was already coming to be heard.
