## 21. The City That Learned to Pray Aloud

The bell over the door of the old charm-stall had not rung in seven years, and when it rang now it sounded almost surprised at itself.

Wren heard it from across the market square and stopped mid-stride, a basket of early plums on her hip, and let herself simply look. The stall was hers—or had been hers, once, before her name was struck from every ledger in Corvale—and she had sold it to a round-faced woman named Bess Arden who had bought charms from her back when buying one could cost a family its standing. Bess had hung the luck-charms on strings of red thread where anyone could see them. She had painted a sign: *CHARMS, HONESTLY MADE. NO PRAYER REFUSED A HEARING.*

It was the last part that would have been unthinkable two years ago. It was the last part that made Wren's throat tighten every time she read it.

The morning was bright with the particular brightness of spring in Corvale, when the smoke of winter finally lifted off the rooftops and the river ran green instead of grey. The square was full. Not festival-full—Corvale did not yet quite trust itself to celebrate—but full the way a body is full of blood, quietly, everywhere at once. Fishwives shouting prices. A knife-grinder working his wheel. Two soldiers of the city watch, their uniforms patched but clean, eating fried dough and arguing about horses. And everywhere, threaded through all of it like sap through wood, the small commerce of the new covenant: charms sold openly, prayers spoken at normal volume, an old woman pressing her palm flat against the doorpost of the shrine that had reopened on Tanner's Row, as if checking whether the wood was warm.

Wren had learned to walk through her own city slowly these days. There was so much to see that she had spent seven years not seeing.

"Wren Thornbury." The voice came from behind the plum cart, wry and familiar. "Or should I say *Witness* Thornbury? I never know which name will make you flinch."

She turned. Marrec stood there in his new grey coat—the coat of the Order of Witness, unbleached wool, deliberately plain—holding two cups of cider he had clearly been waiting for an excuse to offer someone. He looked older than he had the night she'd visited his cell, which was fair; she supposed she looked older too. The burning of the grove had left marks on both of them that had nothing to do with skin.

"Neither," she said, taking a cup. "I'm the woman who wants plums at a fair price."

"Then you've come to the right tyrant." He nodded toward the far end of the square, where a small crowd had gathered around a raised wooden platform—not a scaffold, though everyone in Corvale still flinched a little at platforms, and Marrec knew it, and had insisted the Witness's bench be built low and open on all sides, more like a market stall than a judgment seat. "We have a dispute this morning. You'll want to hear it."

"A dispute?"

"A petty one." His eyes glinted. "Which is exactly why you'll want to hear it. Anyone can be solemn about the end of the world. It takes a healthy city to argue about a fence."

* * *

The dispute, as it turned out, was about a goat.

The complainant was a tanner named Odo Fenn, broad and sunburnt, who stood before the Witness's bench holding his hat in both hands like a man at a funeral. Beside him, equally aggrieved, stood a widow named Sera Quill, small and sharp-eyed, with her arms crossed and her chin set at an angle that suggested she had buried a husband and outlived three landlords and did not intend to lose to a tanner.

"The goat," Odo said, "ate my bark."

"Your bark was stacked against *my* wall," said Sera.

"My bark was stacked on the *lane*, which is common ground—"

"You stacked it leaning on my wall because your yard floods and you know it, and then your goat came over my fence—"

"*My* goat does not climb fences—"

"Then what, Master Fenn, do you propose ate it? A spirit?" A ripple of laughter went through the crowd, and something in the laughter caught: the word *spirit* no longer landed as a joke. It landed as a memory. People glanced at each other. The gods were awake; everyone knew it; nobody yet knew quite how to hold the knowledge in an ordinary mouth.

Marrec let the laughter settle before he spoke. Wren watched him do it—watched him wait, hands loose on the bench, giving the crowd time to become listeners again. He had been an Inquisitor for nineteen years. He had learned patience in a school that taught it with fire, and he had spent two years unlearning everything else that school taught, and what remained was this: a man who listened like listening was labor.

"Master Fenn. Did you see the goat cross the fence?"

"No, Witness."

"Did anyone?"

A boy near the front, maybe ten years old, raised his hand without waiting to be called on, the way children do. "I seen it. It stood on the wall first. Like this." The boy demonstrated, hunching and balancing, to general delight. "Then it jumped down and ate the bark and then it couldn't get back over and it panicked and knocked the whole stack into the flood-water."

"And where is the goat now?"

"In my parlor," said Sera Quill, with grim satisfaction. "Where it has eaten a chair leg."

The crowd roared. Even Marrec's mouth twitched. But Wren, standing at the edge of the gathering, felt the moment underneath the moment—the thing that made her chest ache in a way she could not entirely call pleasant. Two years ago this dispute would have ended with a bribe, or a grudge, or a knife in a dark lane, or silence. Two years ago there had been no bench to bring a goat to. There had been only the Inquisitors' Office, which heard grievances about magic and nothing else, and the debt-collectors, who heard about money and mercy about nothing.

Now a tanner and a widow stood in daylight and told the truth loudly enough for a hundred people to hear, trusting that the truth would be weighed.

That was the covenant. Not the words she had dictated at the charred heart-tree with ash in her hair and dead friends behind her—though those words mattered, they mattered enormously—but this. This ordinary, ridiculous, stubborn faith that a grievance could be spoken aloud and answered fairly. Magic answered by consent; gods walking bounded by witness; mortals keeping the veto. She had written those terms in the language of law because she had been a scribe, and law was the only prayer she had ever trusted. But the terms only lived if people brought their goats to the bench.

Marrec ruled: the fence was Sera's, the stacking was careless, the goat was Odo's responsibility, and the chair leg would be paid for in tanned leather, two hides' worth, delivered within the month. Both parties grumbled. Both parties accepted. And then—this was the part Wren had not expected, the part that still caught her off guard even after a year of mornings like it—Odo Fenn turned to the little roadside shrine at the square's edge, the one with the worn stone under a wooden canopy, and said, loudly, "And may the Small God of Boundaries keep better watch than I do," and pressed a copper into the offering bowl.

The Small God of Boundaries. One of the lesser names that had come drifting back since the renewal—gods so minor that no chronicle had ever recorded their fall, hedge-spirits and threshold-keepers who had survived the Sundering the way weeds survive a paving. Nobody knew if the copper did anything. Nobody knew if anything listened. But the bowl filled anyway, coin by coin, and the shrines stayed swept, and that, Wren thought, was also the covenant: not certainty, but the habit of reaching.

* * *

Bess Arden found her afterward at the plum cart.

"You're doing it again," Bess said, handing her a plum without being asked. "Standing in the middle of things looking like you're about to apologize."

"I don't apologize."

"You apologize constantly, you just do it silently, which is worse." Bess was one of perhaps four people in Corvale who spoke to Wren that way, and Wren treasured her for it the way one treasures a whetstone. "Come look at my stall. I want your opinion."

The stall had changed. Where the luck-charms had hung, Bess had built a second shelf, and on the shelf sat rows of small objects that made Wren go very still: knotted threads, wax seals stamped with tiny images, folded paper prayers sealed with a drop of candle-wax. Charm-work. Real charm-work, done openly, priced plainly, a list of what each item was for nailed to the frame in Bess's blunt handwriting.

"Bess." Wren picked up a knotted thread—a finding-charm, the tag said, *for lost things*. "Who taught you the knotting?"

"I did. Off the pamphlets the Order prints. And I practiced on my sister's cat, which is why we no longer own a cat." Bess shrugged. "I'm not good. I know I'm not good. But there's a girl down Bellows Street whose mother can't afford an Order-blessed charm, and the old way—the hiding way—is dying out, and somebody has to sell the cheap honest version or the cheap dishonest version will move in. So." She spread her hands. "Tell me what's wrong with my knots."

They spent an hour at it, Wren correcting a loop here, a binding-word there, and it was the strangest hour of her season: teaching charm-craft in open daylight, in a market, while a fishwife waited impatiently behind a customer and nobody hid anything. Twice Wren caught herself lowering her voice out of seven years of habit and had to raise it again on purpose, like stretching a muscle that had stiffened.

At the end, Bess held up the corrected finding-charm and squinted at it. "It feels different. Warmer."

"That's because it'll actually work now. Which means—" Wren tapped the tag, "—you change the price. Working charms cost more. That's not greed, that's honesty. And you register it with the Order of Witness, so if it goes wrong, someone hears about it."

"Consent and witness," Bess recited, half-mocking, wholly serious. "You know most people thought the new covenant would be some grand shining thing. Thunder on the mountain. Instead it's bookkeeping."

"Bookkeeping is how you love people at scale," Wren said, and Bess laughed so hard she had to sit down on her own stool.

But after Wren left the stall, walking back through the brightening square, the laugh stayed with her uneasily, the way a good day always did. Because Bess was right, underneath the joke. People had wanted thunder. Some of them still did. She saw it in the faces at the shrines sometimes—not gratitude but appetite, the hungry look of folk who had prayed to silence for a generation and now wanted the silence to answer *loudly*, wanted wonders, wanted the old stories made flesh and marching. The great gods kept their distance; the covenant bound them to consent and witness, and mostly they honored it, and mostly the city was glad of quiet miracles. But there were corners of Corvale—she heard it from Marrec, from the other Witnesses—where someone was always asking why the gods didn't *do* more. Why the renewal hadn't fixed the floods, the debts, the deaths. As if divinity were a well that owed them buckets.

And in one or two corners, darker: the ones who whispered that the covenant was a cage, that Wren Thornbury had cheated the gods of their rightful freedom, that the true renewal was still coming and would sweep away benches and ledgers and witnesses alike. They said it in the taverns past midnight. They said it with the particular fervor of people who had never been burned.

Old fears lingered. New ones grew in the same soil. Wren had learned that peace was not the absence of this—it was the bench, the ledger, the copper in the bowl. The daily, unglamorous choosing.

* * *

In the afternoon, the children came to the heart-shrine.

There was a shrine now at the edge of the Weeping Wood itself—modest, timber-framed, built by volunteer hands on the ground where the pyre-wagons had once been staged—and on feast days the schoolmistress walked her pupils out from the city to leave offerings. Wren timed her walks to avoid them, usually. Today she failed.

They came streaming along the road like a spill of bright beads, twenty-odd children aged five to twelve, each carrying something: a ribbon, a button, a crust saved from breakfast, a drawing executed in charcoal on scrap paper. The schoolmistress, a harried woman named Pell, corralled them at the shrine rail with the air of a shepherd who has done this many times and been betrayed by sheep every time.

"Now," Mistress Pell said, "what do we remember about offerings?"

"They're asks, not demands!" shouted a small girl in front.

"They're *asks*, not demands," Pell corrected. "And what do we say if the ask isn't granted?"

"It might still be heard," the children chorused, in the flat sing-song of the thoroughly drilled, and Wren had to turn her face away toward the trees for a moment.

Because that was it. That was the whole covenant, taught to children in a sing-song, in two sentences. *Asks, not demands. It might still be heard.* She had spent seven years selling harmless charms to desperate people and calling it kindness, and here was a schoolmistress teaching six-year-olds the theology she had bled for, and making it rhyme.

The children filed past the rail. Most left their offerings quickly, the way children do anything ritual—fling, giggle, push. But one boy, small and solemn, maybe six, placed his charcoal drawing carefully upright against the stone and then stood there, frowning at it, dissatisfied.

"What's wrong, love?" asked Mistress Pell.

"It's s'posed to be for the tree," the boy said. "But it's just paper. Paper's not a real thing to give."

Wren crouched down beside him before she knew she meant to. Up close, the drawing resolved: a tree, black-trunked, with blue scribbles falling from its branches. Rain, or tears, or—she looked closer—sap. The child had drawn the Weeping Wood as it had been, as the pamphlets showed it, weeping.

"What's your name?"

"Tommic."

"Tommic. Do you know what the tree gave us, when it burned?"

He shook his head.

"It gave everything. Every prayer anyone ever made there and didn't get answered—it held all of them, all those years, and when it died it poured them out, and that's why the gods came back. Because the tree paid the debt." She touched the drawing, lightly, at the trunk. "So paper is exactly right. Paper is what scribes use. And the tree likes scribes."

His eyes went wide and serious. "Are you a scribe?"

"I am."

"Of the *tree*?"

"Of the tree," Wren said, and something passed over the child's face that she recognized with a start: reverence, unguarded, aimed at her. It was the same look she had once given Magister Halev, back when the Scriptorium was the tallest building she knew and its master the wisest man alive. She had loved Halev before she understood what he'd done, and forgiven him before she understood what forgiving would cost, and she had carried both the love and the forgiveness ever since, the way you carry a healed bone—aware of the weather in it.

Mistress Pell was watching her with careful neutrality. "Witness Thornbury. I didn't expect—"

"No one ever does. It's fine. I come to avoid crowds, which is difficult, as I am one."

The children were being marshaled back into line. Tommic lingered, tugging her sleeve. "Will the tree grow back? My da says it won't. He says burnt's burnt."

Wren looked past him, up the road, to where the Weeping Wood began—the petrified grove, two years greening now at its edges, shoots rising through ash each spring like slow applause. Burnt was burnt. Her grandmother's token lay planted at the heart-tree under a foot of new soil, and every spring she went and checked, and every spring there was nothing yet, and every spring she planted something else beside it and decided that this, too, was tending.

"Your da's right that burnt is burnt," she said. "But burnt ground is where the stubborn things grow best. Ask any gardener."

He considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then he dug in his pocket and produced, of all things, a plum stone—saved, apparently, against just such an occasion—and pressed it into her hand. "For the tree. When it's ready."

She closed her fingers around it. It was warm from his pocket, small and hard and utterly ordinary, and she found she could not speak for a moment, so she nodded instead, and Tommic nodded back, satisfied, and ran for the line.

* * *

She stayed until the light went long and gold, sitting on the shrine steps with the plum stone in her pocket, and that was where Ashen-Vey found her.

He wore the beggar's body still—he had never taken another, though the covenant would have permitted it, and when she had asked him why, once, he had said only, "This is the shape you met," which was either sentiment or strategy, and after everything she had decided she was allowed not to know which. He moved quietly for a god. He sat down beside her on the step without asking, the way he always did, close enough that his shoulder nearly touched hers and never quite did, and together they watched the shadows lengthen across the young green of the recovering grove.

"The city sounds different," he said at last.

"Different how?"

"Louder. In the old days, prayer was a whisper-trade. Everyone praying in cupboards, in cellars, ashamed. Now they pray in queues and over goats." His mouth curved. "A boundary-god got an offering today because of a fence dispute. Do you know how long it has been since anyone fed the Small Gods out of *affection* rather than terror?"

"Is that what it was? Affection?"

"It's what it sounded like from where I sat." He was quiet a moment. "Some of the elder names hate it. The noise. The smallness of it. They remember worship with thrones in it, and this—" he gestured at the market, the shrine, the whole modest evening, "—this looks to them like being kept as pets."

"And what does it look like to you?"

He took his time answering, which she had learned to respect; the lies were quick, the truths slow. "It looks like the thing I argued for," he said finally, "before the exile. Before I understood what I was arguing for. Mercy that costs the merciful something. Power that answers to the powerless." A pause. "It looks like losing, and it feels like winning, and I am still learning the difference."

Wren turned the plum stone over in her pocket. "Tommic gave me this. For the tree, when it's ready."

Ashen-Vey looked at the stone in her palm for a long moment, and something crossed his ancient borrowed face that she had learned to read as close to wonder as a god could safely come. "A seed," he said. "From a child who wasn't alive for the burning." He exhaled. "You understand that this is how it always begins again. Not with thrones. With a stone in a pocket."

"Is that a prophecy?"

"It's a memory," he said. "The oldest kind I have."

They sat as the light failed, and the lamps of Corvale came alight one by one across the valley, and somewhere in the city a bell rang the hour, and the sound rolled out over the roofs and the river and the young green edges of the Weeping Wood, and it was only a bell, marking only an hour, in a city that had learned—at least for today—to pray aloud.

But as they rose to walk back, Wren felt the plum stone shift in her pocket, warm as a held hand, and thought: *when it's ready.*

And far off, at the edge of hearing, beneath the bell and the market-noise and the evening birdsong, the ground itself seemed to give one faint, deep sigh—like something turning over in its sleep, like roots moving where no roots had moved in two years—and neither of them spoke of it, but both of them heard it.
