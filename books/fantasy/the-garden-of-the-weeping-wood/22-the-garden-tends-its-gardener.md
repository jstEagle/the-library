## 22. The Garden Tends Its Gardener

The Weeping Wood had learned to grow again the way a person learns to speak after a long illness—haltingly at first, then all at once, as though it had only been waiting for permission.

Wren walked beneath its branches on a morning in late spring, when the sap ran gold and slow down the trunks like honey poured from a tilted jar. Ten years since the burning. Ten years since she had stood in the heart of the fire with every unanswered prayer in the world pouring through her like light through a cracked bell. The trees that rose around her now were not the ancient petrified sentinels of the old covenant—those had died in the Inquisitors' pyre-wagons' wake—but their children, grown from seed and ash and stubbornness, and they wept still. That was the thing people from outside never understood until they came here. The wood wept even in joy. It wept because remembering was its nature, and it remembered everything.

She had stopped minding the wet paths years ago. Her boots were oiled leather now, made by a cobbler in Corvale who traded charms for shoe repair and called it fair. Her hair had gone silver at the temples early—Ashen-Vey claimed it was the grove's doing, that the heart-tree had marked her the way it marked everything it loved—and she wore it braided back with a cord woven from willow bark, because some habits outlive their reasons.

Beside her, Ashen-Vey walked with his hands clasped behind his back and his face turned up toward the canopy, catching drips of sap on his brow like a man receiving an anointing he no longer believed himself entitled to.

He was strange still. He would always be strange. But the strangeness had changed its flavor. Once, when he had been exiled and burning and full of the old power, people had flinched from him without knowing why—the way animals flinch before a storm breaks. Now they simply looked twice. His eyes were still the color of banked coals, amber shot through with something older than fire, but they did not glow anymore unless he wanted them to, and mostly he did not want to. His body, which had once been a beggar's disguise and then briefly a god's terrible glory, had settled into something merely human-shaped: lean, weathered, gray-streaked at the jaw, with hands that were good at whittling and terrible at darning.

"You're smiling," he said, without looking at her.

"I'm allowed."

"You're smiling *at* me, which is different, and usually precedes an accusation."

"Can't a woman smile at her—" She paused on the word. They had never settled on one. *Husband* implied paperwork neither of them trusted. *Companion* sounded like a dog. *God* was ten years dead and buried under the ash of the old covenant. "At her Ashen-Vey," she finished, "without it being an accusation?"

"He can." A drop of sap caught in his eyelashes and he blinked it away, unbothered. "But you've been wearing that particular smile since the second bend in the path, and I know your smiles, Wren Thornbury. That one means you've noticed something I haven't, and you're letting me walk into it."

"Nothing so dramatic." She let the silence stretch just long enough to make him suspicious, which was, she admitted privately, one of the small cruelties she permitted herself these days, and enjoyed. "There's someone coming up the pilgrim road. Been following us since the gatehouse. Young. Nervous. Trying very hard to sound like she's admiring the trees rather than eavesdropping on two people talking about her."

Ashen-Vey's step didn't falter, but something in the set of his shoulders did—a tension that was habit more than fear, the residue of centuries spent being hunted by things worse than mortals. "Inquisitor?"

"There are no Inquisitors anymore. There are Witnesses, and Witnesses knock first." Wren glanced back through the green lattice of branches. "No. Look at her sleeves."

He looked. The girl—she could not have been more than nineteen, maybe twenty, thin in the way of people who rationed their own meals to stretch a wage—had come around the bend at last, close enough to be seen properly. Her coat was Scriptorium-issue, the plain gray wool that Corvale's debt-bound scribes had worn for generations, patched at the elbows with thread that didn't match. And on her left wrist, where the sleeve had ridden up as she reached to touch a weeping bough, the brand showed: a small circle bisected by a line, inked into skin with the cold precision of the Ledger Office.

A debt-mark. Seven years' servitude, or five, or nine—impossible to tell from here. Wren knew the shape of it better than her own handwriting. She had spent seven years hiding hers beneath a scribe's cuff, pressing her sleeve down whenever she handed a charm across a market stall, flinching from every glance that lingered too long on her wrist.

The girl saw them see her, and froze mid-stride like a rabbit that has heard the hawk but not yet located it.

"It's all right," Wren called gently. "We don't bite. Well. He might, metaphorically. He's very fond of metaphors."

"That was one time," Ashen-Vey said with dignity, "and the metaphor was load-bearing."

The girl laughed—a startled, involuntary sound, quickly smothered, as if laughter itself were something she'd been billed for. Wren knew that laugh. She had worn that laugh for seven years, in a rented room above a chandler's shop, counting coins by candlelight and telling herself that smallness was safety, that if she only wanted little enough, the city would let her keep what little she had.

"Come here," Wren said. "You've walked a long way to stand at a distance. You may as well get the whole journey."

* * *

Her name was Tamsin Reeve, and she told her story the way debt-bound people always told stories: quickly, defensively, with the facts arranged so that none of them could be mistaken for complaint.

She had been bound at fourteen, when her father's fishing boat went down off the Graywater shoals and the family's creditors came calling. The Scriptorium had bought her debt the way the Scriptorium bought everything—cheaply, efficiently, without malice or mercy, because it was incapable of either. Six years she had copied ledgers, contracts, and confession transcripts in the great cold hall where Magister Halev had once ruled, and in six years she had done nothing wrong except one thing.

"She wrote a charm," Ashen-Vey said softly. It wasn't a question. He had heard a thousand such confessions across a thousand years, back when hearing them had been his office and his burden.

"A luck-charm." Tamsin's chin came up, defiant and terrified at once, and Wren recognized that too—the posture of a person who had decided, somewhere on the long road here, that she would rather be punished than apologize. "For my mother. She's ill. The physicians cost more than we ever owed, and the prayers—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "The prayers weren't going anywhere. Everyone says they go nowhere. Everyone says that's just how it is. But I'd read the old accounts, the ones in the restricted shelves, the ones with the marginalia that nobody's bothered to censor because nobody reads them. And I thought—if the covenant's real now, if there's really a Witness-scribe and the gods answer by consent, then maybe a prayer written down properly, in the old forms, might actually—" Her voice cracked. "It worked. That's the whole crime of it. I wrote my mother a charm against wasting sickness, and it worked, and the Ledger Office found out, and they said I'd practiced unsanctioned craft while debt-bound, which doubles the term, and they were going to transfer me to the penitent workshops in Harrowgate, which is—" She stopped again, and this time she couldn't restart. "Which is where people go and don't come back from."

The wind moved through the young trees, and the sap wept gold down the pale bark, and for a moment none of them spoke.

"They gave you three days' leave to settle your affairs," Wren said. "And instead of settling them, you walked to the Wood, because you'd heard there was someone here who'd been where you are."

Tamsin nodded. Then, with the desperate honesty of the truly cornered: "I heard you erased your own debt. That you rewrote the covenant so magic answers to consent, and that you—you undid your own name in the records. I heard the Office had to annul you because there was no life left to owe anything." Her eyes were wet and fierce. "I'm not asking you to erase me. I know that's not—I know the terms don't work that way anymore, debts have to be answered honestly, that's the whole point. I'll pay what I owe. I'll pay it for twenty years if I have to. I'm asking—" and here her voice finally broke open, all the defensive arrangement of facts collapsing at once, "—I'm asking if there's any way at all that I don't have to do it alone."

Wren looked at her for a long moment.

Ten years ago—or eleven, or twelve; the years had begun to blur at their edges like ink left in rain—she had stood in a market stall selling a luck-charm to a desperate mother, and felt the old power stir in her hands, and understood that her careful smallness was over. She had been debt-bound then too. Hunted, too. Alone in exactly this way, convinced that the world's machinery had no gear cut to fit her, that the best she could hope for was to be overlooked.

She had been wrong. It had taken a burned grove, a lying god, a dying mentor, a good man's conscience, and an army of the overlooked to prove her wrong—but she had been wrong, gloriously, and she would spend the rest of her life making sure other people got to be wrong about it faster and cheaper than she had.

"Tamsin," she said. "Do you know what this place actually is? Not what the ballads say—we're not a holy site, whatever the pilgrims think, and we're certainly not a refuge for fugitives, whatever the Ledger Office fears. Do you know what it is?"

The girl shook her head.

"It's a door that opens from the inside." Wren held out her hand. "That was the flaw in the old covenant, the thing my grandmother saw and the gods refused to hear: they built a garden and locked it, and locked doors only protect the people holding the keys. The new terms say otherwise. Magic answers by consent. Debts are answered honestly. And no one—" she said it clearly, so there could be no mistaking, so that if the Ledger Office ever demanded an account of her words, the record would show them plain, "—no one pays what they owe alone. That's not charity. That's the terms. You're not asking me for a favor, Tamsin Reeve. You're invoking the covenant. And I am honor-bound, by oath and by sap and by every stubborn tree in this grove, to hear you."

Tamsin took her hand. Her grip was cold and strong and shaking.

"The debt stands," Wren continued, gentler now. "You'll pay it. But you'll pay it here, in the Wood, as my apprentice—Witness-track, sanctioned craft, the whole curriculum, including the parts where I make you copy out the covenant terms until you can recite them in your sleep, because the first lesson of the new order is that nobody gets to be vague about the rules, least of all the people who benefit from them. Your wages go to the Ledger Office until the mark clears. Your evenings belong to your mother's sickbed, and we'll send what help the grove's healers can give, by consent, freely asked and freely given. And your Sundays—" Wren smiled. "Your Sundays you may spend however you like. That's also in the terms. Article Nine. I fought hard for Article Nine."

Behind her, she heard Ashen-Vey exhale—not quite a laugh, not quite a sigh, something between that he had never quite managed to name in all his long existence.

"You fought hard for Article Nine," he murmured, "because you wanted your Sundays free to argue with me."

"I won every argument. I needed the time."

* * *

They walked her back down the pilgrim road as the afternoon slanted long and gold through the branches, Tamsin in the middle, asking questions in a rush—about the sanctioning process, about the restricted shelves, about whether it was true that the god-host walked openly in the southern provinces now (it was; bounded by witness, as the terms required, and mostly behaving), about whether the heart-tree would speak to her (only if she listened, which was harder). Ashen-Vey answered the theological questions with a patience that would have astonished anyone who had known him during the exile years, when patience had been a strategy rather than a practice. Wren answered the practical ones, and both of them, without discussing it, steered the girl away from the places along the path where the ground still held the shapes of the believers who had died holding the line against the pyre-wagons. There would be time for that history. There was always time for that history; the Wood made sure of it.

At the gatehouse, where the road opened onto the long view of Corvale—its smoke, its bells, its thousand chimneys rising into the soft evening air like prayers made visible—Tamsin paused.

"The magister," she said hesitantly. "The one who came before. Halev. They cleared his name, didn't they? I've seen the record. It's in the public reading now, every year."

"Every year," Wren said. "Marrec reads it himself. He's old now, and his voice cracks on the same line every time, and every year he pretends it doesn't."

"What line?"

"*He sold her to save her, and saved her at the price of himself.*" Wren watched the words land. "Forgiveness isn't absolution, Tamsin. I forgave the man. The system that made selling a child an act of love—that I rebuilt. That's the difference between mercy and surrender, and if you learn nothing else from me, learn that."

The girl nodded slowly, and Wren saw the lesson take root somewhere behind her eyes, alongside the fear and the defiance and the terrible young hope. It would grow crooked, probably. Everything grew crooked here. The trees themselves grew crooked, leaning toward the light and away from the memory of fire. Crooked and alive was the whole doctrine of the place.

Then Tamsin bowed—awkwardly, uncertain whether one bowed to a Witness-scribe, deciding to do it anyway—and passed through the gate toward the city that had once owned her, carrying Wren's letter of apprenticeship in a hand that gripped it like a rope thrown to a drowning woman.

Wren watched her go until the road bent and took her.

"Well," Ashen-Vey said at last. "Another one."

"Another one."

"You know they'll keep coming. Every debt-bound child in Corvale who writes a charm for a sick mother, every scribe who reads the wrong shelf, every prayer that works when it shouldn't—they'll all find their way up this road eventually. The Wood calls them. It's learned that trick from you, I think. You were calling strays long before you had a grove to put them in."

"Mm." Wren leaned against the gatepost, watching the light fail over the city. Somewhere down there, the evening bells were ringing—not the alarm bells of her youth, but the witness-bells, rung at dusk to mark the hour when grievances could be heard and prayers could be written down and no voice in Corvale would go unheard until morning. Imperfect. Fragile. Tested weekly by petty disputes and monthly by real ones, and holding, the way a young tree holds—not because it cannot break, but because enough hands tend its roots.

"Do you regret it?" she asked him quietly. "Any of it? The choice you made at the heart-tree. Freedom without power. Staying bound beside a mortal who argues with you."

Ashen-Vey considered this with the gravity he brought to all honest questions, which was considerable, and to dishonest ones, which was considerably more.

"When I argued for mercy in the Godswar," he said, "it was strategy. I told you that, in the end. I wanted the gods to survive the war, and mercy was the winning move, and I dressed it up as conscience because even then I knew conscience would persuade you where strategy would not." He turned his coal-colored eyes to her, and in them she saw no divinity at all—only age, and attention, and something that had learned, slowly and painfully, to be true. "This is different. This—" he gestured at the grove, the gate, the road, the girl already vanishing into the city's evening, "—this serves no strategy I can find. I stay because staying is mine. That's all. It's the first thing I've ever chosen that wasn't a move."

Wren took his hand. His fingers were sap-sticky and whittler-calloused and warm.

"The garden tends its gardener," she said. "Grandmother's saying. I finally understand it. I thought it meant the Wood takes care of whoever plants it. But it's the other way around, isn't it. All these years—all this tending—it's what kept *me* planted."

Above them, the young trees wept their golden tears into the gathering dark, remembering everything, regretting nothing, growing anyway. And down the pilgrim road, faint with distance, a bell rang the hour, and somewhere in the city a girl with a debt-brand and a stolen charm sat down at a desk to write her first honest prayer, and the door she had knocked on stood open behind her, and would stand open, always, from the inside.
