## 1. The Valley That Forgot the Sky

The ink froze first, always the ink.

Paloma Thackeray had learned to keep the little pot of black against the warmth of her body, tucked into the crook of her elbow beneath three layers of wool, because the Temple of Quiet Hours did not believe in wasting fire on scribes. Fire was for the sanctuary, for the great braziers that burned before the Veiled Altar, and for the provost's private rooms, where the air was said to smell of cinnamon and old money. The scriptorium got what the scriptorium got: a single brazier at the far end, lit at the hour of first copying and dead by the hour of second, and eleven desks arranged in rows like coffins awaiting their occupants.

She blew on her pen and wrote.

*Received, from the household of Marla Ostrow, one hen, brown, in partial settlement of the debt incurred for the mending of a roof-beam, the timber having been supplied by the Temple stores at an interest of one part in ten per season.*

The hen had been dead when it arrived. Paloma knew this because she had been the one sent to receive it, standing in the Ostrows' frozen yard while Marla wrung her chapped hands and explained, twice, that the bird had been alive that very morning, that the cold had simply *taken* it, that surely the Temple would understand. Paloma had understood. She had written *one hen, brown* anyway, because the ledger did not have a column for dead, and because Marla Ostrow had three children, and because the arithmetic of the valley did not permit mercy — only deferral.

*Balance outstanding: two hens, or equivalent.*

She had been writing such lines for eleven years. She could write them in her sleep. Sometimes she suspected she did.

Outside the scriptorium's high, narrow windows, the valley went about its morning beneath a sky the color of unwashed wool. Paloma could see a slice of it from her desk — the eastern shoulder of the pass, white with old snow, and above it the grey ceiling of cloud that had hung there, so far as anyone living knew, for as long as anyone living had been alive to look. The clouds did not move. Or they moved so slowly that movement was indistinguishable from stillness, like the hands of a clock so patient it might as well be broken.

The valley was called Merrowdale, though the Temple called it the Keeping, and the oldest ledgers — the ones locked in the provost's vault, the ones Paloma was not permitted to touch — called it something else entirely, a word she had glimpsed once, years ago, on the spine of a water-stained volume: *the Fold.*

A fold. As for sheep.

She put the thought away, as she put away all such thoughts, in the deep drawer at the back of her mind where she kept the things that were not useful and not safe. The drawer was very full. She was very good at keeping it shut.

"Thackeray."

The voice of Sister Hesk came down the row of desks like a draft under a door. Paloma did not look up. Looking up was an invitation, and invitations cost.

"Your column is drifting," Sister Hesk said, and her shadow fell across the ledger, cold as well-water. "Third entry from the top. The numerals lean."

"They lean because the paper is damp, Sister."

"The paper is damp because you breathe on it. Breathe elsewhere."

Paloma breathed elsewhere. Sister Hesk moved on, her slippers whispering on the stone, and Paloma looked at the numerals and saw that, indeed, they leaned — a fraction, a hair's width, the whole column tilting eastward like a row of gravestones in old ground. She corrected them. She was good at correcting things. It was, she had come to understand, the entire substance of her life: she corrected columns, she corrected errors, she corrected the small crookednesses of other people's accounts, and no one had ever once corrected anything for her.

Her own account lay in the ledger beneath her elbow, the one she was not copying today but which she carried in her head with the terrible precision of a woman who had memorized the dimensions of her cage. *Paloma Thackeray, entered into indenture at age eleven, upon the death of her mother, in settlement of arrears accrued against the estate of the said Thackeray.* Eleven years of service against a debt of four hundred and twelve marks. She had paid down, in eleven years of labor valued at the Temple's own generous accounting, sixty-three.

She had done the arithmetic once, late at night, in the kind of mood that comes from too much ink and too little food. At the current rate, her debt would be discharged in the sixty-first year of her indenture. She would be seventy-two. The Temple's scribes, as a rule, did not reach seventy-two. Their eyes went first, then their lungs, and then they were moved to the infirmary ward on the ground floor, where they could be useful by dying somewhere convenient.

Paloma blew on the pen again and reached for the next page.

* * *

The bell for the noon meal rang, and the scribes rose as one, a small grey congregation shuffling toward the refectory. Paloma fell into line beside Wren, who was not technically a scribe but a temple foundling, eleven years old, assigned to the scriptorium to sweep floors and grind ink and ask questions, which he did the way other children breathed — constantly, involuntarily, and with an air of grievance when interrupted.

"You're doing the face," Wren said.

"What face?"

"The one where you've counted something you didn't want to count."

"I count everything. It's my profession."

"You counted your own debt at breakfast. I saw your lips move."

Paloma said nothing, which was confirmation enough, and Wren had the grace to look briefly sorry before his curiosity, which was a force of nature and knew no mercy, overtook him. "I saw something last night," he said, dropping his voice as they passed under the arch into the cloister. "Something you won't believe."

"I believe everything in ledgers and nothing in children."

"I saw a star go out."

Paloma stopped. Just for a step, just a hitch in her stride, but Wren saw it, because Wren saw everything, and his grin spread like ink through water. "You stopped," he said. "You never stop. You're the most stopped-against person in the whole Temple."

"I stopped because you stepped on my heel."

"I did not."

"There are no stars, Wren." She resumed walking, and he trotted to keep pace, his boots slapping the flagstones. "There is cloud. There has been cloud since before your grandmother's grandmother. Whatever you saw was a gap in the cloud, or a falling ember off the watch-tower, or — most likely — you were asleep, and dreaming of the stories the carter tells when he's been at the cider."

The carter's stories were technically forbidden. They were stories of the Before — of a sky full of cold fires, of lights that wheeled and danced and were given names like *the Hunter* and *the Sisters* and *the Old Ox*, of a night so thick with them that you could supposedly read a page by starlight. The Temple taught that such tales were vanity, and worse than vanity: they were *longing*, and longing was the crack through which discontent entered the soul. The valley had everything it needed. The valley was kept. The wards held, and the wards were mercy, and to look past them was to spit into the hand that fed you.

That was the teaching. Paloma had copied it out four hundred times in her years of penance-copying, and she could recite it in her sleep, and she had never once believed a word of it — though she had been careful, so very careful, never to let that show on her face.

But a star going *out*. Not appearing. Going out. That implied a star that had been there, burning, in a sky that was not supposed to have anything in it at all.

"What did it look like?" she asked, before she could stop herself.

Wren's grin softened into something rarer and more serious. He held up a pinched finger and thumb. "Little. Like — you know when you blow out the rush-light, and the ember goes orange and then red and then just gone? Like that. Except up. Way up. Over the pass, past the Shoulder Stone. And then—" he hesitated, and for the first time since she'd known him, Wren looked uncertain, and the uncertainty was worse than the story. "And then I felt it. In my teeth. Like a bell struck way too far away to hear, but you feel it anyway. In the fillings. In the bones."

"In your bones," Paloma repeated flatly.

"You asked."

"I did. More fool me." They had reached the refectory doors, where the smell of barley soup and the drone of the meal-blessing waited, and Paloma paused with her hand on the cold iron ring. "Listen to me, Wren. Don't say this to anyone else. Not to the other foundlings, not to Sister Hesk, certainly not to the provost. The Temple has no patience for star-talk. You'll earn yourself a penance, and penances come out of the scriptorium's hours, and the scriptorium's hours are mine."

"Yours?"

"I mean — the work is mine. That I must do. Which you slow down." She pushed through the doors, and the blessing swallowed them, and the conversation was over, and Paloma spent the meal tasting nothing and counting, against her will, the ways a boy might mistake a dying ember on the watch-tower for a dying star.

There were, she concluded, at least four. She did not find any of them convincing.

* * *

The afternoon copying was the tithe rolls, which were worse than the debt ledgers, because the debt ledgers at least concerned people, and the tithe rolls concerned grain, and grain had no face to haunt you. Paloma copied. The light thickened toward the grey end of grey. The brazier died its daily death at the far end of the room, and the cold crept up through the floor stones and settled into the scribes' spines like a tenant with a long lease.

It was near the hour of last copying when the provost's bell rang — not the ordinary bell, but the small silver one, the summons bell, the one that meant a scribe was wanted above stairs — and every head in the scriptorium lifted like a field of anxious wheat.

"Thackeray," said Sister Hesk, with the faint relish she permitted herself on such occasions. "The provost wants you. Try not to breathe on anything."

The provost's rooms were warm. That was the first thing Paloma felt, climbing the spiral stair — warmth like a wall, like wading into bathwater, and beneath the warmth the smell of cinnamon and lamp oil and, faintly, of money, which has a smell, though no one likes to admit it. Provost Ellery Vane sat behind a desk of black oak older than the Temple walls, a small neat man with small neat hands and eyes like two drops of ink that had never quite dried. He was writing. He let her stand. He always let her stand. It was a lesson taught without words: *your time is worth less than my convenience.*

"Thackeray," he said at last, not looking up. "Eleven years in my scriptorium."

"Yes, Provost."

"Your hand is the best in the house. Your arithmetic is flawless. You do not gossip, you do not err, you do not ask questions." He set down his pen at last and looked at her, and the ink-drop eyes did their slow blink. "It has occurred to me, from time to time, to wonder why."

"I have no talents beyond my pen, Provost."

"That is a lie, and a well-practiced one, and I admire the craft of it." He folded his hands. "I have a task. A special task. It pays down debt at triple rate — triple, Thackeray, which for a woman in your position ought to make your heart sing. It requires discretion, a flawless hand, and no questions. The texts are old. They are not for the general shelves. What you copy, you copy once, and what you copy is then locked away, and what you have seen is not discussed."

Paloma's heart did something complicated that she chose not to examine. Triple rate. Sixty-three marks down in eleven years; at triple rate, the remainder of her life might become, if not her own, at least *scheduled* to be her own. A door, even a door into a smaller room, was a door.

"What manner of texts?" she asked, and then immediately: "Forgive me. No questions. I withdraw the question."

But the provost was smiling — the thin, dry smile of a man watching a fish take a hook he baited an hour ago. "Pre-warding," he said. "Foundational documents. The histories of the Keeping's establishment. You will find them dry, I expect. Ledgers of a kind, in their way." He slid a single sheet across the desk, a sample, a taste — the way one offers a lick of honey before the medicine. "Begin tomorrow. First hour. And Thackeray — the drawer you use for your own thoughts. Keep it shut. There are texts in my vault that have a way of opening such drawers, and I should hate to lose my best scribe to wondering."

She took the sample page. Her eyes went down it before she could govern them, and caught — between a formula and a date — a single phrase, half a line, in the old hand:

*...and the Choir was set upon the walls, and the Fold was shut, and what slept beneath slept on...*

The words went into her like a splinter. She felt the exact moment they passed the skin.

"Thank you, Provost," she heard herself say, in her ledger-voice, her flat, safe, corrected voice. "I will begin at first hour."

"See that you do." He had already returned to his own papers. "And send the boy Wren to me on your way down. I'm told he's been talking about stars."

* * *

That night Paloma did not sleep. She lay in her narrow cell — three paces by two, a pallet, a shelf, a window slit that framed a hand's-width of the eternal grey — and listened to the Temple settle around her, stone tick and beam groan, the great building breathing the way old buildings breathe, and she turned the phrase over and over like a stone in the palm.

*The Choir was set upon the walls. The Fold was shut. What slept beneath slept on.*

Beneath. Beneath what? The valley floor was all she had ever known — the fields, the river, the town of Merrow with its crooked streets, the temple hill with its white walls. Beneath was cellars and graves and the deep cold water of the wells. Nothing slept there. Nothing needed to.

And the wards. Everyone knew the wards. Every child learned the wards before they learned letters: the Keeping's great unseen shield, raised by the founders' mercy, that kept out the wild magic of the outer world, the unshielded sky, the dangerous open country where, the Temple said, a soul could be unmade by a stray wind. The wards held. The wards were mercy. The wards held.

She had copied that sentence, in one form or another, perhaps a thousand times. And tonight, for the first time in thirty-one years, Paloma Thackeray asked herself the question that a thousand copies had never prompted, the question that was so large she had somehow walked past it her whole life the way one walks past a mountain:

*Kept out what, exactly?*

A shield keeps out enemies. A fold keeps in sheep. The old ledgers called the valley the Fold, and the provost's sample page called it the same, and the wards were set upon the walls like a choir set upon a wall, and something slept *beneath*, and the sky above — the sky that everyone in the valley had been trained not to look at, not to long for, not to name — the sky above was grey, and had been grey, and would be grey, forever, amen.

She got up. She went to the window slit. The cold came through it like a living thing and pressed its face to hers, and she looked east, toward the pass, toward the Shoulder Stone, toward the place where Wren said a star had died.

The cloud hung where it always hung. But — and she would remember this afterward, would turn it over in the terrible days to come, would wonder if she had seen it or made it, if her eyes had finally opened or merely begun to fail — but it seemed to her, in that hour before dawn, that the cloud was thinner over the pass. Thinner, and moving. Actually moving, for the first time in her life, like a curtain stirring in a room where someone has just opened a door.

And through the gap — for one breath, for half of one — something small and cold and very far away looked down at her.

It was gone before she could decide what it was. The cloud closed. The valley lay dark and safe and silent beneath its ceiling, eleven thousand souls asleep in the fold, and Paloma stood at the window with her heart going like a hammer in a locked room, and thought: *it flickered. Wren was right. It flickered, and I saw it, and the sky is not empty, and it never was.*

Behind her, in the dark of the cell, her ink-stained fingers had gone to her own arm without her noticing, tracing the ledger-lines of her debt, column after column after column, all the way down to the bottom of the page — where, in her mind's eye, in the provost's dry voice, a new line was already writing itself:

*Balance outstanding: everything.*
