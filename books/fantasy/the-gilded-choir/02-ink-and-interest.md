## 2. Ink and Interest

The Provost's office smelled of beeswax and old paper and, beneath both, something faintly sour — the smell of money counted too often in a room with no windows.

Paloma stood on the worn flagstones with her hands folded at her waist the way the temple taught its debtors to stand: present, penitent, patient. The posture was drilled into them by the second year of indenture. *You are not a supplicant,* Sister Maren had said, adjusting her elbows with two dry taps of a ruler. *You are an asset. Assets do not fidget.*

Provost Ellery Voss sat behind a desk that had been old when his grandfather held the office, a slab of black valley-oak carved with the sigil of the Quiet Hours — a closed eye above an open book. He was copying figures into a ledger with a gold-nibbed pen, and he did not look up for the better part of three minutes. This, too, was technique. Paloma had watched him do it to merchants, to farmers begging extension on their tithes, to a weeping widow whose husband had died owing four marks and a pig. The silence did its work. Most people began talking just to fill it, and everything they said became another entry in some ledger or other.

Paloma had learned to let the silence sit between them like a third person. She had been coming to this office since she was nine years old, when her mother's debts had passed down her spine like an inheritance nobody wanted, and she knew every trick the room contained.

So she waited, and she watched the candle, and she counted her own breaths the way the temple's bells counted hours — slow, even, unhurried. Outside the shuttered window, the afternoon light of the valley came through the slats in thin gold bars, and dust drifted through them, and somewhere below in the scriptorium a novice was practicing scales of letters, *a-a-a, b-b-b*, the sound rising like small birds trapped under a cloth.

At last Voss set down his pen.

"Thackeray." Her family name, never her given one. Debtors were surnames here; given names were for people who belonged to themselves. "You've requested an audience. That's twice this quarter. Audiences cost time, and time—" he gestured at the ledgers stacked around him like sandbags against a flood, "—is the only currency this temple respects."

"I have a proposal," Paloma said. Her voice came out steadier than she'd expected. She had rehearsed it forty times in the cell, whispering to the dark while Wren slept in the alcove next door, and rehearsal had done what rehearsal always did: made the words feel like someone else's, which made them easier to say. "I'm asking for a reduction."

"A reduction." He leaned back. The chair creaked. "Every debtor in this valley asks for a reduction. The answer has been no since before your grandmother was born, and my answer is no now, and it will be no when you're old enough to be copying your own granddaughter's ledgers. Your balance stands at eleven years, four months. Interest accrues quarterly. Is there anything else?"

"There would be," Paloma said, "if you'd let me finish."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not quite approval. Recognition, maybe — the look of a man who has spent decades among tame animals and sees, unexpectedly, teeth.

"Eleven years," she went on, before the flicker could close over, "at current rate. I'll be thirty-four when it clears. If I live. If the interest doesn't outrun me, which it has for the last six quarters — you know that better than I do, you write the numbers." She kept her eyes on the middle button of his coat, a dull brass thing shaped like a seed. "But there's work in this temple that pays more than copying tithe-rolls. Work you can't ask the ordinary scribes to do."

The candle flame bent slightly, though there was no draft.

"Continue," said the Provost.

* * *

She had found the first thread of it three weeks ago, and she had carried it carefully ever since, the way you carry a coal cupped in cold hands.

It had happened the way most dangerous things happen in the Temple of Quiet Hours: through an accident of filing. A junior archivist — a boy with ink-stained cuffs and a crush on one of the kitchen girls — had mislabeled a crate of restoration copies, and the crate had come to Paloma's desk because she was fast, and cheap, and because nobody thought twice about giving the Thackeray girl anything difficult. Difficult work was for scribes with futures.

Inside the crate, beneath forty years of grain-tallies and boundary disputes, she had found a fragment that did not belong. It was old — older than anything she'd touched, the vellum gone soft as moth-wing, the ink browned to the color of dried blood. It was written in a hand that slanted hard to the left, urgent, as if the writer had been running out of time or light or both. And it was in a dialect of Old Valleyish that the temple only taught to its senior conservators, which meant Paloma could read perhaps one word in five.

But one of the five was *ward*. And another was *before*.

There should not have been a *before*. Every child in the valley learned the Catechism of Enclosure before they learned their letters: *The wards were raised in mercy. The wards were raised in love. What lies beyond is not for us, and we are not for it.* The valley had always been warded. The sky beyond the pass was a story told to frighten children, a country of storms and strangers where the stars burned wrong. There was no *before* the wards, any more than there was a before the mountains.

And yet here was a page that spoke of them in the past tense.

She had copied it — not all of it, not the parts that mattered, but enough — and then she had put it back exactly where it lay, weighted the crate with the grain-tallies, and gone home and lain awake until the bell rang for Matins, listening to Wren breathe in the next alcove and thinking about the shape of the word *before* in a dead man's handwriting.

That was three weeks ago. She had spent them doing what she did best: research. Quietly. In the margins of legitimate work. She pulled consignment records under pretense of auditing the archive's interest schedules — the temple respected nothing so much as an audit — and she traced the fragment's provenance back through four centuries of catalog entries, each one more laconic than the last, until the entries simply stopped, and the trail ended at a sealed shelf in the Undercroft that appeared in no catalog at all.

The Restricted Collection. Every scribe knew it existed the way fish know the surface of water exists. Nobody spoke of it. Nobody was permitted down there except the Provost himself and the two conservators who had taken vows of a severity Paloma couldn't imagine — vows that included, she'd heard once from a drunk lay-brother, *never speaking of what they read, on pain of the debt passing to their kin*.

Even the temple bound its secrets in debt.

Which meant, she reasoned, in the long grey hours between Matins and Lauds, that the temple considered these texts valuable. Valuable things could be bargained with. And the Provost — she had watched him for fifteen years across that black desk — was a man who hoarded value the way misers hoarded coin, but who understood, better than anyone alive, that value unrealized was merely weight.

He needed someone to transcribe the collection. Someone skilled, discreet, and utterly unable to leave. Someone whose entire life was already collateral.

Someone like her.

* * *

"You want access," Voss said slowly, when she had laid it out — not the fragment, not yet, that card she was holding close to her chest — but the shape of it. The Restricted Collection. Transcription work. In exchange, a reduction of principal. "You, a third-rank copyist with a hereditary balance, want me to walk you past the seals my own conservators bled to protect."

"I want to *work*, Provost. You'd be walking me nowhere. I'd be chained to a desk either way — the only difference is which desk." She unfolded her hands and showed him them, palms up, the calluses and the ink stains and the crooked middle finger broken by a falling shelf when she was twelve. "These are the best hands in your scriptorium and you know it. I copy ninety pages a day without error. I restored the Aldermoor land-grant after the damp got into it — you billed the Aldermoors double for that, I saw the entry. Give me the restricted work and I'll give you clean copies of texts no living hand has touched in two hundred years. Texts rotting in the dark right now, while you pay two conservators' keep to guard them instead of use them."

"And why," Voss said, "would a woman who cannot read Old Valleyish be worth anything down there?"

"Because I can learn it." She had prepared this too. "Give me the grammars. Six months. I'll be reading it fluently inside a year — faster, if the material interests me, and you and I both know the only texts in this temple that could interest me are the ones you're hiding."

The candle flame stood very straight. Somewhere in the walls, a pipe ticked as the day's heat left the stones.

Voss rose. It was the first time in fifteen years she had seen him rise during an audience, and her heart slammed once, hard, against her ribs — triumph or terror, she couldn't tell which. He crossed to the window and opened the shutters a hand's width, letting in a blade of valley light: the terraced orchards climbing the eastern slopes, the slate roofs of the town, the pass far above with its twin ward-stones glimmering faintly even in daylight, the way they always did, the way they had for as long as anyone remembered. Beyond the pass, the mountains went up and up into a sky that, from here, looked like nothing at all.

"Do you know what the valley is, Thackeray?" he asked, without turning.

"Home," she said, because it was the catechism answer.

"It is a *balance*." His voice had changed — lower, drier, almost gentle, which frightened her more than anger would have. "Everything in it balances. The tithes balance the temple's keep. The temple's keep balances the crown's levy. The wards balance—" he paused, and she heard him choose his word with the care of a man laying stones across a stream, "—what needs balancing. Every account settled, every line entered, every soul in its proper column. That is not cruelty. That is the only reason there is still a valley to be cruel *about*. The world outside does not keep accounts, Thackeray. It keeps nothing. It spends everything, all at once, forever." He turned back to her. "You are proposing to open books that were closed for excellent reasons."

"With respect, Provost — you bill the dead. You charged Widow Corren interest for the three days between her husband's death and his burial. Do not speak to me about excellent reasons." The words were out before she could weigh them, and she felt them fall between them like a dropped knife.

For a long moment neither of them moved. Then Voss laughed — a single dry note, like a ledger closing.

"Very well," he said. "Here is my offer, since you've earned the courtesy of one. You will have the grammars. You will have supervised access to the Restricted Collection — supervised, Thackeray, meaning Conservator Hale sits within arm's reach of you and reads over your shoulder, and every page you touch is logged. You will produce fair copies of whatever is assigned. You will speak of the contents to no one — not to your little friend the bell-boy, not in your sleep, not to God Himself unless God signs the confidentiality schedule. In exchange: one year of your term forgiven for every year of transcription, and the interest frozen while the work continues." He sat, and drew a fresh sheet toward him, and took up the gold-nibbed pen. "Refuse, and we never speak of it again, and your balance continues as it stands."

One year for one year. The interest frozen. Her breath went out of her slowly, like air from a punctured thing. Eleven years became — if the work lasted — five. Five became possible. Five became a life on the far side of it, a self she might someday meet.

"Yes," she said. Too quickly. She made herself add, "Yes, Provost. On those terms."

He wrote. She watched the pen move, and thought: *he agreed too quickly too.* But the thought was small and far away, a bird crossing a vast grey sky, and the cage door was already swinging open, and she walked through it because walking through open doors was the only thing she had ever been allowed to do.

* * *

The Undercroft smelled of cold stone and time.

Three flights down, past the reliquary and the sealed well that the novices said went to the center of the world, a door of black oak bore three locks and a blessing in Latinized Valleyish that Paloma translated as she waited: *What sleeps, let it sleep.* Conservator Hale unlocked the locks in order — iron, bronze, and a third of some pale metal she didn't recognize, which hummed faintly against his fingers, or perhaps against hers; she couldn't tell, and decided not to think about it.

Beyond the door, shelves descended into lamplight like ribs into a chest cavity. The Restricted Collection was smaller than she had imagined — perhaps two hundred volumes, wrapped in oilcloth, labeled in that same left-slanting urgent hand — and quieter than any place she had ever been. The silence down here was not absence of sound. It was presence of something else, a pressure, like the moment before a choir inhales.

Hale set her at a desk beneath a lamp, placed a single wrapped volume before her, and sat down at arm's reach, exactly as promised, with his own copy-paper and his own pen and his eyes, pale as skimmed milk, fixed on her hands.

"The Founding Accounts," he said. "Volume the third. You will copy it exactly. Errors of transcription will be corrected at a cost of one month per error, added to your term." He paused. "Errors of *understanding* will not be corrected. We do not charge for understanding. We simply do not encourage it."

"What happens," Paloma asked, untying the oilcloth, "to the scribes who understand anyway?"

"No one knows," Hale said. "They stop being recorded."

The volume fell open under her hands, and she bent to the work.

It was slow going, at first — the Old Valleyish grammar fighting her, the letters looping into ligatures that hadn't been used in living memory, the vellum so thin the lamplight shone through it like skin. But her hands knew how to follow strange writing; that was the whole of her trade. Line by line, hour by hour, the language began to unlock, the way a lock begins to unlock — not all at once but in tumblers, small clicks of recognition. *This word is the ancestor of our word for oath. This one for hunger. This one* — she stopped, pen hovering — *this one doesn't exist anymore at all, and means something like the-silence-between-two-notes-that-makes-them-one-song.*

On the fourth day, she reached the passage about the founding.

She had expected piety. The official histories said the founders had been refugees blessed by heaven, led into the valley by saints, warded by prayer. What she found instead was arithmetic. The founding account was written like a contract — clauses, conditions, penalties — and the parties signing it were not saints. One party was *the people of the valley, and their heirs, unto the last generation*. The other party's name had been scraped off the vellum, carefully, thoroughly, by someone long ago — but the scrape-marks still held the ghost of the letters, and Paloma, tilting the page against the lamp, could just make out the shadow of the first one.

It was not a saint's initial. It was not a letter at all, really. It was a symbol: a circle, crossed by a single vertical stroke, like an eye that had been closed by force.

And beneath the signature blocks, in the margin, in a later hand — newer, but still centuries dead — someone had written a note. Small. Almost apologetic. Paloma translated it twice to be sure, her lips moving silently, Hale's milk-pale eyes on her the whole time:

*Let it be remembered that the Choir sang willingly, and that the valley was built upon the song, and that the song was not a wall but a voice — and that a voice may be raised again, or lowered, or silenced, but never unmade. We did not build a prison. We built a throat. God forgive us the difference.*

*The Choir.*

Paloma sat very still. Down in the stone dark, under two hundred years of lamplight and silence, she felt the phrase settle into her the way a seed settles into soil — quietly, inevitably, with roots she would not see for a long time.

A choir. Not masons, not saints. Singers. The valley had been founded on a song, sung willingly, by people whose names were contracts and whose god signed with a closed eye. And the wards — the beautiful, merciful, eternal wards that kept the world out and the valley safe — were not a wall.

They were a throat.

"Conservator Hale," she said, and her voice sounded strange to her, flattened, careful. "In the Catechism. The verse about the raising of the wards. *The wards were raised in mercy, the wards were raised in love.*"

"That is the verse," Hale said.

"Who raised them?"

"Silence, Thackeray." But he said it softly, and he did not look at her, and his pen had stopped moving, and in the pressure of the Undercroft quiet Paloma heard — or imagined she heard, and would spend many nights deciding which — a faint resonance under his words, like the hum a large bell gives off long after it has been struck.

She bent back to her copying. She copied the clause about heirs unto the last generation. She copied the penalty clauses, and the conditions, and the scraped-out name with its closed eye. She copied the marginal note word for word, because exactness was her trade, and because some part of her — the part that had survived fifteen years of ledgers by knowing precisely which lines mattered — understood that she had just become the first person in two centuries to carry this text out of the dark, in her own hand, in her own memory, past three locks and a vow of silence.

Her survival instinct said: *stop reading. Copy what's assigned. Forget the margins. Go home. Count your reduced years. Be safe.*

Her survival instinct had kept her alive for twenty-three years, and she honored it, and she hated it, and that night she disobeyed it for the first time in her life. At the bottom of her day's fair copy, in the space reserved for the transcriber's attestation, she wrote the required formula — *copied true, P. Thackeray, third rank* — and then, beneath it, in the tiny shorthand she used for her private tallies, invisible to anyone who didn't know to look:

*Found the word. It was "choir."*

* * *

Winter came down off the mountains early that year.

By the feast of Deeppeace the pass was white to its shoulders, and the ward-stones burned their faint blue through the snow-haze, and the whole valley drew in around its fires like a hand closing. In the scriptorium the ink froze in the wells overnight and the novices warmed the pots against their stomachs. Wren grew a cold and refused to stay in the infirmary, appearing at her desk instead with red-rimmed eyes and a stolen honeycomb, and she scolded him and split the honeycomb with him and let him sit under her desk warming her feet, which was against about six rules, none of which she cared about.

"How's the secret book?" he whispered one afternoon, chin on her knee, watching her pen.

"There's no secret book."

"There's absolutely a secret book. You've got the face."

"What face?"

"The face you get when you find a number that doesn't add up." He tapped his own forehead. "Right there. Like you swallowed a question mark."

Paloma looked down at him — nine years old, gap-toothed, sold to the temple at six by parents he didn't talk about, quick as a magpie and twice as likely to steal something shiny — and felt the familiar ache that had no column in any ledger. "Go warm someone else's feet, troublemaker."

He went, grinning. She watched him go, and then she looked out the high scriptorium window at the early-darkening sky, at the few stars the valley ever showed — the Shepherd, the Loom, the slow wheel of the Crown turning above the pass — and she caught herself wondering, for the first time in her life, not *what the stars were* but *why there were so few*. The old texts talked about skies full of them. Skies like spilled salt. Skies that turned.

Overhead, the valley's handful of stars burned in their fixed and lawful places, exactly as they had burned every night of her life, exactly as they would burn every night forever.

Except.

She frowned. Leaned closer to the glass, breath fogging it, wiped it with her sleeve. High above the pass, near the wheel of the Crown, one star seemed — it was nothing, it was the frost on the pane, it was her tired eyes — one star seemed to gutter. To shrink. To go out, the way a candle goes out, deliberately, like something being snuffed by a careful hand.

Then it was burning again, steady and small and lawful, and Paloma told herself she had imagined it, and copied the thought out of her mind the way she copied everything else — exactly, completely, and where no one would ever think to look.

Two nights later, Wren came running to her cell after Compline, out of breath, snow in his hair, eyes enormous.

"Paloma," he said. "Paloma, the star. The one I told you about. It flickered again — I *saw* it — and this time it didn't come back."
