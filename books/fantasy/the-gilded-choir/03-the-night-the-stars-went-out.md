## 3. The Night the Stars Went Out

The lamps in the scriptorium guttered at the same moment, all nine of them, as though a great breath had been drawn through the temple.

Paloma looked up from her ledger, quill poised above a column of figures that refused to sum correctly — three barrels of salted eel unaccounted for since the autumn tithes, and no one willing to say where they'd gone. The ink on her nib trembled. Around her, the other scribes lifted their heads like a field of pale flowers turning toward an unseen sun.

"Draft," muttered Brother Ellery, and reached for his cloak.

But there was no draft. The windows of the Temple of Quiet Hours were narrow as arrow slits and sealed against the mountain cold with waxed linen; nothing moved in the corridor beyond except the shadows of the night-brothers making their slow rounds. Paloma set down her quill and pressed her palm flat against the desk, feeling for the vibration she had learned to read the way farmers read frost — the temple's bones creaking under wind, the deep hum of the ward-stones in the cellar that most people never noticed but that Paloma, who spent more nights than she should among the ledgers, had come to think of as the building's heartbeat.

The hum was gone.

She did not know how she knew it, only that its absence was suddenly enormous, the way one notices silence only when a clock stops. For eleven years the ward-stones had murmured beneath the flagstones, so constant that she had stopped hearing them the way she stopped hearing her own pulse. Now the cellar of the world was quiet, and every hair on her arms stood up beneath her sleeves.

"The lamps," said Sister Maren from the far table. Her voice was thin. "Look at the lamps."

The flames were leaning. All of them, all nine, bending eastward toward the pass as if pulled by strings, though the air hung perfectly still. And their light had gone strange — thinner, yellower, the color of old parchment, the color of things about to be erased.

Paloma rose. Her chair scraped back too loudly in the hush. She crossed to the eastern window, the one that faced the valley, and pressed her face to the cold glass.

* * *

The Valley of Quiet Hours sat cupped in the mountains like water in a bowl, and above it, always, hung the sky — or what passed for sky within the wards. Not true stars. Everyone over the age of ten understood this without ever being told: the points of light above the valley were painted there by the founding wards, a ceiling of borrowed constellations, beautiful and fixed and utterly reliable. They wheeled in their slow seasons. They dimmed behind cloud. They had never once gone out.

Paloma had copied the maintenance ledgers herself. Lamp-oil for the ward-keepers, tallow for the observatory candles, a yearly allotment of silver dust for the *renewal of the firmament* — she had written those words a hundred times without once wondering what they meant, the way you can write your own name until it becomes a stranger's.

Now she watched the sky through the window, breath fogging the glass, and saw the first star die.

It did not flicker, as Wren had sworn his had done two weeks ago. It simply went out — a pinprick of light extinguished between one heartbeat and the next, leaving behind it not darkness but a kind of hole, a small round absence that seemed darker than the night around it. Then another. Then three together, near the horizon where the Shepherd's Cart hung low over the western ridge. Then a whole constellation, unraveling star by star like a garment picked apart at the seam.

"Sister Thackeray." Brother Ellery's hand closed on her shoulder. His fingers were shaking. "Sister Thackeray, you will come away from the window."

"They're going out." Her voice sounded very far away to her own ears. "All of them."

And then they were.

It happened all at once, everywhere, the way a candle goes out when the wick is finally drowned — the entire false firmament vanishing in a single instant, and the valley plunged into a blackness so total that Paloma could not see her own hand against the glass. Behind her someone screamed. Somewhere in the cloisters a bell began to ring, wild and arrhythmic, no hour marked by any book. And beneath her feet, rising up through the stone floor and into her teeth and the roots of her hair, came a sound like the world's largest pane of glass beginning to crack.

* * *

They gathered in the nave because there was nowhere else, forty scribes and acolytes and kitchen brothers crowded between the pews while the provost stood on the chancel steps holding a storm-lantern whose flame burned blue and wrong. Outside, the cracking sound continued — not loud, precisely, but vast, a sound felt in the marrow rather than heard by the ear, as though the mountains themselves were being tapped by something patient and enormous.

"The wards are failing," Provost Calloway said, and his voice, usually so smooth, so practiced at announcing debts and deadlines, cracked in the middle of the word *failing*. "The outer wards. The inner lattice may hold. We must remain calm. We must remain inside. The Temple of Quiet Hours has stood for six hundred years and—"

The sound stopped.

In the ringing silence that followed, Paloma heard something she would carry to her grave: a single high note, pure as a struck glass, singing down from the direction of the pass. It lasted perhaps four seconds. It was the most beautiful sound she had ever heard, and it was the sound of something dying.

Then the pressure changed. Her ears popped. Every lamp in the nave flared white and went out, all together, and in the sudden dark the congregation made a sound like a wounded animal, and through the high clerestory windows — real windows, open now to a real sky for the first time in six hundred years — the actual stars looked down.

They were terrible. They were magnificent. There were thousands of them, more than Wren had ever dreamed, more than the painted firmament had ever pretended to, scattered across the black like salt across a slate, and they were *moving*, wheeling in patterns no valley-born eye had ever seen, and Paloma stood in the dark nave with tears running freely down her face and could not have said, even under torture, whether they were tears of grief or joy.

She thought of Wren then. Fourteen years old, sharp-eyed, trouble-prone Wren, who slept in the acolytes' dormitory on the north side and who had been beaten last month for claiming he'd seen a star go out. He would be awake. Of course he would be awake. Every child in the valley would be awake tonight, and every adult pretending not to be.

She found him in the cloister, barefoot in the snow, face tipped up to the impossible sky.

"I told you," he said, when he saw her. His voice was steady but his hands were not. "I told you I saw one go out. Nobody believed me. Paloma, nobody believed me and now they're *all*—" He stopped. Swallowed. "What's happening?"

"I don't know." It was the truest thing she had said in years. Scribes were not permitted ignorance; ignorance was a debt like any other, to be worked off in transcription and penance. But standing under the real stars with the boy who had seen the truth first, she found she had no ledger words left. "I don't know, Wren. Come inside. You'll freeze."

He let her pull him toward the door, but at the threshold he turned back once more, drinking in the sky like a man offered water after a lifetime of thirst. "It's so *big*," he whispered. "Paloma, why did they make it so small?"

She had no answer. She suspected, in some chamber of herself she did not yet dare open, that the answer would be worse than any question.

* * *

Dawn came gray and ordinary, which was somehow the worst part. The sun rose over the eastern ridge exactly as it always had, indifferent, and lit a valley that had changed overnight in ways that grew clearer and more horrifying with each hour.

The mist on the northern slopes did not lift. It lay along the tree line in a long white band, and where the band ended, the world ended too — or rather, the *edge* ended, the soft boundary that everyone had always known was there and never questioned, the sense that the valley was a room and the mountains its walls. Beyond the mist line, refugees began to appear by midmorning. Farmers from the outer holdings, driving carts piled with children and chickens and whatever else could be carried, their faces gray with shock. The pass, they said. The pass was open. Soldiers were coming through it.

"Whose soldiers?" the provost demanded, and received shrugs, and Paloma watched the word *whose* die in his mouth, because for six hundred years the answer had been *no one's*. The valley answered to the temple, the temple answered to the wards, the wards answered to nothing anyone living remembered. That had been the whole architecture of the world.

By noon the bell tower watchers reported columns of men on the high road, banners snapping in the wind — banners none of them recognized, black and gold, a crowned tower stitched in thread that glittered. By midafternoon the vanguard reached the valley floor, and Paloma, watching from the temple gate with the rest of the household drawn up in terrified formation, got her first look at the soldiers of the capital.

They were not monsters. That was the horror of it. They were tired men on tired horses, mud to the knee, faces chapped by the pass winds, and they rode into the Valley of Quiet Hours as casually as men riding into a field they had inherited. Their captain dismounted before the temple steps, saluted the provost with brisk courtesy, and produced a document sealed in black wax.

"By order of the Crown," he read, in the flat tone of a man reciting a market price, "all debts owed by religious houses within the recovered territories are hereby called due. Assessment teams will enter your premises at dawn tomorrow. Noncompliance will be treated as rebellion."

"Recovered territories," the provost repeated faintly. "Captain, this valley has never belonged to any crown. The wards—"

"The wards are down, sir." The captain folded his document away. Something almost like pity crossed his weathered face. "You've been outside the King's peace for six centuries. That ends today. Everything owed is owed now."

* * *

The assessment took less than a day. The temple's coffers held coin enough to cover perhaps a tenth of what the crown claimed — six hundred years of interest calculated by clerks in a capital Paloma had never seen, on debts contracted by abbots dead before her grandmother's grandmother was born. She sat in the provost's study that second evening, transcribing the reckoning into fair copy because someone had to and because her hand was the steadiest, and watched the numbers grow like a disease spreading down the page.

Four thousand crowns. Then seven. Then eleven. The final figure, when the crown's assessor wrote it out, required a second line.

"We cannot pay," the provost said. It was not a plea; it was a statement of arithmetic, delivered in the voice of a man reading his own death warrant. "We are a house of copyists and prayer. Our wealth is in paper and devotion."

"Then the crown will take payment in kind." The assessor was a small, neat man with inkstained fingers, and Paloma hated him instantly and completely for looking so much like her. "Debtors' labor. The temple will surrender persons of account against the balance. Skilled workers fetch a good rate in the capital." He consulted his list. "Your bindery staff. Your illuminators. And—" his finger slid down the page and stopped, and he looked up, and his eyes found hers across the study with the mild precision of a man selecting a horse, "—the senior scribe. Thackeray. Her indenture papers list specialized training. Rare scripts. Pre-warding literacy, is that right?"

The room went very still. Paloma's quill hovered above the page, and a drop of ink fell, blooming black on the record of her own sale.

"That is a matter internal to this house," the provost said carefully.

"Then it's a matter internal to my report." The assessor smiled without warmth. "The girl's family debt has been carried on the temple books for three generations, provost. Three generations of interest, compounding. The crown assumes that obligation now — along with the person. She'll settle it working for His Majesty's archivists. Consider it a consolidation."

Paloma did not remember standing. She remembered the floor tilting, the study's candlelight swimming, the assessor's voice continuing somewhere above her as though from a great height — *papers will be drawn tonight, she departs with the morning convoy* — and she remembered thinking, with a clarity that felt like falling: *I have never left the valley. I have never in my life been farther than the mill pond.*

That night she packed. The dormitory cell she had occupied since childhood held almost nothing: two habits, a psalter, a wooden comb, a bundle of letters from her mother that she had reread so many times the folds had gone soft as cloth. She took the psalter and the letters. At the bottom of her trunk, wrapped in oilcloth, lay the one forbidden thing she owned — three pages copied in secret from the pre-warding archive during her bargain with the provost, pages mentioning the Choir, the founding, the *price of the firmament*. She had copied them out of curiosity, telling herself it was scholarly diligence. Now she wrapped them against her ribs like armor, or like evidence, and could not decide which frightened her more.

Wren found her in the gatehouse courtyard before dawn. The convoy was forming — six prisoners from the temple household, roped wrist to wrist in pairs, surrounded by twenty soldiers whose breath smoked in the freezing air. Above them, the real stars were fading into a dawn that came from a sky with no ceiling, and Wren stared up at the last of them with his jaw tight.

"You're really going." It wasn't a question. His eyes were red-rimmed and furious. "They're just — you're a *person*, not a debt, they can't—"

"Wren." She said his name gently, and it broke something in his face. "Listen to me. Stay here. Stay out of trouble — real trouble, not your usual kind. Keep your ears open. You hear things, you always have. If anything happens, if anything comes for the archives or the brothers—" she hesitated, then gripped his shoulder, "—remember what you saw. The star going out. Remember you were right, and everyone who beat you for it was wrong. That matters. Being right matters, even when it costs."

"It shouldn't cost *you*."

"No," she agreed. "It shouldn't." She smiled at him, and was surprised to find the smile mostly real. "But I've spent my whole life paying what shouldn't be mine. Apparently I'm practiced at it."

The soldier called the convoy forward. Ropes tightened. Boots crunched snow. As the column passed under the gatehouse arch, Paloma turned for one last look at the valley — the terraced fields, the smoke of morning fires, the temple with its bells silent for the first time in six centuries, and above it all the naked sky, vast and star-scattered and utterly indifferent, the sky her ancestors had traded away and she was now being dragged out to meet.

At the edge of the courtyard, small and furious in the gray light, Wren raised his fist to her — not farewell, she understood, but promise.

Then the pass swallowed the road, and the valley that had been the whole world was gone behind her, and Paloma Thackeray walked into the dark with a debt on her back and three stolen pages against her heart, toward a capital she had only ever read about, and a king whose name she did not yet know she would come to fear.
