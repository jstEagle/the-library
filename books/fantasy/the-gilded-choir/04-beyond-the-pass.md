## 4. Beyond the Pass

The world did not end at the pass.

Paloma had assumed it would. Every map in the Temple of Quiet Hours stopped at the pass — not faded, not torn, but *stopped*, the cartographer's ink simply declining to continue, as though the mountains were the edge of a page and beyond them lay nothing worth the cost of vellum. She had spent eleven years copying those maps and had never once wondered what the blankness felt like from inside.

It felt like wind.

Wind that came from nowhere she knew, carrying smells that had no names in her ledger-vocabulary: something resinous and sharp, something like wet iron, something sweet and rotten and green all at once. The column of soldiers moved down the switchbacks in grim silence, boots striking stone in a rhythm she recognized with a lurch — the same four-count as the temple bells marking the quarter hour, as if even the army marched to timekeeping, as if the whole world outside were just another ledger waiting for her pen.

She counted to keep from weeping. It was the only prayer she knew.

*Two hundred and six steps between the third and fourth switchback. Forty-one soldiers. Six prisoners, of whom one is me. One debt, carried forward.*

The valley, when she turned to look — and she looked more often than was wise, earning herself two cuffs from the sergeant and one almost-gentle word from the soldier called Merrin, who seemed embarrassed by the whole business — the valley was already a smudge of gray-green cradled in gray-blue stone, small as a coin at the bottom of a well. Somewhere in that smudge was Wren's raised fist. Somewhere was the Temple of Quiet Hours, where Provost Ellery-Deen would be entering her name into the defaulters' book with a steady hand, *indentured, collateral, transferred to crown custody per Article Nine*, and Paloma's whole life would become a line item in someone else's accounting.

She faced forward. The road went down, and down, and the air grew warmer and thicker, and by the time the sun stood at its height the trees around them had changed from the pines she knew to broad-leafed things whose leaves turned silver when the wind moved through them, all together, like a congregation turning its head.

"Stop staring," muttered the prisoner beside her, an old man named Hobb who'd been taken from the granary for arrears he insisted were his landlord's. "They don't like it."

"They don't like what?"

"Wonder." He said the word the way you'd say *fever*. "Wonder's how they get you. You look too long at a thing out here, the thing looks back. My brother went out with the wool carts twenty years ago. Came back wrong. Kept saying the road had told him his name."

"The road," Paloma repeated carefully.

Hobb shrugged, which in chains is a whole-body event. "You'll see."

* * *

She saw at dusk, when the column halted in a hollow where three roads met, or rather where one road met itself twice, branching and rebranching like a river deciding. The soldiers built no fire. Instead, the sergeant — a woman with a face like a closed ledger, whose name Paloma had learned was Sergeant Vael — walked the perimeter of the camp touching each of six squat stones set at intervals, and where her hand passed, light bloomed.

Not flame. That was the thing Paloma could not make her eyes agree to. There was no wick, no oil-smell, no heat she could feel from ten paces away. The stones simply filled with a pale gold luminescence, steady as noon, and moths did not come to it, and it cast shadows that fell *toward* the light instead of away, and Paloma stood with her mouth open until Vael said, without looking at her, "Close it, scribe. You'll catch flies."

"What are they?" Paloma asked, because eleven years of transcribing had taught her that the only defense against being struck was to seem incurious, and because she had never once in her life managed to seem incurious.

"Lanterns."

"They're not— there's no flame."

Vael finally looked at her. Her eyes were brown and flat and tired. "No," she agreed. "There isn't." And she walked on to the next stone, and Paloma understood that the conversation was over, filed, stamped, and shelved.

That night she could not sleep. She lay on the cold ground with her wrists chafed raw and watched the lantern-light do its impossible arithmetic on the trees, and she took out — carefully, under the blanket, with her heart hammering — the three stolen pages from the pre-warding text, folded now to the size of a communion wafer against her breastbone.

She did not read them. Not yet. Reading required light, and asking for light required explanation, and the pages were the single most criminal object she had ever possessed. But she pressed her palm flat over them and felt their small stiff heartbeat of paper, and she thought: *the provost gave me these to copy so I would owe less. The king's men took me so the temple would owe less. Everyone keeps books. Even here. Even in the wild.*

And then, because she was who she was, she began, silently, to draft the day's entry. *Dusk. Three roads. Lanterns without flame: six. Method unknown. Cost unknown.* She fell asleep mid-inventory, and dreamed of a page so long it unrolled off the edge of the world.

* * *

On the second day she saw the road remember.

It happened at a ford, a shallow stony place where the water ran loud and clear over red pebbles. The column crossed in single file, and Paloma, last of the prisoners, watched the soldier ahead of her step from stone to stone — and then she stepped where he had stepped, because it was easiest, because the footing was surest there.

Except the footing wasn't just sure. It was *warm*. Through the thin soles of her temple-issue shoes she felt it distinctly: each stone the man had touched held a faint residual warmth, like bread taken from the oven an hour ago, while the untouched stones were cold as riverbed. And more than warm — as she put her foot down on the third stone she felt something else, something that traveled up through her ankle and made her gasp aloud: a sensation like hearing her own footsteps echoed back to her from slightly ahead of time. A whisper of *weight expected, weight received*. As if the stone had been told someone was coming, and had prepared.

She stumbled off it onto a cold stone and stood there shaking, ankle-deep in the ford, until Merrin — the young soldier, the embarrassed one — took her elbow and hauled her across without a word.

"You felt it," he said quietly, once they were both on the far bank. It wasn't a question.

"The stones. They—" She didn't have vocabulary. She reached for ledger-language. "They recorded him. His passage. Like an entry."

Merrin glanced back at the column, at Sergeant Vael thirty paces up, then leaned in. His breath smelled of cheap tobacco and fear. "Roads out here are old roads. Old roads get walked on so much they start to — hold it. The way a bell holds a note after you've struck it. Everybody knows. Nobody says." He straightened, resumed his blank soldier face, and added, at full volume, for the benefit of anyone listening: "Keep up, prisoner."

But his ears had gone red, and Paloma spent the next mile turning the moment over like a coin of uncertain mint. *Everybody knows. Nobody says.* In the valley, magic was a story told to children about the bad old times before the wards — before the great shining border that kept the valley safe and sealed and small. Out here, magic was a warm stone underfoot that everyone politely ignored, the way one ignores a relative's drinking, the way one ignores a debt.

Her fingers itched for her pen. She had left it behind, of course — they had taken everything, even the stub of graphite she used for marginalia — but the itch remained, a phantom limb of the self. She was learning that she was a creature who metabolized wonder into inventory, and she did not yet know whether that was a virtue or a disease.

* * *

It was on the third evening that she noticed the deserter watching her.

She had catalogued the escort by then, in the privacy of her skull-ledger: Sergeant Vael, closed book; Merrin, open book pretending to be closed; Corporal Dunmore, who beat Hobb on general principle; and seven others whose names she hadn't caught and whose faces she had sorted into the usual categories — cruel, bored, frightened, hungry. But this one she had misfiled twice.

He was big, the deserter — no. She corrected herself. He *had been* big; whatever he was now, he was a big man gone to seed, muscle melted off the frame like tallow, a sword worn at his hip with the easy contempt of a tool one has stopped believing in. His uniform was regulation except for the collar, which had been unpicked — she noticed everything, it was her affliction — and where the crown's stitched sigil had been removed, the thread-holes remained, a little constellation of absence. Soldiers called him Cassian, when they called him anything, and mostly they didn't call him anything, because he had done something at some point that had earned him this duty — prisoner-wrangling, escort filth, the work nobody wanted — and the something was not spoken of either.

He was watching her. Had been, she realized, since the ford. Not the way the other soldiers watched the prisoners, which was the way one watches livestock or weather. He watched her the way she watched texts in forbidden languages: with hunger wearing the mask of patience.

When she caught him at it — dusk, the lanterns coming up one by one along their invisible circuit, the camp settling into its nightly fiction of safety — he didn't look away. Most people looked away. Looking away was courtesy, was survival, was the whole grammar of the valley. This man held her gaze across the fireless camp with the lantern-light doing its impossible shadows toward him, and he tilted his head, very slightly, like a man reading a difficult line for the second time.

Paloma looked away first. Filed it. *One anomaly, unresolved.*

He came to her that night. She heard him before she saw him — no, that was wrong; she heard nothing, which was itself the tell, because the sentries' boots had been making their rounds in a slow eight-count and the eight-count stopped, and silence has a texture, and the texture changed.

"Don't," he said softly, when she tensed. "Don't call out. It'll go worse for both of us."

"For both of us," Paloma repeated. "Interesting accounting. What do I lose?"

Something moved in the dark that might have been a smile. "Quick," he said. "Good. Scribes usually take longer to get mean." He crouched beside her, close enough that she could see him in the lantern-glow: forties, maybe, gray coming into a black beard, and eyes that had the specific shine of a man running a fever of purpose. "You're the temple scribe. Thackeray."

"I'm a debtor."

"You're the scribe they pulled out of a valley full of scribes, special, by name, on the steward's own order. I read the manifest." He said it without pride, the way you'd confess to a theft. "I read everything. It's a habit from before." From before, she understood, meant before the unpicked collar. "So here's my question, Thackeray, and think hard before you answer, because I'll know if you lie — I'm good at lies, I used to be paid for better ones than yours will ever be." He leaned closer. She smelled woodsmoke and old leather and, beneath it, faintly, ink. Ink. On a soldier. "What did you steal from the archive?"

The night went very still. Even the lanterns seemed to dim by a courteous degree.

"I don't know what you—"

"Your shirtfront," Cassian said, "has been bulging in the same shape for three days. You touch it when you think no one's watching, which is forty times a day, minimum — you'd be amazed what a man sees when everyone thinks he's a half-wit deserter fit only for corpse-duty. You sleep on your right side to keep it from creasing. And today, crossing the scree field, you fell, and you put your arm out to protect *that* before you protected your own face." He sat back on his heels. "Nobody protects paper over their face unless the paper is worth more than the face. So. What did you steal?"

Paloma's mind, trained by eleven years of provosts, ran the options at speed. Deny: he wouldn't believe it, and denial would end the conversation, and the conversation was the first interesting thing to happen to her since the stars went out. Confess: dangerous, possibly fatal, definitely stupid. Deflect—

"I copied it," she heard herself say. "I didn't steal it. Stealing implies removal from legitimate custody. I was *assigned* it. Transcription of restricted pre-warding materials, in exchange for a reduction of principal. The provost handed me the pages himself." A pause. "Although I may have failed to return them."

Cassian stared at her. Then he laughed — one syllable, strangled, careful, the laugh of a man who has forgotten how and is rediscovering it painfully, like walking on a broken foot. "*Failed to return them,*" he breathed. "Saints. You really are one of them. You really think in those little columns."

"And you really read manifests. So you tell me, deserter: why does the steward of the crown want a debt-scribe from a valley temple fetched by name? Because I've been wondering, and the numbers don't balance. I'm nobody. My debts are ordinary. My family is ordinary. There are a hundred scribes in the Temple of Quiet Hours with cleaner hands and faster pens."

"Do you believe that?" he asked. Gently, which was worse than the fever-shine. "Do you actually believe you're nobody? Or is that just what the ledger taught you to write?"

Above them, the stars — the new sky, the stripped sky, the sky with every star plucked out of it like raisins from a bun — wheeled blank and enormous. Paloma had not yet gotten used to it. In the valley you saw perhaps forty stars through the ward-glow, familiar as neighbors. Here the darkness overhead was a vast unmarked page, and she found she could not look at it for long without feeling that she was falling upward into it.

"Three days ago," she said, "every star in the sky went out at once. I was holding a cup of tea. The wards shattered — we heard it, all of us, like glass breaking somewhere behind the moon. And the next morning soldiers came through the pass for the first time in living memory, and took me." She turned to look at him fully. "You were on this side of the pass. You've lived under this empty sky your whole life. So you tell me, deserter: what did we lose? What was the ward *for*?"

For a long moment Cassian said nothing. The lantern-light pulsed once, softly, like a sleeping thing's breath. When he finally spoke, his voice had dropped to almost nothing, a voice meant to slide under doors.

"That," he said, "is the right question. Which means you're the right person, which means I'm either about to save my neck or hang us both." He glanced at the sentry-line, at the dark, at her. "Tomorrow, when we make the river crossing — the wide one, with the ferry ruins. There'll be a moment. Maybe ten seconds, maybe less. When it comes, Thackeray, you can stay with the column and walk safe into the capital and let the steward do whatever it is stewards do with girls who carry stolen pages in their shirts."

His eyes were very bright in the false light.

"Or you can run."

* * *

She did not sleep again. How could she — with the sentence *or you can run* sitting in her chest like a swallowed coal, burning a hole shaped exactly like a choice?

Run where? To what? She was Paloma Thackeray, indentured, indebted, twenty-six years old, and she had never in her life been farther from the temple bells than a day's walk. Every instinct bred into her by the Quiet Hours said: *stay with the column, stay with the known, a debt in the hand is worth two in the dark.* But the coal kept burning, and under it, faint and persistent, she could hear the other voice — the one that had made her copy forbidden texts at midnight, the one that had asked the provost *what was the Choir?* and been told *never mind* — and that voice said: *the road remembers footsteps, Paloma. What will it remember of yours?*

Toward dawn, shivering, she finally drew out the three stolen pages.

The lantern-light was enough to read by — of course it was; it was enough to live by, apparently, this flameless gold that cast its shadows the wrong way. She unfolded the brittle paper with hands that shook, and the old script swam up at her, dense and archaic, the pre-warding hand she had spent three guilty nights learning:

*...and the Choir shall stand at the border of the vale, and sing the Bound One fast, and the price of the singing shall be paid in voices, generation unto generation, until the debt is—*

The rest was torn away. Of course it was. Everything was torn away; everything ended mid-clause; the whole world was a ledger with the crucial column ripped out.

*Until the debt is—*

Paid? Renewed? Collected?

Paloma folded the pages away, pressed her palm flat over them, and looked east, toward the capital she could not yet see, where a steward wanted her, where a king wore a crown over something, where answers waited in gilded rooms for a girl who had never wanted anything but to balance her accounts and be left alone.

Across the dead camp, in the dark, she felt — rather than saw — Cassian watching her read.

The coal in her chest burned steadily on.

*Run*, it said. And for the first time in her careful, counted, catalogued life, Paloma Thackeray began, secretly, to plan exactly how.
