## 5. The Thing Wearing the Crown

They brought her to the capital in a coach with barred windows, which the soldiers seemed to think was a courtesy.

"You're not a prisoner," Captain Vess had said on the third morning of the march, not quite meeting Paloma's eyes. "You're collateral. There's a difference."

Paloma had spent four days in the coach deciding what the difference was and had arrived at no answer she liked. A prisoner was held for what she had done. Collateral was held for what someone else might do — or might have done, generations ago, in ink she had never seen. The distinction felt like the kind of thing a scribe would flag in red and send back to the provost for clarification.

The capital announced itself before she saw it. First came the smell: tanneries and smoke and something sweeter underneath, incense burned in quantities no valley temple could afford. Then the sound — a low, ceaseless hum of ten thousand lives overlapping, cart wheels and hawkers and bells that rang the hours out of habit rather than devotion. And then, cresting the last hill, the city itself: white walls gone grey at the seams, slate roofs stacked like scales, and above it all the palace, gilded so heavily along its western face that it caught the afternoon sun and threw it back as if the building itself were proud.

Paloma pressed her forehead to the bars and looked, because looking was all she had ever been permitted to do freely.

It was larger than any city in the pre-warding texts. Larger than the maps suggested. She had copied descriptions of it once — *the seat of the Aldric line, seven generations upon the throne* — and had imagined something like the temple scaled up, more candles, longer corridors. What she saw instead was a wound of wealth in a landscape of want. The road into the city passed through districts where the houses leaned against each other like tired old men, and then, without transition, into streets where every window had glass in it. Glass. Whole panes of it, in windows, where children in the valley pressed their noses to oiled paper if they were lucky.

"Keep back from the window," said the soldier riding beside the coach. Not unkindly. Which was somehow worse.

The palace swallowed them through gates tall enough to admit a church. Courtyard after courtyard, each one quieter and cleaner than the last, until the silence had a polished quality, like the inside of a bell. Paloma's hands, still smudged faintly with the iron-gall ink that no amount of scrubbing ever fully lifted, felt obscene against the pale stone balustrade they walked her past.

They put her in a room with a bed, a basin, a window that looked out on an inner garden, and a lock on the outside of the door. Dinner came on a tray: meat she couldn't identify, bread soft as cloud, wine dark as the ledger ink. She ate because refusing food was a debt incurred against herself, and she had learned long ago that you never let your body go into arrears.

On the tray's edge, folded beneath the napkin, was a card of heavy cream stock with a single line in a secretary's precise hand:

*His Majesty will receive the scribe Thackeray at court, tomorrow, the ninth hour.*

*Receive.* As though she were an ambassador. As though she had a country.

She did not sleep well. The bed was too soft; it received her body the way water receives a stone, without opinion, and she woke a dozen times reaching for the hard pallet of the scriptorium dormitory and finding silk instead. In the gaps between sleeping, she lay staring at the ceiling and rehearsing the only comfort she owned: *I am a copyist. I know how to be invisible in a room full of important people. I have done it my whole life.*

She repeated it like a warding charm. It worked about as well as one.

* * *

Court, it turned out, was not a place but a performance, and everyone in the hall knew their part except her.

The throne room was vast and cold despite the braziers, its ceiling painted with constellations — real ones, she realized with a jolt, the actual stars of the pre-warding sky rendered in gold leaf across a field of lapis. Someone had known them, once. Someone had stood under the open night and mapped them true. Now the only stars left in the world hung sixty feet above a marble floor, and they were paint.

The courtiers wore the season's colors — deep blues and silver, she gathered, though fashion meant nothing to her — and arranged themselves in a shape so exact it must have been enforced by generations of social ruin. Paloma was placed near the back, beside a woman whose title she didn't catch, and given one instruction by a chamberlain with a voice like dry paper: *"When His Majesty speaks, you look at His Majesty."*

At the ninth hour exactly, trumpets. Doors at the far end swinging wide. And the king came in.

Paloma had seen royalty before, at a distance — a provincial governor's procession through the valley when she was nine, all plumes and horses and borrowed grandeur. She had expected this to be that, scaled up. It was not that.

King Aldric was a big man gone slightly soft with age, silver-bearded, dressed in a robe of cloth-of-gold that must have weighed as much as Wren. He walked slowly, leaning on no cane, and his face was the face of a grandfather carved onto a coin: benign, distant, finished. The court bowed in waves as he passed, a wind moving through wheat.

And Paloma looked at him, because she had been told to, and because looking was the one freedom she hoarded —

— and the bottom dropped out of the world.

There was something behind his eyes.

Not madness. She had seen madness once, in the temple almoner who heard bells no one else could hear, and madness moved fast, darting, wet-eyed. This was slow. This was *deep*. When King Aldric's gaze swept the hall, it swept like a lantern beam, and behind the light of it, far down in the wells of his eyes, something turned over. Something vast adjusting its weight in a space too small for it. The way a whale turns in a bay too shallow, Paloma thought absurdly, the way something that has been asleep for a very long time shifts and finds the walls closer than it remembered.

No one else saw. That was the horror of it. Three hundred people stood in that hall, and every one of them looked at their king with the ordinary mixture of boredom and fear that courtiers reserve for monarchs everywhere, and not one of them flinched. Whatever lived behind those eyes had learned, over years or decades or centuries, exactly how much light to let through.

Then the king spoke, and Paloma understood that she had been wrong about which part was the worst.

"Rise," he said, "and be welcome."

His voice was a fine voice. Deep, measured, trained by a lifetime of command. But underneath the words — underneath, the way a river runs under ice — there was singing.

Not sound. She strained and confirmed it: no one else reacted, the acoustics carried nothing strange. It was not in the air. It was in the *voice*, the way rot is in a beam — the surface sound perfectly sound, and beneath it a second voice, older, patient, humming a melody with no beginning that she could find. It rose and fell with the king's speech like a shadow rising and falling with a body. When he paused between sentences, the song did not pause. When he pronounced a sentence of judgment on some unlucky landholder — a fine, a forfeiture, delivered with coin-bright mercy — the melody beneath swelled, just slightly, the way a throat swells around a swallow.

Paloma gripped the rail in front of her until her knuckles ached.

*I'm ill,* she told herself. *The road, the wine, the sleepless bed. I'm a tired scribe hallucinating music in a cold room.*

But she had spent eleven years copying texts in dead languages, and the first discipline of that work is listening — hearing the structure under the surface, the grammar beneath the words, the second meaning folded inside the first. Her whole life had trained her to hear what was written underneath. And underneath the King of the Valley, written in a hand older than the kingdom, was a song.

She began, helplessly, to transcribe it. In her head, because she had no paper and would die before asking for any. The melody had a mode she'd never encountered — not major, not minor, something that sat between grief and hunger. It cycled. It cycled the way a debtor's interest cycles, growing by increments too small to notice until the sum is unpayable. And woven through it, recurring every ninth phrase like a refrain, was a single figure that made her stomach clench with recognition she couldn't name.

She had heard it before. Where? Where?

The audience dragged on. Petitions rose and fell. A delegation from the eastern orchards knelt and presented tribute and were praised in the king's doubled voice. A woman pleaded a border dispute and was ruled against with such gentle reasonableness that she thanked him through tears. Through all of it the thing behind the eyes watched, and the thing beneath the voice sang, and the court saw a king.

Near the end of the session, the chamberlain touched Paloma's elbow. "His Majesty will receive the scribe now."

The hall emptied around her in reverse order of importance, courtiers draining away like water finding cracks, until the throne room held perhaps thirty people: guards, ministers, a cluster of clerks at their desks along the wall, and the king on his dais with the painted stars above him.

Paloma walked the length of that marble floor alone, and with every step the song grew louder — not in volume but in *clarity*, the way a text resolves as you bring it close to the candle. By the time she reached the foot of the dais and sank into the bow the chamberlain had drilled into her that morning, she could distinguish layers in it. A drone beneath, endless. A middle line, winding. And threading through both, high and thin and wrong, a descant that sounded almost — almost — human.

Almost the way a wax mask looks almost like a face.

"Scribe Thackeray." The king's voice, addressing her directly for the first time, was warm as a hearth. "You've come a long way from your quiet hours."

The words were a kindness. The song beneath them bent around her name like a hook around bait.

"Your Majesty honors me," she said, to the floor.

"Look up, child. I don't bite."

She looked up. She met his eyes, because she was commanded to, and the thing behind them looked back.

For one instant — one — the mask slipped. Not the face; the face stayed perfect, grandfatherly, mildly amused. But the attention behind it *leaned forward*, the way a reader leans toward a page, and Paloma felt herself being read. Ledgered. Assessed for value, entered in a column. The sensation was physical, a pressure against the inside of her skull, delicate and total, like fingers counting coins by touch through a purse of skin.

And in that instant the melody resolved, and she knew where she had heard the refrain before.

The chapel hymnal. The forbidden pre-warding text she had copied for the provost, forty nights of her indenture ago — the founding liturgy, the one with the passage about the Choir. The marginal notation, the one the provost had made her strike from the fair copy: *the melody of the bound one, preserved against need.*

She had copied its notes. She had hummed it absently while grinding ink, for weeks. It was in her head the way the alphabet was in her head.

The king smiled.

"You sing," he said. It was not a question, and it was not about the choir loft of her childhood, and every hair on her arms stood up beneath her sleeves.

"I — copy, Your Majesty. Texts. Music, sometimes, as text."

"Everything sings," said King Aldric kindly, "if you write it down correctly." He gestured, a small motion, and the chamberlain appeared at her shoulder. "We are finished for today. See the scribe is comfortable. We will have further use for her."

*Further use.* The phrasing of a tool. Of a pen, a seal, a key.

Paloma bowed again and walked backward three steps, as drilled, before turning. She kept her pace even the whole length of that impossible floor. She did not run until the doors closed behind her, and then she did not run either, because running would draw eyes, and so she walked very fast through corridors that all looked alike, breathing through her nose, one hand pressed flat against her sternum as if to hold her heart in its column.

In her room, with the door locked from outside and the afternoon light dying in the garden below, she took the stub of pencil and the scrap of wrapping paper she had saved from dinner — a scribe hoards writing surfaces the way a miser hoards coin — and wrote down the refrain from memory. All nine phrases. The mode. The intervals.

Then she sat on the too-soft bed and looked at what she had written, and understood, with the calm that arrives when terror exceeds some internal threshold and simply becomes fact:

The thing wearing the crown was singing the binding melody. The song her ancestors had used to cage it. It sang it constantly, endlessly, the way a prisoner rattles the bars — except the prisoner had learned the rattle by heart, had worn it smooth, had made the bars into a lullaby.

Which meant it was not waiting to get out.

It was learning how to make the cage sing along.

* * *

In the dark of his own bed, in the body that had been his for fifty-nine years and someone else's for twenty-two, Aldric dreamed of the sea.

He always dreamed of the sea. He had never seen the sea. He suspected — in the small, sand-scoured strip of himself that remained his own, the strip he kept hidden even from his dreams, the way a man hides a seedcake in his boot — that the sea belonged to the other one. That he dreamed borrowed dreams now, the way he spoke with a borrowed warmth and loved his daughter with a borrowed tenderness that was nonetheless real, that was the realest thing left, because the other one had studied love for two decades and reproduced it faithfully, the way a good copyist reproduces a hand.

*A copyist,* thought the remnant of Aldric, drifting. *It thinks of itself as a copyist sometimes. It has picked up my word for it.*

Tonight the dream-sea was calm, and that frightened him more than storms did, because calm seas meant it was content, and contentment in that thing was never rest. Contentment was digestion.

He stood on the dream-shore and felt the great presence move beneath the water of himself, slow and enormous, and he did the only thing he could still do, the thing he had done every night for twenty-two years, the rebellion so small that the entity had never bothered to crush it:

He counted.

One. Two. Three. The names of his dead. Four. Five. His daughter's birthday, the true one, not the public one. Six. Seven. The color of the valley in autumn, which he had seen once, as a young prince, before. Eight. Nine.

Nine. Always stop at nine. Nine was the number in the song, the refrain's length, and he did not know why that mattered, only that stopping at nine felt like keeping one door of himself unlatched, one room the tenant could not enter.

Beneath the water, the vast thing turned in its shallow bay, and its attention brushed past the shore of him, and he heard it — as he always heard it, muffled, translated, the way one hears a neighbor's music through a wall — considering the girl.

*The scribe,* it sang, in a voice like interest compounding. *The little copyist. She heard me today. She wrote me down.*

And the remnant of Aldric, standing alone on a shore in a sea that was not his, felt something he had not felt in twenty-two years.

Hope, terrible and small, like a star flickering out — or flickering on.

*She heard me,* the thing sang, pleased, hungry, patient. *Good. Bring me the blood. The cage was built by that hand. It will be opened by that hand, or closed forever by it.*

Aldric counted to nine, and woke, and wept without knowing why, as he did most mornings now, his borrowed face wet with his own salt, the one currency the thing had never learned to counterfeit.

---

Far below the throne room, in an office that smelled of sealing wax and cold tea, the steward read the day's report by lamplight. His pen hovered over the page for a long moment.

*The scribe recognized the refrain,* he wrote at last, in a hand so old no living scholar could date it. *Excellent. Begin the preparations. And double the guard on the archives — if she remembers what she copied, we may not need to persuade her at all.*

He signed it with a name that had died nine hundred years ago, blew out the lamp, and went to see about the choir.
