## 6. A Debt Paid in Blood

They did not take her to a cell.

That was the first thing Paloma noticed, and the noticing frightened her more than chains would have. Cells were for people whose fates had not yet been decided. She was led instead up — up and up, through stairwells of pale stone worn hollow at the centers by centuries of feet, past windows that showed her a city she had no name for, all slate roofs and copper spires gone green with rain, until her legs ached and her breath came shallow and the air itself changed, growing thinner and sweeter, like the air above a candle.

The soldiers who had seized her at the temple had not spoken to her since the pass. They spoke *around* her, in the clipped shorthand of men who had done this many times: *the scribe goes to the Steward's wing, not the holding cells; the scribe is to be fed; the scribe is not to be harmed.* As though she were a ledger being carried carefully between offices. As though her value had been assessed and entered somewhere in a column she could not read.

She was fed. That was its own kind of horror. A woman with kind eyes and a guard's posture brought her bread still warm from an oven somewhere below, and salted fish, and a cup of wine so dark it was nearly black. Paloma ate because her body insisted, wept briefly and without permission while chewing, and hated herself for both. The bread tasted like nothing. Grief had burned out her tongue.

*Wren*, she thought, for the hundredth time since the stars went out. He had been standing in the scriptorium doorway when the soldiers came through the cloister. She had seen him — small, freckled, his ink-stained fingers still curled around the stylus he was never supposed to remove from his desk — and then a sergeant's shoulder had blocked her view, and the world had become marching boots and cold mountain wind and the terrible nakedness of a sky with no stars in it.

He would be safe. The temple took care of its own. The temple took care of its debts, too, and that thought sat in her chest like a swallowed stone: they had given her up. Provost Ellery had stood on the steps with his hands folded into his sleeves and watched them put her in the cart, and he had said, quietly, so only she could hear: *"Serve them well, child. It may count against your balance."*

Even now. Even at the end of the world, there was a balance, and her name written in it, and interest accruing.

* * *

The steward came for her at dusk.

She knew him before he introduced himself, because everything about him was arranged — his grey coat buttoned to the throat, his white hair bound back so tightly it seemed painted on, his boots polished to the dull shine of old pewter. He moved like a man who had spent a lifetime ensuring that no part of him ever surprised another part. In the valley, only the provost carried himself that way. Power, Paloma was beginning to understand, always dressed as tidiness.

"Paloma Thackeray," he said. Not a question. He held a slim folder of papers, and did not open it. "I am Corvess. Steward of the Royal Household. You will have questions. I am going to answer three of them, and then I am going to ask you one, and your answer will determine what happens next. Do you understand?"

"I understand you've kidnapped me," Paloma said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. Somewhere behind her ribs, the scribe who had survived twenty-two years of compound interest found her spine. "That seems like the relevant thing to understand."

Something flickered across Corvess's face — not amusement, but the place where amusement might once have lived before it was paved over. "Kidnapped. Yes. The temple owed the crown eleven thousand crowns in back-tithe, accrued over sixty years. They offered collateral. You were the collateral." He let that settle. "I want to be clear that I did not choose you arbitrarily, Miss Thackeray. Nothing about you is arbitrary."

"I'm a copyist. Third-rate. My handwriting slants left."

"Your handwriting slants left," Corvess agreed, "because you are left-handed and were beaten as a child until you learned to use your right, and you revert when you are tired or frightened. You are currently doing both." He opened the folder at last and laid a single page on the table between them. "You are also the last living heir of Thackeray-of-the-Valley, which is a name you will know from the founding inscriptions in your temple's oldest wall. Tell me what you copied there. As a girl. Before they restricted access to the undercroft."

Paloma looked at the page. It was a transcription — in a careful modern hand — of text she knew better than her own name. She had copied it herself, two years ago, as part of her bargain with the provost: forbidden pre-warding texts, transcribed in exchange for a reduction of her debt. She had never asked where those transcriptions went. She had told herself she didn't want to know. Now here was the answer, sitting on a table in a tower above a city whose name she didn't know, in the possession of a man with paved-over eyes.

She read it anyway. Reading was the one thing fear had never been able to take from her.

*...and the Choir shall bind the Unnamed beneath the valley-stone, seven voices willing, and the line of Thackeray shall keep the words, generation unto generation, until the sky is safe to open...*

"You went through the temple archives," she said slowly. "My transcriptions. They came here."

"They came to me," Corvess said. "Everything comes to me eventually. It is the nature of stewardship." He drew out a second page, older, the vellum stained amber at the edges. On it was a drawing — a family tree, rendered in the formal style of royal genealogists, except that this tree was wrong. Branches had been pruned. Names had been struck through in red ink, one after another after another, down through the centuries, until at the bottom of the page, in fresh black ink, sat a single surviving name.

Hers.

"Do you know what happened to the other branches?" Corvess asked.

Paloma's mouth was dry. "People die."

"People died *conveniently*," Corvess said. "Fever. Childbirth. A fall from a hayloft. A fire that took a farmhouse outside Vell Hollow and everyone sleeping in it. I will not insult you by pretending I don't know how this looks. But I did not prune this tree, Miss Thackeray. Time pruned it. And the crown has watched it, and recorded it, and waited — because the crown has known for six hundred years that someday the binding under your valley would begin to fail, and that when it did, the only hand that could renew it would be a Thackeray's."

"The binding." The word came out of her strangely, as if someone else had borrowed her voice. "The wards. The wards protect us. Everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows what they were taught," Corvess said, "which is what the founders wanted known. The wards keep the valley safe. The wards hold back the wild magic beyond the pass. The wards are why your stars—" he paused, and for the first time something moved behind his face that was not arrangement, something tired and old and grim, "—your stars went out four nights ago, because the wards are failing, and everything they have ever held is pushing outward through the cracks."

He leaned forward. This close, Paloma could see that his eyes were the color of rainwater over slate, and that there were lines around them carved deep enough to hold shadow.

"There is something beneath your valley," he said. "There has been since before your temple, before your town, before the first fence post. Your ancestors found it. Your ancestors *bound* it — seven singers, giving themselves willingly, sealing it under the stone with a song. And the descendants of those singers stayed, generation after generation, keeping the words, keeping the song alive in fragments, never knowing why their grandmothers hummed certain melodies at certain hours, never knowing why the temple forbade the old texts. They thought they were being protected." His voice dropped. "They were being *used*, Miss Thackeray. Every soul in that valley is a brick in a wall, and none of them knows they're a wall. Except you. Because you read. Because you copied the forbidden things. Because some stubborn, debt-ridden, left-handed scribbler kept asking questions the provost paid her to stop asking."

Paloma sat very still. The wine-dark cup trembled faintly where her hand rested beside it, and she flattened her palm to stop it.

Outside the narrow window, the starless dark pressed against the glass like a face.

"What do you want from me," she said. Not a question either.

* * *

What he wanted, it turned out, was everything.

Corvess laid it out with the terrible clarity of a man reading terms of contract, which, Paloma realized as he spoke, was exactly what he was doing. There would be a rite. There was always a rite; the founders had left instructions, and the instructions required a hand of the binding line — not merely present, but *willing*. The original choir had sung freely, and so the renewal must be sung freely, or the seal would take crooked and fail faster.

"You will come to the palace scriptorium tomorrow," Corvess said. "You will study the founder's texts — the true ones, not the fragments your temple hoarded. You will learn the rite. And when the crown determines the moment, you will sing your part, willingly, and the thing beneath your valley will be sealed for another six hundred years, and the stars will return, and your people will go on not knowing, safely, forever."

"And if I refuse?"

Corvess folded his hands. "Then you go to the Crown Workhouse at Marrowgate, where your debt transfers and compounds at the standard rate, and where the average indenture, for a debtor of your principal, runs nineteen years. You are twenty-two. You can do the arithmetic; I'm told you're good at it." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "There is a third option, of course. You could attempt escape. I mention it only to save us both time. The city gates are sealed. The passes are garrisoned. And even if you got out — even if you walked home — the binding fails within the decade regardless, and whatever is under your valley comes up through the cracks, and it will not be gentle, and it will not be slow. Refusal doesn't free you, Miss Thackeray. Refusal just means you'll be home when it ends."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere far below, a bell rang the hour, and the sound climbed the tower like water filling a vessel.

Paloma thought about the workhouse. She had copied enough debt records to know what Marrowgate meant: the dye-vats that ate skin, the looms that ate fingers, the dormitory fevers that swept through every winter like a tithe collector of their own. Nineteen years. She would be forty-one when she came out, if she came out, with nothing to show for it but a number finally crossed off a ledger.

She thought about Wren, humming his little made-up tunes in the scriptorium, safe in the valley — safe inside the cage, inside the lie, inside a town built on top of a sealed hunger, every lullaby a lock and nobody the wiser.

And she thought — this was the shameful thought, the one that arrived unbidden and would not leave — about the phrase from the forbidden text she had copied two years ago. *Seven voices willing.* Willing. The founders had chosen. Had anyone asked the valley? Had anyone asked *her*, before tonight, whether she consented to be born into a wall?

"No," Paloma said.

Corvess's eyebrows rose a fraction.

"I mean — not yet. Not 'no' forever. I mean I won't answer tonight, and I won't answer tomorrow, and I won't sing anything *willingly* for a crown that let my whole life be engineered toward this moment without telling me." Her heart was slamming against her ribs, but her voice held, and she marveled at it from a distance, the way she marveled at good penmanship in an old book. "You want a willing singer, Steward. Then understand what you've bought. Willingness isn't a lever you pull. It's a thing you earn. And right now you have earned exactly nothing."

For a long moment Corvess simply looked at her, and Paloma had the unsettling sense of being read — thoroughly, line by line, like a document he intended to file.

"Complicity," he said at last, softly. "That's what cooperation costs you. I know. I have served this household for thirty years, and I have signed things I will answer for in whatever court exists after death. You will sign things too, if you do this. There is no version of survival that leaves your hands clean." He gathered his pages, squared their edges precisely against the table. "But consider, before you decide what your hands are worth: the alternative to complicity is not innocence. It is only a different grave."

He paused at the door.

"One more thing, since you strike me as a woman who will find out anyway. The king will hold open court in three days' time — the Feast of the Founding, though the founding it celebrates is not the one your temple teaches. You will attend. You will see what it is we serve, and what it is we are trying to contain, and you will make your choice with your eyes open." Something crossed his face then, quick and involuntary — grief, or its fossil. "Pray you are a slower reader than I was."

The door closed behind him. The lock did not turn. Paloma understood that too: she was not a prisoner. Prisoners can run. She was collateral, and collateral stays where it is stored.

She sat by the window a long time, watching the starless dark, turning the word over and over in her mind like a coin she couldn't stop biting.

*Complicity.*

Below her, the city glittered with lantern-light, thousands of small warm fires, all of them burning atop the same buried secret. Above her, nothing. The sky was a locked door with the handle removed.

And it was there, in that long grey silence, that she heard it — faint, from the corridor outside, half-swallowed by stone: footsteps stopping at her door, and a low voice, a stranger's voice, murmuring to the guard on watch. Words she shouldn't have been able to catch. Words that slipped through the gap under the door like smoke:

*"—the deserter asks again. Says tell her the lowlands road is still open, if she's the Thackeray. Says he has something of hers. Says to tell her: the fragment from the chapel walls."*

Paloma went very still.

She had never seen a chapel wall in her life. But she had copied the phrase *seven voices willing* with her own left-slanting hand, and somewhere in the tower below her, a man who should not know her name had just used a word that existed in exactly one place she had ever read:

*Fragment.*

She pressed her palm flat against the cold window glass, over the empty sky, and began, very quietly, to plan.
