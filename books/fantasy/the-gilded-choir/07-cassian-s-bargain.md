## 7. Cassian's Bargain

Cassian had learned to sleep in thirds.

A third of the night for the body, a third for the watch, and a third for the dreams that came anyway — the bridge at Vell's Ford, the mud going red, Sergeant Halloway's mouth opening and opening like a landed fish while the king's own banners burned behind him. He had learned that a man who slept all at once was a man who woke up dead. So he slept in thirds, in the lee of a supply wagon that smelled of tallow and wet rope, with his back against a wheel and his knife loose in its sheath, and he watched the scribe across the fire do exactly the same thing without knowing she was doing it.

Paloma Thackeray slept in thirds too. He'd noticed it on the second night out of the pass. She didn't sprawl or curl; she folded herself down in stages, hands tucked, eyes closing by degrees, and twice each night her lashes would lift — quick, silent inventories of the camp, the guards, the sky — before sliding shut again. A woman counting exits even in her sleep. The temple had taught her something, then, beyond ink and ledgers. Or the temple had *made* her learn it, which amounted to the same thing.

She was not what he'd expected when the quartermaster had assigned him to the baggage detail. He had expected a trembling clerk, someone who wept at the first hard mile and had to be dragged by the collar through the second. Instead she walked twelve hours a day on soft scholar's feet without complaint, ate whatever she was given down to the last crumb, and watched everything — the soldiers' drill rotations, the officers' badges, the way the column tightened whenever they passed a shrine with its face knocked off. She watched the way he used to watch, back when watching had still been his job.

That was the problem. That was why he hadn't slept a full third in four nights.

* * *

The column made camp that evening in the shadow of the Torvane aqueduct, a broken stone spine marching away east into hills that were no longer valley and not yet lowland. Cassian had crossed under those arches three years ago going the other direction, nineteen years old and singing with the rest of them because you sang on the march or you thought about home, and thinking about home was worse. Now the arches stood black against a bruised orange sky, and the road beneath them remembered footsteps — he could feel it faintly through his boot soles, that old pre-warding magic the engineers pretended wasn't there, the road humming with every soldier who'd ever marched it, a thousand boots layered into the stone like rings in a tree.

The scribe felt it too. He saw her stop mid-stride as they came under the first arch, one hand drifting up to touch the pillar, her fingers spreading against the stone the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. Her lips moved. Not prayer — he knew army prayers, and hers had the wrong rhythm. Counting, maybe. Reading. Everything was reading to her.

"You'll want to wash your hands after," he said, coming up beside her. "Roads like this remember soldiers. Mostly they remember us dying."

She pulled her hand back as if scalded. "You can feel that?"

"Anyone from past the pass can feel it. You're the first valley-born I've seen notice." He kept his voice flat, conversational, the voice of a man discussing weather. "Interesting."

"I'm sure I don't know what you mean." But her chin came up, and there it was again — that flicker under the meekness, the ledger-clerk's face over something quicker and harder. "Is there a reason you keep finding reasons to talk to me, deserter?"

The word landed where she meant it to land. He let it sit there. Around them the camp was building itself out of noise — mallets on stakes, the groan of wagons, a farrier swearing at a horse with opinions about shoes. Nobody looked at them. Nobody ever looked closely at Cassian; that was the whole art of him now.

"Corporal," he said. "I kept the rank when I stopped keeping the oath. And yes. There's a reason."

Her eyes went, briefly and professionally, to the nearest guards — forty paces off at the picket line, backs turned, arguing about dice. Then back to him. He revised his estimate of her upward again. She'd have made a decent quartermaster. She'd have made a terrifying spy.

"Not here," she said.

"No," he agreed. "Not here."

* * *

He chose the hour past middle watch, when the sentries' lanterns burned lowest and the wind came down off the aqueduct carrying the smell of old stone and colder country. He'd spent two evenings preparing: learning the rotation, noting that the fourth sentry drank, that the officer of the watch took his supper late and his sleep earlier, that the baggage corral sat in the aqueduct's blind spot where the arches threw shadows deep enough to drown in.

She was awake when he came to the wagon where they'd bedded her down among the tribute chests. Of course she was. She lay with her eyes closed, but her breathing was wrong for sleep, too shallow and too even, the breathing of a woman performing sleep for an audience. He crouched an arm's length away — close enough to speak low, far enough that she wouldn't feel cornered — and waited.

After a long moment her eyes opened. In the dark they were just wet gleams, but he felt their weight settle on him like a hand.

"You've been circling for three days," she whispered. "Say it or go away."

"Straight to business. They said temple folk liked preamble."

"They said wrong. Preamble costs ink." She sat up slowly, keeping the blanket around her shoulders like a cloak, like armor. "Talk."

So he talked. Low and fast, the way you talked when every word had a price tag.

"My name is Cassian Vale. Born in Marrow's Bend, lowlands, which is a village that doesn't exist anymore because the crown burned it in the spring of the purges. I served six years in the Third Foot. Vell's Ford broke me — not the fighting, the *after*. We were ordered to hold a bridge so the tax wagons could get clear, and we held it, and when it was done the captain ordered us to burn the ferry village on the far bank because the ferrymen had tipped off the rebels. Fishermen. Old men. One girl my sister's age." His voice stayed level. He'd practiced that levelness until it was load-bearing. "I put my sword down in the river and walked. That's the shape of it. Now the interesting part."

"The part where you tell me why I shouldn't call the sentries."

"That's the very interesting part, yes." He reached inside his coat — slowly, telegraphing every inch, letting her see his hand empty until the moment it wasn't — and drew out the oilcloth packet. It was the size of a prayer book, wrapped tight, tied with waxed cord. He set it on the wagon bed between them and did not touch it again. "Three weeks ago I deserted properly. Not running — walking, with a purpose. I went back through the archive depot at Cael Sarrow, where I'd been posted as a courier, and I stole this out of the sealed vaults. Which makes me, technically, a thief of state secrets as well as an oath-breaker. The penalty for the first is flogging. For the second, hanging. For the third—" he tapped the packet once, lightly, "—they don't have a word yet. They'd invent one."

Paloma didn't reach for it. Her hands stayed knotted in the blanket, but he saw her fingers flex, saw the hunger she was holding down the way you held down a lid on a boiling pot.

"What is it?"

"A fragment. Six pages, maybe a seventh gone to rot. Pre-warding script, older than anything your provost would dare shelve. I carried dispatches long enough to recognize seal-forms, and these carry the seal of the First Choir." He watched her face carefully as he said it. Nothing. No recognition. Good — that meant she hadn't seen the phrase yet, or hadn't understood it. "The binding rite. The one that raised the wards around your valley four hundred years ago. Or part of it. Enough of it."

The silence stretched. Somewhere down the picket line a horse stamped, and the sound cracked the night like a whip.

"Why," she said finally, "would a deserter risk hanging for old paper?"

"Because of what the steward's men are doing with the new paper." He leaned closer, and now the flatness cracked despite himself, and what showed underneath was the thing he'd been carrying since Cael Sarrow, hot as a swallowed coal. "There's a requisition order, scribe. I copied it out myself before I ran. The Office of the Ward is recalling every pre-warding text in every provincial archive — burning most, sealing the rest in the capital. And they've doubled the tithe levies on the valley provinces, and they're drafting singers. Not soldiers. *Singers.* Boys with high voices, girls with steady pitch, hauled to the capital in wagons with the tax grain. Somebody in that palace is trying to finish something. Something old. And whatever it is, it started the same week the stars went out."

He saw it hit her. Not dramatically — she was too controlled for drama — but her breath caught, and her eyes went up, reflexively, to the sky above the broken arches. To the nothing there. Four hundred years of stars, and now a ceiling of plain black velvet, and every soul in the column pretending not to be afraid of it.

"You felt the wards break," he said softly. "Everyone from inside the pass felt it. Like a tooth coming out. Don't tell me you didn't."

"I don't know what I felt." But her hand had drifted to her own sternum, pressing, as though something in there ached. "Why bring this to me? I copy ledgers. Debts. Grain tallies. I'm nobody's archivist of forbidden things."

"You're Thackeray." He said the name and watched it strike. "The binding rite has a handline, scribe. Every rite does — the name of the hand that wrote it, witnessed and subscribed. The fragment is damaged there, water-eaten, but the family mark survived. A quill crossed with a key." He let that hang. "Your temple seal has the same device. I checked. I'm not clever enough to have imagined that."

For a moment she was utterly still, the kind of stillness that cost something. Then: "Lots of families use quills."

"And lots of rivers are wet. Come with me anyway."

"There it is." Her laugh was small and had no humor in it at all. "The bargain. I wondered how long the courtesy would last."

"It's not a trap. Traps are quieter."

"Everything's quieter than this." She looked at the oilcloth packet between them the way a starving woman looks at bread she suspects is poisoned. "Say the terms plainly. I've spent my life reading contracts; I know the devil lives in the margins."

He respected that. He laid it out the way he'd rehearsed it, stripping every ornament.

"The column reaches the garrison at Harrowgate in five days. After Harrowgate, the tribute wagons turn south to the capital, and you turn with them — collateral, ward of the crown, however they dress it. I've seen what happens to valley hostages in the capital. They become leverage, and leverage gets spent. South of Harrowgate, though, the road forks, and the west fork runs down into the lowlands — rebel country, what's left of it. There's a settlement called Ashfall Ferry, or there was. People there are doing what people do when the crown burns their villages: surviving, organizing, remembering names. I mean to reach them. I mean to put this fragment in hands that will read it and act, before whoever sits in that palace finishes whatever they're finishing." He paused. "And I need you, because the fragment is half cipher and half dead language, and I read neither. But you — you read everything. I watched you work through the quartermaster's marks in an evening, marks I needed a month to learn. You're the only person in this camp who might make sense of it. So: you come with me, tonight, over the aqueduct and west before the moon sets. In exchange—"

"In exchange," she said, "you want my help reaching the lowlands. That's the trade? My freedom for my literacy?"

"My protection for your help. Freedom's not mine to give. Neither of us owns it out here." He shrugged. "But I know which ditches the patrols watch. I know how to move at night, how to find water, how to make a camp that doesn't smoke. Alone, you'd last four days. With me, you might last the season."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I go alone, and you go south, and we both take our chances." He met her eyes and held them, because this was the part that mattered, the part that separated a bargain from a coercion. "I won't drag you, scribe. I've seen what happens to people who get dragged places. I won't be another hand on your collar. If you stay, I'll even leave the fragment's location in your head — you've seen it now, you could describe the seal to whoever holds you next, buy yourself credit. I'd rather you didn't. But I won't stop you."

That surprised her. He saw it — the small widening of her eyes, the recalculation happening behind them. She'd been braced for a blade and gotten terms instead, and it disoriented her more than a threat would have.

* * *

They negotiated for the better part of an hour, crouched in the wagon's shadow, voices pitched under the wind.

She was harder than he'd expected. She wanted the fragment in her possession from the first step — refused to walk anywhere with it strapped to his back. Fair; he'd have demanded the same. She wanted to know his desertion story in full, dates and units, and he gave it, and he watched her weigh it the way she must have weighed a thousand debtors' oaths, looking for the seam of the lie. She wanted to know what happened to her afterward — if the rebels had no use for her, if the fragment proved worthless, if the whole venture died in a ditch. What was her position then?

"Then you're free to walk wherever you like," he said. "No debt. I don't run accounts."

"Everyone runs accounts." Her voice was bitter and very quiet. "You just haven't noticed yours yet."

He almost told her then — about Marrow's Bend, about the account he carried with every name on the ferry village's unmarked graves, the one he intended to pay down in whatever currency the lowlands would accept. He decided she'd earn that story later, if she earned it. Trust given whole was trust wasted; he'd learned that from Sergeant Halloway, who'd trusted the captain, right up until the torches.

In the end the terms were these: the fragment traveled in her shirt, against her skin, wrapped in its oilcloth. They moved at the change of the watch, over the aqueduct's westernmost arch where the parapet had fallen, down through the bracken on the far side. They avoided the west fork road itself — roads were for carts and corpses, he told her, and she wrote the phrase on her face like a marginal note. Three days to the river country, five to Ashfall Ferry if the ferries still ran. If they met patrols, his voice did the talking and her hands stayed visible and empty. If it went wrong, they did not die for each other; they scattered and each took their own chances, and neither owed the other a ghost.

"Cold terms," she said when he finished.

"Honest ones. Cold keeps."

She was silent a long time. Out past the picket line, the black sky pressed down on the camp like a lid, starless, patient. When she spoke again her voice had changed — lower, stripped of the clerk's polish, down to the bare wood of her.

"I had a friend," she said. "Back in the valley. A boy named Wren who swore he saw a star go out, three nights before they all did. Everyone laughed at him. I didn't laugh. I wrote it in the margin of a ledger, because writing things down is the only power I've ever had." Her jaw tightened. "If I go with you, I'm not running from the crown. I'm running toward an answer. Why the wards fell. What the Choir was. What my family's mark is doing on a four-hundred-year-old rite. You give me that chance, deserter, and I'll give you your translation. But if I find out you knew more than you've told me — if there's a margin note in *your* contract — I will make you regret teaching me how few sentries are actually watching this camp."

Something in his chest loosened, unexpectedly, at the threat. It was the first honest thing anyone had said to him since Vell's Ford.

"Agreed," he said, and spat in his palm, and offered it, because the lowlands still sealed bargains the old way and he wanted her to know exactly what kind of man she was dealing with — a superstitious one, a village one, one whose grandmother had taught him that spit was older and stronger than any crown's wax.

She looked at his hand for a long moment. Then she spat in her own and clasped it, and her grip was dry and strong and did not let go a heartbeat early.

* * *

They went over the fallen arch at the change of the watch, two shadows among many, and the road beneath them hummed faintly with the memory of feet. At the top of the arch Paloma stopped — one breath, no more — and looked back at the camp, at the wagons full of tribute and the tents full of soldiers and the whole armed machinery of a kingdom rolling south under a starless sky. Cassian watched her look, and recognized the expression, because he'd worn it on a riverbank three years ago with his sword going into the water: the face of a person stepping off the edge of the only world they knew, unable to see the bottom.

Then she turned away from it, and put her hand flat against the oilcloth over her heart, and climbed down the far side of the arch into the dark.

He followed. And it was only when his boots found the bracken, only when the camp's lantern-glow had shrunk to a smudge behind them, that he allowed himself the thought he'd been refusing for four days:

The steward's requisition order had listed archives by province. Cael Sarrow. Harrowgate. And third on the list, flagged for immediate seizure, circled twice in the steward's own hand —

*Valley of Quiet Hours. Temple archives. Priority absolute.*

Whoever sat in that palace already knew about the valley's books. And Cassian, walking west through the dark with a stolen rite and a scribe who could read it, could not shake the feeling that they were not running ahead of the crown at all.

They were being allowed to run. And somewhere behind them, patient as the starless sky, something was watching to see where they'd lead.
