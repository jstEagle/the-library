## 8. Wren Follows

The soldiers came to the Temple of Quiet Hours on a Tuesday, which Wren thought was a stupid day for the end of the world.

Tuesdays were ledger days. Tuesdays were the smell of ink and cold tea and Paloma's sleeve brushing his shoulder as she reached past him for a fresh quill. Tuesdays were safe. The world had no business ending on one.

He heard them before he saw them — boots on the temple steps, too many boots, and the provost's voice pitched higher than Wren had ever heard it, all its usual honey scraped away. *"The archives are under seal by order of the crown."* Then something that wasn't words at all, just the sound of a door being broken because someone with authority had decided it should be.

Wren had been shelving psalters in the east gallery, three floors above the scriptorium, close enough to the roof that he could see the slice of sky through the clerestory — the sky with no stars in it anymore, only a flat grey nothing where the stars used to be, like a page someone had scrubbed too hard. He pressed his face to the stone and looked down through the gallery rail into the nave.

They were everywhere. Grey cloaks, steel caps, the crown's sunburst stamped on their shoulders in thread so cheap it looked like rust. They moved through the temple the way water moves through a drowned house — patient, total, indifferent to what they ruined. One of them swept an entire shelf of pre-warding texts into a sack without reading a single spine. Another held the sub-deacon's face against the floor with one boot.

And there, at the edge of it all, hands bound with cord, stood Brother Anselm, who had taught Wren his letters, saying nothing, looking at nothing, wearing the expression of a man who has already decided what he will refuse to say later.

Wren did not think. Thinking would have stopped him. He went up.

The roof access was a hatch behind the reliquary that every child in the temple knew about and no adult admitted existed. He climbed out onto slate slick with morning damp and crouched among the gutter-spouts, heart going like a bird in a closed fist, and watched the soldiers load the temple's memory into a cart. His whole life fit in two sacks and a strongbox. Somewhere below, he knew, there was a ledger with his name in it — *Wren, ward of the temple, debt accrued for board and instruction, sum outstanding* — and he understood, in the way children understand things sideways and all at once, that debts could be collected from people as easily as from purses.

They hadn't come for him. Not yet. But ledgers grew, and he was a line in one.

When the column finally snaked out through the gate, down toward the river road, Wren was already three roofs away, moving the way he'd learned moving through the temple at night when sleep wouldn't come: fast, quiet, apologizing silently to every tile.

* * *

Finding them again was easier than losing them, because soldiers are loud and certain and leave a wake behind them like boats. He tracked the dust of the column along the river road all afternoon, hanging back far enough that the dust didn't settle on him, close enough that he could hear the creak of axles and the flat jokes men tell when they're bored and armed.

By dusk the column made camp in a fallow field, and Wren lay flat in a drainage ditch breathing mud-smell and watching the fires bloom one by one, and let himself feel, for the first time since the hatch, how completely stupid this was.

He was eleven years old. He had a stolen heel of bread, a knife better suited to sharpening pencils than anything else, and no plan beyond *follow Paloma*, who had been taken by these same soldiers two nights ago, who was probably already dead or worse, whose face he kept seeing the way you see a lamp after you blow it out — bright, floating, fading.

But here was the thing. Here was the thing he couldn't explain to anyone, least of all himself.

Two nights ago, when the stars went out — he'd been awake for it, he was always awake at night, the night was when the temple felt most honest — he had heard something. Not with his ears. With whatever part of a person hears a room go quiet, or hears a lie. A sound underneath the silence where the stars used to be. A singing. Vast and slow and patient, like something turning over in its sleep at the bottom of a very deep well, and it had frightened everyone else in the dormitory without knowing why, boys crying in the dark and saying it was just the wind.

It hadn't sounded like wind. It had sounded like a voice that had been singing so long it had forgotten there was ever anything else to do.

And it had sounded — this was the part he told no one, the part he turned over in the dark like a coin he was ashamed to own — almost *lonely*. Almost sad. Like the well itself wanted out.

Everyone said the wards falling meant danger. Everyone said the stars going out meant the end of things. But Wren had lain in his cot listening to the deep slow song and felt something he had never once felt inside the valley's walls, inside the temple, inside the ledger of his own small owed life.

He had felt like the song could hear *him*. And had noticed him kindly.

That was the wrong thing to feel. He knew it was the wrong thing to feel the way you know a stair is missing in the dark. He felt it anyway.

So: the ditch, the dark, the fires. He waited until the sentries' rounds found their rhythm — four minutes, then a gap, then four minutes — and he went over the camp's edge like smoke, low and quick, past the picket lines (a horse snorted; he froze; it lost interest), to the baggage carts at the rear, where the army kept everything it valued too little to guard and too heavy to carry.

The third cart from the end was stacked with archive crates, and wedged between two of them was a gap the size of a boy who had spent his whole life making himself small enough not to be noticed. He climbed in. He pulled a sacking over himself. It smelled of mildew and old vellum and, faintly, of ink — of the scriptorium, of home, of Paloma's sleeve — and he pressed his face into it and shook, quietly, the way you shake when you've been brave longer than your body agreed to.

Somewhere out in the dark, beneath the crackle of fires and the mutter of men, the deep song went on. *Sleep,* it seemed to say, or maybe he only wanted it to say that. *Nothing here can find you if you're already lost.*

Wren slept.

* * *

Three days in a cart teaches you things.

It teaches you that armies stop at dawn and march at midmorning and curse at rivers. It teaches you the exact taste of dry bread softened in rainwater caught off the cart's canvas. It teaches you that if you wedge yourself just so between the crate with the psalters and the crate with the provost's sealed correspondence, you can stretch one leg, and that after two days one stretched leg is worth more than gold.

It also teaches you that you will nearly die of boredom, and that boredom is dangerous, because boredom makes you careless.

On the fourth morning he made his mistake. The cart jolted over a rut, a crate shifted, and before he could stop it a psalter slid free and thumped against the tailboard — a small sound, a nothing sound, but the driver was a man named Hobb with ears like a bat and the temperament of a wet cat, and he reined in and climbed back over the freight to look.

Wren flattened into the gap. Hobb's shadow fell across the sacking. A hand closed on the sacking's edge.

And Wren — panicking, eleven, out of options — did the only thing left, which was the strange thing, the thing he'd sworn he'd never do on purpose.

He listened for the song. He reached for it the way you reach for a rope in dark water.

It came. It came *fast*, faster than it ever had in the dormitory, as though it had been waiting just under the surface for him to ask — a chord with no instruments in it, vast and slow, and this time it didn't feel distant at all. It felt like standing next to something enormous in a fog, knowing it could crush him without noticing, feeling it lean down instead and breathe.

Hobb's hand stopped. Through the weave of the sacking Wren saw the man sway slightly, frown, press the heel of his hand against his ear as though clearing water from it. He muttered something — *"damn ringing,"* maybe, or a word Wren didn't catch — and climbed back to the bench, and the cart rolled on.

Wren lay in the dark between the crates with his heart slamming and the song still humming through him like a struck bell that wouldn't quite stop, and he was terrified, properly terrified, for the first time since the hatch.

Not of the soldiers. Of himself. Of how easy that had been. Of how *good* it had felt, the way the warmth of a fire feels good to someone freezing, even if the fire is the reason the house is burning.

*Don't,* he told himself firmly, in Paloma's voice, because his own voice wasn't stern enough. *Don't use it. Don't like it.*

The song, somewhere beneath the road and the wheels and the world, sang on, unbothered, patient as water.

* * *

He got careless a second time on the sixth day, and this time it saved his life, or at least changed it.

They had passed out of country Wren knew even from maps — the river road climbing, the air thinning, pines replacing fields — and near dusk the column halted in a hollow where a shrine stood, some old roadside god with its face worn blank. Wren, stiff and starved and sick of his own smell, risked lifting the sacking's corner for air while the drivers argued about fodder.

Through the gap he saw her.

She was at the column's middle, walking between two soldiers, hands loose at her sides, chin up in that way she had — the way that made the provost call her *difficult* and made Wren want to be difficult too when he grew up. Thinner than she'd been. A bruise gone yellow-green along one jaw. But upright. Upright and squinting at the horizon like she was reading it and finding errors.

Paloma.

Something in Wren's chest cracked open so suddenly and so completely that he nearly cried out. He jammed both hands over his mouth. She was alive. She was *here*. Two nights ago he had lain in the dark telling himself stories about what they'd done to her, and here she was, alive, annoyed, magnificent, ten yards away and utterly unreachable, marching in the middle of forty armed men.

He watched her until the column moved and the trees took her.

Then he lay back between the crates and made a decision with the terrifying clarity of the very young: he was not going to stay in this cart following her at a distance like a dog after a carriage. He was going to get to her. Tonight, tomorrow, somehow. Because whatever the grown-ups thought any of this was — a debt collection, a military escort, a war — Wren knew something none of them knew. He knew what was underneath the silence where the stars used to be. He knew the song. And he had seen, in the soldiers' faces, in the way they flinched at nothing, in the way Hobb had swayed beside the cart — that it was getting louder.

Nobody else could hear it yet. He was almost sure nobody else could.

Somebody had to.

* * *

The chance came that same night, and it came ugly.

They camped in the hollow by the blank-faced shrine, and past midnight the camp woke to shouting — riders coming in from the south at a gallop, torches blooming, officers barking. Wren wormed up between the crates and watched through a knot-hole in the canvas as a mud-splattered courier was hauled before a captain, and though he couldn't hear the words he could read the shape of them in every body in the firelight: bad news, southern news, urgent news. Within minutes the camp was boiling — half the column saddling, torches streaming away southward into the dark like sparks off a struck flint, the rest forming up around the prisoners' wagon with drawn steel.

Including, he realized with a lurch, the two guards on the prisoners' wagon. Who were now six guards, and then four, and then — as another rider arrived screaming something about rebels and fire — two, and then one, and then the last one turned his back to argue with the quartermaster about lantern oil.

Wren went over the tailboard before his fear could vote.

He crossed the camp in the shadows of the carts, barefoot, having abandoned his ruined shoes two days ago, and he came around the flank of the prisoners' wagon expecting to have to whisper, to beg, to convince — and found Cassian instead.

The deserter was already awake. Of course he was. He sat with his bound wrists resting on his knees, watching the camp burn with activity, and when Wren's head rose over the wagon's side Cassian looked at him for a long, long moment with an expression Wren couldn't read at all.

"You," Cassian said finally, very quietly. "The temple boy."

"You remember me?"

"I remember everything about that night. It was instructive." His eyes flicked to the arguing quartermaster, back to Wren, doing arithmetic. "Let me guess. You followed an army across half the province, hid in a baggage cart, and survived on wit and stolen bread."

"And rainwater."

"Naturally." Something moved at the corner of his mouth that was not quite a smile. "You're either the bravest person I've met or the stupidest, and I genuinely cannot tell which."

"Paloma says I'm both."

"Paloma," Cassian repeated, and the not-smile faded into something more careful. "She asks after you. When she thinks no one's listening, she counts heads. You're one of the heads." He shifted, testing his bonds, wincing. "Listen to me, temple boy, because we have perhaps ninety seconds. There's a knife in my right boot. The knots are at my left wrist. If you cut me free, I can't take you with me — I'm worth more to these people loose than either of us is worth together, and I work alone."

"I'm not asking you to take me anywhere," Wren whispered, sawing at the knots with hands that would not stop shaking. "I'm asking you to tell me where they're taking her."

Cassian went still. In the distance, southward, something glowed faintly against the clouds that might have been dawn and was not dawn.

"The lowlands," he said slowly. "If the courier's news is what I think it is, they'll drive the prisoners hard for the lowland garrisons — they'll want every sword they can pull out of escort duty." His wrists came apart. He flexed his fingers, watching Wren with that unreadable look. "You understand what's in the lowlands, boy? What's burning there?"

"No."

"Neither do I. Not yet. But the smoke's been on the wind for two days, and the soldiers won't say the word, and soldiers always say the word eventually." He swung his legs over the wagon's side, paused, and — grudgingly, as if it cost him — added: "There's a supply cart at the rear, third from the end. Driver sleeps like the dead and drinks like the living shouldn't. If a person were foolish enough to be hiding in a baggage cart, that's the one I'd choose. And if that person were to stay hidden until the column reached the lowlands — until things got loud enough that no one counted anything — well." He dropped soundlessly to the ground. "Loud places lose things. Even people."

"That's not advice," Wren said. "That's barely a plan."

"It's the only kind I have." Cassian melted backward into the dark, then paused, one hand on the wheel of the wagon, and looked back up. For a moment his face was open in a way it hadn't been before — tired, and young under the stubble, and afraid. "Temple boy. The night the stars went out. Did you hear anything?"

Wren's throat closed. "Why?"

"Because I did," Cassian said. "And I've been a soldier nine years, and I have never once heard anything like it, and I need to know if I'm going mad."

Above them the sky hung starless and enormous, and beneath it, beneath the earth, beneath everything, the deep song turned over in its sleep — and Wren felt it notice them both, gently, the way a giant notices two ants sharing a crumb.

"No," Wren said. "You're not mad."

Cassian nodded once, as if that confirmed something worse than madness, and was gone.

Wren ran for the third cart from the end.

* * *

He was back in the sacking before the quartermaster finished his argument, curled between crates that smelled of home, shaking again, laughing a little too, because he had a destination now — the lowlands, where Paloma was going, where the smoke was coming from — and a plan, such as it was, and a secret shared with exactly one armed deserter.

The column broke camp before light and turned south, and the land began to change around it. The pines gave way to bare hills, the hills to hedged fields gone strangely untended, and the smell arrived before anything else did — a smell Wren had only ever known from candle-wicks and hearth-sweeps, but vast now, everywhere, soaked into the wind itself.

Smoke. Woodsmoke and worse under it, a charred-and-bitter note that made his stomach clench.

Near midday the road crested a long rise, and the soldiers ahead of him went quiet in a way that was louder than shouting, and Wren, pressing his eye to the knot-hole, saw the lowlands open below —

— and saw, scattered across the green country like coals kicked from a hearth, the fires. Dozens of them. Where villages had been.

The song beneath the world hummed on, low and satisfied, and for the first time it did not sound lonely to Wren at all.

It sounded hungry.
