## 9. The Lowlands Burn

Cassian smelled the smoke before he saw the fire.

That was the first lie his mind told him — that it was still burning, that somewhere ahead there were flames to fight and people to save. He knew better. He had burned enough villages himself to know the difference between a living fire and a dead one. A living fire had a voice: crackle and roar, the shriek of timber giving way, sometimes screaming if you were unlucky or slow. A dead fire said nothing at all. It just breathed its stink into the wind for days afterward, a long gray exhalation of everything that had been.

Three days out from the pass, the wind shifted in the night, and by morning the whole sky over the lowlands was the color of an old bruise.

"Is it a forest fire?" Wren asked from the back of the cart. He'd been riding with Sefa tucked under one arm like a stray cat he'd decided was his, both of them wrapped in the same threadbare blanket against the damp.

"No," Cassian said.

Paloma looked at him across the cart's rail. She didn't ask. She was learning, he thought, how much could be read off him if you watched long enough — the set of his shoulders, the way his hand drifted toward the knife at his belt without ever quite touching it. He wasn't sure he liked being legible. Legibility got men hanged.

"It's not a forest fire," she said quietly, answering her own question, and turned her face forward again.

They came down out of the foothills at midday, along a road that remembered their footsteps — he felt it now, that faint tug under the soles of his boots, the road's old magic leaning into each stride like a hand at the small of the back, hurrying them on. The crown had never managed to kill the lowland roads. They ran too deep, laid down before the warding, before the kingdom, maybe before the kings themselves. Cassian had marched on them as a soldier and hated every mile; they seemed to know what he was doing and disapprove.

Now they crested the last rise above the river valley, and Odile's country opened up below them, and Cassian stopped walking.

He had seen this valley green. He had seen it in high summer, when the reed beds along the water went silver and the ferry crossing glittered like spilled coin, when the villages strung along both banks — Millbrook, Harrow's End, Tarnlow, all the little names that never made it onto capital maps — smoked gently from a hundred hearths and smelled of bread and river mud. He had stood on this exact rise two years ago, in a column of forty soldiers with sealed orders, and looked down at all of it, and told himself what soldiers tell themselves: *orders are orders, someone else would do it, refusing only means they send someone worse.*

The column had come down that hill. And this is what it had left behind.

Not ruins. Ruins had a kind of dignity — walls standing, roofs fallen in, the honest architecture of decay. This was erasure. The villages had been burned so thoroughly that the ash lay over the ground like snowfall, ankle-deep in the hollows, drifting against the foundations of chimneys that stood alone in the gray like grave markers. Here and there a blackened beam jutted up at an angle, and crows sat along them in a row, patient as clerks. The river ran brown-gray with silt and something else. The reed beds were gone, cut or burned to the mudline. No birdsong except the crows. No dogs. No dogs anywhere, and that detail hit Cassian harder than the rest, because armies always killed the dogs first, and he knew why, and he wished he didn't.

"Saints," Paloma breathed beside him. She had gone the color of tallow. "There were four hundred people in Tarnlow. I copied the census rolls. There were *rolls*—"

"There aren't anymore," Cassian said. His voice came out flat, parade-ground flat, and he hated it. But the alternative was the other thing, the thing underneath, and he couldn't afford that yet. Not with Wren listening. Not with the child.

Sefa had climbed up to stand on the cart seat. She looked down at the valley with her head tilted, the way she did when she was listening to something no one else could hear, and began — very softly, barely voiced — to hum.

Cassian's whole body went cold. "Stop her."

"I don't think she means to," Paloma said. "Cassian. Look at her face."

The girl's eyes were wide and wet. Her humming had a shape to it, a descending line, three notes falling away like something dropping down a well. Grief, Cassian realized. The song in her was grieving. Whatever ancient melody lived in that child, whatever the crown wanted her for, right now it was doing what music does — mourning the dead.

Wren put his arms around her and she leaned into him and kept humming, and none of them told her to stop.

* * *

They found the first bodies at the edge of what had been Millbrook.

Not many. That was the horror of it — the purges hadn't left many bodies, because most of the people weren't here. They'd been taken. Cassian knew the arithmetic of it: a village of two hundred might yield thirty dead, those who fought or fled too slowly, and a hundred seventy marched east in chains toward the capital, toward labor camps and conscription and the crown's endless appetite. The dead were the lucky ones, and everyone in the lowlands knew it, which was why some of the older folk had stayed in their houses when the soldiers came. He had watched a man do it once, in a village whose name Cassian had spent two years carefully forgetting. The man had sat down in his chair, folded his hands, and looked at the door. Cassian had been ordered to drag him out. He had done it. The man had died on the road anyway, and Cassian had carried the memory of those folded hands through every day since, the way other men carried coins or knives.

These ones here — seven of them, laid where they'd fallen near the well — had fought. Farming tools mostly, a rusted sword or two. One woman with a billhook still gripped in both hands, frozen mid-swing by death and rigor and the ash that had settled over her like a second skin.

"We can't leave them like this," Paloma said.

"The ground's frozen under the ash," Odile would have said, if Odile had been there. Odile wasn't there yet. It was Wren who said, in a small voice, "Can we even dig?"

"We dig," Cassian said. "Get the shovels off the cart."

They dug for the rest of the day. The ash got into everything — hair, teeth, the wet corners of your eyes until you wept gray. Under it the earth was half-frozen, and every stroke of the shovel rang like a bell. Paloma worked beside him without speaking, her scribe's hands blistering around the haft, and he noticed she never once asked him how he knew the proper depth for a grave, or why he laid the bodies face-up with their hands crossed, or why he murmured the old soldier's words over each one — *the watch is ended, go down and sleep* — in a cadence that predated the kingdom itself. She just watched, and learned, and dug.

Wren and Sefa gathered stones. By late afternoon there was a cairn rising at the edge of the dead village, rough and lopsided, and Sefa sang over it — really sang, for the first time since they'd found her, a low wordless line that made the hairs on Cassian's arms stand up. It sounded the way cold water sounds. It sounded like forgiveness nobody had earned.

When the last stone was placed, Paloma sat back on her heels and looked at the cairn and then at Cassian, and her eyes were red-rimmed and furious.

"You were there," she said. Not a question. "When this happened. Or somewhere like it."

"Yes."

"And you did—" She stopped. He watched her decide, visibly, whether to finish the sentence. She finished it. "You did what soldiers do."

"Yes."

She nodded slowly, once, and picked up her shovel, and went to start the next grave. Somehow that was worse than shouting. Shouting he could have answered.

* * *

They found Odile at the crossing.

Cassian heard her before he saw her — the knock of wood on wood, rhythmic, unhurried, coming from the riverbank where the ferry landing had been. The ferry itself was gone, burned at its moorings; only the pilings remained, charred stubs like pulled teeth. But moored to one of them, tied off with a knot Cassian recognized as lowland river-craft, was a flat-bottomed skow, and standing in it with a mallet in one hand, driving out a cracked plank, was a woman built like the keel of a ship.

She was perhaps fifty, thick-armed and gray-streaked, dressed in oiled leather gone stiff with age and river work. A patch of black cloth covered her left eye. The right one found them the moment they came within fifty paces — found Cassian first, and stayed on him, and narrowed.

"Well," she said. Her voice was gravel rolled in honey. "A deserter walks into my valley with a temple scribe, two children, and a cart full of stolen papers. If I'd prayed for visitors, I'd have prayed for something less interesting."

"You know what I am," Cassian said.

"One eye, love, but it's a good one. You've got a soldier's walk and no soldier's kit, and you flinched when you looked at the cairn up the road." She set down the mallet. "I watched that column march in. Two years past. I watched it march out. So either you were in it, or you've got the same bad conscience as the men who were, and either way I want to know which."

"Cassian," Paloma started, but he raised a hand.

"He's telling the truth," he said. "I was in the column. Third rank, left file. I held my orders and I did my work and I have been running from it since the day I walked off the wall at Greyford with my oath-ring in my pocket." He took the ring out as he said it — he still carried it, gods help him, he'd never been able to throw it in a river — and held it up between two fingers. Then he tossed it to her.

She caught it without looking. Turned it over. Read the garrison mark stamped inside the band.

Then she threw it back, hard, so it stung his palm. "Keep it," she said. "You'll want it later, when you figure out what to do with a thing like that. Throwing it away's too easy. Believe me. I've tried throwing things away." Her eye moved to the children, to Sefa, and something in her face closed like a door. "And what's that? What are you carrying that child for? You know what the crown pays for god-touched stock?"

"She's not stock," Wren said, stepping in front of Sefa with his chin up and his knees visibly shaking. "She's our friend."

Odile looked at the boy for a long moment. Something flickered behind the eye-patch, some muscle-memory of feeling. "Friends," she repeated, tasting the word. "In this ash. Saints preserve the young. They make friends in a graveyard."

"It's not a graveyard," Wren said. "It's — we buried them properly. We gave them stones and a song."

For the first time, the ferrywoman's gaze wavered. She looked away, out at the brown river, and when she spoke again her voice had lost its edge and gained something heavier. "Whose song?"

"Sefa's," Paloma said carefully. "It was — I've never heard anything like it."

"No," Odile said. "You wouldn't have." She was quiet a moment, mallet hanging loose in her grip. Then: "How many graves did you dig today?"

"Seven," Cassian said. "At Millbrook. We saw others on the road. Harrow's End looked—"

"Harrow's End is gone. Tarnlow is gone. The ferry families are gone, or taken, or scattered into the hills eating bark." She said it evenly, the way you recite a ledger, and Cassian thought: *she has said this before, to herself, every morning, to keep it true.* "I've spent two years pulling what I could out of the river. Bodies that floated downstream. Some I buried. Some—" Her jaw worked. "Some the river kept."

"Why help us?" Paloma asked. "Why even talk to us?"

Odile laughed, short and bitter, with no humor anywhere in it. "Help you? Girl, I haven't decided to help you. I've decided not to shoot you, which is different." She jerked her chin at the skow. "But I'll tell you what I want, since you're standing there looking useful. There are eleven of mine under the willow bank upstream, dead these eight months, wrapped in sailcloth because the ground was too hard and I was too alone to dig proper graves. Eleven. I'm one woman with one eye and a bad shoulder." Her voice didn't shake. It got quieter instead, which was worse. "You dig them up, you dig them decent graves, you give them what that child gave the ones at Millbrook — then tomorrow I'll pole you downriver past the checkpoints, through the marsh cuts, all the way to the lowland roads the king's maps forgot exist. I know every channel and every snare-line between here and the rebel holds. Nobody crosses this water without me. Nobody crosses it *with* me unless they've earned the ride."

"That's fair," Cassian said.

"It's not fair," Odile snapped. "Fair would be my valley unburned and my brother alive. This is just a price. Everything's a price. You lot ought to know that better than anyone — you're traveling with a debt-ledger, aren't you?" Her eye flicked to Paloma, who stiffened. "Temple ink smells different from crown ink, but it binds the same."

Paloma's chin came up. "My debts are mine to pay."

"Mm." Odile looked at her a moment longer, and something passed over her face — recognition, almost, of a kindred stubbornness — before she turned away. "Dawn tomorrow. Bring your shovels. And bring that child's song. My people liked singing. It's the least of what they're owed."

* * *

They camped that night in the shell of a riverside boathouse, half-burned, half-standing, its roof open to a sky that still refused to show stars. Cassian took first watch out of habit, sitting in the doorway with his back to the wall and the river muttering beyond.

He couldn't stop seeing the column. Forty men coming down that hill in good order, banners snapping, drums keeping step — and below them, all that green life, unaware. He had known what the orders meant. Sealed orders were never good news, and the purge directives had a particular seal, and every man in the ranks had understood and marched anyway, because that was the machine, that was how it worked, ten thousand small cowardices welded into one large atrocity. He had told himself he'd only done what any of them would do. Standing in the boathouse doorway, listening to Sefa breathe in her sleep — a faint, unconscious thread of melody woven through it, grief still working itself out of her — he finally admitted what he'd known since Greyford: *any of them* was not an excuse. It was an indictment. Every man in that column had been the any-of-them.

Footsteps. Paloma settled herself against the opposite doorpost, a blanket around her shoulders, ash still ghosted in her hairline.

"You should sleep," he said.

"So should you, eventually." She was quiet a while. The river filled it. Then: "The graves today. You've done that before. Many times."

"Yes."

"For people you—"

"Some of them, yes." He kept his eyes on the water. "Most of them, no. Most of them were strangers. That's not better. I used to think it was."

Paloma turned the thought over; he could see her doing it, the scribe's habit, weighing words like coins. "Odile hates you," she said at last. "Or she hates what you were. But she's going to guide us anyway."

"Grief makes strange bargains."

"No." Paloma shook her head slowly. "Grief makes *honest* ones. She's not pretending to forgive you. She's just decided the dead need burying more than her anger needs feeding." A pause. "I think that might be the bravest thing I've seen since the stars went out."

Cassian didn't answer. He wasn't sure he could. Across the dark boathouse, Wren stirred in his sleep and muttered something, and Sefa's breathing carried its thin ghost of song, and the river went on carrying whatever it carried — silt, ash, the unburied past — down toward a sea none of them had ever seen.

Tomorrow they would dig eleven graves. Tomorrow Odile would pole them into the drowned heart of the lowlands, past checkpoints and snare-lines, toward the rebels and whatever waited there. And somewhere behind them, Cassian knew, the crown's hounds were already moving — because a stolen fragment of the binding rite does not stay stolen quietly, and a god-touched child does not stay hidden in a land where every ear is an informer.

He checked the edge of his knife, though it needed nothing, and settled in to keep the watch.

The river, patient as a creditor, kept flowing.
