## 10. What the Wards Were For

The fragment was smaller than a psalter and twice as dangerous.

Paloma had told herself that all morning, turning the stolen pages by candlelight in the back of Odile's ferry-house, where the river slapped against the pilings like a patient hand. The paper was older than any she had ever touched — older than the temple, older than the valley's founding hymn — and it smelled of tallow smoke and something beneath that, something mineral and cold, like the air in a cellar where nothing has lived for a very long time.

She had copied texts for eleven years. She knew paper the way farmers knew weather. This was not made from flax or rag; the fibers caught the light with a faint silvered shimmer, as though someone had beaten starlight into the pulp before the stars went out.

*Careful,* she told herself. *It's only paper.*

But nothing about this was only anything.

Across the room, Odile mended nets with the economy of a woman who had buried her hands in work to keep them from shaking. Cassian sat sharpening a knife he did not need to sharpen, the whetstone whispering in slow circles. Wren slept curled against Sefa on the far bench, both of them breathing in tandem the way children do when they have decided, without discussion, to trust each other. The girl's humming had stopped an hour ago. Paloma had noticed its absence the way you notice silence after a bell.

She bent again over the fragment.

It was written in two hands. The first was formal, liturgical, the careful capitals of a trained scribe — a hand like her own, she realized with a small cold shock, like the hand she used for ledger entries, each letter built to survive scrutiny. The second hand was hurried, slanting, pressed so hard into the page that the stylus had torn through in places, as though the writer were running out of time or courage or both.

The first hand wrote the rite. The second wrote the margins.

*We were not protecting them,* the margin said, in ink gone brown as old blood. *Gods forgive us, we were never protecting them.*

Paloma read it four times. Each time, the words arranged themselves differently in her chest.

"Odile," she said quietly. "When you ferried people out of the lowlands — before the purges — what did they say the wards were?"

The ferrywoman didn't look up. "Safe. That's all anyone says. The valley is safe, the valley is blessed, the Quiet Hours keep us." She tied off a knot with her teeth. "Why?"

"No reason."

"You're a terrible liar, scribe. Your face does a thing." She gestured vaguely at Paloma's whole head. "Like a door trying to decide whether to open."

Paloma looked back down at the page.

* * *

By midday she had transcribed everything legible, and by then her hands had begun to tremble in a way she recognized from long nights over the debt ledgers — not fear exactly, but the specific dread of arithmetic that will not come out right no matter how many times you run it.

The rite itself she could follow. It was a binding — that much matched the stories, the chapel walls, the fragments Cassian had stolen at such cost. A great entity, nameless in this text but unmistakable in its description: *the singer beneath the world, the hunger that harmonizes, the voice that wears.* The founders of the valley had bound it, yes. That was the story every child learned. The wards were the binding's outer edge, the shield, the mercy.

But the first scribe's margins told another story entirely.

*The binding requires a cage. A cage requires walls. Walls require watchers. We could not watch forever — we were mortal, and the thing beneath is patient beyond the patience of stones. So we made the watchers hereditary. We seeded a valley with our own children and taught them to be small. To be quiet. To count their hours and tally their debts and never, ever look up.*

*The wards do not keep magic out. There is nothing outside to keep out. The wards keep IT in — and they keep the watchers blind, because a jailer who knows he is a jailer will one day ask why, and a jailer who asks why will one day open the door.*

Paloma set down her pen.

Her first thought, absurdly, was of the provost's ledger. Of the columns she had copied ten thousand times: debts and interest, debts and interest, the tidy arithmetic of lives measured in obligations. She had thought it cruelty, or greed, or simply the way temples were. But now she saw it differently — saw the whole architecture of her life laid out like a diagram. The indentures. The Quiet Hours, where no one raised their voice. The hymns sung in a narrow register, never above a certain note, never below. The curfew bells. The way no one in the valley ever spoke of the sky except at harvest, and then only briefly, apologetically, as though the stars were guests who might overhear themselves being discussed.

*Taught them to be small.*

Her whole life had been a cell, and she had called it safety because no one had ever shown her the bars.

"Cassian," she said. Her voice came out strange, flattened. He looked up from his knife. "In the capital. When you deserted. What did the officers say about the valley?"

He considered her for a moment — he always considered her, she'd noticed, the way a man considers a bridge before crossing. "That it was a relic. A superstition the crown tolerated because the taxes came regular." He paused. "And that the old regiments wouldn't march there. Not through the pass. They'd go around, weeks out of the way, rather than sleep under those wards." His eyes narrowed. "Soldiers' stories. The valley hums. The valley watches. Why?"

"I need you to tell me if I'm wrong," she said. "Read this. Tell me I'm reading it wrong."

He crossed the room and took the fragment from her with unexpected gentleness, the way you handle something wounded. Odile abandoned her nets and read over his shoulder, her one eye moving fast. For a long time neither of them spoke. Outside, the river kept up its patient applause.

"The second hand," Paloma said, because the silence was unbearable. "The marginal notes. I think — I think it's the same scribe who wrote the fair copy. Same letterforms underneath the haste. She wrote the beautiful version for the record, and then she told the truth in the margins, where no one would look. Where only another scribe would think to look."

"Why would no one look?" Wren asked.

Paloma started. She hadn't heard him wake. He sat up now, hair wild, blinking at the candle, and beside him Sefa watched Paloma with those unsettling still eyes — the eyes of a child who hears things other people only imagine.

"Because the truth was hidden inside the work," Paloma said. "Underneath the official text. That's how it's always done. The provost keeps two ledgers — the one for the crown, and the real one. Every institution does." She laughed, and it came out cracked. "I've spent my life copying the top layer and calling it knowledge."

"And the bottom layer?" Odile asked.

Paloma pulled the fragment back toward herself and read aloud, because some things could not be paraphrased without softening them, and she refused to soften them, not even for her own sake:

"*To my successors, whoever you are, whoever you have been made to be: the valley is not a sanctuary. It is a lock, and you are the tumblers. Your hymns are the mechanism turning. Your debts are the grease. Your smallness is the design. We bound the singer beneath the world, and we could not bear the cost ourselves, so we made our children bear it, and their children, and we called it blessing so they would not call it burden. If you are reading this, the wards have failed or soon will, and I am sorry — I am sorry — we should have told you. We should have let you choose.*"

The last line was nearly illegible, gouged deep:

"*Choose. Whatever else you do — choose.*"

Nobody moved. The candle flame stood perfectly still, as though listening.

"That's why the soldiers won't camp there," Cassian said finally, very quietly. "They can feel it. All those generations, singing the lock shut without knowing—"

"Don't." Paloma's voice came out sharper than she intended. She pressed her palm flat against the table until the wood bit back. "Don't say it like that. Like we're — like I'm—"

"Like what?" Odile asked, relentless as the river.

"Complicit." The word tore free before Paloma could stop it. "Eleven years I copied those ledgers. Eleven years I sang the evening hymn in exactly the register I was taught, and I thought I was praying. I thought the walls around me were love. And the whole time I was—" Her breath hitched. "My family. My mother tallied debts. Her mother before her. The Thackerays have been scribes since the valley was founded — I thought it was tradition. Vocation. It wasn't vocation. It was *assignment*. We weren't serving the temple. We were serving the lock."

The room held still around her.

Wren slid off the bench and came to stand beside her chair. He didn't touch her — he was learning, slowly, which adults wanted touching and which ones needed distance — but he stood close enough that she could feel the warmth of him, small and alive and stubborn.

"My gran said the valley was the safest place in the world," he offered.

"It is," Paloma said bitterly. "That's the point. That's what it was *built* to be. Safe like a strongbox is safe. Safe like a grave."

"But it was also home," Wren said, with the terrible simplicity of children. "Both things can be true. Gran said that too. About hard stuff."

Paloma looked at him. Then at Sefa, who had risen silently and come to stand at Wren's shoulder, watching Paloma with an expression far too old for her face. The girl tilted her head, listening to something, and then said, in her odd, unchildlike cadence:

"It isn't angry that you didn't know."

"What isn't?" Odile demanded.

Sefa frowned, searching for words the way other children searched for lost buttons. "The song under the valley. The one the wards were holding. It's been listening to your hymns for — for always. And it doesn't sound angry. It sounds—" She pressed a hand to her own sternum. "Lonely. Like someone locked in a house where everyone talks about them but never to them."

Paloma felt the floor tilt beneath her certainties.

All her life she had feared what lay beyond the wards — the wild magic, the lawless dark, the things the temple warned would unmake a person. And now she learned there had never been anything to fear outside, because the danger had been underneath her feet the entire time, singing lullabies through the floorboards while generations of her ancestors unknowingly harmonized with its prison. And worse — worse than the betrayal, worse than the lie — was the thing Sefa had just named. Because Paloma knew loneliness. Loneliness was the ledger with her own name in it. Loneliness was copying other people's words for eleven years because no one wanted hers.

She had more in common with the caged thing than she had ever had with the valley that raised her.

The realization went through her like cold water through cloth.

"I need air," she said, and her chair scraped back too loud, and she was out the door and down to the water before anyone could follow.

* * *

The river at dusk was the color of hammered pewter. Paloma walked the bank until the ferry-house lights shrank behind her, until she found the flat stone where Odile moored the boat, and there she sat, knees drawn up, staring across the water at the place where the horizon should have been.

No stars. Still no stars. The sky above the lowlands was a smooth dark ceiling, seamless as the inside of a bell. She had grown up under that same ceiling and called it sky.

*We should have let you choose.*

She turned her marked palm over in the light of the rising moon — the moon still hung up there, indifferent, the one light the wards had never covered — and thought about her ancestors. Generations of Thackerays, bending over their desks in the Temple of Quiet Hours, believing themselves humble servants of a merciful god whose chief commandment was *be small*. Her mother, who died owing the temple four hundred marks and a year of service. Her grandmother, whose hymnal still sat on Paloma's shelf with the margins worn soft by thumbs — worn soft, Paloma understood now, by generations of hands finding comfort in the exact same verses, the exact same narrow band of sound.

Had any of them known? Had any of them, late at night, felt the wrongness and pushed it down? Had her mother ever paused over a ledger entry and wondered why the interest never ended, why the debts compounded generation after generation, why no family in the valley ever truly paid its way free?

*Your debts are the grease.*

A debt that can never be paid is not a debt. It's a chain with paperwork.

Paloma hugged her knees tighter. Somewhere downstream a night bird called, three notes, descending, and she flinched at the melody before she could stop herself — then made herself listen to it, all the way through, as though hearing it for the first time. Three notes. Descending. The same phrase, she realized, as the third verse of the evening hymn. The same phrase the birds had always sung in the valley, the same phrase woven into harvest songs and cradle songs and the little tune children chanted while skipping rope.

Everything sang the lock. The birds. The bells. The babies.

And her.

Footsteps on the bank behind her — uneven, deliberate. Odile lowered herself onto the flat stone with a grunt, uninvited, unhurried, and sat looking out at the water the way she did everything: as though the world owed her an apology and she intended to wait for it.

"Wren's worried about you," Odile said.

"He shouldn't be."

"He is anyway. That's what he's for." She was quiet a moment. "Cassian's pacing. Says we need to move before the hounds pick up our trail. I told him the scribe needs a minute." Her eye slid sideways. "Do you need a minute, or do you need the truth?"

"Aren't they the same thing, coming from you?"

Something that was almost a smile touched the ferrywoman's mouth. Then it faded. "My brother used to sing," she said abruptly, to the river rather than to Paloma. "Before. He had a voice like — like warm bread. Everyone said so." Her jaw worked. "You're sitting there thinking you were a tool your whole life and didn't know it. I knew mine. I knew exactly what I was doing, and I did it anyway, and I've had thirty years to get comfortable with it." She turned and fixed Paloma with the full weight of that single grey eye. "So hear me, scribe: the not-knowing isn't the sin. The sin is what you do in the minute after you find out. There's always a minute. You're in it now."

"And what did you do in yours?"

Odile looked back at the water. "I'll tell you someday. Not today." She rose, joints cracking like kindling, and paused. "But I'll say this much. Whoever wrote that fragment — the one who told the truth in the margins — she didn't fix anything. Knowing the truth and writing it down where only scribes would find it — that's not choosing. That's hiding the key under the mat and hoping someone braver comes along." She shrugged. "Maybe you're the someone braver. Maybe you're just the next one hiding keys. River doesn't care either way. River just carries what falls in it."

She walked back up the bank, uneven and unhurried, leaving Paloma alone with the pewter water and the starless ceiling and the fragment's ghost pressing against her ribs.

*Choose.*

Paloma stayed until the moon was high. She thought about the valley — really thought about it, for the first time, from the outside. The terraced fields going gold in autumn. The bell that rang the Quiet Hours. The smell of bread from the temple kitchens, the scratch of good pens, Wren's laugh echoing in the scriptorium before the provost's frown silenced it. She loved it. That was the unbearable part. You could love a cage. The bird always loved the cage; that was how cages worked, that was their genius, they made the bars feel like ribs.

And somewhere beneath all of it — beneath the barley and the bells and the graves of everyone she had ever loved — something sang. Something that had listened to her ancestors' voices for five hundred years the way a prisoner listens to footsteps overhead. Something lonely. Something that had, if Sefa was right, never once been angry at them for building the walls.

Only at the ones who knew, and chose the mat over the door.

Paloma unrolled the strip of spare paper she carried and, by moonlight, began to write. Not a copy. Not a transcription. Her own hand, her own words, the letters slanting and pressed hard the way the margin-scribe's had been, as though urgency could be transmitted through ink:

*I know now. Whatever comes of this — whatever I choose — let it be recorded that I know, and that I am not hiding the key. I am carrying it. Let anyone who finds this understand: the valley was a lock, and we were its tumblers, and the first act of freedom is to stop turning.*

She signed it. Folded it. Tucked it against her heart, inside her coat, where the ledger of her old life used to sit.

Then she stood, brushed the stone dust from her skirt, and walked back toward the lights of the ferry-house — toward the deserter and the ferrywoman and the two children sleeping in the warmth of a family none of them had planned.

She was halfway there when she heard it.

Faint, at first, threading through the river-sound — a hum, high and clear and impossible, coming not from the ferry-house but from the dark water itself, from downstream, from everywhere. Sefa's melody. But Sefa was asleep inside, Paloma had left her sleeping; she was certain of it, certain as she had once been certain of the wards.

The hum swelled, took shape, became words in no language she knew and somehow, horribly, in all of them. And beneath it, fainter, from the direction of the valley — from the pass, from home, from the lock she had carried inside her all her life — came a second voice answering. Deep. Patient. Rising.

The song under the world had noticed her noticing.

And it was singing her name.
