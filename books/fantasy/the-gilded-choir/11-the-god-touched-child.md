## 11. The God-Touched Child

Wren had learned three things about the road since the temple: that dust got into everything, that Cassian slept with one eye open and both hands near his knife, and that Odile never once said a kind word without making it sound like an insult.

He was learning a fourth thing now, crouched in the bracken above the waystation while the others argued in whispers behind him: that the world outside the valley was full of chains.

"Look at it," Odile breathed. Her single eye glittered like wet slate. "Crown post. Supply relay for the patrols burning the lowlands. Four soldiers, maybe five, and a fat quartermaster who's never held anything sharper than a ledger pen."

"And we need what's in it," Cassian said. "Grain. Boots. If they've got a map case, better still." He lay flat on his stomach in the dead ferns, his chin propped on his fists, studying the station below the way a heron studies water. "We hit it at dusk shift-change. In and out before the bell."

Wren barely heard them. He was looking at the small stone outbuilding at the station's edge — not a granary, not a stable, though it had been built to look like one. He could tell because of the window. Granaries didn't need windows barred from the *outside*.

"There's someone in there," he said.

The whispering stopped.

"You don't know that," Odile said.

"I know it." Wren did not know how he knew it, which was the trouble with most of the things he knew these days. Since the stars went out, since the temple, since the night the soldiers came and Master Ellery had shoved him through the coal chute with a stolen map pressed against his chest — *run, boy, run until the song stops* — Wren had been hearing things other people didn't. Not words. More like weather. The world hummed, and some parts of it hummed wrong.

That little building hummed like a struck bell that wouldn't stop ringing.

"There's someone in there," he said again, "and they're singing."

* * *

Nobody sang anymore. That was another thing Wren had noticed. On the road, in the burned villages they'd skirted wide, in Odile's ferry-house with its smell of tar and old grief — nobody sang. Paloma said people were frightened. Odile said people were stupid. But Wren thought it was something else. He thought the silence was a wound, and everyone was being very careful not to touch it.

So when he heard singing — thin, cracked, coming through the barred window in a voice too small to belong to anyone older than him — something in his chest turned over like a boat in rough water.

"We're here for supplies," Odile hissed as he crept toward the outbuilding. "Not orphans."

"It might be a trap," Cassian added, which was his way of agreeing with Odile while pretending he hadn't.

But Wren was already at the wall, fingers hooked through the bars, pressing his face to the gap. Inside: straw, a bucket, a slop dish, and a girl.

She was sitting cross-legged in the corner with her wrists chained to an iron ring set into the floor, and she was humming. It wasn't any tune Wren knew. It went up where tunes should go down, doubled back on itself, folded through intervals that made his teeth ache pleasantly, like biting foil. And underneath the humming — this was the part that made the hair rise on his arms — underneath it, the whole world seemed to lean in and listen.

She stopped. She looked straight at the window. Straight at him.

Her eyes were the color of river ice, pale gray with a seam of darker blue running through each iris like a vein of ore. She couldn't have been more than ten. There was straw in her cropped hair and a bruise going yellow along her jaw, and when she saw Wren's face at the window she did not cry out or scramble back. She tilted her head, exactly the way a bird does.

"You hear it too," she said.

* * *

They took the waystation in eleven minutes. Wren counted, because counting kept his hands from shaking.

It should have been simple. It was not simple, because Odile fought like a woman settling old accounts, and because one of the soldiers — young, freckled, hardly older than Cassian — threw down his sword halfway through and started crying, and because the quartermaster, cornered behind his own counter, screamed *it's not human, it's not human, take it, it's yours, crown's orders, take the thing away* — and then Cassian hit him with the flat of a blade and the man went quiet, and the whole yard went quiet, except for the humming.

Except for the humming, which had not stopped once. It drifted out of the barred building steady as water from a spring, and Wren realized, standing in the middle of the trampled yard with his heart slamming, that the two soldiers still conscious had gone pale and still, and that even Odile's knife hand had slowed, and that the sound was doing something to all of them — smoothing them, the way a thumb smooths a wrinkle from cloth.

"Stop that," Odile snarled at the building, though of course the building couldn't hear her.

"She can't help it," Wren said.

And then he was moving, because Paloma had already pried the bar off the door with a crowbar from the tack room, and the door was open, and the girl was looking up at all of them with those ore-veined eyes, and Wren found he could not bear the distance between the doorway and the straw.

He crossed it in four steps and knelt down in front of her.

Up close she smelled of dust and iron and, strangely, of juniper — the sharp clean smell of the high fells around the valley, though they were a hundred miles from any fells. Her wrists were raw inside the shackles. One of the links had worn nearly through; she'd been working at it, patiently, for who knew how long.

"Hello," Wren said. His voice came out smaller than he wanted. "I'm Wren."

The girl considered him. Then she reached out — as far as the chain allowed — and touched his collarbone, right over his heartbeat, with two grimy fingers.

"You're loud," she said. "Inside. You're very loud inside."

"Oh," said Wren, who did not know what that meant but suspected it was true. "Sorry."

"Don't be sorry." A ghost of a smile, quick and crooked. "It's nice. Everyone else is so quiet lately. You're like a kettle."

Behind him, Paloma made a sound that was half laugh, half sob. Wren looked back and saw her standing in the doorway with the crowbar still in her hands, taking in the shackles, the straw, the slop dish, the barred window, and her face was doing something complicated — grief and fury braided together, the look of a scribe who has just read a ledger entry she cannot un-read.

"What's your name?" Paloma asked gently.

"Sefa." The girl said it the way you'd set down something heavy you'd been carrying a long way. Then her eyes flicked past them all, to the yard, to the sky, and the smile vanished. "You shouldn't have come. They'll come back for me. They always come back."

"Who will?" Cassian asked, from the door, knife still bare.

"The gray men." Sefa's fingers curled into the straw. "The ones who smell like cold. They put me in a wagon with no windows and drove me for nine days, and every night the man in the fine coat came and listened to me sing, and wrote things down, and smiled like a door opening onto nothing." She looked up at Paloma. "He said I was a tuning fork. He said the king needed tuning."

The humming had stopped while she spoke, and Wren felt its absence like a tooth pulled — the whole world slightly off-balance without it. He understood suddenly, completely, why the quartermaster had screamed. Whatever lived under that melody, whatever it leaned toward when she sang — it was vast, and old, and it was *listening*, and it was listening through her the way you'd listen through a keyhole.

And she was ten years old, and she was chained to a floor because of it.

"Well," said Odile, from the doorway, in a voice like a slammed shutter. "Now we know why the crown wants her. Now we know what she is. So we leave her."

* * *

The argument happened in the tack room, which meant Wren heard every word, because he refused to leave the outbuilding and therefore everyone had to come to him, which suited him fine.

"She's bait," Odile said. She stood with her arms crossed, planted in front of the door like a fencepost with opinions. "You heard her. Gray men. Fine coats. They hunt her, they track her, and the moment we take her onto the road, we're carrying a lit lantern through a dark country. Every hunter in three provinces will see us coming."

"She's a child," Paloma said.

"She's a *tuning fork*. Her own words. You want to carry the thing the hollow king needs most, wrapped in a blanket, sleeping next to us? You want to explain to the rebels in the lowlands why the hounds found their camp?"

"Yes," Paloma said, and her voice didn't rise at all, which somehow made it worse. "Yes, Odile. That's exactly what I want to do. Because I spent six years copying ledgers in a temple that taught me the first rule of arithmetic: that a debt is a debt no matter whose name is on it. That girl is chained to a floor because of what she *is*. Not what she's done. What she is. And if we walk away—"

"If we walk away, we live."

"—then we're just the crown with dirtier hands."

Silence. Cassian sat on a grain sack, turning his knife over and over, saying nothing, which Wren had learned was how Cassian thought. Through the wall, faintly, the humming had started again — Sefa singing to herself in the empty outbuilding, folding the air into shapes that hurt nicely.

Odile's eye went to the wall. Something moved behind it — not softness, Wren thought. Recognition. The look of a woman hearing a sound she'd spent years teaching herself not to flinch from.

"I buried forty-one people in the lowlands," Odile said quietly. "I dug every grave myself. I know what following the wrong song costs." She jabbed a finger at Wren. "And you. Boy. You've known her half a day. You don't know what she is. None of us do."

"I know she shared her bread with me," Wren said. "While she was still chained up. She had half a heel left and she broke it in half and gave me the bigger piece, and she didn't even think about it first." His voice cracked and he hated it and kept going anyway. "I know she's been in that room long enough to wear a shackle link halfway through with a rock, and she wasn't crying when we found her, she was *singing*, because singing is the only thing they couldn't take off her. And I know—" his throat closed, opened, "—I know what it's like when everybody looks at you and sees a problem instead of a person. Everybody in that temple looked at me like I was a debt walking around in shoes. Somebody has to be the one who doesn't."

The tack room was very quiet. Somewhere outside, a crow complained about the corpses.

Cassian flipped the knife, caught it, sheathed it. "For what it's worth," he said, "the girl picked the lock on her own wrist cuff while we were arguing. She's been free for ten minutes and hasn't run." He raised his voice slightly. "Have you?"

"No," Sefa called back, mild as milk. "I'm eating."

Another silence. Then Odile laughed — one short, harsh bark, aimed mostly at herself — and dragged her hand down her face.

"Fine," she said. "*Fine.* But hear me, all of you. If the gray men come — and they will come — and it's her life or ours, I won't hesitate. I've hesitated before. Once. Never again." She pointed at Wren last, hardest. "And you, kettle-boy. She's your charge. You feed her, you watch her, you keep her singing *quiet*. If she brings the king's hounds down on us, that weight sits on your shoulders, not mine. Understood?"

"Understood," Wren said, and meant it, and would carry it, and did not care even a little, because through the wall the humming swelled — Sefa singing again, freely now, unchained, and the tune unfolded like something blooming in fast motion, and Wren pressed his palm flat against the warm stones of the wall and felt the whole world lean in and listen, and thought: *let it listen. Let it hear that somebody's still singing.*

* * *

They left before dawn, the four of them and the girl, on a cart full of stolen grain with a stolen tarp over it and a stolen horse named Turnip, because Wren named her and nobody stopped him in time.

Sefa rode in the cart bed between the sacks, wrapped in Odile's spare cloak, and for the first hour she said nothing at all, just watched the sky go from ash to rose with an expression Wren couldn't read — hunger, maybe, or homesickness for a home she'd never had.

Then, as the light strengthened and the road bent north toward the lowlands, she scooted over next to him, tucked her shoulder against his arm, and began, very softly, to hum.

The ache-sweet melody. The one that made the world lean in.

But this time Wren noticed something he hadn't at the waystation, something he'd been too scared and too busy to notice: she wasn't singing it *exactly*. She'd changed it. Bent the third phrase upward, slipped a rest where the fold should be, so that the vast listening thing underneath heard the melody the way you'd hear your own name shouted across a crowded market — recognizable, but not quite answering to it. Mocked by it, almost.

She was singing the entity's song *wrong on purpose*. Singing it into a joke. Singing it into a lullaby.

Sefa caught him staring and grinned, quick and crooked, straw-dust still in her hair.

"He hates that bit," she whispered.

Wren laughed — really laughed, the first time since the stars went out — and the sound startled a lark up out of the hedgerow, and ahead of them Odile muttered something about children and fools and turned her one eye back to the road, and behind them, far to the south, past the hills and the pass and the gilded rot of the capital, something ancient shifted in its sleep and began, patiently, to listen for a small wrong note in the world.

They didn't see the riders on the ridge until noon.

Three of them. Gray cloaks. Sitting motionless against the brightening sky, not following, not leaving. Watching.

"They've found us already," Paloma breathed.

"No," Sefa said softly, and her hand found Wren's and gripped it, and her eyes had gone the color of deep winter. "Those aren't hunters. Hunters ride *after* you." She swallowed. "Those are riding *ahead*. They're not chasing us to the lowlands."

"They're telling someone we're coming."
