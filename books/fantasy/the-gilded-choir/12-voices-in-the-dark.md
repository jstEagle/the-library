## 12. Voices in the Dark

The girl sang in her sleep.

Paloma had noticed it first on the third night after the waystation, when they'd made camp in a hollow of blackthorn and Sefa had curled up against Wren's side like a cat claiming a warm hearthstone. The humming had started low, almost beneath hearing — a thread of melody that wound through the crackle of their small fire and out into the dark. Paloma had sat up sharply, hand going to the mark on her palm, certain the entity had found them.

But it was only Sefa. Sleeping soundly, eyes fluttering behind their lids, lips parted around a tune that had no words and needed none.

It should have been terrifying. Paloma knew what that melody was; she carried its echo burned into her skin. She had heard it sung by a hollow king in a gilded hall, had felt it crawl through her bones like frost through mortar. And here it came again, out of a child's sleeping mouth, soft as a lullaby.

Except it wasn't the same. That was the thing Paloma couldn't stop turning over, night after night, watching the firelight move on Sefa's face. The entity's song devoured. It pulled at the edges of you, made your memories feel porous, your name feel borrowed. But this version — this small, wandering, wordless thread — didn't pull at all. It simply *was*, the way a stream is, the way wind through wheat is. It passed through the camp and asked nothing.

Odile hated it. Paloma could see it in the set of the ferrywoman's shoulders every time the humming started, the way her one eye fixed on the middle distance and her jaw went hard as river stone. Odile never said anything. She just moved her bedroll a little farther from the girl each night, and each morning she was brisker than the day before, all business, all forward motion, as if the road itself might outrun whatever followed them.

Wren, meanwhile, had appointed himself Sefa's shadow. He walked beside her, ate beside her, sat beside her at the fire with his knees drawn up and his chin on them, listening to her hum with an expression Paloma couldn't quite read — wonder braided tight with something like grief. When she woke confused and embarrassed to find she'd been singing, Wren always said the same thing: "It's nice. You shouldn't be sorry for it."

Tonight, though, the song changed.

They had made camp early, in the lee of a fallen oak, because the sky had been bruising purple since noon and Cassian wanted distance between them and the king's road before dark. The storm held off but never quite broke, and the air lay thick and expectant, like a held breath. Paloma was copying out the stolen fragment of the binding rite by firelight — her third attempt, her fingers cramped, the old script fighting her at every turn — when Sefa's voice rose from her blankets and did something it had never done before.

It turned.

The melody bent, deliberately, unmistakably, toward the north and west. Toward the hills.

Paloma's pen stopped. She looked across the fire. Cassian was already awake, already sitting up, his hand near his knife. His eyes met hers across the flames, and she saw her own thought reflected there: *it knows where it's going.*

Odile was on her feet. "Wake her," she said flatly. "Wake her up and make it stop."

"It doesn't work like that." Wren's voice, quiet but stubborn, from the shadows where he kept watch over the girl. "You've seen me try. She just keeps singing. Louder, if you shake her."

"Then we gag her—"

"She's a child, Odile."

"She's a *compass*," Odile snapped, "pointing at the thing that emptied my brother, and I will not sit in the dark and listen to it call us—"

"*Listen*," Paloma said.

And they listened.

Sefa's voice climbed. Not louder — clearer. The melody shed its wandering quality and became purposeful, structured, and underneath it Paloma began to hear something she'd missed before: harmonics. Faint ones, ghost-faint, as if the girl were singing several lines at once, or as if the air itself were answering her. The fire guttered though there was no wind. The hair on Paloma's arms stood up.

And then, threading through the child's voice, thin and distant and impossible, came a second line. A descant. Answering from somewhere far away in the dark hills.

Cassian stood slowly. "That's not her."

"No," Paloma breathed. "It's echoing off something. Off—" She stopped, because the shape of the thought arriving in her mind was too large and too strange, and saying it aloud would make it real.

But Wren said it for her, hushed and awed: "Something's singing back."

* * *

They argued until the moon rose, which was not long, because the moon was a sliver and the clouds ate even that.

Odile wanted to go south, hard south, away from the hills and the song and the girl who served as its lure. "We have a destination," she said, jabbing a finger at the ground as if the map were drawn there. "The underways. The capital. Every day we wander is a day the steward gets closer, and I've buried enough people to know how that arithmetic ends."

"The underways are a rumor," Cassian said. "A good one — I believe you — but we don't know the entrance, and we can't ask directions wearing these faces. Meanwhile something ancient is answering a god-touched child from inside those hills, and I would very much like to know what, before it decides to come down and introduce itself."

"You want to walk *toward* it."

"I want to choose the ground," Cassian said. "There's a difference."

Paloma said nothing at first. She had unrolled the stolen fragment across her knees and was reading it again by firelight, really reading it, letting her scribe's training do what fear had been preventing. The text was a fragment — literally so, torn along one edge, half a rite — but fragments have shapes, and the shape of this one implied a whole. A binding. Voices. An anchoring. And a phrase that had nagged at her for days, that she had translated three different ways hoping two of them would prove wrong:

*a life freely given.*

She looked up at the hills, black against the lesser black of the sky, toward whatever was singing back.

"My family wrote this," she said quietly, and the argument died. "My bloodline. The steward said it, and I hated him for it, but the script confirms it — this is Thackeray handwork, the ligatures, the contraction marks. We bound something once. Or helped to. And if there's a piece of that working still alive in the world, singing back to Sefa from inside a hill..." She folded the fragment with hands that were steadier than she felt. "I need to see it. Whatever it costs."

"That's what frightens me," Odile said, but she was already kicking dirt over the fire. "Fine. North and west. But we move quiet, and if the girl leads us into a trap, I'm throwing her to whatever's waiting and rowing home alone."

No one answered that. No one needed to. They all knew, by now, that Odile's threats were load-bearing walls — things she built between herself and the grief underneath.

They broke camp in the dark, and Sefa woke without being called, rubbed her eyes, and pointed northwest with the serene certainty of someone reporting the weather. "That way," she said. "It's been waiting a long time. It's lonely."

* * *

They walked for two days.

The country changed as they went, shedding the softness of the lowlands. The farms gave out into rough pasture, the pasture into moorland stitched with streams, and the streams into stone. The hills gathered themselves into something older and less forgiving, crowned with standing stones that leaned like tired sentries. There were no roads here. There were traces of roads — sunken lanes grown over with turf, the ghosts of wheel-ruts — as if people had once come this way often and then, all together, at some point long ago, had simply stopped.

On the second afternoon they found the first grave.

It was a cairn, waist-high, furred with moss, standing alone in a fold of the moor. Then, within the hour, another. Then dozens. Cairns by the hundred, scattered across the slopes like a flock petrified mid-migration, each one stacked with a care that centuries of weather hadn't fully erased. Someone had carried stones here, one by one, over and over, for a very long time.

"They're not war graves," Cassian said, crouching by the nearest. He brushed moss from a flat capstone and went very still. "Look."

Carved into the stone, worn nearly smooth, was a symbol Paloma recognized from the fragment: the interlocking circles that stood, in the old script, for *voice joined to voice*. Below it, barely legible, a single word.

*Sang.*

Sang. Sang and sang and sang. They had been singers, all of them, buried on this moor within earshot of whatever slept in the hill.

Sefa walked among the cairns without fear, trailing her fingers over the stones, humming softly. And here was the thing that raised the hair on Paloma's neck: as the girl passed each cairn, the hum seemed to deepen, to pick up a resonance, as though the stones themselves remembered the note.

"They're not empty," Wren whispered. He wasn't asking a question either.

"No," Paloma said. "I don't think they are."

They camped that night among the dead singers, and no one suggested it might be bad luck. It seemed somehow worse than bad luck — it seemed deliberate, like walking into a story that had been waiting for them specifically.

In the morning, Sefa stopped at the base of the largest hill and said, simply: "Here."

There was nothing there. Grass, scree, a hawthorn twisted by wind. But when Paloma looked — really looked, with the sense the mark had given her, the unwelcome gift of seeing the world's seams — she saw it. A faint wrongness in the slope, a straightness that grass cannot hide forever. The ghost of a doorway.

"It's sealed," she said. "Buried. Deliberately."

"How do we get in?" Cassian asked.

Paloma approached the hidden door slowly. Up close she could see the seam of it, a rectangle of fitted stone sunk into the hillside, and carved across its face — dense, deep, untouched by weather because it had never seen weather — ran lines of the old script. Rows upon rows of it. Her breath caught.

It wasn't a warning. It was a *lock*. And the lock was a question.

She knelt and began to read, tracing the characters with her fingertip, translating aloud in a whisper because some part of her felt the hill was listening:

*"Who enters? — A voice of the line that bound.*
*What does it carry? — The debt unpaid.*
*What does it seek? — What was buried with the singers.*
*Let it speak, and the door remembers."*

"It wants a Thackeray," she said. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears, flattened, far away. "It wants me to say it out loud."

"Paloma." Cassian's voice, careful. "You don't have to."

"Yes, I do." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "Don't you see? This is what the fragment was for. This is why my family — why the debt, the ledgers, the whole wretched inheritance. We weren't just jailers. We were *keepers*. Somebody had to remember how to get back in."

She stood before the buried door, closed her eyes, and spoke in the old tongue, her accent clumsy but her diction exact, twenty years of copying other people's words finally amounting to something:

"I am Paloma Thackeray. I carry the debt unpaid. I seek what was buried with the singers."

For a moment, nothing. The moor wind moved. Somewhere behind her, Sefa hummed a single sustained note, low and patient.

Then the hill *sighed*.

It was the only word for it — a vast exhalation of dust and displaced air, a groan of stone that had held one position for centuries deciding, reluctantly, to hold another. The seam brightened with a light that wasn't quite light, and the door swung inward, exhaling air that smelled of cold rock, old incense, and something else — something sweet and faded, like flowers pressed in a book for five hundred years.

Darkness waited inside. And in the darkness, faint but unmistakable, the answer to Sefa's song still hung in the air, unfinished, holding its last note the way a singer holds a breath.

Waiting for them to come and complete it.

* * *

Inside, the chapel was small.

That was Paloma's first thought, and it surprised her — she had expected vastness, a cavern, something scaled to the size of the thing it had imprisoned. Instead: a single round chamber, maybe thirty feet across, its ceiling lost in shadow above, its walls rising in smooth courses of pale stone. Seven niches were cut into the walls at even intervals, and in each niche rested objects that time had not dared touch: a bell with no clapper. A bowl of what looked like frozen water. A robe folded with sleeves crossed. Small things. Personal things. Offerings, or relics, or the belongings of the dead, arranged with love.

The floor was the shock.

Every inch of it — every flagstone, edge to edge — was carved with names. Thousands of them, tens of thousands, interlocking in flowing lines of the old script, radiating outward from a central circle like ripples from a dropped stone. Paloma knelt and put her palm flat against the nearest name and felt, distinctly, warmth. As if the stone remembered being touched by living hands.

"The Choir," she whispered. "This is them. All of them."

"Read the walls," Sefa said. She had gone to the center of the room and stood in the central circle, head tilted, listening to something. "The walls tell it better."

So Paloma read.

The script here was older than the fragment, older than the temple's forbidden archives, and harder — the letters cut deep and sure by hands that knew they were writing the most important sentence of their age. But she had spent her life reading dead men's handwriting, and slowly, line by line, the chapel told its story.

*A thing came out of the dark between stars,* the wall said. *It was hungry the way winter is hungry. It wore our king as a coat. We could not kill it, for it was not alive. We could not drive it out, for it held fast to the world through him.*

*So we sang it smaller.*

*Voice by voice, we took its song and gave it ours. We wrapped it in harmony, and harmony is a cage when the singers are willing, for willing voices do not break. We bound it beneath the valley, and we set wards above it — not to keep the world safe from it, but to keep the world from hearing it call.*

*The binding holds while the choir sings. The choir must be full. The voices must be willing. And one must begin — one First Voice, who gives the song its shape and holds it true.*

*The First Voice gives freely, wholly, and does not rise from the floor when the singing ends.*

Paloma stopped reading.

She read it again. Then a third time, moving her finger under each word as though slowness might change them, as though she were the provost checking a novice's transcription and could send the sentence back for revision.

*Does not rise from the floor when the singing ends.*

Behind her, she heard Cassian read the same passage over her shoulder, and heard his breathing change. Heard Odile, by the door, say nothing at all, which from Odile was the loudest sound in the room. Heard Wren ask, in a small voice, "What does 'does not rise' mean?" and heard no one answer him, because none of them could bear to make it plainer than the wall already had.

The binding required a full choir of willing voices. Fine. Difficult, dangerous, but fine — they had, God help them, already begun to become that, five mismatched survivors rehearsing trust around campfires. But the rite demanded more. It demanded a keel. An anchor. A First Voice to begin the song and pour themselves into it completely, willingly, *freely given* —

— and the First Voice dies.

That was the hook inside the hope. That was the tooth hidden in the bait. Every generation since the first binding, the stewards and kings and priests who maintained the cage had needed exactly one thing: a person willing enough, desperate enough, devoted enough, to stand at the head of the choir and give everything. And the chapel floor was carpeted with names because the names were the price, paid over and over, century after century, by singers who had walked into rooms like this one and chosen to become the foundation stone.

Paloma looked at Sefa.

The girl stood in the center circle, humming, her face lit with something terrible and serene, and Paloma understood with sickening clarity that the child had known. Had perhaps always known. Had heard the walls' truth in the song long before any of them could read it, and had followed it anyway, leading them across the moor like a lamb walking confidently toward an altar, and the worst of it — the very worst — was the expression on her face. Not fear. *Relief.* The look of someone who has finally been shown the shape of the thing she was made for.

"No," Paloma said aloud, to the girl, to the walls, to the whole ancient machinery of sacrifice. "No. Find another way. There's always another way. I refuse to believe my ancestors were clever enough to build this and too stupid to leave a door open."

But the walls said what they said. The names on the floor said what they said. And the air in the chapel still held that unfinished note, patient as stone, waiting for a First Voice to complete it.

Outside, far off across the moor, thunder rolled — or something that was not quite thunder. And on Paloma's palm, the entity's mark stirred, warm as a waking animal, as if it too had heard the walls.

As if it knew they'd found the instructions.

And were running out of time to decide who would pay them.
