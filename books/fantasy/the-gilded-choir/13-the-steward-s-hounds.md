## 13. The Steward's Hounds

Cassian had learned to read weather the way other men read faces, and the weather that morning was lying.

The sky over the lowlands was a clean, washed blue, the kind that promised an easy day's travel, and that was precisely what made his neck prickle. In eleven years of soldiering — six of them honest, five of them not — he had never once been granted a genuinely easy day by a sky that looked like this. Easy skies were what the crown's hunters marched under. They wanted good light for tracking, and they wanted their quarry comfortable, and comfort made people slow.

"You keep looking up," Odile said from the head of the cart. She didn't turn around. Her one eye stayed fixed on the road ahead, but her voice carried back to him with the flat certainty of a woman who had been reading men longer than he'd been reading weather.

"Clouds are too pretty," Cassian said.

"Hm." She flicked the reins. "My mother used to say a beautiful day is the world holding its breath."

"Your mother sounds like she'd have made a fine sergeant."

"She made a better smuggler." A pause, and then, quieter: "She died on a beautiful day too."

The cart creaked on. Behind him, in the bed of it, Sefa slept with her head in Wren's lap, her small hands curled against her chest like a sleeping bird's feet. Even asleep she hummed — barely, a thread of sound so faint that anyone else would have taken it for wind through the cart slats. But Cassian heard it now. He'd been hearing it for three days, ever since the waystation, ever since they'd cut her chains and run. The melody lived under everything, patient as water working at stone. Wren said it was pretty. Paloma said it was the entity's song, worn smooth by passing through a child's throat until it came out something almost gentle.

Cassian thought it sounded like a lullaby someone had taught to a knife.

He shifted on the bench and let his hand rest near the knife at his belt, which was where his hand rested most of the time anyway. Old habits. The useful ones kept you alive; the useless ones kept you company.

* * *

They stopped at noon beside a stream that ran brown with autumn leaf-rot, and Paloma spread the stolen fragment of the binding rite on a flat rock and stared at it the way she always did — like a woman trying to read a letter written by someone who hated her.

Cassian watched her while pretending to check the cart's axle. He'd gotten good at watching her without being caught at it, which was a skill he wasn't proud of. Soldiers developed it. You watched the person most likely to break, because the breaking always started somewhere, and if you saw it early enough you could sometimes stop it.

Paloma wouldn't break, though. That was the strange thing. She bent — gods, how she bent, every new horror folding her smaller — and then she straightened again, slowly, like green wood. It was Wren he worried about. And Sefa. And, increasingly, himself, though he kept that worry folded up tight in the pocket where he kept all his other unforgivable things.

"The bridge," Odile said suddenly.

Cassian followed her eye. Two miles on, maybe less, the road crossed the River Sorrel on a humpbacked span of old grey stone — the kind of bridge built before the wards went up, before the valley forgot the sky, back when people still built things meant to last past their own grandchildren. He could see the far side of it clearly in the noon light. Open meadow. A hedgerow. Nothing moving.

Nothing moving was worse than something moving.

"We could ford," he said.

"Not with the cart." Odile spat into the leaves. "Not with the girl sleeping. The Sorrel runs fast this time of year, and I've pulled drowned children out of shallower water than that." Her jaw worked. "We cross the bridge or we abandon the cart, and we abandon the cart, we abandon the supplies, and we abandon the supplies, we're eating bark inside four days."

"So we cross fast."

"So we cross fast," she agreed, in the tone of a woman agreeing with a plan she intended to survive out of spite rather than confidence.

Paloma looked up from the fragment. "What aren't you two saying?"

"That the bridge is a choke point," Cassian said, because lying to her had stopped feeling possible somewhere around the burned villages, "and choke points are where hunters stop tracking and start waiting."

Wren's hand found Sefa's shoulder, protective even in sleep. The boy had changed since the waystation. There was something harder set in his face now, some line drawn that hadn't been there when Cassian first saw him — a temple brat clutching a stolen apple in the back of a cart. He still cried easy. But he'd also stood between Sefa and a loaded crossbow with nothing but his own skinny body and a voice cracking with fury, and Cassian had seen enough men freeze in front of crossbows to know that the boy's courage was real, and real courage frightened him more than cowardice ever had.

"Then we don't all cross at once," Paloma said. She was already thinking it through; he could see the ledger-keeper's mind working, columns and sums. "Odile takes the cart across slow, like any farmer. Wren walks ahead of it, ordinary, just a boy stretching his legs. Sefa—"

"Sefa stays with me," Cassian said.

Everyone looked at him.

"She hums," he said. "Even asleep. If there are hunters on that bridge, they're listening for exactly that. She crosses with me, after, once we know it's clean. If it isn't clean—" He shrugged, and made his voice do the thing it did in the army, the flat thing, the thing that made plans sound like arithmetic instead of lives. "Then the cart turns around and we find another way, and nobody's trapped on the wrong side of a river with a child the whole crown is hunting."

"And you?" Paloma asked.

"I'll be right behind you."

It was, he reflected later, the first outright lie he told her. Not the last. But the first one he noticed.

* * *

The bridge was clean.

That was the second lie the day told them. The sky had lied about the weather and the bridge lied about the hunters, and Cassian walked across it behind Odile's cart with his heart hammering a rhythm he hadn't felt since Vell's Ford, since the battle he didn't talk about, since the day he'd turned deserter and discovered that running from a thing and escaping it were very different animals.

The stones were slick with moss. The river sang underneath, brown and quick. On the far side, the meadow opened gold and harmless, and Wren stood at the edge of it waving them on like an idiot child, and Odile took the cart up the far bank, and for perhaps forty seconds Cassian allowed himself to believe the day might simply be a day.

Then he saw the dust.

It came off the southern road behind them — not the road they'd come by, but the parallel track along the ridge, the one Odile had avoided for two days on instinct. Dust in columns. Organized dust. Loose riders kick up scatter; soldiers kick up lines.

"Paloma." His voice came out low and even, army-flat, and he was distantly proud of that. "Get everyone off the road. Now."

She turned. She saw it too — she was quick, quicker than she gave herself credit for — and he watched the understanding move across her face like frost across a window. "How many?"

"Six. Maybe eight. Light cavalry, moving at a trot." He did the arithmetic without wanting to. "Twenty minutes. Less if they've got a tracker."

"They'll have a tracker." Odile had already turned the cart, her knuckles white on the reins. "They've had a tracker since the waystation. That's how they found us. That's how they'll always find us, as long as—" Her eye cut toward Sefa, and stopped, and Cassian saw the thought finish itself in her face without needing words: *as long as we have her.*

Sefa had woken. She stood in the cart between the supply sacks, small and dark-eyed, and she was humming louder now — not afraid-loud, not panicked. Something else. Something almost like recognition.

"They're singing," she said.

Nobody answered her. Nobody wanted to ask what she meant.

* * *

They ran.

There was no dignity in it and no strategy either, at first — just the cart lurching off the road into the meadow grass, wheels jolting over tussocks, Wren stumbling alongside with his hand hooked in the cart rail, Paloma carrying the binding fragment pressed flat against her chest like a shield that was only paper. The hedgerow was their only cover and it was a hundred yards off and gaining it felt like swimming through honey.

Cassian ran at the rear. That was where he'd always run, in the army — rear guard, the position they gave to men they trusted and men they didn't, depending on the day. He counted strides and listened past his own breath, and what he heard made his stomach drop: the hoofbeats weren't following the road anymore. They'd cut the angle. Of course they had. Open ground, visible quarry, and a commander who knew what he was doing.

Which meant the hounds had a handler. Which meant the steward had sent someone competent, which meant—

"Cart's done!" Odile shouted, and she was right; the left wheel had found a drainage ditch hidden in the grass and cracked its hub with a sound like a bone breaking. The cart slewed, tipped, and settled at a broken angle, supplies spilling — flour, salt fish, the oilcloth bundle of spare clothes, everything their survival for the next week had been stacked in.

"Take what you can carry," Paloma said. "Food and the fragment. Leave the rest."

They scattered into the hedgerow like startled birds, and the hedge swallowed them — blackthorn and briar, scratching and clinging, a green tunnel barely wide enough for a child. Cassian shoved Sefa through ahead of him, then Wren, then turned to haul Paloma bodily through a gap she'd have taken a minute to negotiate alone.

Behind them, across the open meadow, the riders crested the rise.

Eight. He'd been close. Red-and-black livery, the crown's colors, and at their head a rider in no colors at all — just grey, unmarked grey, the way hunters dressed when they didn't want to be remembered. The grey rider raised a hand and the column split without a word spoken, fanning out across the meadow in a pattern Cassian knew intimately, because he'd drilled it himself, because he'd *led* it himself, in another life, under another banner, before Vell's Ford had shown him what the banner was actually for.

The pincer. Left flank through the meadow, right flank along the river, closing on the hedgerow from both ends.

They had maybe three minutes before the hedge stopped being a tunnel and became a trap.

* * *

"There," Paloma gasped. She was pointing down the hedge-line, south, where the blackthorn thinned toward the riverbank. "If we reach the water—"

"They'll see us cross it," Cassian said. "Open banks. We'd be ducks on a pond."

"Then what? We can't stay in the hedge, they'll ride it down both ends, we can't go forward, we can't go back—" Her voice was climbing, and he understood why. This was the part the ledgers never taught you, the part where the numbers stopped balancing and you had to choose which column to lose.

"I can hold them."

Everything stopped. Even the wind seemed to pause in the blackthorn.

"Cassian," Paloma said carefully.

"A bridge holds them better than a hedge does." He was already talking fast, already building the argument, because he'd built this argument before, in his head, a hundred times since the waystation — the argument he'd hoped never to need. "A narrow place against eight riders. One man with a sword and a bowline of hedge behind him. They can't flank a bridge, Paloma. They can't surround it. They can come at me one and two at a time or they can sit and wait, and sitting and waiting costs them daylight, and daylight is the one thing a hunter can't buy back."

"You'll die."

"Maybe." He met her eyes. Hers were wet and furious and he made himself hold them, because looking away would be the same as admitting what he was actually doing. "But maybe buys you the river crossing at dusk, when the light's bad. Maybe buys you the ferry at Corrow Ford, Odile knows the ferryman. Maybe is worth something."

"No." That was Wren, and the boy's voice cracked straight down the middle. "No, you don't get to — that's not — we don't *do* that, we don't leave people, that's what *they* do—"

"Wren." Cassian crouched to the boy's level, which was a thing he'd never done for anyone in uniform and did now without thinking. "Listen to me. I deserted. Do you understand what that means? I left. I left my post, I left my company, I left men who trusted me, and some of them died because of it, and I have been running from that for five years." The words came out of him rough, torn loose like splinters. "This is the first time in my life I get to be the one who *stays*. Let me have it. Please."

"That's not brave, that's just sad," Wren said, crying openly now, furious about crying. "That's the saddest thing I've ever heard."

"Yeah," Cassian agreed. "Probably."

Sefa tugged his sleeve.

He looked down. The girl was staring past him, through the hedge, toward the meadow, and she was humming — full voice now, clear and sweet and terrible, and Cassian felt the melody slide into his chest and settle there like a hand on his sternum. Her eyes had gone somewhere else. Somewhere older.

"He's not angry," she said. "The man in grey. He's *empty*. Like a house where the fire's gone out." A pause. Her small brow furrowed. "And there's something behind him. Far away. It's waking up, and it knows our song now, and it's—"

"Sefa." Paloma knelt and took the girl's face in her hands, gentle but urgent. "Sweetheart. Look at me. Later. Whatever you hear, tell us later, when we're safe. Can you do that?"

Sefa blinked, and came back, and nodded, and the humming stopped, and somehow the silence was worse.

* * *

The riders reached the near end of the hedgerow and began to work down it, methodical, unhurried, the way men move when they believe the outcome is already decided. Cassian could hear the horses' breathing, the creak of leather, the soft metallic music of equipment worn by people who expected to use it.

He pressed his knife into Paloma's hand.

She stared at it. At him.

"Bridge is half a mile north," he said. "You double back through the hedge while I make noise at the south end. Cross at dusk. Corrow Ford. Odile's ferryman — a fat man named Brecht, tell him the Sorrel owes him a winter." He closed her fingers over the knife grip. "Don't wait for me. Waiting is how families die."

"We're not leaving you." Her voice shook. "I'm not — Cassian, I have spent my entire life being *taken*, being sold, being collateral, being carried off like a sack of grain, and I am not going to stand on the far side of a river and add your name to the list of things that were taken from me while I held still. Do you hear me?"

Something in his chest cracked along an old fault line.

"Then don't hold still," he said. "Run. Running isn't holding still. Go be loud and alive and impossible to catch, and write it down someday, in one of your ledgers — *the deserter stayed, and it was the only debt he ever paid gladly.*"

"You absolute fool," she whispered, and then she kissed his cheek, quick and fierce, like sealing a letter, and turned, and pushed Wren and Sefa ahead of her into the deep of the hedge, and ran.

Cassian watched them go for exactly one breath. Then he drew his sword, stepped out of the blackthorn into the open gold of the meadow, and started shouting insults at the crown of Aldric in a voice that carried half a mile, because noise was the job now, noise was the whole job, noise bought them dusk.

* * *

He killed the first one honestly.

The grey rider sent men at him in pairs, exactly as predicted, exactly as drilled, and the first pair came confident and sloppy across the tussock grass, and Cassian took the horse's legs out from under the nearer rider and put his sword through the gap in the man's gorget as the animal fell. Clean. Quick. The kind of kill they teach you in the third year, when they stop teaching you to fight and start teaching you to end things.

The second pair were not sloppy.

They came high and low together, lance and saber, and Cassian gave ground, giving it grudgingly, foot by foot, trading the open meadow for the riverbank, because the plan — his plan, the good plan, the plan that was supposed to buy them dusk — required him to pull them north, away from the hedge, away from Paloma and the children, away toward the bridge he'd promised to hold.

He got them to the bridge.

He even held it, for a while. Long enough to feel, absurdly, briefly, like the man Wren had decided he was. The stones were slick and narrow and the riders couldn't bring more than two abreast onto it, and Cassian fought the way he'd fought at Vell's Ford before the order came that broke him — low, economical, mean, each stroke spending exactly as much as it needed to and not a drop more. He put one rider into the river. He broke another's knee with a pommel strike and let the man's own horse trample him off the span. The grey rider hung back at the meadow's edge and watched, and did not commit, and Cassian understood with cold clarity that he was not fighting to win. He was fighting to measure.

The grey rider was measuring how long the bridge would take.

* * *

It was the horn that ended it.

A single note from the south, from the hedgerow, from the direction he had *sent them* — and Cassian's heart went from his chest into his throat, because that was a hunting horn, that was the crown's signal call, and it meant the flanking party had found something. Had found *someone*. Had found, gods, please no, a dropped bundle, a scrap of cloth, anything, anything but—

But then he heard the screaming, and it wasn't Paloma's voice, and it wasn't Wren's, and the relief hit him so hard he nearly lost his guard, and losing his guard was the mistake, because the third pair of riders had been waiting all along for exactly that — for the half-second where the rear guard's mind left the bridge and flew south after his people.

The net took him across the shoulders.

Weighted cord, lead-shot at the knots, thrown by a man who'd done it a hundred times — it wrapped him mid-turn and dragged him off his feet and onto the bridge stones, and his sword rang away into the Sorrel, and boots arrived on him from three directions at once, practiced, professional, pinning wrist and elbow and throat.

Cassian fought anyway. Of course he fought. He fought until the world narrowed to knees and rope and the taste of blood, until a boot heel found his temple and rang it like a bell, until the grey rider crouched beside him on the ancient stones and studied him with a face as calm and vacant as an empty house.

"Deserter," the grey man said. Not a question. He'd read the brand, probably — the faded scar on Cassian's forearm that the army gave its runners when it caught them, back before it stopped bothering to catch them. "Vell's Ford company. I remember the file." He smiled without any warmth arriving in his face. "The steward will be pleased. He collects interesting men."

Through the ringing in his skull, through the ropes and the boots and the copper taste flooding his mouth, Cassian turned his head and looked south, at the hedgerow, at the empty gold afternoon where his family had been.

Gone. They were gone. He could feel it the way you feel a fire gone out in the next room — by the absence, by the cold. Whatever the horn had been, whatever the screaming had been, Paloma and the children were moving, running, alive, and the grey man's attention was here, on him, drinking him in like ink into blotting paper.

*Hold the bridge,* he'd promised.

He'd held it.

The grey rider leaned closer, and behind those calm empty eyes something moved — something vast and patient and hungry, looking out through a borrowed face the way light comes through a cracked door — and it spoke with the grey man's mouth in a voice that was not the grey man's, a voice like a choir heard underwater:

"*Tell me, little runner. Where does a song hide, when it wants to live?*"

The last thing Cassian saw, before the hood came down and the world went black, was the bridge stones — old, older than the valley, older than the wards — and carved into one of them, half-worn, half-hidden, a spiral mark he'd walked over a hundred times without seeing.

The same spiral that was branded into Paloma's palm.

Then the hood, and the dark, and hands lifting him like cargo, and the river singing on beneath the bridge without him —

and somewhere far to the south, thin as a thread, a child's voice, humming.
