## 14. Odile's Price

The fire was small. Odile built it that way on purpose — a fist of flame tucked into the hollow of the ruined granary, where the walls still stood to waist height and the wind coming off the burned fields could not find them. Small fires were a habit of survival. You learned it young in the lowlands: light drew soldiers, and soldiers drew ash.

She sat with her back to the wall, one eye on the dark and the other — her only other — on the four of them. The scribe, Paloma, hunched over the stolen fragment of rite with a stub of charcoal, copying by firelight the way she must have copied everything once, careful and quick and half-starved for knowledge. The deserter, Cassian, cleaning a blade he had already cleaned twice. The boy, Wren, asleep sitting up with his head against Sefa's shoulder, and the girl herself awake, humming under her breath — that melody, always that melody, soft as water over stones.

Odile had heard that song before. That was the whole of her trouble, laid out plain as a ferry rope across black water.

She had been waiting three days to say what she had to say. She had told herself she would say it at the ford, then at the crossroads, then when they reached the granary. Each time her tongue had turned to tar. But tomorrow they would reach the mouth of the underways, and after that there would be no more chances to turn back, and Odile Vasseur did not guide people into graves without telling them first how deep the holes went.

"Girl," she said.

Sefa stopped humming. Wren stirred but did not wake. Paloma looked up, charcoal poised.

"Not you." Odile tipped her chin toward the scribe. "You. Put down your pen a moment. What I have to say wants hearing with both ears."

Cassian's hand stilled on the whetstone. He was not a stupid man; deserters who were stupid died in their first winter. He had been watching her since the burning villages, watching the way she watched Sefa, and Odile had known he was counting something behind his eyes. Time to let him see the sum.

* * *

"I had a brother," she said.

The words came out flat, the way she said everything, because if she gave them any shape at all they would break her open, and she could not afford to be broken tonight. Tomorrow night, maybe. Not tonight.

"Milo. Older than me by two years, which meant older than me forever, the way brothers are." She fed a stick to the fire. "We grew up on the river. Our father ran the ferry before me, and his father before him, back past anyone's remembering. Ferry folk don't keep much. A boat, a house on stilts, a name. But we kept our voices. My mother sang the crossing-song every dusk, all of us together, and I want you to understand that before anything else I tell you. We were singers. All of us. It was the one wealth we had."

Paloma had gone very still. Odile saw the scribe's mind working, reaching ahead the way clever minds do, and she raised a hand.

"Don't guess. If you guess right too early you'll flinch, and I'll lose my nerve, and then we'll all walk into the capital blind. Let an old woman talk."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, a night bird called and got no answer. Even the birds were thin out here now, since the purges. Everything alive had gone quiet or gone away.

"Milo had the best voice of any of us," Odile said. "That's not a sister talking. Ask anyone from the river country who remembers — though most of them are ash now, so ask the reeds instead. When Milo sang, dogs came out of their yards to listen. When Milo sang in the temple at harvest, the priest forgot the words of his own blessing. And when the king's men came up the river looking for voices for the royal choir, they heard Milo before they ever saw him."

"The royal choir," Cassian said quietly. "The one in the palace."

"The same. Though we didn't know then what it was for. Nobody in the lowlands knew. We thought it was an honor." Odile smiled, and felt her scar pull at the corner of her eye, and hated that smile. "An honor. They took him in autumn, when I was fourteen. Gave our father a purse heavy enough to buy a new boat and a lie heavy enough to swallow it. *His voice will serve the crown,* they said. *He'll sing for the king himself.*"

"And did he?" Paloma asked. Her voice was gentle, and Odile almost snapped at her for it, almost told her not to pity-ferry her across this water. But the gentleness was real, and there was no cruelty in it, and so she let it pass.

"He did. For eleven years. Letters came, maybe twice a year, written by a palace clerk because Milo's letters home were full of things the clerk crossed out. He wrote about the singing. He wrote that the choir sang every night, even when there was no feast, even when there was no audience, and that afterward he was tired in a way sleep didn't touch. He wrote that the king never came to hear them, which he thought strange, because why keep a hundred voices in a gilded room if the king doesn't like music?" She looked at each of them in turn. "I know now what the tiredness was. So do you. You've seen the girl hum one line of that thing's song and felt the air go strange. Now imagine a hundred voices, every night, for years. Feeding it."

Wren had woken somewhere in the telling. He didn't say anything — the boy had learned when to be silent, mostly — but his arm had come around Sefa's shoulders, and the girl was listening with her whole body, the way children listen, as if the story might reach out and touch her. In a way, Odile thought, it already had.

* * *

"When the valley wards fell," she went on, "everything changed. You remember that night better than I do, scribe — you were inside the cage when the bars broke. Out here, we felt it different. The river ran backward for an hour. My dogs wouldn't stop howling. And Milo came home."

She had rehearsed this part many times, lying awake in ferry-houses and ditches, and it never got easier. It got heavier, like a stone carried uphill.

"He walked up the ferry road on a morning in late spring, and he looked wrong. That's the truth of it, plain. He was thirty-one years old and he moved like an old man and stood like a soldier and when he smiled at me it wasn't — " Her jaw worked. "It wasn't Milo's smile. It was a smile someone had taught him, the way you teach a puppet. But underneath it, deep down, there were moments. Moments when his eyes cleared and it was my brother looking out, drowning, and those moments were worse than the rest. Because in those moments he'd grab my wrist and say, *Odile, it sings through me, it won't stop, make it stop.*"

"What was it?" Wren asked. His voice cracked on the second word. "In him. What was it?"

"The same thing that's in the king now." Odile met the boy's eyes. "The same thing your friend hums. Old beyond old. It wore kings before Aldric, and queens, and priests, and one temple scribe whose face is hanging in a gallery in the palace if the stories hold true. And for six years, off and on, it wore my brother."

Sefa made a small sound. Not a word. Odile looked at her — the girl's hands pressed flat against her own throat, as if checking that her voice was still hers — and something in Odile's chest turned over and lay still.

"It used him," Odile said. "It rode him up and down the river country in the first year after the wards fell, and everywhere it went, people changed. Not all at once. Little by little. Neighbors forgetting debts owed to neighbors. Villages going quiet, then going cruel. It was testing, do you see? Testing how far its song reached outside the valley walls. And the crown — the steward and his men — they followed behind it, burning whatever it touched. They called it cleansing. They called my brother's body the source of the corruption." She spat, deliberately, into the fire. "They were half right. The corruption was real. But it wasn't Milo's."

"So they hunted him," Cassian said. "And you?"

"And me." Odile folded her hands so they would not shake. "I ferried him. That's the bitter joke of it. Soldiers on one bank, my brother on the other, and me poling between them, and every time I took him across I told myself I was keeping him close, keeping him safe, keeping watch. Six years I told myself that. Six years I poled that boat with that thing sitting in the stern wearing my brother's face, humming its song, and I listened, and I watched, and I waited for my brother to come back to me."

The fire had burned low. She did not feed it. Some stories wanted dimness.

"One night in the sixth year, he came to the crossing at moonrise, and he was clear. Fully clear, first time in months. His eyes were his own, wet and terrified, and he grabbed the gunwale of my boat and said, *Odile, it's sleeping tonight, it sleeps some nights now, and when it sleeps I can feel the edges of myself. There's a way to end it. There's a rite. The old chapel under the hills — the Choir bound it once and the binding can be unmade or remade, but someone has to carry word to the lowland singers, someone has to gather them — * and he was crying, and I believed him. Gods forgive me, I believed every word."

"You didn't," Paloma said softly.

"I did worse than not believe him." Odile's voice did not rise. It never rose. It just went colder, the way river water goes cold a fathom down. "I believed him, and I was afraid. Because I knew what the crown did to places the entity touched, and I knew that if I helped my brother gather singers, the soldiers would come to my crossing too, and burn my house, and drown my dogs, and put my name on the same list as his. And I was tired, scribe. Tired the way Milo's letters said he was tired. Six years of sleeping with one ear open, six years of loving a face that sometimes wasn't a face. So when he asked me to pole him downriver that night — away from the soldiers who were closing in, toward the old chapel, toward hope — I poled him. I poled him straight to the crown's encampment."

Nobody spoke. The night bird called again, and again nothing answered it.

"They paid me in silver," Odile said. "A purse of it. Enough to rebuild the ferry twice over. They promised me he wouldn't be hurt. They said they'd take him to the capital and draw the thing out of him, that they had ways, that the king's own scholars knew the rites." She laughed, one dry note with no music in it at all. "I was fourteen when they took his voice for the choir and I believed that lie too. You'd think a woman learns."

"What happened to him?" Wren's voice was barely above the fire's crackle.

"They couldn't draw it out. Whatever the scholars knew, it wasn't enough, or it wasn't true. So they did what the crown does with problems it can't solve." Odile looked into the coals. "They burned him. On the far bank, in an iron cage, with the whole encampment watching, because the steward wanted the lowlands to see what became of vessels. And the thing inside him — it left him at the last. I know because I was there, on the water, close enough to hear. At the very end, in the smoke, my brother's voice came out of that cage clear as harvest-morning, and he sang the crossing-song. Mother's song. All three verses, alone, while he burned. And then it was done, and the thing that had worn him went — elsewhere. Looking, I suppose, for a finer cage." She lifted her eyes to the dark east, where the capital lay. "It found one."

* * *

Silence held the granary for a long time. The coals settled. Wren was crying without noise, the way children cry when they've learned crying is dangerous, and Sefa had taken his hand without either of them seeming to notice.

It was Cassian who spoke first, and Odile marked that he spoke like a soldier, not like a judge. "Why tell us this now?"

"Because tomorrow I take you to the underways, and the underways go under the palace, and under the palace is the thing that killed my brother." She turned to Paloma. "And because you're the one carrying the rite, scribe, and I've watched you read it for three days, and I know what you're going to find in it eventually, because I found it in Milo's fever-talk six years ago. The binding wants a life. Freely given. And when that day comes — and it's coming, girl, it's coming faster than any of you want — someone in this little company is going to have to stand up and pay it. Or someone is going to have to stop the others from paying it. And I need to know, before we take one more step toward that city, which kind of person you are."

Paloma set down the charcoal. Her hands, Odile noticed, were ink-stained to the second knuckle, the hands of someone who had spent her life writing down other people's accounts. "I don't know," the scribe said. "Honestly. I don't know yet."

Odile grunted. It was, she reflected, the first honest answer she'd gotten from any of them.

"Good," she said. "A liar would have said something pretty."

"But you want a promise anyway," Cassian said. "I can see it loading behind your eye."

"Aye. Here it is, and it's the price of my guiding, and I'll not bend it." Odile leaned forward, into the coal-light, and let them see all of her face — the scar, the old burn along the jaw she'd never explained, the eye that had paid its own tithe. "When the moment comes — and it will come, in a throne room or a tunnel or a burning field — do not choose the safe shore. Whatever the safe shore looks like. Silver purses look safe. Locked gates look safe. Standing still looks safe. I chose the safe shore six years ago and I have been poling back and forth across that night ever since, and there is no crossing back to the bank where my brother stood. So promise me, scribe. Promise me you'll be a coward's opposite. Promise me that when it costs everything — and it will cost everything — you'll pay it standing, among your people, singing. Promise me you won't hand anyone to a cage to save your own skin."

Paloma was quiet a long moment. The firelight moved on her face. "I promise," she said at last. "Not because I'm sure I'm brave. Because I've spent my whole life paying debts I never chose, and I swear on every ledger I ever copied — I will not let another person be handed over in my place. Not for safety. Not for anything."

Odile studied her. Then she nodded, once, and felt something in her chest shift — not lighter, exactly. More like a stone set down after so long it had grown into the shoulder.

"That'll do," she said. "Sleep, all of you. We move at grey light."

* * *

But sleep did not come for Odile, and she did not pretend it would. She sat her watch as she always did, facing the dark, and the others curled around the dead fire like boats drawn up on a shingle, and the hours went by the way hours go when you are alone with your oldest grief.

Near the coldest hour, small feet padded across the floor, and Sefa sat down beside her without asking, the way children do, the way the childless forget children do.

For a while the girl said nothing. Then, very softly, she began to hum — and Odile's whole body went rigid, hand halfway to the knife, before she understood what she was hearing. It was not the entity's melody. It was the crossing-song. Three notes of it, four, the opening phrase her mother had sung at every dusk of Odile's childhood, sung slightly wrong, the way a child learns a song secondhand.

"Where did you hear that?" Odile whispered.

Sefa shrugged. "It was in the walls of the waystation. Songs get stuck in walls. That one was stuck deep, under the screaming." She looked up with her strange, unafraid eyes. "Was it yours?"

Odile looked out at the dark fields, at the buried hills beyond them where an old chapel waited under the roots of the world, at the distant smudge of the capital's lights against the sky like a wound that glowed.

"It was mine," she said. "It was my brother's. And someday, girl — if you're willing, and only if you're willing — I'll teach you all three verses."

Sefa nodded solemnly, and leaned her head against Odile's arm, and slept.

Odile sat unmoving until grey light, the child's warmth against her side, and watched the east. And it was there, in that cold hour, that she saw it: a single rider on the far road, moving fast, raising dust where no dust should be at dawn — riding hard, and riding toward them.

Her hand closed on Cassian's shoulder, and she shook him awake.

"We have company," she said.
