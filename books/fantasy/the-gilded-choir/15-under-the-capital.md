## 15. Under the Capital

The water in the underways was black and warm, like something that had been breathing a long time.

Paloma kept her hand on the wall — slick stone, older than any masonry she had ever copied into a ledger, older perhaps than the temple itself — and tried not to think about what the warmth meant. Odile walked ahead with a lantern that burned without flame, its light a pale bruise-green that made everyone's faces look drowned. Wren clung to the back of Sefa's collar like a burr. Cassian brought up the rear, sword drawn, though Paloma suspected he drew it less for enemies than for the comfort of holding something.

"Mind the third step," Odile said, without turning. "It minds you."

The step, when Paloma reached it, was not there. Her foot swung through empty air over a channel of rushing water, and only Odile's fist closing on her sleeve kept her from following the current down into whatever throat it fed.

"I said mind it," Odile said.

"You said—"

"I know what I said." The ferrywoman's single eye caught the lantern-light. "Down here, saying is doing. That's why they built it this way."

*They.* She never said who. Paloma had stopped asking somewhere around the second mile, when the ceiling had begun to show them carvings: rows upon rows of faces, mouths open, ascending toward a keystone shaped like a crown. The stone-cutters had given every face a different expression of ecstasy or agony, and after a while Paloma could no longer tell which was which.

Sefa could. The girl walked with her chin lifted, humming under her breath — that melody again, the one that lived in Paloma's burned palm now like a splinter of sound. Since the mark, Paloma heard it everywhere. In the drip of water. In the echo of their own footsteps. And always, faintly, in Wren's absent-minded whistling, which frightened her more than anything else in the dark.

"How much farther?" Cassian asked.

"Ask the city." Odile gestured with the lantern. Ahead, the tunnel opened its mouth.

---

They came up out of the underways through a grate in the floor of a cistern-house, and Paloma's first sight of the capital was upside down: reflected whole in a pool of standing water, spires hanging from the sky like stalactites, gilded roofs burning in the late sun. Then she turned her head and saw it right way round, and somehow that was worse.

She had copied descriptions of the capital. Dozens of them. Trade manifests listing silk by the bolt and grain by the ton; pilgrims' accounts of the Great Spire; a tax ledger, once, so heavy it took two clerks to carry, recording the tithes of forty thousand households. She had thought she understood the scale of it the way a scribe understands everything — precisely, and from a great safe distance.

But no ledger told you about the smell. Woodsmoke and horse and hot metal and, underneath it all, something sweetish and rotten, like fruit left too long in a golden bowl. No ledger told you about the sound: not the noise of a city but a *hum*, low and constant, rising out of the ground itself, out of the walls, out of the throats of everyone who passed them in the street without seeming to notice they were making it.

"They're singing," Wren whispered. "Everyone's singing."

"No one's singing," Cassian said.

"Listen." Wren closed his eyes. His face went strange — old, almost, on his twelve-year-old bones. "Underneath. They can't help it."

Paloma listened. He was right. It was not that people were performing songs; it was that their voices leaked, a note here, a fragment there, a woman murmuring thirds to herself as she hauled water, a guard humming a descending line that made Paloma's mark ache like a burn held near a fire. The whole city was an instrument someone had left half-played.

"The royal choir," she said quietly. "How many singers?"

Cassian's jaw tightened. "Officially? Three hundred. Unofficially—" He glanced at Sefa, then at the street, and did not finish.

Odile finished it for him, flat as slate. "However many the crown needs. They press them, girl. Street singers first, then temple cantors, then anyone whose voice tests true. You'll see."

Paloma did see. That night, from the window of the tannery-district safehouse — a room above reeking vats where the hum in the stones was mercifully muffled by the stink — she watched the choir process to evening service at the Great Spire. They came down the Processional Road in ranks, robed in white and gold, and even at this distance she could see what was wrong with them.

They were thin. Not poor-thin, not laborer-thin, but *hollowed*-thin, cheekbones like knife-handles above collars that no longer sat right on necks gone corded and frail. They walked in perfect step without appearing to choose to. Their mouths moved as they walked, shaping silent syllables, rehearsing even now, even exhausted, because some engine behind their ribs would not let them stop.

And their shadows were wrong.

Paloma pressed her forehead to the cold glass. The torches along the Processional Road threw each singer's shadow long across the cobbles — and the shadows *moved differently*. They lagged. They swayed. When one singer stumbled and was hauled upright by his neighbor, his shadow kept stumbling a moment longer, arms windmilling, before snapping back into line like a dog jerked by its leash.

"What happens to them," Paloma said. It was not a question, but Odile answered anyway.

"Every night. Service at the Spire, sundown to midnight. They sing for the court, and the court thinks it's entertainment." The old woman cleaned her fingernails with a fish-knife, unhurried. "And the thing in the king eats. Voice is the oldest food there is, girl. Grief, joy, prayer — it's all just song wearing different clothes. Three hundred voices, poured down that gilded throat every night, and by morning the singers can't remember their mothers' names. Some of them can't remember their own. The crown calls it *refinement*. Says the choir's voices are being 'concentrated for the glory of the realm.'"

"And the families?"

"What families?" Odile smiled without any humor in it at all. "You get taken into the choir, your household gets a stipend and a sealed letter saying your child has been *honored*. Most take the money. The ones who ask questions—" she tapped her eye patch "—the capital has a lot of stairs, and the guards are very busy men."

Wren had gone white. Sefa sat perfectly still in the corner, hands folded, watching the procession with an expression Paloma couldn't read — until she realized it was recognition. The girl knew that music. It lived in her too, the same melody, and she alone seemed able to hold it without being held by it.

"It hurts them," Sefa said softly. "The song. When it takes too much, it starts taking the person instead." She looked up at Paloma, and her eyes were ancient and nine years old at once. "That's what happened to the king. Isn't it."

No one answered. Nobody had to.

---

Two days later, Odile led them through the servants' warrens beneath the palace itself — tunnels she claimed to remember from a life she refused to describe, though sometimes, in the dark, her lips moved around a name she'd lost to the storm of un-song. They surfaced in a disused laundry chute, climbed through linen-smelling dark, and came out in a gallery overlooking the palace's lesser chapel, where the royal choir rehearsed by day.

Paloma would carry what she saw there for the rest of her life.

The chapel was beautiful. That was the obscenity of it. Sunlight fell through windows of honey-colored glass onto a floor of pale marble veined with gold, and the acoustics were so fine that a single voice became a hundred, and a hundred became weather. The choir stood in their carved oak stalls, three hundred strong, and they sang a setting of the Vespers of Quiet Hours — *her* Vespers, the ones she had grown up copying, the plainchant of the valley temple rendered in harmonies so lush they made her eyes sting.

And they were starving.

Up close, the thinness was worse than spectacle; it was arithmetic. She could count ribs through surcoats. She could see the tremor in a boy's jaw — he couldn't have been older than Wren — as he held a high line far past the point where breath should have failed him, sustained by something that was not breath. A woman two stalls down sang with tears running silently down her face, and no one wiped them, and her voice never wavered, because it wasn't entirely hers anymore. It was being *used*.

At the chapel's head stood a conductor in cloth-of-gold, a slim man with a face like a folded document, and when he raised his hand the entire choir inhaled as one body. When he lowered it, three hundred voices poured out — and Paloma felt it leave them. There was no other way to say it. She *felt* it, in her marked palm first and then in her teeth, in the roots of her hair: a great slow draught of something invisible flowing out of those open mouths and up, up through the honeyed glass, up through the gilded roof, toward the Spire and the throne and the thing that waited there with its mouth already open.

The singers sagged as they sang. Between phrases, they swayed like wheat. One elderly tenor simply stopped, mid-verse, and slid sideways off his stall bench, and ushers in livery bore him out through a side door with the brisk tenderness of men handling furniture, and the choir closed ranks around the gap without missing a note.

"That's the fourth this month," Cassian breathed beside her. "I served two years in the palace guard before I ran. I told myself it was ceremony. Pageantry. Old ritual." His knuckles were bone-white on the gallery rail. "I *heard* it every night and called it music."

"It is music," Paloma said. Her voice came out strange, scraped. "That's what makes it monstrous. It's the most beautiful thing I've ever heard, and it's a feeding trough."

Below, the conductor in gold turned his head — slowly, slowly — and looked directly up at the gallery where they hid.

For one heartbeat the chapel held nothing but the dying resonance of the final amen. Then the conductor smiled, a small courteous curve of the lips, and turned back to his choir, and raised his hand for the next piece as if he had seen nothing at all.

But Paloma's palm was burning, and the melody in it had changed key, and she understood, with sick certainty, that they had just been *noticed*.

---

They retreated the way they'd come, through linen-dark and dripping stone, and regrouped in the tannery safehouse with the shutters barred and Odile's unlit lantern between them like a council fire.

"We go in," Paloma said.

Four faces turned to her.

"The choir. We have to get inside it." She spread her hands on the table, and the mark on her palm caught the grey light through the shutter-slats. "Everything we need is in there. The rite needs willing voices — real ones, unbroken ones — and the only people in this city who understand what's being done to them are the people it's being done *to*. Three hundred singers. If even fifty of them could be freed long enough to choose—" She stopped, hearing herself, hearing the ledger-clerk's arithmetic creep back into her voice, counting lives like columns. "If they could be freed at all."

"And how does a debt-indentured scribe walk into the most guarded institution in the palace?" Odile asked.

"As a copyist." It came out before Paloma had decided to say it, the way truth sometimes does. "The choir needs new parts written. Arrangements. The conductor commissions transcriptions — Cassian, you must know that, you served here—"

"He does," Cassian said slowly. "There's a copy-office off the choir cloister. Hired scribes, civilians, brought in under bond of silence. I never thought—" He looked at her with something dawning and terrible in his face. "Paloma. If they test your blood. If anyone looks too closely at your name—"

"Then I'll have gotten further than sitting here." She met his eyes. "You crossed a bridge to buy us time. Let me cross a threshold to spend it."

Wren grabbed her sleeve. "He *saw* you. Today. The gold man looked right at us."

"Yes," Paloma said. "Which means he's curious. Curiosity opens doors." She did not add what else she believed: that the entity wore its vessels' attention like fingers, and that something behind the conductor's folded-paper face wanted her closer, the way a spider wants the fly informed of the web. She was walking into a mouth. She knew it. But every road from here ran through that throat, and she would rather go in singing than be swallowed unknowing.

Sefa spoke from the corner, where she had been silent all evening, turning her mother's chipped bead between her fingers.

"I'll teach you the descant," the girl said. "The one the walls want. If you're going to stand inside the song, you should carry something it can't eat."

Outside, the sun went down over the gilded spires, and the hum in the stones deepened, and somewhere above them three hundred stolen voices rose toward an open mouth. Paloma flexed her marked hand, once, twice, and began — softly, so only the room could hear — to learn the melody that would either save them or unmake them.

The copy-office opened at dawn.
