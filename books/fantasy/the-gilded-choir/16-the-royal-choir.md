## 16. The Royal Choir

The summons came on paper the color of old teeth.

*By order of the Lord Steward: one scribe, competent in musical notation, to serve the Royal Choir for a period of fourteen days. Room and board provided. Compensation waived against outstanding debts.*

Paloma read it three times in the tannery safehouse's guttering light, and each time her eyes snagged on the same word. *Waived.* Not paid — nothing was ever paid, only moved from one ledger column to another, debt shuffling sideways like water finding its level. The crown had learned her name. The crown knew what she wanted. And now it was offering her exactly the door she'd been trying to pick.

"They're inviting you into the wolf's mouth," Odile said from the doorway, arms crossed over her patched coat. Her single eye caught the lamplight like a coin at the bottom of a well. "You understand that."

"I understand they think I'll come quietly because of a line item."

"And will you?"

Paloma folded the letter along its crease, carefully, the way she folded everything — a habit from ten years of making small things fit into small spaces. "Cassian is in there. Somewhere. And Sefa's melody is in there too — Wren heard it in his sleep again last night, pulling toward the palace like a tide." She looked up. "If they're harvesting voices, then every night I don't go, someone else's voice goes into that thing instead."

Odile was quiet a long moment. Outside, the tannery district breathed its chemical stink through the shuttered windows, and somewhere a patrol's boots rang off wet cobbles in the rhythm the city had learned since the purges began: quick, quick, pause. Quick, quick, pause.

"You write your copyist hand," Odile said finally. "You keep your head down. You find out where they're keeping him and how many guards and when the singing happens. And you do *not* do anything brave."

"I never do anything brave."

"Liar," said Odile, without heat, and left her alone with the letter and the dark.

* * *

They processed her at the palace's service gate the way they processed everything — as inventory.

A clerk with ink-stained cuffs checked her hands first, turning them palm-up, examining the callus on her middle finger and the faded burn-scar across her knuckles with the detached interest of a man appraising a horse's teeth. Then her eyes. Then, briefly, her throat, two fingers pressing lightly under her jaw as though feeling for something. Paloma kept her breathing slow and her face arranged into the particular blankness of a woman who had been poor and frightened her whole life and expected nothing better.

"Musical notation?" the clerk asked.

"Temple-trained. Pre-warding notation as well. Neumes, mensural signs, the old modal marks." She recited it flatly, a list memorized from years of copying forbidden texts by candlelight, texts that had taught her more than the temple ever intended. "I can transcribe anything that can be sung."

"Anything that can be sung," the clerk repeated, and wrote something down, and something about the way he wrote it — quickly, not looking at the page — made the hair rise along her arms.

They gave her a grey smock, a room six feet by eight in the servants' warren beneath the east wing, and a schedule. The schedule was the first thing that truly frightened her, though it took her until midnight, lying on a straw pallet listening to the palace settle around her like a great ship, to understand why.

The choir rehearsed at dawn. The choir performed at dusk, in the Hall of Harmonies, before the court. And between those hours — marked in the clerk's neat hand as *Restoration* — the choir was unavailable.

Not resting. *Restored.*

She turned the word over in the dark. In the pre-warding texts, in the marginalia of the founding documents she'd copied for the provost in another life, *restoration* had meant one thing only: putting back what had been taken out.

* * *

The Hall of Harmonies was beautiful the way a wound dressed in silk is beautiful.

Paloma saw it for the first time from the copyists' gallery, a raked wooden shelf behind a screen of gilded lattice, high above the choir stalls. Below her, forty singers stood in perfect rows on a floor of pale stone, and the hall rose around them in arches carved with vines and faces — hundreds of faces, generations of them, their stone mouths open in eternal song. The acoustics were a marvel; she could feel it in her teeth before a single note was sung, the architecture itself shaped like an instrument, like cupped hands, like a trap.

And the singers —

She had grown up among the valley's plain-chant congregations, voices roughened by work and weather, beauty arriving despite itself. These were different. These were voices that had been *refined*, the way ore is refined, all impurity burned away. When the conductor — a thin man with a baton of white bone — lifted his hand, forty throats opened and produced a sound so seamless it barely seemed human. It poured upward into the vaulted dark and did not echo. It was *swallowed*. Paloma watched, her pen frozen above her transcription slate, as the music rose and rose and simply stopped, absorbed somewhere above the highest arch, taken in the way dry ground takes rain.

Nothing came back down.

Her mark — the brand the entity had pressed into her palm in the throne room, the thing she had spent nine days pretending was healing — woke up. It didn't hurt, exactly. It *hummed*, low and satisfied, a tuning fork struck against bone, and beneath the humming she felt something vast lean closer, the way a body leans toward a fire.

*There,* the mark said, in the wordless language of hunger. *More.*

She bent over her slate and copied the notation with a steady hand while her stomach turned to lead. That was the trick of it, she was learning. The hands could be steady even when nothing else was. Ten years of ledger-work had taught her that much: the numbers went in the columns no matter what was happening behind your eyes.

* * *

She found Cassian on the third day.

She hadn't been looking for him — or rather, she had been looking everywhere for him, scanning every grey face in the servants' corridors, every hooded figure hauling water, and finding nothing, and beginning to let the cold thought in, the one that whispered *the interrogation rooms, the river, the lime pits* — and then she walked into the choir's rehearsal room to deliver fresh transcription slates and stopped dead just inside the door.

He stood in the second row. Bass section. His hair had been cut short, prison-short, and he'd lost weight enough that his cheekbones had become architecture, but it was him — spine straight, jaw set, eyes forward with the fixed blankness of a man enduring. He wore the choir's uniform, white and gold, and it hung on him like a shroud on a scarecrow.

He did not look at her. Of course he didn't. He was a prisoner performing compliance, and prisoners who noticed things died noticing things.

But as she moved along the row distributing slates, as her shadow crossed his, his chest expanded — one breath, deep and deliberate, the kind soldiers took before going over walls — and he sang.

It was woven into the rehearsal, seamless, invisible. The conductor had set them a scale exercise, rising fifths, and Cassian's bass carried the pattern perfectly, note after obedient note. But threaded inside the fifth, quiet as a heartbeat under cloth, was a rhythm. Three short, one long. Three short, one long. Repeated, patient, buried so deep in the harmony that only someone who had marched beside him, who knew his habits the way she'd learned them on the road — only someone listening *for* him would ever hear it.

Three short, one long. The signal they'd used crossing the lowlands. *Danger here. Move careful.*

Her hands did not shake. She handed him his slate, and their fingers did not touch, and his eyes stayed fixed on the middle distance, and she walked out of the rehearsal room with her heart hammering so hard she could feel it in her ears, in her wrists, in the scarred palm hidden inside her sleeve.

Alive. He was alive. He was *signaling*.

She made it back to the gallery before her knees informed her they had opinions, and she sat down among the other copyists and pretended to sharpen a pen for a very long time.

* * *

The Restoration happened at midnight, and she was not supposed to see it, and she saw it anyway.

She had learned the servants' warren in two nights the way she learned everything — obsessively, silently, mapping corridors the way she mapped debts. There was a gap in the wall behind the linen stores where the plaster had cracked, and through the crack ran warm air that smelled of candle smoke and, faintly, of something else. Something green and old, like a forest floor after rain, which made no sense in the heart of a stone palace.

On the fourth night, drawn by that smell and by the hum in her palm, which had been growing nightly, insistent as a toothache, she put her eye to the crack.

Below, visible through a seam in the paneling, lay a chamber she had no name for. It was round. Its floor was the pale stone of the Hall of Harmonies continued downward, and carved into that floor were channels — grooves, shallow and branching, running inward from forty alcoves set around the rim. In each alcove stood a chair. In each chair sat a singer from the royal choir, upright, eyes closed, mouths slightly open as if mid-note.

They were not moving. They were not asleep. They were *draining*.

Light moved in the channels — a thin luminescence, silver-gold, like moonlight made liquid — flowing from each seated figure, down the grooves, converging at the chamber's center where a basin of black glass held the gathered light the way a bowl holds water. And above the basin, coiling slowly in the air like smoke that had decided to be a serpent, hung something that was almost a shape. Almost a voice. It drank, and the drinking had a sound, and the sound was the entity's melody — the same phrase Sefa hummed, the same phrase that lived now under Paloma's skin — played at quarter-speed, stretched into something endless and patient and obscene.

One singer in the far alcove swayed. A woman, older, her white gown spotted at the collar. As the light pulled out of her, her mouth worked, shaping words with no breath behind them, and Paloma read her lips through the crack in the wall, and what she read was: *please. Please. Please.*

Nobody answered her. The channels flowed. The serpent-smoke drank.

Paloma stepped back from the wall and was sick into a linen basket, silently, the way she did everything now, and then she cleaned herself up with a servant's rag and went back to her pallet and lay staring at the ceiling until the bells rang dawn.

Fourteen days, the letter had said. Fourteen nights of this. Forty voices, thinned a little more each night, poured into that black glass bowl to feed the thing wearing the king. And somewhere in the palace's arithmetic, she understood suddenly and completely, was the final sum: the steward wasn't maintaining the entity. He was *fattening* it. Every harvested voice was a coin in a treasury, and treasuries existed to be spent.

* * *

"I'm getting him out."

She said it to Odile and Wren on the sixth day, in the safehouse, having smuggled herself out through the coal delivery — a hole in the schedule the size of an hour, bought with a bribe she'd have to repay in copying. Wren looked up from mending a strap, his thin face brightening, and Odile looked at her the way she looked at floodwater.

"Out how?"

"I don't know yet. But I know where. Second row, bass section. He's unharmed. He's—" Her voice caught, and she hated it, and pushed through. "He signaled me. He's been signaling since the first day. He knows I'm there, Odile. He's been waiting for me to see him."

"That's either hope or a trap."

"It's Cassian," said Wren, with the absolute authority of a twelve-year-old, and Odile closed her eye as if asking patience of some god who had stopped taking her calls.

"Say I believe you," she said. "Say we get him out of a locked choir in the middle of a palace full of hunters who already know your face. Then what? The entity feels everything in that building, girl. You told us yourself — the mark hums when the singing starts. You think you can move a prisoner through those halls without it noticing? It's not a guard problem. It's not even a magic problem. It's—" She searched for the word and found it and hated it. "It's a *listening* problem. The whole palace is an ear."

Paloma had thought about this. She had thought about little else for six days, through rehearsals and transcriptions and midnight horrors, turning the problem like a stone in her hands until her fingers bled on its edges.

"Not the whole palace," she said. "When the choir sings, the entity *listens* — really listens, the way you'd watch a meal being carried to your table. Everything else goes quiet inside it. Greedy things are blind to everything except the eating." She held up her marked palm. "And I know exactly when the singing starts. I can feel it before it starts. If we move during the performance — during the Harvest, when every guard's attention is on the court and every scrap of that thing's attention is on the voices — there's a window. Maybe four minutes. Maybe less."

"Maybe," Odile repeated.

"Maybe is what we have."

Wren had gone still, the strap forgotten in his lap. "The singers," he said quietly. "The forty. If you take Cassian during the singing, the others are still in their chairs afterward. Still being—" He couldn't say it. He looked at Paloma. "We can't just take one."

The silence stretched. Down in the street, boots passed: quick, quick, pause. Quick, quick, pause.

"No," Paloma said. "We can't. And we can't take forty, not with four minutes and three of us. So we take Cassian, and we take what he knows, and we come back for the rest with enough force to matter — the rebels, the lowland cells, everyone Odile can raise. This isn't the rescue, Wren. This is the reconnaissance." She heard how it sounded — ledger-language, debt-language, the cold arithmetic she'd sworn she'd escaped — and made herself say the rest plainly. "People get left behind tonight. I hate it. I'll carry it. But if we try to save everyone with what we have, we save no one, and the steward finishes whatever he's building in that black bowl."

Wren's jaw worked. He was learning, she thought, the terrible curriculum of adulthood — not how to be brave, but how to survive being brave and insufficient. "Sefa could help," he said at last. "Her song. She gets *inside* things. She got inside the chapel walls without touching them."

"Sefa stays hidden," Odile said instantly. "That child is the single most hunted person in this kingdom, and you want to bring her to the palace gates—"

"She wouldn't come to the gates. She'd—"

"*No.*"

They argued it until the light failed, and settled nothing, and Paloma walked back to the coal delivery with the argument still ringing in her ears and the plan already revising itself behind her eyes, because Wren was right, and Odile was right, and both truths could not fit in the same plan, and that was what plans were: places where impossible things were stacked carefully and hoped not to collapse.

* * *

On the eighth evening, everything changed.

She was in the gallery, copying, when the doors at the far end of the Hall of Harmonies opened and the court entered — and behind the court, flanked by guards, walking with the slow ceremonial gait of a king but the wrongness of a puppet whose strings had been tuned by something patient — came Aldric.

The entity wore him well these days. Better than it had in the throne room, weeks ago. The hollow places behind his eyes had filled with a borrowed warmth, a practiced humanity, and the court bowed and smiled and saw nothing, because people see what a crown tells them to see. But Paloma's mark saw. Her mark *screamed*, low and continuous, a dog pressed whimpering to the floor, and through the scream she heard it — the melody, the true one, vast and slow, turning over beneath the king's ribs like a leviathan under a boat.

Aldric paused below the gallery. His head tilted. Very slightly. The way a listener tilts.

And then, in the silence before the downbeat, the King of the Valley looked up — directly up, through the gilded lattice, into the one pair of eyes in the hall that could see him — and smiled.

It was not Aldric's smile. It was not any living person's smile. It was the smile of something that had just found a familiar scent on the wind, something it had been promised, something bred for it across nine hundred years and eleven generations, sitting obediently in the copyists' gallery with a slate in her hands and a brand on her palm.

His lips moved. No sound came out. None needed to.

*Soon,* his mouth said.

The conductor raised his baton. The choir sang. The light rose and was swallowed, and Paloma copied notes with a steady hand while her blood turned to ice water, because the window she had counted on — the blindness of the feeding, the greedy deafness of the thing below — had just closed like an eye.

It knew she was there. It had maybe always known.

Which meant the rescue was no longer a question of whether. It was a question of whether she could reach Cassian before *soon* arrived.
