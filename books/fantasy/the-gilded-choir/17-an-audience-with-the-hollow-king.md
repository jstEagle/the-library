## 17. An Audience with the Hollow King

They came for her at the hour when the palace lamps were trimmed, two guards in livery so fine it looked poured rather than sewn, and Paloma understood, watching them wait with the patience of furniture, that her refusal had never once been a possibility. The steward's summons had arrived on vellum the color of old teeth, sealed in black wax that smelled faintly of cold iron, and now she was being walked through corridors that seemed to have been designed by someone who hated both light and corners.

The palace of Aldric's line was not beautiful. She had expected gilding — everyone expected gilding; the capital's very name meant *gold-keeper* in the old tongue she'd transcribed for the provost — but what she found instead was weight. Everything here was heavy. Doors thick as tomb lids. Carpets that swallowed footsteps whole. Windows of glass so old and so thick that the daylight coming through them arrived already tired, stained the color of weak tea and honey and bruise. Even the air had heft to it, scented with something sweet and wrong underneath — beeswax and lilies laid over something older, something like the inside of a sealed room.

*Like a temple,* she thought, and then corrected herself: *like a crypt pretending to be a temple.*

The guards did not speak. Neither did she. Her scribe's habit kept her cataloguing even as fear climbed her throat: *corridor, forty paces, torch brackets unlit though the wall soot says they burned within the year; mosaic floor, geometric, pre-warding style — I've seen this pattern in the forbidden folios, it's a binding diagram, they are walking me down the spine of a binding diagram.* That last realization landed somewhere below her ribs and stayed there, humming.

At the end of the corridor stood doors banded in bronze, and before the doors, waiting alone, was the steward.

He was smaller than Paloma remembered from the debt-hearing, a dry stick of a man in grey silk, with hands folded in front of him like a man attending his own funeral. His eyes were the worst part. Not cruel — *patient*. Eyes that had watched things finish.

"Thackeray," he said, and her family name in his mouth sounded like an inventory item being confirmed. "You'll want to know the rules. There is only one. When the king speaks, listen as though your life depends on hearing him correctly." A pause, thin as paper. "It does."

He pushed the doors open himself.

* * *

The throne room was vast and nearly empty, which somehow made it worse. Vast rooms full of courtiers could be read like ledgers — who stood where, who owed whom, where the exits were. This room offered nothing to read. The ceiling arched up into gloom hung with banners so faded their sigils had become suggestions. The floor stretched away in pale stone worn into a shallow valley down its center, as if a river of people had flowed toward the throne for centuries and the stone remembered.

And there, at the far end, beneath a canopy worked with thread-of-gold stars — *stars*, she thought, *they gave him stars because the real ones were taken away* — sat King Aldric.

She had seen him at court, three days after her arrival, from the gallery among the clerks. Then he had been a figure at a distance, robed in white and gold, speaking judgments in a voice that carried without effort. Now, close, she saw what the distance had hidden.

He was not old. That was the first horror. He should have been — the portraits in the archive showed a man crowned forty years past, and men aged — but his face had the smoothness of something preserved rather than young, skin like wax left too near a flame. His hands lay on the arms of the throne, and they did not move at all. Not the small movements living hands make. Nothing.

Then he turned his head toward her, and his eyes opened, and Paloma felt the floor tilt under her certainty.

Because behind those eyes — behind them, deeper than them, looking *through* them the way one looks through a window at night when the lamp inside is lit — was something else. Something that regarded her with an attention so total it felt physical, like standing in front of an open oven door. And beneath her breastbone, in the place where her magic-sense lived, the thing she'd been trying not to name since the night the stars went out, she heard it.

Singing.

Not aloud. The king's mouth stayed closed. But the song was there all the same, threading up through the stone, through the soles of her shoes, through the marrow of her — a melody with no beginning, patient as water finding the low ground. It was beautiful. That was the trap of it. It was the most beautiful thing she had ever heard, and every note of it was hunger.

"Paloma Thackeray."

The voice came from the king's mouth, and it was wrong in a way she couldn't immediately parse until she did: it was warm. Human voices have edges, weariness, weather. This voice had none. It was warmth itself, the memory of every kind voice she'd ever been shown by someone about to take something from her.

"You are wondering how I know your name." The king's lips barely moved. "I knew your grandmother's name. And hers. I have been listening to your family for four hundred years, little scribe. You are the first of your line to stand in this room while I am awake enough to enjoy it."

Paloma's knees wanted to fold. She locked them and made her voice work. "Your Majesty."

A sound from the throne that might have been laughter, if laughter were played backwards. "Majesty. Yes. They do love that word. Say what you actually came to say, or rather, let us skip to what you will say, since I have read you already. You want to know why you are here. You want to know what I am. And underneath both, quiet as a mouse under floorboards, you want to know whether any of this can be survived." The wax-smooth face inclined. "Ask. I am feeling generous tonight. It has been — some time — since anyone stood in front of me who could hear the song properly. Your blood hears it the way wells hear rain."

"The wards," Paloma said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "Did you break them?"

"I did not break them. I *ended* them. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole of your history." The entity leaned forward — the body leaned, the thing behind it merely attended more closely. "Your ancestors built a cage around a valley and called it protection, and they wrote the lock in their own blood so no one else could turn it, and then they died, generation upon generation, keeping the secret so well that they forgot it themselves. Four hundred years of jailers' children playing at being safe. I simply stopped knocking."

"And the stars?"

"The stars were the bars' shadows on the sky. Remove the cage, remove the shadow." It tilted the preserved head. "You mourned them. I watched you mourn them. Do you know what I thought? I thought: *there. That grief. That is the grief of a bird born in a cage, mourning the door.*"

"Stop—" Paloma pressed her palm flat against her sternum, against the song vibrating there. "Stop singing at me."

"I am not singing *at* you. I am singing, and you are able to hear. Those are different crimes, and only one of them is mine." The entity settled back, and its attention withdrew a fraction, and Paloma realized she was shaking, and made herself stop, deliberately, muscle by muscle, the way she straightened a crooked ledger line. "But you did not come for a lecture on cosmology. You came because the steward told you what your blood is for. Renewal of the binding. Or its ending. Your hand turns the lock either way. So let us speak of terms, Thackeray, because I am going to make you an offer, and I would like you to hear it clearly, without the noise of terror drowning out the fine print."

* * *

What followed, Paloma would spend the rest of her life failing to describe accurately.

The entity did not move from the throne. The king's body did not rise. But the room changed — or her perception of it did — and suddenly she was not standing in the throne room at all. She was standing in the Temple of Quiet Hours, in the scriptorium, at her own desk, with the afternoon light falling exactly as it always fell, and the smell of ink and dust and Wren's terrible herbal tea, and her own handwriting marching across a page in front of her.

Her heart broke a little just looking at it.

"This is not real," she said aloud, to the air, to herself, to whatever was listening.

"No," agreed the voice, everywhere now, gentle as a hand on her hair. "It is better than real. It is real as you remember it, and memory flatters everything. Look at the ledger, Paloma."

She looked. She couldn't not. Her own indenture lay open on the desk, the columns of her debt tallied in her own careful hand — and as she watched, the numbers began to fade, the ink lifting off the page like mist off morning water, until the ledger was blank, and then the ledger was gone, and her hands were empty, and they were *light*. Light in a way she had not felt since she was nine years old, before her father died and the debt passed to her like an inheritance of stones.

"Do you know what I can offer?" the entity said, and now it was beside her, wearing the king's body but moving through her memory like it owned the deed. "Not power. Power is for kings and fools, and I have been both. I offer subtraction. I can take the debt out of you — not paid, not forgiven, *unwritten*, as though it never existed. I can take the fear. Every night you woke counting what you owed — gone. Every time you flinched at a soldier's boot heels — gone. I can reach further back, if you like. Your father's death. The look on his face when he signed you over. The nine-year-old who learned that love comes with interest." The voice softened, and horribly, it sounded sincere. "I can give you back the girl who was never broken. She still exists, in the mathematics of what might have been. I can make her the only version of you there ever was."

Paloma stood in the false scriptorium and wept without deciding to. It was the offer's precision that undid her — not grandeur, not a crown, but the exact shape of her wounds, held up in healing light. Whoever this thing was, it had studied her the way she studied texts: closely, patiently, line by line.

"And the price," she managed.

"Ah. Yes. There is always a price; your people taught the world that lesson and then wrote it in blood so they'd remember." The entity moved to the window of the false temple, and beyond the glass the valley lay green and whole, wards invisible, stars — impossible, merciful, lying — scattered across a dusk sky. "Complete the binding. Renew it as your ancestors wrote it, in your blood, with your hand. Make the cage strong again. Bring the valley its stars back, bring the people their safety, bring yourself your peace. In exchange, I ask only what I have always asked: to remain. Comfortably. Quietly. Behind a strong lock, in a strong cage, with my music to keep me company." The king's head turned, and the eyes were kind, and that kindness was the most frightening thing in the world. "You see? I am not asking you to hurt anyone. I am asking you to build a prison — which your family did for four hundred years without losing a night's sleep. All I add is this: afterward, you will not remember building it. You will simply wake in the valley, debtless, whole, and the stars overhead, and no memory of me at all. No mark. No wound. No choice, even. Just peace."

*Peace without knowledge,* Paloma thought. *A cage with me inside it too.*

And some traitorous part of her — the exhausted part, the part that had been tallying debts since childhood, the part that missed her father with a pain that had become structural, load-bearing — that part whispered: *take it, take it, you could be happy, you could be the girl before—*

She thought of Wren. Of his thin shoulders and his stubborn chin and the way he'd sworn he saw a star flicker out, and how she'd believed him when no adult had. If she took the offer, Wren would be a stranger to her. Every hard-won thing between them — erased, unwritten, subtracted along with the pain. The entity had offered to remove her suffering, and it had quietly included in the removal every person she'd suffered *for*.

"No," Paloma said.

The false scriptorium shivered like a struck bell.

"No," she said again, louder, and reached into the deepest pocket of herself, the one where she kept the things that were true. "You're offering to erase my debt. But my debt is the only reason I ever learned to read a ledger honestly. You're offering to erase my father's death — and his death is the reason I know what signing away a child looks like, and I will never un-know it, because knowing it is how I keep other children from being signed away. You're offering me the girl I might have been. But she wouldn't have Wren. She wouldn't have—" Cassian's wary eyes, Odile's one-eyed glare, Sefa's impossible humming — "she wouldn't have *them*. You're not offering me my life back. You're offering me a smaller one, and calling it peace."

The entity was silent for a long moment. When it spoke again, the warmth had cooled by a single degree, and that single degree was terrifying.

"Four hundred years," it said, "and none of your line ever refused me twice."

"I'm not refusing twice," Paloma said. "I'm refusing *once*, completely."

The scriptorium tore away like wet paper.

* * *

She came back to herself standing in the throne room, in the same spot, the same tired light through the ancient glass, the king on his throne exactly as before. Only minutes could have passed. Her cheeks were wet. Her legs held.

The entity regarded her through Aldric's eyes, and now it did not bother with warmth.

"So," it said. "The steward will be disappointed. He had such hopes for the soft approach." The preserved face smiled, and the smile did not reach anything behind it, because there was nothing behind it to reach. "Very well, little scribe. You have heard my offer and declined it. Know this, then, as payment for your courage: I do not need your consent. Consent is a courtesy I extend to your bloodline because your bloodline wrote the lock, and locks written in blood answer better to willing hands. But unwilling hands can turn a lock too. It simply takes longer, and it is louder, and I have been patient for four hundred years." The king rose from the throne — the body moved stiffly, joints like hinges unused — and descended the three steps, and stopped before her, close enough that she could see that his pupils reflected nothing, no lamp, no window, no her. "You will renew the binding or you will watch me take the binding from others. There are singers in my choir whose throats I have kept for decades. Their voices are mine. Their lives are mine. And soon, child, I will need so many more of them."

He extended one preserved hand, palm up, and in the center of the royal palm a mark bloomed — a circle of interlocking lines, black as burnt ink — and then it faded, and appeared instead on *her* palm, rising on her skin like a bruise surfacing, burning as it came, a ring of interlocking lines seared into the flesh of her right hand.

Paloma cried out. The pain was white and total, and then it ebbed to a deep burn, and when she looked at her palm the mark remained, neat as a scribe's finest penwork, in her own skin, in the exact style of the diagrams she'd spent her life copying.

"A receipt," the entity said. "So that you remember our transaction. You refused the gift; you keep the bill. And more than a receipt — a key. Through it, you will hear me wherever I sing. In the palace. In the city. In the valley, when I return to it. In the throat of anyone I choose to borrow." The hollow king bent slightly, courteously, a host seeing a guest to the door. "Listen carefully, Thackeray. You may learn something useful. My enemies usually do — briefly."

The guards took her arms. As they turned her toward the doors, she looked back once at the throne, at the man-shaped thing sitting down again with the slow mechanical grace of a puppet returned to its hook.

Behind the king's eyes, the ancient attention was already elsewhere. Already listening. Already, she understood with a chill that outlasted the burn in her hand, *singing to someone else*.

And through the fresh brand in her palm, faint and far away and horribly familiar, Paloma heard it: a small, uncertain tenor, humming a melody no child should know.

Wren.
