## 18. Refusal

The corridor behind the throne room was long and gold and wrong.

Paloma had walked palace corridors before — in ledgers, in inventories, in the dry columns of supply lists that crossed her desk at the Temple of Quiet Hours: *two hundred candles for the west gallery, forty yards of velvet for the king's bedchamber.* She had imagined them as places where numbers became objects. She had not imagined them as throats.

That was what they were. That was what she understood now, walking between two guards whose gauntlets bit her arms, with the brand still cooling in her palm like a swallowed coal. The corridors were throats. The whole palace was a gullet, gilded and gracious, and everything in it — the tapestries, the courtiers, the choir rehearsing somewhere below in voices too smooth to be entirely human — was being swallowed a little at a time.

And she had said no.

She turned the word over as the guards marched her down, testing it the way she tested a transcription against its original, looking for the flaw, the place where she must have copied something incorrectly. *No.* She had said it to a thing older than the valley's walls. It had worn nine hundred years of kings like coats, and it had offered to wipe her clean — debt, past, pain, all of it, gone like ink blotted from a page — and she had stood on the mosaic floor with her heart hammering out a scribe's arithmetic of survival and said *no*.

Barely. That was the honest accounting. Barely. There had been a moment — she could locate it precisely, the way she could locate any error in a ledger, third line from the bottom — when the offer had felt less like temptation than like rest. Like setting down a ledger she had carried since childhood. Like being told the debt was never hers at all, and here, look, we've torn up the page.

Then she had thought of Wren's face when he described the star going out. Not frightened. *Attentive.* A boy who looked up instead of down, who noticed the sky losing a single light among thousands, who cared about things too far away to matter to his supper. If she took the entity's bargain, she would never look up again. She would be a kept thing in a walled valley, safe as a pressed flower, and she would call it peace until she died.

So: *no*. Barely. And now the burn in her hand, and the song in her head, and Wren's small tenor threading through both of them like a needle through cloth.

"Where are you taking me?" she asked the guard on her left. He didn't answer. Neither did the other. They had the flat courtesy of men who had been instructed not to hear certain questions, and Paloma recognized the instruction because she had lived under it her whole life. The temple had its own silences, its own questions scribes learned not to ask. The valley had simply been smaller about it.

The brand pulsed. Not painfully — worse than painfully, *invitingly*, a warmth that wanted to be leaned into, the way an open ledger wants to be written in. And beneath the warmth, the song.

It was everywhere. That was the first true horror of the mark, and it arrived not as a revelation but as an accumulation, the way dread does. In the first corridor there had been only the distant choir below, their harvested voices rising through the floors like smoke. By the second corridor she could hear under it a lower line, vast and patient, the entity's own melody moving through the palace's stones the way water moves through the foundations of a house — invisible, structural, taken for granted by everyone who lived there. By the third corridor she could hear it in the guards' footsteps. In her own heartbeat, almost. Almost.

*This is what it offered me,* she thought. *Not silence. Company.*

Her hand throbbed. She made herself look at it when the guards paused at a landing to wait for some signal — made herself uncurl the fingers she'd been holding clenched. The mark sat in the center of her palm like a brand from a seal-ring, but it was not any sigil she knew from the temple's books, and it was not the crown's. It was a spiral of fine lines, dense as script, and when she tilted it toward the lamplight the lines seemed to move the way grain moves in watered silk. Writing. It might be writing. Somewhere in the forbidden texts she had transcribed for the provost — in the pre-warding fragments, the founding records, the pages about the Choir — there might be a key to these characters, if she lived long enough to find it.

If. Her favorite word lately. It did more work than any other word in her vocabulary.

The guards resumed walking. Paloma closed her fist over the mark and let the darkness have it, and listened instead for the one thread of song she could bear: high, uncertain, a child's hum threading through the palace's vast harmony like a single bright stitch in dark cloth.

Wren. Somewhere in this throat of a city, Wren was singing the entity's melody without knowing what it was, and the mark carried him to her as clearly as a name called across a courtyard.

She followed the sound the way a drowning woman follows light.

* * *

They did not take her to a cell. They took her to a room.

That was somehow more frightening. Cells were honest; cells said *you are held*. The room said *you are expected*, which was a different sentence entirely. It was on the fourth floor of a guest wing, comfortably appointed, with a fire already lit and a covered dish on the table that smelled of roasted fowl and rosemary — a meal better than any she had eaten in six years of indenture. On the writing desk, squared neatly beside an inkwell and a fresh quill, lay a stack of blank paper and, on top of it, a familiar folio: the binding fragment, the stolen rite, returned to her like a borrowed book.

*Your work continues,* the room said. *You are a scribe. Scribes write. We have been generous.*

Paloma stood in the doorway a long time, listening. The guards had withdrawn — she heard their boots recede down the corridor, unhurried, unafraid of her escape, which was its own kind of message. Through the mark, the palace sang its patient architecture of hunger. Below it, fainter, the royal choir rehearsed. And threaded through everything, that small tenor, closer now or perhaps only clearer, humming a phrase that made her teeth ache.

She went to the window. The capital spread below her in a haze of lamplight and chimney smoke, enormous, indifferent, alive in ways the valley had never permitted itself to be. Beyond the city walls, beyond the lit ring of the streets, the land fell away into genuine darkness — the lowlands, the rebel country, the burned villages Cassian had spoken of in his flat deserter's voice. Somewhere out there was a road. Somewhere out there were people who hated the crown enough to hide a runaway scribe.

Somewhere out there, presumably, was Wren — except the mark said otherwise. The mark said the boy's humming came from inside the palace grounds, from the direction of the servants' courts, and it was getting no farther away.

He hadn't run. Or he had run, and run *here*, following the same thread she was following, because he could hear the song too — he had always been able to hear it, that was the terrible gift the soldiers had come to the archives for, and he had heard it calling and gone toward it the way children go toward rivers.

Paloma pressed her branded palm flat against the cold window glass. The spiral flared, and for one vertiginous instant the whole city's song resolved into something almost legible — thousands of small human noises, footsteps and prayers and arguments and lullabies, all of it underlaid by the great slow melody, and woven into the melody, distinct as a signature, three threads she knew: the hollow king on his throne, the steward moving through his rounds below, and Wren, asleep or pretending to sleep, humming in his dreams.

Three threads. And a fourth, faint and fraying, somewhere in the city's tannery quarter: a bass voice she knew, roughened by interrogation, still stubbornly, stupidly alive.

Cassian.

She took her hand off the glass. The song dimmed to its ordinary murmur, and she found she was shaking.

"All right," she said aloud, to the room, to the paper, to whatever patient attention was listening through the walls. "All right. You want a scribe."

She sat down at the desk. She pulled the binding fragment toward her. And she began, deliberately, methodically, to do the only thing she had ever been good at: she read.

* * *

The fragment was worse the second time. Everything was worse the second time; that was the nature of reading, that understanding was just the accumulation of things you couldn't unknow.

She had studied it in the temple under the provost's eye, hunting for the mechanics of renewal — the clauses, the conditions, the price. Now she read it as what it actually was: a contract, drafted by her ancestors, and contracts were written by whoever held the pen. Every clause bent toward the drafter. The binding required willing voices, yes — the chapel walls had said so, and the fragment confirmed it. But the fragment also specified *whose* willingness counted, and how it was to be obtained, and the obtaining was written in language so old and so careful that its cruelty took a moment to arrive, the way deep cold does.

*A life freely given,* the text said, in the old formula. And then, in the commentary gloss beneath, in a later hand, in ink she recognized with a sick lurch as her own family's characteristic slant: *freely given may be cultivated. Necessity is the surest gardener.*

Necessity is the surest gardener. Generations of Thackerays, bred and indebted and funneled toward this exact desk, this exact choice — and the ones who set it up had written themselves a note about gardening.

Paloma put down the quill. Her hand — the unbranded one — was steady. That surprised her. She had expected rage to shake her; instead it settled over her like a coat, heavy and warm and clarifying. Rage, she discovered, was a form of literacy. She could read the whole document now, every clause, every gloss, every generation of quiet complicity, and she could read the room around it too: the fire laid for her comfort, the meal under its cover, the blank paper waiting for her compliance.

They thought she would cooperate because she always had. Because debt was obedience wearing a ledger's clothes, and she had worn the clothes her whole life.

Through the mark, faintly, the small tenor shifted in its sleep, and hummed a new phrase — and Paloma went cold, because the phrase was not from the entity's melody. It was from the descant. The hidden one, the counter-melody she had found buried in the pre-warding texts and copied out in secret, the one line in all the valley's music that moved *against* the binding rather than within it. She had never sung it aloud. She had barely dared hum it alone. And Wren, three floors and two courtyards away, was dreaming it note-perfect.

Either the boy had found her hidden copy. Or the song itself was teaching him resistance, the way a body raises a fever.

She didn't know which possibility frightened her more. She suspected she would find out.

Paloma looked at the blank paper for a long moment. Then she dipped the quill, and wrote — not the renewal, not the translation the steward wanted, but a letter, small and coded in the mercantile shorthand every temple scribe learned and no soldier could read, addressed to a name she had seen in Cassian's effects: a contact in the tannery district. *The bird is caged but the cage has windows,* she wrote. *The little singer is inside the walls. Come singing.*

She folded it small, and waited for the fire to burn low, and did what scribes have always done with the things power must not see: she hid the truth in plain sight, stitched into the hem of the ordinary.

Then she lay down on the too-comfortable bed, closed her eyes, and let the mark carry her outward, thread by thread, through the palace's golden throat — past the king's hollow dreaming, past the steward's wakeful scheming, down and out along the frayed bass line of a captured deserter, and home, always home, to the small bright tenor humming defiance in its sleep.

Outside the window, far to the south, clouds were gathering over the lowlands. The mark heard them coming before she did: a pressure in the song, a dissonance gathering at the edges of the city's harmony like a storm front made of sound.

Something was coming for the palace. Something that sang.

And in the morning, when the bells rang and the guards opened her door with breakfast, Paloma Thackeray smiled her best debtor's smile, and asked — politely, reasonably, in the tone of a woman who had finally accepted the inevitable — whether she might be permitted to inspect the royal choir's archives.

"After all," she said, flexing her branded hand where they could see it, watching the spiral glint. "I'm told I'm to renew the binding. I'll need to hear the voices I'm working with."

The guards looked at each other. Somewhere below, the choir began to sing.

And through the mark, clear across the city, a small voice answered — Wren, awake now, humming the descant like a key fitted to a lock, waiting for someone to turn it.
