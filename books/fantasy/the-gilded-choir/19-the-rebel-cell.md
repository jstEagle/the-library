## 19. The Rebel Cell

Wren had learned to count in the temple, but not with numbers.

He counted the way the provost's clerks counted debts — by what could be taken away. One mother, gone. One valley, gone. One sky full of stars, gone and not come back. He kept the tally in his chest where nobody could read it, and he added to it now as they came down into the tannery district through streets that smelled like a wound: hides rotting in lime pits, tallow rendered thick enough to chew, smoke from a hundred curing-fires pressing down under a sky the color of an old bruise.

The city above the district was gilded. The city here was stained to the elbows.

"Stay close," Cassian said, for the fourth time since dawn. He wore a dyer's smock over his soldier's shoulders, and it made him look wrong, like a sword wrapped in a flour sack. "The contact is a woman called Maret. She keeps the counting-house on Tanner's Row. You say nothing. Paloma does the talking."

"I know," Wren said.

"You always say you know."

"Because I always do."

Cassian looked at him then — that long, measuring look he gave everyone, the one that weighed a person like a butcher weighing meat — and whatever he found in Wren's face seemed to satisfy him, or at least to be unfixable before noon. He turned back to the street.

Odile walked behind them all, her single eye moving over the rooftops with the patience of a woman who had spent her life reading water and now had to read stone. Sefa walked beside Wren, holding the hem of his sleeve between two fingers, the way she did when the streets got loud. She hadn't spoken since the lowlands. She didn't need to. When she was frightened, the air around her went thin and bright, like the moment before a bell is struck, and Wren had learned to feel it against his skin.

Right now the air around Sefa was very thin indeed.

* * *

The counting-house was a narrow building squeezed between two tanneries, its windows fogged with salt and fat. Inside, ledgers rose in towers along every wall — the rebels kept accounts too, Wren realized. Debts of grain, debts of boots, debts of names. Everyone in the world was keeping a ledger. It was only ever a question of what you were willing to owe.

Maret was small and gray and quick, with ink stains to her wrists and eyes that flicked to each of them once and filed them away forever. She listened to Paloma explain — the chapel walls, the binding rite, the child who sang without being consumed — with her hands flat on the table, absolutely still, the way people go still when they are deciding something enormous.

"You're telling me," she said at last, "that the king is hollow. That there's a thing inside him older than the wards. And that this girl—" her chin tipped toward Sefa, not unkindly, but not gently either, "—can carry its song without drowning in it."

"Yes," Paloma said.

"And you brought her into my district."

"She has nowhere else to be."

"No." Maret exhaled through her nose. "Nobody does. That's rather the point of everything." She drummed her fingers once, twice. "There are forty-one of us on Tanner's Row. Forty-one names in my ledger. I don't spend them lightly. But if half of what you say is true, then the crown isn't hunting rebels anymore. It's hunting *her*. And that means every hour she stands in my district, my forty-one names get heavier." She looked at Sefa again, longer this time. "Can you control it? The singing?"

Sefa shook her head.

"Does it happen when you sleep?"

A pause. Then, smaller: "Sometimes."

Maret closed her eyes briefly, the way Odile did when someone said something true and terrible. "Then we keep her below ground tonight, and we move her out with the dye-carts at first light. There's a cellarsman two streets over who owes me. Nobody sings above ground. Nobody hums. Nobody so much as whistles. Am I understood?"

They understood. They were shown to a back room smelling of oak gall and old wool, given bread and dripping, told to wait for dark. And Wren sat with his shoulder against Sefa's and thought: *this is what safe feels like.* Walls, and bread, and people who have decided, for one night, that you are worth the risk of their names.

He should have known better. He'd been raised by a ledger. Nothing in a ledger stays balanced for long.

* * *

It happened in the hour before dawn, which Wren would later understand was not bad luck but arithmetic. The patrols changed shift then; the streets were emptiest of witnesses and fullest of soldiers. If you wanted to take a district quietly, that was the hour you chose.

He woke because the silence changed.

Not broke — *changed*, the way water changes just before it freezes. Sefa was sitting up in the dark beside him, her eyes open, her lips parted, and the sound coming out of her was barely sound at all: a thread of melody, high and sweet and wrong, winding up through the floorboards toward the street like smoke finding a chimney. Her face in the dimness was peaceful. That was the worst part. She looked like Wren felt on the rare nights he dreamed about his mother.

"Sefa." He touched her shoulder. "Sefa, wake up—"

Her eyes focused on him slowly, from very far away. The song thinned, wavered—

And outside, iron rang on iron. A whistle. Then another, answering from the east. Then a third from the west, closing.

Boots. So many boots.

The door burst inward before Cassian even reached it. Wren had seen soldiers before — from the far side of a border, from the safe side of a story — but he had never seen them *arrive*, the way floodwater arrives, filling a room from the doorway up. Gray coats. Drawn steel. Lantern-light swinging wild across the ledger towers until the whole room seemed to be made of falling shadows.

"Down! Down, everyone down—"

What happened next took less time than it takes to tell. Maret's people came up from the cellar stairs with hammers and boat-hooks and the desperate courage of people whose names were already in someone else's book. There was shouting — Maret's voice cracking on a name Wren never learned — and the splinter-crack of a doorframe, and Odile's arm across Wren's chest, iron-hard, hauling him backward through the rear window into the reeking alley while the room he'd slept in filled with gray coats and screaming.

Paloma was already outside. Cassian came last, bleeding from a cut over his eye, his face set in the expression Wren would learn to dread: the one that meant he was counting costs and finding the sum unbearable.

They ran. Behind them, the tannery district lit up lantern by lantern, and the whistles kept coming, and somewhere in the middle of it all Wren heard — or thought he heard — Maret's voice stop.

By full light they were crouched in a drainage culvert beyond the district wall, four of them where there should have been six, because Maret and three of her people had been taken, and the rest had scattered into the city like spilled ink.

Nobody spoke for a long time. The culvert dripped. Somewhere above, carts rolled past, ordinary as morning.

Then Odile said, flat as a ferry plank: "She sang."

Everyone looked at Sefa.

She was pressed into the culvert's curve, arms around her knees, making herself as small as a child can make herself, which is very small indeed. Her eyes were enormous. She knew. Of course she knew. Children always know exactly which way the wind of blame is blowing; they've been reading it since before they could walk.

"It wasn't her fault," Wren said.

"The patrols came to *her*," Odile said. Not cruelly. That was the horrible thing — she wasn't being cruel. She was being accurate, the way weather is accurate. "I've watched that song pull at her since the lowlands. Last night it pulled hard enough to bring half the garrison down on a house full of sleeping people. Four names in Maret's ledger, girl. Maybe five."

"She was asleep!"

"Asleep is where it happens. That doesn't make it safe. That makes it worse — because you can't guard a person against her own dreams."

"She's eight years old!"

"I buried children younger than that in the lowlands." Odile's voice didn't rise at all. "I burned them because there wasn't ground soft enough to dig. Don't talk to me about how old she is. Old enough is a thing the world decides, not us."

"That's not fair—"

"No," Odile agreed. "It isn't. Fair left this country a long time ago. We're just living in what's left."

"Stop." Paloma's voice, quiet and cracked and final. She had a hand pressed over her eyes. When she lowered it, Wren saw she was crying without any drama at all, tears simply present, like rain. "Both of you. Stop." She crouched in front of Sefa. "Look at me, little bird. Did you mean to sing?"

Sefa shook her head, a tiny, jerking motion.

"Do you want the soldiers to find your friends?"

Another shake. Faster. A sob caught sideways in her throat.

"Then that's the whole of it," Paloma said, glaring up at Odile with a ferocity Wren had never seen in her. "She is not a weapon that misfired. She is a child who was *chained in a waystation* by people who feared exactly what you're fearing right now, and if we start looking at her the way they looked at her, then we've become the crown with better manners, and I will not — I will *not* —"

Her voice broke. Cassian put a hand on her shoulder. For once he didn't say anything sensible.

Odile held Paloma's gaze for a long moment. Then she looked away, out at the grey drip of the culvert mouth, and her jaw worked once, twice, like a woman swallowing something that would not go down.

"I didn't say give her to them," she said finally. "I said the truth out loud, because somebody in this ditch has to. You want to pretend she's just a little girl who likes songs. Fine. Pretend. But the entity knows her voice now. And after tonight, so does the steward." She stood, joints cracking, and picked up her bundle. "We need new shelter. And we need it before dark, and we need it *away* from anywhere she sleeps near other people. That's not cruelty. That's the price of keeping her. Somebody has to pay it."

She climbed out of the culvert and was gone into the morning mist.

* * *

They walked most of the day, taking the long ways, the hidden ways, Cassian leading them through a city he clearly hated with the intimacy of an old soldier. Nobody talked much. Sefa stayed glued to Wren's side, and he kept his sleeve within her reach and said nothing about any of it, because there was nothing to say that the walking didn't say better.

But the splinter was in the group now, and Wren could feel it working. It was in the way Odile scouted ahead alone instead of calling Cassian to share the watch. It was in the way Paloma kept touching Sefa's hair, too often, the way you touch a thing you're afraid of losing. It was in the way Cassian's eyes went distant whenever Sefa hummed — even humming a *nonsense* song, a skipping song, a song about a duck — and Wren saw him doing sums behind his face. Costs. Names. Ledgers.

At dusk they holed up in the loft of a disused rope-walk, a long shed smelling of hemp and tar, and Cassian laid out the situation with a stick in the dust: the tannery cell was blown, Maret's network broken, the lowland route south closed. Which left, he said, tapping the stick against the dust-map, the underways. Old servant tunnels under the capital. If Odile knew them — and she'd hinted she did — they might still get inside before coronation night.

"If," he said. "Odile hasn't spoken three words since the culvert."

"She'll speak," Paloma said tiredly. "She always speaks. It's the speaking *kindly* part she skips."

"She's not wrong, though." Cassian rubbed his wounded brow, careful of the cut. "That's what makes it unbearable. Every word she said was true. The song drew them. Sefa is dangerous. And none of it changes what's right." He looked up at Wren, unexpectedly. "You defended her well today."

Wren shrugged, ears going hot. "Somebody had to."

"Somebody always has to. That's usually how you know it matters." Cassian studied him a moment longer. "You're braver than you think, boy. Braver than me, maybe. I ran from the army. You run *toward* things."

"I'm not brave," Wren said. "I just hate watching people get blamed for things that aren't their fault." He thought of his mother, and didn't say more, and Cassian, who noticed everything, kindly pretended not to notice anything at all.

Below them, faint through the floorboards, Sefa was singing the duck song again — softly, tentatively, testing whether the world would punish her for it. And Wren lay back on the hemp bales and listened, and made himself add a new line to the tally in his chest, not a loss this time but a debt owed forward:

*Maret. Three others. Their names.*

*I'll carry those too.*

Above the rope-walk, through gaps in the slates, the starless sky pressed down like a lid. But somewhere south, in a cell probably no bigger than a confessional, Maret was sitting in the dark with her forty-one names, and Wren swore to the dark above him that before this was over, every name in every ledger would be read aloud — including hers, including his own, including the entity's, whatever its true name turned out to be.

He was still swearing it when the ladder creaked.

Footsteps — slow, deliberate, familiar — and then Odile's head rose through the hatch, her one eye finding Wren in the gloom with unsettling precision.

"Get the others," she said. "All of you. There's something you need to hear, and I'll only say it once." Her jaw tightened, and for a heartbeat she looked less like a ferrywoman and more like a woman standing at the edge of deep water, deciding. "It's about my brother. And about what I did to him."

Wren sat up. Below, the duck song had stopped.

Down in the tar-smelling dark, the family gathered — bruised, splintered, unfinished — to hear the confession that would change everything.
