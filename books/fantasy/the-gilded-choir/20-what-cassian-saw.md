## 20. What Cassian Saw

They took his boots first.

Cassian understood the logic of it even as they did it — a soldier's logic, the same he'd used on prisoners in another life, before the desertion, before the bridge, before the moment a boy with a stolen fragment of parchment had looked at him and asked *are you one of the good ones?* Boots meant no running. No kicking. A man without boots was already half-broken; the body understood surrender before the mind agreed to it.

So he sat on the cold flagstones of his cell in his stockinged feet and let them take everything else in order: his belt, his knife, the cord from his collar, the little tin star of his old company that he'd carried for eleven years like a stone in his shoe. The sergeant who catalogued these things had kind eyes and careful hands, and that was the worst part. Cruelty you could hate. Kindness doing its work anyway — that was the machinery of the crown, polished smooth by centuries of use.

"Name?" the sergeant said, though they both knew it.

"Cassian Vael."

"Rank?"

"None. Deserter."

The pen scratched. Somewhere above them, muffled through three floors of stone, a choir was singing. He'd heard it when they marched him through the palace underways — a hundred voices braided into something vast and silver, rising through the walls like water through chalk. He'd thought, absurdly, of Paloma. Of her saying *the wards were never protection but a cage*, her voice cracking on the word *cage*, and how he'd wanted to argue and hadn't been able to find the argument anywhere in himself.

Now, listening to that beautiful sound seep down through the prison stones, he began to understand what she had heard that he hadn't.

* * *

The interrogator came at dusk, or what passed for dusk underground — a bell rung somewhere, lamps trimmed, the day officially ended so that the night's work could begin with clean hands. He was not what Cassian expected. No scarred brute, no smiling sadist. A small man, grey as ash, with ink-stained fingers and the patient manner of a schoolmaster. He brought a stool. He brought tea. He set the tea where Cassian could smell it and did not offer it.

"You were at Marrow Ford," the man said. "Third company, left flank."

Cassian said nothing.

"I have your service record. It's very good. That's the problem, you see." The interrogator folded his hands. "Good soldiers don't desert over nothing. So either something happened at Marrow Ford, or something happened before it, and either way I would very much like to know what. Not for punishment." A small, honest-seeming smile. "Punishment is decided well above me. I only want to understand. Understanding is my trade."

"And what do you trade it for?"

"Ah. A talker. Good." The grey man poured himself tea, unhurried, letting the steam curl between them. "I'll tell you what I know, and you can correct me where I'm wrong. You deserted because you saw the purge lists. Villages in the lowlands marked for burning, and no crime attached to the names — just coordinates. Just numbers. You asked your captain why, and he showed you the seal, and the seal was answer enough for him and not for you. Am I close?"

Cassian's jaw tightened. Close was dangerous. Close meant the man had other sources, other deserters pulled apart thread by thread until the whole weave of the rebellion lay on his table.

"You burned villages," Cassian said instead. "I don't need to understand that. I need to stop it."

"Yes," the interrogator said mildly. "Everyone says that eventually. The stopping. It's remarkable how few people ask what the burning is *for*."

He let that sit. Outside the cell, in the corridor, boots passed and repassed — measured, unhurried, the gait of men guarding something rather than someone. And beneath it all, faint as a pulse through stone, the choir went on singing.

"Do you hear it?" the grey man asked suddenly, tilting his head toward the ceiling. "The royal choir. Two hundred and forty voices, rehearsed six hours daily. Finest in the world — we say that proudly, in the broadsheets. Finest in the world." He sipped his tea. "Do you know what they're rehearsing tonight?"

"No."

"The Vigil Mass. For the coronation eve. His Majesty wishes the city filled with song from dusk to dawn — every church, every square, every hall. A great unbroken music, wall to wall, so that no corner of the capital is silent when the hour comes." The interrogator smiled again, and this time the smile had something underneath it, something cold and wet like a stone turned over in a streambed. "It will be beautiful. It will be *necessary*. And in the morning, most of those singers will be dead."

The words landed in the cell like dropped glass.

Cassian laughed — one short, ugly bark, reflexive, the sound a man makes when his mind refuses a thing too large to hold. "That's madness. You don't kill the royal choir. They're — they're the king's own—"

"They're fuel," the grey man said, without cruelty, which made it infinitely worse. "Two hundred and forty voices in the palace, and four thousand more across the city's churches and choirs and singing-schools, all singing at once, all willing or coerced or simply present, their voices raised in the old forms without knowing what the old forms are. Do you know what a binding rite is, deserter? At bottom? Strip away the incense and the Latin of it?" He leaned forward. "It is many voices agreeing on one shape. That's all. That's all it has ever been. A choir is a machine for making agreement audible. And if you have enough voices, agreeing hard enough, on the right shape—" he spread his ink-stained hands, "—you can agree a thing into a cage. Or agree it shut forever. The founders of your valley knew this. They used a handful of lives. We intend to use thousands, and we intend to make the cage eternal, and we intend to do it on coronation eve while the whole city sings itself into the lock."

The floor seemed to tilt. Cassian gripped the edge of the bench and found his hands were shaking, and hated them for it.

"That's not — the valley wards fell. The border's open. Your king walked out of—"

"Our king," the grey man corrected gently, "is the *lock*. Has been for thirty years. A vessel holds the door. But a vessel dies, deserter, vessels always die, and each new binding costs more than the last, because the thing behind the door learns the shape of every key. The steward has done the arithmetic. A single First Voice, freely given — that was the old way, cheap and temporary, a hundred years per life. Inefficient. Sentimental." He shook his head like a tutor disappointed in a dull pupil. "The steward prefers scale. Why buy the door when you can build the wall? Four thousand voices, harvested in a single night, sung into permanence. The entity bound so deep it will never surface again — not in a vessel, not in a dream, not in a child's humming. Forever. Total. *Safe.*"

"Safe," Cassian repeated. The word tasted like ash and river water. He thought of Odile's burned ferry landing. Of the lowlands' ash drifting like black snow. Of Sefa, chained in a waystation, humming a melody that frightened everyone but Wren. *Safe*, written in the smoke over nine villages.

"For whom?" he asked.

The interrogator considered this with genuine scholarly interest, the way a man considers a fly on his page. "For everyone who remains," he said. "Which is the only *for* that has ever mattered to anyone in power. You included, I suspect. You deserted to save yourself from complicity. Very few desert to save others. It's why I find you interesting."

* * *

They moved him after that, or time moved around him; he lost track. The cell had no window. The choir above sang in shifts — he learned its schedule by heart the way a prisoner learns anything, the way he'd once learned patrol rotations: dawn rehearsal, midday rest, evening performance, the long silver thread of it running through the stones and into his sleep until he dreamed in harmony and woke with someone else's melody caught in his teeth.

He told them things. He was not proud of the ledger of it, and he kept the ledger honestly, because Odile wasn't there to keep it for him: he gave up the name of a smuggler on the river road. He gave up the location of a cache of arms in the tannery district — half-empty, he told himself, mostly gone, *they'd have moved it anyway*. He gave up the route they'd taken through the underways, or the parts of it that were already known. Each item he priced against another hour of not being strapped to the chair in the room down the corridor, from which, twice a night, he heard sounds he refused to describe even to himself.

And each item he weighed against the one thing he did not give up: the fragment. The stolen piece of the binding rite sewn into the lining of his coat, which they had taken with the coat, which they had surely read, which meant—

Which meant they knew what Paloma knew. Which meant the race was not a race anymore. It was an ambush.

On the fourth night — he counted bells, four days' worth of them — the grey man came back with a document.

"This is the order of service," he said, laying it flat on the bench between them. "Coronation eve. Every choir in the capital, timed to the minute. Look at the final movement."

Cassian looked. The notation was dense, archaic, and he could not read music — but he could read structure, the grammar of commands, and the final movement was annotated in a clerk's precise hand: *all voices, sustained, until relieved. No singer departs the floor. Relief will not come.*

"How many?" he asked. His voice came out sanded flat.

"In the palace alone? Two hundred and forty. Citywide, if the parish choirs hold their notes as instructed — and they will, they've rehearsed — four thousand one hundred and six." The grey man gathered his papers. "The steward calls it the Great Agreement. There's a poem about it in the archives. Very stirring. All about harmony and unity and the blessed silence after." He stood, brushed off his coat, and paused at the door. "You were at Marrow Ford. Something happened there. When you're ready to tell me what, the tea will still be warm."

After he left, Cassian sat in the dark with his bootless feet and did the arithmetic the grey man had left him, because a soldier's mind does arithmetic whether you want it to or not.

Four thousand one hundred and six lives, spent in one night, to build a cage that would never need rebuilding. Against — what? Five ragged exiles and a child who hummed. A burned-out rebel cell. A ferrywoman losing her memories to the storm. A scribe whose whole inheritance was a trap.

And yet.

And yet the grey man had shown him the order of service. Had told him the number. Had explained the entire design with a schoolmaster's patience, to a prisoner, for no reason Cassian could see — unless the reason was that the steward *wanted* this knowledge to walk out of this cell on deserter's legs. Unless the plan required the rebels to come. Unless the trap needed bait that believed itself to be a knife.

He lay back on the bench and stared at the ceiling where the choir's song pooled like light on water, and he held both truths at once, the way he'd learned to hold a blade and a rope: *they are going to murder four thousand singers, and they may be letting me leave so that I lead my friends into the killing floor.*

Both true. Both certain. Choose anyway. That was the whole of courage; nobody had ever told him that before, and now nobody ever would, because the ones who might have were a valley away and running out of nights.

* * *

The transfer came on the sixth day.

He heard it before he saw it — the cell block waking around him, doors unbarred in sequence, the particular brisk rhythm of a prison being emptied on orders from above. A guard appeared at his door with keys and a clipboard. "Vael. On your feet. Transfer to the palace barracks — you're being remanded to the Marshal of the Lists for judgment in the coronation games. Traitor's trial, public, before the ceremony." The guard grinned, young and pleased to deliver bad news. "Steward wants the city in a festive mood."

They gave him back his boots. That was the detail that rang the alarm, later, in the quiet room of his memory: they gave him back his boots. A man scheduled for public trial did not need boots. A man being moved as *bait* needed to be able to walk convincingly.

Four guards. A covered cart. A route through the underways that Cassian knew better than they did — he'd mapped these tunnels himself, two years ago, in another life, carrying dispatches for a captain whose seal had been answer enough. The cart rattled through the dark past the drowned chapel stair, past the tannery sluice, and at the junction where the old processional tunnel forked toward the palace, the escort slowed for the checkpoint ahead.

There was no checkpoint. There was a barricade fallen inward, splintered, and beyond it darkness, and the guards' lantern-light swung toward it, and in that half-second of swung light Cassian did the only arithmetic left to him.

He drove his newly returned boot heel into the knee of the guard beside the cart gate, took the man's baton as he folded, broke the second guard's grip on the rail with the same motion, and rolled out of the moving cart into the dark of the side tunnel — the one that flooded at high water, the one no sane route used, the one he'd chosen precisely because it was terrible. Behind him: shouting, a whistle, the crack of a crossbow bolt off wet stone an inch from his ear. Ahead: black water to the thigh, cold as a grave, pulling at his bandaged ribs where the interrogation chair had left its quiet bruises.

He waded. He climbed. He crawled through a gap in the foundation stones that the city had forgotten it owned, and came out at last through a coal chute into the tannery district, into the reek of lime and hides, into air that tasted like the world.

He knelt in the alley and was sick, and then he laughed, and then he did neither, and just breathed, because his body was a soldier's body and it knew the order of operations.

Broken. Yes. He could feel exactly how broken — the ribs, the wrists, the places inside where the grey man's patience had pried boards loose that would never sit flush again. He had sold names and routes and caches to buy these hours, and the debt for that would come due, and Odile would collect it with one eye and no mercy, and she would be right to.

But certain. Gods, he was *certain*. Certain as he had never been at Marrow Ford, certain as he had never been under any captain's seal. Four thousand one hundred and six voices, harvested in one night, to build a cage forever — and a coronation eve between now and then, and somewhere across the city five impossible people preparing to walk into the mouth of it without knowing the size of the teeth.

He got up. He started walking toward the safehouse, through streets already strung with bunting for a festival that was also a massacre, past church doors open to the evening, where inside, unseen, the first rehearsals had begun — sopranos climbing scales like larks that didn't know the sky was a lid.

One thought kept his legs moving, one sentence, worn smooth as a riverstone by the end of the first mile:

*Hold the note, Paloma. Whatever happens — whatever they offer, whatever it costs — do not let them finish the song.*

Because he had seen the order of service. He had seen the final movement. And he knew now, with the certainty of a man who has read his own death warrant and found the date blank, that the last note of the Great Agreement was written for a voice he knew —

— and that somewhere in the city, the steward was already rehearsing her part.
