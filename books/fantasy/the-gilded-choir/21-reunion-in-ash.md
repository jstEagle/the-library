## 21. Reunion in Ash

The tannery district smelled of lime and old blood even after the fires had gone out of it, and Paloma had learned to breathe through her mouth the way Odile did, shallow and quick, like a woman crossing a river she didn't trust.

They had been hiding three days in the loft above a cooper's abandoned workshop when Wren heard the knock.

Not a knock, exactly. A rhythm. Three slow taps, a pause, two more — the pattern Cassian had drilled into them back at the waystation, back when they had all still believed the road would be long but survivable. Wren was on his feet before the sound finished, and Sefa's head came up from her half-sleep like a hound scenting rain, and Paloma felt her own heart do something complicated and painful that she would later have no words for.

"Could be anyone," Odile said flatly. She did not move from her seat by the window, where she sat with her one eye on the street below and her knife loose in its sheath, which was how she sat always, as if the world were a door someone might kick open at any hour. "Hounds know patterns too."

"It's him," Wren said. His voice cracked on *him*, and Paloma saw him hate himself for it, twelve years old and already ashamed of hope.

Paloma went down the ladder first. She told herself it was because she was the oldest, the responsible one, the scribe who weighed everything twice before committing ink to page. That was not why. She went down first because if it was not Cassian — if it was the steward's men wearing his knock like a stolen coat — she wanted to be the one standing between that door and the children.

She lifted the bar. Opened the door a hand's width. Ash-light from the overcast sky fell across a figure sitting on the step with his back against the doorframe, knees drawn up, head bowed, as though he had been knocking for some time and had run out of strength to stand.

He looked worse than the bridge. On the bridge he had been a soldier holding a line; now he was what the line had cost. His left eye was swollen nearly shut, the skin around it gone the deep plum of storm clouds. His knuckles were split. Someone had taken his boots — he wore wrapped rags where boots should have been, and his feet beneath the wrappings were gray with cold. When he raised his face to her, Paloma saw that they had also taken something else, something that did not bruise: whatever certainty had lived behind his eyes when she'd first met him on the prison road, watching her too closely, weighing her like coin.

"Cassian," she said.

"I brought company once," he said, and his voice was gravel dragged over gravel. "Two of the hounds' outriders. I lost them in the dye-works. I think. I walked past this door four times to make sure." He tried to smile and it went wrong halfway up his face. "You shouldn't let me in."

Paloma stood aside and let him in.

* * *

Odile fed him before she spoke to him, which was its own kind of verdict. She set down barley soup gone soft from reheating, bread with the green mold cut away, watered wine, and she put the bowl into his ruined hands and watched until he ate, and only then did she sit back, fold her arms, and say, in the tone she used for weather and for wounds:

"They gave you something. To walk you here."

It was not a question. Cassian kept eating anyway, methodically, the way soldiers eat, because the body is a debt that must be serviced no matter what the soul is doing. But Paloma saw his jaw tighten around the swallow.

"Cassian," Odile said again.

"I told them about the lowland cells." He said it to the bowl. "Names. Two safehouses. The ferry schedule on the Vell." He set the spoon down with exaggerated care, the way a man sets down a blade he is afraid his own hand will use. "I told them the girl was god-touched. I didn't know her name yet then, so I couldn't give it. Small mercies." The laugh that came out of him was terrible. "They were very reasonable about it. Did you know that? Interrogation, when it's done by professionals, is almost polite. There was tea. There was a man who apologized before every question, and meant it, I think, the way a butcher means it when he sharpens the knife so the animal won't feel it. And there was a hook, and there was a dark room, and there was the understanding — never spoken, just understood — that the tea could go on forever or stop forever, and it was mostly my choice."

"You held the bridge," Wren said from the ladder, hanging there half-climbed, knuckles white. "You stayed so we could run. You *stayed*—"

"Wren." Paloma said it quietly, and the boy stopped, trembling.

"And then you talked," Odile said. Not cruelly. That was the worst of it. Her voice was level as a mason's line. "People died because of what you gave them. The cell on Tanner's Row. Maren and her boys. You knew Maren. You drank with her people. Two are in the crown's cells now and one is dead, and the dead one's sister is downstairs right now asking me if we're going to do anything about the traitor problem, and I haven't answered her yet, because I wanted to hear what you'd say first."

Cassian nodded slowly, as though each word were a stone being added to a pack he intended to carry the rest of his life. "Yes," he said. "That's fair. All of it."

"Don't do that," Odile snapped, and for the first time something moved under the flatness — anger, or grief wearing anger's coat. "Don't *agree* with me. Agreeing is easy. Agreeing costs nothing. I betrayed my brother to save myself and I spent twenty years agreeing with everyone who condemned me for it, and it bought me exactly nothing except the ability to keep walking. So don't agree. Tell me why. Or tell me you don't know why. Say something true, soldier, or get out of my sight before I decide what Maren's sister deserves an answer to."

The loft was very quiet. Rain had begun somewhere above them, ticking on the slates. Sefa had come down the ladder without anyone noticing — she did that, moved through rooms like a draft — and she stood beside Wren with her small hand fisted in his sleeve, humming under her breath, barely audible, the melody she hummed when the entity's song pressed close. Paloma had learned to read the tune's tempo like a barometer. It was slow now. Sad. Even the thing in the king's crown, perhaps, recognized a moment like this one.

Cassian looked at his hands. At the split knuckles. At the bowl.

"The truth," he said finally, "is that I lasted nine days. And I counted them, because counting is what I'm good at, and on the ninth day they brought in a boy from Tanner's Row, sixteen years old, one of Maren's runners, and they said: tell us what we want, or he goes into the room instead. And I looked at this child who had done nothing, whose whole crime was carrying messages for people who wanted their valley back, and I understood that the choice was never mine. It was never going to be mine. They build the choice so that whichever way you break, you break *usefully*. That's the whole art of it. They don't need you to be weak. They just need you to love something." His voice frayed. "So yes. I sold names. I sold houses. I would sell them again tomorrow, and the day after, and I will carry Maren's face until I die, and none of that undoes any of it, and I am not asking you to forgive me. I'm telling you what happened so you can decide, with your eyes open, whether I sleep under your roof tonight or back on that step in the rain."

Silence again. The rain ticked. Sefa's hum wound through it, thin as wire.

Odile stared at him a long moment with her single eye, and Paloma watched two decades of self-loathing pass behind it like weather over water. Then the ferrywoman stood, took Cassian's empty bowl, and refilled it from the pot without a word — which, from Odile, was a speech.

"He sleeps inside," she said. "Maren's sister gets the truth tomorrow, from both our mouths, mine included. And after that—" she set the bowl down harder than necessary, "—after that we stop bleeding on each other and start bleeding on the enemy. We don't have time for anything else. None of us. You especially."

* * *

Later, when Wren and Sefa had been sent up to the loft — over their loud objections — and Odile had taken the first watch at the window, Paloma sat across the dying fire from Cassian and mended what mending could be mended. She cleaned the cuts on his knuckles with spirits that made him hiss. She bound his ribs where the bruising said a boot had found them, more than once. She worked in silence, because she was a scribe and silence was her native tongue, and because she was composing.

When the last bandage was tied she sat back on her heels and said: "The provost used to tell us that a ledger is a moral document. Every entry balanced, every debt honored, and a man's worth is the sum of what he owes and what he's owed. I believed it for twenty years. I tallied my own indenture every morning like a prayer." She turned her hands over, palms up, and looked at them — at the ink stains that had faded these past months, at the unmarked skin where the entity's brand had burned and healed and vanished. "Do you know what I've learned since the stars went out? Ledgers don't balance. They can't. Nothing evens out. You pay a debt and it opens another one somewhere else. You save a life and cost another. You hold a bridge and lose a row of houses. The arithmetic is broken, Cassian. It was always broken. The temple just kept the books badly enough that nobody noticed."

"That's a generous theology," Cassian said hoarsely.

"It's not theology. It's bookkeeping." She reached out and took his damaged hand, carefully, the way you handle a page that might tear. "Odile named what you did plainly, and she was right to, and you'll answer for it — not to me, not with words, but with what you do next and the day after that. Forgiveness isn't absolution. I'm not wiping your account. I'm telling you that this family doesn't run on accounts." She squeezed, gently, feeling the fragile architecture of bone beneath the bandages. "Wren stole bread for three years before I met him. Odile handed her own brother to the thing in the crown. I copied forbidden texts to shave pennies off my indenture and never asked who suffered for what I read. Sefa carries a song that has eaten kingdoms. Every one of us paid ugly prices to still be sitting by this fire. Ugly prices make ugly people, sometimes. That's not the same as bad. Bad would have been breaking clean, and none of us broke clean, and thank every vanished star for that."

Cassian's throat worked. For a moment Paloma thought the soldier would cry, and she looked away to give him the privacy of the firelight, because that was a kindness she understood.

"Why?" he managed, finally. "Why forgive me? You owe me nothing. Less than nothing. I got caught because I stayed, and I talked because I'm weak, and now Maren's blood is on this roof along with everything else."

"Because on the prison road," Paloma said, "when you had every reason to see me as cargo, you saw a person. Because you taught a frightened boy a knock pattern so he'd know safety when he heard it. Because you held a bridge." She smiled, tired and crooked. "And because somebody forgave me once, when I hadn't earned it, and it turned out to be the only currency that ever actually changed what I did next. Debts just made me smaller. This—" she tapped his bandaged hand, "—this might make you braver. Worth the risk. I was a gambler's daughter, before the temple. I know odds."

"I didn't know you had a father."

"Nobody does, anymore. That's rather the point of the whole story."

* * *

He slept by the fire like a man sleeping at the bottom of a well, deeply and without movement, and Paloma sat up a while longer because sleep would not come and the mark's absence itched worse than a mark ever had. Since the audience with the hollow king — since the refusal and the flight and the burn that had faded from her palm — she had expected the silence in her head to feel like relief. Instead it felt like deafness. She had grown accustomed to hearing the entity's song underneath the world, the way valley folk grew accustomed to the river's sound, and now the river had gone quiet and she kept straining toward a current that wasn't there.

Or wasn't there *yet*.

Sefa came down the ladder near midnight, silent as ever, and settled beside her without asking. The girl did that — arrived like weather. For a while they simply sat, the scribe and the god-touched child, watching the fire eat itself down to a red memory.

"He'll stay," Sefa said at last. It was not quite a question. Sefa's statements often sounded like questions and her questions often sounded like prophecies, and Paloma had given up sorting them.

"If Odile allows it."

"She'll allow it. She hates him a little because he's her." Sefa pulled her knees up, chin resting on them. In the firelight her eyes had that depth they sometimes got, the depth that made Paloma forget she was maybe ten years old. "The song was quiet tonight. While you were talking. Did you notice? It goes quiet when you do that. When you choose each other anyway." She frowned, listening to something far away. "It doesn't like that. The song. It's been alone so long it thinks choosing is a kind of lie."

Paloma went cold in a way the fire couldn't touch. "Sefa. Can you hear it now?"

"A little. Through the walls of the city. Under the streets." The girl's hum started again, soft, and this time Paloma recognized the shape of it — the melody from the buried chapel, the binding-song, slowed and bent out of true, like a reflection in disturbed water. "It's getting ready for something. It's gathering voices. Lots of them. Hundreds." Sefa looked up, and her face in the firelight was terribly calm. "It's practicing, Paloma. Like we are."

Above them, through the ticking rain, the city of the crown lay vast and sleepless, and somewhere in its gilded heart a choir of stolen throats was being tuned for a rite that would cage the world. Paloma sat with the child's head heavy against her shoulder and understood, with a scribe's certainty, that their rehearsal in the ash and the enemy's rehearsal in the gold had become the same rehearsal — two choirs learning the same song from opposite ends, and only one of them could finish it first.

"We leave tomorrow," she said to the dark. "All of us. Whatever state we're in."

From the window, without turning, Odile said: "I'll have the route by dawn."

And under the floorboards, faint as a pulse, the song went on counting.
