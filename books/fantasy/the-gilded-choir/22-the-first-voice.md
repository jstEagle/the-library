## 22. The First Voice

Sefa woke before the others, as she always did, because the song never let her sleep past the grey hour.

It was quieter here than it had been in the waystation, quieter than it had been in the chains. That should have been a mercy. Instead it felt like the hush before someone speaks your name — the whole world holding its breath around her, waiting to see what she would do with the morning.

She lay on her pallet between Wren's slow breathing and Odile's absence — the ferrywoman slept sitting up, by the door, one hand always near the knife — and she listened. Under the floorboards of the rebel safehouse, beneath the smell of tanned hides and woodsmoke and old rain, the melody turned over like something dreaming. It did not know words anymore. It had forgotten them so long ago that forgetting had become its shape. But Sefa knew what it wanted, the way you know hunger when you hear a stranger's stomach growl.

It wanted to be sung *whole*.

She slipped out from under the blanket without waking Wren. He slept curled like a comma, one arm flung toward where she'd been, and even asleep he reached for people. She liked that about him. She liked most about him that he was not afraid of her, and had never once pretended not to be afraid of other things instead.

The main room of the safehouse was cold enough to see her breath. Paloma was already there.

Of course she was. Sefa had learned the scribe's rhythms in the weeks since the chapel: Paloma rose with the dark, took the first cup of bitter tea standing, and worked until her fingers were ink-stained to the second knuckle. This morning the chapel rubbings were spread across three tables pushed together, charcoal impressions of the wall-texts weighted down with cups and candlesticks and one boot Cassian had left as a paperweight. Paloma stood over them with her hair escaping its braid, and she was not reading.

She was crying. Quietly, the way she did everything — efficiently, as if tears were another ledger to be balanced and put away.

Sefa knew better than to announce herself. She poured tea for two and set one cup within Paloma's reach without touching the papers, and waited.

It took a long time. The fire ticked. Somewhere above, a shutter banged in the wind that had been rising for two days now, a wind that tasted wrong to Sefa's tongue, like a note held past its measure.

"The last line," Paloma said at last, without looking up. "I couldn't read it before. The rubbing was too faint. I thought it was damage." Her voice was very level. "It isn't damage. It's an instruction."

Sefa sat down across from her and wrapped both hands around her cup. "Read it to me."

Paloma's jaw tightened. For a moment Sefa thought she wouldn't — the adults had developed a habit lately of deciding things about Sefa in rooms Sefa wasn't in, and then telling her only the parts they thought she could carry. But Paloma had never lied to her, not once, not even with softening. It was why Sefa trusted her more than anyone except Wren.

"*The Choir is begun by the First Voice,*" Paloma read. "*One who sings willingly, knowing the cost. The First Voice anchors all others; through her the binding holds or breaks. Let none be chosen. Let none be taken. The Voice must give herself.*" She stopped. Her thumb pressed flat against the page as if to hold the words down. "And underneath, in older script — a list. Names. Nine names, Sefa. Nine First Voices across nine hundred years. And beside each one—"

"I know," Sefa said.

Paloma looked at her then, really looked, and Sefa watched her understand that the child at the table was not asking what came next.

"You know," Paloma repeated.

"Every choir has a first voice." Sefa blew on her tea. Steam curled between them. "And every song ends somewhere. The wall says *willingly*. That's the part everyone keeps stumbling over. They keep hearing *dies* and forgetting *willingly*."

"They're the same word on this wall," Paloma said, harsher than Sefa had ever heard her. "Nine names, nine deaths. The first was the entity's own first vessel's daughter. The last—" her finger moved down the column, stopped, pressed harder, "—the last was a temple scribe. Thackeray. My family's name, Sefa. My blood wrote this rite, and my blood has paid for it nine times, and I will not add a tenth name to that column. Especially not yours."

Sefa drank her tea. It was terrible, burnt, the way rebels made it, and she loved it because no one had ever made her anything terrible before. In the crown's keeping she had been fed carefully, precisely, food measured out like she was a lamp being kept lit. Burnt tea meant someone had been distracted. Distracted meant human.

"Then don't," she said.

Paloma blinked.

"Don't add my name. I'll write it myself." Sefa set down the cup. "That's what *willingly* means, isn't it? Nobody can do it for me. Not even to stop me."

For a long moment neither of them moved. Then Paloma did something Sefa had never seen her do: she swept the entire table clear — rubbings, cups, the boot — with one arm, papers scattering to the floor, and gripped the table's edge with both hands as if the room were tilting.

"No," she said. "No. You are eight years old."

"I'm nine in the spring. Probably. We didn't have calendars where I was born."

"That is not the point—"

"It's exactly the point." Sefa heard her own voice come out steady, and was surprised by it, and decided not to be surprised. The song had taught her this much: a note does not waver because the singer is small. "Everyone keeps counting my years like they're coins in a purse. Like there's a number that makes it fine. There isn't. There's just willing and unwilling, and I've been unwilling my whole life — chained, carried, hidden, argued over. This is the first thing anyone's ever offered me that's actually mine." She stood. "So I'm claiming it. Before you decide it for me again."

"Sefa—"

But Sefa was already walking away, small feet silent on the boards, and behind her she heard Paloma say, to the empty room, in a whisper that cracked down the middle: "*Not again.*"

* * *

By midmorning the whole household knew, because Paloma told them — told them plainly, all of it, the ninth line and the nine names, because she was Paloma and she would rather burn the plan than lie to build it.

They gathered in the main room like weather coming in. Cassian stood by the door with his arms crossed, which was how he stood when he was frightened. Odile stayed in her chair by the window, whittling nothing, her single eye fixed on the middle distance. Wren sat next to Sefa on the bench, close enough that their shoulders touched, and his knee bounced and bounced.

"No," Cassian said. Flat, immediate, the way he refused orders he considered stupid. "Absolutely not. Find another way."

"There is no other way." Paloma's voice was scraped raw. "The rite requires a willing anchor. Every substitute fails — the walls are specific. A coerced anchor makes the binding into a cage, and we've seen what the cage grows into. A false anchor—" she gestured at the scattered rubbings, "—there's a passage about what happens. I read it four times hoping I'd misread it."

"So we don't do the rite," Cassian said. "We run. Lowlands, coast, over the sea. The entity rots in its king for another century and we live."

"And the royal choir?" Odile said, without looking up. Wood curled off her knife. "Two hundred singers in the palace undercroft, being drained nightly. Your friend among them. You'd leave them singing for it until they die?"

Cassian's jaw worked. "That's not fair."

"Nothing is. That's the lesson. Do it anyway or don't, but don't pretend running is clean."

"We're not running," Sefa said.

Every head turned. She hadn't meant to speak; the words had simply come, the way the melody sometimes came, arriving fully formed. She stood up on the bench so she could see all of them at once — she hated talking up at people, hated being a small shape at the edge of every conversation — and she saw their faces and understood, suddenly and completely, what she was looking at.

They were not angry at her. They were terrified *for* her, each in their own dialect. Cassian's fear wore armor. Odile's wore contempt. Wren's just wore his face, open as a window.

"You all think I don't understand what dying is," Sefa said. "Because I'm young."

Nobody spoke.

"I lived in the crown's cells for two years. I heard the ones taken for the choir go down the corridor. Some of them sang on the way. Not because they wanted to — because the stewards' hooks pulled the sound out of them like thread from a spool. I've heard unwilling singing." Her hands were steady at her sides. "It's the worst sound in the world. It sounds like a door closing. What the wall asks for is the opposite of that. It asks for a door opening." She looked at each of them. "You want to protect me from the one thing in my life I'd get to choose."

Wren made a small broken noise beside her and grabbed her hand, gripping hard, and Sefa squeezed back because she loved him, she loved all of them, and that was the whole unbearable point.

"She's right," Odile said.

"Odile—" Cassian started.

"I said she's right, and I'll say the rest too, since none of you have the stomach." The ferrywoman finally looked up, and her eye was dry and terrible. "I betrayed my brother to save myself. I know exactly what a person is worth when they're spending someone else. Sefa's offering to spend herself. That's the only currency in this room that doesn't stink." She went back to her whittling. "I won't chain her. I won't lie to her. And I won't thank any of you for trying to make her a prisoner one more time in the name of keeping her safe."

Silence. The wind outside had risen further; the shutters rattled in a rhythm that Sefa's ear caught and hated — syncopated, wrong, a rhythm that unmade other rhythms. She noticed Cassian noticing her notice, and the fear in him doubling.

"Then we vote," Paloma said hoarsely. "All of us. Including her."

"No," said Cassian.

"Yes," said Wren, surprising everyone, his voice cracking but loud. "Yes, vote. Because if you just forbid her she'll do it anyway — look at her, Cassian, *look* at her — and at least if we vote she'll know we heard her." His hand was still in Sefa's, crushing it, and his eyes were wet and furious. "I vote no. I vote no with everything I have. But she gets to be counted."

They voted. Cassian, no. Odile, abstained — "I don't get a yes or no after what I am; I get a promise, and I've given it" — Wren, no, weeping openly now and not ashamed. Paloma stood last, longest, her ink-stained hands flat on the table, and when she finally spoke her voice was the voice Sefa imagined she used for the temple ledgers: precise, exhausted, true.

"I vote no. And I will spend every hour between now and the end trying to find a third answer, because the wall is old and old things are always incomplete. But—" and here her composure finally failed, and Sefa watched the scribe become simply a tired woman who had lost too many names, "—but I will not forge her signature. If it comes to it. If there is no third answer. I will not take this from her the way everything else was taken."

Sefa exhaled. It felt like surfacing.

"Then it's settled," she said. "When the time comes, I sing first."

* * *

She found Wren on the roof that evening, because of course he was on the roof; he went up whenever his feelings got too big for indoors, and lay on his back staring at where the stars weren't.

Sefa climbed up beside him. The tiles still held the day's warmth. Below, muffled, she could hear Paloma and Cassian arguing in low voices about routes and risks, and Odile's silence cutting through both of them like a keel through water.

"You're really going to do it," Wren said. Not a question.

"If there's no other way."

"There's always another way. Paloma says—"

"Paloma hopes there's always another way. It's different." Sefa lay back too. Above them the sky was a smooth black nothing, starless for months now, and she felt the song beneath the town answering the emptiness like a tongue probing a missing tooth. "Wren. Can I tell you something I've never told anyone?"

He rolled onto his side to look at her. His hair stuck up. His eyes were red-rimmed and fierce and kind. "Always."

"When they chained me, in the waystation — before you came — the man with the hooks asked me why I hummed it. Why I didn't scream like the others. And I didn't know how to tell him." She watched the blank sky. "It's because the song is lonely. That's all it is, underneath everything. It's the loneliest thing alive. It eats voices because it wants company and it doesn't know any other way to ask. And when I sing it, it goes quiet for a little while. Like something finally listening." She swallowed. "I'm not brave, Wren. I just know what it's like to be a thing nobody listens to. And if the only way to make it listen — really listen, one time, all the way through — is a voice given freely..." She shrugged. "Then I'd rather be the listening than the hunger."

Wren was quiet for a long time. When he spoke, his voice was thick. "If you do it. When you do it. Will you — will it hurt?"

"I don't know."

"Will you be scared?"

"Probably."

"Can I hold your hand?"

Sefa turned her head. Wren was watching her with his whole heart on his face, no armor anywhere, twelve years old and offering the only thing he had.

"Yeah," she said. "You can hold my hand."

He nodded, satisfied, and lay back down, and after a while his breathing slowed toward sleep up there on the warm roof, trusting her to watch the dark. Sefa stayed awake. Far below, under the floorboards, the song turned and turned, and for the first time she let herself feel the shape of what she'd promised: not death, exactly. An ending. A final note, held willingly, that would teach a starving thing what music was for.

The wind changed.

She felt it before she heard it — a pressure against her ears like descending into water, a silence eating the crickets, the night sounds, the town's distant dogs, everything. She sat up. To the north, over the spire of the capital, far beyond the hills, the starless sky was beginning to move. Clouds where no clouds had been, boiling up out of nothing, black on blacker, and threading through them a sound she knew down to her bones:

Un-song. The melody played backward. Memory unraveling.

"Wren," she said, shaking him. "Wren, wake up. Wake up — *everyone wake up* —"

Below, a door banged. Odile's voice, sharp with a confusion that frightened Sefa worse than the sky did: "What was I — why am I holding this — *what was I doing?*"

The storm began to come down the valley toward them, and it was singing, and it was erasing everything it touched.
