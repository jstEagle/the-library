## 23. Storm Over the Spire

Odile had named her boat after a lie.

That was the first thing she thought when the sky over the spire turned the color of a drowned man's lips. *The Kindly Tide*, she'd called it, painted in flaking white across a hull that had carried more corpses than passengers. A joke she'd told herself for thirty years until the joke wore smooth and became something like truth. That was how memory worked. You repeated a thing until it held your weight.

She stood at the ferry's rail and watched the storm come down out of the mountains, and she knew — the way old sailors know weather in their teeth, in the ache of old breaks — that this was no storm at all.

"Get everyone below," she said. "Now."

Cassian was already moving, hauling Wren up from where he'd been mending a net on the deck. Sefa stood at the bow with her face tipped toward the coming dark, and Odile saw the girl's mouth open — not in fear but in recognition, the way you open your mouth to greet someone whose footsteps you know on the stairs.

"No," Odile said, and crossed the deck, and put her one good hand on the girl's shoulder. "Whatever it is saying to you — don't answer."

"It isn't saying anything," Sefa whispered. Her eyes were wide and black as river water. "It's *un*saying. Can't you hear it? It's taking everything back."

---

They had made camp in the ruins of a waystation chapel two miles inland from the crossing, because the lowlands had nothing left standing above knee-height except chapels. The crown's purges had been thorough about granaries and meeting-houses and anything with a roof large enough to hold a crowd, but they'd left the little God-shrines alone — either out of piety or indifference, and Odile had long ago stopped caring which. This one had a bell tower, half-collapsed, leaning against the sky like a drunk against a doorframe. From its broken top you could see the spire: the pale needle of stone rising out of the valley's throat thirty miles north, the place the old maps marked with a word none of them could read anymore.

Paloma had spent three days copying the chapel walls by lamplight, her fingers black to the second knuckle, muttering fragments of the binding rite under her breath like a woman learning a prayer backwards. Cassian sharpened knives that were already sharp. Wren taught Sefa card games with a deck missing the sevens. And Odile kept watch, because keeping watch was the only prayer she still knew how to say.

The storm came at dusk, though dusk had already come once that day.

She felt it before she saw it — a pressure behind her eyes, a wrongness in the sound of the wind, which moved through the grass without bending it. Then the light went strange. Not dark; the sun was still up, a smeared coin behind cloud. But the colors drained out of the world the way color drains from a face, and the horizon north of them turned the color of a drowned man's lips, and the air began to hum.

Not thunder. Humming. A single held note, vast and patient, like the drone-pipe of an organ built into the bones of the world.

"That's it," Paloma said from the doorway of the chapel, her copied pages clutched to her chest. "That's the melody. Sefa hums it — it's the entity's song, but it's—" She stopped. Odile watched her face do something complicated. "It's not singing forward. It's singing backward. It's unwinding."

The first wave hit them like cold water.

There was no wind, no rain, not yet. Just the note, deepening, and then Odile was somewhere else entirely: she was nineteen years old, hauling nets on her father's boat, and her hands were whole and brown and there were calluses on her palms she hadn't felt in decades, and her brother was laughing at the stern —

Her brother was laughing at the stern.

The vision broke like an egg against a rail. She was back in the present, on her knees in wet grass she didn't remember kneeling in, and the evening light had shifted by a full hour, and Cassian was shouting her name from the chapel door.

"How long," she said. Her voice came out scraped. "How long was I gone?"

"You didn't go anywhere." His face was gray. "You were standing right there. For forty minutes. Staring north. We couldn't wake you."

Forty minutes. She'd lost forty minutes the way a hole loses water, and she had been *there* for all of it, nineteen years old on her father's boat, and she could still smell the tar and the herring blood, could still feel the rope-burn, and none of it was hers anymore. None of it had happened in this hour, this life. It had been taken out and shown to her and put back crooked.

"Everyone inside," she said. "Bar the doors. And if anyone starts humming — *anyone* — you shake them until they stop."

---

Inside the chapel, by the light of a single lamp, they waited out the first night of the storm.

It did not behave like weather. Rain came eventually, but it fell upward in places, silver threads climbing off the flagstones and vanishing into the dark. The bell in the leaning tower rang without being struck — a slow, mournful tolling, always the same interval, always slightly too late, like a heartbeat remembered rather than heard. And through it all ran the note, the great backward song, and Odile sat with her back against the wall and her knife across her knees and counted what she could remember, the way a miser counts coin in a burning house.

Her name. Odile. Good. Her trade. Ferrywoman, the crossing at Marrow Ford, thirty-one years at the oar. Her eye — lost to a pike-fish when she was forty-two, a stupid accident, a story she'd told a hundred times. Her father's boat. The *Kindly Tide*. Her brother —

Her brother.

She reached for him the way you reach for a stair in the dark, and for one horrible, weightless moment there was nothing there. No name. No face. Just a shape at the stern of a boat, laughing, and a hole in the world shaped exactly like him.

Then it came back — *Tomas, his name is Tomas* — flooding in so hard her eyes stung, and she pressed her palm flat against the cold floor and breathed until her heart stopped trying to climb out of her chest.

Across the chapel, Sefa sat perfectly still with her hands in her lap, listening to the storm the way other children listened to lullabies. Wren had wedged himself between the girl and the wall, asleep sitting up, his head lolling against her shoulder every time he nodded off and jerking awake every time the bell tolled. Paloma was writing. Of course she was writing. By lamplight, hunched over her pages, copying the same passage again and again, checking each line against the last.

"Your ink's going to run out before the storm does," Odile said quietly.

"I'm not copying the walls." Paloma didn't look up. "I'm copying us. Everything we remember. Names, dates, the road we've traveled, what we've decided and why. If the storm takes any of us—" She paused, pen hovering. "I want to be able to give it back."

Odile looked at her for a long moment. The scribe had ink on her cheekbone and shadows under her eyes like bruises, and her hands shook slightly, and she was doing the most Odile thing Odile had ever seen another person do: building a ledger against oblivion.

"Put me down first," Odile said. "And write it plain. I'm not trusting my own head tonight."

---

The second wave came near dawn, and it took more than time.

Odile woke — or surfaced, or was spat back — standing in the chapel doorway with the door open and rain crawling up her sleeves, and she had no memory of getting there. Worse: the last thing she remembered clearly was the evening meal, and between that meal and this doorway lay a night full of holes. Cassian told her later that she'd talked in her sleep, steady and conversational, answering questions no one had asked. He wouldn't tell her what she'd said. That frightened her more than the holes did.

But the hours were only the beginning. The storm was learning them.

By midmorning the un-song had found the seams in each of them and begun working at them individually. Wren forgot, for six terrible minutes, how to read — sat staring at Paloma's pages with his mouth open, tears running silently down his face, saying *it's just marks, it's just marks, why can't I—* until the knowledge flooded back and he sobbed once and hid his face. Cassian lost the layout of the palace he'd served in for eleven years, and with it, briefly, the plan they'd built their whole desperate hope upon; he paced the chapel like a caged animal, muttering *corridors, there were corridors,* until Paloma read his own words back to him from her ledger and he stopped pacing as though struck.

And Odile lost years.

She was talking to Paloma about the crossing — practical things, tide-tables, which fords would hold a cart in spring — when she realized she was describing a ferry route she hadn't run since before the purges. Before the burning. She was speaking of the lowlands as though the villages still stood, as though the people were still in them, and Paloma's face was very carefully, very terribly kind.

"What year is it?" Paloma asked gently.

Odile opened her mouth. Closed it. Reached for the number and found the recent years gone soft and gray, blurred like ink in rain. She knew the purges had happened. She knew it the way you know a fact in a book. But she could not feel it, and the absence where the grief should have been was the worst thing she had ever felt, worse than grief itself, because grief at least meant the love was still there.

"Tomas," she said aloud, testing. Holding on. "My brother's name is Tomas. I betrayed him. I gave him to the crown's men to save my own skin, and he was taken to the capital, and I have hated myself for it every day for twenty years, and his name is Tomas."

The words hung in the smoky air of the chapel. Paloma wrote them down, line by line, her pen scratching like a small animal.

"Say it again," Paloma said. "Every day, if we have to. Say it until the storm gets bored."

"The storm doesn't get bored," Odile said. "Storms get stronger."

---

On the third day the bell tower finally fell.

It went without drama, a slow sigh of old stone folding into older stone, and the dust of it rose straight up in the windless air and hung there, refusing to fall, and through the hanging dust the note came clearer than ever. Odile understood then, with a sailor's certainty, that the storm was not attacking them at random. It was circling. It had found the chapel the way a hawk finds a vole, and it was tightening.

"We move tonight," Cassian said. "South, off the high ground, into the tannery district. The rebels have cellars the storm can't sing into."

"The rebels blame Sefa," Wren said. He had barely let go of the girl in three days. "Half of them think she called the patrols down on them."

"The rebels are dry and alive," Cassian said. "They'll manage their opinions."

Odile said nothing. She was watching Sefa, who stood in the fallen tower's dust with her face turned north, toward the spire, toward the source of the note, and who was — Odile saw it now, saw it plainly — mouthing along. Following the great backward song the way a child follows a familiar hymn, syllable by syllable, hearing words in it that no one else could.

The girl turned. Caught her looking. And smiled — a small, apologetic, heartbreaking smile, the smile of someone caught humming in church.

"It's lonely," Sefa said. "That's all it is, underneath. Did you know that? All that hunger, and underneath it's just — alone. It's been alone so long it's forgetting what it's hungry *for*. That's why it takes memories. It thinks if it holds enough of ours, it'll remember itself."

"And what happens," Odile said slowly, "when it remembers?"

Sefa's smile faded. She looked, for a moment, much younger than her years, and much older than her years, both at once.

"I think that's what the binding was for," she said. "I think that's what the First Voice was for. Someone has to teach it its own name. Or sing it a new one."

Above them, through the ruined roof, the sky had gone fully the color of a drowned man's lips, and the bell — buried now under its own tower — tolled anyway, muffled, patient, from beneath the earth.

---

They packed in silence. There wasn't much to pack. Paloma sealed her memory-ledger in oilcloth and bound it to her body with straps, and showed them all where the duplicate copy was hidden, and made each of them recite their own entry back to her like schoolchildren. When Odile's turn came she said the words flat and complete: *Odile. Ferrywoman. One eye. Brother Tomas, betrayed, twenty years of hate. Afraid of becoming her brother's mirror.* She did not stumble. She had said it forty times in three days.

But as they filed out the chapel door into the bruised and humming twilight, Odile paused on the threshold and looked back at the dark interior, at the lamp guttering over Paloma's abandoned inkpot, and she tested herself one last time, quietly, the way you test a rotten plank before you trust it with your weight.

Her name. Odile. Her trade. Her eye. Her hate.

*Tomas.*

His name was—

The hole opened under her, sudden and total, and she grabbed the doorframe with both hands and hung on while the world tilted, while thirty years of self-loathing — the one load-bearing beam of her entire soul — swung loose and swayed. *He was laughing at the stern. He had her father's laugh. He was — he was—*

"*Tomas*," Paloma said sharply, from behind her, reading the crisis in her shoulders the way she read everything. "*Tomas. Say it with me. Tom-as.*"

"Tomas." Odile's voice cracked on it. She said it again, louder, spitting it into the storm like a challenge. "Tomas. Tomas. Write it bigger in your book, scribe. Write it on the inside of my skull."

"I will," Paloma said. "Every day. Until the storm gets bored."

And Odile laughed — a short, ugly, genuine bark of laughter — because the girl had stolen her own words back and handed them to her like a rope.

They went south together through the singing rain, five small figures on a drowned road, carrying their names in oilcloth and in their mouths, saying them over and over like a rosary against the dark. Behind them the spire stood pale and patient against the ruin of the sky, and the note it sang wound tighter and tighter, a clock winding down, and Odile marched at the rear with her hand fisted around a scrap of paper on which, in Paloma's careful hand, was written a single word she refused to need twice:

*Tomas.*

Three days south, in a tannery cellar that smelled of lime and lye, a smuggled letter was waiting — though none of them knew it yet, and the letter would change everything.
