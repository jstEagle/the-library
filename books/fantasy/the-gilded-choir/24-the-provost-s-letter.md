## 24. The Provost's Letter

The letter came folded inside a hymnal.

Not one of the rebel safehouse's battered songbooks — those were water-stained and thumb-soft with use — but a temple edition, its boards still crisp, its gilt edges unscratched. Paloma knew the binding style before she touched it. She had spent eleven years making books exactly like it in the scriptorium of the Temple of Quiet Hours: pasteboard covered in dove-grey linen, spine sewn two-on-two-off, the title stamped in dull silver so as not to gleam. *Canticles for the Hour of Stillness.* A book so common in the valley that no one would look at it twice.

No one had looked at it twice. That was the point.

It arrived in a crate of tallow candles from a chandler in the middle town, passed hand to hand through three intermediaries whose names Odile refused to give even under direct questioning, and it sat on the safehouse shelf for four days among cook pots and mended boots before Wren pulled it out, meaning to read aloud to Sefa, and felt the wrongness of the weight.

"There's paper in here that isn't pages," he said, and brought it to Paloma the way he brought her everything — injured birds, interesting beetles, questions too large for his mouth.

She was mending a shirt by candlelight when he set the hymnal in her lap. She opened it. Between the false back board and the true one lay a letter, folded twice, sealed with plain wax and no signet, addressed in a hand she knew better than her own.

*To the scribe Thackeray, if she lives. If she does not, burn this unread.*

Provost Ellery Vance wrote the way he spoke: small, precise letters, ink rationed like everything else in the valley, not one word wasted and not one word given freely. Paloma had copied his correspondence for six years. She knew his abbreviations, his habit of crossing his sevens, the way his pen pressed harder when he was afraid.

His pen had pressed very hard indeed.

*You will have learned by now what the crown wanted with you,* the letter began. *I will not insult you by pretending I did not know. Read to the end before you judge me, or do not read at all. Both are honest choices.*

Outside the safehouse, rain worked at the shutters. Somewhere below, Cassian was drilling two of the rebels' younger fighters in the use of a billhook, his voice a low patient rhythm beneath the weather. Sefa was asleep. She slept more now, and deeper, and when she woke she sometimes hummed a phrase of melody that made the candle flames lean toward her like listeners.

Paloma unfolded the second page.

*Your indenture is not what you believe it to be. It is older than you, older than your mother, older than the ledger that records it. The Thackeray debt has been renewed every generation for two hundred and forty years — not because your family could not pay, but because they were never meant to.*

She stopped. Read it again. The words did not change.

*I found the original instrument while cataloguing the deep archive after the stars went out — the archive beneath the scriptorium, the one we were both told held only damp and rats. It holds more than damp. It holds the founding papers of the valley, and among them, a contract of maintenance, drawn up by the first Choir. Your ancestor Maren Thackeray was one of the nine who bound the thing that wore the king. Her line was charged with keeping the binding's written half: the words, the tallies, the renewals. The debt was the leash. Every Thackeray born into the temple's ledger was born to be exactly where you are — close to the texts, close to the numbers, close enough to renew the cage when the time came.*

*They bred you for it, Paloma. Not the crown. Us. The temple has been the cage's warden since before the temple had a name, and your family has been the key, and neither of us was ever told.*

*When the soldiers came, I gave them your name. I will not pretend otherwise. I will tell you what I told myself: that a living Thackeray inside the capital might learn what we outside could not. That your survival served the valley. I believed it when I said it. I am an old man who has spent his life believing convenient things, and I no longer trust my own beliefs, which is why I am sending you this instead of silence.*

*One thing more, and then I will take whatever comes. The contract names a condition. If the Thackeray line fails — dies out, or refuses — the binding lapses within a generation. They did not build a cage that could outlive its keepers' consent. Whatever you choose, know that refusing them is also an answer, and it may be the truer one.*

*May the Quiet Hours hold you. Or, since the hours have proven anything but quiet — may you hold yourself.*

*E.V.*

The letter ended. Paloma discovered she was standing. She had no memory of rising. The candle had guttered low and the room was all shadow and orange rim-light, and the shirt she'd been mending lay on the floor where it had slid from her hands.

Bred. The word sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Two hundred and forty years. Nine generations of Thackerays copying ledgers, tallying candle-wax and roof-slate and the price of their own servitude, each one taught to bow to the provost, each one dying in arrears — and none of them ever told. Her mother, who had pressed Paloma's small fingers to ink-stained copywork and said *this will buy your freedom someday, little bird*, had died believing a lie someone had written down before the valley's first grandmother was born.

And the provost had known. Had known and sold her name to the crown's men with a clear conscience and a hard pen, and then — this was the part that made her hands shake, this was the part that made her want to put her fist through the shuttered window — had sent her a letter asking, in his careful rationed way, to be forgiven for it. *Read to the end before you judge me.* As if judgment were hers to withhold. As if she owed him the courtesy of understanding.

She owed no one courtesy. She owed nothing to anyone ever again, and everyone who had ever claimed otherwise had been lying to her since before her birth.

The mark on her palm woke.

It did not hurt, exactly. It warmed, the way a hearthstone warms long after the fire, and through the warmth came the voice — not sound, never sound, but something underneath sound, a pressure in the marrow that arranged itself into sense. The entity's offer, the one it had made in the throne room with Aldric's borrowed mouth, returned to her whole and patient:

*Let me erase it. The debt, the ledger, the lie. Two hundred and forty years of theft, unwritten. You would be the first free Thackeray — free of the temple, free of the crown, free of the cage and the key and the breeding and the grief. One word. One signature. Complete the binding my way, and no one will ever own you again.*

That was the poison in it, she thought distantly, watching her own thoughts as if from across the room. It wasn't lying. Everything it offered was true. The debt *would* vanish. The rage *would* go quiet. She had stood in front of the hollow king and felt the seduction of it like heat from an open oven on a winter day, and she had refused, barely, and fled with her palm burned.

But tonight the oven door stood open in a cold room, and she was so tired.

"Paloma?"

Wren stood in the doorway with a lamp, and behind his shoulder the corridor stretched dark toward the rooms where Sefa slept and Odile kept her one eye on everything. He looked at her face, then at the letter in her hand, then at her palm, which she realized she had been pressing flat against her sternum as though holding a wound closed.

"It's him," Wren said. "The provost."

"How did you—"

"You get a line between your eyebrows." He crossed the room without waiting to be invited, the way he always did, thirteen years old and utterly certain of his welcome, and sat on the floor beside her chair with his knees drawn up. "Like a fold in paper. What does it say?"

She should not tell him. He was a child; the words were filth and ruin; there were things you kept from children the way you kept knives from—

Except no one had kept anything from her, and look how well that had gone. Look at the Gallery of Vessels. Look at the portrait of the scribe with her face, hanging among kings.

So she told him. All of it, in a flat voice, reading some passages aloud because paraphrase seemed somehow worse — *they bred you for it* — and Wren listened the way he listened to everything, completely, his whole body turned toward her like a plant toward light. When she finished, the candle had died entirely and only his lamp remained, and the rain had thickened, and somewhere below Cassian's drill had gone quiet.

"He's sorry," Wren said at last.

"He's *covering himself*. There's a difference."

"Maybe. But he sent the letter anyway. He didn't have to." Wren picked at a thread on his knee. "Sefa says the song gets loudest when people are most afraid of losing something. Did you know that? She hears it better than any of us. She says it doesn't offer people what they want. It offers them what they're afraid they can't live without." He looked up at her, and in the lamplight his eyes were very steady for a boy who still cried over dead birds. "What are you afraid you can't live without?"

"The truth," Paloma said, before she could stop herself. "Someone to have told me. Someone to have—" Her voice cracked and she hated it. "Two hundred and forty years, Wren. My mother scrubbed the temple floors until her knuckles bled to pay a debt that was invented to keep her there. And I lit candles for that provost. I prayed for his health. I thought he was *kind*."

"He was kind," Wren said. "Kind and cruel aren't opposites. Sefa taught me that too. She says the entity is kind, sometimes. That's how it gets in."

The mark pulsed, warm, reasonable. *They used you,* it murmured, wearing a voice like her own gentled. *Every hand that touched your life was taking something. I am the only one who offers. Sign, and be done with being owned.*

"Paloma." Wren had taken her wrist — the unmarked one — in both his hands. His grip was thin and stubborn. "Say what you're thinking. The real thing. The one underneath."

"The real thing," she repeated, and heard her own voice come out strange, distant, almost curious. "The real thing is that it would work. Don't you see? If I finish the binding its way, the debt dissolves, the ledger burns, the temple loses its claim, the crown loses its leash on me — everyone who ever profited from my family's suffering loses their profit, forever. It's not even a bad bargain, Wren. That's what nobody understands. It's a *good* bargain. It's a good bargain the way a trap is a good shelter. And I am so tired of paying for other people's architecture."

The lamp flame leaned. Outside, the rain faltered, hung, resumed — as if the night itself had paused to listen.

Wren did not let go of her wrist.

"When my star went out," he said quietly, "the first one, before all of them — I told you and you believed me. Everyone else laughed. Brother Anselm said I'd dreamed it. You got me extra bread from the refectory and you sat with me on the bell tower and you said, 'Tell me exactly what you saw,' and you wrote it down in your own hand, and you dated it, and you signed it." His voice wobbled and steadied. "Nobody had ever done that. Written down something of mine like it mattered. So I'm going to do it for you now, and you can laugh if you want."

He took a breath.

"You are not the cage, Paloma. You're not the key either. Those are things they *made* and *hung on you*, like the ledger, like the indenture, like the mark." His eyes flicked to her palm. "The provost's letter says your family was bred to keep the binding. Fine. But the chapel walls say the binding needs willing voices. Willing. That's the one thing nobody can breed into you. That's the one thing they couldn't steal, no matter how many generations they tried. And if you sign that thing's contract, you give up the last piece of yourself they never touched."

The mark flared — not painfully, but insistently, a hand tightening on hers. *He is a child. Children say beautiful foolish things. Sign, and I will spare him too. No workhouse. No conscription. No hunger. You could keep him safe—*

And there it was. The real hook, sunk past the bone. Not her freedom. *Wren's.*

"That's enough," Paloma whispered.

The warmth surged, eager, mistaking surrender for consent—

"I said *that's enough*." She tore her marked hand away from her chest and held it up between herself and the lamp, palm open, the sigil black against her skin like a brand seen from the far side of the fire. "Listen to me, whatever you are. You do not get to use him. You do not get to wear my love for him like a glove and reach into the world with it. I know what you are now. I've read the founding papers, I've walked the Gallery of Vessels, I've seen what you leave behind when you're done with a body — and I would rather die in debt, in rage, in the full ugly truth of every theft ever committed against my family, than owe you one single breath of my peace."

The mark cooled. Slowly, grudgingly, like a banked fire resenting the wet ash thrown over it. The pressure in her marrow receded, but it did not vanish — it never vanished anymore — it simply withdrew to its distance, a listener beyond the wall, patient as arithmetic.

Paloma became aware that she was crying, silently, and that Wren had climbed onto the arm of her chair and put his arms around her shoulders, and that she was holding on to him hard enough to wrinkle his shirt.

"I nearly said yes," she admitted into his hair.

"I know. The lamp was doing a thing." He sniffed. "Odile says the flames lean toward it when it's talking. We're going to need a lot of candles."

A wet, broken laugh escaped her — the first in days. "We are going to need a *chandelier*."

They stayed like that while the rain found its rhythm again. Then Paloma drew back, wiped her face with the heel of her hand, and looked down at the letter, still crumpled in her fist. Carefully, deliberately, she smoothed it flat against her knee.

"We don't burn this," she said. "We copy it. Three copies, in three different hands, and they go to three different places — the rebels, the ferry routes, and wherever Odile's chandler friends can carry them. If the temple engineered my family, the valley deserves to read it in the provost's own letters. Let him stand in the Quiet Hours and hear what his quiet built."

Wren grinned, fierce and sudden. "That's very scary. I like it."

"And then—" She stopped. Because the thought had been forming all evening, underneath the rage, underneath the grief, assembling itself the way her mind assembled tallies: column by column, until the sum declared itself. She looked at her open palm, at the faded sigil, and the sum was this: *the binding needs willing voices, and a life freely given, and every First Voice in two centuries had died alone — but what if the rite was never meant to be sung by one?*

"What?" Wren prompted.

"The chapel walls," she said slowly. "The fragment Cassian stole. I've been reading them as instructions for a soloist — one First Voice, one sacrifice, the way it's always been done. But the provost's letter changes the mathematics. The Thackeray line was charged with the *written* half. Maren Thackeray was one of *nine*. Nine voices, Wren. The original binding was a choir. It was always a choir. We've been trying to solve it like a debt — one payer, one price — because that's the only shape anyone in this valley knows." She was on her feet now, pacing, the exhaustion burning off like morning fog. "Get Cassian. Get Odile. Wake Sefa — gently, and stay between her and the door until she's fully awake. Tonight. Before the storm takes anything else from anyone."

Wren scrambled up, lamp in hand, then paused at the doorway.

"Paloma? The thing in your hand — when it offered to keep me safe. Did you want to say yes?"

She met his eyes across the small lamplit room, and told him the truth, because he had earned it, because he had once gotten her extra bread and sat with her on a bell tower, because he was the first person in two hundred and forty years of Thackerays to be asked nothing and give everything anyway.

"Every part of me," she said. "That's why it offered. Now go — we have a choir to build, and I intend to write every note of it myself."

* * *

They gathered around the safehouse table by the light of three lamps and one candle stub, and Paloma laid out the letter, the stolen fragment, and her own transcription of the chapel walls side by side, and began, at last, to teach them the song.

None of them knew yet that the steward was already moving — that in the capital, orders were being sealed, chains counted, and a cart with a locked cage at its center assigned a route through the hills. None of them knew that the piece they could least afford to lose was about to be taken.

But the candle flames leaned toward the table, listening, and for the first time they leaned *toward* something, not away — and far off, beyond the vanished stars, something ancient and hungry felt the shape of the music change, and did not yet understand why it was afraid.
