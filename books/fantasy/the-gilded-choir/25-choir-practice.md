## 25. Choir Practice

The safehouse had been a tannery office once, before the rebels took it. Wren knew this because the walls still smelled of salt and old hides, and because there was a hook by the door where someone had hung a ledger of skins sold, the ink faded to a ghost of itself. He liked the room for that — it was a room that remembered what things were for.

It did not, however, have very good acoustics.

"Again," said Paloma, "from the top."

Wren's heart sank all the way down into his boots, which were too big anyway, borrowed from a dead man whose name nobody knew. From the top meant starting with Paloma's line, which meant starting with reading, which meant Paloma watching him over the edge of her papers with that look she had — not unkind, exactly, but patient in the way water is patient with stone.

He cleared his throat. He looked at his page. The notes were written in her small, precise hand, and above each one she had written the words, and above some of the words she had written other words in even smaller script, like *hold* or *soft* or *do not rush this, Wren, I mean you specifically.*

"I know that last note is aimed at me," he said.

"It is."

"You wrote my name."

"I wrote your name," Paloma agreed, "because the last four times we reached that bar, you ran through it like a boy fleeing a wasp nest."

"I am a boy fleeing a wasp nest," Wren said. "Have you met our lives?"

Cassian laughed from his corner, where he sat with his back to the wall out of old habit, one knee up, his bass voice resting in his chest like a sleeping animal he'd learned to carry carefully. His ribs were still strapped from the bridge, from the hounds, from everything the steward's people had done to him in that cell, and when he laughed it cost him something. Wren noticed that Cassian paid anyway. That seemed important. That seemed like the whole point of the evening, actually, though nobody would ever say so out loud.

"From the top," Paloma said again, and this time nobody argued, and Sefa began.

* * *

Here is what Wren understood about Sefa's singing, though he could never have explained it to anyone who hadn't heard it: it was like standing at the mouth of a cave and feeling breath come out of the dark. Not wind. Breath. Something on the other side of the world inhaling and exhaling, and her voice caught the rhythm of it and carried it into the room.

She sang the lead line of the binding rite — the true one, the one they'd copied off the buried chapel walls in Odile's ferry-lantern light while the storm of un-song chewed holes in their memories behind them — and the tannery office went strange around her. The salt smell sharpened. The candle flames stood up straighter, attentive, like soldiers called to attention. Wren felt the hair on his arms lift and his eyes sting and something in his chest open like a door he hadn't known was there.

Then Cassian came in underneath her, low and steady as a river under ice, and the door in Wren's chest stayed open instead of swinging wild.

Then Odile.

Odile's alto did not enter so much as arrive, the way weather arrives, the way grief arrives. It keened up through Cassian's bass and wrapped around Sefa's lead and made the whole thing ache. She sang with her one eye closed and her good eye fixed on the middle distance, on nothing, on maybe a brother whose name she had lost to the storm three weeks ago and still reached for every morning like a tongue reaching for a missing tooth. When she sang, Wren thought, she sounded like she was arguing with the sky and winning.

Then Paloma, then Wren, and this was where the cathedral of it collapsed into a barn fire.

Because Wren came in late, and sharp, and about a third too loud, and the note he chose was not the note on the page but its ambitious cousin two steps higher, and the beautiful terrible woven thing that the four of them had built fell apart around him like wet paper.

They stopped. Sefa stopped first, her voice folding away neatly, the way it always did, like she could put it in her pocket. The candles sagged back to being ordinary candles. The salt smell went back to just being salt.

Silence.

"That was my fault," Wren said quickly.

"Yes," said everyone, more or less at once, though Odile said it driest and Cassian said it kindest and Paloma said it with the particular weariness of a scribe watching ink dry wrong.

Sefa patted his arm. She was eleven, small for it, with rope scars at her wrists that she never hid and never explained, and she had the most terrifying singing voice in the world, and right now she was looking at him with the gentle pity of an adult comforting a child, which was a thing Wren had decided he would never get used to and secretly never wanted to.

"It's all right," she said. "You're getting braver."

"I'm getting louder."

"Same thing," said Odile, "in my experience."

* * *

They broke for supper, which was barley and onions and a smoked fish Cassian had traded three days' worth of guard-rota knowledge for, and they ate sitting in a circle on the floor because the safehouse had one chair and Odile had claimed it on the grounds of seniority, meaning age, meaning she refused to specify further.

Wren ate with his bowl balanced on his knees and watched them, and thought about how they had gotten here, all of them, to this circle on this floor.

Paloma had been a temple scribe who owed a debt she couldn't count. Now she had a burn-mark on her palm shaped like a clef no musician used anymore, and she read the entity's song in it the way she read ledgers — reluctantly, thoroughly, and with annotations. She'd spread her notes out next to her bowl, because of course she had, because Paloma Thackeray annotated her own supper.

Cassian had been a deserter, then a prisoner, then a man who'd sold information to stay alive and been named for it plainly by Odile and forgiven — not absolved, forgiven — by Paloma. He ate slowly, guarding his food without meaning to, an old cell habit. When Sefa leaned over and stole a piece of his fish he let her, and pretended not to notice, and Wren saw him notice, and saw him decide to have let her.

Odile had lost her brother twice: once to the thing in the crown, once to the storm that took his name out of her head. She still set out two cups sometimes. Nobody mentioned it. Everybody knew.

And Wren himself — Wren had been a temple boy who swore he saw a star flicker out, and been right, and been dragged through the end of the world because of it. He'd stowed away in a cart. He'd defended Sefa against half a rebel cell with his fists and his fury and not much else. He'd held the line in the gallery of vessels while the entity sang his worst memory back at him — the provost's hand on his shoulder, the ledger, the word *indentured* spoken soft as a blessing — and he'd kept singing anyway, badly, loudly, bravely.

Bravely and badly. Those were his two settings. He was beginning to suspect they might be enough, if the others carried the rest.

"So," he said, into the quiet, because somebody had to, and it was usually going to be him. "Be honest. Are we terrible?"

"Yes," said Odile.

"Completely," said Cassian.

"There are moments," Paloma said, in the tone of a woman drafting a diplomatic letter, "of accidental beauty."

"But?" Wren pressed.

"But," Paloma said, setting down her bowl and pulling the chapel transcription toward her, "the rite doesn't require us to be good. I've read the walls six times now. It requires us to be *willing*. Every voice freely given, in harmony of intent, not harmony of pitch." She tapped the page. "The original Choir weren't trained singers. They were farmers and ferrymen and — " she paused, and Wren saw her touch the place where the mark lived under her sleeve — "and scribes. They were ordinary people who chose. The choosing is the instrument."

"The choosing is the instrument," Odile repeated, skeptical. "That's very temple."

"It's very *chapel*," Paloma corrected. "Before the temple. Before the wards. Before anyone decided magic needed to be caged." She looked around the circle. "Which means we don't need to sound like the royal choir. Thank all the forgotten stars. We need to sound like *us*, together, meaning the same thing at the same time."

"We mean different things," Cassian said quietly.

"Name one thing we all mean."

Cassian considered. His eyes went around the circle — to Sefa, who he'd helped free from chains; to Odile, who'd named his sin and walked beside him anyway; to Paloma; to Wren. Whatever he found there, he nodded slowly.

"Sefa goes home safe," he said. "To wherever home turns out to be. All the way there. None of us stop until she's there."

Nobody argued. Sefa went pink and studied her barley very hard, and Wren saw her hand drift to her wrist scars and then deliberately away, and he wanted to say something kind and didn't, because he'd learned from Odile that sometimes the kindest thing was to let a person have their silence.

"Well," said Odile, draining her cup. "There's our harmony of intent. Back to work. If we're going to unmake a god, we should at least be able to get through the second verse."

* * *

The second verse was worse than the first.

The second verse required Wren to hold a note for eight counts while descending, which he maintained was unnatural, "like walking down stairs backwards," and which Paloma maintained was basic musicianship, and which ended with him running out of breath entirely and producing a sound like a kettle giving up.

Cassian tried to help. Cassian's method of helping was to put one large warm hand flat against Wren's back and say "here — breathe from here, low, like you're filling your belly, not your chest," and this worked astonishingly well for exactly as long as Wren didn't think about it, and then he thought about it, and got self-conscious, and squeaked.

"Stop thinking," Cassian advised.

"I'm bad at that."

"Noticed," said Odile.

But here was the thing, and Wren felt it happen and couldn't stop it and didn't want to: they kept going. They went again, and again, and between failures they laughed — really laughed, the ugly snorting kind, Cassian wheezing and holding his strapped ribs and laughing anyway, Odile making a sound like a gate hinge that had opinions, Sefa giggling into both hands, Paloma pressing her lips together in a doomed attempt at dignity before the laugh got her too, and Wren realized somewhere in the middle of the fourth attempt that his face hurt from smiling and he couldn't remember the last time that had happened. Maybe ever. Temple boys didn't smile much. Temple boys counted debts.

They were not a choir. They were five people in a smelly room failing at music. And they were also — Wren felt it settle over him like a blanket, warm and slightly embarrassing — a family. A family that rehearsed. A family with homework. A family whose matriarch was a one-eyed ferrywoman who threatened to throw people off her boat for singing flat, and whose oldest child was a deserter with broken ribs, and whose baby sister could sing the end of the world into submission.

On the sixth attempt, something happened.

It wasn't much. It was barely anything. Wren came in on time — actually on time, on the actual beat — and his note was only a little sharp, and Cassian's bass rose to meet it and steadied it, and Odile's alto poured through the middle of the sound like dark water, and Paloma's line wove in clean and certain, the scribe's line, the line that held the words, and Sefa —

Sefa lifted.

That was the only word for it. Her voice went up and up and the room went with it, the candles straightening, the salt-smell blooming into something older, and for three bars — three! — the tannery office became the inside of a bell, and Wren was not a boy failing at a note, he was a thread in a fabric, and the fabric was holding.

Then he got excited, and rushed the eighth bar, and it all came down like a market stall in a gale.

But they'd heard it. All of them had heard it.

"*There,*" Paloma breathed, and her eyes were shining, and the mark on her palm had gone faintly luminous through her sleeve. "That. Whatever that was — do that."

"Do what?" Wren asked. "I rushed it."

"You rushed it," Paloma agreed, "and it didn't matter. Because for three bars we meant the same thing." She looked down at her palm, and her voice went quieter. "It heard us. Just now. Whatever's left of it, out past the stars — it heard us, and it *stopped*. Just for a moment. Like it was listening."

The warmth in the room changed temperature. Not colder, exactly. More careful.

"Is that good?" Wren asked. "Or is that the beginning of chapter twenty-six's problem?"

Everyone looked at him.

"What? I've started thinking in chapters. It's a coping mechanism."

"It's good," Paloma said slowly, "and it's dangerous, and those are usually the same thing." She gathered her pages, tapping them square. "We run it twice more tonight. Then sleep. Tomorrow we take it to the rebel cell in the tannery district — if we're going to reach the palace before coronation night, we'll need more voices than five. A real choir. Willing ones."

"And if the cell says no?" Cassian asked.

"Then it's five of us and a god," Odile said, rising from her chair at last, joints cracking like green wood. "I've fought worse odds. I've *been* worse odds."

They ran it twice more. The second of the two was nearly as good as the miracle, and Wren only rushed one bar, and afterward Sefa fell asleep against Cassian's shoulder mid-sentence, the way children do, trust made visible, and nobody moved for a long time because moving would wake her and waking her would end the evening, and none of them wanted the evening to end.

Wren lay later on his pallet by the wall, listening to the safehouse breathe — Cassian's slow careful breathing, Odile's occasional muttering in a dialect he didn't know, Paloma still scratching notes by candlelight because of course she was — and he thought about the star he'd seen flicker out, a lifetime ago, from the temple steps.

He'd told the truth and nobody believed him. Then the stars had all gone out, and the world had ended, and he'd been dragged through its wreckage, and somewhere in the wreckage he'd acquired a family by accident, and tomorrow they were going to ask strangers to sing with them against a god.

His last thought, drifting down into sleep, was that the star hadn't gone out at all, probably. It had just gone somewhere it could see better.

He was almost asleep when the knock came — three quick raps, then two slow, the rebels' signal for *urgent, open, trouble* — and the candle guttered, and outside in the street a dog began to bark, and far off, at the edge of hearing, something in the city began to hum a melody Wren knew.

Sefa's melody.

Played backward.
