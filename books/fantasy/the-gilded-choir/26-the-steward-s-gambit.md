## 26. The Steward's Gambit

The safehouse smelled of tallow and wet wool, and for three days it had smelled of hope.

Paloma sat at the scarred table with the chapel rubbings spread before her, pinning each sheet flat with whatever came to hand — a candle stub, a chipped cup, Cassian's knife. She had worked through the night translating the last of the binding rite from the old temple script into plain hand, her fingers black to the second knuckle, and when she finally set down the pen the room had gone quiet in a way that made her look up.

They were all watching her.

"It's done," she said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "The full rite. First Voice, counter-melody, the anchoring line. It's all here." She pressed her palm flat against the page — against the mark beneath the skin of her palm, which pulsed once, faintly, like something turning over in its sleep. "We can practice it properly now. We can *finish* this."

Wren whooped. Odile did not whoop — Odile never whooped — but the corner of her mouth moved, which from Odile was a parade. Sefa laughed, that strange silver laugh of hers, and hummed three notes of the melody as if tasting it, and the candle flames all leaned toward her at once, gently, like flowers toward sun.

Later, Paloma would remember that lean. Later, she would understand it had not been wonder. It had been hunger answering hunger.

But that evening there was stew, thin and onion-heavy, and Wren fell asleep mid-sentence with his head on the table, and Cassian carried him to the pallets in the back room with a tenderness he would have denied under torture. And Paloma sat by the banked fire with the finished rite in her lap and let herself believe, for one whole hour, that they might win.

* * *

She woke to silence of the wrong kind.

Not quiet — silence. The difference was a held breath. The fire had died to white ash. The window's shutter had come loose and knocked softly against its frame, but even that sound seemed muffled, as though the night itself had been wrapped in cloth. And the mark on her palm was burning.

She sat up. The back room: Wren, breathing slow. Cassian, sprawled with a soldier's economy, one hand near his knife even in sleep. Odile's pallet empty — she often kept watch, or walked, or stood in doorways staring at nothing, carrying her dead brother's name like a stone she refused to set down.

Sefa's pallet was empty too.

Paloma was on her feet before thought caught up. She crossed the main room, heart hammering against her ribs, and saw it then: the front door standing open a hand's width, night air sliding through, and on the floor beside it —

Chalk.

A circle of chalk, drawn with a draftsman's precision, and inside it, pressed into the wood like a seal, a single gold coin stamped with the crowned face of King Aldric. Payment. Exact payment, left where it could not be missed, because the steward was a creature of ledgers too, and he wanted them to know the account had been settled fairly.

"No," Paloma said aloud, and the word cracked in half. "No, no —"

"Wren!" She shook him hard enough to hurt. "Cassian! She's gone — *she's taken* —"

The room exploded into motion. Cassian was up with his knife drawn and his eyes still fogged with sleep, scanning corners, reading the door, the chalk, the coin, in the time it took Paloma to say it twice. Wren stood frozen in his shift, hair wild, looking at Sefa's empty blanket as if it might explain itself.

"The window," Odile said from the doorway, and her voice was flat as slate. "Look at the sill."

Paloma looked. Scratches in the wood — no. Not scratches. *Letters*, tiny ones, cut with a stylus point, in the old temple script Paloma had spent three nights translating. She knelt and read them by the grey light coming up over the hills, and as she read, the mark in her palm flared so hot she gasped.

*"One voice freely given is worth ten thousand taken,"* the inscription said. *"I have taken mine. Come and count the cost."*

Below it, smaller, almost gentle:

*"Thackeray. Bring the rite. You know where."*

"He didn't take her because we failed," Cassian said quietly. He had come to stand behind her, and she could feel the fury coming off him like heat off a forge, controlled, banked, terrible. "He took her because we *succeeded*. We finished the translation. He couldn't risk us doing the binding our way, so he's going to do it his way first."

"With the royal choir," Paloma whispered. The realization arrived whole, cold, complete. "Thousands of voices. Taken, not given. He doesn't need willing singers if he has enough unwilling ones — the chapel walls said the choir must be *willing*, but the steward wrote those walls, didn't he? He's had nine hundred years to find the loopholes in his own prison."

"Where?" Odile demanded. "Where would he do it?"

And Paloma already knew, because the mark in her palm was pulling — not burning now but *tugging*, a fishhook of heat set behind her knuckles, drawing steadily eastward, toward the capital, toward the palace, toward the spire whose gilded crown she could just see from the high road on clear days. She raised her hand and watched the mark brighten as her fingers swung toward the city and dim as they swung away.

"I can track her," she said. Her own voice sounded far away. "It marked me in the throne room. I thought it was a wound. It's a *leash* — and leashes run both ways."

* * *

They left within the hour, in the blue-black before dawn, taking the cart and the mule and every weapon the safehouse held. Wren sat in the cart bed clutching Sefa's shawl — she had left it folded on her pallet, deliberately, the way you leave a token for someone following you, and Paloma chose not to think about what it meant that a nine-year-old understood such things.

"She knew," Wren said, an hour into the ride, when the capital's smoke-smudge had risen on the horizon. His voice was small and very steady, the way it went steady when he was most frightened. "Last night. Before supper she asked me what the capital was like. She asked if the palace had windows. She'd heard the song change, Paloma. She told me it had gotten *hungrier* and I thought she meant — I don't know what I thought she meant."

"You couldn't have known," Cassian said.

"Neither could any of you." Wren's jaw set. "That's not the same as nothing being known."

Paloma reached back and gripped his ankle through the blanket, and he covered her hand with his, and neither of them said anything else, because there was nothing else to say that wasn't a promise they might not keep.

The mark guided them along roads Paloma had only ever read about in confiscated atlases — the pilgrim road, the salt road, the long descent through pine country where the air turned cold and resinous. By midday the pull in her palm had sharpened to a wire. By late afternoon, when they crested the last ridge and the capital lay below them in its bowl of haze — a sprawl of slate roofs and chimney smoke and, at its heart, the palace rising pale and vast as a bleached bone — the mark was singing.

That was the only word for it. A thin, sweet, horrible note, sustained, like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass. She could hear it without ears. She could feel it in her teeth.

"It knows we're coming," she said.

"Good," said Odile, cracking the reins.

* * *

They left the cart with a tanner Cassian trusted — a man with a burned face and no questions — and entered the city on foot as the lamps were lit, four travelers among hundreds streaming home through gates that no longer bothered to check papers, because the crown had stopped fearing its people and started simply hunting the ones that mattered.

The streets near the palace were wrong.

Paloma felt it before she could name it. The crowds thinned. The shuttered windows multiplied. And everywhere, everywhere, the royal choir's song drifted down from the spire — except it was not drifting anymore. It was *pouring*. Nightly harvest had become continuous harvest; she could hear the voices braided together into that vast, exhausted harmony, and threaded through it, underneath it, a second melody she recognized with a lurch: Sefa's lead line, sung by a child's voice, clear and unbroken and utterly alone.

"They've already begun," Paloma breathed. "The false binding. He's — oh, gods, he's using her as the anchor *now*. Before we even arrive. He wants the rite completed before we can—"

"Then we go in tonight," Cassian said.

"We don't even know where—"

"I know where." His face had gone the color of the marble around them. "There's a gallery under the choir loft. I saw it when they marched me through, after interrogation. Vessels, they call it. Portraits. And beyond it, stairs down to the old consecrated crypt — the one place in the palace old enough to hold a rite this size. If he's doing it properly, doing it *old*, that's where he'll be. Under everything. Where the foundations are."

"How do you know the crypt exists?"

"Because I helped build the wall across its door." He said it without flinching, and Paloma remembered — they all remembered — what he had sold during interrogation to stay alive. What he had traded. "You want to be angry about that later, fine. Be angry later. Right now every ugly thing I know is the only map we have."

Odile spat, eloquently, at the cobbles. But she nodded.

* * *

They spent the last hours of light preparing in a collapsed wine cellar three streets from the palace wall, and it was there, rolling bandages and sharpening knives, that the mark finally showed Paloma what it had been saving.

It happened when she closed her eyes. The moment her lids fell, she was *elsewhere* — standing in a vast round chamber hewn from living rock, lit by a hundred candles that burned without flame, and the ceiling above her was painted with stars, real stars, the old constellations, the sky as it had been before the wards. Around the chamber's rim rose tiered benches, and on the benches sat the royal choir — two hundred singers at least, maybe more, in their gilded robes, swaying slightly, mouths open, pouring out the harmony she had been hearing all day. Their eyes were closed. Some of them were crying without any change in their expression, tears simply falling, as mechanical as rain.

At the chamber's center stood a pillar of black stone, and chained to it, small and straight-backed, was Sefa.

She was singing. Of course she was singing; they could not stop her singing; that was the whole horror of her and the whole glory. But her eyes were open, and they were moving — scanning the tiers, the pillars, the painted stars — and Paloma understood with a jolt that went through her like cold water that the girl was *looking for them*. That some part of Sefa knew exactly who would come, and was leaving herself visible on purpose.

Then the vision tilted, and Paloma saw what stood behind the pillar, and her stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

The steward. Not the dry court functionary she had glimpsed at Aldric's side, all ink-stained cuffs and bowed shoulders. This was the steward unbound — tall, terribly tall, in robes the color of deep water, his face smooth and ageless and faintly translucent, as though nine hundred years of borrowed vessels had worn the man himself down to a membrane. And behind his face, moving like a lamp behind horn, was the entity. Looking out. Waiting.

Its gaze swept the chamber — and stopped.

Stopped on *her*, on the point in the dark cellar where Paloma Thackeray sat with her eyes squeezed shut and her marked fist pressed against her mouth to keep from screaming.

It smiled. She felt the smile land on her skin like a moth.

*"Bring the rite, scribe,"* it said, and its voice was the glass-rim note, was the song, was every lullaby the valley had ever forgotten. *"Or I will finish it without you, and your child's voice will be the first thing I swallow."*

Paloma opened her eyes. The cellar. The candle. Cassian's face inches from hers, alarmed.

"What did you see?"

She told them. All of it, in a voice that only shook twice. When she finished, the silence was total, and Wren was crying without any change in his expression, tears simply falling, and Paloma thought: *like the choir, like the choir, we are all the choir now.*

"There's one more thing," she said. "In the vision. The chains on Sefa — they're not iron. They're *ledger-chains*. Links of vellum and wire, written all over. Debt-script." She looked down at her own hands, the hands that had copied debts for eleven years, the hands bred for this work by generations she had never chosen. "He bound her the way the temple binds people. With paper. With accounts."

"Then we unbind her the same way," Odile said.

Everyone looked at her.

The ferrywoman was honing her boat-hook's edge with slow, even strokes, and she did not look up. "Everything in this cursed country runs on debt. So we default." Now she looked up, and her single eye was bright and hard as a coin. "We walk into that hole in the ground tomorrow night, and we cancel the account. All of it. Every debt the crown holds, every debt the temple holds, every debt that thing has been collecting for nine hundred years." A pause. The whetstone whispered. "Starting with mine."

* * *

They went over the wall at moonrise, Cassian leading them along a drain-shadow he had memorized a lifetime ago, Odile finding servant tunnels beneath it that her body remembered even though her mind had lost the years that taught them. Paloma came last, one hand on Wren's collar, the other fist clenched so the mark's glow wouldn't show — though it was glowing anyway, brighter than she had ever seen it, pulling her forward through the dark like a compass needle made of pain.

Above them, the spire sang. Below them, the crypt waited. Somewhere between, a child was holding a note that held the world together, and she was running out of breath.

Paloma put her foot on the first stair down into the Gallery of Vessels, and the mark flared white, and somewhere far beneath the stone, something ancient and patient began, softly, to laugh.

Down they went.
