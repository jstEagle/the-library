## 27. Into the Palace

The city smelled of tallow and fear.

Cassian had grown up in the capital's shadow — a garrison brat, then a soldier, then a deserter — and he knew its smells the way other men knew their mothers' voices. But tonight the smell was wrong. Underneath the usual reek of smoke and horse and river-mud there was something sweeter, something like lilies left too long in a closed room, and he knew what it was before his mind could name it. It was the scent of the palace on high holy days, when the royal choir sang and the thing behind the king's eyes drank deep.

Coronation eve. The entity's power would crest at midnight like a tide.

"Stop breathing like that," Odile muttered beside him. She stood hip-shot against a wall of the tannery district's last standing building, one eye fixed on him, the other socket a pale crescent of scar. "You breathe like a man about to be sick."

"I am a man about to be sick."

"Good. Means you're honest." She pushed off the wall and slung her coil of rope over her shoulder. "Save it for after."

They gathered in the ruin's back room by the light of a single shuttered lamp: Paloma with her satchel of copied pages pressed flat beneath her ribs; Wren, too thin, too awake, chewing the skin around his thumbnail; Sefa sitting cross-legged on the floor with her hands folded in her lap as though she were waiting for a lesson to begin. The mark on Paloma's palm caught the lamplight when she moved — a black sigil like a treble clef drawn by someone who hated music.

"They've taken her," Paloma said, without preamble. She'd said it four times since dawn, each time as if saying it again might change the shape of the sentence. "The mark pulls toward the palace. Toward the choir hall. She's alive — I can feel that much — but she's *singing*, Cassian. Even now. Even from here." Her voice frayed at the edges. "I can hear her through my own teeth."

"Then we go tonight," Cassian said. "Before midnight. Before they can use her to finish whatever the steward is building."

"And how," Odile said, "does the deserting sergeant propose we walk through three rings of walls, past two thousand guards, into the most guarded room in the kingdom?"

Cassian took out the paper he'd been carrying for six days, soft as cloth now from handling. He'd drawn it in the dark of a safehouse cellar from memory: guard rotations, shift changes, the names of captains who drank and captains who didn't, the gaps where ambition or boredom thinned the watch.

"I served eleven years in this palace," he said. "I know which gates sleep and which ones only pretend to. And I know—" he looked at Odile, and made himself say it plainly, because she deserved plainness, "—that none of it matters if you can't get us off the streets. That part has to be you."

Odile was quiet a long moment. Then she smiled, and it was not a kind smile.

"My brother used to run wine down the servant tunnels under the east terraces," she said. "Back when we were both young and stupid and the crown still paid us. I haven't thought about those tunnels in thirty years. I told you I forgot them." Her jaw tightened. "The storm took more than I admitted. But some things come back when you need them, like a song you swore you'd lost. Give me an hour."

* * *

She found them in forty minutes.

"Underway runs from the old tannery cistern to the palace kitchens' cold stores," she said, unrolling a scrap of leather marked with charcoal lines. "It floods in spring, so we'll be walking in shin-deep water most of the way. There are three grates. Two are rusted shut. The third—" she tapped it, "—my brother bribed a mason to leave loose, thirty years ago. Either it's still loose, or we're swimming back."

"And if the steward knows about them?" Wren asked. His voice cracked on *steward*. He'd been doing that lately, Cassian noticed. Growing up in fast, ugly increments.

"Then we're dead in the dark and no one will ever find our bones," Odile said cheerfully. "Welcome to smuggling."

Sefa spoke for the first time all evening. "I dreamed the water," she said. Everyone went still. The girl rarely spoke of her dreams, and when she did, the air changed pressure, like a room before lightning. "It was warm. And there were candles burning underwater, and a woman singing in a language with no words. She wasn't one of us. She was older." Sefa looked up at Paloma, and her eyes were too calm for a child's. "She said the doors remember being opened kindly."

Nobody asked what that meant. Nobody wanted to.

"We move at dusk," Cassian said, because someone had to, and because if he stopped moving his hands would shake. "Paloma, you carry the rite. Wren stays between me and Odile. Sefa—" he hesitated, hating himself for the hesitation, hating the steward more for making it necessary, "—if anything happens to us, you run. You don't sing, you don't look back. You run until you're past the walls and then you keep running."

"No," Sefa said.

"Sefa—"

"You don't get to spend me and then tell me to save myself." She said it without heat, the way she said everything, and it landed harder than any shout could have. "I'm coming all the way in. I'm the reason they took me. I'm going to be the reason they regret it."

Cassian looked at Paloma. Paloma looked at the floor. In the end it was Odile who shrugged and said, "She's right, sergeant. We've all been spent by better liars than you."

* * *

The cistern mouth opened in the hillside above the river like a rotten tooth, half-swallowed by ivy and older garbage. The water inside was black and smelled of iron and something faintly sweet — that same lily-rot sweetness, stronger here, and Cassian understood with a lurch that the tunnels ran close beneath the choir hall, close enough that the entity's feeding seeped down through the stone like grease through cloth.

They waded in single file. Odile led with a hooded lantern turned down to a coin of light; Cassian came second with his knife drawn, though he couldn't have said what he'd do with it against anything that lived down here. The tunnel swallowed sound. Their footsteps became soft percussions, their breathing became weather. Once, far ahead, something dripped in a rhythm almost like a melody, and Sefa hummed three notes of answer before Wren clapped a hand over her mouth — gently, instinctively, the way you'd quiet a child near a sleeping wolf.

"Sorry," she whispered afterward, genuinely puzzled. "It asked."

"Don't answer things down here," Cassian said. "Nothing down here asks nicely."

The first grate was rusted solid, exactly as promised. The second had been replaced with new iron, bolted from the far side, and they stood before it in silence while everyone privately did the arithmetic of turning back. It was Paloma who put her marked palm flat against the metal and went rigid, listening to something none of them could hear.

"It's not warded," she breathed. "It's *ignored*. The entity doesn't care about these tunnels. Why would it? Nothing down here can hurt it. We're ants walking under a throne."

"That's the best news I've heard all week," Odile said, and shouldered past to the third grate.

It swung open at her touch with a groan that seemed to Cassian loud enough to wake the dead and the worse-than-dead both. Beyond it, stairs climbed into darkness, and the darkness at the top was not entirely dark — it pulsed, faintly, gold, like light through eyelids.

"Cold stores," Odile whispered. "Then the servants' passage along the east terrace. Then—" she drew a breath, "—the gallery above the choir hall. My brother's route, end to end. He used to say the palace had a spine and he knew every vertebra."

"Where is your brother now?" Wren asked, and immediately winced.

"Dead," Odile said. "By my hand, indirectly, by my silence, directly. Walk."

* * *

The cold stores were hung with carcasses and wheels of cheese, and the sweet rot-smell was thick enough to taste. They crossed it single file, past a sleeping kitchen boy whom Odile considered, briefly, with a professional eye, and decided not to rob. The servants' passage beyond was narrow and lined with peeling gilt — even the hidden corridors of this place were gilded, Cassian thought, everything in this kingdom wearing paint over rot — and through hairline cracks in the wall they could hear the palace above them: boots, bells, the endless rehearsal drone of the royal choir warming up for tomorrow's coronation.

Except it wasn't rehearsal. Cassian stopped beneath one of the cracks and listened, and his blood went cold.

They were singing the binding.

He knew it now — Paloma had made them learn it, note by note, over weeks of terrible practice in the rebel safehouse, Wren flubbing his line and Odile complaining that keening was not a skill one *learned*. The enslaved choir above was singing the First Voice's melody, the anchoring phrase, but wrong — stretched, dragged, sung by throats that did not want to sing it, and the wrongness had a texture he could feel in his fillings. It was like hearing a prayer recited by a man who'd never believed in anything.

"They're practicing with stolen voices," Paloma whispered. Her face in the dark was gray. "The steward doesn't need Sefa for the melody. He needs her for the *willingness*. A thousand forced voices make the cage, but it won't hold unless one voice enters freely. That's why they took her. That's why they haven't finished." Her marked hand curled into a fist. "They're waiting for coronation night. Midnight. When it's strongest. They'll force her consent somehow, or wear her down until she breaks, or—"

"Or she'll give it," Sefa said quietly, "to save the others. If they threaten the choir. If they threaten *you*." She looked up at Paloma with that terrible calm. "You know that's what I'd do."

"Then we make sure it never comes to that," Cassian said, harder than he meant to. "Move."

* * *

The gallery above the choir hall was a long balcony of white stone behind a screen of carved wooden latticework — built, Cassian remembered from his garrison years, so that the royal family could attend services unseen. Kings had watched their choirs from here for nine hundred years. He wondered, with a nausea that felt like vertigo, how many of those kings had been watching, and how many had been *watched through*.

Through the lattice, the choir hall spread below like the inside of a bell.

A thousand candles. Tiered stalls of dark wood. And the singers — gods, the singers. Cassian had expected dozens. There were hundreds, packed into the stalls and spilling onto benches brought in for the occasion, men and women and children in the grey smocks of crown wards, their faces slack, their mouths opening and closing in unison. The sound they made filled the hall the way water fills a drowned church, and threaded through it, unmistakable, small and clear and horribly brave, came Sefa's voice from the head of the first stall — chained, he could see even from here, a chain of dull silver running from her throat-collar back into the dark behind the choir.

And at the far end, on a platform before the empty throne, stood the steward.

Even at this distance Cassian felt the wrongness of him. The man was tall and dry as a winter stalk, and he conducted the choir with both hands raised, and his shadow on the candlelit wall did not match his movements. It moved a half-beat late. Sometimes it moved when he did not.

"There," Odile breathed. "Behind the third pillar on the gallery stair — the guard post. Two men, changing at the bell." She glanced at Cassian. "Your rotations, sergeant. Tell me the bell's about to ring."

Cassian counted in his head, counting backward from the cathedral bells he'd lived under for eleven years, and his mouth had gone dry as paper. "Forty seconds," he said. "When it rings, they'll turn to the rail. Old habit — you watch the crowd on feast nights. Forty seconds, maybe fifty, and then the relief comes up the stair and we're trapped on this balcony with nowhere to go but down into the choir."

"Then in forty seconds," Paloma said, "we go down into the choir."

Below, the false binding swelled toward its cadence. The steward's mismatched shadow spread its arms wide. And in the gallery's shadows, five people who had no business being alive, let alone here — a deserter, a ferrywoman, a scribe, a boy, and a god-touched child in chains — waited for a bell.

It rang.

Cassian moved.
