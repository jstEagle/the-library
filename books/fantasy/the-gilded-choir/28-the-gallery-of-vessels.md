## 28. The Gallery of Vessels

The servant tunnel Odile remembered — or half-remembered; the un-song had taken pieces of her like a dog taking scraps from a table — smelled of river rot and old candle smoke. They moved in single file through a passage so narrow that Paloma's shoulders brushed both walls at once, her palm pressed flat against stone that wept cold. Above them, muffled by three floors of palace, the coronation-eve festivities had already begun. She could hear it as a heartbeat heard through a wall: drums, laughter, the swell of a hundred voices singing something bright and rehearsed.

*They don't know what they're singing for,* she thought, and then corrected herself. *Some of them do. Some of them are singing because if they stop, they die.*

Cassian moved ahead of her with the certainty of a man walking a route he'd memorized in another life. Twice he stopped, held up a fist, and they waited in the dark while boots passed overhead — she could see the tremor of torchlight through gaps between flagstones, thin gold lines that crawled across Odile's face and made her one eye gleam like a coin.

"Left here," Cassian whispered. "Then up. The gallery runs behind the throne room. If the steward keeps his rite where I think he does—"

"If," Odile said.

"Then we come at it from behind."

Wren pressed close against Paloma's back. He hadn't spoken since the tannery district, since Sefa had been torn out of their hands by men who moved with the smooth, unhurried confidence of people who had never once been told no. But his hand found the hem of her sleeve now and gripped it, and that was its own kind of sentence.

*We'll get her back.* She didn't say it aloud. In these tunnels, promises felt like debts, and she had sworn off making debts she couldn't tally.

The passage ended at a door so old its hinges had been replaced twice — she could tell by the bright new iron set into wood gone black with age. Cassian lifted the latch a finger's width. Cold air spilled through, smelling of dust and lamp oil and, beneath it, something else. Something sweetish and wrong, like fruit rotting in a locked room.

Paloma's mark woke.

It had been quiet since the underways — a dull heat in her palm, an ache like a healing burn. Now it flared, and with it came the song: faint, distant, threaded through the walls themselves, humming in the mortar the way water hums in pipes. The entity was awake above them. It was *listening* for something.

For someone.

"Cassian," she breathed. "It knows we're in the building."

"It knew before we were in the building." He said it flatly, without looking back. "Question is whether it cares. Move."

* * *

The door opened onto a corridor that should not have existed.

Paloma had copied floor plans once — a punishment detail, transcribing the palace surveyor's ledgers for a provost who wanted leverage over the crown architect. She knew the palace the way she knew any text: thoroughly, and from the outside. This corridor appeared on none of those pages. It ran straight and high-vaulted where the surveyor's ink said there should be solid rock, and it was lit by lamps that burned without flame — the same pale, cold light she'd first seen on the road beyond the pass, magic worn casually, architecturally, like plasterwork.

And the walls were covered in faces.

Portraits. Hundreds of them. They rose from wainscot height to the vaulting, frame pressed against frame, oil on wood and silk and vellum, some cracked with age, some so fresh the paint still shone. Kings in coronation robes. Queens with scepters. Generals, abbots, a high priestess whose mitre towered over her stern gray face. Every one of them painted alone. Every one of them painted facing outward, eyes meeting the viewer's, mouths closed or slightly parted —

— or open. Singing.

"Oh," Odile said softly. Her hand had gone to the knife at her belt without her noticing; Paloma watched her knuckles whiten around the grip. "Oh, I know this place. I carried candles down here. When I was a girl in service. They told us it was the kings' memorial hall. We weren't allowed to look at the faces."

"Why not?" Wren whispered.

"Because the faces look back."

He laughed — a small, terrified sound — and then stopped laughing, because the nearest portrait did seem to follow him with its eyes. Paint could not do that. Paint did not do that. And yet.

Paloma walked the gallery slowly, reading it the way she read everything: left to right, top to bottom, oldest to newest. That was the trick of it, she realized. The frames were arranged chronologically. The earliest portraits were crude, almost primitive — pigments ground from earth and ash, faces more suggestion than likeness. But even in those, even four or five centuries back, there was something in the eyes. A stillness too complete. A patience.

She began to count reigns. She lost track past thirty.

"Dates," Cassian murmured beside her. He'd seen it too; of course he had. "Look at the dates under the names. There's never more than forty years between them. Sometimes less. Sometimes—" He stopped at a portrait of a young king, handsome, hollow-eyed, the plaque beneath reading only *Aldric the Second, Beloved of the Choir*. "Sometimes the vessel doesn't last long."

"The entity burns through them," Paloma said. Her voice sounded far away to herself. "Like candles."

"Not burns." Odile had drifted to the far wall, to a cluster of portraits from what looked like two centuries past. Her voice came out strange, scraped thin. "Wears. My brother used to say — when it talked through him, near the end — it used to say it had worn many coats. That it chose them carefully. That it liked—" She stopped. Swallowed. "It liked bloodlines. Said a coat wears better if it's been cut to the pattern."

Paloma turned to ask her what she meant, and saw the portrait instead.

It hung at eye level in a niche of its own, apart from the crowded ranks of kings — given space, given honor, given a lamp of its own. A woman in temple gray. Scribe's ink stains on her fingers, rendered lovingly, precisely, by a painter who had known those hands. Dark hair pinned back. A mouth set in the particular line of someone holding back words she could not afford to say.

Paloma knew that mouth. She shaved around it every morning. She bit it when she lied and worried it when she tallied sums that would never balance.

She knew the small scar on the chin, too. She'd gotten hers falling from the temple library ladder at nine years old. The woman in the painting wore hers like an old companion.

"No," she said.

The plaque beneath read: *Sister Mereth Thackeray, Keeper of Hours. Gave her voice gladly.*

"That's not possible." Wren had come up beside her, and his face, when she glanced at it, was white as the lamp-glow. "That's — Paloma, that's your—"

"I know whose face it is." Her own voice was very calm. Calm the way ice is calm. "It's mine."

* * *

She did not remember deciding to walk further down the gallery. Her feet simply went, and the others followed, and the mark in her palm sang louder with every step, and the portraits kept coming.

Thackeray. The name recurred the way a refrain recurs. *Brother Aldous Thackeray, First Cantor. Sister Liria Thackeray, Keeper of Hours. Magister Corwin Thackeray, Who Understood.* Interspersed among them, other names, other lines — but always circling back, generation after generation, century after century, to her family's name written in gold leaf on plaques in a corridor that did not exist on any map.

Kings were vessels. She had understood that since the capital. The king wore the crown; the thing wore the king. Fine. Horrible, but comprehensible — the way a plague is comprehensible.

But the gallery told a different story. The kings were the *coat*, yes. And the tailors — the keepers, the cantors, the scribes, the ones who wrote the bindings and renewed the bindings and taught each generation how to be worn — the tailors were Thackerays.

"We didn't fight it," she heard herself say. She had stopped in front of a triptych: three Thackeray women across three generations, painted in profile, all bent over writing desks, all with the same ink-stained fingers. "We built it. We maintained it. My family — the binding, the wards, the valley — we weren't the jailers' descendants. We were the jailers. We were the *wardens*. We kept the cage oiled. We kept the prisoner fed."

"And got painted for it," Cassian said quietly. "Honored. Given lamps."

"Gave their voices gladly." She touched the plaque under Sister Mereth's portrait. The gilt letters were warm. Not sun-warm. *Body*-warm, like skin. She snatched her hand back. "What does that mean? What does *gladly* mean? Did they choose it? Did they know what they were—"

"Paloma." Wren's voice, very small. "Look at the last ones."

The most recent portraits hung at the gallery's end, nearest the door that must open onto the throne room antechamber — she could hear the music clearly now, could feel it in her teeth. Five frames. She made herself look at them one at a time, the way she'd been taught to approach a difficult ledger: line by line, no flinching.

First: a broad-shouldered man in ferryman's clothes, one-eyed, his remaining eye fierce with a grief so fresh the painter had caught it wet. No name. Only: *Who Refused.*

Odile made a sound like she'd been struck.

Second: a young woman in rebel colors, jaw set, throat bared. *Who Sang Alone.*

Third: a soldier, desertion's brand visible on his cheekbone even in paint. *Who Came Back.*

Fourth: a boy of perhaps twelve, freckled, frightened, furious. *Who Would Not Be Quiet.*

Wren stared at his own face for a long time. Then he said, in a voice with nothing childlike left in it: "It's already painted me."

"It paints everyone," Paloma said. "It's painted everyone for nine hundred years. It paints you *before*—"

Fifth frame.

She had known, in the way you know the last line of a sum before you finish carrying the digits. She had known since the plaque under Sister Mereth. Even so, her body tried to refuse it: her vision swam, her knees loosened, the mark in her palm blazed so hot she gasped.

The fifth portrait was unfinished. The canvas showed a woman in temple gray, ink-stained fingers, dark hair coming loose from its pin — but the face was bare primer, and the mouth was a smudge of unplaced color, and the painter had not yet decided whether her lips were closed or open. Whether she was silent or singing.

Whether she gave her voice gladly.

Beneath it, the plaque was blank. Waiting. As if the gilt itself were holding its breath.

* * *

"Paloma." Cassian's hand on her shoulder, steady, warm, human. "Paloma. Breathe."

"I'm breathing."

"You're not. Look at me. Breathe."

She breathed. The gallery steadied around her — the lamps, the faces, the hundreds of eyes that did not quite follow her but did not quite not. Somewhere beyond the far door, the music swelled toward some ceremonial peak, and beneath it, so low only she and Wren and maybe Sefa could ever have heard it, the entity's true song pulsed through the stone like blood through a heart.

"It bred us," she said. "Do you understand? The debt. The indenture. The provost's letter — my family *bred* for this role, the letter said, and I thought it meant the binding, the rite, the writing. But it's this. It's the whole gallery. Nine hundred years of Thackerays keeping the cage, renewing the binding, feeding it singers — and being *honored* for it. Portraits. Lamps. 'Gave her voice gladly.' It's not a prison, Cassian. It's a *family business*."

"Then quit the family business."

She laughed — a broken, ugly sound — and turned on him, and the fury surprised her with its size. "Quit? You think it's a wage? It wrote my grandmother's grandmother's grandmother into the walls. It has been *patient* with my bloodline for longer than the valley has had a name. Everything I am — the copying, the counting, the way I can't stop reading a text once I've started, the way I *heard* it the first night and knew the melody — that's not me. That's breeding. That's a trap built so slowly that the rat thinks the walls are home."

The words rang in the vaulted dark. Wren had gone very still. Odile stood with her back to her own refused portrait, as if she could hold it against the wall by the weight of her spine.

Cassian took it without flinching. He always took things without flinching; she was beginning to understand that this was not courage but practice, years of it, the hardening of a man who had learned that flinching got people killed.

"Maybe," he said. "Maybe everything you are is a leash. Here's what I know about leashes, Paloma — I wore one for eleven years. The crown had my oath, my wages, my sister's safety. Every choice I made inside it, I told myself was mine. It wasn't. And then one night I walked out anyway, knowing they'd hunt me, knowing the brand would mark me forever — and that *was* mine. The leash doesn't get to decide whether you can leave. It only decides what leaving costs."

"And what does leaving cost here?" She gestured at the blank plaque, the unfinished face. "You've seen the chapel walls. The binding needs a First Voice. A life freely given. Every First Voice in that record died, and now I find my ancestors' faces on this wall, *giving their voices gladly*, and you want to tell me about cost?"

"Yes," Cassian said. "Exactly that. Because there's a difference between a cost you choose and a cost that's chosen for you. Your whole life, Paloma, other people have held your ledger. The temple. The provost. The steward. That thing." He nodded upward, toward the music, the throne room, the hunger. "This is the first time anyone's ever handed you the pen."

She looked at the unfinished portrait. At the mouth the painter had not yet decided.

And the mark in her palm answered — not with pain this time, but with a whisper, intimate as breath against her ear, wearing a voice like her own:

*You could stop the painter's hand. Wear the crown. Keep them safe forever. All you have to do is stop singing.*

"No," she said aloud, to the empty air, to the thing listening through the stone.

Down the gallery, every lamp flickered at once.

And beyond the far door, the ceremonial music stopped mid-phrase — cut clean, like a throat — and in the ringing silence that followed, Paloma heard, clear and small and terribly brave, a child's voice begin to sing.

*Sefa.*

"They're starting the rite," Cassian said. "Now. Tonight. Come on — *come on!*"

He wrenched the far door open onto golden light, and the choir of the dead pulled Paloma Thackeray forward into the throne room, toward the song.
