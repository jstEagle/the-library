## 29. Sefa's Song

Sefa had learned the song before she learned her own name.

That was what the crown's hunters never understood, when they came with their iron collars and their ledgers of the god-touched. They thought the melody was something she'd caught, like a fever, like a curse passed in a market crowd. But it was older than fever. Older than names. It lived in her the way breath lived in her — not chosen, not wanted, simply *there*, rising and falling beneath her ribs since before memory.

She had hated it for most of her life. She had sung it into wells to hear it echo back smaller. She had hummed it under the sound of cartwheels and rain so no one would know she carried it. And still they came for her, because the ones who hunted could always hear it, even whispered, even drowned.

Now, chained at the head of the royal choir, she sang it full-voiced for the first time in her life, and it was the most beautiful thing she had ever done, and she wanted to die rather than be doing it.

* * *

They had brought her up through the palace's bones in the last hour before midnight, on coronation eve, when the whole gilded city held its breath. The chains were new — pale metal, cold as riverbed stones, etched with the same script that crawled across the steward's rings. They did not weigh much. That was the cruelty of them. They weighed exactly enough to remind her they were there, and not one ounce more, as if whoever forged them had measured the precise burden a child could carry without stumbling.

The throne room opened around her like the inside of a bell.

Sefa had never seen a space built for sound before. The temple in the valley had been built for silence — thick walls, low ceilings, everything padded and muffled, the world kept at arm's length. This was its opposite. The ceiling soared up into painted dark where golden figures played instruments no one alive had ever held. The walls were ribbed with arches, each one tuned — she could feel it, the way you feel a wrong note in your teeth — each arch shaped to catch a particular voice and carry it outward, multiplying it, giving it back larger than it went in.

A room made to make one voice into many. A room made to make many voices into one.

She understood, then, why they had taken her here instead of killing her. You did not kill a tuning fork. You used it.

The royal choir stood in their crescent below the dais, two hundred singers in white and gold, and every one of them wore the hollow look she knew from her own reflection in the worst years. Their throats moved when the handlers gestured. Their eyes stayed somewhere far away, in whatever small country the mind retreats to when the body is owned. Some of them were old. One, near the crescent's end, looked barely older than Wren.

And at the center of the dais, robed in midnight blue stitched with constellations that had gone out a year ago, stood the steward.

He was not what she expected. She had imagined him vast, monstrous, a thing too large for its skin. Instead he was a thin man with kind lines around his mouth, the sort of face that belonged to someone who had once been gentle, and that was worse. When he smiled at her, the smile reached all the way to his eyes, and behind his eyes there was nothing at all — just depth, just distance, just the long fall of nine hundred years.

"Little First Voice," he said, and his voice was his own and also not, layered, like one singer standing in front of another standing in front of another, going back and back into the dark. "Do you know how long I have listened for you?"

"I'm not a First Voice," Sefa said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "I'm just a girl who can't stop humming."

"That is all any of us are." He gestured, and the handlers tightened the chains, drawing her arms out from her body, arranging her at the apex of the choir's crescent like the point of a compass. "The song needs a beginning. It has been waiting nine centuries for a beginning it didn't have to steal."

"You stole plenty," Sefa said. "Paloma told me about the chapel. About the Choir that bound it the first time. They sang *willingly*. That was the whole point. Willing voices, freely given." She lifted her chin. "You've got two hundred people down there who'd stop singing if you cut their throats first."

Something flickered across the steward's face — grief, maybe, or the memory of grief, worn smooth as a river stone by nine hundred years of handling.

"Yes," he said softly. "I know better than anyone what unwillingness does to the rite. I was there when it failed the first time." He turned away from her, toward the choir, and raised one hand. "But unwillingness can be drowned, little fork. Drowned in enough voice. Two hundred tonight. Thousands, if the city wakes wrong. The song doesn't care how it's fed. Only that it's fed."

He lowered his hand.

And the royal choir began to sing.

* * *

It was not the entity's song. Not at first.

What rose from the crescent was a coronation hymn, old and formal, the kind of music built to flatter power — broad chords, obedient harmonies, every voice moving in lockstep like soldiers on parade. Sefa had heard hymns like it through temple walls. Music that existed to say *yes*. Yes to the king, yes to the order of things, yes to the cage.

But the room was built to multiply what entered it, and the hymn entered the arches and came back changed. Each pass through the vaulted dark stripped something from it — the formality first, then the obedience, then the words themselves, until what remained was only structure, only harmony, only the naked architecture of many voices agreeing.

And into that agreement, the other song began to pour.

Sefa felt it happen the way you feel water finding a crack in a wall. The hymn's chord progressions widened, almost imperceptibly, half-steps sliding where no half-steps belonged. The tempo breathed. The unison frayed into intervals that no conservatory taught — intervals that hurt beautifully, that sounded like the moment before crying, like the pause between lightning and thunder stretched out and made audible.

The singers felt it too. She watched it move through them like wind through wheat. Shoulders stiffened. Eyes came back from far away, wide with horror, mouths still moving, still producing the notes their captors' gestures demanded — but now the notes were *changing*, bending toward the melody that lived in Sefa's chest, and their bodies were betraying them into harmony with it.

"No," whispered the young one at the crescent's end, between phrases. His voice cracked on it. His mouth kept singing anyway. "*No — please—*"

The room gave his plea back to him multiplied, gorgeous, ruined.

Because that was the terrible arithmetic of it, Sefa realized. The room didn't care what the voices meant. Grief, terror, resistance — pour anything into those arches and it came out beautiful. The enslaved choir's horror harmonized with the entity's hunger as cleanly as joy would have. There was no wrong note available to them. Their captivity itself had become an instrument.

The false binding didn't need willing voices. It needed only *enough* voice, and the will would be worn away like a coastline, note by note, until nothing was left that remembered refusing.

The chains at her wrists grew warm.

* * *

Here is what no one had told Sefa, because no one who wasn't god-touched could have known it: the song was not hungry the way the stories said. Hunger was too simple, too human. What stirred beneath the hymn's dissolving harmonies was lonelier than hunger. It was the loneliness of a thing that had been singing alone in the dark since before the valley had a name, hearing, at last, an answer.

It loved the music. That was the horror underneath all the other horrors. It genuinely, achingly loved the music, the way a drowning man loves air, and it could not tell the difference between a song given and a song taken, between a choir and a cage, because to it those had always been the same word.

Sefa felt its attention turn toward her like a lighthouse beam sweeping a dark sea.

*There,* it said, without words, in the register below hearing where her own melody lived. *There you are. I wrote you. Do you know that? Nine hundred years ago I pressed my ear against the wall of the world, and your line heard me listening, and ever since, your blood has been my echo.*

"I'm not your echo," Sefa said aloud, and the room took her defiance and multiplied it into something that made the nearest handlers stagger, hands over ears, weeping without knowing why.

*You sing me every day,* the presence said, gentle, reasonable, infinitely patient. *In the well. Under the cartwheels. In your sleep, walking, waking. You have carried me more faithfully than any vessel I have ever worn. The scribe-child fights me. The deserter bleeds for strangers. The ferrywoman has forgotten her own brother's name. But you — you and I have been duetting since before you could talk.*

It was true. That was the trap inside the trap. Every hour she had spent hating the melody had been an hour spent keeping it alive; her hatred was a kind of attention, and attention was food. She had fed it her whole childhood without knowing.

The chains were hot now. The gold on the walls seemed to breathe. Above, in the painted dark, the golden musicians had begun — she was almost sure, though she refused to trust her eyes — to shift in their frozen poses, instruments rising, mouths opening, the ceiling itself rehearsing.

Below the dais, the royal choir's hymn had fully dissolved. Two hundred voices now sang the entity's melody in perfect, involuntary unison, and the sound filled the bell of the room and poured out through the high windows into the coronation-eve night, over the sleeping city, and Sefa knew — she could feel it in her teeth, in the marrow-deep place where the song lived — that somewhere out in that darkness, thousands of sleepers were beginning to hum in their beds.

The false binding was not waiting for the rite to complete. It had already begun. The city itself was becoming the choir.

*Sing with me,* said the presence, and for the first time it sounded less like a god and more like something begging. *I am so tired of singing alone.*

And Sefa, chained at the head of two hundred stolen voices, with the melody rising in her chest like floodwater, understood suddenly and completely what Paloma had tried to explain in the safehouse, what the chapel walls had meant, what the whole terrible year had been building toward:

The song could not be silenced. It could only be answered.

Somewhere above, in the gallery's shadowed curve, a door eased open — and Sefa, singing, weeping, heard five sets of footsteps trying very hard to be quiet, and one familiar uncertain tenor breathing hard with fear.

They had come. All of them. Into the bell of the room, at the peak of its power, with no plan left but each other.

The entity felt them too. Its attention swung upward, vast and delighted, like a host turning to greet guests finally arrived at a feast prepared for centuries.

*Ah,* it sang, through two hundred throats and the throat of the thin man with the kind mouth, filling the gilded dark from floor to painted heaven. *At last. Everyone is here.*
