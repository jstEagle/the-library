## 30. Counterpoint

**Paloma**

The gallery smelled of dust and old gilt and, beneath it, something sweeter and far worse — the smell of a voice being spent like coin.

Paloma pressed her palms to the balustrade and looked down into the throne room, and what she saw there she knew she would see every time she closed her eyes for the rest of her life, however long that life proved to be.

The royal choir stood in tiers before the dais, two hundred singers in white robes gone grey at the hems, their mouths open in perfect unison. No conductor stood before them. They did not need one. The melody pouring out of them rose in slow, honeyed spirals toward the vaulted ceiling, and where it touched the stone the gold leaf darkened, drinking it in. At the choir's head, chained at the throat and wrists to a pillar of black iron, Sefa sang too — her small voice threaded through theirs like a silver wire through rope — and her eyes were open, and they were fixed on the gallery, on Paloma, and she was not singing because she wanted to.

*Hold on*, Paloma thought at her, uselessly, the way you think at someone across a river. *Hold on, we're coming.*

Beside her, Cassian checked the edge of his knife with his thumb and said nothing, because there was nothing left to say. Below them Odile crouched at the gallery stair with her one eye closed, listening the way other people prayed. And Wren — Wren had Sefa's melody written on his forearm in ink he'd copied from the chapel walls, and he kept touching it as though checking a pulse.

"The false binding's already begun," Paloma whispered. "Look at the floor."

She saw it clearly now, with the mark on her palm burning like a coal: lines of light crawling out from the dais in widening rings, each ring inscribed with script so old it predated the valley, predated the wards, predated the lie her family had been bred to keep. The rings were closing inward. When the last of them reached the throne, the entity would be bound again — but this time fed by two hundred harvested voices and whatever remained of a god-touched child, and this time the binding would be permanent, and this time the cage would be the whole world.

The steward stood beside the throne with his hands folded, patient as a man waiting for bread to rise. He did not look up at the gallery. He didn't need to. He knew they were there. He had known since the moment they entered the underways; perhaps since the moment Paloma was born.

"It has to be now," Cassian breathed. "While it's focused on the rite. While its attention is *down there*."

Paloma looked down at the mark on her palm — the entity's signature, burned into her by her own refusal, the wound that let her hear its song in everything. She had hated it. She had cursed it in three languages. But it was also, she understood suddenly, an ear turned the other way. It heard the entity. And if she could hear it —

"Then it can hear us," she said.

She unrolled the page she had copied from the buried chapel wall, the counter-melody the original Choir had sung against the thing's hunger-song, and laid it flat on the balustrade where all four of them could see it. Her handwriting. Her line. The descant she'd once transcribed in secret and hummed into her pillow for years without knowing why.

"We don't fight it," she said. "We answer it."

Odile opened her eye. Wren swallowed hard and nodded. Cassian — who could not read music, who had never in his life sung anything but marching songs and tavern rounds — set his jaw like a man setting a shield.

"On my count," Paloma said, and took the deepest breath of her life.

**Cassian**

Cassian had held bridges before. He had held a ford at Marrow Creek with eleven men against sixty, and he had held a gatehouse for six hours with a broken arm, and he had thought he understood what holding meant.

He understood better now.

The first note left his chest and felt like tearing. His voice was a bass note, low and steady, the anchor-line Paloma had assigned him — *"You don't have to be good, you just have to be constant"* — and for eight bars it was. Eight bars of the counter-melody rolling out over the throne room, joining Paloma's descant and Odile's keening alto and Wren's thin, wavering tenor, four voices from a dusty gallery falling against two hundred enslaved ones like a hand against a door.

And then the entity noticed them.

It did not stop singing. That was the horror of it — the choir below never faltered, never missed a beat, while the thing behind the king's eyes simply *turned*, the way a great fish turns in deep water, and looked at them through Aldric's lifted face. The king's body rose from the throne like a puppet remembering it had strings. Its mouth opened, and what came out was still beautiful, and it was aimed directly at Cassian.

He knew the song immediately. That was the trap of it — no time to brace, no warning, just the melody arriving inside his skull already half-sung, in a voice he had spent nine years trying to forget.

*Sergeant Vey, Sergeant Vey, the bridge is lost, the bridge is lost—*

Marrow Creek. Not the battle — the after. The mud, and the men in it, and the boy — Tomas, nineteen, from the orchard country, who had grabbed Cassian's sleeve as the water came up and asked him, *Sergeant, do we go back?* And Cassian had said yes, because the order said yes, and Tomas had gone back into the water and the water had kept him.

The entity sang it back to him with every detail intact. The cold. The taste of the river. The sound Tomas made, which Cassian had never told anyone, which he had never told *himself*, which he had buried so deep he'd genuinely believed it was gone — and here it was, excavated, polished, offered back to him in perfect pitch as the counter-melody wavered in his throat.

His note slipped. Just a fraction. But in a rite built on harmony, a fraction was a breach, and he felt the melody pour through the gap like water through a cracked dike, pulling at him, offering him the old arithmetic: *you couldn't have saved him, you can't save any of them, put the voice down, put it down, the water is quiet, the water doesn't ask anything of you—*

"*Cassian.*"

Paloma's voice, cutting across the entity's like a blade through silk. She wasn't even looking at him — she was reading her line, eyes on the page, one hand stretched out behind her without looking, palm open, and he understood. She couldn't hold his part for him. Nobody could. But she could remind him it existed.

He thought: *Tomas asked me a question and I answered it wrong. I've been answering it wrong for nine years.*

He thought: *Not tonight.*

Cassian planted his feet the way he'd plant them on a bridge under assault, gripped the balustrade until the gilt flaked off under his fingers, and drove the bass note back up from somewhere below his ribs, below his fear, below the river itself — a note like a foundation stone, like a wall, like a man saying *no* in the only language that had ever mattered.

The entity's song broke against it and recoiled.

Below, on the dais, the king's head tilted. Somewhere inside the ancient thing wearing him, Cassian felt certain, something recalculated.

**Odile**

Odile had made her bargain with grief a long time ago, and the terms were simple: she carried it, it didn't drown her, and neither of them spoke about her brother.

The entity broke those terms in the first verse.

It didn't come at her with the night she'd betrayed Perrin — she had expected that, had braced for it, had almost *wanted* it, because that wound was cauterized and familiar. Instead it went deeper. It went to before. It sang the day Perrin first hummed the melody, the two of them mending nets on the ferry dock, his voice catching on the strange rising phrase he'd learned from nowhere, and Odile laughing and telling him he sounded like a gull with a fishbone stuck in its throat — and Perrin laughing back, and the sun on the water, and neither of them knowing yet, either of them, what was already coiling toward him through the world's long dark.

It sang the ordinary happiness. That was the weapon. Anyone could twist a wound; it took an ancient and patient cruelty to sing a person their joy, intact, complete, with the ending cut off — so that the memory hung there forever on the edge of its own catastrophe, and the listener stood inside it knowing exactly what came next, unable to warn him, unable to look away, unable to stop loving him.

Her alto faltered. Slid. Broke.

The counter-melody thinned to three voices, and Paloma's descant bent to cover the gap and couldn't quite, and below them the rings of light on the floor crept another arm's length inward, and Sefa's voice in the chains cracked — just once, just slightly — and Odile heard it and it was like hearing Perrin.

*You promised,* she told herself. *You stood in the ash of forty-one graves and you made the girl promise not to be you.*

But knowing your cowardice, she discovered, and refusing it were different countries, and the road between them ran straight through the middle of her brother's laugh on a sunny dock, and the entity stood on that road singing it in rounds.

Then a small hand found hers.

Wren. The boy had shifted along the balustrade without breaking his own tenor line — she didn't know how, the child sang like he breathed, like the music was a place he lived and the rest of them were only visiting — and now his fingers were laced through hers, and he was looking at her, and he was singing *at* her, shaping the words of the counter-melody so she could read them on his lips:

*What was given may be given back. What was sung may be unsung.*

The chapel words. The hope with a hook in it. But Wren's face held no hooks, only a fierce, uncomplicated certainty that Odile had not possessed since she was younger than he was, and she thought: *he believes it. Let me borrow it. Just for tonight. I'll pay him back in graves dug and miles walked and every fierce unsentimental thing I know how to do.*

Odile opened her eye. She let the memory of the dock stand in front of her — all of it, the sun, the nets, the laugh — and instead of fleeing it she stepped forward into it and *sang it back*, alto rising, taking her brother's melody out of the entity's mouth and claiming it, making it hers, giving it the ending the thing had cut away: *and I will carry him to the coast, and I will find what it took from him, and I will not stop, and that is my line, and I am singing it.*

Her voice came back like a tide.

On the dais, the light-rings shuddered.

**Wren**

Wren had always known he was a coward about some things — the dark under beds, the provost's cane, saying the true thing out loud. But standing in the gallery with the entity's attention swinging toward him like a searchlight, he discovered the strange, clean fact that none of those fears were available anymore. There wasn't room. He was holding a tenor line, and Sefa was down there in the chains, and the line was the only rope between her and the dark, and cowards could not afford to drop ropes.

So when the entity sang at him, he was almost ready.

Almost.

It didn't use a memory. That was what undid him — he'd braced for the temple, for the night the soldiers came, for the sound of the archive doors being broken. Instead the entity simply *stopped*, for him alone, and let him hear the silence underneath all songs: the silence of the space between stars, vast and patient and absolutely indifferent, the silence that had been there before anything learned to sing and would be there after, and inside that silence a voice like dry paper said:

*Little echo. You carry a dead choir's notes in your skin. Do you think they chose you? You are a copy of a copy of a copy. When this is done there will be no one left who remembers the original, and you will finally understand what you have always been: nothing, humming.*

Wren's voice shook. The tenor line wobbled like a candle flame.

And then — and he would never be able to explain this, not to Paloma, not to himself, not in all the years of telling the story afterward by firelight — Wren found that he wasn't afraid of the silence. Because he had *lived* next to silence his whole life. He had grown up in the Temple of Quiet Hours, where silence was a discipline and a wall, and he had spent every year of it pressing his ear against that wall and hearing, faint and far and real, the flicker of a star going out.

Silence was not empty. Silence was where you listened.

"You're wrong," Wren said aloud, mid-phrase, breaking every rule of the rite, and sang the next bar anyway — clear, uncertain, unbroken. "I'm not a copy. I'm a *chorus*. Everybody I've ever loved is in here."

And he sang louder.

Something in the entity's vast attention flinched. Wren felt it flinch, felt it the way you feel a door slam in a house you're standing in, and he gripped Odile's hand harder and held the line, held it, held it, a boy of twelve years holding a note against a thing older than stars, and the note held.

**Paloma**

They were losing anyway.

Paloma could feel it in the mathematics of the rite, in the geometry of the light-rings crawling across the floor, in the drag of exhaustion pulling at every voice in her little choir. Four singers against an ancient hunger with two hundred throats and a child in chains. The counter-melody was working — the rings had stopped closing, the entity's song had frayed at its edges, the king's vessel staggered twice on the dais like a man dizzy — but working was not winning, and they had minutes, maybe less, before the entity stopped playing fair and simply crushed them.

The mark on her palm was screaming. Through it she could feel the entity's whole attention now, and beneath its fury she felt something worse: *amusement*. It was enjoying this. It had worn vessels for nine hundred years; it had eaten choirs before; it knew every shape this night could take, and in every one of them, it believed, it won.

Unless.

Paloma stared down at the steward, standing calm beside the throne, hands folded, watching the rite proceed with the satisfaction of a gardener watching rain. The entity moved through the king, fought through the king, sang through the king — but the *rite* belonged to the steward. The false binding was his design, his patience, his life's monstrous work. And a rite, Paloma knew — she was a scribe, she had copied ten thousand contracts, she knew what bound things together better than anyone alive — a rite was only a kind of contract.

And every contract had a name at the bottom of it.

The chapel walls had given them the counter-melody. But the walls had given more than that — pages and pages of names, the roster of the original Choir, every singer who had stood in the buried chapel and given their voice. Paloma had copied them all in her careful scribe's hand, and one name she had copied twice, checking it, disbelieving it, because it appeared both in the choir roster *and* in the record of the entity's first vessel, and the two records were separated by four hundred years, and the name was the same.

The steward had been the entity's first vessel. Before the kings. Before the crown. A singer, once — the chapel walls said so — a First Voice who had broken, who had bargained, who had traded his choir for centuries of hollow service. Every horror in this room had begun with him. And the entity wore him still, the way a favorite coat is worn, oldest and closest to the skin.

If the coat could be torn away—

"Paloma." Cassian's voice, ragged. "The rings are moving again. Whatever you're thinking—"

"I need thirty seconds," she said. "Everyone. Give me everything you have left — not at the entity. At the *steward*. Sing *at* him. Make him listen."

Odile's eye narrowed; she understood first, because Odile always understood the shape of a betrayal. "You're going to call him out."

"I'm going to call him *home*," Paloma said, and flipped to the last page of her transcription, to the name she had copied twice, and drew breath enough to crack stone.

Below them, the entity sensed the shift and threw everything it had into the song — the choir's volume swelling, the rings blazing, Sefa crying out as the chains drank deeper — and into that flood, small and mortal and absolutely deliberate, Paloma Thackeray began to sing a single name.

Nine hundred years old. Buried under four kings and a stolen valley and a ledger of debts.

A name that no one living had spoken aloud.

The steward's head turned.

For the first time in nine hundred years, the thing's oldest servant *looked* — truly looked, with whatever was left of the man inside — up at the gallery, at the scribe with the page in her hands, and his folded hands came apart, and his mouth opened, and what came out of it was not the entity's voice at all.
