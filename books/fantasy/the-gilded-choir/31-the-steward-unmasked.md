## 31. The Steward Unmasked

The false binding had a sound.

Paloma had expected thunder, or the shriek of tearing wards, or some great grinding of the world's hinges. Instead it was *tidy*. That was the horror of it. The steward stood at the foot of the dais with his arms raised and his palms open, and the royal choir — two hundred throats in their gilded collars — sang a chord so smooth it seemed to polish the air. The notes went into the throne like water into cracked stone. The thing wearing Aldric drank them, and its hunger made a silence around itself that Paloma could feel against her teeth, a pressure like deep water, like the moment before glass gives way.

From the gallery's shadow she watched Sefa at the choir's head, chained at wrist and ankle to a stand of black iron, her small voice threaded through the harmony like silver wire through cloth. She was not fighting it. Paloma had understood, watching her for three terrible minutes, that Sefa could not fight it — the melody was written into her the way rivers are written into valleys — but she was *steering* it. Slowing it. Every phrase she led bent a half-tone from where the steward wanted it, and every half-tone bought seconds, and seconds were all they had.

Cassian crouched beside her in the dark behind the gallery balustrade, blood still crusted at his temple where the hounds' chains had torn him. Odile knelt with Wren pressed against her side, one hand over the boy's mouth more out of habit than need; he had not made a sound since they'd entered the palace, but he trembled like a struck bell.

"The name," Cassian breathed. "Tell me you have the name."

Paloma opened the satchel against her chest. Inside lay the rubbings she had taken from the buried chapel walls on the night Sefa's song had led them there — charcoal on linen, the old Choir's script in columns, and among them, in a hand older than the valley, older than the wards, a list. Not a list of prisoners. A list of *voices*. The First Choir, nine hundred years gone: eleven names, each followed by a small glyph she had finally learned to read only two days ago, in a rebel safehouse by candlelight, while Wren slept against her shoulder.

The glyph meant *given*. Each name had been given up — surrendered by its owner as the price of the binding. The First Voice had given her life. The others had given their names, which was nearly the same thing, because a name freely surrendered cannot be called back, and a singer without a name has no self left to sing from.

And one name on that list did not belong to the Choir.

It sat at the bottom, appended later, in a different ink, by a different hand. It had no *given* glyph beside it. It had something else — a spiral, coiling inward, that Paloma now understood was the mark of a vessel. The first vessel. The man who had let the entity wear him before it ever wore a king, who had held it through the founding years, who had sung the binding into place with the Choir and then been left holding the cage alone when the singers died.

The steward.

He had been the twelfth voice. He had never been named on the walls because he had never been released from the work — and the entity, patient beyond the patience of mountains, had kept him. Nine hundred years. Longer than the valley. Longer than the debt-ledgers, longer than the temple, longer than the word *Thackeray*.

"His name," Paloma whispered back. "I have it."

"Then sing it," Odile said. Her single eye caught what little light the gallery allowed. "Sing it before he finishes, girl, or none of this—"

"I know." Paloma's hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against the cold stone. "But listen to me. If I call his name — his true name, the one he gave up — it will unmake everything he is. Everything the thing built inside him. He isn't a man we're freeing. He's a wall we're pulling down, and the flood is behind the wall."

Wren pulled Odile's hand from his mouth. "Then pull it down fast," he said, "and be ready to sing."

* * *

She had rehearsed it, God help her, the way she rehearsed everything — copying it out three times, whispering it in the dark of the tannery loft, learning where the stresses fell, how the old vowels opened like doors. But rehearsal had not prepared her for what it would feel like to aim a name at another human being like an arrow.

The steward's voice rose. Below, the choir swelled toward the rite's turning point — she could hear it coming, the harmonic pivot the chapel walls had diagrammed, the place where the binding inverted and became a cage again, this time fueled by two hundred lives instead of eleven willing ones. Sefa's steering was failing; her voice had gone thin, fraying at the edges, and the iron stand she was chained to had begun to hum with a light that hurt to look at.

On the throne, King Aldric's head turned. Slowly. Toward the gallery.

The eyes were not his. They had not been his for a long time, but tonight they were less his than ever — twin wells of that deep-water dark, and in them Paloma saw attention gather like a storm seeing land.

*Little scribe,* said a voice that was not a voice, arriving inside her skull the way the mark on her palm had taught her to expect. *You brought me my own history. How thoughtful.*

She stood up.

It was the bravest and stupidest thing she had ever done, and she did it before courage or cowardice could argue, because she knew if she gave herself one second to think she would fold down behind the balustrade and stay there until the world ended politely around her. She stepped into the gallery's edge, into the spill of the choir-light, and the whole hall below turned — guards, courtiers, the hollow king, the chained child — and looked up at a thin woman in a copyist's grey dress with a satchel of linen rubbings held against her chest like a shield.

"You want history," Paloma said, and her voice carried farther than it should have; the mark on her palm burned, feeding her words into the hall's living acoustics. "Here is your history. Here is the name you've been hiding behind for nine hundred years."

"Don't—" The steward's voice cracked. For the first time since she'd seen him hold court, since she'd seen him move through the palace like a man walking through his own house, something in his face *moved*. Something under the stillness. "Scribe. Don't."

"You kept him," she said to the thing on the throne, and her voice shook and she let it shake, because the Choir's walls had taught her that a trembling voice is still a voice. "Nine centuries. You wore him first and you never let him go. You took his name and you never gave it back. Well. I read the walls. I read *everything*. It's what I'm for."

She filled her lungs.

The steward lunged forward — actually lunged, the composed mask shattering, and beneath it Paloma glimpsed something so old and so tired that her throat closed around the name for one awful heartbeat. Guards moved. Cassian moved faster, vaulting the gallery rail to crash into them below, buying her three seconds with his body. Odile began to sing the low keening line they'd practiced, the anchor tone, and Wren's uncertain tenor wove in above it, wavering, terrified, *holding*.

Paloma sang the steward's name.

It was not a word in any language spoken now. It was older than speech in the valley, shaped from the same tongue that had raised the wards and dug the chapel and bound the hungry thing beneath the stars. It came out of her in a ribbon of sound that seemed to bend the torchlight, and where it touched the air the false binding's tidy chord *shivered*, like a mirror struck by a stone.

The effect on the steward was not shattering. It was worse. It was *emptying*.

He stopped mid-gesture, arms still raised, and Paloma watched nine hundred years leave him. It went out of him the way breath leaves a dying man — in stages, reluctantly, each layer peeling back to show another beneath. The courtier's silk composure went first. Then the steward's crisp authority. Then something harder and colder, the entity's architecture, its scaffolding of control built bone by bone inside him across thirty reigns. And beneath all of it, at the very bottom, small and blinking in the light like a man dragged out of a cellar after a lifetime underground —

a young man. Dark-haired, ordinary-faced, with the chapped hands of a working singer and an expression of such raw, astonished grief that Paloma felt it strike her own chest like a physical blow.

The entity recoiled.

She felt it recoil — the mark on her palm flared white-hot and then went strangely quiet, and the pressure in the hall lifted as the presence withdrew from the steward's body like a tide going out. It did not leave angrily. It left the way a parasite leaves a host that has become useless, with a kind of cold economy, and Paloma understood in that instant that she had not wounded it. She had *discarded* its oldest tool. It slid backward along whatever unseen thread connected it to the throne, gathering itself around Aldric like a cloak, and the king's body arched on the seat and his mouth opened in a soundless scream.

But the steward — the man — fell.

He fell the way timber falls, all at once, knees hitting marble hard enough to crack, and the sound he made was not a scream either. It was a sob. One enormous, ragged sob, the first breath of a man surfacing after drowning, and then he was weeping — openly, horribly, the weeping of someone who has just remembered everything at once.

"My wife," he said. His voice was ruined, rusted, a voice unused for centuries except to serve. "Her name was — her name was —"

He couldn't find it. Paloma's stomach dropped. Of course he couldn't. The name had been *his*, and the name had been taken, and everything anchored to it — every memory tied to his identity, every love addressed by name — had gone with it into the walls of the chapel, into the ink of the rubbings now pressed against her ribs.

"Maren," Paloma said.

She didn't know why she knew it. Later she would understand: the chapel walls listed the wives too, in smaller script, the women who had waited out the binding years. But in that moment it came to her tongue unbidden, the way Sefa's songs came to hers, and she knelt — knelt, on the marble of the enemy's hall, in front of the monster who had hunted them across half the kingdom — and said it again.

"Maren. Your wife was Maren. You planted apple trees behind the chapel. The walls say you sang bass. The walls say you were kind."

He looked at her. And the terrible thing, the thing Paloma knew she would carry for the rest of her life, was that his eyes were *grateful*.

"Kind," he repeated. He tested the word like a coin bitten to see if it was real. Tears cut clean lines through the dust on his face. "I tried. In the early years I tried to — there were rules, I set rules, I wouldn't let it take children, I —" His breath hitched. "How long?"

Paloma could not answer.

"How long?" he demanded, and gripped her wrist with fingers that had no strength left in them at all. "The king — the last king I remember was — what year is it? What *year*?"

"Nine hundred years," Wren said softly, from the gallery rail above. The boy's voice carried, clear and pitiless and gentle all at once. "Give or take."

The steward — the man, the twelfth voice, the first vessel, whose name she had just spent like a blade — made a sound Paloma had never heard from anything with a human face. Then he laughed. It was a broken, bubbling, almost joyful laugh, and it frightened her more than the weeping had.

"Nine hundred," he whispered. "Maren's apples. The trees will be — I held it as long as I — tell me I held it long enough. Tell me the valley lived. Tell me people were *safe*."

"They were safe," Paloma said. Because it was true, and because he was dying — she could see it now, the way the emptied body was simply closing, a lamp running dry, nine centuries of borrowed time settling its account all at once. "They were safe, and they never knew, and it wasn't enough, and it was also — it mattered. What you did mattered."

"It wasn't enough," he agreed. Wonderingly. "It was never going to be enough. I knew. Even at the beginning, I knew, and I let them bind it anyway because the alternative was —" His gaze drifted past her, up, toward the throne where Aldric still convulsed silently in the entity's grip. "Don't do what I did. Whatever it offers you. It offered me *her*. It said if I wore it willingly, Maren would live out her days untouched. I believed it. I wanted to believe it so much that I —"

He coughed. There was blood on his lips now, bright against the white.

"It lies," he said. "It always lies. But the lie is made of true things, and that's why it works. Remember that, little scribe. When it offers you —"

His hand slackened on her wrist.

For one breath — one full breath, in and out, the length of a rest between phrases — his face was peaceful. Utterly, completely peaceful, the peace of a man setting down a weight no one should have been asked to carry, and Paloma thought: *there. That's what free looks like. One breath of it, after nine hundred years, and even one breath was worth the weeping.*

Then the breath went out of him and did not come back, and the first vessel of the thing beneath the stars lay still on the marble of the hall he had ruled for thirty reigns, looking, at the end, like exactly what he had always been: a singer.

* * *

The hall erupted.

Guards shouting, courtiers fleeing, the choir breaking apart in their collars like a net full of fish — but Paloma barely heard any of it, because the mark on her palm had begun to burn again, and through it she felt the entity turn its whole vast attention away from the chaos and onto *her*.

*You cost me nine hundred years of patience,* it said, inside her skull, calm as deep water. *Do you imagine I cannot afford another century? Do you imagine I have not worn Thackerays before?*

Below, on the throne, Aldric's body rose. Not stood — *rose*, lifted by something that used his limbs like puppet-strings, and the dark in his eyes spread outward now, bleeding into the air around his face, and the temperature of the hall dropped so fast that breath fogged.

"Paloma!" Cassian was bleeding from a split brow, wrestling a guard's sword away from him. "The choir — get to the choir — we end this now or we don't—"

But she was already moving, satchel clutched to her chest, Wren's hand finding hers in the smoke and screaming, Odile's keening alto rising behind them like a war-horn — and ahead, at the head of the scattered choir, Sefa lifted her chained wrists and began, alone, unaccompanied, unbearably clear, to sing the First Voice.

The stars themselves seemed to lean in to listen.

Paloma ran toward the sound.
