## 32. The Offer Repeated

The silence after the steward fell was not silence at all.

Paloma stood in the wreckage of the rite with her ears ringing and her throat raw, and beneath the ringing she could hear it still — that vast, patient melody threading through the stones of the throne room, through the pillars gilded until they wept gold down their flanks like candlewax, through the very air. The entity had no mouth now. The steward lay crumpled at the foot of the dais, a man-shaped husk nine hundred years empty, weeping without sound, his chest rising and falling in the shallow rhythm of a body that had forgotten how to be one. But the song did not need him anymore. It was everywhere. It was in the walls. It was in her burned palm.

Around her, the royal choir knelt where the false binding had held them — two hundred singers in white and gold, chained at the ankles not with iron but with something older, cords of woven light that pulsed in time with the entity's melody. Their faces were slack. Their mouths moved. Even now, even with the rite broken, they harmonized against their will, voices siphoned upward into the dark above the throne where the air itself had begun to thicken, to gather, to *look* at them.

Sefa was still chained at the choir's head.

She hung forward against her bonds, small and fierce, her braid coming loose, her voice hoarse from singing the steward's counterfeit lead. She was not unconscious. Her eyes found Paloma across the distance of the throne room — one vast chamber built to make every person in it feel like an insect — and she shook her head once, slowly. *Don't listen.*

Too late for that. Paloma had been listening since the chapel walls first taught her what the entity's voice sounded like. And now, with its vessel dead on the marble and its rite half-spun and unraveling, it turned the whole of its attention upon her, and listening stopped being a choice.

*Paloma Thackeray.*

The voice did not come through her ears. It arrived fully formed inside her skull, the way a memory arrives, the way grief arrives — intimate, inevitable, already hers before she knew it was being given. It was beautiful. That was the worst of it. Nine hundred years of wearing kings had taught it how to sound like everything a lonely girl had ever wanted to hear: her mother's lullaby cadence, the provost's approving murmur, Wren's laugh, all braided together into one warm chord.

*You broke my servant. Well done. He was tired anyway. They are always tired, in the end. I keep them too long.*

"Stay out of my head," Paloma said aloud, because saying it aloud made it a wall instead of a surrender.

*I am not in your head. I am in your palm. There is a difference, though I understand why you would not care for either.* Something like amusement rippled through the chord. *Look at your hand, scribe.*

She looked. She wished she hadn't. The mark the entity had burned there in the gallery — the spiral sigil, the seal of vessels — had opened like a flower. Fine lines of light ran outward from it along her wrist, up the veins of her forearm, delicate as frost ferns, delicate as ink drying on vellum. It did not hurt. That frightened her more than pain would have. Pain meant her body was still arguing.

*You have questions. You have always had questions; it is your disease. Your mother had it too. And her mother. I have watched your line scratch at my cage for six generations, and I have never once minded, because asking is so much better than answering, isn't it? Answering ends things.*

The throne room seemed to recede. The kneeling choir, the dead steward, the smoke of Odile's keening still hanging in the rafters — all of it softened, blurred, became scenery. What remained was the voice, and the warmth of it, and the terrible sense of being *seen* all the way down.

*So let me answer,* it said. *Once. Plainly. No riddles, no rite, no choir required. You have earned plain speech. Few get it.*

"I don't want anything from you."

*You want your debt gone.* The words landed like a needle finding the seam. *You want the ledger in the Temple of Quiet Hours to show a zero where your name is written. You have wanted that since you were nine years old and learned what indenture meant. You want your father's shame unwritten. You want the valley safe. You want Wren fed and Sefa unchained and Cassian's hands steady and Odile's brother's name restored to her before the storm takes another year of her. You want, Paloma Thackeray. Wanting is the oldest song there is, and I know all its verses.*

Behind her, somewhere near the shattered doors, she heard Cassian shout her name — heard boots on marble, heard Wren's thinner voice crying out — but they sounded very far away, as if the entity had folded the distance around her like a page closing over a line of text.

*Here is my offer. The last one. I will not make it twice, and I will not lie in it, because lies are for creatures who fear being refused.*

The throne room changed.

Not vanished — she would have known, would have felt the lie of it — but *shifted*, the way a dream shifts when it wants to prove a point. She stood in the Temple of Quiet Hours. Evening light came through the high windows the way it always had, honey-colored, thick with dust motes. The ledgers lay open before her, and her own hand — younger, unburned — moved across the columns in her neat, careful script. Somewhere in the building, Wren was laughing about something. Somewhere beyond the walls, the wards hummed their low, seamless note, and the stars — the stars were *there*, wheeling overhead when she glanced through the window, whole and countless and close.

*This,* the entity said gently. *This is what I am offering. Not power. Power is for kings, and look what kings become in my keeping. I am offering the world as it was the night before you learned it was a cage. The valley warded. The sky intact. Every star burning. Wren safe behind walls that cannot be breached. Sefa — yes, even her; I am generous — singing in your school instead of bleeding in my throne room. No more storms of un-song. No more purges. No more graves in the lowlands. Aldric will be healed and grateful and harmless. The steward's crimes will be answered. I will even let the lowlands go free; I have never cared for geography, only for quiet.*

The temple light warmed her shoulders. Her pen scratched. It would go on scratching forever, and it would never hurt again.

*And the price,* the entity continued, and here its voice slowed, became almost tender, almost honest, *is exactly what you think it is. Stop singing. Stop the counter-melody. Let the rite die unfinished, as rites do, and take up your pen, and live. That is all. One small silence, freely chosen. You were never a singer, Paloma. You are a copyist. You copy what others have sung. Is that not proof enough that your place was never in the choir?*

Her hand stilled on the page.

That was the knife of it, and the entity knew precisely where to set the edge. Because it was true — or true enough to draw blood. She had spent her whole life transcribing other people's music, other people's histories, other people's debts. The descant she loved best she had copied forty times before she ever dared hum it in the dark of her cell, alone, ashamed, as if wanting a voice of her own were just one more entry in the ledger of things she owed for.

"You're lying," she whispered.

*Am I?* The warmth did not falter. *Test me. Name one thing I have offered that I cannot give. I hollowed a king for nine hundred years and kept his kingdom prosperous — ask any farmer in the valley whether the crown has been cruel to them. I kept the peace. I kept the wards. I kept the stars in the sky for three centuries while your ancestors told their children the sky was a gift. I am not your enemy, little scribe. I am the wall between you and everything that eats little girls in the dark. The rebels burn villages. The stewards chain children. The storms unmake grandmothers' names. I offer an ending to all of it, and you flinch — why?*

"Because it's a cage," Paloma said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "You just described a cage with the door painted shut."

*A cage keeps wolves out.*

"A cage keeps everyone in." She looked up from the ledger — from the beautiful, endless, harmless ledger — and met the thing behind the light. "I've lived in your cage my whole life. I know how it feels from the inside. It feels like safety right up until the night you realize you've never once seen the sky choose anything."

For a moment — one long, ringing moment — the warmth went out of the voice.

What remained underneath was old beyond reckoning, and cold, and vast, and hungry in a way that had nothing to do with food. She felt it the way she imagined a mouse feels the stillness of a barn owl: not hearing the predator, but hearing the *absence* the predator makes.

*They sang me into the dark once,* it said. *Your ancestors. Willing voices, freely given, a life laid down — and they bound me beneath their pretty valley and called it protection and raised their children on the lie. Six generations of jailers who thought themselves sheep. And now you stand in my house, having broken my servant and scattered my rite, and you quote their catechism back at me. Freedom. Openness. Stars.* A pause, and then, softer, which was worse: *Do you know what I hear, when I hear your choir? I hear the same song that caged me. Different words. Same melody. You will build your walls too, Paloma Thackeray. You will call them schools. You will call them councils. You will call them anything but cages, and your grandchildren will forget, and someday someone will sing me loose again, and I will make this same offer to them, and they will be wiser than you, and they will say yes.*

"No," Paloma said.

*No?*

"No," she said again, louder, and the dream-temple shuddered around her like a struck bell. "Not because you're wrong about us. Maybe you're not. Maybe we'll build new cages and paint the doors and call them freedom — people do, I've read enough history to know it. But here's what you don't understand, sitting in your dark for nine hundred years, counting our songs like coins." She stood. The ledger slid from her lap and dissolved into gold dust before it hit the floor. "A cage you choose to leave is worth more than a garden you're locked inside. That's the whole difference. That's *all* the difference, and it's everything."

*And if leaving costs you everything you love?* The entity's voice curled around the question like smoke. *The storm took Odile's brother's name. It will take more. Wren stands in a battlefield in a stolen coat. Sefa hangs in chains ten feet from where you dreamed this dream. Cassian has already bled for you once and will bleed again — I have seen it; I see many endings. Stay silent, and none of it happens. Sing, and you may lose them all, and it will be your song that loses them. Is that not a debt, scribe? The largest one ever entered under your name?*

Paloma's breath caught.

Because it was. Of course it was. She had spent her life learning that every gift carried a figure in the margin, every kindness accrued interest, every open hand waited to present its bill. The entity had studied her line for six generations. It knew exactly which wound to press.

But —

But Wren, age seven, pressing his forehead against the temple window, swearing he'd seen a star flicker. *Did you see it, Paloma? Did you?* And her own cowardly answer: *There's nothing out there, Wren. There's never been anything out there.* And the way he'd looked at her — not angry, just sad, the way children are sad when adults confirm the world is smaller than they hoped.

She had spent her whole life paying debts. She would not pay this one.

"Yes," she said. "It might cost everything. That's what makes it mine."

The dream broke.

* * *

She came back to herself on the marble of the throne room with Cassian's hand gripping her shoulder and Wren's arms around her waist and the taste of blood in her mouth — she had bitten her tongue, or perhaps the transition had. Above the throne, the gathered dark convulsed. The entity's attention withdrew from her like a tide going out, and in its wake the whole chamber rang with a fury that was almost, almost admiration.

*Then sing,* it said, and now the beauty was gone entirely, and what spoke was the thing the wards had been built against, the thing the Choir had died to bind, the reason valleys were walled and skies were shuttered and children were taught not to look up. *Sing, and be unmade by the singing. I have eaten choirs before. I remember every note.*

"It didn't accept," Cassian breathed. "Paloma — did it—"

"It gave me the choice." She pushed herself upright. Her burned palm blazed, the frost-lines of light crawling past her elbow now, and she understood with cold clarity that the mark had been a leash all along, a channel waiting to be opened, and that she had just refused to walk through the door it opened. "It wanted me to stop singing. That was the whole trap. Safety in exchange for silence."

"And you said—" Wren began.

"She said no," said a small voice from the head of the choir.

Sefa.

She had lifted her head. Her chains of woven light still held her, still pulsed with the entity's rhythm, but her eyes were clear and furious and terribly, terribly awake, and when she drew breath, the whole throne room drew breath with her.

"The rite's unfinished," she said. "Half-bound, half-loose. It's never been this open. Never." Her gaze found Paloma's, and in it was none of a child's pleading — only the flat, ancient certainty that had frightened Odile from the first day, the understanding of sacrifice that no child should carry and this child could not put down. "One voice starts it. One willing voice anchors the rest. You know the words. You copied them off the chapel wall in your own hand."

"Sefa, no." Wren's grip tightened on Paloma's arm. "No, we said—"

"We said we wouldn't *let* her," Paloma said quietly. Her heart was slamming against her ribs. Above the throne the dark was gathering itself, coiling, and she could feel through her mark how much strength the broken rite had left it — and how little time. "We never figured out how to stop her."

Above them, the entity began to sing.

It was not a melody. It was the *un*-melody — the storm-song, the memory-eater, the sound of names dissolving — and it came down through the gilded pillars and into the kneeling choir, and two hundred enslaved throats opened and harmonized with it despite themselves, tears streaming down their faces, and the light-cords at their ankles flared white. Somewhere in the ranks, Odile's keening alto rose to fight it and cracked. Somewhere, Cassian pulled Wren behind him. Somewhere, the last of the steward's life guttered out on the marble, unnoticed, unmourned, free for less than a breath.

And Sefa, chained at the head of the enemy's choir, looked at Paloma one last time — and smiled, small and bright and absolutely unafraid — and opened her mouth, and began.

The First Voice rose through the throne room like dawn through a cracked door.

Paloma's mark burned white-hot. The frost-lines of light raced up her arm, over her shoulder, across her collarbone — and then, impossibly, they turned to *music*, every glowing thread resolving into a written line, a descant, a part she alone could sing, scored in light along her own skin.

Her part. Written for her. Waiting for her all along.

"Wren," she said, taking his hand. "Cassian. Get Odile on her feet. We're singing — *now*."
