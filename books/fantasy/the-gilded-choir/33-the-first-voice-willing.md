## 33. The First Voice, Willing

Sefa had always known the song would ask her for something.

She had known it since she was small enough to fit inside a grain barrel, since the first night the melody came into her mouth uninvited and she hummed it back instead of screaming. The others in the waystation had screamed. The soldiers who chained her had prayed. But Sefa had listened, the way you listen to rain on a roof, the way you listen to a dog crying three farms over — with your whole chest, helplessly — and somewhere in that listening she had understood: the song was not evil. It was only hungry. And hunger was not the same as malice. Hunger was just a shape loneliness made.

Now she stood at the head of the royal choir's dais, her wrists still ringed with the memory of chains, and the steward's false binding rose around her like a cathedral built of wrong notes.

The throne room had changed. She could feel it changing, note by note, the way dough rises under a cloth. The gilded pillars sweated a thin golden light. The banners along the walls had gone stiff and strange, their embroidered stars unraveling into threads that drifted upward like smoke that had forgotten which way was down. And above it all, coiling through the rafters where the ceiling should have been, the entity turned — vast, slow, patient as erosion — its attention fixed on the two hundred singers below it whose voices poured out of them whether they willed it or not.

Sefa could hear what they were singing. That was the worst part. It was beautiful.

The steward had taken their voices the way a tax collector takes grain — a portion from each, never quite enough to kill, always enough to weaken — and he had braided those stolen portions into a binding-shape, a cage of harmony with the entity at its center. Except it was not a cage. Sefa knew cages. Cages were small and cold and smelled of iron and other people's fear. This was something else. This was a net drawn tight around a drowning thing, and the steward meant to pull it closed forever, and every thread of the net was a person.

She could hear them inside it. A woman near the front whose voice cracked on every third note because she had been singing against her will for eleven years. A boy no older than Wren whose high line trembled like a wire in wind. An old bass who had stopped trying to fight and simply sang, because singing was the last thing that was still his.

*They don't want this,* Sefa thought. *None of them want this.*

And the melody in her own throat — the true one, the one the entity had carried across centuries and continents and the bodies of kings — stirred and said, without words, in the language of pure pitch: *then give them back their choice.*

Below the dais, hidden in the shadow of the gallery's stone lip, she could see them. Her people. Paloma with ink-stained fingers pressed flat against the balustrade, lips moving around the descant she'd copied in secret a lifetime ago. Cassian, gray-faced, one hand pressed to the wound in his side, standing anyway. Odile with her single eye lifted and her jaw set like a woman walking into a river she'd sworn never to enter again. Wren — small, freckled, terrified Wren — gripping his scrap of copied notation so hard the paper had gone soft as cloth.

They were waiting for her.

Not for permission. Sefa understood that much, and it mattered. They were not going to let her do this alone, and they were not going to order her to do it at all. They were waiting the way an audience waits before the first note — with that held-breath silence that is itself a kind of love.

*Willingly,* Paloma had told her, kneeling in the smuggler's tunnel, holding Sefa's face in both hands. *Knowingly. That's the whole rite, Sefa. Not a life taken. A voice given. Do you understand the difference?*

Sefa had understood. She had understood since the grain barrel.

She stepped forward to the edge of the dais.

The steward saw her move. He stood beside the throne, beneath the slumped figure of King Aldric — poor hollow Aldric, folded into his seat like a coat someone had left behind — and his face was smooth as an egg, nine hundred years of borrowed time stretched over nothing. When he spoke, his voice came out layered, two voices in one throat, the man's dry rasp underneath and the entity's vast resonance above, and both of them said:

"Little vessel. You are early."

"I'm not a vessel," Sefa said.

Her voice did not carry the way she expected. It carried the way the first drop of rain carries before a storm breaks — small, and then suddenly everywhere. The enslaved choir faltered. Two hundred heads turned toward her, and in two hundred faces Sefa watched the same expression bloom: recognition. They knew the melody. Of course they did. They had been forced to sing its bones every night of their captivity. Hearing it sung freely, by a child, without chains — it went through them like sunlight through dirty glass.

"Stop her," the steward said.

The nearest guards started up the dais steps. Sefa did not run. Running was for people who had somewhere else to be. Instead she opened her mouth and began.

* * *

The First Voice is not loud.

That was the first thing Sefa learned in the space between one heartbeat and the next. All her life she had thought the great songs must be shouted, that power lived in volume, that the entity's vastness required a vastness to answer it. But the First Voice — the anchoring note, the one the chapel walls described in spirals of pre-warding script — was quiet. It was the note a mother hums over a cradle. It was the note you sing when there is no one left to hear and you sing anyway.

It was a door held open.

She felt it leave her — not painfully, not like the harvesters pulling at the choir's voices, but the way breath leaves you when you finally stop holding it. The melody rose out of her throat and hung in the air above the dais, visible now, a thread of pale gold light trembling in the ruined air of the throne room. And it waited. Because a First Voice alone is only a beginning. The chapel walls had been clear about that, and Paloma had read them aloud twice so no one could pretend they hadn't heard.

*A full choir of willing voices. Each one choosing. Each one staying.*

The guards reached the bottom step and stopped, uncertain, because the note was doing something to them too — softening the anger in their shoulders, loosening the orders in their ears. One of them lowered his spear an inch. Then another inch.

And from the gallery, Paloma Thackeray began to sing.

* * *

Later — in the days after, when Sefa tried to tell Odile what it had felt like from the inside — she would never find the right words. But she would keep trying, because some things deserve the attempt.

Paloma's voice entered the gold thread like a second color entering a weave. It was not a trained voice. It wavered on the high phrases; it caught where the descant climbed; once, horribly, it nearly broke altogether. And none of that mattered, because it was *chosen*. Sefa could feel the choosing in it the way you feel warmth through a wall. Every note Paloma sang was a note she decided to sing, in a room full of soldiers, facing a god, with her debt-ledger heart wide open — and the melody took her wavering, imperfect, magnificent willingness and wrapped it around itself like a second strand.

Then Cassian.

His bass came in low and steady, and Sefa felt the whole structure settle, the way a table settles when you find the leg that wobbles. He was bleeding. She could hear it — not in his voice, exactly, but around it, a faint ragged edge where pain frayed each phrase — and he sang through it, note after deliberate note, a deserter's voice that had spent years learning how to survive quietly now spending itself loudly, gloriously, on purpose.

Then Odile.

Oh, Odile. Her alto came in keening, raw, half grief and half fury, the sound of a woman who had betrayed a brother to save herself and had carried that betrayal for thirty years like a stone in her boot. The entity heard it and lunged — Sefa felt it lunge, a cold tidal pull toward the gallery, toward the wound in Odile's voice, ready to pour itself into her guilt and wear her like it had worn kings —

— and Odile sang *through* it. Through the memory of her brother's name. Through the ferry crossing where she had left him. She put all of it into the note, every ugly ounce, and the note held, because a willing voice does not have to be a clean one. It only has to be given.

Then Wren.

Wren's tenor cracked on his very first note and Sefa almost laughed, there in the middle of the end of the world, because of course it did. He was twelve. His voice was changing. He gripped the balustrade and squeezed his eyes shut and came in again, cracked again, came in a third time — and on the third try the boy found the note and held it with the stubbornness of someone who had once stowed away in a cart because leaving people behind was worse than being afraid. The gold thread brightened around his small determined sound like a fire taking new wood.

Five voices. The chapel walls had asked for more, but five was enough to begin, and beginnings are contagious.

From the choir stalls below, a single voice slipped free of the steward's net.

It was the old bass. Sefa heard him first as a rumble beneath the stolen harmonies, off-pattern, off-command, *his own*. The steward's head snapped toward him. The golden light convulsed. But the old man kept singing, and beside him the woman with the cracking voice tore free and joined him, and then the boy with the wire-tremble, and then four more, and eight, and twenty — voices ripping loose one by one like stitches giving way, each one landing in the open chord above with a soundless shock Sefa felt in her teeth.

Two hundred singers. Some fought free. Some were dragged under deeper. But enough — enough chose, in the space of eleven bars of music, to stop being fuel and start being a choir.

The false binding shuddered.

* * *

The entity did not rage. That was the terrible thing. It *sang back*.

Sefa felt the counter-melody rise out of the rafters, and it was made of everything each of them feared most. To Cassian it sang the bridge at dusk, the moment of his capture, his friends' footsteps fading while he bled. To Odile it sang her brother's face. To Paloma it sang the ledger — page after page of debt, her name written in her ancestors' hands, generations deep, *you were bred for this, you were always owned.* To Wren it sang the temple burning. To Sefa it sang—

To Sefa it sang nothing. It sang *silence*, the silence of the grain barrel, the silence of everyone who had ever heard her melody and reached for chains instead of listening. *No one will ever choose you,* the silence said. *You are a weapon that learned to hum. Sing your little song. We will simply be louder.*

And it was louder. For three bars, for five, for seven, the entity's counter-song swelled until the windows wept golden light and the pillars groaned and the freed singers clapped their hands over their ears and even the First Voice flickered like a candle in a draft.

But a choir is not a candle. A choir is a room full of lungs.

Paloma bent the descant upward and the others followed, and the melody that had been a thread became a rope became a river, and Sefa stood at the head of it and understood, with a clarity she would carry for the rest of her life, what the chapel builders had known: the entity had spent nine hundred years wearing single bodies — one king, one queen, one scribe at a time — because it could only ever be *one* voice in *one* throat. It had never once been part of anything. Every vessel it took, it took alone. Every century it endured, it endured alone.

It did not know how to be accompanied.

The choir rose around it — imperfect, cracking, bleeding, grieving, twelve years old, sixty years old, every flaw intact and every voice chosen — and the binding inverted. Sefa felt it turn like a key turning, like a lock deciding to become a door. The net the steward had woven did not close. It opened. Wide. Wider than the throne room, wider than the valley, wider than the sky the wards had hidden for three hundred years — and the entity, which had come into the world hungry and had stayed because the world was a cage and a cage at least holds you, felt the whole sky open at once and had nowhere left to hold on to.

It tried the king one last time.

Aldric's body jerked upright in the throne. For a single moment his eyes cleared — brown, human, horrified — and his mouth shaped words with no voice behind them: *I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I'm sorry.* Then the entity left him the way a tide leaves a shore, withdrawing through the crown, through the gold, through the gilded rot of nine hundred years, gathering itself smaller and smaller and quieter and quieter until—

Until it was only a far note, rising.

Until it was gone.

* * *

The silence afterward was enormous.

Sefa stood at the head of the dais with her hands empty and her throat raw and the melody finally, truly, hers alone — just a girl's song now, no god inside it, no hunger, no history. She felt strangely light. She felt strangely *ordinary*. She felt Wren's arms around her before she saw him coming, knocking the breath out of her, and then Cassian's hand heavy on her hair, and then Odile's rough palm against her cheek, brief as a struck match.

"Stubborn child," Odile said, which in Odile's language was a psalm.

Paloma reached them last. She looked older and younger at once, wrung out like laundry, ink still on her fingers, and she knelt — actually knelt, on the marble, in front of everyone — to look Sefa in the eye.

"You gave it," Paloma said. "Freely. Knowingly." Her voice broke on the second word. "The bravest thing I have ever seen, and I have read about every brave thing there is."

"It wasn't brave," Sefa said, because it was important to be honest. "I've just always known the song wanted a door, not a cage. I was only ever going to be the one who opened it."

Behind them, a wet, ragged sound. They turned.

King Aldric had slid from the throne onto the floor, and he was weeping — great, helpless, human sobs, the crying of a man waking from a nightmare nine centuries long to find his hands stained with centuries he couldn't remember. The crown had rolled from his head and lay spinning slowly on the marble, catching the light, empty as a dropped coin. Cassian moved toward him with his sword half-drawn and Paloma caught his arm, and for a long moment nobody did anything at all except watch a hollowed king learn how to breathe on his own.

Above them, unnoticed by anyone but Sefa, the first star of the returning sky showed through a crack in the ruined ceiling.

Small. Pale. Patient.

And far away — farther than the valley, farther than the maps, out past the edge of everything — something that had worn the world like a coat for nine hundred years was drifting, dissolving, growing thin as breath on glass. It was frightened. It was lighter than it had ever been. And as it thinned into the dark between the stars, it heard, faint and absurd and utterly incomprehensible, a small valley's worth of people beginning — badly, joyfully, out of tune — to sing.
