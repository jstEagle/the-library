## 35. Debts and Ledgers

The ledger burned the way all honest things burn: slowly at first, then all at once.

Paloma had chosen the temple steps for it, in the hour after dawn when the mist still clung to the valley floor like a held breath. She had chosen them deliberately — those same worn steps where she had knelt as a girl of nine, pressing her small palm to the stone and swearing the oath of indenture while the provost read out the sum of her family's debt in a voice like arithmetic. *Four hundred and eleven marks, seven coppers, carried forward from the estate of Thackeray, deceased.* She had not understood then that a number could be a chain. She understood now.

She had also chosen the steps because word traveled fast in a valley that had spent three hundred years with nothing to talk about but weather and debt. By the time she struck the flint, there were perhaps forty people gathered in the square below — market women with their baskets still on their arms, a pair of wardens who had not yet decided whether to be officials or neighbors, old Marta from the dye-works who had once shared her bread ration with Paloma during the hungry winter. And Wren, of course, standing at the front with his arms crossed and his chin lifted, wearing the expression of a boy who has personally arranged a miracle and intends to be credited for it.

"You don't have to make a speech," he'd said earlier, when she told him what she meant to do.

"I know."

"Good. Because you're terrible at speeches. You alphabetize when you're nervous."

"I do not alphabetize when I'm nervous."

"You alphabetized the rebel safehouse, Paloma. The *dishes*."

She had no answer for that, so she struck the flint.

The ledger was a fat thing, its cover scuffed to the color of old tallow, its spine broken from years of being opened to the same page. Her page. She had carried it up from the records room herself, past the shelves where she had spent fourteen years copying other people's obligations in a hand so fine the provost had once called it "an asset to the temple." An asset. As though a person could be entered into an inventory between the candle stock and the roof lead.

The flame caught the first leaf and she watched her own name begin to curl. *Thackeray, Paloma, daughter of Thackeray, Ilse.* The ink darkened before it browned, browned before it blackened, and the numbers that had governed every hour of her waking life since childhood went up in a smell like a snuffed candle.

Someone in the crowd gasped. Someone else laughed — a short, startled sound, quickly smothered, the laugh of a person who has just realized they are allowed.

Paloma held the book until the heat reached her fingers, until the binding split and half of it dropped glowing onto the stone, until there was nothing left worth holding and her palms were striped with small red burns that she would carry, she suspected, longer than the scars on her hands from the entity's mark — which had faded already, which had faded the very morning the stars came back, taking with them, inexplicably, everything else. But that was a grief she was not ready to open. This morning was for something simpler.

She let the last pages fall and turned to face the square.

"I'm not making a speech," she announced.

Wren made a noise that was almost a cheer.

"The debt is paid," she said. "That's all. That's the whole of it. I burned the record because the record was a lie — not the numbers, the numbers were true enough, but the idea that a child inherits a sum the way she inherits her mother's eyes. The idea that a life can be owed." Her voice did not shake, which surprised her; she had expected it to. Fourteen years of keeping her voice level in the provost's office had apparently left a residue of steadiness she could draw on even now. "I don't know what I am without it yet. But I'm going to find out. And none of you owe anything either. Not to the temple. Not to the crown. Not to the dead."

Old Marta was crying quietly into her apron. One of the wardens took off his hat, which in the valley's language of gestures meant roughly what a bow meant everywhere else.

And that was when the bells began.

* * *

They were not the temple bells. The temple bells rang the hours, and this was not an hour. These were the deep, cracked bells of the moot-hall across the square, rung only for council sessions and funerals, and the sound of them rolled through the valley like a stone dropped down a well.

"He's here," Wren said. All the triumph went out of his face at once. "Paloma. He's *here*. Odile said he wouldn't come, she said—"

"Odile," Paloma said, "is on a ferry somewhere past the lowlands, and the provost has never in his life let another man's prediction stand in place of his own certainty. Go find Cassian. If he's still at the militia yard, tell him—" She stopped. Told herself to think about what she actually wanted. "Tell him nothing. Tell him to come watch. He'll understand."

Wren hesitated on the bottom step, torn visibly between obedience and the urge to stay and glower protectively at whatever came up the road. In the end he did both, running off toward the yard with frequent backward glances, and Paloma climbed the rest of the temple steps alone, into the shadow of the portico where she had spent her whole life, to wait for the man who owned her.

Except he didn't. That was the thing she kept having to remind herself of, the way you probe a missing tooth. He didn't own her. The paper said otherwise, but the paper was ash on the wind now, drifting out over the square in gray flakes that settled on the market baskets like the first dirty snow of a season nobody had asked for.

Provost Hallowell came up the valley road on foot, as he always did, because horses unsettled him and because walking gave him time to compose grievances into their proper order. He was smaller than she remembered, or perhaps she had grown around him the way a tree grows around a fence post. His robes were immaculate. His face was the face of a man who has done sums all night and found the total unacceptable.

"Thackeray," he said, when he reached the foot of the steps. Not *Sister Thackeray*, which she had earned by her twelfth year. Not *Paloma*, which he had never once used. Just the name, flat as a coin on a table.

"Provost."

"I am given to understand," he said, "that you have committed destruction of temple property. Publicly. With witnesses." His eyes went to the scorched stain on the steps, the ghost of a book. "That ledger represented four hundred years of the temple's financial continuity. Your family's obligation was one entry among thousands, but the principle—"

"The principle," Paloma said, "was that people are accounts."

He blinked. It was such a small thing, that blink, but she had spent fourteen years learning to read him the way she read difficult texts — closely, patiently, line by line — and she saw it land. He had not expected argument. He had expected, perhaps, apology. Or tears. Or the particular exhausted surrender of a debtor called to account, the posture she had worn so long it had shaped her spine.

"The council is convening," he said instead. "This morning. There is a matter of restitution to be heard. You will attend."

"Yes," Paloma said. "I suppose I will."

And she followed him down the steps, through the parting crowd, toward the moot-hall whose bells were still dying away — and it was only halfway across the square that she realized she had walked beside him rather than behind him, and that he had not corrected her, and that she had not waited to be corrected.

* * *

The moot-hall had never in living memory been full. It had been built, according to the pre-warding texts Paloma had once copied in secret, to hold every voice in the valley at once — a founding generation that believed decisions should be made loudly and together, in a building with acoustics designed so that even a whisper from the back benches reached the speaker's platform. The wards had changed all that. For three hundred years the hall had stood mostly empty, its galleries gathering dust, its great curved walls carrying nothing but the coughs of a dozen elders.

Today it was full to the rafters.

They had come from every village in the valley — Paloma saw faces she knew from the ledgers, names she had written a thousand times in columns of debt: the Kettering brothers who owed two marks for a disputed fence; the widow Sorrel, whose late husband's weaving debt had passed to sons too young to hold a shuttle; the ferry-folk from the shallows, brown-faced and river-smelling, who had walked two days to be here. They filled the benches and stood along the walls and crowded the gallery, and the sound of them was a low ocean-roar that the old walls caught and multiplied until it seemed the building itself was murmuring.

Cassian found her near the front. He looked better than he had a right to — the bridge had cost him a broken wrist and a scar across the jaw that was still angry-red, but he wore both like medals, and he had developed, in the weeks since the coronation night, a way of standing in crowds that made it clear he was watching the exits out of habit rather than fear.

"Wren said you burned it," he said by way of greeting.

"Word travels fast."

"In a valley with nothing to talk about? Paloma, you set fire to the only scripture some of these people ever feared. Of course word travels fast." He glanced at the crowd, at the widow Sorrel sitting very straight with her hands folded, at the Ketterings whispering together with the furtive excitement of men watching a door they'd assumed was painted on. "You know what you've started, don't you?"

"I burned one book."

"You burned *the* book. Every one of these people is doing the same arithmetic right now. If hers can be ash, why not mine?" He said it without judgment — Cassian had long ago stopped judging the mathematics of hope — but with the wariness of a man who had seen crowds turn, and knew they turned in every direction eventually.

The council took the platform: five elders chosen in the chaotic fortnight after Aldric's abdication, none of them noble, one of them — to everyone's private astonishment — the ferrywoman's cousin, a broad quiet woman named Bette who smelled of tar and spoke perhaps ten sentences a day. The provost sat to one side of the platform in a chair that had clearly been placed there as a courtesy and looked, from certain angles, like a dock.

The session opened with the mundane business of a valley learning to govern itself: seed grain allocations, the repair of the mill race, the question of what to do with the soldiers' abandoned garrison. Paloma listened with half an ear, her heart ticking steadily louder as the list shortened. Beside her, Wren had gone uncharacteristically still, and when she glanced down at him she found him watching her with an expression she couldn't parse — something between pride and worry, the look of a boy holding a kite string in changing wind.

Then Bette said, "Next matter. The Thackeray debt," and the whole hall seemed to lean in at once, and the acoustics did their ancient work, carrying the rustle of four hundred people into a single held breath.

The provost rose. He had prepared documents; of course he had prepared documents, he had probably prepared them by candlelight with the same fine hand he demanded of his scribes. He read out the figure — four hundred and eleven marks, seven coppers, plus fourteen years of accrued interest calculated at the temple rate, a sum so swollen with compound arithmetic that several people in the gallery gasped aloud. He described the destruction of property. He cited precedent going back to the first provosts. He requested, in a voice pitched carefully between reason and sorrow, that the debt be transferred to the temple's general fund and that Paloma Thackeray be remanded to the workhouse at Coldbrook for a term of years sufficient to—

"Provost."

It was Bette. Ten sentences a day, and she had spent one of them on his name alone. The hall went silent in a way that must have been familiar to him from his own chapel — that terrible congregational silence — and Paloma watched him falter, watched the documents tremble very slightly in his grip.

"The council has heard your figures," Bette said. "We've heard them our whole lives. My grandmother heard them. Question is different now." She looked out at the crowd, then back at him, unhurried as tide. "Question is: what was bought with it?"

"That is not— the terms of indenture are quite clear—"

"What. Was bought. With it."

And here was the thing Paloma had not anticipated, the thing she would turn over in her mind for many nights afterward: the provost looked at her. Across the hall, past four hundred faces, he looked directly at her — and for one unguarded moment his expression was not the face of a creditor at all. It was the face of a man standing at the edge of an understanding he had spent his whole career avoiding, a man who had built a life on the premise that the ledgers were the world, and who had just heard, in Bette's flat river voice, the question that unravels all ledgers everywhere.

What was bought with it.

Because the answer, and everyone in the hall knew it, and he knew it, was: *nothing.* The debt had bought nothing. It had never been meant to be repaid — it had been engineered, generations ago, bred into her bloodline like a trait, a leash disguised as arithmetic, a mechanism to keep a Thackeray inside the temple walls until the day the temple's masters needed one. The provost might not know all of it. But he knew the shape of it. He had known, Paloma realized with a strange, cold clarity, for longer than he admitted even to himself. No one audits a debt that honestly expects to be paid.

"The council rules," Bette said, when the silence had gone on long enough to become an answer in itself, "the debt void. Paid in full." She paused, and something almost dry moved at the corner of her mouth. "By the night's work. Let the record show the valley considers itself well paid."

The roar that came off the walls was physical. Paloma felt it in her sternum, in her teeth, in the small burns on her palms. Widow Sorrel was on her feet. The Kettering brothers were shouting something joyful and incoherent. Somewhere in the gallery a child was laughing, high and bright, and the old acoustics caught the laugh and multiplied it until the whole hall rang with it like a bell.

The provost sat down. He gathered his documents. He did not look at anyone, and no one looked at him, which Paloma thought was perhaps the cruelest mercy the valley knew how to give.

* * *

Afterward, the square outside was a festival nobody had planned. Someone had brought out cider. Someone else had brought a fiddle, and the music was bad and getting worse, and no one cared. Wren was teaching Sefa's letters to a ring of children using a stick and the flagstones, drawing enormous wobbling A's and B's and narrating each one as though it were a battle maneuver, and the children were hanging on his nonsense as though it were gospel.

Paloma stood at the edge of it with a cup of cider she wasn't drinking, waiting for the feeling to arrive.

It didn't. That was the strange part. She had imagined — she had *planned*, insofar as she allowed herself plans — that liberation would feel like something. Like the wards coming down, like the stars returning, like the moment in the throne room when the choir inverted the binding and the air itself had seemed to exhale after nine hundred years. Some clean, ringing note. Instead she felt — light. Unbearably, uncomfortably light, like a scribe who has set down a ledger she has carried so long her shoulders have grown around its weight, and now stands crooked under nothing, reaching for a strap that isn't there.

Every morning for fourteen years she had woken knowing exactly what she was: a debtor. It had ordered her days, rationed her hopes, excused her smallness. *I cannot leave; I owe. I cannot want; I owe. I cannot be more than this; I owe.* And now the account was closed, and the terrifying question underneath all the others rose up through the silence it left behind:

*Then who are you?*

"You're doing the face," Wren said, appearing at her elbow with cider on his chin.

"What face?"

"The one where you alphabetize your feelings. Cider, anger, dread..." He bumped his shoulder against her arm. "It's allowed to feel weird. Odile said it would. She said freedom feels like falling until you remember you were always allowed to put your feet down."

"When did Odile say that?"

"On the ferry dock. Right before she pretended she had something in her eye." He shrugged, elaborately casual, in the way that meant the moment had mattered enormously to him. "She also said to tell you letters come every month or she's coming back to yell at us in person. Both of us. She was very specific about the yelling."

Paloma looked out at the square — at the fiddle and the cider and the children and the widow Sorrel dancing with a warden, at the valley that had spent three hundred years afraid of the sky and was now, tentatively, learning the names of stars. Hers. All of it hers to walk through without asking permission. The lightness was still there, the vertigo of it, the unmoored sway of a woman with no debt and no magic and no title and no ledger to tell her who she was.

But her feet were on the ground. She checked. They were.

"Cassian says the school charter comes before the council next week," Wren said. "If you want it. He says the valley needs someone to teach what actually happened — the real history, not the temple version. He says—" Wren stopped, and grinned, and delivered the blow with relish. "He says they'll want the woman who burned the book to write the new ones."

Paloma looked down at her palms, at the small red burns from the ledger's funeral, already fading.

"Well," she said, and found she was smiling, and found the smile fit strangely, like a garment cut for someone she was only just becoming. "I suppose I'd better learn to make speeches."

From the direction of the moot-hall came the sound of raised voices — the council reconvening, arguing already about the garrison, about the temple's lands, about a hundred questions with no precedents left to answer them. And cutting through it, clear and sharp, came a runner from the pass road, waving a folded paper, shouting a name that stopped the music mid-bar:

A letter. From the coast. Sealed with grey wax, and addressed in a hand Paloma did not recognize — but the mark pressed into the wax was a single open eye, and beneath it, in the old pre-warding script she had once copied in secret, a single word:

*Choir.*
