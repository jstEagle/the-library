## 36. Odile's Crossing

The ferry at Marrow's Landing had been named *Patient* by some long-dead optimist, and Odile thought about that name every time she looked at it. The boat sat low in the grey water, its hull streaked with salt-bloom, its single sail furled like a sleeping bird's wing. It was not patient. It was old, and it leaked in two places she knew of, and it would get them down the coast if the weather held and drown them all if it didn't. She had made her living on worse.

"Stare at it any longer," she said to Sefa, "and it'll stare back."

Sefa did not laugh. The girl had not laughed much since the palace — since the throne room, since the singing, since whatever had passed through her small body and out of it that night, leaving her lighter and stranger than before. She stood at the edge of the dock with her bare toes curled over the boards, watching the water move the way other people watched fires: with a hunger that had nothing to do with warmth.

"It goes somewhere," Sefa said. "All of it. Every river, every rain. It remembers where it's going."

"Aye, well." Odile shifted the pack on her shoulder — heavier than it should have been, because Paloma had packed it as though they were emigrating rather than traveling. Dried plums. A tin of ink. Three spare quills wrapped in oilcloth, because Paloma could not bear the thought of anyone writing with an inferior nib. "Some of us just go where the current shoves."

That was a lie, and both of them knew it. Odile had spent fifty years letting currents shove her — out of the valley, onto the river, away from the burning villages, away from her brother's name. She was done being shoved. That was rather the point of this whole wretched journey.

She heard them coming before she saw them: boots on the dock planks, too many boots, moving without soldier rhythm. Cassian walked like a man counting his steps for someone else's benefit. Wren walked like a boy who had never learned that his elbows were weapons. And between them came Paloma, carrying a basket that smelled of bread and, faintly, of temple incense — a smell Odile had not expected to find comforting, and found comforting anyway, which annoyed her.

"You're late," Odile said.

"The baker was late," Paloma said. "I threatened him with an audit."

"And he wept?"

"He gave me the loaves half-price and asked me to marry his daughter."

Cassian snorted. Wren said, "Which daughter?" with such genuine interest that Odile almost smiled. Almost. Her face had gone stiff in the old ways over the past year; smiling took effort, like rowing against tide. But there was more of it now than there used to be. She noticed these things. She counted them, the way she'd once counted coins she didn't have.

* * *

They ate on the dock because Sefa refused to eat anywhere the sky wasn't visible. This was new, too. Before the palace, the girl had flinched from open sky the way valley folk once flinched from the dark beyond the wards — as if openness itself were a kind of teeth. Now she needed it. Odile understood better than she wanted to. You spend enough time in a cage, even a gilded one, even one you were born in, and either you learn to love the bars or you spend the rest of your life needing to see the edges of everything.

Wren talked with his mouth full, which Paloma corrected twice and then gave up correcting forever. He was fourteen now, or near enough — none of them knew his true age, least of all him — and he had shot up that spring like a weed after floodwater. His voice cracked in the middle of sentences. It cracked in the middle of songs, too, which he pretended not to mind and minded enormously.

"—so Provost Halloway wrote back, actually wrote back, real ink and everything—"

"The same provost who indentured Paloma," Cassian said mildly.

"The same one who's now teaching history instead of debt-collection, yes, thank you, I'm telling a story." Wren swallowed. "He says three families left the valley last month. Just walked out through the pass. Nobody stopped them. There's nothing to stop them *with* anymore." He said this last part carefully, glancing at Paloma, whose magic had gone out of her like a candle pinched between wet fingers, and who never spoke of it, and who everyone therefore spoke around constantly, which Odile suspected was its own particular torture.

Paloma only nodded and tore the loaf and handed pieces around, and Odile saw her hand pause a half-second over Sefa's share — the way you pause over something precious, something you're not sure you deserve to touch.

"We'll write," Paloma said. It was not the first time she'd said it. "Every week. Odile, you'll actually read them?"

"I can read," Odile said. "I choose not to, mostly. Books are full of lies with good posture."

"I'll read them aloud, then."

"To the boat?"

"To whoever's listening."

There would be no one listening, most nights. A ferrywoman and a god-touched child alone on the coast road, asking after origins in towns that had burned their own histories decades ago. But Odile let her say it. Letting people say hopeful things was a kindness she'd learned late and practiced badly.

* * *

The loading took less time than the leaving. That was always the way of it. Odile stowed the packs under the stern bench, checked the bilge, cursed the starboard leak with the fluency of long acquaintance, and rigged the sail while Sefa watched from the bow with her chin on her knees.

It was Wren who broke first. Of course it was Wren.

He tried to hide it — turned toward the water, scrubbed at his face with his sleeve, did the thing boys did where they coughed violently for no reason. But his shoulders shook, and when Paloma put a hand on his back he came apart entirely, folding down onto the dock boards like his strings had been cut.

"I hate this," he said into his sleeves. "I hate that it's always — everyone leaves, that's just what happens, people leave, and you say you'll write and then the letters come slower and then they stop and then—" He ran out of air. "You promised. Both of you. You *promised*."

Odile crouched beside him. Up close, his eyes were red-rimmed and furious and young, so young, younger than she had ever been allowed to be.

"Boy," she said. "Look at me."

He looked.

"I don't make promises. Promises are debts, and I've spent my whole life drowning in other people's debts, and I swore off them the day I buried my brother." She held up one scarred hand before he could protest. "But I'll tell you what I'll do instead. I'll send word. Not letters, maybe — sometimes letters. Sometimes a song, because your girl Sefa taught me three coastal tunes and I sing them badly and the fishermen throw things. Sometimes just a mark on a slate at the Marrow's Landing tavern, the little spiral, you know it. That means *alive, well, still moving*. And if the marks ever stop—" she gripped his chin, gently, forcing his wet eyes to stay on hers, "—you come find us. You and Cassian and Paloma and every able body you can shame into coming along. Do you hear me? Distance is not death. I've died a dozen times waiting for people who were only ever a river away. I won't have you doing the same arithmetic."

Wren hiccuped. "That's basically a promise."

"It's better than a promise. Promises break. Arithmetic doesn't."

"That's not — that's not how arithmetic works—"

"Hush." She pulled him up by the collar, dusted off his knees with two brisk swats, and pushed him toward Sefa, who had risen silently and stood waiting with her hands clasped behind her back in the manner of someone performing a farewell she had rehearsed.

* * *

What Sefa and Wren said to each other, Odile did not hear, and made a point of not hearing. She busied herself with the sail. But she saw the shape of it: the boy pressing something into the girl's palm — a river stone, smooth and grey, the kind children collected and adults forgot — and the girl pressing something back, folded paper, and neither of them speaking, and both of them grinning like idiots at the end of it, which was how the young survived partings, by refusing to treat them as real.

Cassian shook Odile's hand like a comrade's, which it was, now, whatever they'd been at the start — two wary animals circling the same fire. He had filled out since the bridge. The scars there had healed ugly across his forearms, and he no longer bothered hiding them.

"The militia drills are Thursdays," he said. "If you pass through Lowmarsh, come watch. The farmers are terrible. It's very funny."

"I'll bring fruit to throw."

"Bring hard fruit."

Paloma embraced her last, briefly, fiercely, the way she did everything — as if afraid the moment would be taken from her and determined to steal it first. "The chapel walls," she murmured against Odile's ear. "If you find anything on the coast — any record, any song, anything about the Choir's founding — copy it exactly. Don't paraphrase. Don't trust your memory."

"My memory's the only honest thing about me."

"Copy it *exactly*, Odile."

"Aye." She stepped back, and looked at the four of them standing on the dock in the flat morning light — the scribe, the deserter, the boy, all of them thinner and harder and kinder than the ragged creatures who'd crawled out of the lowlands ash a year ago — and felt the old tightness in her throat, the one that meant grief and gratitude wearing each other's clothes. "Well," she said. "Go on, then. Standing there staring makes the boat nervous."

* * *

They cast off on the afternoon tide. Odile worked the tiller; Sefa handled the jib with a competence that surprised no one anymore, the sea taking to her the way roads had taken to Paloma, as if the world recognized its own. The wind came off the land smelling of cut hay and woodsmoke, and the coast unspooled to the north in green and grey, and for a long while the dock and its four figures stayed visible, shrinking but not leaving.

Wren ran along the shore. That was the thing Odile remembered afterward, the thing she would carry down the whole length of the coast and into whatever waited there: the boy running, keeping pace with the ferry as long as the shoreline allowed, waving his arm in great sweeping arcs, shouting something the wind shredded to nothing. Running until the headland rose between them and the water bent away, and then he was gone, and Sefa stood at the stern looking back at the empty stretch of sand where he'd been, holding the river stone so tightly her knuckles went white.

"He'll be fine," Odile said.

"I know."

"He cries at everything. Always has. Cried when the stew was good, once. I was there."

Sefa laughed — sudden, startled out of her, bright as a struck bell. Then she turned forward, toward the open water, and hummed three notes of a tune Odile didn't know, and the tune was new, and it was hers, and nothing ancient moved inside it. Just a girl, making music because music was hers now, unowned.

Odile let the tiller rest in the crook of her arm and dug into her coat for the folded paper Wren had given her. She read it with the boat steady on its heading, the coast sliding by.

*Come back before the stars' anniversary,* it said, in handwriting that still leaned like a fence in a storm. *We're going to sing on the hill where the wards used to be. Everyone. Even the angry ones. Especially the angry ones. There's a descant nobody can reach yet and Paloma says it needs a voice like yours. She wouldn't say why. She went red. Come back.*

Odile folded it away, careful with the creases, and did not wipe her eyes, because there was spray on the wind and anyone who said otherwise could fight her.

* * *

They made the first anchorage at dusk, in a cove called Hollowmere on the charts and nothing at all by the locals, who had burned their charts during the purges and named their places by what survived. Odile cooked barley and salt fish over a driftwood fire while Sefa walked the tideline, picking up shells and setting most of them back down, keeping only the ones that sang — and they did sing, faintly, when she held them to her ear; Odile had checked, expecting trickery, and heard it herself: a thin ringing like a wet finger on glass.

"They remember the sea's voice," Sefa said, sitting down across the fire. "Everything here remembers something. That's why I had to come. My song didn't start with me, Odile. Songs don't start anywhere. They just pass through." She turned a shell in her fingers. "Somewhere up the coast there's a place where mine started. Maybe a mother. Maybe just a village that hummed to their babies the way valley folk hummed to theirs. I want to stand in it."

"And if the place is ash? If the people are dead or scattered or don't remember you at all?"

Sefa considered this with the terrible patience she had, the patience of someone who had been chained and hunted and sung-through and had come out the far side knowing exactly how much of life was not up to her.

"Then I'll stand in the ash," she said, "and I'll sing, and the place will have heard its own song again, wherever it went. That's not nothing."

Odile poked the fire harder than it needed poking. Nine hundred years of vessels, Paloma had told her once, reading the gallery walls — kings and queens and scribes, worn like coats. And this child had carried the same melody and kept her own name. Odile had betrayed her brother to save her skin and told herself for forty years it had been the only choice. She was done telling herself things. That was the other reason for this journey: to walk up the coast and look at what she'd run from, one burned village at a time, until the running was finished.

"Sleep," she said. "Tide turns before dawn."

* * *

They went north through the autumn, town by town, and Odile sent her marks as promised — the little spiral scratched on tavern slates, a letter in her blunt fist-forged hand when a town had a post, once a bundle of sheet music copied out by a hired scrivener because her own writing shamed her. The replies came back slow and irregular, carried by peddlers and pilgrims, and every one of them was read aloud on the deck of the *Patient* while Sefa steered, and every one of them mentioned Wren, because Wren had apparently appointed himself the family's chronicler and wrote as though paper were free.

*The school has forty students now,* Paloma wrote in the third month. *Half of them are adults who were taught lies as children and are furious about it, which Wren says is the best kind of student. Aldric came through last week with his work crew — he's rebuilding the mill at Thornbridge, doing the hauling himself, refusing the cart. Cassian drilled the valley militia on the flats and Aldric stood at the back and learned the footwork with everyone else. No one cheered him. No one drove him off either. Wren says that's how penance works: slowly, and in public, and without applause. Your brother's name came up in class today. Odile — I taught them his name. I hope that was right. It felt right.*

Odile read that line four times. Then she folded the letter, and put it in the tin with the others, and stood at the rail a long while watching the water remember where it was going.

They found the first trace of Sefa's origin in a fishing village called Greywether, where an old woman heard the girl humming and dropped her net-mending and wept, saying her own grandmother had sung that melody and called it the *coming-home song*, and that her grandmother's people had come from further north, from a place drowned or abandoned, she couldn't recall which. They went further north. In a chapel ruin roofless to the sky, Sefa found a wall carving — waves, and above them a line of small figures with their mouths open, singing the sea calm — and pressed her palm to it and hummed, and the acoustics of the broken roof caught her voice and gave it back multiplied, a choir of one made many, and Odile stood in the doorway with cold wind on her face and understood that they had found the beginning, or the edge of it.

That night, anchored off the chapel shore, Odile wrote the longest letter she had ever written. It took her until the moon set. She described the carving, the acoustics, the old woman's words, the exact melody as Sefa sang it — and then, in a paragraph she nearly burned twice, she described her brother: his name, his laugh, the way he'd hummed while mending nets, the way the entity had worn him gentle before it wore him hollow, and the way she had chosen herself over him and carried it like ballast ever since. She wrote it because Paloma had said *copy it exactly*, and because a family that kept ledgers of debt ought also to keep ledgers of truth, and because Wren cried at everything and someone in this family had to put the crying down on paper where it could be reread and survived.

She sealed it and addressed it to the Temple of Quiet Hours, care of Paloma Thackeray, and in the morning she paid a coastal trader triple rate to carry it south with the winter mails.

Then she stood on the deck of the leaking, lying, stubbornly seaworthy *Patient*, with the drowned chapel at her back and the whole grey unknown coast ahead, and felt the strangest thing: lightness. Not the lightness of having put something down. The lightness of carrying something properly, at last, with both hands.

"Sefa," she called. "Up anchor. We've got a coast to ask questions of."

The girl moved to obey — and then stopped, head cocked, facing north into the wind, where the water darkened under a cloud that hadn't been there a moment before. She was humming before Odile could ask why. Three notes, four, rising — not the coming-home song, not anything Sefa had sung before. Something coming *off* the wind, thin and far, threaded through the salt air like wire through cloth.

"What is it?" Odile said. "What do you hear?"

Sefa's eyes were wide and bright and afraid in the old way, the way she had not been afraid since the palace.

"A voice," she said. "Far away. Someone's singing where no one should be — and Odile—" she swallowed, gripping the rail as the first cold drops of the storm struck the deck, "—it sounds like it's calling *us* home."
