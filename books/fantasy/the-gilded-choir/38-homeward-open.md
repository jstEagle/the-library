## 38. Homeward, Open

The pass looked smaller than Paloma remembered.

That was the first lie the road told her — that it had ever been small. For twenty-three years the pass had been the edge of everything, a gray seam in the mountains where the world simply stopped, where the wards had hung like frost on glass and no one thought to wonder what glass was for. She had copied ledgers beneath that seam. She had tallied debts of grain and candlewax and years of her own life beneath it, and she had never once looked up.

Now she stood at the mouth of it with Wren at her side and a mule named Turnip who had opinions about elevation, and the pass was just stone. Wind came through it carrying pine and snowmelt and the ordinary smell of distance. No shimmer. No hum. The wards were gone, and the air where they had been felt the way a missing tooth feels — a bright absence the tongue keeps returning to.

"You're doing the face," Wren said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're writing something in your head and it's sad."

Paloma huffed. "I'm not writing anything."

"You're always writing something." He adjusted the strap of his pack — too big for him still, though he'd grown half a hand's width since the capital, since the choir, since the night the sky tore open and gave them all back to themselves. Thirteen years old and he carried his satchel of sheet music like a soldier carries a shield. "You used to do it over the ledgers. Provost Merrin called it your ledger-face. I called it your going-to-crie-but-in-neat-handwriting face."

"Provost Merrin," Paloma said, "is going to be a problem."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Below them, through the last fold of the pass, the valley opened up: the terraced fields gone gold with late barley, the river stitching silver through its floor, the town of Quiet Hours itself with its slate roofs and its temple bell tower rising like a finger raised in permanent admonition. Smoke rose from chimneys in thin, orderly columns. Everything exactly as they had left it.

Everything nothing like they had left it.

"It looks the same," Wren said, and there was something wounded in how he said it, as if the valley owed them more than this — owed them visible scars, some mark of what had been done to it and undone.

"No," Paloma said slowly. "Look again. Look at the fields."

He looked. And then he saw it too, the way you see a room has been rearranged only after you've walked into the wall where the door used to be. The fields were planted differently. Crooked rows where straight rows had been mandated for generations. A fallow strip left wild along the riverbank, weeds shoulder-high, flowering. Someone had let land go unplanted on purpose, which in the valley of Quiet Hours was not agriculture but heresy.

"They don't know what to do," Paloma said. "No one's told them what to do. So they're guessing."

"That's terrible."

"That's freedom," she said. "It looks terrible from above. It feels different from inside."

They started down.

* * *

The town received them the way a body receives a splinter it has half-forgotten: with a small, localized inflammation.

Word ran ahead of them, as word does — the ferryman's boy saw them at the bridge and by the time they reached the market square perhaps forty people had found reasons to be carrying water or inspecting shutters within eyeshot. Paloma recognized nearly all of them. Mistress Halloway, who had sold her eggs to the temple kitchens for thirty years and had once, when Paloma was nine, given her a heel of bread without entering it in any ledger. Old Tam Voss, who had taught her to whistle through a blade of grass before the temple taught her that whistling was idle. Faces she had copied into debt columns. Names she had written ten thousand times in ink that outlasted the hands that earned them.

Nobody shouted. That was the thing about Quiet Hours; even its anger kept its voice down.

But nobody cheered either. And when Wren waved — actually waved, that open-handed, whole-arm wave he had learned from Odile, who believed greetings should cost something — the faces turned away one by one, politely, like pages being closed.

"They hate us," Wren said under his breath.

"They're afraid," Paloma said. "It looks similar from the outside."

She understood it better than she wanted to. She had spent the whole road home composing speeches — she was a scribe, composition was how she prayed — and every speech had assumed an audience that wanted to hear it. Standing in the square now, smelling bread and dung and river mud, hearing the temple bell toll the third hour as it had tolled every hour of her life, she understood that the valley did not want a speech. The valley wanted its stars back. The valley wanted the sky to make sense again, and no one alive could give it that, because the sky had never made sense; it had only been covered.

A woman detached from the shutter-inspectors and crossed the square with the deliberate pace of someone who has decided to be brave and resents every step of it. Mistress Halloway. Older. Her hands more knuckled. But the same level brown eyes that had once assessed a hungry child and decided, off the books, to feed her.

"Paloma Thackeray," she said. Not warmly. Not coldly. Testing the name against the person, like a coin bitten.

"Mistress Halloway."

"They say you burned the ledgers."

"I burned my ledger," Paloma said. "In the capital. Publicly. It was very satisfying and slightly illegal."

Something moved at the corner of the woman's mouth and was suppressed. "And now you've come back."

"This is my home."

"Is it?" Mistress Halloway looked past her, at the pass, at the empty sky beyond it where wards had been and stars would come tonight, if stars still came. "My son is in the lowlands now. Went through the pass in spring, first anyone's gone through in living memory. He sends letters." She said *letters* the way other people said *heresy*. "He says there are cities, Paloma. Cities bigger than the whole valley. He says the king — the real one, the poor hollowed man — he says Aldric himself is out breaking rocks rebuilding villages his soldiers burned." Her voice tightened. "None of that was in any book in the temple. None of it. We lived our whole lives inside a lie with a bell on top of it, and you knew."

"I didn't know," Paloma said. "Not until it was almost too late to matter."

"You suspected."

Yes. She had suspected. In the archive, among the forbidden pre-warding texts, she had read the phrase *the Choir* and felt the ground tilt. She had chosen to keep copying instead of asking why the provost locked those shelves. She had chosen survival over truth for years, one small cowardly transcription at a time, and the interest on that debt had compounded exactly the way the temple taught that all debts compound.

"Yes," she said. "I suspected. And I'm sorry. And sorry isn't worth much, so I've brought something else instead."

Mistress Halloway waited.

"A school," Paloma said.

* * *

The provost received them in the scriptorium, because of course he did; Merrin had never in his life held a difficult conversation anywhere with a window.

He had aged badly in the year she'd been gone. The wards' collapse had taken the temple's purpose with them — the Quiet Hours had been founded, everyone now knew, to maintain the cage, to keep the ledger of the jailers' descendants, to file the valley's captivity under *piety* — and Merrin wore the loss like a wet coat. He sat behind the great copyist's desk that had been his master's and his master's master's, and his fingers drummed on its edge, and he did not rise when she entered.

"Sister Thackeray." He still used the honorific. It was either courtesy or a claim, and with Merrin it was always a claim. "You bring the child back. Good. The archives suffered losses during the unrest. We require trained hands."

"The archives," Paloma said, "are half lies."

"The archives are *order*."

"The archives are a cage's maintenance manual," she said, "bound in calfskin and shelved alphabetically. I read them, Provost. All of them, in the capital, with time and light enough to understand what I was reading. The founding texts. The ward-tithes. The *Choir* fragment you let me copy because you thought curiosity would keep me obedient." She laid her satchel on his desk — the same gesture, she realized, that she had once used to present finished work for his approval, and she hated herself a little for the echo, and did it anyway. "I brought copies. Of everything. The true history. Who built the wards. Who paid for them. Whose blood was bred to renew them." She tapped the satchel. "Mine. Generation after generation of mine. You knew. Your predecessors knew. There's a letter in here from a provost two hundred years dead thanking the Thackerays for their 'continued service,' as if we were a well he was grateful hadn't run dry."

Merrin looked at the satchel the way a man looks at a snake someone has set gently on his desk.

"The people can't bear this," he said finally, and his voice cracked on *can't*, and she saw that he believed it — that underneath the institutional arrogance there was a tired old copyist who genuinely thought he was holding a roof up. "You want to hand them the truth all at once? Half of them will kneel down and start praying to the thing in the dark just to have something to pray to. Fear needs walls, girl. Take the walls away and you must give fear somewhere else to live, or it moves into the people."

"Then give it a school," Paloma said. "Let it live there, where we can question it."

"And who teaches? You?" His laugh was dry as scraped vellum. "You're a debtor. Or you were. The council in the capital voided your indenture, yes, I've read the proclamation — the council that replaced a king with a *committee*, may the Hours preserve us. You have no standing here. The temple has standing. The temple has fed this valley, buried its dead, kept its hours—"

"The temple caged its sky."

"The temple kept it *safe*!"

The shout rang off the scriptorium's low ceiling and died fast, embarrassed. In the silence Paloma heard the bell toll the fourth hour, and heard, beneath it, Wren's voice in the courtyard below — he had slipped out while she talked, of course he had — teaching someone to whistle through a blade of grass. A child's laughter answered him. Then another.

Merrin heard it too. Something in his face folded.

"Some will stay angry," Paloma said quietly. "At me, at you, at the sky. Some will want the wards back and will tell you I stole their safety, and they'll be right in a way, and I'll live with it. But many will come, Provost. To learn. They already are — I watched them plant crooked fields. People are guessing, out there. Guessing is just learning with the fear showing." She picked up her satchel. "Give them the building. Not the debt-house — the school. Same stones. Different doors."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I'll teach under the linden tree in the square, in all weather, and everyone will notice which of us is standing outside."

She was at the door when he spoke again, and his voice had gone small, the voice of the man behind the office, the copyist whose whole life had been faithful transcription of things that deserved burning.

"Was it true?" he asked. "What they say. That the binding needed a life freely given. That children—" He stopped. Started again. "Did the founders really—"

"Read the copies," Paloma said. "When you're ready. They'll be in the school. Door's open."

* * *

Some stayed angry. She had promised herself they would, and they obliged.

Old Deun, the warden of the granary, spat when she passed and told anyone who'd listen that the Thackeray witch had broken God's fence and let the wolves of heaven in. Three families pulled their children from the classes in the temple's west hall — the keys had arrived on her doorstep one morning with no note, which from Merrin was practically a love letter — and sent them instead to the provost's remaining scriptorium, where the old hours were still kept and the old texts still copied and nothing new was ever, ever learned. A letter appeared nailed to the temple door, written in a careful hand: *We were safe. We did not ask to be free.* Paloma read it twice. It hurt more than shouting would have, because it was honest, because she remembered being the person who would have signed it, the girl in the gallery who had thought the ceiling was the sky and called the cage a canopy.

She left the letter up. She wrote beneath it, in her neat hand, the only answer she trusted: *Neither did I. Come ask questions anyway. First lesson free.*

Many came.

They came the way water comes through a cracked dam — a trickle first, then threads, then a singing pressure. Mistress Halloway came on the second day and sat in the back row with her arms folded like a border guard, and stayed until winter, and eventually asked a question so good that Paloma wrote it above the door: *If the wards were a cage, what were we keeping in, and did it suffer?* Tam Voss came and taught the children grass-whistling between history lessons, unrepentant. Farmers came in off the fields still smelling of barley to hear about the Gallery of Vessels, about the scribe with her own face hanging in a palace a mountain-range away, about the nine-hundred-year-old man who had died weeping and free. Mothers came. The angry ones' children came, sneaking, and were fed, and went home with questions that did Paloma's arguing for her.

Wren taught music. It had been his idea and it was the best one anyone had. "You can't teach them the true history without the songs," he said, with the ferocious certainty of thirteen. "The entity sang. The Choir sang. Sefa sang. It's all singing, Paloma, it was always singing, and they took the music out of the valley and nobody even noticed because the bell was loud enough." So there were hymns unlearned and rounds learned, and the descant from the pre-warding texts that Paloma had copied in secret at fifteen, guilty as theft, beautiful as dawn — she taught it line by line, and the children's voices caught it and carried it out the open windows into the square, where the angry people heard it going by and could not, quite, close their shutters against it.

On the evening the first star reappeared — a full year after the night they all went out, as if the sky too needed time to trust the open air — the whole valley stood in the streets and fields and pointed, and cried, and argued about whether it was a sign, and Paloma stood among them with Wren's head at her shoulder height now, absurdly, when had that happened, and felt the absence in her chest where her magic-sense had been. She could not feel the star. Could not feel anything of the deep song anymore; that had gone with the mark, spent like a candle spent lighting others. But she could see the star. Anyone could. Everyone could.

Maybe, she thought, that was the better sense.

* * *

Winter came down off the passes and the school filled like a warm room.

They held the solstice session by lamplight, forty bodies and a fire, and Paloma stood before them with the founding texts open on the lectern and realized, mid-sentence, that she was not afraid. A year ago she had been a debtor copying other people's words in fear of a ledger. Now the words were hers, the room was hers, the fear had been spent on larger things and had not refilled. She looked out at the faces — Halloway, Voss, the miller's skeptical sons, three of Deun's grandchildren smuggled in by their mother — and she saw the valley looking back at her, terrified and furious and free, and she loved it with a love that had teeth.

"One more thing before the songs," she said. "One year ago tonight, the stars went out. Next year, on that night, I propose we do something no one in this valley has ever done." She let the silence lean in. "We go outside. All of us. Into the square, onto the hillsides, wherever we like — and we sing. Openly. Under the actual sky. No walls, no wards, no permission asked. The Gilded Choir, they called the thing that caged us. Let's take the name back and gild it with our own voices."

Someone laughed — nervous, then less so. Tam Voss began, gravely, to tune a blade of grass.

And it was then, in the warm noise of the room, that Wren touched her elbow and held out the letter he'd been carrying all day, unopened, patient as Wren. The seal was coastal wax, blue-green, stamped with a ferry oar. Odile's hand on the direction, blunt and unsentimental. And beneath it, in a different hand entirely — round, careful, a child's hand grown steadier over many months of practice — a single line on the outer leaf:

*We found where I come from. There are more of us. P.S. Can choirs have two first voices?*

Paloma read it twice. Outside, the winter stars were coming out one by one, thickening, remembering. Inside, Wren was watching her face with the expression of a boy who already knew, the way he had known about the star flickering out a lifetime ago, that the world was about to get bigger again.

"Well?" he said.

Paloma laughed, and reached for her pen.
