## 1. The Keeper's Round

The bell in the gatehouse tower had no clapper. It never had, as far as Theo knew—nine generations of Underhills had climbed the same worn steps to the same rope, and nine generations had rung a silent bell each dawn, and not one of them had ever asked why. Theo asked sometimes. Asking was allowed. Answering was not.

They pulled the rope the way their mother had taught them: three slow swings, letting the dead weight of it speak through their palms rather than their ears. *One* for the valley. *Two* for the gate. *Three* for whatever waited on the other side of both.

Outside, Hollowmere was still blue with the last of the night. Frost furred the bracken along the switchback path, and the river below ran thin and bright as spun glass, and somewhere up-slope a woodpecker was knocking at the world as if it owed him an answer. Theo stood in the belfry with their breath ghosting silver and let the quiet settle over them like a coat that fit exactly right.

This was the part of the day they loved best—the part before anyone else was awake to need anything from them. In an hour the village would be stirring, and Tam Wicker would come shuffling up with his rheumatic complaints, and the Aldery children would race their goats past the well, and someone would inevitably have a question about the gate, or the wards, or the rule, the one rule, the only rule Hollowmere had ever had:

*Nobody leaves.*

But for now the valley belonged to Theo alone, and to the work.

They took the round the way it had always been taken: sunwise, starting east.

* * *

The eastern ward-stones stood in a broken crescent above the terraced fields, twelve of them, each taller than a man and older than any language the valley remembered. They were unremarkable things to look at—grey granite, lichen-crusted, leaning like tired shepherds—but Theo knew better than to trust their eyes with the wards. Eyes were for the village. For the stones, they used their hands.

Theo pressed both palms flat against the first stone and closed their eyes.

Cold first. Then, beneath the cold, the hum. Every Underhill felt it differently, their grandmother had said; hers had been a taste like iron, her father's a sound like distant surf. For Theo it was warmth—a low, banked warmth, like a hearth-fire slept beside, like a hand held loosely around theirs. The ward-line ran from stone to stone through the frozen ground, and Theo walked it palm to palm, reading it the way a shepherd reads a flock: counting, checking, noting what was well and what was not.

Stone one: steady. Stone two: steady. Stone three had a hairline flaw near its base where lightning had kissed it forty years ago, and it always ran a little warmer there, a little rougher, like a voice gone slightly hoarse. Steady enough. Stone four—

Theo stopped.

The warmth stuttered.

It lasted less than a heartbeat. One moment the line sang smooth and continuous under their hands, and the next it *skipped*, the way a heart skips, the way a candle gutters in a room with no draft—and then it was back, seamless, as if nothing had happened at all.

Theo stood with their palms pressed to the cold granite for a long time, long enough for the frost to melt in two hand-shaped prints, long enough for the woodpecker to give up and go elsewhere.

Twice this month.

The first time had been eleven days ago, on the western arc, so brief they'd told themselves they'd imagined it—that they'd been tired, that the lambing season had worn them thin. Keepers were human. Hands slipped. Minds wandered.

The second time had been four days ago, here in the east, and it had lasted longer, and Theo had spent four days telling themselves the same story with less conviction each time.

And now a third.

They finished the round because the round must always be finished—that was the first thing every Underhill learned, before they learned the words of the warding, before they learned why the bell had no clapper. You finish the round. You do not stop in the middle of a thing because it frightened you. Stones five through twelve were steady, warm, whole. The flaw at stone three was no worse than it had been in their mother's day. By every measure that mattered, the wards of Hollowmere were sound.

By every measure except the one that counted twice this month and now a third time, and which Theo had told no one about.

There was no one to tell, that was the thing. That was the joke of it, if it was a joke. Nine generations of keepers, and Theo was the last, and the knowledge lived in exactly one skull now, and that skull belonged to a person who had never once left the valley, who had never seen the sea or a city or even the far side of the pass, who had inherited a duty the size of a kingdom and a kingdom's worth of questions that no living soul could answer.

*Mother would know,* they thought, and then, as always, the thought turned over and showed its underside: *Mother is four years in the churchyard, and she never told me either.*

Theo wiped their hands on their coat and climbed down from the eastern terrace to feed the beacon.

* * *

The beacon-fire burned in a stone cradle at the valley's throat, just inside the gate—a squat ring of fitted blocks, black with centuries of smoke, holding a flame that had not gone out in living memory. It wasn't a signal fire. It heated nothing, lit nothing, cooked nothing. Travelers' tales said the old kings of Aldenmere had built a thousand such beacons along their roads, and all of them had gone cold when the dynasty faltered, but Hollowmere's had burned on, tended by keepers who could not have said what it was for.

Theo fed it the way the journal said—not the family journal, the *other* one, the one chained to the lectern in the gatehouse archive, written in a hand so old the ink had gone brown as dried blood. Split birch first, then ash, then a single log of rowan, and while each piece caught, you spoke the keeping-words. Theo didn't know what the words meant. They knew how they felt in the mouth: like swallowing a warm stone.

*"Ash for the roof-beam, rowan for the door,"* they murmured, laying the log across the flames. *"Birch for the road that comes no more."*

The fire took it greedily. And for a moment—just a moment, the way it always did—the flame burned a color that fire has no business burning: a deep gold shot through with green, like sunlight through bottle glass, like the eyes of a cat in a dark room. Then it settled back into ordinary orange, and Theo exhaled, and the morning went on being ordinary.

They crouched by the cradle and held their hands out to the heat, watching the light move on the frost-bright grass beyond the gate. Through the arch of the gate itself—the great stone portal, empty of any door these hundred years and more—they could see the road winding away north between the shoulders of the fells, climbing toward the pass, disappearing into the grey. The road was the strangest thing in Hollowmere, if you thought about it. A road implies destinations. A road implies people coming and going. And yet no cart had rolled through the gate in Theo's lifetime, no rider had come down it, no pilgrim or peddler or lost soul had ever staggered out of the pass asking for shelter.

The road simply went away, and nothing ever came back down it, and nobody in the village seemed to find this strange except Theo, who had learned very young not to say so.

*Why does no one leave?* they'd asked their mother at seven years old, tugging her sleeve in the market square.

*Because the valley keeps us safe,* she'd said, weighing out barley with her quick, capable hands.

*Safe from what?*

She'd paused then—just a fraction, the barley still ticking into the scale—and smiled at the customer instead, and answered the question that hadn't been asked. *Morning, Widow Marsh! Lovely frost we're having.*

That was the shape of every answer Theo had ever gotten. Not a wall. A smile, turned slightly aside.

* * *

By midmorning the frost had burned off and the village was awake, and Theo came down the hill to find Tam Wicker waiting for them outside the forge with his cap in his hands, which meant trouble, because Tam only removed his cap when he wanted something he suspected he wouldn't get.

"Keeper." He nodded. His breath smelled of the morning's first cider. "Might I have a word."

"You're having several," Theo said. "Go on."

"It's my lad. Jory." Tam worked the cap in his fists. "He's nineteen now, and strong as an ox, and there's naught for him here. The Alderys have all their sons set to work, and the mill takes none, and—" He stopped, gathered himself. "There's work over the pass. Real work. Coin work. His cousin sends word from the river towns—Bellwether way—says the mapmakers' guild is taking lads on, honest trade, and Jory's got a good eye for lines and figures, always has—"

"Tam."

"—and I know, I *know* the rule, but I'm asking you, keeper to neighbor, man to man—is it forever? Is it truly forever? Because my grandfather asked your grandmother the same question, and she gave him the same look you're giving me now, and nobody in this valley has given me a straight answer in ninety years—"

"Tam." Theo put a hand on his shoulder. Under the wool, the old man was trembling slightly, though whether from cold or something else was hard to say. "You know I can't open the gate."

"I'm not asking you to open it. I'm asking *why*. Just once. Just tell me why, and I'll carry it home to my boy, and that'll be the end of it."

And there it was. The question underneath every question, the one Theo had been turning over like a river stone since childhood, feeling for the answer beneath it and finding only more stone. *Why.* Their mother had died without saying. Their grandmother had died without saying. The chained journal in the archive said many things in its brown old ink, but not that—or not plainly, or not in any cipher Theo had cracked, and Theo had spent entire winters trying.

What Theo said was: "The wards are failing."

It came out before they could stop it—not the truth, but shaped like the truth, close enough to the truth that it didn't curdle on the tongue. Tam blinked.

"Failing?"

"There's sickness beyond the pass. Bad sickness. The kind that gets into a place and doesn't leave." Theo heard their own voice doing what every Underhill's voice had done for nine generations: steady, sorry, final. "The gate stays shut until it passes. That's the whole of it, Tam. It isn't cruelty. It's the reason the valley exists at all."

Tam looked at them for a long moment. Behind him the forge smoke rose straight up into the windless sky, and somewhere a dog was barking at nothing, and the beacon-fire's thread of smoke was visible even from here, standing off the gatehouse like a plumb line dropped from heaven.

"My boy's going to hate this valley," Tam said quietly. "He's going to hate it, and one day he's going to walk up to that gate and keep walking, and what will you do then, keeper? Will you stop him?"

"No," Theo said. "I'll warn him. And I'll tend the fire after."

Something moved behind the old man's eyes—pity, maybe, or its cousin. He put his cap back on. "Your mother used to give that same answer. Word for word." He turned away, then paused. "Folk say the wards flickered Tuesday night. Saw it from the high pasture. Green light, under the eastern stones, like lightning underground. Did you see it?"

Theo's stomach dropped through the frozen ground, through the ward-line, through the bedrock of the world.

"No," they said. "Must've been foxfire."

Tam nodded slowly, unconvinced, and shuffled off toward the forge, and Theo stood alone in the lane with the lie sitting in their chest like a swallowed coal.

Because they *had* seen it. They had seen it every time—the green flash beneath the stones, the gutter of the line, the skip in the valley's heartbeat. Three times now. Three times in one month, when in their mother's whole lifetime it had happened perhaps once a decade, and always in deep winter, always harmless, always followed by nothing.

Three times, and Theo had told no one, because there was no one to tell, and because some stubborn Underhill pride whispered that a keeper who couldn't read her own wards had no business alarming a valley full of people who trusted her name.

But tonight, they promised themselves, staring up at the silent bell-tower. Tonight I'll take the chained journal down and read it cover to cover, every cipher, every marginal scrawl, until I know what we've been guarding all this time—or until I know we never knew either.

The bell had no clapper. The road led nowhere. The fire burned a color fire shouldn't burn.

And that evening, as the light failed over the fells and Theo climbed the tower steps to ring the dusk-peal, they saw something on the northern road that made them stop with their hand still on the rope.

A figure. Small, staggering, wrong-gaited, coming *down* out of the pass.

Nothing ever came down out of the pass.

Theo watched it fall, and rise, and fall again—and saw, even at this distance, even in the dying light, the dark trail it left on the frosted road behind it.

Then they were running, down the stairs three at a time, out through the empty gate, onto the road their family had guarded for nine generations, toward the first stranger Hollowmere had ever let in.
