## 4. What the Wards Were For

They buried Sera Vane at dawn, in the strip of ground between the gatehouse and the beacon-fire, where the soil was softest and the wards ran strongest. Theo dug the grave alone because there was no one else to ask, and because their hands needed something honest to do. The earth came up dark and cold-smelling, threaded with old roots, and each spadeful felt like a question Theo couldn't answer.

She had died in the night. Theo had known it would happen—had sat beside her through the small hours with a basin of water and a cloth, watching the magic-wounds do what gatekeeper's salves could not touch—and still, when her breathing finally stopped, it had landed like a blow. She'd gone quietly, between one shallow breath and the next, her hand curled loosely around nothing.

Around nothing, because she had given the map away hours before. It lay now on Theo's table, weighted at the corners with river stones, and Theo could not stop looking at it.

*Unbind him before solstice.*

The words had come out of her cracked and barely voiced, more breath than sound, and Theo had leaned close to catch them and caught something else instead: the terrible steadiness in her eyes. She hadn't been delirious. She had chosen this valley, chosen this gate, chosen *them*, with whatever strength she had left. A dying woman had crossed half a kingdom to put a bloodstained map into the hands of a stranger, and called it by name.

*Underhill.*

Theo finished the grave, laid her in it wrapped in the good blanket from the third-floor chest—the one that smelled of cedar and their grandmother's soap—and filled it in. They said no formal words, because Underhills didn't have any for strangers; they said only, "I'll find out what you were carrying," which was not a prayer but felt close enough.

Then they went inside, washed the grave-dirt from under their nails, and did the thing they had been avoiding since the moment Sera whispered that word.

They opened the archives.

* * *

The gatehouse archive occupied the lowest floor of the oldest part of the building, down a stair worn into a smooth trough by nine generations of descending feet. Theo knew every shelf of it the way other people knew their own cupboards. Ward-records going back two centuries, ink faded to the color of weak tea. Maintenance ledgers for the beacon-fire, its fuel measured in cords and seasons. The great ward-diagrams, drawn on vellum stiff as bark, showing the ring of standing stones and the buried lines that connected them—a closed circle, always a closed circle, unbroken since the founding.

Nine generations of Underhills had kept those records. Nine generations had taught their children the same catechism: *the wards keep magic out. The valley is safe because nothing of the other world can cross our border. This is your duty. Guard it, and guard the silence around it, for knowledge invites hunger.*

Theo had believed it the way they believed in stone.

But Sera had said *Underhill* like a key turning in a lock, and she had said *unbind him*, and *the crown is eating him*, and none of those words fit inside the catechism. So Theo took down the oldest ledgers first—the founding records, the ones no one read because everyone knew what they said—and began, for the first time in their life, to actually look.

The morning passed. Dust settled on their sleeves. The beacon-fire ticked and shifted overhead, its warmth bleeding down through the floorboards, and Theo read.

And found nothing. That was the first wrongness. The founding generation had been meticulous about everything—every stone, every fuel delivery, every flicker logged in a neat brown hand—but of the *purpose* of the wards, the actual reason nine generations had given their lives to this valley, there was nothing. Not one sentence. The records simply began, as if the Underhills had sprung full-grown from the hillside with a duty already in their pockets.

People don't build a cage and forget why. People hide the why.

Theo stood in the middle of the archive with dust motes drifting through the lamplight and let themselves think the thought they'd been circling all morning: *Grandmother knew.* Their grandmother, Edith Underhill, who had taught them the catechism with such fierce tenderness, who had held their hands over the first ward-stone and made them swear the oath of silence—who had, in her last year, spent long evenings alone down here, doing something she never explained.

Doing something she hid.

Theo started with her chair. It stood in the corner where it had always stood, a high-backed thing upholstered in horsehair gone bald at the arms. Theo turned it over. Nothing under the seat, nothing in the frame. They searched the desk next, then the map chest, then the false drawer behind the real one that every child in the family discovered at age six and was sworn to secrecy about at age seven. Empty, empty, empty.

It was nearly dusk when they thought to look at the shelves themselves.

The west wall held the ward-diagrams, mounted on iron brackets. Theo had taken them down and replaced them a thousand times without ever asking why the brackets were set so deep, or why the wall behind them was faced with stone slightly newer than the rest—newer, and fitted with a care that drew the eye if you let it. They pulled down the diagrams, all seven, stacked them on the table, and pressed their palm flat against the wall.

Cold. Colder than the rest of the cellar, cold in a way that had texture, like touching the skin of deep water. And beneath their palm, faint as a heartbeat heard through a door, something answered. A hum. The same note the outer stones sang when Theo checked them at dawn—except inverted. The wards outside pushed outward. This pulled *in*.

"Oh," Theo said aloud, to the empty cellar. "Oh, you clever, frightened woman."

There was no seam, no handle, no hinge. There didn't need to be. The wall was warded shut, and Theo had been raised on ward-craft the way farmers' children are raised on weather. They found the anchor-sigil by feel, traced its counter-line with a steady finger, and spoke the releasing word their grandmother had made them memorize at eight years old—*for emergencies only, little wren, though I pray you'll never need it*—and the stone sighed apart along a line no eye could find, swinging inward on darkness and the smell of paper and tallow and time.

A hidden room, no bigger than a coffin stood on end. And inside it, on a single shelf, a journal bound in grey leather, with Edith Underhill's name tooled on the cover in her own careful hand.

Theo carried it to the lamp and did not open it right away. Their heart was behaving strangely—too slow, too loud, each beat arriving separately like footsteps in an empty house. Somewhere above, the beacon-fire popped. Outside, true dark was coming down over the valley, and the outer wards would be singing their evening round, steady as ever, steady as lies.

They opened the journal.

* * *

The first entry was dated sixty-one years back, the year before Theo's mother was born. The handwriting was young, quick, slanting hard to the right.

*I asked Father today why we keep the silence. He gave me the catechism. Magic out, valley safe, knowledge invites hunger. I asked him who built the wards and he said our ancestors, and I asked him what they were afraid of, and he looked at me the way he looks at the fire—as if I'd asked the flame why it burns. I am sixteen years old and I have just understood that my father does not know either. We are all of us guarding a door whose other side no living Underhill has seen.*

Page after page. Theo read standing, then sitting, then kneeling on the cold flags because their legs had stopped agreeing to hold them. The journal tracked Edith's whole life in fragments—her marriage, her children, her rise to keeper—and threaded through all of it, one question, asked again and again in different hands' worth of years:

*Why does the circle pull inward?*

Because that was the thing. Theo had checked those stones ten thousand mornings and never once questioned the direction of the song. Outward push at the border—that was the catechism, magic cannot cross. But the diagrams told another story if you knew how to read them, and Edith had known. The outer ring pushed. Everything beneath it, everything at the center, the buried lines running inward from every stone like roots toward a taproot—those *pulled*. The valley wasn't a shield. It was a funnel. Everything the wards caught, everything they turned away at the border, drained down and inward, toward Hollowmere itself, toward the gatehouse, toward—

Toward the beacon-fire.

Theo's eyes went to the ceiling. To the fire they had fed every morning of their life, the fire they had tended like a hearth, like a sacred trust, the fire their grandmother had taught them was the *heart* of the wards.

*The beacon is not a light,* Edith had written, forty years into the journal, in handwriting gone thin and precise. *God forgive me, it has never been a light. It is a mouth. Or rather—it is what keeps the mouth closed. The thing beneath the valley feeds on what the wards gather, and the fire binds it, the way a stitch binds a wound. As long as it burns, the thing sleeps. My grandfather tended it. His grandmother tended it. None of us knew what we were stitching shut until I followed the lines down.*

*It is kin to the thing in the crown of Aldenmere. That much the old papers make plain, in language I wish I had never learned to read. When the last king was crowned, they bound a hunger into his circlet—an anchor, a leash, a way of holding the creature to one body, one will, one slow devouring, so that it could not run loose and wake its siblings. And the wards of Hollowmere were raised for the same purpose, in the same age, by the same hands. We were never the kingdom's shield. We are its second lock. The crown holds one of them. We hold the other.*

*Every catechism I ever taught my children was a kindness shaped like a lie. Magic cannot cross our borders—true, because the wards drink it first and feed it to the sleeper below. The valley is safe—true, for the sleeper. Knowledge invites hunger—true, oh, how true, for the hunger is not in the knowledge. It is in the ground.*

Theo stopped reading. The cellar was very cold, or Theo was. Their breath showed faintly in the lamplight.

Below them. Below the archive, below the foundations, below the taproot of the ward-lines, something slept, and had slept for centuries, and nine generations of Underhills had walked over it, planted over it, raised children over it, sung the morning rounds above it—all unknowing, all trusting, all lied to by the ones who came before, gently, out of love, out of terror, out of the simple arithmetic that a sleeping thing is best left undiscussed.

And the wards had flickered. Twice this month. Twice, and Theo hadn't told anyone, because there was no one to tell, because telling was forbidden, because the silence was the duty—

*The binding weakens as the crown weakens,* Edith had written near the end, in a hand that shook. *They are stitched to each other, crown and valley, anchor and anchor. If the crown fails—if the king is consumed and the circlet's purpose dies with him—then our sleeper wakes, and no fire and no fence will hold it. I have written this down so that someone after me might understand in time. Forgive me, little wren. I could not say it aloud. I hope you never read this. I hope you read it soon.*

The last entry was dated three weeks before her death. One line only:

*The courier came today. I sent her away. God help me, I sent her away.*

Theo sat on the cold floor of the archive for a long time, the journal open in their lap, and let the betrayal move through them like weather. It was not rage, exactly. Rage would have been simpler. It was the vertigo of a floor revealed as a lid—the discovery that every contented morning of their life had been spent standing on a prisoner, that the safety they had guarded so proudly was a cell's quiet, that the loneliness they had made peace with had been manufactured, maintained, *required*. Nine generations of Underhills had not been protectors. They had been jailers who were never told they were jailers, guarding a secret even from themselves, and the kindest person Theo had ever loved had known, and had chosen to pass the lie on with warm hands and a gentle voice.

*I could not say it aloud.*

No. But a dying stranger had. Sera Vane, bleeding against the outer stones, whispering *Underhill* like a password—which it had been, Theo realized, it must have been, some old sign between the crown's servants and the valley's keepers, a thread of connection Edith had refused sixty years ago and Sera had come to collect at the end of her life. *Unbind him before solstice.* Not save the king. Unbind the *binding*. Because the crown and the valley were one knot, and you could not loosen one without loosening both.

And the wards had flickered twice this month. And solstice was nineteen days away.

Theo closed the journal. Held it against their chest. Above them the beacon-fire crackled on, patient and enormous, and for the first time in their life Theo heard it correctly—not a hearth, not a heart, but a held breath. A stitch straining. A mouth kept shut by firelight.

They climbed the stairs slowly, the journal under their arm, and stood a while in the doorway of the great room, looking at the map spread on the table, weighted with its four river stones. Sera's blood had dried brown along one edge. The route it showed ran east, out of the valley, across country Theo had never seen, to a capital full of gilded lies and a king being eaten alive.

Nineteen days. The wards flickering. A sleeper underneath, and a knot that could only be untied at the far end of its thread.

Theo looked at the gate—the great outer gate, barred and warded, unopened in their lifetime—and understood, with a calm that frightened them more than fear would have, what they were going to have to do.

They would have to leave. They would have to open the door.

Outside, in the blue last light, the ward-stones began their evening song—and halfway through the round, the note stuttered.

Twice this month had become three times. And this time, Theo was almost certain it had happened the moment they touched the journal.
