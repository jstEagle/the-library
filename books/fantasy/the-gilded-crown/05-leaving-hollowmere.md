## 5. Leaving Hollowmere

The gate had never been opened in Theo's lifetime.

That fact sat in their chest like a swallowed stone as they stood before the great ward-door at dawn, pack on their shoulders, the bloodstained map folded against their heart. Nine generations of Underhills had walked this threshold and turned back. Nine generations had fed the beacon-fire, renewed the sigils, kept the silence—and never once, not once, stepped through.

Theo pressed their palm flat against the cold iron of the door's central plate. The metal was frost-slick even in autumn, and beneath it they felt the wards humming, that deep subterranean note they'd known all their life the way other people knew their own heartbeat. It was a good hum this morning. A steady hum. The kind that meant the valley slept safely behind them.

*Behind them.* The words felt like a foreign language.

"You don't have to do it today," they said aloud, to themselves, because there was no one else to say it to anymore. Sera Vane was buried two days now beneath the rowan at the crossroads, wrapped in Theo's own spare blanket, her courier's badge tucked into her folded hands. Her last words lived in Theo's memory with the persistence of a fever: *unbind him before solstice.*

Solstice was forty-one days away.

Theo closed their eyes. Their grandmother's journal lay heavy in the pack—the leather cover cracked with age, the pages full of a handwriting Theo had known and loved since childhood, now transfigured into something terrible by what it said. *The valley is not a sanctuary. The valley is a lock. And we are the ones who turn the key away from the door.*

They thought about staying. They genuinely tried.

They imagined the rest of their life in miniature: morning rounds, beacon-fire, ward-checks, the lie told again and again to neighbors—*the roads are unsafe, the king's men closed the passes, no one may leave for their own protection*—until the lie wore grooves so deep in Theo's tongue it would be indistinguishable from truth. They imagined growing old as their mother had grown old, and their grandmother before her: competent, respected, and alone inside a duty that was actually a cell.

Then they imagined Sera bleeding out against the outer stones, magic-made wounds knitting shut only because the valley refused to let the magic that made them follow her in. They imagined a king somewhere east, wearing a crown that was eating him alive, while forty-one days drained away like sand through a cracked hourglass.

"No," Theo said to the door. "I suppose I don't."

The unlocking was not complicated. That was the horror of it. All those years of mystery, all those childhood questions—*what happens if you open it, what's on the other side*—and the answer was: you press your hand to the plate, you speak the keeper's word your grandmother taught you when you were seven years old because every Underhill learned it young in case the worst happened, and the door simply... opens.

The wards did not stop it. The wards were not built to keep anyone in.

They were built to keep something else from getting out—or in, Theo still wasn't sure which, and the distinction made their skin crawl—and a keeper leaving was, apparently, an event the old craft accounted for. The iron plates rotated inward with a grinding whisper, releasing air that smelled of rain and pine and something else, something wilder and greener than anything that grew in Hollowmere.

The wards screamed.

There was no better word for it. Not sound exactly—it passed through Theo's bones rather than their ears—but a rising, keeling wail of displaced power, like a harp string snapped under tension, like a kettle of grief boiling over. Every ward-stone along the valley rim lit at once, blue-white fire racing from peak to peak in a chain of light Theo had only ever seen described in the archives' oldest diagrams, diagrams labeled *in case of breach.*

Not a breach, Theo wanted to tell the stones. A departure. Just one person. Just me.

But the wards didn't distinguish. The wards had been woven to announce exactly one thing to exactly one audience: *the door is open.* And whatever that audience was—whatever waited outside the valley, patient as geology—it heard.

Theo stepped through.

* * *

The world hit them like a wave.

They'd prepared for it, or thought they had. They'd read Maren Osk's stolen maps until the ink swam; they'd memorized the road east to Bellwether, the river towns, the distances. But maps, Theo discovered in the first ten steps beyond the gate, are lies drawn by people who have already stopped being astonished.

The sky was the first shock. In Hollowmere, the valley walls cropped the heavens into a familiar oval, a bowl of blue Theo could hold in a single glance. Here the sky simply went on, vaulting away in every direction, enormous and indifferent, and Theo's balance tilted with vertigo so sudden they staggered and put a hand against a birch trunk just to convince their body which way was down. Trees without number marched toward horizons in every direction. No rim of stone. No beacon-fire visible behind them. Nothing to say *this far and no farther.*

It should have been freedom. It felt like falling.

And underneath the vertigo, threaded through it like silver wire through grey cloth: the magic. Theo felt it immediately, the way a deaf person feels thunder through the floorboards. Outside the valley, power saturated everything—in the soil, in the running water, in the slow green breathing of the forest itself. It pressed against Theo's skin like humidity before a storm. For twenty-six years they had lived in a place where magic could not cross the border, where the world was held to a low, flat hum. Now the whole symphony crashed over them at once, gorgeous and overwhelming and utterly unguarded.

*This is what we locked out,* Theo thought, and then corrected themselves, remembering the journal. *No. This is what we locked in with us—kept at bay. Kept starving.*

Beautiful and wrong. Both at once, inseparable. A hillside ablaze with late-blooming firethorn, each blossom perfect, each one faintly, subtly *too much*, colors a half-shade richer than any dye in Hollowmere's market, as if the land here had been painted by someone showing off. A stream running so clear it looked like liquid glass, and if you watched too long, the reflections moved a heartbeat out of step with reality. Beauty deployed the way a hunter deploys a lure.

Theo walked anyway. There was no other direction that mattered but east.

The road found them within the hour—a proper road, rutted and cart-worn, running along a ridge above a river valley. Theo had seen roads only in books and Maren's charts, and the reality was humbler and stranger: wheel-grooves filled with rainwater, hoof-churned mud drying in ridges, a leaning milestone carved with distances to places that existed only in Theo's imagination. *Bellwether — 34.* Thirty-four miles. Four days' walk, maybe three if the weather held and Theo's nerve did.

They walked. The sun climbed. The vertigo receded by degrees, replaced by something more dangerous—an exhilaration so fierce it was almost painful, a feeling like the moment on the high pasture when the mist burns off and you can suddenly see the whole shape of your own life. Theo was outside. Theo was *walking to a capital city to save a king*. Somewhere in the middle of the terror was a child who had spent twenty-six years pressing their face against the inside of a window, and that child was screaming with joy.

Theo let it scream. They figured they'd earned one hour of joy. Maybe two.

The wards gave them ninety minutes.

It started as a prickle between the shoulder blades—that old gatekeeper instinct, the one that knew when a ward-stone needed re-dressing, when the beacon needed feeding, when something was wrong in the pattern. Theo stopped mid-stride and listened with the only sense that mattered out here, the trained one, the one nine generations had honed for exactly this purpose even though none of them had ever expected to use it beyond the valley rim.

Far behind them, faint as a struck bell heard across water: the wards. Still singing. Still announcing the open door.

And answering them, from somewhere north and west—from the direction of the high pass, the one that wound down toward Hollowmere from the outside world—came a second note. Lower. Hungrier. Like a wolf lifting its head.

"Oh," Theo said quietly. "You noticed."

They didn't know what *you* was. The journal hadn't said. Grandmother Ashgrove's careful script had danced around it for three hundred pages, naming it only ever as *the Kin*, capitalized, underlined twice, as though even ink were reluctant to hold the word. Whatever the crown of Aldenmere held bound, its kin were real, and they were out here, in this beautiful wrong world, and at least one of them had been waiting a very long time for a door to open.

Theo ran.

Not blindly—they were a gatekeeper, and gatekeepers did not run blindly—but with the ground-eating lope their father had drilled into them on the high pastures, pack cinched tight, map-hand memorizing landmarks at a glance. The road bent southeast around a shoulder of granite; Theo took it, then left it, cutting downslope through bracken when the instinct said the road was too exposed. Behind them, the second note grew no closer and no fainter, which was somehow worse than either. It paced them. It *accompanied* them.

By midafternoon Theo had put perhaps twelve miles between themselves and the gate, and the light was turning amber, and exhaustion was arriving with interest on top of the night they'd barely slept. They allowed themselves a halt in a hollow of old oaks, back to a trunk, eyes on the slope they'd descended. They ate hard bread and dried apples and made themselves chew slowly, methodically, the way you eat when panic wants you to choke.

That was when they saw the hoofprints.

They were in the soft earth at the bottom of the hollow, where rainwater had gathered and drained, leaving mud smooth as poured candle wax. Prints—shod hooves, large horses, several animals—circled the hollow. Not passing through. *Circling.* The track looped twice around the oak stand at a walking pace, close enough that any rider must have looked directly at the trunk Theo currently leaned against, and then climbed out of the hollow on the eastern side and continued east.

East. Toward Bellwether. Along the exact route Maren's map marked as the fastest road to the river town.

Theo crouched over the prints for a long time, the exhilaration of the morning dead and buried somewhere back up the slope. They measured one print against their spread hand. Bigger than any farm horse in the valley. The stride-length of the walk was long, disciplined, cavalry-trained. And pressed faintly into the margin of the deepest print, where a rider's stirrup-leather had swung and dragged: the ghost of an official seal, wax-red residue on a saddle strap's buckle. Crown issue.

Someone had come through the hollow after the gate opened. Someone riding crown horses, at a walk, in a circle, looking. Looking for a gatekeeper who had broken nine generations of silence, carrying a dead courier's map, with forty-one days left on the clock.

They weren't chasing Theo yet. Chasing was noisy, and noise let prey run. This was herding—patient, wide-circled herding, the kind that leaves the prey exactly one road and lets it choose the trap on its own.

Theo rose slowly, knees cracking, and looked east down the darkening corridor of the road. Twelve miles from home, one day into the rest of their life, and the wider world had already sent its greeting.

"Fine," Theo said to the hoofprints, to the listening woods, to whatever answered the wards from the high pass. "You know I'm coming. Good. Come find me."

Then, quieter, with Sera's voice in their ears:

"But I'm not the one who should be afraid of solstice."

They shouldered the pack and walked east, into the dusk, taking care to leave no prints of their own.

* * *

Night caught them in a shepherd's bothy half-fallen into the hillside, three miles short of the milestone that promised Bellwether. Theo risked no fire. They ate cold, drank from their skins, and sat in the doorway watching the stars come out—more stars than the valley sky had ever shown them, a spillage of light so dense it looked like the night had ruptured.

Even the stars were more beautiful out here. Even the stars might be lying.

Sleep came in shallow pulls. Once, near midnight, Theo woke certain they'd heard a horse on the road below—not approaching, not departing, just *there*, a slow walking rhythm passing in the dark, unhurried, as if its rider had all the time in the world and knew precisely where everyone in it was. Theo lay rigid in the blackness until the sound faded, hand curled around the little knife that was the only weapon they owned, and told themselves the truth they'd carry east like a second heartbeat:

Whatever opened the door behind them, it hadn't needed to chase.

It had simply started walking.
