## 6. The Cartographer's Debt

Bellwether smelled of fish and rot and river-mud, the way it always did in late autumn when the water ran low and the tanneries had their way with the air. Maren Osk sat in her usual corner of the Drowned Gull with a cup of wine she was nursing rather than drinking, because wine cost coin and coin had become a rumor in her life, something other people spoke of.

She was mending nets.

That was what her life had come to. Twenty-two years as Royal Cartographer to the Court of Aldenmere—twenty-two years of surveying passes no soldier had crossed, of charting coastlines from a pitching deck while sailors laughed at her, of drawing the king's roads so fine that ambassadors wept over them—and now she sat in a tavern that smelled like a gutted eel, reweaving nets for the fishermen who tolerated her because her hands were quick and she asked no questions.

Her right hand still remembered the pen. That was the cruelest part. Some mornings she woke with her fingers curled around an invisible stylus, tracing coastlines on the ceiling, and she would lie there in her rented room above the chandler's shop and feel the old hunger rise in her like tide. She could draw Bellwether's harbor from memory to the width of a hair. She could draw the whole river road from here to the capital, every ford, every ferry-fee, every mile-stone. What use was that to anyone? The crown had made very sure her maps were worth nothing at all.

*Disgraced*, they called her. *The forger Osk.* As if she had forged anything but her own signature onto work that was already hers—as if the crime were not that Chancellor Vess had taken her master atlas of the Northern Reaches, scraped her name from the vellum, and set his nephew's beneath it, and that when she'd protested, loudly, in front of the court, the punishment had fallen on her instead.

She flexed her fingers and went back to the net.

That was when the stranger came in out of the rain.

Maren noticed strangers the way she noticed everything—with a cartographer's eye, automatic, cataloguing. This one was young, maybe twenty-five, wrapped in a hooded cloak gone dark with wet, and they moved wrong for a traveler. Not limping, exactly. *Careful.* Like someone who had spent their whole life walking on ground they knew and had just stepped onto ground they didn't. They stood a moment in the doorway, letting the lamplight find them: sharp-boned face, river-colored eyes, mud to the knee, and under the cloak the unmistakable bulge of a satchel worn crosswise and kept close.

They scanned the room the way you scan a room when you're looking for one person and afraid of finding a different one.

Then they saw Maren—or saw something near her—and made for the corner by the hearth where an old man sold candles from a basket, and Maren understood, with the small cold click of a lock turning, that they hadn't been scanning for a person at all. They'd been scanning for a *map*. And the only thing in the Drowned Gull that resembled a map was the faded chart of the Inner Sea nailed above the bar, which Maren herself had drawn eleven years ago, before the fall, before everything, its corners curling now and someone's boot-print stained across the Bay of Sighs.

They were staring at her coastline. Her handiwork. Looking at it the way you look at a letter from home.

Maren put down the net.

* * *

She gave them time. That was craft; you didn't rush a mark or a quarry. She finished her wine, watched the stranger buy a bowl of barley stew with coins counted out one by one from a purse that had clearly been full recently and was not now, watched them eat hunched over the bowl like someone guarding it, watched them glance twice more at the chart on the wall and once, quickly, at the door.

Rain hammered the shutters. The hearth spat. Somewhere down the street a drunk was singing about the solstice, off-key and weeks early, and the song wound through the tavern and died against the low beams.

When the stranger finally rose, left a copper beside the bowl, and pushed out into the dark, Maren waited the length of ten heartbeats, then followed.

The alley behind the chandler's shop was the obvious route to the inn—the only inn in Bellwether worth the name—and Maren took the shortcut through the cooper's yard, coming out ahead of them, which was the whole point of knowing a town the way she knew this one. She stepped into the alley mouth with her hood up and a knife loose in her sleeve, and said, in the flat voice she'd learned from twenty years of dealing with courtiers:

"You're carrying something that belongs to me."

The stranger stopped. Rain ran off their hood. Under it, those river-colored eyes went wide—not with fear, Maren noted, but with a kind of startled recognition, as if she'd said something in a language they half remembered.

"I don't—" they began.

"The satchel." She let the knife show, just the blade's edge catching the lamplight from the street. "Crosswise strap, waxed canvas, buckled left over right. You've been sleeping with it. You don't know what's in it, or you'd have sold it by now, because whatever it is, it's got hunters on your heels—I counted two of Vess's gray-cloaks asking after a lone traveler at the ferry landing yesterday, and they don't chase beggars." She took one step closer. Water gurgled in the gutter between them. "So here's how this goes. You give it to me, and I tell you which road doesn't have gray-cloaks on it. Or you keep it, and I take it, and you learn what the inside of the river feels like this time of year. Your choice."

It was a good speech. It had worked twice before, on smugglers and once on a tax-collector who'd deserved it. But the stranger didn't reach for the satchel and didn't run. Instead they did the one thing Maren had not planned for.

They looked at her hands.

"You're left-handed," they said slowly. "But you hold the knife like a right-handed person pretending. Which means you've never—" They stopped. Their brow furrowed, and something shifted in their face, some door opening inward. "Wait. *Osk.* You said it belongs to you. Are you—Maren Osk? The cartographer?"

The name hit her like cold water thrown in the face. It had been six years since anyone in this kingdom had spoken it without the word *forger* attached, and here was a mud-soaked stranger in a rainy alley saying it like a title.

"Who told you that name?" she said, and the knife came up without her deciding it should.

"No one. I mean—" The stranger's hand went to the satchel, hesitated, and then, absurdly, gently, began working the buckle. "Sera. The woman who gave me this. She said—if anything happened to her, find the mapmaker's mark on the northern sheet. She said the mark would be a compass rose with eight points instead of sixteen, drawn backwards, because—" they swallowed— "because the cartographer who drew it hid her signature where only another mapmaker would look."

The rain filled the silence.

Maren stood very still. Eight points, reversed. She had drawn that rose exactly four times in her life, on the four sheets of the atlas she'd been proudest of, the survey of the high passes and the old ward-lines that the Chancellery had ordered sealed and then, three years later, had announced to the court as the work of Vess's nephew—a boy who couldn't find north with both hands and a lodestone.

"They killed her," the stranger said quietly. "The people chasing this. Two days ago, at the edge of Hollowmere valley. She was dying when I found her. She made me promise." Their jaw tightened. "I'm Theo. Theo Underhill. And I think you'd better look at this map, because I've looked at it until my eyes ache, and I can read the roads and the rivers fine, but there are marks on it I don't understand, and Sera said the woman who drew them would."

*Would.* Not *might*. Sera Vane—whoever she had been—had sent this child into the world with Maren's name in their mouth like a key.

Maren should have taken the satchel then. That had been the plan; that had been the entire architecture of her last six years, small thefts and smaller mercies, taking back from a crown that had taken everything. Her hand actually moved toward it. And then Theo turned the satchel around and unfolded the top sheet right there in the rain, angling it under the distant lamp, and Maren saw her own linework—her own *linework*, the double-weighted rivers, the little hooked ticks she used for fords, the pass elevations written in her cramped sideways numerals—and below the roads, in ink that had faded to the color of dried blood, the marks.

Ward-sigils. Old ones. Older than any she'd ever been asked to chart.

She forgot the knife. She forgot the rain. She leaned in, and her breath fogged the vellum, and her mind—the mind they'd said was wasted, the mind of a forger—went through the sigils like water through an open sluice. These weren't decorative. They were structural. Each one anchored to a place on the map, and each place was a gate, and the gates were strung together in a line running east from the mountains toward the capital like beads on a wire, and at the end of the wire, drawn in gold leaf that someone had applied *after* her inks had dried, sat the palace itself, circled, crowned with a symbol she had seen exactly once before—

On the sealed sheets. The ones the Chancellery burned in front of her while charging her with forgery.

"The coronation circlet," she heard herself say. Her voice sounded far away. "This is the binding-line. This is the *crown's* binding-line." She looked up, and the stranger's face was pale and steady in the lamplight. "Who told you the king is dying?"

"Sera did. With her last breath." Theo's voice cracked, just slightly, on the name. "She said the crown is eating him. She said it has to be unbound before the solstice, or—" They stopped. "Or I don't know what. She didn't get that far."

Maren straightened. The rain ran cold down the back of her neck and she barely felt it.

Here was the arithmetic she'd lived by for six years: trust nothing, expect the knife, take what you can carry. Here was the other arithmetic, older, buried deeper, the one she'd sworn off in a courtroom while a chancellor smiled: *if the map is true, then the king is true, and if the king is true, then the seal order was true, and if the seal order was true, then they ruined me to bury this.*

A gambler's thought. A fool's thought. The exact thought that had gotten her disgraced the first time—*they must have a reason, the crown wouldn't lie about something this large*—except now she knew the size of the lie, and it was the size of a kingdom.

And underneath the cynicism, in the flooded cellar of her where she kept the things she didn't look at, something stirred. Hope is a debt, her father used to say. It comes due with interest. She had spent six years refusing all credit.

"You said gray-cloaks," Theo said. "Two of them. At the ferry landing."

"At least two. Probably more on the south road." Maren folded the map with a mapmaker's care, along her own original creases, and held it a moment longer than she needed to. When she handed it back, her decision had already been made somewhere below thought, the way a river decides. "The ferry's watched. There's a barge that runs night cargo to Thorn's Landing—smugglers, but honest ones, if you follow me. I know the man who poles it. He owes me."

"Why would he owe you?"

"Because I redrew his smuggling routes for free once, and never told him I also copied them." She pulled her hood lower and started up the alley, then paused. "Come on, Underhill. And walk like you belong to me. In Bellwether tonight, that's the safest thing you can be."

Theo fell into step beside her, close, trusting, ridiculous. After half a block they said, quietly, "You believe me. About the king."

Maren watched the rain stitch silver threads down the middle of the empty street, and thought of Sera Vane dying with a stolen map and a dead escort and enough faith in a disgraced stranger's hidden signature to send a child to her door. Thought of the gold leaf on the palace. Thought of the burned sheets, and the smiling chancellor, and six years of nets.

"I believe the map," she said at last. "The map doesn't lie. People do. It's why I love it." She glanced sideways at the young keeper of Hollowmere, soaked and stubborn and carrying a dead woman's promise like a lit coal. "And if the map's telling the truth about the capital, then in nine days' time we're going to wish very hard that it wasn't. Because there's a second set of marks on that northern sheet, Underhill. Sigils I couldn't read when I drew them, and can now. They aren't wards keeping something out."

Ahead, at the mouth of the harbor lane, a lantern swung—gray cloak, moving unhurried through the rain toward them, and Maren's hand found Theo's sleeve and steered them both into shadow.

"They're keeping something *in*," she breathed. "And it's almost solstice."
