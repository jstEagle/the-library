## 7. Hedge-Witch on the Road

The hedge-witch found us before we found him, which Maren said afterward was the first thing she should have been suspicious of, and the second thing was his boots.

We had made Bellwether by the third morning after the ford, and we left it on the fourth, because Maren woke me at the grey hour with a hand over my mouth and a whisper like a knife: *crown riders in the market square, asking after a girl with a map.* So we went out through the tannery gate while the river fog still lay on the town like a wet wool blanket, and we took the old drover's road south, and for two days we did not speak above a breath.

It was on that road—narrow, rutted, hemmed in by alder and the memory of rain—that Brannoc stepped out of the trees.

I want to say I was brave about it. I want to say I put myself between him and Sera's map the way I'd put myself between the hunters and the ford. But the truth is that I screamed, short and undignified, and dropped my pack in a puddle, and Maren had her knife out so fast the air whistled, and the man simply stood there with his hands raised and let us point steel at him until he got tired of waiting.

"If you're going to rob me," he said, "could you do it somewhere drier? My knees are seventy years old, or near enough, and this ground is a swamp."

He was not what I expected a witch to look like. In Hollowmere, the stories of witches were all crooked backs and crow feathers, teeth like fence posts. Brannoc was round and soft as a loaf, with a white beard trimmed carefully short and eyes the brown of strong tea. He wore a good coat gone shabby at the cuffs, and mud-spattered boots that had once been very fine indeed—and that, Maren told me later, was the tell. Fine boots on a man walking the drover's road meant a man who had fallen out of someplace comfortable, fast.

"Brannoc Hale," he said, when Maren demanded a name. "Hedge-witch, formerly of the palace at Aldenmere. Formerly of several places." His gaze slid to my pack, to the shape of the map-roll wrapped in oilcloth against my spine, and something moved behind his eyes like a fish under ice. "You're carrying her work. Osk's work. And you're carrying—" he stopped himself, jaw working, "—you're carrying *her* story, aren't you? The courier. Vane."

The rain chose that moment to arrive, fat drops slapping the alder leaves, and perhaps that is why I didn't ask how he knew. Perhaps it was only that he looked at me the way no one had looked at me since Sera died: as if I were carrying something heavy and he could see the shape of it through the canvas.

"You served the coronation," I said. It wasn't a question. Sera had named him, in the fevered hours before the end, in fragments—*the witch who lit the crown, the witch who lit the crown, find Brannoc.*

"I did." He said it the way a man confesses to a crime, because it was one. "Eleven years ago. I laid the blessing-wards on Cassian's brow, and I bound the circlet to his bloodline, and I did it beautifully, if I may flatter myself, because I was the finest hedge-craft in three counties and I never once asked what I was binding *to*." His voice cracked on the last word, and he covered it with a cough. "May I walk with you? Not for protection—I'm useless in a fight, I faint at the sight of my own blood. For penance. It's slower, but it lasts longer."

Maren looked at me. I looked at the road ahead, south, toward a capital full of gilded lies and a king being eaten alive, and I thought about Sera pressing a bloodstained map into my hands and choosing me, of all people, deliberately, and I heard myself say:

"Keep up or fall behind. We don't wait."

He kept up. For an old man with bad knees, he kept up remarkably well.

* * *

We walked, and the country unrolled around us, and I learned that the world outside Hollowmere was larger than any map could hold—not because the distances were great, though they were, but because everything in it was *busy*. In the valley, a field was a field. Here, a field was forty arguments between crows, a scarecrow wearing someone's dead father's coat, a stone wall built by hands four hundred years gone and still standing out of sheer stubbornness. Every mile had a name and every name had a story and Maren knew half of them and lied charmingly about the other half.

Brannoc talked too, when the walking was easy. He talked about herbs and weather and the correct way to brew willowbark, and he did not talk about the coronation, and I understood that the silence was itself a kind of wound he was keeping clean.

It was on the fifth night, camped under a ruined tithe-barn with the fire small and the rain drumming the broken roof, that I began to tell Sera's story aloud.

I don't know why I started. That isn't true—I know exactly why. We were passing a wineskin Maren had bought in Bellwether, and Brannoc asked, gently, the way old men ask things they already suspect, "What was she like? The courier?" And I opened my mouth to say *I barely knew her, she died in my arms within a day,* and what came out instead was:

"She cheated at cards."

Maren snorted. Brannoc blinked.

"She cheated at cards," I said again, and found that it was true, that I had watched her do it through the haze of her fever, her fingers quick and light over a deck she'd demanded from my pack because she said dying people deserved entertainment. "Badly. She thought she was subtle. She'd palm a card and look at you like she dared you to notice, and when you noticed, she'd grin like noticing was the game." I turned the wineskin in my hands. "She was delirious most of the time I knew her. But even delirious, she was—*direct*. She'd grab your wrist hard enough to bruise and make you promise things. Deliver the map. Find the cartographer. Unbind him before solstice." My voice caught on the last one, the way it always did. "She never wasted a minute. Even her last minutes were spent *aimed*."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, an owl asked its question and got no answer.

"What color was her hair?" Brannoc asked quietly.

"Black," I said. "Black with grey coming in at the temples, and she hated it. She was maybe forty. She had a scar through her left eyebrow and she told me a different story about it every time she remembered to lie—one time bandits, one time a falling chandelier, one time a jealous duke." I laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, wet at the edges. "I think it was her brother. I think everything was her brother, in the end. I think she traded the whole kingdom for him and would have done it twice."

I hadn't known I knew that. It surfaced as I spoke, the way truth does when you stop gripping it—Sera, lucid for one bright hour on the second night, saying *if anyone asks, I did it for Aldenmere*, with a bitterness in her mouth like coin-tarnish, and me too young and too frightened to ask what she meant.

Maren reached over without looking and squeezed my shoulder once, hard, the way she did everything. Brannoc fed a stick to the fire and watched it catch.

"Tell the rest tomorrow," he said. "And the day after. A person stays alive as long as someone's telling their story. That's older magic than anything I ever worked, and it holds better."

So I told it. Night after night, walking days between, I told Sera Vane in pieces: the card-cheating, the wrist-grabbing, the way she pronounced *Underhill* like a password she'd carried across a kingdom. How she'd come to my gate bleeding from wounds no knife could make, because magic cannot cross into Hollowmere but it can certainly chase you to the edge of it. How she pressed the map into my hands with blood still wet on the vellum and said *unbind him* and then, softer, with the last of her breath, *don't let them make you carry it alone.*

I did not tell them that last part. Some stories are load-bearing, and I wasn't ready to take the weight off that wall.

But the telling changed something in the three of us. Maren started adding details she couldn't possibly have known—*she sounds like she'd have hated my maps, all that patience required*—building Sera taller with each telling, until she was less a dead woman than a fourth traveler walking just behind us, laughing at our mistakes. And Brannoc listened like a man drinking water after a long thirst, and one night he added his own piece, unprompted, staring into the coals:

"I saw her once. At the palace. She was arguing with the captain of the courier corps about a route through the Thornwood—said the safe road was a trap because everyone knew it was safe." He huffed. "She was right, as it happened. Three couriers lost on the Thornwood road that winter. She took the long way and delivered early." He looked up at me. "That was the kind of mind the crown sent to guard its secrets. And then it sent knives after her. I keep trying to make those two facts sit together in the same skull, and they won't."

"That's because one of them belongs to Vhalor," Maren said flatly, "and one belongs to Cassian, and the crown has been both for eleven years."

Brannoc flinched at the name. I noticed that he always flinched at the name.

* * *

On the eighth day out of Bellwether, the road climbed onto a long bare ridge where the wind came straight off the northern hills with nothing to slow it, and Brannoc stopped walking so suddenly that I nearly went into his back.

"What is it?" Maren's hand went to her knife on reflex; since the ford, it lived there.

"The birds," Brannoc said.

I looked. Crows, a dozen of them, sitting along the ridge-top fence line in a row, facing us. Not feeding. Not fighting. Just *facing*, with the terrible patience of things that are paid to wait.

"They've been doing that since yesterday," Brannoc said. His voice had gone thin. "I told myself it was carrion. There's no carrion, Theo. Look at the fence posts. Count them."

I counted. Twelve crows, twelve posts, evenly spaced, like an honor guard. Like a picket line.

"Crows follow travelers," Maren said, but her voice didn't believe her.

"Not like this." Brannoc turned to face us fully, and I saw for the first time how old he actually was, how the guilt had been holding him upright like a prop under a sagging beam. "I need to tell you something, and I need you to hear all of it before you decide whether to leave me on this ridge. Which you'd be right to do."

The wind pushed at our backs, impatient, as if it wanted us moving south before whatever was north arrived.

"When I bound the crown, I used a rite of attunement. It's standard—it lets the binder feel the binding, monitor its health, know if it frays." He swallowed. "What I didn't understand—what nobody understood—is that attunement runs both ways. When the binding began to fail, when the thing inside began to wake, it felt *me*. It has felt me for years. A thread, thin as spider silk, running from that circlet to this—" he held up his right hand, and in the firelight of the following dusk I would see it, though now in daylight there was nothing: a faint dark line circling his ring finger, like a ring worn so long it grew into the flesh. "It doesn't command me. I want to be clear about that. I checked. I checked every way a witch can check. But it *whispers*. And lately, the whispers have had a direction. South. Toward you."

Maren's knife was out. I didn't blame her. I stood very still, the map-roll a hot weight against my spine.

"You're saying it knows where we are," I said.

"I'm saying worse." Brannoc met my eyes, and his tea-brown eyes were steady, and that steadiness was the only reason I didn't run. "A leash works both ways. If it can feel me, it can feel what I feel. And last night, around the fire, listening to you tell the courier's story—" his voice broke and reassembled itself, "—I felt *happy*. First time in eleven years. And this morning the crows came, and they are not crows, and they have been reporting our position since dawn." He spread his empty hands. "The crown knows we're coming, Theo. It has known since Bellwether, maybe since the ford. Everything from here to the capital is its ground, and we are walking down its throat with our names written on our packs."

Silence, except the wind and the crows, which had begun, softly, horribly, to laugh among themselves.

"So turn me out," Brannoc said. "Cut the thread if you can find it—I can't, I've tried, it's grown into the bone. Or use me. Because I know the palace wards from the inside, I know which gates the crown's eye watches and which it thinks beneath notice, and I know the old chapel-ward under the east cloister where the original binding texts are kept, because *I shelved them there myself*." He drew a breath. "I am a liability and a key, and I am asking you, with everything left in me, to treat me as the second thing."

I looked at Maren. Maren looked at the crows. And I thought of Sera—Sera, who had bargained with the enemy's agents and carried a double mission in her blood and still, at the very end, aimed her last breath at saving a king she'd never met. Sera, whom I had been making larger every night precisely so that I could live up to her.

"We keep you," I said. "But we change the plan. No more roads. No more towns. We go the way the crows can't follow—the fens, the old drover tunnels, whatever Maren's stolen maps show. And Brannoc—" I stepped closer, and the wind shoved at both of us, and far down the southern sky, past the ridge's edge, I saw what I had been pretending not to see for an hour: a smudge of dust on the road behind us, moving fast. Too fast for carts. "Whatever it whispers to you, you tell us. Every word. Starting now."

Brannoc nodded. Then his face went strange, slack, listening, and his lips moved around words that weren't his, and when he came back to himself he was grey as the ridge-stone.

"It says," he whispered, "that it liked the story about the cards. And that it remembers her too. And that we should hurry, because—" he swallowed— "*because the king is almost finished, and solstice is in nine days.*"

Behind us, the dust cloud crested the hill. Ahead, the road ran south, straight and open as an offered hand.

We ran.
