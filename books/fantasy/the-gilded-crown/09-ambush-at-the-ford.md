## 9. Ambush at the Ford

The river called the Liss had been our companion for three days, and like most companions it grew tiresome by the third. It chattered constantly—over stones, under roots, around the drowned trunks of alders—and it smelled of iron and cold and the particular green rot of autumn going down into winter. Maren said the Liss ran all the way to the capital, that barges rode it heavy with grain and wine and the taxes of a dozen shires. I only knew that its sound made it hard to hear anything else, which is a dangerous thing when you have spent your whole life listening.

At Hollowmere, silence was a craft. My grandmother taught me to stand in the beacon-house with my eyes shut and count every sound the valley made: the tick of the ward-stones cooling at dusk, the wingbeat of a heron over the mere, the small dry whisper of the wards themselves, like pages turning in a book no one was reading. A gatekeeper who cannot hear her gate is no gatekeeper at all. So when the river's voice changed on the morning of the fourth day—not louder, not softer, but *thicker*, as though something upstream were drinking from it—I stopped on the bank and listened until Brannoc asked me what I was staring at.

"Water," I said. "It sounds wrong."

Maren looked up from the map she was pretending not to study in the open. "Rivers sound like rivers."

"Not to me." I crouched and put my palm flat against the current where it curled over a stone. Cold climbed my arm like a rope being hauled hand over hand. Beneath the water-voice there was another rhythm, faint but steady, the way you can feel a second heartbeat if you press your ear to someone's chest. Hooves. Many hooves, striking stone somewhere above us, muffled by miles and water and my own wish not to hear them.

I did not tell them how many. I told myself I wasn't sure.

* * *

The ford lay half a mile downstream, where the Liss spread itself thin across a shelf of pale rock and forgot, for thirty yards, that it was supposed to be deep. On the far side the road climbed through birch scrub toward the high moor, and beyond the moor, if Maren's stolen-and-reclaimed maps were true, the king's highway ran straight as a spear to Aldenmere City. We meant to cross by midday and be three days gone before anyone knew we'd passed.

We should have crossed at dawn. We didn't, because Brannoc insisted on brewing his bitter hedge-tea first—"for the nerves," he said, though whose nerves he never specified—and because Maren wanted to re-copy a section of the map onto oilcloth in case the original was taken, and because I let them. I was the one who said yes, wait. Remember that. In the nights afterward, when sleep came grudgingly, I remembered it often: that I was the keeper of the gate, and I opened it myself.

The riders came out of the birches while Maren was still blotting the ink.

There were six of them, and they did not come like bandits or like soldiers. They came like weather—a gray pressure moving through the trees without hurry, without noise, their horses' hooves wrapped in felt so that even now, even remembering, I cannot give you the sound of their arrival. Only the sight: six shapes detaching from white trunks and shadow, and at their center a man in a long coat the color of wet slate, wearing a badge I did not recognize and understood immediately anyway. A crown stamped in gold foil, cheap and bright, like a seal on a letter ordering an execution.

"Crown hunters," Maren breathed. Her hands went still over the map. "Theo—that's Warden-Captain Hesk. He hunted smugglers on the Liss for twenty years. They say he never lost cargo." She swallowed. "Or people."

"Hold the ford," I said. It came out steadier than I felt. "Get across. Take the map."

"Theo—"

"*Go.*"

They went. I heard Brannoc swearing, heard the splash and scramble of horses forced into the shallows, heard Maren cry out—and then I stopped hearing any of it, because the man in the slate coat raised one gloved hand, and the world narrowed to the width of a river.

* * *

Here is what nine generations of Underhills gave me, and it was not magic. The wards of Hollowmere answered to my blood, yes, but the keeping of them was older than any spell. It was watching. It was knowing ground. It was standing in one place for your whole life until the place became an extension of your body, until you could feel a loose stone under the moss the way you feel a splinter under skin. My grandmother used to walk me along the gate-road in the dark and ask, "What changed?" and I would have to name it—the branch fallen, the rabbit run abandoned, the stone turned by frost. *What changed.* That question kept me alive at the ford, and I want that written plainly, because everything else about that day I would gladly burn.

The ford changed. That was the first thing. The hunters did not rush it; they spread along the near bank in a loose crescent, two upstream, two downstream, two holding the center with the captain, and I saw that they had chosen their ground the way I would have chosen it—which meant they had stood here before, maybe days ago, maybe weeks, walking this shelf of rock and learning it. Learning *us*. The felt-wrapped hooves. The unhurried approach. You do not prepare a place like that for travelers you hope to frighten away.

The captain spoke first. His voice carried flat across the water, unbothered by the river's chatter, as if the Liss itself had agreed to be quiet for him.

"You carry property of the crown."

"I carry nothing," I said.

"Then step aside, and we will search your companions instead. No one needs to be hurt." He said it the way you read a tax notice. Somewhere behind him, one of the riders shifted, and I caught the glint of a crossbow being leveled, casual as a man resting a tool against a fence.

I looked past him, once, quickly. Maren was halfway across, her mare floundering on the slick rock, Brannoc beside her—and between them and the far bank, nothing. Open water. Open ground beyond. If I held the ford's mouth, they might make it.

"What is the property?" I called back. Stall. Learn them. *What changed.*

"A map," the captain said, and smiled for the first time, and the smile was worse than the crossbows. "You'll understand—we're instructed it must come back *undamaged*. Every line. Every sigil." He drew a long knife, not threateningly, just the way a man takes out his lunch. "So do think about that, gatekeeper. We know what you are. We know what walks behind you. And we know you won't let the woman bleed."

That was when I understood they had been herding us, though I did not yet know how completely. But I am ahead of myself. First there was the fight, and I will tell it plainly, because I have decided I owe the truth to everyone now, including myself.

* * *

The crossbow loosed first, and I learned the second thing nine generations gave me: gatekeepers are fast, because gates punish hesitation. I was already moving when the bolt came—sideways, off the shelf-rock and into the deeper channel where the water hit my thighs like a fist—and the bolt hissed past where my chest had been and buried itself in a stone with a crack like ice breaking. The cold took my breath. I let it. Cold is honest; it tells you exactly where you are.

The nearest rider came into the water after me, and I met him with the gate-pike.

I should explain the gate-pike. It is not a weapon, whatever the ballads Maren hums would have you believe. It is a tool, seven feet of blackened ash with an iron shoe, used to test ward-stones, clear debris from the gate-track, and lever open the great door when the hinges swell in winter. I had carried one since I was twelve years old. I had never struck anything living with it. But I knew its weight the way I knew my own hands, and when the rider's sword came down at me through the spray, I did not parry it—I stepped inside it, inside the arc where a mounted man is weakest, and drove the pike's iron shoe up under his shield rim into the hollow below his arm.

He made a sound I will not repeat. Not because it was terrible—it was—but because it was *small*. He sounded young. He fell sideways into the shallows and the river took him a little way downstream and turned him over, and I looked at his face for exactly as long as it took to understand that I would see it again whenever I closed my eyes, and then I looked away, because there were five more.

The second one nearly had me. He was better than the first—dismounted, low, using the water's noise—and his blade opened my sleeve and the skin beneath in a long burning line before I felt anything at all. What saved me was not skill. It was the ford. I knew the rock under the water the way I knew the floor of my own gatehouse: the shelf, the drop-off, the hidden pothole where the current drilled a socket in the stone a hundred years ago. I gave ground deliberately, giving it the way you feed a rope, and when he lunged I stepped left onto solid shelf and he stepped right into the pothole, and the Liss took his balance and I took the rest. He went under. I held him under with the pike across the back of his neck, and I felt the exact moment the fighting left him, and I let go too late, and I have decided not to describe it further except to say that the river ran redder for a moment and then, indifferently, did not.

Two dead. Four left, plus the captain, who had not moved.

"Enough," he said, and there was something new in his voice—not respect, nothing so warm. Recalculation. "You're full of surprises, Underhill. But look behind you."

I did not want to. I looked.

Maren was down.

She had almost made the far bank—ten yards from the birches, close enough to touch the shadow—and then one of the two riders I had thought were upstream came out of the reeds on the *far* side, where no rider should have been, where we had scouted and seen nothing. They had crossed in the night. They had known the ford before we ever reached it. The rider's horse shouldered Maren's mare and the mare screamed and slipped on the wet rock and went down, and Maren went with her, and I saw the moment her leg folded under her, wrong, the way wood folds before it breaks. The map-case flew from her grip and landed in the shallows, and the rider dismounted and picked it up with a gauntleted hand, and Brannoc stood over Maren with his hedge-knife shaking in his fist, an old man defending a wounded woman with a blade meant for cutting herbs.

And the rider with the map did not run. He stood there. Holding it. Waiting.

*They wanted the map intact,* the captain had said. *Every line. Every sigil.* And they had it, and they were not leaving with it, and the captain was not crossing to take it, and in the space of one heartbeat the whole shape of the morning turned over in my mind like a stone showing its underside.

They were not trying to take the map from us.

They were trying to make sure the map stayed *with* us.

* * *

I don't remember deciding. I remember the water, and the pike coming up, and my own voice shouting words I hadn't planned: "The map stays with us! Your orders say intact—so either I walk to my friends, or you explain to whoever holds your leash why the courier's work came home soaked in blood!" And I walked. Straight up the middle of the ford, slow, pike grounded, bleeding from the arm, daring them. The captain watched me the whole way with those flat gray eyes, and his riders watched him, waiting for the word, and the word never came. Because I was right, and he knew I was right, and there is no order a warden-captain fears more than the one he cannot fulfill.

At the far bank, the rider set the map-case down in the shallows—set it *down*, gently, like a child returning a borrowed toy—and backed his horse away, and the six of them withdrew into the birches the way they had come, unhurried, felt-silent, gone.

Brannoc pulled me up the bank. Maren was conscious, gray-lipped, her left leg bent at the knee at an angle knees decline. She was cursing with impressive fluency, which reassured me more than any prayer. Brannoc splinted her with alder branches and willow-bark and his own belt, muttering the whole time, and I stood in the dripping shallows and retrieved the map-case with hands that would not stop shaking, and understood, all the way down, what I had understood in the water:

They could have taken it. Six against four, on ground they had prepared, and they let us keep it. They killed nothing they didn't have to kill—they killed *me*, a little, which apparently doesn't count—and they let us keep the thing we are dying to deliver. Which means the map is not the prize. Which means the map is the *bait*, and we are the fish, and somewhere ahead of us on the king's highway there is a hook, and behind us the felt-wrapped hoofprints circled the ford twice before they vanished, the way a shepherd circles a flock he is moving toward a gate.

Not toward the capital.

Through it.

* * *

We traveled that evening as far as Maren's leg allowed, which was not far, and camped in a hollow under the moor's first rise with no fire because I could not bear the thought of light. Maren slept finally, willow and exhaustion winning. Brannoc sat across from me in the dark, and after a long time he said, quietly, "First one?"

"Yes."

"It doesn't get smaller," he said. "The carrying of it. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something." He paused. "But it gets shared, if you let it. That's the trick nobody teaches."

I didn't answer. I was thinking of Sera, dying against the ward-stones with blood on her lips, asking a stranger to save a king. I was thinking of how lightly she had seemed to carry it, and wondering for the first time what it had cost her, and whether she had ever been given the trick Brannoc spoke of, or whether she had died carrying it alone because no one had thought to offer.

And I was thinking of the captain's smile, and the map lying safe against my ribs, and the road ahead running straight as a spear through country we did not know, toward a city that knew we were coming—had always known, perhaps, had arranged our whole journey the way a cook arranges ingredients—and of the last thing the captain said as he turned his horse, so quietly I almost missed it under the river's voice:

"See you at the solstice, gatekeeper."

The winter solstice was nineteen days away.

We had eighteen hundred miles of baited road between us and the truth, and every mile of it, I now understood, had been mapped for us by someone else's hand.
