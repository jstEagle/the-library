## 10. Sera's Secret

Brannoc had buried three friends in his life and had never once had to bury a lie alongside them.

He thought about that as he sat in the lean-to behind the waystation at Grayfen Hollow, turning Sera Vane's satchel over in his hands. The leather was cracked with road-wear, darkened along one seam where her blood had soaked through and dried to a shine like lacquer. He had carried it since the ford—since Maren went down with an arrow-graze across her ribs and Theo held the crossing alone with nothing but a gatekeeper's staff and nine generations of stubbornness in their spine—and he had not opened it until now.

Not until tonight. Not until the others slept, and the fire had burned down to that red, thoughtful glow that made honest men confess things to themselves they'd been avoiding for weeks.

The truth was simple: he was afraid of what she'd left behind.

He was afraid of what she *was*.

* * *

He had known Sera Vane for eleven days before she died, which was not enough time to know anyone, and somehow more than enough. She had joined them at Bellwether's edge with frostbitten fingers and a story half-told, pressing a bloodstained map into Theo Underhill's hands with the last of her strength, whispering *unbind him before solstice* like a woman paying off a debt she could feel accruing interest even as she spoke.

They had burned her body on a hill above the river road, because Brannoc knew the rites and Maren didn't and Theo—Theo had stood there with ash on their cheeks and refused to leave until the fire was nothing but gray lace on the stones. Brannoc had sung the old hedge-witch's passing-song, the one his grandmother used for strangers, the one that asked the road to remember the traveler even when no one else did.

And all the while he sang it, some small cold thing in the back of his skull had been whispering: *you don't know who you're singing for.*

Hedge-witches learned early to read people. It was survival. A hedge-witch who couldn't tell a desperate mother from a bounty hunter didn't live long enough to grow a beard. And Sera Vane, dying or not, bleeding out against the ward-stones of Hollowmere—there had been something in her that Brannoc's craft had tasted and he had chosen, deliberately, willfully, to ignore.

Guilt. He'd read it as guilt. The guilt of a courier who'd failed her escort, who'd watched good people die for the leather tube strapped to her ribs.

It might have been that.

It might also have been the guilt of a woman who knew exactly what she was carrying, and exactly who wanted it, and exactly what she'd promised them.

* * *

The satchel's buckles were stiff with dried blood. He worked them loose with his thumbnail, wincing at the small wet sound the leather made, and laid out the contents on his bedroll by firelight.

A spare shirt, patched at both elbows. A tin cup. A comb of horn, one tooth missing. A knife—not a fighting knife, a working one, the handle worn smooth by years of use. A purse with eleven coppers and a silver bit, which told him she hadn't been paid recently, or hadn't been paid honestly. Couriers for the crown carried better than that, even disgraced ones.

And letters.

Three of them, folded small, tucked into a false seam along the satchel's spine—the kind of hiding place a smuggler uses, or a spy. His fingers hesitated over them. Outside, the wind moved through the bare alders with a sound like someone slowly turning pages. Somewhere up the slope, Theo was standing the second watch, a thin silhouette against the stars, keeping their vigil the way they kept everything: quietly, completely, without any apparent awareness of the cost.

Brannoc picked up the first letter.

The wax seal had been broken and re-melted carelessly, a thumbprint pressed into the cooling wax like a signature of haste. Inside, in a cramped, slanting hand:

*Sera—if you are reading this then I am dead or worse, and neither outcome excuses what I am about to ask of you. The map is incomplete. You know this. You have always known this. What you do not know—or what you have pretended not to know—is why. The key is not a thing that can be carried. It is a thing that must be kept where no searcher would think to look, and so I have kept it in the only vault I trust, which is myself. Swallow it, sister. Let it sit in your belly like a stone of oath. If the worst comes, let them cut it out of you and curse my name while they do it.*

*I am sorry. I was always going to be sorry. Love, Tam.*

Brannoc read it twice. Then a third time, slower, because the first two readings had been performed by a man hoping to be wrong.

*Tam.* He turned the name over. In eleven days of travel, Sera had spoken of almost nothing from her old life—a habit of couriers, he'd assumed, whose trade was forgetting where they'd been. But once, half-asleep against Maren's cartography roll, she had murmured a word that might have been *Tammy*, and Maren had looked at Brannoc, and Brannoc had shrugged, and they had all agreed silently to pretend the moment hadn't happened.

Her brother. Her brother had written to her. Her brother had *instructed* her. And her brother wrote like a man composing instructions for a mission, not comfort for a sibling—which meant either that grief had made him formal, or that formality had always been the shape of his love.

Or that the letter was never meant to be a letter at all.

* * *

The second letter was older. The paper was soft at the folds, handled often, and the handwriting was not Tam's. It was precise, elegant, each letter placed like a stone in a garden path—and Brannoc felt the hair rise on his forearms before he'd read three lines, because he knew that hand. He had seen it once before, four years ago, on the coronation documents he'd been permitted to witness as part of the rite's outer circle. He had seen it signed at the bottom of a page that bound a king.

It was written in the voice of the crown itself. Or rather, in the voice of those who served what wore the crown.

*Mistress Vane, your petition has been heard. Your brother's debts to the realm may be satisfied by service rather than suffering. Deliver the map intact to the capital by the winter solstice, place it in the hands designated, and Tammas Vane walks free under writ of pardon. Fail, delay, or divert, and his cell becomes his tomb, quietly and without record. We do not threaten. We merely describe.*

*You will be followed by our friends. Do not fear them; they are instructed to protect the cargo, not the carrier. Should you be wounded, they will recover the map and leave the rest to the crows. This is not cruelty. It is economy.*

*Serve well, and be remembered.*

There was no signature. There didn't need to be. Brannoc set the letter down on his knee and found that his hands were shaking, and that the shaking had nothing to do with the cold.

*A double agent.* No—not even that. A double agent chose their side. Sera Vane had been a rope in a tug-of-war, pulled between two masters, belonging wholly to neither. Deliver the map and her brother lived. But deliver it to *whom?* The letter said *the hands designated*, and Brannoc had spent four years trying not to think about the hands that ran the palace's shadow-work, the smooth-voiced clerks who smelled of ink and incense, the ones who had stood at the edges of the binding rite murmuring words that were not in any liturgy Brannoc knew.

She hadn't been carrying the map to save the king.

She'd been carrying it to feed him.

* * *

Except.

He picked up the third letter, and this one stopped his breath.

It was in Sera's own hand—quick, untidy, the writing of a woman who thought faster than her pen could follow. Undated. Unsealed. Unsent. It had the raw, unguarded quality of something written in the dark hours, meant for no one, or meant for whoever eventually dug through her belongings and cared enough to read.

*If you're reading this, I'm dead, and you're either a friend or a thief, and I've decided I don't care which.*

*Here is the truth, since I never managed to say it aloud to anyone: I took the bargain. Of course I took it. He's my brother. You'd have taken it too, whatever you're telling yourself right now. But here is the other truth, the one I've been chewing on since the ward-stones at Hollowmere, since the girl with the staff dragged me inside and broke every rule she had to do it:*

*I don't think the bargain was ever real.*

*Think. Why does the crown need the map delivered? They have agents everywhere. They have "friends" who could take it off me any day of the week—they said so themselves. They want ME to carry it. They want it delivered by MY hand, to THEIR designees, on THEIR schedule. That's not logistics. That's ritual. Everything that thing in the king's head does is ritual. I'm not a courier anymore. I'm an ingredient.*

*So I've decided to spoil the recipe.*

*The map is real. The key is real—I swallowed it, Tam, just like you said, and I'm sorry, I'm sorry, but if I'd told you what I intended you'd have tried to stop me and one of us would be dead instead of both of us being useful. The key is a sliver of true-silver etched with the counter-sigil. Without it the map shows the way to the binding but not the way THROUGH it. Let them have the incomplete thing. Let them perform their little ceremony with a broken instrument. And let whoever finds this letter find the rest.*

*The key passes with my body. Hedge-witch can draw it out—he'll hate it, he'll grumble, but he owes the crown a debt and this is how he pays it. Tell him the debt dies with the delivery. Tell him Sera Vane settled her accounts.*

*To the one holding this: I chose you. Not the crown. Not the agents. When I crawled to those ward-stones I could have gone east on the low road and reached the capital in six days. I came north instead. I came to the valley, because everything in me that isn't bought says the answer starts where the magic stops.*

*Don't waste it on grief. Waste it on the solstice.*

*—S.*

Brannoc sat very still for a long time.

The fire had burned down to coals. Out past the lean-to, the alders went on turning their invisible pages, and somewhere on the slope Theo walked their watch, unaware that the dead woman they mourned had just handed them, posthumously, the whole weight of her war.

*He'll hate it, he'll grumble, but he owes the crown a debt and this is how he pays it.*

She had known. Eleven days on the road together, and she had known what he was, what he'd done at the coronation, what he owed—and she had planned around him the way a general plans around terrain. Used his guilt as surely as the crown's agents had used her love. Everyone in this story was a lever, and everyone thought they were the hand.

And the nausea rose in him then, slow and tidal, the moral sickness he'd been swallowing since the night he'd first drawn the sigils that helped bind a living man inside his own skull. Because here was the arithmetic of it, laid out plain: Sera Vane had betrayed the crown to save her brother, betrayed her brother's bargain to save the king, and spent her last living act steering the map toward a stranger she'd known for minutes—all of it tangled, all of it compromised, every choice stained—and somehow, impossibly, the tangle pointed true. She had been a traitor in every direction at once, and every treason had landed, like a drunk archer's arrow, in the same honest target.

While Brannoc himself—sober, willing, fully informed—had helped forge the chain in the first place.

* * *

He built the key's extraction in his mind the way he built any working: components, costs, risks. True-silver resisted flesh; that was its nature and its virtue. A sliver of it, swallowed, would sit in the gut like a splinter of winter, doing slow damage, unless it was warded—and it would have been warded, because Tam Vane was careful. A containment sigil, probably inked onto the metal itself, holding it inert, harmless, patient. Drawing it out meant unpicking that sigil without breaking it, because the counter-mark Sera described would be cut into the same face, and if the containment shattered mid-extraction the true-silver would wake and start eating the nearest living thing.

Which would be him.

He should wake Maren. Maren had steady hands and no sentimentality and would hold the lamp without flinching. He should wake Theo, because it was Theo's quest, Theo's burden, Theo's dead woman, and secrets had rotted this kingdom once already.

He did none of these things. Instead he knelt by the banked fire, spread a cloth, laid out his knives and his chalk and the small clay pot of ash-and-tallow ink he'd carried for twenty years, and began to work.

The rite took two hours. He sang most of it, the low wordless humming that steadied the hands, and twice he had to stop and press his forehead against his knees until the world stopped tilting. When it was done, a sliver of metal no longer than his smallest finger lay on the cloth, dull gray, etched on one face with a containment lattice fine as spiderwork and on the other with a single sigil that seemed to move when he wasn't looking directly at it—the counter-mark, the way *through* the binding rather than merely to it.

He closed his fist around it and felt it cold as a coin from a riverbed, and he understood, finally, the full shape of what Sera Vane had done. She hadn't just hidden a key in her body. She'd hidden a *choice*. Every step of this journey, the crown's agents had believed they were herding a compliant courier toward a scripted delivery—and the whole time, the script's final page had been traveling inside a corpse, waiting for a guilty hedge-witch to dig it out.

She'd made him her instrument too. And the terrible thing, the thing that made his eyes sting and his throat close, was that he didn't mind. It was the first useful thing anyone had asked of him in four years.

* * *

Dawn came up gray and thin over Grayfen Hollow. Brannoc sat by the dead fire with the key wrapped in oilcloth against his chest and rehearsed what he would say—*I found letters, I should have looked sooner, I'm sorry*—and rehearsed again, and got no better at it.

Then Theo came down the slope from the watch, stamping frost from their boots, and stopped dead ten feet away, and Brannoc saw their gaze go to the open satchel, the scattered letters, the bloodied cloth where he'd worked—and saw the exact moment understanding arrived, sharp as the cold.

"You went through her things," Theo said. Not accusing. Worse than accusing—*waiting*.

"I did." Brannoc rose, knees cracking, and drew out the oilcloth bundle, and held it out on his open palm between them like an offering or a confession. "And Theo—she left us more than a map. She left us the way through. And she left a warning besides."

Theo's eyes narrowed. "What warning?"

Brannoc glanced back at the letters, at the elegant, unsigned hand of the second one, and felt the morning turn colder. "The crown's agents weren't following us to take the map," he said. "They were following us to make sure we *delivered* it. Which means everything we've done since Bellwether—the ford, the detours, all of it—we haven't been running from them."

He watched the color drain from Theo's face as the implication landed.

"We've been walking exactly where they want us. Straight to the capital gates. And they don't know yet that their courier spoiled the recipe."

Far off to the south, faint on the windless air, came a sound Brannoc had hoped never to hear again: a hunting horn, high and clear, sounding once—and then, after a long pause, once more. Closer.
