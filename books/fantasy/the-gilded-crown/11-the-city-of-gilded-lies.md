## 11. The City of Gilded Lies

They smelled the capital before they saw it.

Brannoc named it first, standing in the wagon bed with his nostrils flared like a hound's. "Smoke and tallow," he said. "Ten thousand hearths burning at once. And under it—" he paused, and his weathered face went strange, "—something sweet. Like honey left too long in the sun."

Theo climbed up beside him and breathed deep. The road had carried them for eleven days out of the hill country, down through bare orchards and frozen fields where scarecrows wore solstice ribbons, and the whole time the world had been growing louder, brighter, more crowded—villages becoming towns becoming a sprawl of torchlit suburbs that clung to the great road like barnacles to a ship's hull. But this was different. This was not the smell of people. This was something underneath the people, thick as resin, sweet as rot.

"I smell it too," Maren said from the driver's bench, her wounded arm bound tight against her chest, her hands steady on the reins. She had not been steady since the ford. "It gets stronger the closer you come to the palace. I told myself it was the incense they burn for the solstice rites. I told myself that for twenty years."

"And now?" Theo asked.

Maren flicked the reins. "Now I don't tell myself things anymore."

The road crested a long rise, and there it was: Aldenmere City, spread across three hills above a frozen river, wearing its winter festival like a bride wears lace. Every roofline glittered. Every spire was capped with gold or gilt or something pretending to be either, and the low sun caught all of it at once so that the whole city seemed to be on fire without burning—a single vast flame shaped like a place where people lived. Banners streamed from every window, white and crimson, the king's colors. Even at this distance Theo could hear the bells: dozens of them, hundreds, ringing out of rhythm with one another so that the sound was less music than weather, rolling over the fields in great slow waves.

"Solstice eve is tomorrow," Brannoc said quietly. "The Renewal. They'll have the ceremony at dusk, when the light dies. Everyone in the city will be there. Everyone *wants* to be there." He said the last part oddly, as if reporting a symptom rather than a custom.

Theo looked at him. "You've been before."

"I crowned him." Brannoc's voice was flat as slate. "Nine years ago. I stood on the dais with the circlet in my hands and I felt it pull at me like a hook through the soft part of my skull, and I set it on his head anyway, because the rite said to, because the archpriest was watching, because I owed the crown a debt I didn't understand yet." He turned away and busied himself with the bundles in the wagon bed. "Aye. I've been before."

* * *

They entered the city at the River Gate, in a line of carts and pilgrims and farmers' wagons heaped with winter squash, and the gate guards barely glanced at them—the city was swallowing thousands a day, everyone come for the festival, and four travel-worn strangers in a borrowed cart were nothing worth a second look. Theo had expected checkpoints, scrutiny, the hunters from the ford waiting with drawn steel. Instead there was only the crowd, cheerful and crushing, sweeping them inward like a current.

And then Theo stepped beneath the gate arch, and stopped breathing.

Wards.

Not the rough country craft of Hollowmere's boundary stones—not even close—but the same *grammar*. Theo had spent their whole life reading ward-lines the way other people read street signs: the gatehouse wards, the beacon-fire's containment ring, the old boundary script their grandmother had drilled into them stroke by stroke until they could draw it blindfolded in frost. And what ran through the stones of the River Gate was that script, grown vast and ornate, the way a river delta is the same water as the mountain spring. Layered sigils. Anchor-points sunk deep. A containment lattice woven so thoroughly into the architecture that no one who lived inside it would ever think to look for it, any more than a fish thinks to look for water.

"Hollowmere," Theo whispered.

Maren heard. Of course she did; she'd wedged herself close in the crowd, keeping Theo within reach of her good arm since the ford. "What?"

"The gate." Theo pressed their palm flat against the cold stone, ignoring the jostling bodies, letting their trained senses open the way they did each morning at the valley's edge. "These are Underhill wards. Or—they're built on the same root-craft. Older, maybe. The valley's version is a dialect. This is the original tongue." Their fingers traced a sigil half-buried under centuries of repointing mortar. "Whoever raised Hollowmere learned here. Or whoever built this learned *there*, before there was a there."

"That's not possible," Maren said. "The palace wards predate your valley by centuries. Any cartographer knows the founding charters—Hollowmere was granted after the Second Succession, three hundred years—"

"Then the valley wasn't the copy." Theo pulled their hand back. It tingled, faintly, the way a limb tingles when it wakes. "The valley was the *echo*."

They came out the far side of the gate into the festival crush, and Theo understood, suddenly and completely, why Sera had died rather than let the map fall back into the city's hands—and why she had chosen a gatekeeper to carry it. Not because Theo was brave. Because Theo was the only kind of person alive who would feel this lattice and *know it for what it was*.

* * *

Maren found them lodging in the house of a woman she'd known in another life—a deposed minor functionary, a widow named Odile who took coin for beds and asked no questions, in a crooked lane in the Tanners' Quarter where the festival banners hung faded and the gilding on the door-knockers had worn down to dull brass. From the attic window Theo could see the palace on its high hill, blazing with light, and between them and it the whole city laid out like a map Maren hadn't drawn yet.

That evening they went out into the streets, because Brannoc needed to see the ceremony grounds and Maren needed to fix the palace's approaches in her head, and Theo—Theo needed to walk through the thing they had felt in the gate-stones and confirm what their skin already knew.

The city at night was unbearable and beautiful. Solstice lanterns everywhere, paper and horn and glass, strung across every alley like low-hung stars. Roasting-chestnut smoke. Choirs rehearsing in doorways. Children running past with tin crowns on their heads, cheap and bright, and adults wearing them too, and wine flowing in the gutters almost literally, spilled from toasts to the king's health, to the king's long reign, to the king, the king, the king.

And everywhere, everywhere, the gold.

Theo began to notice it in the first hour and could not stop noticing after. It was not that the city was wealthy—though it was, obscenely, ostentatiously, in a way that made Hollowmere's careful poverty seem like another country's custom. It was that the wealth was *wrong*. Gilt on the shrine-posts had been applied in patterns that resolved, when Theo squinted, into sigils. The gold leaf on the tavern signs curled at the edges in spirals that matched the spiral at the center of the crown's binding-work—or so Theo guessed; they'd only seen the crown once, in a woodcut in Sera's papers, but the shape had burned itself into memory. Fountain basins lined with soft yellow metal hummed, very faintly, at the edge of hearing, a note Theo felt in their back teeth. Even the coins in Theo's pocket seemed warmer than copper should be.

"It's in everything," Theo said, stopping in the mouth of a square where a great gilded statue of some dead king reared over the crowd, its raised sword catching lantern-light. "The metal. It's not decoration. It's *conduit*. Vhalor doesn't just sit in the crown—he drinks through it. Through all of it. Every gilded surface in this city is a straw stuck into ten thousand people."

Brannoc had gone gray. "I never saw it. Nine years, and I never saw it."

"You weren't taught to see it. Nobody was. That's the genius of it." Theo watched the crowd flow around the statue's base, touching its gilded foot for luck as they passed, hundreds of hands an hour, an unbroken stream of contact. "In Hollowmere we kept the wards fed with fire and blood-oath and nine generations of deliberate attention. Here—" Theo gestured at the square, the lanterns, the singing, the joy. "Here the wards are fed by *festival*. By love. They've made the feeding feel like a holiday. These people aren't Vhalor's prisoners. They're his congregation, and they don't know it, and if you told them they'd burn you for blasphemy against the king."

Maren stood very still, looking up at the statue. Snow had begun to fall, fine and dry, settling on the dead king's shoulders. "My master maps," she said. "When I drew this city, I drew the gilding. Every commission, every survey—I recorded it as ornament. Gold leaf, decorative, value such-and-such." Her laugh had no humor in it at all. "Twenty years of honest work, and I was drawing his veins and calling them jewelry."

* * *

They reached the palace hill near midnight, keeping to shadowed streets, and stopped where the processional way began: a broad avenue climbing toward gates of white stone and gold that even the festival crowds did not press close to. Guards in crimson stood in ranks. Above them, floodlit by bonfires, the gates themselves blazed.

Theo had known. Had known since the River Gate, had spent the whole evening knowing, and still the sight of it struck like a blow to the sternum.

The palace gates were warded exactly as the Hollowmere gatehouse was warded.

Not similar. Not analogous. *Identical*—the anchor-sequence, the triple containment ring, the keystone sigil that Theo's grandmother had called the Keeper's Knot and taught them to renew with their own blood on the longest night of every year. There it was, eight feet tall, wrought in gold on white stone, holding shut a pair of doors through which no magic could pass—which meant, which could only mean—

"It's a cage," Theo said aloud. Their voice sounded very small under the bonfire-crackle. "The palace. The whole palace is a cell. The same craft as our gate, inverted—we ward to keep things out, and this—" They couldn't finish. Their eyes were doing something embarrassing and they let it happen, staring at the beautiful terrible gates through a blur.

Maren's good hand closed on their shoulder. "Say it plainly."

"The crown isn't anchored to the king." Theo wiped their face with the heel of their hand, roughly, angrily. "The crown is anchored to the *palace*. To the city. Maybe to the kingdom—the ward-lines run out of these gates and down through the whole city, I felt them at the river, they're everywhere, under every street. Vhalor isn't a prisoner in Cassian's head. Cassian's head is the lock, and the kingdom is the chain, and the chain runs through every gilded thing from here to the border." They laughed, and hated the sound of it. "We warded one valley. He got warded into an entire country. That's what the crown *is*. That's what the Renewal is for. Tomorrow they'll gather the whole city to watch, and every loving heart in the crowd will pour itself into the lattice, and the cage will hold another year."

Brannoc sat down slowly on a mounting-block, as if his legs had been taken from him without notice. "The debt I owe the crown," he said. "I thought it was for the coronation. For setting the crown on him." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and ancient. "It's for building the cage. My line built this, Theo. Hedge-witches, crown-sworn, generation after generation—we raised the lattice, we taught the gilders the patterns, we called it holy work. I crowned him because my grandmother crowned the king before him, and she did it because hers did, all the way back." He put his face in his hands. "Your family kept one small lie for nine generations. Mine built the big one."

For a long moment none of them spoke. The snow fell. Somewhere behind them the city sang, thousands of voices raised in a solstice hymn Theo now heard correctly for the first time—an old song about a king who holds the darkness, who bears it for his people, who must never, ever lay it down. Children were singing it. They sounded happy.

Theo looked at the gates, at the Keeper's Knot blazing gold above the guards' heads, and felt the last of their childhood fall away. Hollowmere had been a lie, yes—a loving lie, a small one, a wall around a garden. But this. This was the truth the lie had been hiding from. The kingdom was the valley writ large, and the king was the prisoner, and every bell and banner and tin crown was a link in the chain, and tomorrow at dusk the whole congregation would gather to tighten it.

"We can't go through those gates," Maren said at last, practically, because someone had to be. "Not as petitioners, not with the hunters looking for us. So we don't. We go under." She turned to Theo, and in the bonfire-light her scarred face held something Theo had never seen there before: not cynicism, not hope, but the fierce cold clarity of a gambler pushing everything onto one card. "Every palace has bones underneath it. Old foundations, sealed wells, archive-tunnels. I mapped half of them before they stripped my credentials—and the half I mapped, I mapped *honestly*, which is more than anyone in that building can say." She jerked her chin at the gates. "Tomorrow, while the whole city watches the Renewal, we go in through the dark."

Brannoc lifted his head. "There's a chapel-ward under the east foundation. Consecrated ground, disused sixty years. If anywhere in that palace still stands outside the lattice—"

"Then that's where we make camp," Maren said. "And that's where we wait for whatever chance the solstice gives us."

Theo took one last look at the gates—at the knot of gold that was, stroke for stroke, their own family's signature—and felt something settle in their chest, hard and bright as a coal drawn from the beacon-fire.

*Home,* they thought. *He's been locked in a counterfeit of home.*

They turned away from the light and followed Maren down into the dark of the streets, toward the tunnels, toward the solstice—and behind them, high on its hill, the palace blazed like a lamp in which something enormous was slowly drowning.

None of them saw the figure detach from the shadows by the mounting-block and slip away uphill, moving with a hedge-witch's silent tread, toward the guards, toward the gates, toward whatever leash still ran from his throat to his master's hand.
