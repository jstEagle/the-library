## 12. An Audience of One

The lucidity came the way a fish comes to the surface of a frozen lake—suddenly, gasping, and certain to be brief.

Cassian knew it at once for what it was: a crack in the ice of his own skull. One moment he was watching from the deep places, pressed flat against the inside of his own eyes while Vhalor signed a decree raising the grain levy by a third, and the next moment his hand was his hand again, the quill trembling in fingers that remembered how to shake.

*Ten minutes,* said some cold arithmetic part of him that had survived nine months of captivity in its own body. *Perhaps less. Do not waste them on despair.*

He was alone in the map room. That was luck, and luck was a currency Vhalor did not believe in spending on him. The candles had burned down to stubs; through the tall windows the solstice preparations glowed across the city like a fever—lanterns strung between towers, scaffolds rising in the great square where the renewal ceremony would be held, garlands of gold leaf already wilting in the cold. Three days until solstice. Three days until the binding was renewed and whatever remained of Cassian Aldenmere was scraped out like the last of a jar of honey.

He set down the quill. His hand left a smear of gold ink on the decree—he would remember that later, the smear, the small human mess of it—and he stood very still, listening.

Vhalor was elsewhere. Not gone—the thing was never gone; it coiled through him like smoke through a locked room—but *elsewhere*, attention pulled taut toward something outside the palace walls. Toward the river country. Toward four travelers it had been herding for weeks with hunters and forged orders and roads that closed behind them like a fist.

*Toward the map.*

Cassian had felt it when they entered the city. He didn't know how he knew—they were strangers, ghosts, names he'd only caught in fragments of Vhalor's hunting thoughts—but he knew the way a man knows rain is coming, in the ache of old wounds. Four of them. And one of them carried something that made the thing inside him go quiet and hungry and *afraid*.

"Ten minutes," he said aloud, and his voice cracked on the words, rusted from disuse. When had he last spoken as himself? He screamed, sometimes, inside. He had not spoken *out loud* since autumn.

He moved. There was no time to be careful, so he was fast instead.

The map room's ledger cabinet stood against the north wall, oak and iron, holding the kingdom's tax records—dull, voluminous, beneath anyone's interest, which made it the safest place in the palace. Cassian had spent months of half-awareness hiding things there the way a drowning man hides breaths: a name here, a date there, written in his own hand during flickers too brief to act upon, tucked into columns of figures no one would ever read. A message in installments. A letter to whoever might someday come looking.

He pulled the ledger for the winter quarter. His hands were steadier than he deserved. Between the entry for the millers' guild and the salt tax he found what past-Cassian had left: three lines in cipher, the cipher being the one he and his sister used as children, which no living soul should have known, because Elira was dead, and dead sisters kept secrets well.

*The rite has two halves. Vhalor holds one. I hold the other. Chapel-ward under the old sanctuary, third stone from the hearth. Take it. Do not come for me.*

Below that, in a shakier hand, added during some worse night:

*I am sorry. Whatever I sign, it isn't me.*

Cassian pressed his palm flat against the page. Nine months of being a passenger in his own flesh, of watching his body smile and sentence and sign, and here was proof he had still been fighting the whole time—small, futile, magnificent fighting, the kind that changed nothing and meant everything.

Then the crack in the ice widened, and he nearly fell to his knees with the sheer *size* of being himself.

It was like surfacing after drowning. Every sensation arrived at once and honestly: the candle-smoke bitter in his nose, the cold radiating off the window glass, the ache in his jaw where Vhalor clenched his teeth at night. He could feel his own heartbeat. He had forgotten he had one. It was fast and frightened and gloriously, stupidly alive.

And underneath everything, faint as a thread of silver under black water, he could feel the binding itself—the crown's grip on his skull, loosened tonight by whatever alchemy of distance and solstice-eve strain had bought him this hour. The circlet sat in its reliquary two floors below, and even from here he could feel it *thinking*. It had noticed the weakening. It was reaching for him even now, patient as tide.

Minutes. He had minutes.

He tore the page from the ledger, folded it into a square small enough to hide in a palm, and then stood in the middle of the room with no idea what to do next—because the message said *do not come for me*, but the message assumed no one would ever find it, and now someone might, and every plan he had made in the dark corners of his captivity fell apart in the light of an actual chance.

A knock at the door.

Cassian's blood went to ice. For one vertiginous second he couldn't remember whose face he was wearing—*his own, it's your own, you are the king*—and then the door opened without waiting, because servants did not wait on kings who had stopped noticing they were men.

It was the under-steward, Pellam, a gray man with gray eyes who reported to Vhalor directly and pretended otherwise. "Your Majesty. The Master of Ceremonies requests confirmation of the solstice order."

"Send him tomorrow," Cassian said, and heard with horror how easily the king's voice came to him, how naturally authority sat in his mouth. Had he always sounded like this? Or had Vhalor worn the voice so long it had learned the shape?

Pellam hesitated. His gaze flicked—to the torn ledger? To the folded paper? No. To the window, the city, the scaffold in the square. "The procession route, Majesty. Through the Gilded Quarter, or the longer way by the river?"

"The river," Cassian said, though he had no idea why, except that the longer way bought time, and time was the only coin he had left. "Now leave me. I am not to be disturbed tonight. By anyone."

Something moved behind Pellam's gray eyes—not suspicion exactly. Recognition, maybe, of a tone slightly wrong, a king slightly *present*. "As Your Majesty wishes." He bowed and withdrew, and Cassian counted his own heartbeats until the footsteps faded, and understood that he had perhaps just spent a full minute of his ten on a conversation about parade routes.

*Four minutes left, if you're lucky. Move.*

* * *

He found them because the binding let him—or rather, because the binding's weakness ran both ways, and for these few minutes the wall between Cassian and Vhalor was thin enough that he could feel what Vhalor felt, reversed like light through a lens. The entity's attention lay on the city's southern edge, on the tannery district where the streets smelled of lime and lye and no respectable person went after dark. It was watching a safehouse there. Watching four travelers it believed it had cornered.

So Cassian went to them the only way he could: he walked out of the palace.

It should have been impossible. The gates were warded—*he* had warded them, years ago, in another life, with the same craft his ancestors had used on Hollowmere valley, and he felt the wards recognize him and hesitate, king and prisoner and monster all wearing the same face. But the bindings were fraying everywhere tonight; the whole city's gilding hummed with strain, and the ward-lines parted around him like water around a stone, grudging and uncertain. Perhaps they could not tell anymore what he was. Neither could he.

The tannery district received him with indifference. He wore a plain cloak over plain clothes—found in a servant's corridor, taken without thought, the first theft of his reign—and walked with his head down through streets where children had chalked sunbursts on the walls for solstice, and every sunburst had been drawn with one ray too many, crooked, wrong, the way everything in this city was subtly wrong now, gilded and infected. He passed a baker's window full of solstice bread shaped like crowns, and had to stop and press his hand against the cold brick until the laughter died in his throat, because it wasn't laughter, it was something else, something that wanted to howl.

The safehouse was above a chandler's shop, up stairs that groaned like an old ship. Vhalor's watchers were across the street—a fruit stall that had sold no fruit in days, two men playing dice with dice they never threw. Cassian walked past them without turning his head. They saw a cloaked figure and dismissed it; no one looked at the king's face when the king was not expected to exist.

He climbed the stairs and knocked, four beats, then two, a rhythm he chose from nothing, from hope.

Silence. Then, low, through the wood: "We're closed."

"I know what Sera Vane carried," Cassian said. "I know what she died for. And I have perhaps three minutes before I am not myself again, so either open this door or let me say my piece to a plank of wood."

A long pause. Then bolts, drawn one by one, and the door opened on lamplight and four faces.

He knew none of them, and all of them. The cartographer—Maren Osk, disgraced, he remembered the scandal now, maps redrawn to flatter a lord—stood with a knife held low and professional. The hedge-witch Brannoc hovered behind her, and the sight of him landed in Cassian like a blow: Brannoc had officiated at his coronation, had bound the crown to his brow with words older than the kingdom, and the guilt on the man's face said he had been counting the cost ever since. A big young man with a gatekeeper's calloused hands filled the doorway's other half.

And beside him—

Beside him stood a woman Cassian had never seen, and yet the binding *recoiled* from her, a flinch he felt in his own teeth. She had dust-colored hair and eyes the grey-green of valley slate, and she held herself the way people do who have spent their lives standing watch over something. Around her neck, on a cord, hung a scrap of stained vellum, and even folded small, even hidden, Cassian could feel it pulling at the thing inside him like a hook in a great fish's jaw.

"You're the king," she said. Not a question. Her voice was steady, but her knuckles were white on the doorframe.

"I'm the cage," Cassian said. "The king is what's inside it. May I come in before the difference stops mattering?"

* * *

They gave him a stool near the lamp and stood arrayed around him like a tribunal, and he supposed he deserved it. Maren kept the knife. Brannoc wouldn't look at him. The big one—Theo, the others called him, Theo Underhill—stayed nearest the door, and Cassian understood that too: keep the exit clear, keep the stranger between yourself and escape.

But it was the woman, Sera—no. He corrected himself as they spoke. Sera was dead. This was another, a stranger whose name he caught in pieces—Wren, they called her, Wren of somewhere east—and she watched him with the particular stillness of someone deciding whether a story was true.

"You have minutes," Maren said. "Use them."

"The binding rite has two halves." He said it plainly, because there was no time for anything else. "When the crown was first forged—centuries ago, before Aldenmere was Aldenmere—the binders split the rite deliberately. Half went into the crown, into the metal and the words spoken at each renewal. The other half they hid, so that no one power could ever unmake the binding alone. Kings were trusted with the crown. Someone else was trusted with the rest." He looked at Brannoc. "You know this. You've known since you served at my coronation."

Brannoc's face was gray. "The order keeps its half. The Wardens' Vigil. I thought—" His voice failed and restarted. "I thought the Vigil still existed."

"It exists," Cassian said. "As a ruin in the mountains and a list of dead names. Vhalor found their chapter house eleven years ago, before it ever wore me. It burned the rite's outer texts. What remains of the true sequence, I have." He drew the folded ledger page from his cloak and held it out—to Theo, because Theo was the one who hadn't threatened him, and because something in the gatekeeper's stillness said *this one carries weight for a living*. "Third stone from the hearth, chapel-ward beneath the old sanctuary. My half of the rite, and the counterwords. It's incomplete without what the courier swallowed—I know about the key; Vhalor's rage when it learned the map was crippled told me enough—but incomplete may be enough, if the bearer is willing."

"Willing," Theo repeated. The word came out carefully, the way a man tests ice.

"The binding must be *accepted* to be broken cleanly. Forced, it shatters, and what's in me goes free in a city full of solstice crowds—a thousand wills gathered in one square, ripe for the taking. That's why the ceremony matters. That's why Vhalor moved it up." Cassian heard his own voice flatten and made himself stop, made himself look at each of them in turn. "So hear the rest, and hear it clearly."

The lamp guttered. Somewhere below, the chandler's dog began to bark and then thought better of it.

"When you break the binding, there is a moment—a breath, no more—when the burden has nowhere to live. It will take the nearest willing vessel. That is how it was designed; mercy and trap in the same mechanism, because the original binders knew that freeing a thing like Vhalor requires someone to hold the door shut while it dies." His hands were shaking now, and he let them. "Someone must wear it. And the wearing, done rightly, kills. Done wrongly, it doesn't even manage that—it just makes a new host, and you'll have traded a king for a gatekeeper, and I will have helped you murder a stranger."

"No," Theo said. Simply, immediately, the way other men say *hello*.

"You don't get to refuse on my behalf."

"I'm not refusing on your behalf." Theo's eyes were terrible and calm. "I'm telling you how this ends differently than you think. But go on. Finish begging us to let you die."

Cassian laughed—one cracked, astonished note—and it hurt his throat and his heart in equal measure. "Yes. All right. Here is my begging, then, gatekeeper." He leaned forward, and the lamp caught his face, and he watched them see it: the hollows, the strain, the king-shaped ruin of a man nine months eaten. "If it comes to a choice—if the rite offers you my life or the world's safety—choose the world. Do not bargain. Do not improvise. Do not stand in the square and decide that a king you met for five minutes is worth a kingdom. Let me die with the thing in me, and count it a mercy, because I have been dead since last spring in every way that matters, and the only thing keeping me animate is the fear of what I'd become if I stopped fighting." His voice broke on the last of it, and he hated that, and let it break anyway. "Promise me. All of you. Promise me you'll let me go."

Silence. Maren looked away first. Brannoc's lips moved soundlessly, some old penitent's formula. Wren watched Cassian with an expression he couldn't read—grief, maybe, for a man she'd known for minutes, or recognition of something she'd already lost once.

It was Theo who answered.

"I promise," Theo said, "that no one will be left carrying it alone."

"That is not what I asked."

"It's what I can give." The gatekeeper's voice didn't rise at all, and somehow that was worse, or better; it had the finality of ward-stone. "I've watched my family carry things alone for nine generations. It doesn't work. It's never worked. It just makes the carrying heavier until the carrier breaks. So no—I won't promise to let you die alone, and I won't promise to save you alone, and I won't promise anything except that whatever happens at solstice, it happens shared. That's the only oath I have."

Cassian stared at him. Something in his chest—something he'd thought Vhalor had already eaten—turned over painfully, like a sleeper waking.

"That," he said finally, "is the most frightening thing anyone has said to me all year. And this year a god ate my mind."

Somewhere far below, felt more than heard, a bell began to toll the midnight hour—and with it, at the base of Cassian's skull, the ice began to close. He felt Vhalor turning back toward him through the corridors of his own body, vast and irritated, a landlord returning early. The edges of the room started to dim, as though the lamp were burning underwater.

"Time," he said, standing. "Listen, quickly. The renewal ceremony is a trap—it's meant to feed on the crowd, a thousand wills to make it strong enough to survive losing the map's key. Vhalor will expect you to strike at the ceremony. So don't. The chapel-ward, the old sanctuary—the binding's roots run deepest there, where the first rite was spoken. Strike at the root while it stands in the square believing itself untouchable." He was moving as he talked, toward the door, because he could not afford to be found here, could not afford what Vhalor would do with the knowledge—or worse, what Vhalor would make *him* do. "The paper tells you the rest. Burn it once memorized. And gatekeeper—"

He paused at the threshold, hand on the frame, and looked back at the four of them in their lamplight: a thief with a knife, a witch with a debt, a stranger wearing a dead woman's mission, and a keeper of doors who refused to let any door close on a person.

"If I falter tomorrow—in the square, publicly, if the king stumbles and cannot finish the litany—that will be me. Fighting. Buy me those seconds. Spend them well."

Then he was down the stairs and into the dark, walking fast, feeling the last of himself fold away like a banner in a rising wind—and the last thing he carried down into the deep with him, the thing he would hold against all the long dark months of not-being, was not hope exactly.

It was the memory of a stranger saying *no one will be left carrying it alone*, and the wild, treasonous, unbearable possibility that it might be true.
