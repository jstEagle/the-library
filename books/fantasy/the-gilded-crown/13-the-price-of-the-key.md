## 13. The Price of the Key

The prison at Ketter's Reach had been built by men who believed that stone could hold a secret the way a jar holds water. Maren Osk had drawn its plans once—twenty years ago, a young cartographer with ink-stained fingers and a commission from the Crown, standing in the warden's office while he bragged about his walls. *Three feet thick,* he'd said, tapping her draft with a sausage-thick finger. *No one gets out of Ketter's Reach.*

She had never told him that no one got in, either, and that was the flaw. A building designed against escape was a building that forgot to watch its doors.

Now she stood in the frozen ditch below the eastern wall, breath fogging, and studied the shape she'd put on paper two decades past. The snow had softened everything—the crenellations, the guard-towers, the ugly squat bulk of it—but memory is a cruel kind of map. It doesn't fade. It just waits.

"The drainage culvert," she murmured. "South face, under the tannery run. I drew it myself. They never filled it."

Theo crouched beside her, hood up, cheeks raw with cold. In the weeks since Bellwether they had all gone lean and quiet, but Theo had changed most—not harder exactly, though there was that too, the ford had seen to that—but *narrower*, somehow. Focused down to a single bright thread. Maren recognized it because she'd seen it in herself once, before the Crown took her maps and her name and left her with neither.

"You're sure this is where they'd keep him?" Theo asked.

"Sera's letter said the Reach." Brannoc shifted behind them, staff wrapped in wool so it wouldn't knock against stone. The hedge-witch looked older than his years tonight, gray-faced in the moonlight. "And the letter wasn't wrong about anything else."

*The letter.* Maren still felt the shape of it like a bruise. Sera Vane, dead in a gatehouse in Hollowmere, had written one last message and hidden it in the lining of her boot—a woman who planned for her own death the way other people planned for supper. *They have Tam. Deliver the map whole and he walks free. Fail, and they'll feed him to whatever wears the king's face.* A bargain signed in a dying woman's blood. A double agent, Brannoc had called her, and then hated himself for saying it aloud.

Maren hadn't hated herself. She'd understood. She had spent twenty years learning exactly what a person would trade when the Crown held the scales.

"Guards?" Theo asked.

"Four on the wall walk, two at the gate, changing at midnight." Maren tapped the numbers against her knee. "But the culvert comes up inside the laundry yard, and the laundry yard door locks from the inside after evening count. Sloppy. I told them it was sloppy when I drew it." She allowed herself a thin smile. "Nobody listens to cartographers. We only tell you where everything is."

Brannoc made a sound that might have been a laugh if it had gotten all the way out. "Then let's go be ignored."

* * *

The culvert smelled of tallow and rot and old iron, and it was exactly as narrow as she remembered, which meant crawling on elbows through ice-slick muck while the prison's weight pressed down overhead like a hand on the back of the neck. Maren went first. She told herself it was because she knew the way. She told herself a lot of things these days.

Twice the passage narrowed where frost had heaved the stones, and twice she had to exhale everything in her lungs and pull herself through by fingertips, thinking, absurdly, of her old drafting table in the capital, the smell of fresh vellum, the clean certainty of lines on paper. Lines didn't bleed. Lines didn't crawl through sewage toward a dead man's cell. Somewhere along the road from Bellwether she had stopped drawing the world and started living inside the worst map she'd ever made.

Light ahead. The grate over the laundry yard, rusted through at one corner. She lifted it an inch, waited, listened to the wind move across the yard, and slid out into shadow.

Theo came up behind her, silent as spilled water. Brannoc followed, wheezing, muttering something under his breath that was either a prayer or a curse—in hedge-witchcraft, Maren had learned, the distinction was mostly academic.

The yard door was locked from the inside, just as she'd promised. Theo set their shoulder to it, and the old wood gave with a crack that sounded, in the dead silence, like a cathedral bell. All three of them froze. Above them, on the wall walk, a guard coughed and stamped his feet and moved on.

Inside: a corridor lit by a single lamp, cells on either side, each door studded with iron and marked with a chalked number. The air was warm here—heated, Maren realized, by the ovens of the kitchen above—and that warmth made it worse somehow. Ketter's Reach wasn't cruel the way some prisons were cruel. It was comfortable. Comfortable, and sealed, and patient. The Crown's favorite kind of cruelty: the kind that could afford to wait.

"Cell forty-one," Brannoc whispered. He had the letter again, though he couldn't read it in the dark; he simply needed his hand on it. "End of the row."

They walked. Their footsteps were soft. The lamp flame leaned as they passed, as if bowing, or warning.

Forty-one was the last door, smaller than the others, set into the foundation wall itself. No window. No brazier grate. A cell for someone the Crown wanted forgotten rather than punished—punishment required an audience, and audiences required light.

Maren put her eye to the keyhole and saw nothing but dark.

"Tam Vane," she said quietly, to the door. "Royal courier's brother. Held under seal." Nothing moved. "We've come about your sister."

Still nothing. And the quality of the nothing began to change, the way silence does when it has been silence for a very long time.

Theo reached past her and tried the door. Unlocked. That was the first wrong thing. Prisons did not leave doors unlocked; doors were the entire point of prisons. The second wrong thing was the smell that seeped out when the hinges groaned—not the reek of a neglected man, not filth or fever, but something sweeter and colder underneath. Incense. Myrrh and ash.

Crown incense. The kind burned in coronation rites.

"No," Brannoc breathed, and pushed past them both into the dark.

Maren struck flint to the lamp she'd carried from the yard, and the light came up yellow and trembling, and showed them what the Crown's patience looked like.

Tam Vane lay on the narrow cot with his hands folded on his chest, arranged—*arranged*, that was the word that lodged in Maren's throat like a fishhook—with a care that was worse than violence. Someone had washed him. Someone had combed his hair back from his forehead. Someone had dressed him in a white shift and folded his hands and closed his eyes and set a sprig of dried myrrh between his fingers, and then someone had left him here in the dark to wait for whoever came asking, because the message was the point. The body was the message.

He had been dead for months.

"How—" Theo's voice cracked and steadied, the way it had learned to since the ford. "How long?"

Brannoc knelt and touched the wrist, professional despite everything, the hedge-witch's old habit of reading bodies like weather. His jaw worked. "Cold season. Before we ever left Bellwether. Before—" He stopped. Before Sera died. Before Sera had crawled half-mad across the moors to Hollowmere carrying a map and a bargain for a brother who was already three months in the ground.

"They killed him when she took the mission," Maren said. Her own voice sounded far away, flat, like a voice heard through a wall. "Or shortly after. It doesn't matter. The bargain was void the day she agreed to it. They just—" She gestured at the cot, at the myrrh, at the terrible tidiness. "They kept him fresh. In case she succeeded. In case they needed to reward her."

Or in case they needed to punish her, she didn't say. Both messages lived in the same arranged corpse. *Here is your prize. Here is your price.*

Theo stood very still in the doorway. Maren had watched this person hold a river ford alone against six armed hunters, had watched them kill a man with a gatekeeper's pole-arm and a gatekeeper's terrible calm, and none of it had looked like this. This was not battle-stillness. This was the stillness of someone watching a house burn down around a room they'd slept in all their life.

"She died for nothing," Theo said.

"No." Brannoc's voice came out rough. "No, listen to me. If the bargain was void, then she wasn't bound by it anymore. Whatever she did after—choosing Theo, choosing us, dying on that threshold instead of running—none of it bought her anything. There was no payment coming. She *knew*." He looked up, and his eyes were wet and furious. "A courier knows how bargains work. She must have known, or suspected, or—"

"Or hoped," Maren said quietly.

That was the thing none of them could get around. Hope was the cruelest currency the Crown dealt in, because it cost the bearer everything and the mint nothing. Sera Vane had swallowed a key and carried a map across a kingdom on the strength of a promise made by people who wore myrrh and ash and arranged corpses in the dark, and maybe she'd known, deep down, in the place couriers keep the things they can't afford to look at directly. Maybe that was why she'd been so desperate at the end. Maybe that was why she'd chosen a stranger named Underhill instead of riding on to the capital and her own salvation.

You don't gamble everything on a loaded game unless part of you already knows the dice are crooked.

Maren crossed to the cell's one piece of furniture besides the cot—a shelf bolted to the stone, holding a prisoner's few allowed effects. A wooden cup. A spoon. A prayer-book, spine broken, pages swollen with damp. And beneath the prayer-book, wrapped in oilcloth with a soldier's neatness that no prisoner would have had time for—

Her hands stopped.

The wrapping was recent. The folding was Crown-work. They had *left* it, the way they had left the door unlocked, the way they had left the lamp burning in the corridor. An invitation. A trap laid for whoever came asking after Sera Vane's ghost.

"It's here," she said. "The key."

"Don't touch it," Brannoc said sharply.

"I know." She didn't move. She looked at the little bundle the way she'd once looked at unmapped rivers—at the beautiful, treacherous blank space where the danger lived. "It's a gift, and it's a snare, and it's both at once, and they want us to take it anyway, because taking it means we're committed. Means we're going where they expect." She exhaled slowly. "Sera's letter said the key opens the Undervaults. The binding rite needs it. We take it or we lose."

"So we take it knowing it's bait," Theo said.

"So we take it knowing it's bait," Maren agreed. "There's a difference between walking into a trap blind and walking in with your eyes open. The first is death. The second is a negotiation."

She unwrapped the oilcloth.

The key was small—smaller than she'd imagined, no longer than her thumb, cast in dull black metal that drank the lamplight and gave nothing back. No wards crawled on its surface, no sigils, no ornament. It was the plainest object in the cell and the only one that hummed, faintly, at the edge of hearing, the way a struck bell hums after the sound has supposedly ended. Against her palm it was warm. Body-warm. As if it had been held recently. As if someone had been keeping it ready.

She thought of Sera swallowing such a thing—of the iron of it going down, of carrying it through weeks of flight in the secret hollow of her body, closer than any hiding place, trusting it to no one. And then she thought of the agents of the crown digging it back out—or not, perhaps they'd let the body keep it, perhaps the key had passed from corpse to corpse in some gilded evidence-room until tonight, when someone had placed it here like a card turned face-up.

"You were better than they deserved," Maren said to the dead woman on the cot. "Both of you."

She wrapped the key again and put it inside her coat, against her heart, where maps used to go.

* * *

They were halfway back down the corridor when the lamps changed.

It was subtle—one moment the flames burned steady, the next every flame in Ketter's Reach leaned east, all together, all at once, like grass bending under a wind that wasn't blowing. Brannoc swore in three languages. Theo's head came up sharply, the gatekeeper's instinct, the sense for wards that Maren had watched save their lives at the ford.

"What is it?" she whispered.

"Something noticed," Theo said. "Not here. Not us. Something big, somewhere south, and everything with a spark in it just turned to look." A pause. "The solstice is close. The binding's thinning. Whatever just happened—we're feeling the edges of it."

Maren thought of the capital, four days' hard ride south. Thought of gilded halls and a king who signed decrees with a stranger's handwriting. Thought of the myrrh-and-ash smell in a dead man's cell, coronation incense, the scent of a binding renewed.

"Move," she said. "Quietly. We're leaving through the culvert, and then we're riding through the night, because I think we just ran out of time."

Behind them, in cell forty-one, the lamp they'd left burning guttered once and stood up straight again, its flame pointing south like a compass needle, like an accusation, like a road.

They climbed into the dark of the drainage run, and above them the prison settled back into its long patience, having delivered its message at last:

*Come. You're expected.*
