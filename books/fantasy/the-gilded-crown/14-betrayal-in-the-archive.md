## 14. Betrayal in the Archive

The archive beneath the palace smelled of dust and tallow and something older—something like the inside of a sealed jar, air that had been waiting a long time for someone to breathe it. Theo held the lantern high and watched the light crawl along shelves that rose out of the dark like the ribs of some drowned ship.

"Careful where you step," Maren murmured behind them. "The floor's marked. Old surveyor's trick—certain tiles ring hollow, and the guards below hear everything."

Theo nodded without looking back. They had been in the capital three days, three days of gilded wrongness and solstice crowds, and tonight was the first night the plan had felt real instead of merely desperate. The rite fragment Cassian had pressed into their hand named this place: *the under-archive, where the first binding was drafted.* If they were going to unmake a crown, they needed to know how it had been made.

Brannoc walked last, as he always did. Theo heard his staff tap the tiles, tap, pause, tap—the rhythm of a man counting something. They had been counting for days, Theo realized now. Counting doors. Counting guards. Counting the hours between their movements.

But that realization hadn't come yet. Not quite yet.

* * *

It came an hour later, in the reading alcove at the archive's heart, where a desk of black oak sat beneath a dome pierced by star-shaped vents. Snow sifted through the vents in fine gray threads, melting on the warm stone. Theo spread the rite fragment beside Sera's map and the key Maren had taken from her brother's cell, and for a while there was only the scratch of Maren's pencil as she cross-referenced ward-lines on her stolen masterwork.

Then Brannoc said, quietly, "You should know that I've been lying to you since Bellwether."

Maren's pencil stopped.

Theo did not look up. Some part of them had known—had known since the ford, maybe, when the hunters had come around the bend of the river thirty seconds after Brannoc went to scout ahead. Some part of them had known and had chosen not to know, because the alternative was walking into the capital with two allies instead of three, and three had seemed like the difference between a chance and a prayer.

"Say it plainly," Theo said.

"I was cornered." Brannoc's voice was steady in the way of a man who has rehearsed steadiness for months. "Before I ever met you. After the coronation, when I ran. Vhalor's agents found me in a ditch outside Corvane with my throat opened to the windpipe and a healer's sigil sewn into the wound to keep it from closing. They told me the sigil would stay until they cut it out, and that they'd cut it out when I gave them what they wanted." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as old paper. "I thought I could outlast them. I lasted four days."

"What did they want?" Maren asked, though her voice said she already knew.

"A leash," Brannoc said. "A person close to anyone who might come for the king. They didn't know who. They just wanted ears and eyes threaded through every hedge-circle and river-temple in the north, because the crown *knew*—it has always known—that somewhere there were people who remembered what binding meant. And then a courier died in a valley that shouldn't exist, carrying a map that shouldn't exist, and suddenly they knew exactly who."

Theo finally looked up. In the lantern light, Brannoc's face had gone the color of the snow sifting through the vents. The healer's sigil was visible now that Theo knew to look—a fine silver thread of scar tissue circling his throat like a collar worn so long the flesh had grown around it.

"The ambush at the ford," Theo said.

"I lit the signal. A hedge-mark on a willow branch. It means *crossing watched, take them alive.*" His hands were flat on the table, pressing down as if the stone might buckle. "They wanted the map intact. They've wanted it intact since Sera took it. That's why she ran instead of handing it over—that's why they killed her escort but let her reach your valley. They couldn't touch the map while she carried it willingly. Compulsion slides off a willing carrier. So they followed her to the one place magic couldn't go, and waited for her to die, and waited for the map to fall into someone else's hands, and then—" he gestured, a small, broken motion that encompassed Theo, the map, the whole long road, "—then they needed only to follow the map wherever it went. With me walking behind it."

The silence stretched. Somewhere far above, muffled by stone and snow, the solstice bells began their evening count.

"You told them about the prison," Maren said flatly. "About Sera's brother. About the key."

"I told them everything. Every campsite. Every plan. I told them you'd found the key before you found it." Brannoc turned to Theo, and his eyes were wet and furious and pleading all at once. "I want you to understand—I fought it. Every time. There are gaps, whole days I can't account for, where I was walking and talking and some other will was steering. But the worst of it isn't the gaps. The worst is that I remember *choosing*. They don't take the choice away, gatekeeper. They just make the price of refusing a slow death in a ditch, and then they let you choose. Over and over. Every day, you choose again."

"That's not choosing," Theo said.

"It is. That's what makes it unforgivable."

And here was the thing—the thing Theo would carry home to Hollowmere someday, if there was a home or a someday: they believed him. Completely. Because Theo had grown up inside a compulsion too, one woven from love instead of malice, an oath of silence laid over nine generations like the silver thread around Brannoc's throat. *Keep the gate. Keep the secret. Keep everyone safe by keeping them blind.* Theo had chosen that oath every day of their life, and had never once been shown the price of refusing it, and had called that loyalty.

Loyalty and truth, tearing in opposite directions. Theo's whole life had been the space between them.

"When do they come?" Theo asked.

Brannoc blinked. "What?"

"You didn't tell us this to confess. You're not built for confession—you're built for guilt, which is different and much less useful. You told us because the sigil is failing, or because they're done with you, or because some splinter of you has been clawing toward this moment since Bellwether." Theo stood, and the lantern light threw their shadow tall across the shelves. "When do they come?"

"They're already here." Brannoc's voice cracked. "The archive was their suggestion. They *gave* me the location to feed you—it's the one room in the palace where the king's own wards can't hear what's said. They think I'm delivering you to them right now. They gave me until the third bell to walk you out the east stair, and if I don't—" He touched the scar at his throat. "I don't know what happens. I've never refused before. That's the truth, Underhill. You have all of it now, every rotten piece."

Maren was already moving, sweeping the map and the rite fragment into her coat, blowing out her pencil-light. "Third bell," she said. "How long?"

Theo listened. The bells above finished their count; the last note faded into the snow-hush. "We have perhaps half an hour. Less, if they don't trust him to walk us out and have simply been following since we entered."

"They'll be at the east stair," Brannoc said. "Both stairs. All the doors. This room has one way in and one way out, and they chose it for that reason, and I led you into it like a lamb on a rope, and I am so sorry, I am so—"

"Brannoc." Theo crossed to him and took his face in both hands, the way their grandmother used to take theirs when the nightmares came, when the wards flickered in the dark and a child needed anchoring to something solid. His skin was cold and rough as bark. "Look at me. You chose to tell us. Whatever they made you, whatever they hold over you—in this room, tonight, you chose. That's the only piece of you that matters now. Hold onto it."

He made a sound that was half laugh, half sob. "You forgive me? Just like that?"

"No," Theo said honestly. "Not just like that. Maybe not ever entirely. Forgiveness isn't a door that opens once; it's a fire that has to be fed. But you're standing in my fire right now, and I'm not putting you out of it. We survive this together or not at all—and either way, you told the truth, and the truth is worth more than the lie ever was. Even this lie. Even now."

For a moment Brannoc simply breathed, like a man surfacing from deep water. Then Maren's hand closed on Theo's shoulder, hard.

"There's no way out," she said. "Except—wait. Wait." She pulled free her masterwork, the great stolen atlas of her disgrace, and flipped it open across the black oak desk, pages whispering. Her finger traced down through the palace plans, past the throne room, past the guard barracks, down, down, to a page Theo had never seen her consult: a survey so old its ink had gone brown, labeled in a dead surveyor's hand. *Undervaults. Disused. Unmapped since the reign of Aldric II.*

"Not unmapped," Maren said softly. "*Mis*mapped. By me, forty years ago, when I was young and the crown paid me to lose things it didn't want found. There's a service tunnel off this archive's foundation—a chute for moving records to the deep stacks. I drew it as collapsed. It isn't. I checked, years later, for reasons of my own." She looked up, and there was the gambler's hope in her face, fierce and thin as a blade. "It drops into the Undervaults. Nobody who hunts us knows those tunnels—nobody living knows them but me, and the version they'd find in the royal archive shows them sealed."

"And the sigil?" Theo asked Brannoc. "If you leave this room with us—if you refuse—"

"Then we find out together what they do to a leash that slips its collar." He straightened, and something in his spine changed, some weight shifting from carried to shouldered. "I've spent months being their eyes. I'd rather spend one hour being blind on my own terms."

* * *

The record-chute was exactly where Maren's false map said it wasn't: a square of iron behind a shelf of tax ledgers, rusted shut until Maren and Brannoc levered it with the staff and a crowbar from the surveyor's kit she apparently never traveled without. Cold air breathed up out of the dark, smelling of stone and stillness and the deep bones of the mountain the palace stood on.

Theo went last. At the lip of the chute they paused and looked back at the reading alcove—at the desk where the rite fragment had lain, at the star-shaped vents letting down threads of snow, at the small circle of lantern light that had held, for one hour, the whole honest truth of five people's ruin and rescue.

Above, faint through stone, a bell began to ring. Not the third bell. Something faster. An alarm, thin and urgent, spreading through the palace like a crack through ice.

"They know," Brannoc whispered.

"Then we stop being quiet," Maren said, and dropped into the dark.

Theo swung their legs over the edge. Below, Maren's lantern was already a falling star, dwindling. Brannoc went next, staff first, and Theo took one breath of the archive's sealed, waiting air—breathed it in like a promise—and let themselves fall after.

Behind them, far above, boots were pounding down the east stair.

Ahead, the Undervaults opened their mouths, and Maren Osk, holding her own life's lies in her hands like a lamp, led them into the dark to burn them.
