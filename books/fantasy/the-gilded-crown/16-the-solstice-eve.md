## 16. The Solstice Eve

The old chapel-ward had been a place of worship once, before the gilding crept up the walls and the priests either fled or forgot what they had been worshipping. Now it was a shell of whitewash and pigeon droppings tucked behind a dyer's shop in the Tanners' Quarter, where the smell of lye and rotting hides covered every other scent in the city—including, Maren said, the faint copper tang of Vhalor's attention.

"It's dead ground," she'd told them, tapping the floorboards with her boot heel. "The dye vats poisoned the well sixty years ago. No ward-line runs through here. Even Vhalor's people don't bother looking at places that stink."

So they hid in the stink, and Theo was grateful for it. Grateful for the dark that came early through the one cracked window, grateful for the cold that kept Brannoc dozing by the brazier instead of talking, grateful above all for the hours alone with the fragment of parchment Cassian had pressed into their hands—ten lucid minutes, the king had spent them well, and Theo intended to spend every hour since unworthily or not at all.

They sat cross-legged on the floor with the parchment unrolled across their knees and a stub of tallow candle guttering beside them, and they read the binding rite for the eleventh time.

* * *

The handwriting was Cassian's own—shaky, hurried, the letters leaning like drunks against each other—but the words beneath were older. Theo could feel their age the way they could feel the age of the gatehouse stones back home: a weight in the syllables, a gravity. This was not a text anyone had composed. It was a text someone had *survived*, and written down after, hands still shaking.

*That which is bound must be held while it is loosed,* the rite began. *A binding is a knot, and a knot cannot be untied from a distance. One hand must hold the rope.*

Theo read on. The candle hissed. Somewhere above them, the solstice crowds were already gathering—the city had been filling for three days, pilgrims and merchants and the desperate poor drawn in by the promise of the king's great Renewal Ceremony, free bread, free spectacle, free blessings from a crown that ate blessings the way a hearth ate wood. A thousand wills gathered in one square, Brannoc had said, white-faced. That's what the ceremony was *for*. Not celebration. Harvest.

*The holder must be willing.* Theo's lips moved silently along the old words, translating the archaic Aldenmere script into plain speech as they went, the way they'd translated their grandmother's journal by lamplight a lifetime ago—a month ago; it felt like another life, another person wearing their skin. *Willingness is the lock's admission. An unwilling bearer strengthens the knot. Only consent opens it wide enough to cut.*

There it was again. The word that had been waiting for them since the first reading, patient as a spider.

*Consent.*

Theo read the rest anyway. They were thorough. It was the first thing their mother had ever taught them about the wards: *Be thorough, little keeper. The valley doesn't forgive half-read sigils, and neither will anything else.* So they read the passages on preparation—fasting, silence, the tracing of anchor-sigils on the bearer's skin so the burden would have somewhere to sit besides the heart—and the passages on the cutting, which required two hands besides the bearer's, both of them steady, both of them knowing exactly where to sever. And then they read the last passage, the one they always read last because it was shortest, and shortest things cut deepest.

*No bearer has yet set down the crown and risen unchanged. Let none undertake this who fears to remain.*

*Let none undertake this who fears to remain.*

Eleven readings, and the sentence had not softened. Theo closed their eyes and let the words sit in them like a stone dropped in a well, falling and falling without reaching bottom.

Likely dies, then. Not certainly. *Not yet risen unchanged* left room—left a crack, the width of a hair, the kind of crack Theo had spent their whole life watching for in the valley wards. But the histories Brannoc had dredged up agreed: the rite was performed twice before, in the reigns of Aldric the Second and Maravel the First, when earlier vessels had grown too strong for their bindings. Both times, the willing bearer had held the knot open. Both times, the entity had been cut loose and dispersed. And both times—

"Both times the bearer didn't walk out," Theo murmured to the dark. Saying it aloud made it no more real. They said it aloud anyway. Their grandmother had taught them that too: *Name the thing you fear, and it loses the right to sneak up on you.*

Across the room, Brannoc stirred in his sleep and muttered something that might have been a name or an apology. He talked in his sleep now, more than he used to. Guilt did that, Theo was learning. It leaked out of a person however it could.

* * *

Maren came back an hour past midnight smelling of dye-works and snow, shaking slush off her cloak in the doorway with the exaggerated care of a woman who had spent thirty years making sure she left no tracks anywhere. She carried bread, a twist of salt, and news.

"They've moved the ceremony up." She crouched by the brazier, feeding it a knot of kindling, and the firelight turned her grey-streaked hair to tarnished silver. "Dawn tomorrow. Not dusk. I heard the criers myself—*His Majesty desires to greet the new sun bound anew*, all very pious. Which means he knows we're close, or he suspects, and he wants the harvest done before anyone can spoil the field."

"Dawn." Theo looked down at the parchment. "That's—"

"Four hours, if the bells are honest. They're never honest anymore, but call it four." Maren's eyes flicked to the rite, then away, with the practiced courtesy of someone who had decided weeks ago not to ask what Theo read every night. "Brannoc thinks he can get us into the undercroft below the ceremony square through the old census tunnels. My maps. There's a passage behind the tax assessor's vault that—" She stopped. Looked at Theo properly. "You haven't slept."

"I slept."

"You're sitting in exactly the position you were sitting in when I left, and the candle's burned down to a thumb-width. You've been reading all night." She said it without accusation, which was worse. Maren Osk could gut a man with indifference better than most could with knives. "Whatever the king gave you, it doesn't get less terrible the eleventh time you read it. Believe me. I've redrawn my own disgrace enough times to know."

Theo almost laughed. Almost told her. The words climbed up their throat—*someone has to wear it, Maren, someone has to hold the knot while you cut it, and the rite says no one comes back unchanged, and I've been doing the arithmetic for eleven readings and there's only one answer that works*—and then climbed back down again, because Brannoc was asleep six feet away, and sleeping men woke at the wrong moments, and compelled men woke at worse ones.

"I'll sleep after," Theo said. "After tomorrow."

Maren studied them for a long moment. Snowmelt dripped off her cloak onto the boards, each drop loud as a footstep. "You know," she said finally, "when I met you, you lied to me about who you were, badly, and I robbed you anyway, and you still wouldn't tell me the truth until it was useful. You've gotten worse at lying since Hollowmere. Or I've gotten better at you." She tossed the bread into Theo's lap. "Eat. Then sleep, or pretend to. Dawn comes whether we're ready or not—it always does. That's the one honest thing about dawns."

She rolled herself into her cloak by the brazier and was asleep within minutes, the way soldiers and cartographers sleep, instantly and lightly, one ear always listening.

Theo ate the bread. It tasted of nothing. Everything had tasted of nothing much lately, except the two minutes after they'd touched Sera's map and felt her bargain humming in its fibers—that had tasted of iron and grief, and they'd been chasing the flavor ever since without admitting it.

Then they took out the knife, and the scrap of good paper Maren had given them for copying map margins, and began to write.

* * *

*Mother.*

The word sat alone on the page for a long time. Outside, the city murmured even at this hour—solstice eve kept everyone awake, some with joy and some with dread, and Theo could no longer tell which murmur was which. In Hollowmere, night had a sound too: wind in the beacon-fire, the creak of the gate chains, the particular hush of a valley holding its breath around a secret. This was nothing like that. This was a thousand small fears pretending to be song.

*I found Grandmother's journal. You probably knew it existed. You probably knew what it said, or guessed, and let me grow up sweeping the same floor she swept, feeding the same fire, telling the same lie to the neighbors about why no one leaves. Maybe you didn't know. Maybe the oath eats memory as well as truth. I'd like to believe you didn't know. I find that I need to believe it, so I'm going to, the way you believed in the wards your whole life: because the alternative is a house with no floor.*

Theo stopped. Read it back. It was crueler than they meant to be—or truer than they meant to be, which amounted to the same thing on paper. They kept writing anyway. Letters home, they suspected, were allowed to be true in ways faces weren't.

*The king is not a monster. That's the thing no one says. He's a man with something wearing him, and he spent his ten clear minutes—not asking us to save him, Mother, begging us NOT to save him if saving meant freeing it—and then he handed us the knife anyway, because hope is stupid and kings are men and men are stupid with hope. I liked him. I liked him in ten minutes. I think I would have liked him for years.*

*Here is the part you'll hate. Here is the part I hate.*

*The rite needs a bearer. Willing. That's not poetry; it's mechanics, the way the gate is mechanics. Something has to hold the knot open from inside while the others cut. Everyone who's done it before stayed holding. I've read it eleven times and the words don't change and I don't change them, because I'm an Underhill and we don't flinch from what's written, we just sweep the floor and feed the fire and keep the round.*

*But I want you to know—I need someone to know, even if this never reaches you—that I'm not doing it because of the oath. The oath was a lie built on top of a lie, and I'm done paying rent to it. I'm doing it because Sera died in my arms saying* unbind him before solstice *and chose ME, out of everyone alive, to carry that. Because Maren burned her life's work to buy us an hour and called it a fair trade. Because Brannoc walked back into the light for people who owed him nothing but suspicion. Because Cassian spends his fragments of himself buying strangers seconds. Everyone I've met out here gives pieces of themselves away like it's nothing, like breath, and I have been hoarding myself my whole life behind a gate, keeping the round, keeping silent, keeping—*

*Keeping. That's all the oath ever was. Keeping. And I am so tired of being a door that only shuts.*

*If I come back, I'll bring winter apples from Bellwether and Maren's terrible jokes and a story you won't believe. If I don't—leave the gate open. Not broken. Open. Ward it by choice, not by fear. Light the beacon for travelers instead of warnings. Let someone else keep the round, and make sure they know why, and make sure they're allowed to say why out loud.*

*Tell them I was thorough.*

Theo read the letter through twice. The candle had nearly drowned in its own wax. Their eyes were dry, which surprised them—they'd expected tears, had braced for them the way one braces for the wards' scream—but what came instead was quieter and stranger: a stillness, deep and level, like the moment after the beacon-fire catches and before the flame stands up.

It wasn't peace, exactly. It was resolve wearing peace's clothes. But it would do. It would have to do.

They folded the letter along the lines Maren used for her charts—thrice, against weather—and slid it inside their coat, over their heart, where the anchor-sigils would go in a few hours. Not sent. Not yet. Perhaps not ever. Some letters were less for delivering than for carrying; some weights you agreed to bear precisely so you could write the letter at all.

* * *

"You're going to do it."

Theo hadn't heard Brannoc wake. The hedge-witch stood at the edge of the brazier-light, wrapped in his patched robe, hollow-eyed, and there was something in his face Theo had learned to dread over these weeks: the look of a man watching a debt come due.

"The rite," Brannoc said. His voice was rough with sleep and something older. "I served the coronation, boy. I know how these texts are built. I watched you read it night after night like a man counting coins he's afraid to spend, and tonight you wrote a letter instead of sleeping, and nobody writes letters on the eve of a battle unless—" He stopped. Pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "Tell me I'm wrong."

The honest answer rose easily. Theo had gotten better at honesty; it was the one skill this road had granted freely. But they looked at Brannoc—at the guilt that had dragged him across half a kingdom, at the compulsion coiled in him like a wire in wool, at the way his hands shook unless he was mending something—and they thought: *if I tell you, you'll try to take it from me. And if you try to take it from me, you'll break, because you've been breaking for months already, and the knot needs a bearer who isn't broken.*

So Theo did the last dishonest thing they would ever do, and made it count.

"You're wrong," they said quietly. "I wrote to my mother. That's all. Go back to sleep, Brannoc. We march at first bell."

He searched their face for a long moment, firelight flickering in the hollows of him. Then, slowly, he nodded and folded himself back down by the coals—and Theo sat alone in the dark with the rite on their knees and the letter over their heart, and outside, far across the sleeping, gilded city, the first bell of the solstice dawn began to ring.

Four hours. Theo folded the parchment with steady hands, and stood, and went to wake Maren.

The bells rang on, and somewhere beneath their bronze voices, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, something vast and hungry turned toward the sound of its own renewal—and smiled.
