## 20. Share the Weight

The binding was a country, and Theo was drowning in it.

That was the only way they could think of it afterward, when words came back to them at all. Inside the crown's death-throes there was geography: vast plains of cold, mountain-ranges of pressure, rivers of something that was not fire but burned the way fire wished it could. Vhalor had been unmade—Maren and Cassian had done it, the rite sung true, the circlet cracked like an egg against the edge of a bowl—but the binding itself, the great woven thing that had held him for a century, did not know how to end. It had been built by people who believed that burdens should be carried alone. It had been built, Theo understood now with a clarity that felt like a knife turning over in their chest, exactly the way Hollowmere had been built.

And it would not release a willing bearer.

Theo hung in the center of it, arms spread as if still holding up the sky, and felt the whole weight of a dead god's prison settle onto their shoulders like a mantle fitted to them across nine generations. It knew them. That was the horror of it—the binding recognized the shape of their soul, the gatekeeper's discipline worn smooth into the bones, the long habit of *I will carry this and I will not complain and I will not tell anyone*. It fit them the way their grandmother's coat had fit them. It fit them like inheritance.

*Let go*, Maren was screaming somewhere far above. Theo could not see her. Could not see anything except the slow unfolding of Vhalor's last dream, playing out around them in colors that had no names: a throne room of black glass, a coronation that never ended, a king kneeling forever before a crown that drank him.

*You have to let go.*

*I can't,* Theo tried to say. The words didn't travel. Nothing traveled here. They were alone in the way no Underhill had ever been alone—not behind a ward-line with neighbors a mile off, but truly, absolutely alone, sealed inside the last act of a dying spell, holding up a weight that had eaten a king for twenty years and would eat them in twenty minutes.

*This is what we were for,* Theo thought, and was surprised to find no bitterness in it. Only a kind of terrible homecoming. Nine generations of Underhills had kept the gate so that this moment, somewhere, sometime, would find someone ready. Their grandmother had known. The journal had said it plainly, once Theo learned how to read it: *The valley is the practice ground. The child who keeps the small door will one day keep the large one.* Theo had thought it meant wards and beacon-fires and lying kindly to neighbors about why the roads were closed.

It meant this. It had always meant this.

So they held on. They held on the way they had fed the beacon-fire every dawn of their life: without drama, without hope of thanks, because the work was the work. The binding pressed down and Theo bent and did not break, and somewhere outside their body Maren was shouting and Cassian—who should have been free, who had spent twenty years drowning and deserved to breathe—was refusing to leave. Theo felt them both the way you feel weather through a wall. Distant. Real. Unreachable.

Then, in the deep cold at the center of everything, Theo heard Sera's voice.

* * *

Not a ghost. Theo knew ghosts; the valley had its share, thin things that drifted along the ward-lines complaining about the weather. This was older than a ghost and smaller than one—a residue, a stain of intention left in the weave, the way a letter leaves an impression on the page beneath it.

Sera Vane had touched this binding. Had *written herself into it*. Theo realized, with the vertigo of understanding arriving too late to save anyone, that the map she had died delivering had never been just ink and sigils. It was a working. She had bargained with Vhalor's agents for her brother's life, and the bargain had a price, and Sera—practical, stubborn, dying Sera, who swallowed a key rather than surrender it and bled out against a stranger's ward-stone rather than stop walking—had paid the price in the only coin she had left.

She had put herself into the map. Into the plan. Into the very binding she'd helped deliver the means to destroy.

*Underhill,* her voice said, and it was not an echo. It was her—dry, wry, impatient, the voice of a woman who had crawled half a kingdom on a wound that should have killed her three days sooner. *You're doing the thing. The noble stupid thing. I can hear it from here.*

*Sera.* Theo's thoughts cracked open like ice on a spring river. *You're dead.*

*Observant. No wonder they made you a keeper.* A pause, and when she spoke again the dryness had thinned, and underneath it Theo heard the thing she had never said aloud on any of the nights Theo told her story to Brannoc on the road: fear, and love, and the specific loneliness of someone carrying a secret toward people she would never meet. *Listen to me. You can't let go because the binding won't accept unwilling release—it'll shatter and take the city's ward-lines with it, half the kingdom's protections gone in midwinter. And you can't keep holding it because you're one person, and one person is exactly how much a burden like this eats. That's the design. That was always the design. One bearer, alone, devoured slowly. Your family kept a valley; my king kept a crown; same architecture, same lie.*

*What do I do?*

*Ask.*

The word landed in Theo like a stone dropped down a well, falling and falling without reaching bottom.

*No one ever asks,* Sera said. *That's the flaw in the whole craft. Warders, binders, kings—they build these things assuming the bearer will be proud, or ashamed, or too frightened to reach out. Nine generations of Underhills and not one of you ever asked for help, did you? Not once. That's why your grandmother's journal was hidden. That's why the gate stayed shut. The binding holds because solitude holds it. So break the solitude.*

*I don't know how,* Theo said, and it was the truest thing they had ever thought. *I've never—I don't—Sera, I left the valley four weeks ago. Before that I'd never spoken to more than six people in a day. I don't know how to need someone.*

*I know,* she said, and her voice went soft in a way Theo had only heard once, at the threshold, with blood on her lips and one word left in her. *I watched you drag a dying stranger inside when protocol said leave her. You already know how. You've known since the night we met. You just keep calling it breaking the rules instead of what it is.*

*Which is?*

*Opening the gate.*

* * *

Outside, in the ruined coronation hall, Maren Osk saw the body on the dais shudder.

She had seen many terrible things in her years as royal cartographer—most of them, lately, of her own drafting—and none of them had stopped her breath the way this did. Theo lay where they had fallen when they took the crown, curled on their side among the shattered gold, and the light coming off them was wrong: not the sick gilding of Vhalor's influence but a deep, patient silver, the color of ward-stones at dusk, the color of Hollowmere's beacon-fire as Maren had imagined it from Theo's descriptions on a hundred cold nights on the road.

They were holding it. Alone. The binding, unanchored from its dead god, had found the next willing bearer and begun the old work of consumption, and Maren—cynic, thief, gambler, the woman who had once sold a kingdom's secrets to buy back her own name—discovered that she could not stand by and watch it happen.

"Theo." She knelt, heedless of the broken glass and the drifting ash of the unmade crown. "Theo, listen to me. The rite is finished. Vhalor is dispersed. Whatever you're holding, you can put it down."

Theo's eyes opened. They were silver to the rims, and behind the silver Maren saw something that made her chest ache: her friend, looking out at her from a very long way away, through a window that was closing.

*"Can't,"* Theo said. The voice came out layered, doubled, a human voice with a vast cold harmony beneath it. *"Won't release. Willing bearer. Has to be—"*

"I know," Maren said. "Believe me, I read the same archives you did. There has to be another way."

"There isn't." That was Cassian, and Maren turned to see the king standing at the base of the dais, swaying, hollowed out, twenty years thinner than his portraits. He looked like a man who had walked out of a burning house and kept walking. "There isn't another way. I lived inside that binding for two decades, Mistress Osk. I know its grammar better than I know my own name. It takes one bearer. It consumes one bearer. That is its entire architecture." His voice broke, and he steadied it with visible effort. "I should know. I was the architecture's proof."

"Then we take turns," Maren said wildly. "We pull them out and someone else—"

"And feed it the next person? No." Cassian climbed the dais steps, one at a time, like an old man climbing a hill he had resigned himself to die on. He knelt on Theo's other side. "But I have spent twenty years thinking about this binding from the inside, and there is one thing I learned that the binders never wrote down, because they were ashamed of it." He looked at Maren, and his eyes were wet and furious and clear. "It is *lonely*. All the way down, at the root of the working, under every layer of force and law—it is built on loneliness. On the assumption that the bearer has no one. Every seal I ever felt was, at bottom, a wall between me and everyone I loved. If that assumption fails—"

"If the bearer isn't alone," Maren whispered.

"Then the binding doesn't know what shape it's in." Cassian set his hands on Theo's shoulders. "I am going to take hold of one side of this. Whatever comes, don't let go of me, and don't let go of them."

"What are you—Your Majesty, you've barely been free for an hour—"

"Cassian," he said. "My name is Cassian, and I have been alone inside a cage for twenty years, and I will be damned—again, further, permanently—if I watch this child carry what I carried." He closed his eyes. "Now. Take the other side."

Maren took it. She took Theo's hand, the one nearest her, laced her fingers through theirs—the fingers of a gatekeeper, calloused from ward-work and beacon-tending and nine generations of solitary duty—and braced herself.

Inside the binding, Theo felt hands close on the weight.

* * *

It did not feel the way they expected. They had expected relief, and there was some of that, sharp and shocking as cold water—but mostly it felt like being *seen*. The binding convulsed around them, its whole architecture thrown into confusion, because for the first time in its existence the burden it was applying had arrived distributed. Three shoulders. Three sets of hands. The great solitary machine of the spell strained against a mathematics it had never been taught.

And in the center of the confusion, Sera laughed.

*There it is,* she said. *Oh, look at that. Look at it flounder. A hundred years of craft and nobody ever thought to just—hold it together.*

*Sera—*

*I'm fading, Underhill. Bargains don't linger. But listen, because this is the last thing I'll ever get to tell you, and I've been saving it since the ward-stones.* Her voice was going thin, unraveling into the weave even as she spoke, and Theo clung to it the way they had once clung to the map. *You asked me once—you didn't know you were asking, but you asked—why I chose your valley. Why I crawled to your gate out of all the gates in the world. It wasn't chance. The map wanted a keeper, and I wanted... I wanted to be let in. Just once. By someone whose whole life was keeping people out.* A breath that was not a breath. *You let me in. Best thing anyone ever did for me. Now let them in too. Let them all the way in. That's the whole secret. That's the thing your family forgot and mine never learned: the weight was never meant to be carried by one. It was meant to be carried by us.*

*Sera—wait—*

*Tell me a story sometime,* she said, and her voice was almost gone, smiling at the edges. *Tell them the one about the courier who died at the gate of the only honest person in Aldenmere. Make me brave in it. Make me funny. You always made me funnier than I was.*

And then she was weave, and warmth, and gone—dissolved into the binding's fabric, and Theo felt her pass through them and out, a final gift moving along the lines like light along water, strengthening every thread she touched.

Theo opened their mouth and did the thing no Underhill had done in nine generations.

"Help me," they said aloud. "Please. I can't hold this alone. Help me."

* * *

Cassian took the north side of the burden, and it was like watching a man pick up a familiar instrument after years away—terrible familiarity, grief and skill in the same motion. He knew this weight. He had been married to it for twenty years. He took its measure in a breath and set his shoulder under it, and the binding groaned.

Maren took the south side, and she had no skill at all, only stubbornness, which turned out to be its own kind of craft. She had redrawn coastlines that didn't exist and mountains that lied for a living; she knew precisely how to hold a shape steady while the truth caught up to it. She held the binding's shape steady. She refused, with the full fury of her considerable will, to let it collapse into the form it wanted—solitary, devouring, one bearer alone—and forced it instead toward a new geometry, three-cornered, shared, *bearable*.

And Theo, in the middle, felt the weight change.

It did not lighten—that was the miracle's honest cost, and Theo would spend the rest of their life explaining this to people who wanted the story simpler. The weight did not lessen. It remained the full, crushing, ancestral mass of a bound god's prison, all of it, every ounce. But it *moved*. It shifted between them like a load passed hand to hand along a line of laborers, like a bucket passed up a chain, like a child handed over a wall. When it grew unbearable in Theo's arms, it flowed to Cassian, who knew its rhythms; when it grew unbearable in his, it flowed to Maren, who refused on principle to be crushed by anything; and when it came back around to Theo it came back *changed*, worn a little smoother each circuit, the way stones are worn by shared water.

Three ways. The binding, shared three ways, became bearable enough to walk out of.

They walked out of it together. There was no other word for it, though later the chroniclers would write about the silver light rising from the dais like a tide going out, and the sound—three voices, one of them a king's, one of them a cartographer's, one of them a gatekeeper's—speaking in unison a word that no record managed to transcribe. The binding unwove itself thread by thread, and each thread, before it dissolved, passed through all three of them, acknowledged, witnessed, *shared*, and then let go. The walls between Theo and the world came down gently, like snow sliding from a branch. The last of Vhalor's prison dispersed into the ward-lines of the city, and the city's wards drank it and brightened, and everywhere in Aldenmere, on that solstice night, old protections woke up stronger than they had been in a century—fed at last by something other than a consumed king.

Theo opened their eyes on the floor of the coronation hall, with Cassian's hand on one shoulder and Maren's grip still locked around theirs, and the first thing they saw was the ceiling, vast and dark and ordinary, and the second thing was Maren crying and pretending she wasn't, and the third thing was nothing at all, because they were laughing—laughing so hard it hurt, the helpless laughter of someone who has set down a weight they expected to die under.

"You absolute idiot," Maren said, her voice wrecked. "You were going to do that alone. You wrote a letter. Brannoc told us. You were going to *die* and leave us a *letter*."

"It was a good letter," Theo rasped.

"It was going to be insufferable. Full of duty and gratitude and 'don't blame yourselves.'" She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, smearing ash across her cheek. "I'm going to read it aloud at your funeral someday when you actually die of old age, just to embarrass you."

"Promise?" Theo said.

Above them, Cassian sat down heavily on the steps of his own dais—the first time in twenty years he had sat anywhere as a free man—and began, quietly, to weep. Neither of them moved to comfort him. Some weights, newly set down, need a moment before they can be witnessed. But Theo reached out and took his hand, the way Maren had taken theirs, and the king gripped back like a drowning man gripping a rope, and the three of them stayed that way as the solstice bells began to ring across the city—out of rhythm, ragged, joyful, the sound of a kingdom that did not yet know what had been done for it.

Later—much later, when the hall had been found by guards, when the story had begun its long distortion into legend—someone would ask Theo how they had survived the crown. And Theo, who had spent a lifetime learning silence, would give the only answer that had ever been true:

"We didn't survive it. We survived *each other's* surviving it. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference, and I wish to God someone had written it down nine generations ago."

But that was later. That night, there was only the floor, and the bells, and Maren's hand, and Cassian's, and somewhere in the weave of the world, faint as heat off a stone at dusk, the smell of a courier's road dust and the ghost of a laugh, satisfied, already halfway to legend:

*Told you, Underhill. Open the gate.*

Theo smiled at the ceiling of the gilded hall, and for the first time since a dying woman had pressed a bloodstained map into their hands, the weight on their shoulders weighed nothing at all.

* * *

Dawn found them in the chapel-ward, the old sanctuary where Theo had studied the rite and written the unsent letter. Maren slept sitting up against the altar, ink-stained fingers still curled around a stub of charcoal. Cassian slept properly for the first time in twenty years, flat on the chapel floor with his cloak folded under his head like a soldier, and did not stir even when the bells rang matins.

Theo sat by the window with the letter in their lap. They had not sent it. They had not needed to. But they read it once more, all the same—their own handwriting, steady, addressed to no one and to everyone, beginning *If you are reading this, I did not come home*—and then, deliberately, in front of the waking light, they folded it into a small square and tucked it into the lining of their coat, over the heart, where a weight belonged.

Not a farewell anymore. A reminder.

Outside, the city was waking to a solstice morning that smelled of snow and woodsmoke and, very faintly, of something clean and green that had no business in the capital in midwinter—as if the ward-lines themselves, newly fed, were pushing up through the stone like the first shoots of a thaw. Somewhere beyond the mountains, past Bellwether and the ford and the long gray road, a valley lay under morning mist with its gate shut and its beacon cold, waiting for its keeper to come home.

Theo stood, shrugged their coat over their shoulders, and went to wake their friends.

There was a gate to open.
