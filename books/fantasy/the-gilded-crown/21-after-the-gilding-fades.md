## 21. After the Gilding Fades

The maps were the first thing to go wrong.

Maren Osk had drawn the kingdom of Aldenmere four times in her life—once as an apprentice copying her master's plates, twice for the Royal Survey under a crown's seal, and once, in disgrace, from memory in a rented room above a fishmonger in Bellwether, because a cartographer who cannot stop mapping is like a river that cannot stop running. She had prided herself on accuracy. Every ford measured at low water and high. Every mountain pass walked or interviewed out of some half-starved shepherd. Every ward-stone marked with the little crowned circle that meant *royal protection extends here*.

She had believed all of it. That was the humiliation that outlasted the disgrace: not that she'd been caught falsifying a border, but that she had spent forty years drawing a lie so complete she'd never once suspected it was one.

Now she sat in a workroom in the palace of Aldenmere—the same palace whose gilded gates had made her stomach turn the first time she'd seen them through Theo's eyes—and redrew the kingdom a fifth time. This one had no crown on it anywhere.

* * *

The workroom had been Cassian's idea, though he'd delivered it with the self-deprecating shrug of a man still learning which of his impulses were his own. "You spent your career making the kingdom look the way we wanted it to look," he'd said, standing in the doorway with ink already on his fingers—he was always inked now, ruling his own decrees instead of signing whatever Vhalor slid beneath his hand. "Spend the next one making it look the way it is. If you can bear it."

She could not bear it. She bore it anyway. That seemed to be the whole of what survival meant these days.

Outside the tall windows, the city went about its mending. Solstice had come and gone three weeks past, and Aldenmere City wore its scars the way a soldier wears a healed wound—with a certain grim vanity. The ceremonial square where Vhalor's renewal rite had collapsed into chaos still bore scorch-marks fanning out from the dais; no one had scrubbed them, and children had taken to tracing them with chalk, inventing games about the king who fell down in front of everyone. The great gilding that had crept across the city like lichen during Vhalor's reign—the gold leaf on every lintel, every shrine, every beggar's cup—was flaking now. Workers went up on scaffolds with vinegar and scrapers, and underneath the gold the old paint emerged in colors Maren had forgotten the city owned: a deep honest blue, an oxblood red, the green of copper left to weather. The whole capital looked like a face after a mask is peeled away. Raw. Blotchy. Real.

She dipped her pen and began the coast.

Here was the first wound in the old map: the Sea Ward. On every royal plate, the southern coast ran smooth and guarded, dotted with the crowned circles of protective wards. In truth—as Brannoc had confirmed, gray-faced, in the weeks after the unbinding—there had been no wards on the southern coast in two hundred years. There had been *feeders*. Thin places where the crown's entity had sipped at the land's own magic the way a leech feeds at the ankle, leaving the villages there gray and luckless and wondering why their children were born quiet and their crops came in thin. Maren drew the true coastline, and where the crowned circles had been she set a new mark of her own devising: a small open ring, unfinished, like a door left ajar. *Ward-line, broken. To be rebuilt.*

There were ninety-one of them along the south coast alone.

Her hand shook. She set the pen down and pressed her palm flat against the vellum until the trembling stilled, and thought—not for the first time, but with undiminished force—of Sera Vane.

* * *

They had buried nothing of Sera. Her body had gone into the river at the ford, in the country of her pursuers, and the letter Brannoc had found—the letter that named her brother, named the bargain, named the price—was all that remained of her except the map itself, which Maren kept in a flat cedar case under her bed and did not open more than once a week, and then only when the workroom was empty.

Because Sera's blood was still on it. Dried now to a brown so dark it was nearly black, a smear across the northern ranges where she'd clutched it while dying, and a single perfect print near the western edge where her thumb had pressed down—whether to steady herself or to say *here*, Maren would never know. The conservators at the archive had offered, delicately, to clean it. Maren had told them, less delicately, exactly where they could put their cleaning solvents.

A map should carry the cost of its making. That had become something like a creed for her, in the weeks since. All her life she had believed a map was a window—clean glass, nothing of the maker in it. Now she knew better. A map was a confession. Every line said *I chose to include this*. Every omission said *I chose to hide it*. And the crown had understood that better than any cartographer alive: you don't need to burn the maps if you can get the mapmakers to draw the world smaller than it is.

So: the new atlas. Commissioned by royal decree, authored by Maren Osk, restored to her name and her post and then immediately promoted past both into something with no precedent—a Warden-Chartist, first of an order that didn't exist yet and was being invented around her whether she liked it or not.

She didn't entirely like it. She liked even less how much she loved it.

* * *

Cassian came at midday, as he always did, without ceremony—which was itself a ceremony, she'd come to understand, the deliberate practice of a king walking into a workroom unannounced because for months his body had done nothing but announce things.

He looked better. That was the strange part, the part the ballads already getting sung in the taverns refused to touch: the king looked *worse* and *better* at once, thinner, grayer at the temples where he was too young for gray, with a tremor in his left hand that the healers said might never fully leave him. But his eyes were his own. Maren had met Vhalor-wearing-Cassian exactly once, across the width of a throne room, and she had learned what a stolen face looked like—how the expression arrived a half-beat late, how the smile sat on the mouth like a hat set on a peg. The man who came into her workroom now smiled crookedly and too soon and sometimes not at all when smiling would have been politic, and every failure of his face was a small victory.

"Progress?" He nodded at the vellum.

"Ninety-one broken wards on the south coast." She turned the sheet toward him. "Forty-three on the east. And I haven't started on the interior, because the interior—" She stopped. Made herself finish. "The interior is worse. The Hollowmere gate wasn't the last seal, it turns out. It was the last *honest* seal. Everything else has been quietly rotting since before my grandmother held a stylus."

Cassian studied the map the way he studied everything now—thoroughly, hungrily, as a man relearning ownership of his own eyes. "And the order? How many have sworn?"

"Eleven. Four of them former crown hunters." She watched his face for the flinch and saw it, controlled but present. Good. She wanted him to flinch. A king who could not look at what had been done in his name—or with his hands—would make the same cage again with different bars. "Brannoc is training them. He says guilt makes excellent curriculum."

"He would know."

"He would." She softened it with a shrug. "He also says they're learning faster than any class he's taught, because they've all seen what happens when wards are built on lies. Fear is a fine whetstone."

"And the fifth founder?" Cassian asked lightly, too lightly. "Has our gatekeeper given an answer?"

Maren looked out the window, at the scaffolds and the scraping and the slow revelation of blue paint, and gave him the truth, because lying to this king felt like a sin against the dead. "Theo hasn't answered anything. Theo went home. Or toward home—I had a letter from Bellwether ten days ago, no signature, just a sketch of the valley gate on the back of a toll receipt and one line underneath." She recited it from memory, because she had read it perhaps two hundred times. *'The wards hold. So do I. Ask me again in spring.'*

Cassian laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him, and he pressed his trembling hand over his mouth as if to keep it in, and failed, and let it run. "Ask me again in spring," he repeated. "That's the most Underhill sentence ever written. Nine generations of silence and they've learned to negotiate in a single season."

"They learned more than negotiating," Maren said. "They learned to ask for help. Do you understand what that cost? I've watched proud people break before. I've *been* proud people breaking. But Theo—" She shook her head. "You took hold of one side of that thing. I took the other. And between us we couldn't have lifted it alone, and Theo knew it, and asked anyway, in front of witnesses, having been raised from the cradle to believe that asking was the one unforgivable weakness. Whatever the new order becomes, it should be built on that. Not on walls. On the willingness to say *I cannot carry this alone*."

The room was quiet. Somewhere below, a scraper worked at the palace's own facade, and flakes of gold drifted down past the window like the world's most expensive snow.

"I'm going to put that in the founding charter," Cassian said at last. "Nearly word for word. You'll be cited."

"I'll deny everything. It's a habit."

* * *

That evening she worked late, and the work turned inward, as it always did at the day's end, toward the page she had been avoiding for three weeks: the map of Hollowmere.

It was the smallest sheet in the atlas and the hardest. Not because the valley was complex—it was a bowl of green with a river through it and a gatehouse at the throat, a child could memorize it. It was hard because every other sheet in the atlas was an act of correction, and this one was an act of *surrender*. For nine generations the valley had appeared on no map at all. The crown's surveyors had been turned back at the border with polite, immovable courtesy; the blank space on the royal plates had been filled with a decorative cartouche of oak leaves, as if the kingdom simply grew a knot of foliage there. Maren herself, in her second commission, had drawn that cartouche. She remembered the exact curve of the oak leaves. She had thought it charming.

Now she drew the valley true. The gatehouse. The beacon-fire's position, marked with a flame sigil. The ward-lines radiating from the gate like the roots of an inverted tree—and here her pen hesitated, because the old understanding of those lines was dead and the new understanding was still soft as a callus. They weren't walls anymore. Theo had left them standing open. *Warded by choice and company rather than lies*—that was how Theo had put it, in the one long letter that had come before the terse ones, written in a hand that shook for the first paragraph and steadied after, as if honesty were a muscle warming up.

So Maren invented a new symbol. An open gate: two posts, no bar. And beside it, in the margin, in her own small precise hand, she wrote the note that would travel with the atlas into every school and survey office and ship's cabin in the kingdom:

*Hollowmere. Open by the keeper's will. Travelers welcome. Truth told freely within.*

She sat back and looked at it and found her eyes were wet, which annoyed her profoundly. Forty years a professional; weeping over marginalia.

But it wasn't the marginalia. It was the arithmetic underneath it. Ninety-one broken wards on the south coast. Forty-three on the east. Whole regions of the kingdom where the luck had been leeched out of the soil for centuries and the people had blamed themselves—blamed their gods, their bloodlines, their bad choices—for a poverty that had been *administered*. The world was worse than she had ever drawn it. Worse than she had ever imagined, and she had imagined professionally for four decades. Every honest line she laid down revealed another dishonesty behind it, like peeling the gold off the city and finding not one layer of old paint but seven.

And yet.

She lit the lamp against the dusk and looked at the growing stack of true maps—at the coasts with their open rings waiting to be closed, at the interior with its rotting lines flagged for the new wardens, at the little valley with its open gate—and felt the thing she had decided to trust even though it arrived most days looking suspiciously like exhaustion: the world was worse than they'd thought, and worth it anyway. Worth the scraping and the charter-writing and the eleven nervous recruits. Worth the king practicing his own signature in the margins of state papers like a schoolboy. Worth a dead courier's bloodstain preserved under glass. Worth a gate standing open in the cold.

Hope, she was learning, was not the belief that the damage was small. It was the decision that the damage was addressable. She had never once believed that, in forty years of drawing a beautiful lie. Now she believed it over a truthful ruin, and the difference felt like being able to breathe.

* * *

She was rolling the Hollowmere sheet when the knock came—two taps, hesitant, the rhythm of someone who had been told the Warden-Chartist worked late and hadn't quite believed they'd be welcome.

"Enter."

One of the new wardens, a former hunter named Dell with a burn-scarred jaw and the careful manner of a man relearning gentleness. He stood at attention, then visibly decided against it and just stood.

"Beg pardon, Warden. Courier came up from the south road. Bellwether direction." He held out a folded paper, sealed with plain wax. "For you. But—" he shifted, uncomfortable, "—it's marked for the whole founding company. Says it's to be read together. And there's a second enclosure." He produced a small object wrapped in linen. "The courier said the sender was specific about it. Said you'd know why."

Maren broke the seal. One page, unsigned, in a handwriting she knew—she had seen it on a toll receipt, on a long shaking letter, on the flyleaf of a grandmother's journal reproduced in careful copy. Two lines only:

*The beacon is lit. Come see what we've built. Bring the atlas when it's ready—there's a gap in the map where I live, and I want to walk you through it myself.*

*Also: ask me again in spring. It's nearly spring.*

Her hands, very steady, unwrapped the linen.

Inside lay a key. Old iron, gatekeeper-made, worn bright at the bow by generations of the same grip—and threaded through the bow, tied in a careful knot, a strand of hair the color of river-reed and a scrap of cloth she recognized with a jolt that went through her like cold water: courier's blue, dyed deep, the same weave as the torn cloak they had buried symbolically at the solstice memorial.

Not Theo's doing. Someone else had sent this, through Theo's hands or alongside them. Someone who had been to the valley. Someone carrying a piece of Sera Vane home at last.

Dell cleared his throat. "Is it trouble, Warden?"

Maren looked at the key, at the blue thread, at the two lines in a gatekeeper's hand, and felt the tired hope in her chest tip forward into motion, the way a cart tips when you finally lean your weight against it.

"No," she said, reaching for her coat. "It's an invitation. Wake the others. We ride at dawn."
