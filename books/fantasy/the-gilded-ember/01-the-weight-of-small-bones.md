## 1. The Weight of Small Bones

The market of Vell woke the way it always did: in increments, like a body remembering itself. First the fishmongers, hauling their brine-slick crates down from the canal barges while gulls screamed their grievances overhead. Then the bakers, whose ovens had been burning since the third bell, sending up a smell that made even the beggars feel briefly rich. By the time the sun cleared the rooftops of the Tanner's Quarter, the whole of Copperlane Market was a living thing—haggling, cursing, laughing, sweating, alive.

Odile Brightwater loved it and feared it in equal measure.

He had held the same stall for twelve years, wedged between a candle-dipper who never spoke to him and a fruit-seller who spoke too much, and in all that time he had sold nothing but harmless things. Love charms carved from goose bone, so slight they trembled if you breathed on them. Knucklebones for gamblers, polished smooth as river stones. Little protective tokens for children's wrists—a rabbit, a wren, a fish—that meant nothing at all and were priced accordingly.

That was the point. That was the whole architecture of his survival: he sold *nothing*, and so he was *no one*, and no one was exactly what a man wanted to be in Vell, where magic had been outlawed for a generation and the Inquisition's gray cloaks moved through the crowds like herons through shallow water, patient and hungry.

What Odile actually did, he did at night, alone, with the shutters barred and a single tallow candle burned down to a thumb's width.

Because Odile could hear the dead.

It was not a dramatic gift. There were no ghosts rattling chains, no voices thundering from beyond any veil. It was quieter than that, and stranger. When he held bone in his hands—the older the better, but even fresh bone would do—he heard what the dead had left behind. Not their souls, precisely. More like the shape their words had worn into them. A grandmother's lullaby caught in a finger joint. A soldier's last curse sunk deep in a rib. A child's name, repeated ten thousand times by a grieving mother, still ringing faintly in a milk tooth.

And Odile carved. He took the messages out of the bone the way you'd draw a splinter from a wound, translating them into lines and whorls, tiny glyphs that hummed against the fingertips of anyone who touched them. He didn't sell those. He couldn't. Instead he left them where they needed to go—a carved button on the coat of a widow who'd forgotten her husband's last words; a bead slipped into a beggar's cup containing her drowned son saying *it didn't hurt, Mam, I promise*. Twelve years of small mercies, smuggled into the world disguised as trash.

Twelve years without being caught. He had begun, lately, to believe it might hold forever.

The morning the courier came, Odile was carving a love charm for a girl with flour on her sleeves, telling her with complete sincerity that goose bone worked just as well as dove bone and cost half as much.

"That's superstition," she said, laughing.

"Everything worth having is," Odile said, and then the screaming started.

Not near him—not yet. It came from the mouth of Cooper's Row, a ragged sound that cut through the market noise the way a knife goes through wet cloth. The crowd flinched and parted, the way crowds do, everyone craning to see and no one wanting to be involved, and through the gap came a man running.

He was young, maybe twenty-five, dressed in the dust-gray leathers of a royal courier, and he was leaving a trail behind him that made the fishmonger swear and the candle-dipper finally speak, a single horrified word. Three arrows stood in him—one in the shoulder, one in the thigh, and one low in his back, which was the one that mattered. Odile knew death when he saw it walking. He had spent twelve years listening to what it left behind.

The courier crossed the market in a staggering line, knocking over a basket of plums, ignoring every hand that reached toward him, and came straight for Odile's stall as though Odile's face were the only fixed point in a spinning world.

"You," the courier rasped. "The carver. They said—" A cough brought blood to his lips. "They said the carver in Copperlane listens."

Odile's blood went cold. *They said.* Someone knew. Someone had talked, or guessed, or seen too much, and now this dying boy had run through half of Vell looking for the one thing Odile had spent a decade making sure he could not be found.

"I sell charms," Odile said quietly. "Rabbit bones. Nothing more."

The courier's hand shot across the stall and seized Odile's wrist with a grip that had no business being that strong in a man already dead. Into his palm, the courier pressed something hard and light and warm.

"Then sell *this*," he whispered. "Sell it somewhere far from here. Take it to the mountains, past the Ashen Ford, and don't stop until—" Another cough, worse. His knees buckled; Odile caught him, and together they went down behind the stall in a clatter of falling charms. The crowd was pressing close now, a ring of faces, and above their heads Odile could see the gray cloaks already turning at the far end of the row.

"Listen to me." The courier's eyes were fever-bright and terribly clear. "The king is not the king. Do you understand? He hasn't been the king for six years. Something wears him. Something *eats* him. It eats all of us—all the ones they took, all the silenced ones—it keeps them in the dark under the palace and it *feeds*—" His voice cracked apart. With the last of his strength he folded Odile's fingers closed around the thing in his palm. "The map shows the way to its heart. You hear the dead. So *hear me*: the king is already gone. And if you don't carry that past the Ford, no one ever will."

Odile looked down at his closed fist. Between his fingers, through the wrapping of oilcloth, he felt it: bone. Old bone, older than anything he had ever touched, and it was not silent. It was never silent—but this was different. This was a chorus. Dozens of voices, dozens, layered over each other like strata in stone, and beneath them all, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, something vast and golden and *hungry*, turning in its sleep.

Every hair on Odile's arms rose.

"Who sent you?" he whispered.

"Doesn't matter." The courier smiled, and it was the worst thing Odile had ever seen, because it was peaceful. "You're the last one they haven't found. Keep it that way. Don't let them find you now."

And then the light went out of him, between one breath and the next, right there in Odile's arms in front of forty witnesses.

* * *

Afterward, everything happened very fast and very slowly at once.

The crowd murmured. Someone shouted for the watch. The fruit-seller pressed a hand to her mouth and said, "Saints preserve us, he came right to *you*, Brightwater. Why'd he come to you?"

"I don't know," Odile said, and it was almost true, because he genuinely did not know who had betrayed him, only that someone had.

He tucked the wrapped bone into his apron pocket, beneath the display of knucklebones, and his hands were steady, which surprised him. Inside, everything was screaming.

He heard the gray cloaks before he saw them. He always heard the Inquisition before he saw them—not with his gift, which had nothing to say about the living, but with the animal hearing of a prey species. The particular rhythm of their boots. The way conversation died ahead of them in a spreading wave, like water freezing.

There were three of them. Two walked the row with the loose, bored stride of men doing routine work. The third stopped at the mouth of Copperlane Row and simply stood there, hood back, a woman with a long face and eyes the color of slate, watching the market the way a hawk watches a hedgerow. Odile knew her by reputation, as everyone in Vell did. Sera Vane. The Inquisitor who asked questions instead of confessions. The one they said had once been the Ember's favorite, before she started wondering aloud why the outlawry needed so many prisons.

She looked at the crowd. She looked at the dead courier, already being covered with a fishmonger's tarpaulin. Then she looked at Odile's stall—at the little bones laid out on their cloth, the rabbits and wrens and fish—and Odile watched her gaze snag there, just for a moment, the way a sleeve snags on a nail.

"Inquisitor," Odile said, when she stopped in front of him. His voice came out level. Twelve years of practice, spent in a single sentence.

"Carver." Her voice was mild. Mild as a blade is mild, before it's sharpened. "Busy morning."

"Market mornings usually are."

"Mm." She picked up a love charm, turned it in her fingers. Goose bone. Harmless. Odile watched her thumb move across it and thought, absurdly, *I should have charged the flour-girl more.* "A royal courier runs bleeding through your market, dies at your stall, calling your name. And you're selling trinkets."

"People die in markets, Inquisitor. Doesn't make it my doing."

"No." She set the charm down, precisely, in the center of the cloth. "It rarely does. It's just remarkable how often the dead end up in the hands of the same quiet men." She leaned in, close enough that Odile could smell the ink and iron of her cloak, and her voice dropped to something meant only for him. "I'm going to ask you three questions, carver, and you're going to answer none of them well, and I'm going to know everything I need anyway. Where do you get your bone?"

"Chandlers. Butchers. Whatever the market leaves."

"What did the courier give you?"

"Nothing. He was looking for a healer."

"And the third question," Sera Vane said, straightening, and here her slate eyes did something terrible—they softened, just slightly, with something that might have been pity, "is whether you're going to run. Because the others will burn this stall tonight and ask questions after, and I'd rather they didn't. You have until dark."

She turned and walked away, gray cloak swallowing her into the crowd, and Odile stood behind his table of harmless things with a chorus of the dead singing against his hip and his twelve years of silence lying broken around his feet like dropped glass.

He looked at the sky. High, blue, indifferent. Hours of light left. Enough time, if he ran now, to reach the city gates and lose himself on the south road—alone, empty-handed except for a dead man's map, carrying nothing of the life he'd built.

But the courier's words were still in his ears, and underneath them, fainter, the voices in the bone. Dozens of them. Silenced mages, kept in the dark under the palace, feeding something golden and starving. And threaded somewhere through that chorus—Odile couldn't be sure, couldn't bear to be sure, but the bone seemed to bend toward the thought the way a flower bends toward light—was a voice he would have known anywhere in the world, alive or dead.

*Mother?*

Odile Brightwater looked at his stall, at the life he had spent twelve years building out of nothing and harmlessness, and understood that it was already ash. Safety had been a thing he owned, and he had spent it in the space of one morning, on a dying boy he'd never met.

He began, with steady hands, to pack up his bones.

By dark, he would need allies. People who knew the ways in and out of Vell, people who had their own reasons to hate the gray cloaks, people who were, like him, already lost. He knew exactly three such people in the whole city, and he wasn't certain any of them would forgive him for what he was about to ask.

He picked up the wrapped map, felt the chorus stir against his palm, and stepped out from behind his stall into the crowded, dangerous, waking world.

Behind him, unnoticed, the slate-eyed inquisitor watched him go—and smiled.
