## 2. A Company of the Unwanted

The map was warm in Odile's pocket, warmer than bone had any right to be, and it had not cooled once in the two days since the courier died on his stall. He could feel it through the wool of his coat like a coal wrapped in linen, patient as a heartbeat.

He did not sleep. Sleep meant dreams, and dreams meant hearing, and lately the dead of Vell had grown loud—whispering from the gutters, from the walls, from the mouths of the fountains where the inquisitors had burned the last public witch eleven years ago. *He is already gone,* the courier had said, blood bubbling between his teeth. *The king is already gone.* And then he had said nothing at all, forever, which was the one silence Odile had never learned to bear.

So he packed what mattered: carving knives, a roll of riverbone, his mother's unfinished sparrow with its wing still rough as bark. He left the charms on the stall. Let the market think him fled or dead; either story would keep them safe.

He made it four streets before the bells began.

* * *

In the Amber Hall, behind doors gilded so thickly they drank the light, the king sat upon his throne and did not move for six hours.

Courtiers came and went. A chamberlain read petitions. Somewhere below, a child wept in a corridor no map admitted to. The king's eyes stayed fixed on the middle distance, on nothing, on everything—the Ember wearing him the way a hand wears a glove, feeling out each finger of the man it had hollowed: the old griefs, the buried kindnesses, the name that had been scraped off him like paint.

*They are gathering,* the Ember thought, in the voice it had stolen first, its own voice, which was hunger given grammar. It felt them the way a spider feels rain: Sera Vane, walking out of the Inquisition's black tower with her oath broken and her sash burning in a brazier. A child slipping through a ward in the Tanner's Quarter like water through fingers. An old woman in the marshes whose garden grew things that should not grow.

*And the bone-carver.* The Ember smiled with the king's mouth, and the chamberlain flinched and pretended not to see. *The little listener. Bring me his ears.*

* * *

Sera found him before he found her—which, he would learn later, was how she always worked.

He had gone to the Tanner's Quarter looking for Bramble, the ward-walking thief the courier's dying whispers had named twice, and instead found a woman sitting on the steps of a shuttered dye-house with a sword across her knees and an inquisitor's coat stripped of its silver buttons, selling the buttons one by one to passersby who didn't dare look at her.

"Odile Brightwater," she said, without looking up. "Bone-carver. The courier Maren was your cousin's son."

"He wasn't." His mouth went dry. "I never had a cousin."

"No," she agreed. "But Maren believed you did, because believing it kept you both deniable. He was my friend. He was also my informant, and three days ago he stopped reporting, and I want to know why the Inquisition's own records now say there was never any such person." She looked up then. Her eyes were the gray of storm-water and just as warm. "Records don't erase themselves, Brightwater. Something in that palace is eating people out of history. I've seen the ledgers. I've seen names gone smooth as river stones. I burned my oath over it."

"You'll be hunted," Odile said.

"I'm already hunted. I'd rather be hunted *toward* something." She stood, sheathing the sword. "Maren trusted you with whatever he carried. So either you're the bravest man in Vell or the most foolish, and I find I don't care which. I'm coming."

* * *

Bramble was harder. Bramble was, by professional principle, hard.

Odile found the child at last in the roof-gutters above the chandlers' row, a small sharp-faced figure of perhaps ten years, gnawing a stolen apple and watching him with the flat patience of a heron.

"I know about the wards," Odile called up. "Maren said you can walk through anything."

"Maren's dead," Bramble said, around the apple. "Dead men talk too much."

"Then hear it from me. There's a thing wearing the king. It eats voices. It's been eating them for twelve years, ever since the outlawry, and I have a map to where it keeps what it's stolen."

Silence. The apple lowered. For the first time the child's stillness looked less like patience and more like a held breath.

"My mum's voice went quiet when the outlawry came," Bramble said at last. "Not dead-quiet. *Taken*-quiet. They marched her to the palace and she never shouted again, not even my name, and I was seven, and I thought—" The apple hit the cobbles, forgotten. "I thought maybe if I got good enough at going where I shouldn't, someday I'd go where *she* is."

Odile's chest ached. "Then come with us."

"I get a share of anything shiny."

"Anything shiny," he promised, and knew he meant it, and knew the promise was a lie neither of them believed.

* * *

Old Tamsin's house stood in the marshes south of the city, half-sunk in peat, its garden growing feverfew beside flowers with too many petals and a small tree that bore fruit out of season out of pure spite. She met them at the gate with a shotgun older than the outlawry and eyes that catalogued all three of them in a single sweep.

"A disgraced inquisitor, a ward-thief, and the bone-carver." She snorted. "The Ember sends its regards, does it? Took my license first, then my daughters, then my craft. Left me the garden because even devils have bad aim." She uncocked the gun. "Well. Come in before the marsh hears you. It gossips."

Inside, over nettle tea, Odile finally unwrapped the map.

It was not a map of roads. It was a map of *depths*—the palace rendered in cross-section, floors peeling away like onion skin until only the under-vault remained: a chamber beneath the throne room, ringed in wards, filled with what the courier's careful carvings named *the choir*. Hundreds of small marks, each one a voice. Each one a mage taken in the night after the outlawry, their gifts and their words drawn out and stored like wine in a cellar.

And at the center, feeding, a shape the carver had rendered as a sun with a crown.

"It doesn't rule the city," Tamsin breathed, tracing the vault with a knuckle. "It *eats* it. Every silenced voice, every outlawed gift—it's been drinking twelve years of us. That's how it holds the king. That's how it holds everything."

"The choir," Sera said quietly. "I signed the transport orders. Gods forgive me, I signed them and I thought I was moving prisoners." Her hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against the table. "My sister was in one of those wagons. I told myself the records were wrong. Then the records started being wrong on purpose, and I couldn't tell myself anything anymore."

Bramble said nothing at all, but stared at the hundreds of small marks, counting, lips moving, and Odile understood the child had found their mother among them and could not stop counting.

Odile touched the map's center, and the bone sang—a thin, terrible harmony, dozens of voices braided into one note—and beneath it, faint as a thread of gold in dark cloth, a voice he knew better than his own heartbeat.

His mother. Twelve years silent. Singing.

He pulled his hand back as if burned.

"Well?" Tamsin said, watching him with those cataloguing eyes. "What did it say?"

"That we're going to the palace," Odile said, and heard his own voice steady itself by force. "Through the wards, past the Inquisition, down under the throne. All of us."

"Foolish," Tamsin said. Then she reached for her staff, leaning against the wall where a broom should be. "Pack the rest of the tea."

* * *

In the Amber Hall, the Ember rose from its throne and walked to the window, and the whole court held its breath, because the king had not stood in a year.

Far to the south, past the rooftops, it could feel them: four small flames moving together, drawing close, drawing *warm*. The carver. The traitor. The child who slipped through locks. The hedge-witch whose sisters it had swallowed and whose hatred it could taste across the miles like pepper on the tongue.

*Come,* it thought, with the king's lungs, with the king's breath, with the king's stolen face turned toward the marshes and smiling. *Bring the map. Bring the ears. The choir has room for four more voices.*

Below the throne, in the vault of voices, something that had once been a woman named Brightwater began, very softly, to sing louder.
