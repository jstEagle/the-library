## 3. The Hollow Crown

The palace of Vell had a hundred faces, and Bramble knew every one of them.

"Servants' stair's no good," the child said, crouched in the shadow of the aqueduct where it met the outer wall, her fingers tracing a seam in the stone that Odile could not see. "Warded. Everything aboveground is warded now. But the Ember's proud, see? Proud things don't guard their own rot." She grinned up at him, gap-toothed and filthy. "There's a drain under the east cistern that hasn't been opened since the old king drowned his brother in it. Nobody remembers it because nobody remembers *him*."

"That's cheerful," Sera murmured. She had traded her inquisitor's white for dyed wool and a hood, but she still stood like one of them—spine straight, hands loose near her belt knife, eyes always moving. Twelve years hunting mages had taught her how hunters thought. It was why they needed her. It was also, some nights, why Odile could not look at her.

Old Tamsin said nothing. She hadn't said much since the map first unrolled across her kitchen table three days ago, its bone surface crawling with channels like veins, all of them converging on a single point beneath the throne room. She had only touched it once, with one finger, and wept without sound.

Now she pressed a charm into Odile's palm—knotted rowan, ugly as a fist. "For your ears," she said. "When you get down there, there'll be more voices than any living thing should hold. This'll keep the worst out."

"And the best?" Odile asked.

Tamsin looked at him for a long moment. "Nothing keeps out what's already inside you, boy."

* * *

The drain smelled of a century of forgetting. They went down by lamplight, single file through a passage barely wide enough for Bramble's narrow shoulders, the child walking wards-blind through knots of old magic that made Sera's teeth ache and Tamsin mutter prayers to gods who had been outlawed alongside everything else. Twice the passage forked; twice Bramble chose without hesitation, following routes stolen from a pickpocketed architect twenty years before she was born.

Odile counted his heartbeats to keep from counting the voices.

He could feel them already, faint as frost forming—hundreds of presences pressed thin beneath the city, humming through the stone like a struck bell that never finished ringing. His gift had been a curse in Vell for twelve years precisely because the dead were never quiet here. The outlawry had not silenced magic. It had only taught it to scream underground.

Then the passage opened, and he stopped counting.

The vault was a hollow sphere of black granite, older than the palace above it, older perhaps than Vell itself. And it was full.

They hung in the air like moths in amber—voices, thousands of them, each one a small pale light shaped vaguely like a throat, a mouth, a person. They did not speak. That was the horror of it. They *wanted* to. Odile could see the wanting in the way they trembled, straining toward sound the way drowning people strain toward air, and no sound came. The Gilded Ember fed on silence the way other things fed on bread.

"Oh," Tamsin whispered. Her hand found Odile's sleeve. "Oh, you poor things."

"Bramble." Sera's voice was flat and careful. "How many ways out of this room?"

"One," Bramble said quietly. All the mischief had gone out of her face. "And it just closed."

The light changed. It came from everywhere—the gold spreading along the walls like honey poured over glass, and in it a shape assembled itself: tall, crowned, beautiful the way a knife is beautiful. It wore the king's body somewhere far above them, but here, in its feeding place, it wore only itself.

"You brought me a bone-carver," the Ember said, and its voice was every voice in the vault speaking at once, flattened into one. "Twelve years I have felt you listening, little craftsman. Do you know what you are? You are a door. And doors are so much more useful than windows."

Odile's mouth went dry. "You know what I am."

"I know what everyone is. I eat what they were." The Ember drifted closer, and the trapped voices shrank from it, pressing against the far wall of the vault like fish against ice. "But you—you could *hear* them. Every voice I've swallowed for thirty years, and you could have given them back their words. So here is my offer, carver, and I will make it only once."

It gestured, almost gently, and the gold light coiled around Sera and Bramble and Tamsin like a closing hand.

"Your gift," the Ember said. "Your hearing, surrendered willingly—and your silence after. You will carve nothing true again. You will live, and they will live, and you will forget this room. That is mercy, Odile Brightwater. I am told I rarely offer it twice."

The vault was very quiet. Somewhere in the amber mass of voices, one light flickered differently than the others—a rhythm Odile knew better than his own heartbeat, a cadence he had heard last when he was fourteen years old, singing half-remembered lullabies over a grave he'd dug himself.

*Mother.*

She couldn't speak. None of them could. But the light pulsed, once, twice, in the pattern she had used to call him home to supper, and twelve years of hiding cracked open in his chest like an egg.

He could say yes. He could walk away whole. He could live.

Sera saw it on his face. She didn't beg. She just said, quietly, "Whatever you choose, carver—I chose wrong for twelve years too. We're even."

Bramble was crying without noise, which was somehow worse.

"No," Odile said.

The word fell into the vault like a stone into a well.

"She sang to me," he said, and his voice shook, and he let it shake, because he was done being silent. "Every one of them sang to someone. You didn't steal voices. You stole *being heard*. And I would rather be deaf and dead and forgotten than spend one more year safe in a city built on this."

He raised the bone map high and drove his carving knife into it.

The vault erupted.

Light tore outward from the map like a scream given form—the dead king's final message finally delivered, every channel in the bone flaring open at once. The trapped voices surged toward the breach, and the Ember shrieked with ten thousand stolen throats, its golden shape splintering, unraveling, reaching—

—and catching Bramble.

The child screamed as the gold poured into her mouth, her eyes flooding bright, her small body arching like a drawn bow. Tamsin lunged for her and was thrown back against the wall. Sera's knife glanced off the light as if it were armor.

"*Take me instead!*" Odile roared, and flung himself between the Ember and the girl, hands outstretched, feeling his gift roar awake for the first time in twelve years—not hiding, not whispering, but *listening*, wide open, pulling the Ember's attention the way a struck bell pulls every ear in a city.

The gold hesitated. Wavered. Began to turn toward him—

—and the vault's ceiling split.

Above them, through the cracking granite, came the sound of the palace itself waking: bells, shouting, and beneath it all a new voice rising through the broken floor, vast and furious and *human*—a voice none of them had ever heard, because it had been erased from every record in Vell.

The king's own name, remembered at last, calling down into the dark:

*"EMBER. LET GO OF MY PEOPLE."*

The gold recoiled. Bramble dropped, gasping, into Sera's arms. And through the widening wound in the ceiling, torchlight spilled down into the vault, and with it a silhouette wearing a crown that no longer fit a hollow head.

Odile stared up at it, deaf suddenly to everything but the pounding of his own heart, and understood that whatever happened next would happen in the throne room above.

The Ember, bleeding light, began to climb.
