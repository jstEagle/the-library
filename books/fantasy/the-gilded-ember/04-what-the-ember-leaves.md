## 4. What the Ember Leaves

The throne room of Vell had been built to make visitors feel small, and it succeeded even now, with its windows shattered and its gilded pillars scorched black. Morning light came through the broken glass in long amber blades, cutting across a floor littered with the ash of things the Ember had burned for warmth. Not wood. Odile tried not to think about what else it might have fed on in its hunger years.

The throne itself sat at the far end like a wound. And upon it, slumped and swaying, was the king.

What remained of him.

He had been a broad man once—Odile could see the architecture of it beneath the ruin—but the Gilded Ember had eaten from the inside out, leaving skin stretched over absence. His eyes were open. They had to be; the thing wearing him kept them open so the courtiers' ghosts would believe he still watched. But there was nothing behind them now except a gold light guttering in the dark, like a candle drowning in its own wax.

*It knows we're here,* Sera signed from the doorway, her inquisitor's blade drawn low. Her left arm hung wrong where the vault had taken its toll, but she stood anyway. She always stood anyway.

Bramble crouched at Odile's heel, small and quick-eyed, fingers twitching toward every shadowed exit out of habit that no longer served. Old Tamsin leaned on her staff behind them both, breathing hard, her hedge-magic flickering around her like heat off summer stone.

And beneath it all—the voices.

They had followed Odile up from the vault like water finding its level. Hundreds of them. Thousands. The silenced mages of twelve outlawed years, pressed into the Ember's gilded body, singing without sound. He heard them the way he had always heard the dead: not in his ears, but in his bones, in the marrow the way his mother had taught him to listen before the listening became a crime.

Among them, faint as a thread pulled through cloth: *Odile. My love. My winter child.*

His mother.

"Not yet," he whispered, and did not know if he said it to her or to himself.

The Ember turned the king's head. The motion was wrong—all glide and no weight, a puppet remembering flesh incorrectly. When it spoke, the voice was beautiful, and that was the worst thing about it. It had stolen beauty too.

"You brought them to me." The gold light brightened, pleased. "All these years I have dined on whispers, morsels, scraps of the condemned. But you—" the ruined hand extended, palm up, an invitation or a demand, "—you carry a whole voice, carver. A living one. Give it freely and I will let your little family walk out of my house. You know I keep my bargains."

It did keep its bargains. That was the horror of it. In the vault it had offered, and Odile had refused, and Sera had paid the price of his refusal with blood and Bramble had nearly gone into the light screaming. The Ember honored its word the way a knife honors a sheath.

Odile walked forward.

"Odile—" Sera's warning cracked across the hall.

"I hear you," he said, and meant more than the words. "I've spent twelve years hearing everything and saying nothing. Let me finish this."

He climbed the three steps to the throne. Up close, the king smelled of cold metal and old honey. The gold light in his eyes tracked Odile like a starving animal tracks a hand that might feed it—or hold a knife.

"You want my gift," Odile said. "My hearing. Come and take it."

The Ember laughed, and the laugh moved through the hollow king like wind through a dead reed bed. "Freely given, then. Kneel."

"No." Odile reached into his coat and drew out his last carving.

He had made it in the vault's aftermath, by candle stub, while Bramble slept and Sera bound her arm and Tamsin muttered wards against the morning. He had made it from the only bone he had left—the knuckle-bone of the courier who had died pressing the map into his hands, who had spent his final breath saying *the king is already gone* as though it were a mercy, as though it were news anyone could use.

On that bone, in letters fine as spider silk, Odile had carved a single word.

A name.

Not the Ember's name—it had none, names were the first thing it devoured—but the king's own. Aldric Vane. It had been erased from every record, every coin, every wall when the Ember took the crown, because a hollowed king cannot rule if someone remembers what a whole man sounded like. Odile knew it the way he knew all the dead's secrets: the courier's bones had whispered it to him in the market, in the first terrible hour, and he had carried it since like a coal cupped in bare hands.

"What is that?" The Ember's voice lost some of its music.

"A gift," Odile said. "For the man you ate."

He reached up—slowly, deliberately, letting the thing see every movement—and pressed the carved bone against the iron circlet above the king's brow. Then he drew his carving knife along the crown itself, tracing the name into the metal, each stroke a small ringing note that fell into the silence of the hall like stones into a well.

*Aldric. Vane.*

The Ember screamed.

It was not a sound so much as a tearing—a sound like a tapestry ripped lengthwise, like a choir forced to hold one note past endurance. The gold light in the king's eyes flared, guttered, flared again, and Odile felt it turn on him with the full weight of its attention, vast and furious and suddenly afraid.

"Take it back."

"No."

"I will unmake them. I will pour the girl through my teeth and swallow the witch whole—"

"You'll try," said Old Tamsin from the foot of the steps, and her staff struck the floor, and every ward she had ever woven into this palace's bones woke at once, old hedge-magic older than the outlawry, older than the Ember's clever thefts. The doors sealed. The light bent inward.

"Bramble," Odile said quietly. "Now."

The child grinned—wild, gap-toothed, unafraid in the way only children who have already survived the worst thing can be—and ran straight through the barrier of gold fire the Ember threw up, because wards had never once held Bramble, and fire was only a ward wearing a louder coat. Small hands caught Odile's wrist. "Got you," Bramble breathed. "Hold still, carver."

And Sera—Sera drove her inquisitor's blade into the throne's base, into the seam where the Ember anchored itself to the seat of Vell, and shouted the words of her own disgrace, the truth she had been exiled for speaking: *"The crown belongs to no spirit. The crown belongs to the man."*

The name on the crown began to glow.

*

Odile felt the Ember come for him then—not with hands, which it no longer had, but with hunger, vast and ancient and precise. It poured itself into the channel of his gift, into the twelve-year-open door of his hearing, and it pulled.

He let it.

That was the choice, and he made it with his eyes open. Every voice he had ever carried—the dead of the market stalls, the courier, the condemned mages, his mother's thread of song woven through them all—he opened the marrow-door wide and gave the Ember exactly what it had demanded. Freely given. All of it.

The Ember gorged. The Ember drowned.

It had spent twelve years sipping through a straw, and Odile handed it the ocean. The stolen voices rushed through him and out, unbound, unfettered, a flood of the silenced pouring into the hollow spaces of the king—and the Ember, stuffed past bearing with the very thing it had starved for, began to unravel like a rope whose fibers had finally been asked to hold too much. Gold light streamed from the king's eyes, his mouth, the seams of him, and in the light Odile saw shapes—faces, hands, mouths opening after years of enforced silence—and then the light went out.

The king fell forward.

Odile caught him. His arms ached; his head rang with a silence so total it was almost a sound, a deafness of the soul where for twelve years there had never once been quiet. The dead were gone from him. All of them. Even—

Even her.

He knelt on the scorched stones holding a stranger's weight, and wept without sound, because he had given away the only part of himself that had ever known how to listen.

But the man in his arms stirred. Drew breath. And in a voice rusted from disuse, cracked and small and unmistakably his own, King Aldric Vane said:

"My name. Someone remembered my name."

"Yes," Odile said. "Yes. It's yours. Take it back."

*

They repealed the outlawry in the spring.

Sera read the proclamation herself, standing in the same square where inquisitors had once read sentences, her arm healed crooked and her rank restored and refused. Bramble sat on the proclamation platform picking pockets with ceremonial impunity. Old Tamsin got a garden, and a title she used to prop open her gate.

The king ruled gently, the way men do who have seen the inside of their own absence. He sent for Odile twice. Both times Odile came, and both times they sat in a room with tea going cold between them, two people who had survived the same event in different bodies, and found they had very little to say. It was enough. Some debts are paid simply by continuing to exist.

Odile opened a shop.

Not a stall—a shop, with a door that locked and a sign he carved himself: **BRIGHTWATER CARVINGS**. He sold charms again, harmless ones mostly, though he could no longer tell harmless from haunted and had stopped worrying about the difference. His hands still knew their work. Bone still took an edge beautifully. He simply carved now for the living, for weddings and births and griefs that had not yet happened, and if the pieces sometimes seemed to hum in the night, he chose—as a kindness to himself—to sleep through it.

The silence in his head was not empty anymore. It was just quiet, the way a house is quiet, the way a life is quiet.

Customers came. That was the miracle he had never expected—that people would speak to him, ordinary people, about ordinary things, and he would answer, and neither of them would need magic to bridge the space between. A widow bought a charm for her son's wedding and stayed an hour talking about her late husband, and Odile listened with nothing but his ears, and it was enough. It was more than enough.

One evening near closing, a woman came in with winter-grey eyes and his own jawline, carrying a bundle wrapped in linen.

She set it on the counter and unwound the cloth, and inside lay a bone comb, old and yellowed, worn smooth by a decade of a dead woman's hand.

"I found this among her things," the woman said. "I'm told you're the one who listens. Or—I'm told you *were*." She smiled, uncertain, hopeful, terrified. "I never knew her. She died when I was born. I thought... maybe something survives in the bone. Maybe you'd look at it. Even without the gift. Maybe you'd just—tell me what you see."

Odile looked at the comb. He looked at the woman who wore his mother's cheekbones and did not know it, who had crossed the city to find him without knowing why the name Brightwater had pulled at her like a tide.

He picked up the comb. Turned it in the light. And began, carefully, to speak.
