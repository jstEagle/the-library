## 1. Charms for the Godless

The rain came to Greyharrow the way it always did—sideways, gray, and smelling of coal smoke—and Jonah Whitlock stood at his shop window and watched it fall with the particular hunger of a man watching a lover he was forbidden to touch.

Five years, and he still counted the drops.

He knew better. He had trained himself out of a hundred small habits: no more turning his face up to storms, no more letting his fingers twitch in time with distant thunder, no more standing too long at windows when lightning walked the hills beyond the city walls. A storm-caller who wanted to stay alive in Greyharrow learned to be bored by weather. Boredom was armor. Boredom was the whole discipline.

*You are a charm-seller,* he reminded himself, as he did every morning, in the flat voice of ritual. *You are a small man with a small trade and no gift at all.*

The shop was called Whitlock's Curiosities, which was a lie of exactly the right size. The charms in its cases were honest frauds: river stones drilled and strung on waxed cord, rabbit bones wrapped in copper wire, bits of sea glass polished until they caught the lamplight like captured stars. He sold them as wards against headache, against unfaithful husbands, against the evil eye of mothers-in-law. None of them worked. That was the point. In Greyharrow, where the Edict of Ash hung over every door like a drawn blade, a charm that actually *worked* would get you a quiet cart ride to the citadel and a name scratched into some clerk's list. A charm that didn't work got you a copper coin and a blessing.

Jonah had gotten very good at not working.

He turned the sign to OPEN and began his morning liturgy: banking the coals in the little stove, straightening the jars of dried herbs that were only herbs, checking the false bottom of the counter where he kept nothing incriminating because keeping something incriminating there would be the first mistake of an amateur. There was nothing under the floorboards either. Nothing in the walls. The only dangerous thing in Whitlock's Curiosities was the man himself, and he kept that folded down so small and so deep that some mornings he almost believed it.

Almost.

Because here was the truth he carried like a stone behind his breastbone: the gift didn't leave. You could starve it, silence it, bury it under five years of careful mediocrity, but it lived in him the way the sea lives in a harbor—present even at low tide, waiting, patient, tasting the air for weather. On dry days he barely felt it. But when storms gathered over the Sereth foothills and rolled toward the city, something in his chest turned over like a sleeper, and Jonah had to lie very still inside himself and pretend to be empty.

Today the sky over Greyharrow was the color of dirty wool, heavy but harmless. He felt only the faintest pull, a fish tugging at a far line, and he ignored it the way he ignored all of them.

The bell above the door rang at midmorning.

"Closed for inventory," Jonah said without looking up, because the morning's first customer was usually Master Hobbes from the chandlery wanting to haggle over candle-charms, and Hobbes could smell desperation through oak.

"It's about my mother."

The voice stopped him. Young—a girl, or nearly—and frayed at the edges, the way thread frays before it snaps. Jonah looked up.

She stood just inside the door with her hood still up, water dripping off its hem onto his clean floor, and she held her hands pulled into her sleeves the way people do when they're trying to make themselves smaller than they already are. Seventeen, maybe eighteen. Thin as a winter wren, brown-haired, sharp-chinned, with eyes that were doing too much work—flicking to the window, to the street, to the corners of his shop, cataloguing exits. He'd seen that look before. He wore it himself, in mirrors.

"Your mother," Jonah said carefully.

"She can't sleep. Since father died she lies awake all night staring at the ceiling, and the physicker's tonics don't help, and Tamla next door said—" The girl swallowed. "Tamla said you sell charms. For sleep. For grief."

"I sell trinkets," Jonah said. It was automatic, the first wall of many. "River stones. Pretty things. If your mother finds them comforting, that's comfort worth a copper, but I'll not pretend they're anything more."

Something flickered across the girl's face—not disappointment exactly. Recognition, maybe. Or relief. She glanced at the door again, and this time Jonah followed her glance, and saw what he should have seen the moment the bell rang.

There was a man across the street.

He stood in the shadow of the cooper's awning, out of the rain, doing nothing at all—which was itself the tell. No one in Greyharrow stood still in doorways on a market morning unless they were paid to. He wore the drab brown of a minor clerk, and he held a ledger against his chest, and he was reading it, or pretending to read it, and his eyes lifted to Jonah's window once every dozen breaths like a needle finding north.

Jonah's stomach went cold and quiet, the way it did before lightning.

"Come away from the window," he said, mild as milk. "Draft's wicked. Let me show you the sleep-stones."

He steered her gently toward the back counter, putting his own body between her and the glass, and made a show of unlocking the case, laying out three smooth gray stones on velvet gone bald with use. His hands were steady. He'd had practice being steady while everything under his skin screamed.

"What's your name?" he asked.

A pause. He watched her decide whether to lie, and respected her for the length of it.

"Wren," she said finally.

"And your mother's trouble is sleeping, Wren? Only sleeping?"

Her jaw tightened. She looked at the stones and not at him. "She hears things," Wren said, very quietly. "At night. Voices. And last month she knocked over a lamp without touching it, and the fire started green, and I put it out with my bare hands and it didn't burn me." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "And two days ago a man in clerk's brown asked our landlady questions about us. About whether strange things happened in our rooms."

The cold in Jonah's gut spread outward, precise as frost on glass.

Clerk's brown. Ledger against the chest. The same uniform as the man across the street.

"They're called inquisitors now," Jonah said. He kept his voice level, bored, the voice of a tradesman discussing weather. "Under the new king. They used to be called assessors, and before that, recorders. The crown keeps changing what it calls them, but the work stays the same." He picked up one of the stones, turned it in his fingers. "They go door to door. They ask neighbors about neighbors. Neighbors, being what they are, answer."

Wren's hands had come out of her sleeves. They were trembling. "Is it true what they say in the market? That the gifted get taken to the citadel?"

"I wouldn't know anything about the gifted," Jonah said. "I sell rocks."

For a moment—one dangerous, naked moment—the girl looked at him directly, and Jonah felt seen in a way he hadn't felt in five years. Not seen as Whitlock the charm-seller, the small safe man. Seen as whatever she thought she recognized underneath. It was like standing in a doorway during a storm with the wind pulling at your coat, asking to be let in.

"You put the fire out with your hands," Jonah said quietly, "and it didn't burn you. And you came to a stranger's shop instead of running. Why?"

"Because running is what they watch for," Wren said. "Everyone knows that. People who run look guilty. I needed—" Her breath hitched. "I needed to buy something ordinary. From someone ordinary. So if anyone asks, I was buying a stone for my mother's headaches, same as half the city does."

It was, Jonah reflected, exactly what he would have done. Exactly what he *had* done, five years ago, arriving in Greyharrow with singed boots and a dead master's warning ringing in his ears, and buying a shop lease within the week. The frightened learn the same grammar everywhere.

He wrapped the largest stone in cloth, tucked a second one beside it unasked, and tied the string with a shopkeeper's unhurried knots. "Two coppers," he said. "The gray one for sleep. The darker one is for headaches. River stones, both. Utterly worthless."

Wren set the coins on the counter. Her fingers brushed his as she took the bundle, and Jonah felt it—not magic, nothing so dramatic, just the static-hum of a gift waking in a body too young and too alone to know what it was carrying. Like feeling heat radiate off a stove in a dark room. She had power in her the way a dry field has fire in it, and she had no idea, and the crown's clerks were already writing her street name in their ledgers.

"The man across the street," Jonah said, as she turned to go. He said it lightly, tossing it after her like a scrap. "In the brown coat, by the cooper's. He's been there since before you arrived. He may be nothing. Clerks are often nothing. But a clever girl might leave by the back way, just for variety."

Wren went very still. Then, without turning around, without changing her expression at all, she said, "Thank you for the stones, master. My mother will be glad of them."

"A good day to you," Jonah said, equally bland. "Mind the puddles."

The bell rang. She was gone.

* * *

He gave it a quarter hour. Long enough to seem unconnected, short enough to matter. He flipped the sign to CLOSED FOR INVENTORY, banked the stove, and went out the back into the alley, where the rain had found every gap in his collar within six steps.

The alley let onto Tanner's Row; Tanner's Row bent into the Ropewalk; the Ropewalk spilled into Cordwainer Street, and somewhere along that winding route Jonah allowed himself to do the thing he never did: he looked for the girl.

Not hard. Looking hard was how you found people, and being found was death. But Greyharrow's streets were a loom he'd studied for five years, and he knew the weft of them, and it took him only ten minutes of deliberate aimlessness to spot the brown hood ducking into the covered stair by the fullers' yard—two streets from her landlady's, moving smart, taking the route a person takes when someone has told them about variety.

Good girl.

And behind her, half a street back and gaining, walked a second man in clerk's brown. Not the one from the cooper's awning—or perhaps it was, changed somehow, or perhaps there had simply never been only one. This one moved differently. No ledger clutched to his chest. His hands hung loose, and he walked the way wolves walk through markets: like a creature temporarily agreeing to pretend the world is not made of meat.

An assessor-in-training, then. Or worse. The crown had begun sending pairs.

Jonah stood beneath a rope-maker's awning, rain drumming the canvas overhead, and felt the old familiar argument open inside him—the one he'd been having with himself since the day he buried his past under a shopkeeper's name.

*Walk away.* She's nothing to you. One frightened girl among hundreds, and the Audit hasn't even begun, and if you so much as sneeze sideways the whole five years burns down around you. You survived Corvane. You survived the exile. Don't die for a stranger's arithmetic.

But his feet had already decided. That was the treachery of the gift, or maybe just of him: it had never made him call lightning. It had made him unable to stand still under a blackening sky.

He cut through the ropewalk at a pace just short of hurrying, took the covered stair two steps at a time, and came out into the fullers' court in time to see the wolf-walked clerk catch Wren's sleeve at the far arch.

Jonah didn't think. Thinking was for men with time.

He reached for the sky instead—for the weight of the water hanging over Greyharrow, the gray tons of it, the patient pressure he'd spent five years pretending not to feel. It answered the way it always answered: instantly, joyfully, like a dog hearing its name after years of silence. He didn't call lightning. He didn't dare. He only pulled, gently, delicately, a thread of intent through a mile of cloud—

—and the sky over the fullers' court opened like a dropped jug.

The rain came down in a single blinding sheet, a white roaring curtain that swallowed the arch, the clerk, the girl, the whole world. Gutters leapt. Awning-canvas snapped taut. Somewhere a cart horse screamed and slipped its traces. And in the chaos, in the drowning white noise of it, Jonah walked forward through the flood, took Wren's arm as naturally as a neighbor steadying a neighbor, and said, "This way, miss—you'll drown standing there," and turned her down the narrow drain-passage between the fuller's sheds, away from the arch, away from the clerk, whose curses vanished behind the wall of water as though the sky itself had stepped between them.

They came out by the tannery sluice, soaked to the bone, breathless. Behind them the downpour was already slackening—he couldn't hold a shape that size long, not without leaving fingerprints all over the sky—and ahead of them Greyharrow went on shouting about the weather, ordinary and oblivious.

Wren stared at him. Water ran off her chin. Her eyes were enormous.

"That was you," she whispered.

"Don't," Jonah said. His heart was slamming against his ribs like something trying to get out. Five years of discipline, spent in thirty seconds, for a girl whose name he'd known for a quarter hour. "Don't say it. Don't ever say it. Not to me, not to anyone, not in your prayers."

"But you—"

"I sell trinkets," Jonah said. "I sell worthless river stones to frightened people, and today the weather happened to turn, and that is all that happened, and if you have any sense at all you will believe me completely."

She believed him the way people believe a locked door is secure—with hope rather than conviction. He could see it. He could also see, with a clarity that felt like falling, that it no longer mattered what she believed. The clerk had seen a storm arrive on command's schedule. The sky over Greyharrow had just spoken with Jonah Whitlock's voice, and somewhere in the citadel a pen was already moving across a page.

Above the rooftops, the clouds he'd pulled apart were closing again, heavier now, grayer, gathering themselves for evening. And threading through the ordinary noise of the city—carts, gulls, a bell calling the hour—came another sound, faint and wrong, from the direction of the great square.

A crier's bell. Ringing twice. Then twice again.

The rhythm of an announcement. The rhythm of something new.

Jonah stood in the rain he had made, holding the arm of a girl he had just doomed, and listened to the bell ring, and understood with perfect, terrible certainty that the five quiet years were over.
