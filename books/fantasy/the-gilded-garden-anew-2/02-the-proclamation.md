## 2. The Proclamation

The bells of Greyharrow were wrong.

Jonah Whitlock noticed it before he was fully awake—before the grey light had finished seeping through the shutters of his room above the charm shop, before the kettle on his little iron stove had begun its low complaint. The city's bells rang at dawn and dusk and for funerals and for the Feast of the Founding, and in five years he had learned their voices the way a sailor learns the sea: the fat bronze tongue of the Cathedral of Saint Verren, the thin cracked note of the Harbormaster's tower, the small sweet bell over the door of his own shop that he'd tuned himself with a jeweler's hammer so that it sang just slightly flat, unremarkable, forgettable.

These bells were none of those. These bells rang fast and hard, all nine towers at once, an urgent brazen clatter that meant one thing only in the grammar of Greyharrow: *assembly*. The last time they had rung this way, three years ago, a grain ship had burned at the quay and half the Lower Terraces had come down to fight the fire with buckets and wet blankets. Before that—not in Jonah's time—the criers had used them to call the city to witness an execution.

He stood at his window a long moment, watching the street below fill with people in nightclothes and work aprons, faces tilted up toward the cathedral steps, breath steaming in the early cold. Somewhere a dog was barking itself hoarse. Somewhere a mother was calling a child home from wherever children went when bells like these rang.

Jonah dressed carefully. He always dressed carefully—it was part of the discipline—but this morning he took particular pains with the details. The frayed cuffs of his coat, mended with thread that matched badly on purpose. The ink stains on his fingers that he renewed each morning with a drop from a stoppered vial, because a charm-seller who dabbled in forgery to survive was plausible, while a charm-seller whose hands were clean as a priest's invited questions. The dull pewter ring on his right hand, set with a chip of river glass that did nothing at all except look like something a mediocre man would wear for luck.

Then he went down and opened his shop, because whatever was coming, the safest place for a man like him to be was exactly where everyone expected him to be.

* * *

The square in front of the cathedral was called Verren's Green, though there had been no green in it for a hundred years—only cobblestones worn smooth and greasy by ten thousand feet, and the iron fountain where washerwomen argued, and the long stone steps where the criers stood. By the time Jonah reached it, wedging himself into the crowd near a baker's stall where he could see without being seen, the steps were full.

There were six criers, which was itself an answer. One crier was news. Two was an edict. Six was a decree from the crown itself, read in every district of every city on the same morning, so that no one could claim they hadn't heard, so that ignorance would be no shelter anywhere in Aldric's kingdom.

The criers wore the king's colors—black and gold, black and gold everywhere now, since the old blue-and-silver banners of the House of Merren had come down two winters past—and they wore them well, new wool, new boots, fed men with fed voices. The centermost one unrolled a scroll sealed with wax the size of a plum, and the crowd hushed the way crowds do, that strange collective instinct toward silence even when silence is the last thing anyone wants.

"Hear ye," the man called out, and his voice carried off the cathedral stones and into the grey sky. "Hear ye. By grace of the throne and by the will of His Majesty King Aldric, First of His Name, Protector of the Realm, Keeper of the Peace—" the titles went on a while; usurpers always needed longer lists than kings born to it, Jonah thought, "—let it be known throughout the city of Greyharrow and all lands owing fealty, that a Great Audit shall be held."

A murmur moved through the crowd like wind through wheat. *Audit.* It was a clerk's word, a tax-man's word, and it sounded harmless, and everyone knew it wasn't going to be about taxes.

"Let every soul within these walls, of every age and station, present themselves for the Testing of the Gift, beginning on the first day of the coming month. Let none refuse. Let none delay. Let none conceal themselves or another." The crier's voice was flat, professional, bored almost, and somehow that made it worse. "Those found gifted shall be registered in the King's Ledger, that their talents may be numbered, ordered, and put to lawful use."

*Registered.*

The word landed in the crowd and sat there, and Jonah watched it land. He watched the baker beside him—a broad, floury man named Hesk whom Jonah had sold a fertility charm to two years back, a lump of clay and chicken bone worth four coppers—go very still. He watched a washerwoman pull her daughter closer by the shoulder. He watched old Tam Fitcher, the rag-and-bone man who slept under the aqueduct, mouth the word to himself like he was tasting it.

Everyone in Greyharrow knew what *registered* meant. Everyone had heard the stories drifting up the trade roads for a year now, from the eastern provinces where the Audit had already passed: the quiet carts, the doors marked in chalk, the families who woke to find a son or grandmother gone and no record that such a person had ever lived in the house at all. Registered. Numbered. Ordered. Put to use. The way you register cattle. The way you register weapons.

"Furthermore," the crier continued, and here his voice gained something, a relish he probably didn't know he had, "the Crown offers reward for information leading to the discovery of any gifted person concealing their nature. Ten crowns silver for each true naming."

Ten crowns. A year's wages for a laborer. Jonah felt the number move through the crowd like a current through water, felt people shift, felt eyes begin—just begin—to slide sideways, to measure neighbors. Beside him, Hesk the baker had stopped looking at the criers and started looking at the crowd, and his face had gone through fear into something harder and more calculating, and Jonah thought: *there it is.* Five years of careful mediocrity, five years of being nobody worth a second glance, and the whole architecture of it was dissolving around him in the space of six sentences, because Aldric had understood something Jonah's own discipline had been built on denying: that the cheapest way to find a hidden thing is not to search for it, but to make everyone around it want to find it for you.

The crier finished the formalities—the penalties for refusal, the dates, the districts—and rolled his scroll, and for a moment the square was silent except for the bells still tolling somewhere above, slower now, winding down.

Then a woman's voice, high and cracked, from somewhere near the fountain: "My boy's only seven!"

And a man's voice answering her, not unkindly but not gently either: "Then they'll test him and let him go, won't they? If he's got nothing in him." And then, quieter, in the tone of a man thinking aloud what everyone was thinking: "It's the ones with something in them we ought to be asking after."

The crowd broke apart after that, not all at once but in pieces, the way ice breaks, along lines no one could have drawn yesterday. Jonah stayed where he was a long time, letting the current flow around him, doing what he had trained himself to do for five years: nothing remarkable. A charm-seller listening to news. A man with nowhere better to be.

But inside, beneath the practiced stillness, everything in him was screaming.

* * *

He walked home the long way, through the fish market and up Cooper's Lane, because the long way gave him time to think and because habit told him never to take the same route twice when the city's mood was turning. Habit had kept him alive through things most Greyharrow folk couldn't imagine. Habit, and mediocrity, and the daily small surrender of a power that lived in him like a second heart.

Because that was the truth of Jonah Whitlock, the truth under the ink-stained fingers and the badly mended cuffs: he was a storm-caller. Not a hedge-mage with tricks for finding lost buttons, not a cunning-man with herbs and bluff. A storm-caller, blooded and trained, one of the last students of Master Corvane of the High Reaches, able to taste the pressure of the sky three days out and pull lightning down out of a clear noon if he had to. He had done it once, here, in his first frightened month in Greyharrow—a drunk with a knife in an alley, rain gathering out of a cloudless dark, thunder answering him like a dog hearing its name—and he had spent every day since making sure it would never happen again. He had learned to hold the gift down the way you hold a drowning man under: both hands, constantly, forever, knowing it was stronger than you and only waiting.

Five years. Five years of selling trinkets that did nothing, of laughing at magic in taverns louder than anyone, of flinching from his own reflection on stormy nights because the light in his eyes wasn't entirely his. Five years of a strategy with three legs: be boring, be useful, be invisible. And this morning, on the cathedral steps, he had watched the crown kick two of those legs away. Boring wouldn't matter when the Testing came—whatever the Testing was, and it would be thorough, because Aldric hadn't built a kingdom on carelessness. Useful wouldn't matter when ten crowns was on the table and every neighbor was a bounty. Which left invisible, and invisibility, Jonah knew better than anyone alive, was not a state but a discipline, and disciplines failed exactly when they were needed most.

He was halfway up Cooper's Lane when he saw the chalk.

A door—the door of the Weary Cartwright, a tavern he'd drunk in a hundred times, whose keeper, Marta, watered his ale and asked no questions—was marked. A single symbol in white chalk, waist-high, drawn neat and quick: a circle bisected by a line, with three small ticks beneath. Jonah had seen it before, in the stories from the east. The Audit wagons' mark. *Tested. Taken.* Except the Audit hadn't come to Greyharrow yet. The wagons weren't here. Which meant someone had drawn it themselves, tonight or at dawn, someone who wanted Marta gone before the crown ever arrived, someone who would collect their ten crowns on the strength of a lie if they had to.

Or maybe it wasn't a lie. Maybe Marta's grandmother had been a wise-woman. Maybe Marta herself stirred something into the stew besides salt. In Greyharrow, half the households kept some small inherited knack and called it luck or recipe or religion, and had for generations, and had mostly gotten away with it, because who cared about a woman whose bread rose better than yours? But the Audit didn't care about degrees. The ledger didn't weigh. It counted.

Jonah stood in front of the chalked door for a long moment, and the terrible thing, the thing he would remember afterward with shame, was that his first feeling was not grief for Marta. It was arithmetic. *If they're marking doors already, I have less than a month. If they're paying for names, I have less than that.*

His second feeling was worse: *I should wipe it off.* And his third, arriving on its heels like a shadow: *and if I do, whoever drew it will draw it again, and start wondering why a charm-seller cares.*

He walked on. He heard, behind him, a window open, and a voice call down the lane—casual, too casual—"Morning, Master Whitlock! Early today!" And he raised a hand and smiled the smile he kept for exactly this, and said, "Bells woke me. Same as everyone," and kept walking, and did not let his stride change, and hated himself with a clean and total efficiency that was almost restful.

* * *

The shop was cold. He lit the lamp, though the daylight was sufficient, because lamplight made him look ordinary, and swept the floor that didn't need sweeping, and laid out his stock with hands that wanted to shake and were not permitted to. Copper charms against headache. Tin medals for safe travel. Knotted threads for faithfulness, painted beads for good dreams, dried lavender sewn into squares of linen for melancholy. Nothing worked. That was the genius of the stock, the joke he alone was in on: every item was honestly worthless, and therefore honestly legal, and therefore the perfect camouflage for a man whose least thought could split the sky.

He was arranging the lavender squares for the third time when the bell over the door sang its flat little note, and Wren came in.

She came often enough that he knew her by more than sight: the young woman from Tanner's Row with the sharp chin and the ink-blot freckles and the way she looked at his wares like she was reading them rather than shopping for them. She bought small things, cheap things, always paying in exact copper, always glancing at the window first. Today she glanced at the window twice, and her cloak was buttoned wrong, one hole off, and there was a bruise-yellow smudge under one eye that she'd tried to powder over.

"Master Whitlock." Her voice was steady. Her hands, setting her coins on the counter, were not. "The dream-beads. Two strings, if you have them."

"I have them." He took the strings down slowly, giving his eyes an excuse to move, checking the street behind her in the dark glass of the window. Empty. Mostly. A figure across the lane, by the cooper's shuttered stall, standing in a way that suggested standing was its assignment. Grey coat. Unremarkable boots. The kind of man cities grew in cracks lately, the kind paid to watch and report and be forgotten. Jonah had catalogued three of them in his district over the past week and told himself each time that they were watching smugglers, debtors, anyone, everyone—that a man as boring as Jonah Whitlock would never make the list.

Now the list had a name. *The Great Audit.* Now the list was everyone.

"Bad dreams?" he asked, mild as milk, wrapping the beads in paper.

"Something like that." She took the packet and didn't leave. She stood there with her thumb moving over the paper's fold, back and forth, and Jonah saw that she was deciding something, and that deciding it was costing her. When she spoke again her voice had dropped to nearly nothing. "They say the Testing finds anything. Even little things. Even things you didn't know you had." Her eyes came up, sharp and frightened and—this was the part that caught him, that snagged in him like a hook—*guilty*. She looked guilty, this girl who bought dream-beads and paid in exact copper, guilty the way only the innocent can afford to look. "Is that true? You sell charms. You'd know what they can sense."

What could Jonah say? *Yes. They will find you, whatever you are, and I cannot protect you, because protecting you means showing them me.* What he said was: "The Testing is a scarecrow, mostly. Crowns love scarecrows. They get obedience for free." It was even partly true. It was also, he knew even as he said it, a lie he no longer believed, and Wren's eyes—sharp, frightened, guilty—told him she didn't believe it either.

Across the lane, the grey-coated man shifted his weight, and turned his head, and for one unguarded instant looked directly through the shop window at the two of them.

Wren left quickly after that, coins exact, thanks murmured, the bell singing flat behind her. Jonah stood in his empty shop among his honest worthless charms, and listened to the last of the assembly bells die away over the rooftops, and understood with a clarity that felt like falling that the five years were over. The strategy was dead. There was no version of the coming month in which careful mediocrity saved him; there was only flight, or fight, and both began with the same unbearable question—*what am I willing to lose?*

He was still standing there when he noticed the paper Wren had left behind, tucked under the edge of the coin tray where a receipt should be. Not a receipt. A scrap, torn from a cheaper page, covered in her quick slanted hand—or not her hand, quite. Sigils. Old ones, the pre-Compact script that predated the crown's ban by centuries, the kind that appeared in no book legally printed. She had copied them from somewhere, clumsily, hopefully, the way a child copies letters from a language overheard in dreams.

And Jonah, who had not let himself read a sigil in five years, who had trained his eyes to slide off such marks the way a horse slides off a snake, looked at them—and felt the storm under his skin turn over in its sleep.

One of the sigils he recognized. It was a binding-mark. A debtor's mark. The kind written into contracts between desperate mortals and older, patient things—the kind signed in a court beneath a mountain whose name Jonah had spent five years not saying even in the privacy of his skull.

It was signed with a name he almost knew.
