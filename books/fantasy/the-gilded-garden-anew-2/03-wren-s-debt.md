## 3. Wren's Debt

The rain had followed Wren across three districts, and she was beginning to suspect it was following her on purpose.

She told herself that was nonsense. Rain did not follow people. Rain fell because the sky was heavy and the river breathed its damp breath up through the cobbles of Greyharrow, and if the drops seemed to find her hood's every gap and seam, that was only the city's old grudge against anyone too poor for a roof that didn't leak. She had lived in Greyharrow five years now. She knew how the city felt about the poor, about women alone, about hedge-witches most of all—though no one in Greyharrow would use that last word aloud, not since the purges, not since the quiet carts began.

Still. The rain followed her.

It had started at the fish market, a single cold finger down the back of her collar while every other awning in the row stayed dry. It had thickened as she crossed Tanner's Bridge, drumming on the planks around her boots in a pattern too regular to be chance—*tap-tap-tap, pause, tap-tap-tap*—like something impatient being polite. And now, as she stood beneath the crooked eave of Whitlock's Charm Shop with her coins counted out in her palm and her heart hammering like a trapped bird, the rain had stopped entirely above her head, hanging there in the grey air as though waiting to see what she would do.

Wren did what she always did when the world behaved strangely: she pretended not to notice. Noticing was dangerous. Noticing was how they found you.

The bell over the shop door gave its cracked little jangle, and warmth folded around her—tallow smoke, dried lavender, the green smell of crushed herbs, and underneath it all something else, something she had never been able to name in five years of coming here. It smelled like the moment before lightning. Like the air holding its breath.

Jonah Whitlock looked up from his workbench, where he was doing whatever charm-sellers did with wire and river-stones and patience. He was a plain man, she'd always thought, deliberately so—the kind of face you forgot the moment you turned away, brown hair going grey at the temples, hands that moved with a carefulness that seemed almost apologetic. He kept his shop dim and his voice quieter than it needed to be. In a city that feared magic the way Greyharrow feared plague, he had made himself into a man it was difficult to be afraid of.

"Back again," he said. Not unkindly. "Third time this month."

"The mother-charm worked." Wren set her coins on the counter, arranging them by size because her fingers needed something orderly to do. "For Mrs. Halloway. Her fever broke two nights past."

Something flickered across Jonah's face—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. "Charms don't break fevers," he said. "I tell everyone that. I'm legally required to tell everyone that."

"You are legally required to say your charms are only trinkets. You are not required to believe it."

"Wren." He said her name carefully, the way a man sets down something fragile. "You shouldn't say things like that. Not here. Not anywhere."

"I know." She did know. That was the whole rotten architecture of her life in this city: she knew exactly which words were forbidden, which gestures, which glances. She had spent five years folding herself smaller and smaller, a hedge-witch pressed flat into the shape of an ordinary woman, and she had gotten so good at it that some mornings she woke and couldn't remember what her own magic had felt like before she'd buried it. Just that it had been small. Hearth-sparks and healing-hands and the ability to make cut flowers last three days longer than they should. Nothing worth a pyre. Nothing worth a cart.

And yet.

"I need another one," she said. "A stronger one. Or—" She heard her own voice drop, and hated herself for the risk even as she took it. "Or whatever you actually do, when the charms aren't enough."

The silence that followed lasted long enough for her to count four of her own heartbeats. Outside, the held-back rain tapped once against the window glass, *tap*, like a knock.

Jonah came around the counter slowly. He glanced past her shoulder at the street—at the grey light, the empty lane, the shuttered windows—and then he pulled the door shut behind her and turned the little brass lock, and Wren's stomach dropped through the floorboards, because locked doors went both ways.

"Who's watching you?" he asked quietly.

The question hit her like cold water. "No one. No one's—"

"There was a man outside my shop yesterday. Grey coat, court boots, standing under the sign of the Drowned Bell like he was waiting for someone who never came. Today there were two. They're not watching me, Wren. I've been nothing but boring for five years. Boring is armor." His eyes, which she had always thought of as mild, were anything but now. "They're watching you. So I'll ask again, and I need you to answer me honestly, because honest is the only thing that keeps people alive this season. Who is watching you, and why?"

And Wren—who had carried her secret alone for six years, who had told no living soul, who had learned that secrets kept you safe precisely because they weighed so much you could never run far with them—felt the whole weight shift in her chest and begin, terribly, to tip.

"It's not what you think," she said. "I haven't done anything. Not recently. Not—not in years. But I did do something, once, and I think—" Her voice failed. She tried again. "I think it finally caught up with me."

* * *

He made tea. Of course he did; it was that kind of confession, the kind that needed something warm in your hands or you'd never get the words out. He cleared the workbench of wire and stones, and Wren sat on the stool across from him in the lamplight with the cup cradled in her palms, and outside the window the rain still hung suspended in the air like a held breath, and neither of them remarked on it, though she saw him glance at it twice.

"When I was nineteen," she began, "I lived in Marrowdale. Little farming town upriver, before the crown cared much about the provinces. My grandmother taught me the craft—hearth-craft, mostly. Small things. You could be a hedge-witch in Marrowdale and people brought you bread for it instead of torches." She smiled, and it hurt. "There was a caravan accident on the river road. Cart went off the ferry landing in flood season. Three men pulled from the water, and two of them were fine, and one of them was dying."

She stared into her tea. The leaves swirled in patterns she refused to read.

"He'd taken water into his lungs, and fever set in by nightfall, and it wasn't a fever herbs could touch. It was the drowning itself, somehow—it had gotten into him, into his breath, like the river wouldn't give him back. I sat with him two days and two nights and everything I knew wasn't enough. His skin had gone grey as ash. And on the second night he stopped breathing, just for a moment, just long enough, and I panicked."

"What did you do?" Jonah asked. His voice was very soft.

"There's an old rite. Grandmother called it the Deep Bargain, and she said it the way you'd say *don't touch the stove*. The court beneath Mount Sereth—they keep a ledger, an old one, older than the kingdom maybe. A ledger of souls. And if you're desperate enough, and foolish enough, you can sell yours. One soul, entered in the book, in exchange for one miracle. Grandmother said the mountain always collects. She said no one who signed ever slept right again." Wren laughed, a thin, awful sound. "I was nineteen and a man was dying in front of me and I didn't care about sleeping."

She looked up, and met Jonah's eyes, and the lamplight between them seemed to bend.

"So I did it. There's a way—I won't say how, it doesn't matter—but you speak your name into the dark, and you offer it, and you name your price. And the mountain answers. Not in words. It's more like... like the whole night leans toward you. And then it's done, and you feel it, a kind of cold settling into your bones that never quite leaves, and somewhere far away a pen scratches your name onto a page." Her hands had begun to shake; she steadied them against the warm cup. "And the stranger breathed. Right there, in front of me, he drew a breath like a man surfacing from deep water, and by morning the grey was out of his skin, and three days later he walked out of Marrowdale and I never even learned his name."

The room was very quiet. Even the fire in the little stove seemed to have gone still, flames standing straight as painted ones.

"That was six years ago," Wren said. "And I told myself the mountain had forgotten, that small souls slip through cracks, that I'd gotten away with it. And then the criers came last week with the Great Audit, and I heard the word *registered*, and I thought—ledger. Ledger of souls. If Aldric's men are hunting the gifted, if they're compiling their lists..." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "They won't have to look for me, Jonah. My name's already written down. Somewhere under that mountain there's a page with *Wren* on it, and all they have to do is turn to it."

She had expected many reactions—pity, fear, perhaps the worst one, the slow backing away of a man realizing what sat at his table. What she had not expected was the look that actually crossed Jonah Whitlock's face.

He had gone white. Not pale—white, the color of paper, of bone, and his hand had come up to grip the edge of the workbench hard enough that his knuckles stood out like knots in wood. He was staring at her as though she had said something impossible, something that had reached inside his chest and taken hold of something there and squeezed.

"Jonah?"

"A stranger," he said. His voice came out strange, flattened, like a man reading words he didn't understand. "Drowning. On the river road near Marrowdale. Six years ago."

"Yes. A merchant, I think, or a courier—his pack had washed away, we never—"

"What month?" The question cracked out of him like a whip. "What month, Wren?"

"I—high summer. The flood season. Why does it—"

But she saw it now, saw it the way you see a pattern in spilled grain, suddenly and all at once and forever. Jonah Whitlock had come to Greyharrow five years ago, a man with no history the neighbors could agree on. He flinched, very slightly, at running water—she had noticed it once at the millrace and filed it away as nothing. He kept his sleeves long in all seasons. And he had a scar, high on his forearm, that she had glimpsed a dozen times and never asked about, a scar shaped like branching frost, like the mark water leaves when it takes hold of something and lets it go.

"Oh," Wren said. The sound was very small. "Oh, no."

"Wren—"

"It was you." The teacup rattled against its saucer because her hands would not stop shaking now, would not be stilled by any warmth in the world. "The dying stranger. The man I sold my soul for. It was *you*."

He didn't deny it. That was the thing she would remember afterward, in all the long dark nights that came after—that he didn't deny it, didn't scramble for a lie, didn't do any of the ten thousand easy cowardly things a man might do. He just sat there in the lamplight with his guilt rising off him like heat off summer stone, and said, "I never knew who paid for me. I woke up in a farmhouse with my lungs full of river and my life somehow back, and I never knew. I swear to you, Wren, I never knew."

"And I never knew it was you." She was standing now; she didn't remember standing. "Six years. Six years I've carried this cold in my bones, this—this *debt*, this page with my name on it in some black book under a mountain, and the whole time the man I bought was selling charms three streets from my room." A laugh tore out of her, jagged. "Do you know what the Audit means? Do you understand what happens now? When Aldric's clerks open that ledger to build their lists, my name will be *right there*. Written in ink. Confessed. Signed. I won't even have to be caught. I'm already caught. I've been caught for six years and I didn't—"

"Wren. Listen to me." He rose too, and his hands hovered near hers without touching, as if he understood that touching her right now might shatter something. "Listen. This is not your ending. This is not—"

"Don't you dare comfort me." Her voice broke on it. "Don't you *dare*. You want to know the joke, Jonah? The beautiful, perfect joke? I gave up my soul to save a stranger, and the stranger grew up to be the one person in Greyharrow who might have helped me, and neither of us knew, and now it's too late, because the criers have already read the proclamation and the wagons are already on the roads and there is nowhere in this whole godless city for a girl whose name is written in a mountain—"

"*Then we go to the mountain.*"

The words dropped into the room like a stone into a well. Wren stopped. The suspended rain outside tapped the window, once, twice, as if applauding.

"What?"

"We go to the mountain." Jonah's voice had changed. The mildness was gone from it, the careful mediocrity, the whole five-year costume of the harmless charm-seller—and underneath it she heard something she had only ever felt in the air before storms, a low gathering pressure, a vastness held barely leashed. "If your name is in that ledger, then it can be found. And if it can be found, it can be reached. And if it can be reached—" His jaw tightened. "Then it can be stolen back."

"You're talking about Mount Sereth. The court of King Aldric. The most guarded place in the seven kingdoms. You're talking about walking into the throat of the machine that eats the gifted and asking it nicely to give one soul back."

"I'm talking about a debt," Jonah said quietly. "Mine, not yours. You paid for my life with everything you had, and you didn't even know whose life it was, and I have been walking around for six years breathing air I never paid for." He looked at her then, fully, and for the first time she saw that his mildness had always been a mask worn over something enormous, the way a calm sea wears a mask over its depths. "I don't know yet how. But I know this, Wren: I am not going to let them turn your page. Whatever it costs. Whatever it takes. That debt gets settled."

Outside, the rain that had followed her all day finally fell—all at once, in a single great sheet, hammering the cobbles like a thousand small drums—and Wren stood in the lamplight of a locked shop with her soul forfeit and her heart in ruins, and felt something new take root in the wreckage. Not hope, not quite. Something harder. Something with edges.

Because if Jonah Whitlock meant what he said—if he truly meant to go to the mountain—then he would need help. And Wren knew, in the way hedge-witches know things, in the marrow-deep way her grandmother's craft had taught her to know things, exactly where a man might find smugglers willing to move what couldn't legally be moved.

"You'll need a guide," she said. "And you'll need more than charms to pay for one."

Jonah raised an eyebrow. "You know someone?"

"I know," Wren said, "of a woman named Odessa Vane. And I think you should hear what she charges."
