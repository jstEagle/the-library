## 4. The Smuggler's Table

The Saltcellar did not advertise itself, which was the first thing Jonah liked about it. It sat at the end of a crooked lane behind the tanneries, where the smell of lye and rotting hides kept the curious away and the desperate indifferent. Its sign was a wooden board painted with a fish so faded it might have been a boot. The door had no handle on the outside; you knocked in the rhythm of the tide, three slow, two fast, and if you got it wrong you were either a stranger or an idiot, and both were turned away.

Jonah stood across the lane for a long while, hood up against a drizzle that had no storm in it—just Greyharrow's ordinary grey weeping, the city's own weather, flat and charmless as everything else here. He had dressed down for the occasion: patched coat, cracked boots, the satchel of charms left at home except for one piece he'd brought deliberately, wrapped in cloth at the bottom like contraband, which in a sense it was.

He had asked after Odessa Vane in three taverns and two pawnshops before someone gave him the tide-knock without being asked twice. That was how her reputation worked, he was learning. People didn't speak her name loudly. They passed it along like something warm held in cupped hands, and they looked over their shoulders when they did.

*Ungifted*, they called the ones she'd moved. A bitter joke of a word. In the years before Aldric sealed the borders, when the purges in the capital were still called *relocations*, Odessa Vane had run gifted families out of Greyharrow in fish barrels and grain wagons and once, famously, inside a traveling menagerie's fake unicorn. She'd charged them nothing she couldn't justify and lied to everyone about everything, and then one day she'd stopped, closed the route, and gone back to moving brandy and silk like any other smuggler with a debt to the harbor master. No one knew why she stopped. No one asked.

Jonah knocked the tide-rhythm. The door opened a hand's width, revealing one eye and the business end of a crossbow.

"I'm looking for passage," he said.

"Passage is a broad word."

"Mount Sereth," Jonah said. "And back, if there is a back."

The eye studied him. It belonged to a woman perhaps fifty, weathered brown as old rope, with grey shot through black hair cropped short enough to be practical and not short enough to be humble. She looked at him the way he imagined she looked at cargo: estimating weight, value, and how much trouble he'd be if the hold took water.

"You're the charm-seller off Tanner's Row," she said. "Whitlock. You sold my dockhand's wife a luck-charm last spring. Charged her four coppers."

"It was a good charm."

"It was a painted river-stone."

"It was a good stone," Jonah said. "She's still lucky, isn't she?"

Something that was almost a smile crossed the woman's face and died there. "That's Odessa's table you're asking for. I'm just the door." She stepped back anyway. "In. Hands where I can see them. Touch nothing."

* * *

The interior of the Saltcellar was dim, low-ceilinged, and smelled of tar, salt pork, and old smoke. A dozen tables, half of them occupied by men and women who stopped talking the moment Jonah entered and resumed it the moment they decided he was nobody—which, he noted, took only seconds. Five years of careful mediocrity had made him excellent at being nobody. He almost resented how well it worked.

Odessa Vane sat at the table farthest from the fire, alone, working through a ledger of her own—the mundane kind, columns of figures in a sharp hand. She was smaller than her reputation, narrow-shouldered, with a face that might have been pretty before the sea and the years had planed it down to function. Her eyes were the color of shallow water over sand, pale and quick, and they went to Jonah's hands first, then his face, then his hands again.

"Charm-seller," she said, without looking up. "Sit. Don't sell me anything."

"That's rather the opposite of why I'm here."

"Mm." She finished her column, blotted it, closed the ledger, and folded her hands atop it. Up close, Jonah could see the fine web of scars on her knuckles, rope-burns and worse. "I know who you are, Whitlock. Or I know what the Row says you are, which is a man who sells painted stones to frightened women and never once, in five years, has anyone caught him doing actual magic. Either you're the most disciplined fraud in Greyharrow, or you're something else wearing mediocrity like a coat." She tilted her head. "Which is it?"

Jonah sat. "Does it matter?"

"It matters enormously. If you're a fraud, I throw you out and go back to my figures. If you're something else—" her pale eyes flicked toward the room, then back, voice dropping, "—then you're either very brave or very stupid to come to me, because half the people in this room would sell you to the crown for less than the price of your coat."

"And the other half?"

"The other half already work for me." She leaned back. "Say your piece. Quickly."

So he said it. Not all of it—he kept Wren's name out of it, kept the girl herself out of it, a small dishonesty that sat in his chest like a swallowed hook—but enough. The Audit. The lists. The mountain court that kept a ledger of souls, compiling names for the king's hunters. And a name in that ledger that needed to come out before the Audit wagons reached Greyharrow.

"I need passage to Mount Sereth," he finished. "For myself and possibly two others. Quietly. Ahead of the crown's own travel."

Odessa was quiet for a long moment. Around them the tavern murmured on, oblivious or performing oblivion. When she spoke again, her voice had changed—not softer, but more careful, the way ice changes underfoot before it gives.

"Do you know why I stopped running the ungifted routes?"

"No."

"Because the last family I moved out—a tanner, his wife, a boy of six—the boy was gifted. Fire, small stuff, sparks when he cried. His parents paid me everything they had to get him over the border ahead of the inquisitors." Her thumb traced one of the scars on her knuckle, back and forth, an unconscious motion. "We made it to the coast. My ship was searched at anchor. I don't know who talked. The inquisitors took the boy off my deck in front of his parents, and do you know what they charged *me* with? Smuggling. Contraband. They wrote him up in their reports as *goods*, Whitlock. A child, listed between a crate of unlicensed brandy and a bolt of stolen silk." Her eyes came up, and they were not wet, but they were very bright, the way metal is bright. "I spent eight months in a crown cell for it. When I came out, I decided the world could keep its miracles. I move silk now. Silk doesn't scream."

The fire popped. Somewhere behind them a dice game resumed its low clatter.

"So you'll say no," Jonah said.

"I haven't said anything yet." She unfolded her hands. "What are you offering? Because passage to Sereth isn't silk-run money. That road is watched now, since the Audit was proclaimed. I'd be risking my ship, my crew, my neck, and every favor I've banked in twenty years. What do you have that's worth that?"

And here it was—the thing he had rehearsed on the walk over, turning it over in his mind like a coin he wasn't sure was gold. He leaned forward and lowered his voice until it was barely above the fire's crackle.

"My gift," he said. "Storm-calling. Full strength. I'm told the court pays fortunes for bound callers—they use them to clear the passes, drive the war-fleets. Sell me. Hand me to Aldric's recruiters yourself, collect the bounty, and I'll go willingly, quietly, no escape attempts. One soul, freely given, worth more than any cargo you'll carry this year."

He had expected her to consider it. He had expected greed, or fear, or the quick arithmetic of a smuggler weighing a hold full of trouble against a fortune. What he had not expected was the way her face went still—not thoughtful-still but *frozen*-still, like a bird watching a snake.

Then she laughed. It was a terrible sound, short and hard, with nothing in it of humor.

"A curse," she said. "You're offering to pay me in a curse."

"It's the most valuable thing I own."

"That's exactly what makes it worthless." She leaned across the table, and this close he could see the exhaustion underneath the iron in her, years of it, packed down like ballast. "You think I watched them list a child as *goods* because I hate magic? I watched it because I hate what power does when it gets counted. Your gift isn't currency, Whitlock. It's the reason there's a ledger at all. Every horror coming out of that mountain started with someone deciding a soul was a thing you could price." She sat back. "No. Find another way to pay me."

"Then I have no other way."

"Then you have no passage." She reopened her ledger, picked up her pen. Conversation over, as far as she was concerned.

Jonah didn't move. He had walked into this room with one coin and been told it was counterfeit, and beneath the disappointment he felt something worse stirring—something that felt humiliatingly like relief. Part of him, the part that had spent five years pretending, had hoped she'd refuse. Now the hope was dead and he simply needed another way, and there wasn't one, and Wren's name sat in a vault beneath a mountain growing heavier by the day.

"There's a debt," he said quietly. "Isn't there. Everyone says you stopped the routes overnight. Debts don't stop overnight unless they're paid or they break you."

The pen stopped moving.

"I'm not asking you to tell me," Jonah said. "I'm telling you I know there's something. And I'm telling you what I know about ledgers—that the court keeps one, that names can be written in it and struck from it, that souls get bought and sold and entered like cargo." He paused. "If there's a name in that mountain that matters to you, I'm going there anyway. With passage or without. I will find that ledger if it's the last thing I do, and whatever name you're carrying—if it's in those pages—I'll strike it myself, or die trying, and it won't cost you a single favor you haven't already spent."

The silence stretched so long that the dice game behind them ended, and the room began to empty, and still Odessa Vane did not move. When she finally set down the pen, her hand was steady, but her voice, when it came, was not entirely.

"The boy," she said. "The tanner's son. They didn't execute him. Gifted children go to the mountain. For *training*." The word came out like something spat. "His mother writes me letters through a cousin of a cousin. Begging letters. Asking if I've heard anything, if I know anyone who's seen the mountain's rolls, if her boy is even alive." She met Jonah's eyes. "I don't know. Twenty years of contacts and I don't know, because nobody comes *out* of Sereth. Nobody has ever come out."

"I'm going to change that."

"You're going to *try*." She snorted, but there was no contempt in it—or rather, the contempt was aimed somewhere past him, at the mountain, at the king, at twenty years of a trade that had taught her exactly what the world cost. Then she pulled a fresh page toward her and began writing, fast, in that sharp hand. "Terms. Listen carefully, because I'll only say them once. I provide passage for you and whoever you're bringing—quietly, ahead of the crown's traffic, my usual routes. You provide nothing. No payment, no bounty, no heroic offers of your cursed self. In exchange, you get my brother Bram, who knows the mountain roads better than any crown guide alive, and you get me, because I don't trust anyone else to watch you. And when we reach Sereth, if there's a ledger, you look for a name. *Tam Vane.* Age eleven when he went in. Six years ago."

"Tam," Jonah repeated.

"If he's alive, he comes out with us. If he's dead—" her pen paused, just barely, "—I want to see the entry. I want to see it in their own ink, in their own hand, so I can stop wondering. Wondering is worse than grief, Whitlock. Grief ends. Wondering just gets heavier." She signed the page with a flourish that was almost violent and pushed it across the table. "That's the price. That's the whole price. Do we have terms?"

Jonah looked at the page. It was, he realized, the first honest contract he'd ever been offered—no coin, no barter, just two people trading the same desperation back and forth until it became something like trust. He thought of Wren's face in the market, the fear she'd tried to hide behind defiance. He thought of a boy listed between brandy and silk.

"We have terms," he said, and signed.

Odessa took the page back, blew on the ink, and tucked it away inside her coat. Then she stood, and the tiredness fell away from her like a dropped cloak, and Jonah saw for the first time the woman the harbor masters feared—quick, decisive, already three moves ahead.

"Be at the tannery gate tomorrow, an hour before dawn. Bring your two others, bring nothing you can't run with, and tell no one—not the girl, not the priest, not your own reflection." She paused at the edge of the firelight, and for a moment the iron in her face shifted into something older and wearier. "One more thing, charm-seller. Whatever you're hiding—and you're hiding something, I've watched liars for twenty years and you have the posture of a man carrying a secret in his teeth—whatever it is, decide tonight whether you're going to tell it on the road. Because secrets rot faster in company, and if yours breaks open at the wrong moment, it won't just drown you. It'll drown all of us."

She didn't wait for an answer. She was already moving toward the door, calling orders to the remaining drinkers in a voice that brooked no argument, and Jonah sat alone at the table with the fire dying and the taste of ink on his tongue.

Tomorrow, an hour before dawn. Two others—Wren, and whoever else he could gather by then, though God knew who. And a secret in his teeth that already tasted of copper.

Outside, the drizzle had thickened into true rain, and for the first time in five years, Jonah felt it calling to him like a dog hearing its name—and for the first time, he let himself imagine answering.
