## 5. What the Rain Remembers

The rain had been threatening all day, and Jonah had spent every hour of it pretending not to notice.

That was the cruelty of Greyharrow's weather—it spoke to him. Not in words, never in words, but in pressures and promises, a low conversation conducted along his spine like fingers drawn over harp strings. A gray morning meant the clouds were gathering their courage over the western hills. The taste of ozone on the wind at midday meant they were arguing about where to break. And by late afternoon, when the air went heavy and sweet and still, he knew—they were waiting for him. They were always, in some patient and terrible way, waiting for him.

Five years of practice had taught him to let them wait.

He walked at the rear of Odessa Vane's little company as it threaded through the tanner's quarter toward the south gate, hood up, hands in his pockets, a charm-seller like any other charm-seller who happened to be leaving town with two smugglers, a hedge-witch, and a defected royal scribe. Bram walked ahead of him, broad as a door and nearly as talkative, his hand resting on the cart's rail. Wren sat among the crates with her knees drawn up, turning one of Jonah's trinkets over in her fingers—a copper disc stamped with a spiral that meant nothing and cost three coppers. Pell brought up the middle, sweating despite the cool, his scribe's grey cloak folded away in his pack because a scribe's colors would buy passage at a checkpoint or a knife in the dark, depending on who was asking.

And Odessa rode point, as she always did, straight-backed on the cart bench, reading the street the way other people read ledgers.

"South gate's got a double post," she said without turning around. "Saw it from the roof this morning. Crown men, not city watch. That means lists."

"Lists," Jonah repeated.

"Names checked against names." She said it lightly, the way she said everything, but her eyes flicked back to him once—quick, measuring. "You four are on somebody's paper by now. Maybe not spelled right. Maybe not yet. But paper has a way of catching up."

The cart rattled over a drain grate. Wren looked up at Jonah, and there was a question in her face she'd been carrying since the night at her tenement, since the moment he'd told her to pack one bag and trust him and asked no questions of his own. He hadn't told her why the Audit frightened him particularly. He hadn't told her what he was. Some debts you pay in silence, he'd thought then. Now silence felt less like payment and more like theft.

"Checkpoint at the gate," Odessa went on. "So we don't use the gate. There's a drainage cut under the old wall east of the chandlers' row—Bram and I used it during the refugee years. Comes out past the toll houses, into the fen road."

"And if they've found it?" Pell asked quietly.

"Then we're wet, disappointed, and looking for another way." Odessa shrugged. "But crown men think in doors and gates. They count what can be counted. It's their whole religion." Her mouth curved, thin and humorless. "Mine too, come to that."

Jonah watched the sky over the rooftops. The clouds had finished their argument. They had settled low and dark over the western hills like a congregation settling into pews, and beneath them the air had gone so still that smoke from the chimneys rose in perfectly straight lines, as if the whole city were holding its breath.

They know, he thought. Not the crown men. The clouds. Something in me has already started answering them, and I haven't given permission.

* * *

The drainage cut smelled of a decade of tannery runoff and older things besides, and it was exactly as wide as Bram's shoulders and no wider. They went through in single file, bent double, Wren's small lantern shielded behind her cupped palm, and Jonah spent the length of it cataloguing the ways his body had learned to be ordinary: breathe shallow, walk flat, want nothing. For five years those disciplines had been as natural to him as his own heartbeat. Tonight they felt like a suit sewn for a younger man.

They came out through a grate into a ditch choked with reeds, and the fen road unrolled before them under a sky the color of a bruise going green. Behind them, Greyharrow's walls stood black against the last light. Ahead, the road ran flat and empty toward the hills and the free cities beyond them, and Jonah allowed himself—one breath only—the feeling of having slipped a knot.

Odessa did not allow herself anything. She stood in the ditch with the water soaking her boots and listened—not to the road, but to the town—and Jonah saw the moment her listening was rewarded. Her head turned. Southward. Toward the gate.

"Lanterns," she said. "Four of them. Moving fast along the wall."

"They've seen us?" Pell's voice cracked on the second word.

"No. They've seen *something*." She was already moving, hauling herself up the ditch bank, waving the others forward. "Cart first. Move, move—if they reach the toll houses before we clear the rise, we're a story someone tells later."

They ran. Or the cart ran, Bram hauling it with a strength that seemed to embarrass him whenever anyone noticed, wheels grinding through the mud of the verge while the rest of them pushed and stumbled alongside. The rise ahead was gentle, treeless, crowned with a ruined shepherd's cot that would put a half-mile of dead ground between them and the road. Half a mile. Jonah measured distances in heartbeats now, and he had perhaps sixty of them left.

The lanterns came off the wall. He heard them without looking—boots on stone, the jingle of a man running with a polearm, and underneath it, fainter, worse: a sound like a struck tuning fork, high and pure and wrong. Pell heard it too. He went white.

"That's a resonance bell," he said. "Court issue. They ring them near suspected gifted. If there's talent within thirty paces, the tone—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. The tone changed, climbing, sharpening, finding its note like a hound finding scent.

"It's found something," Wren said. She had stopped running. She was staring at her own hands as though they belonged to a stranger. "It's found *me*, hasn't it. My little workings—I light candles, I mend fevers, it's nothing, it's *nothing*—"

"It's not you," Jonah said.

The words came out of him before he understood them, and when he understood them, it was already too late to take them back, because the bell had swung its attention, and everyone in the company had turned to look at him, and the sky chose that moment to answer.

* * *

Later—he would think about this later, in the years after, when people asked him how it felt to call a storm for the first time in five years—he would say that it felt like a door opening in a house he'd been told had burned down.

In the moment, it felt like drowning in reverse.

The pressure that had been building along his spine all day broke all at once, and the clouds over the western hills came down to meet him. Not drifted. *Came.* They poured over the ridge like ink poured into water, swallowing the last violet of the sunset, and the temperature dropped so fast that Wren's breath plumed white, and the first thunder rolled not across the sky but up out of the ground, out of the stones of the road, out of Jonah's own chest, as if the storm had been standing behind him all evening with its hand on his shoulder, waiting to be introduced.

"*Jonah*," Odessa said, and it wasn't a question anymore.

"Get them behind the cot," he said. His voice sounded strange to him. Layered. As if someone were speaking just beneath it. "All of you. Whatever happens, stay down and don't run for the cart until I say."

"You can't fight six crown men with weather—"

"I'm not going to fight them." He was already walking away from her, out into the open road, arms loose at his sides, and the rain arrived in earnest now, fat drops the size of coins, then sheets of it, a curtain falling between the company and the world. "I'm going to make them wish they'd stayed home."

The checkpoint riders came around the toll houses at a canter, six of them, cloaked against the rain, the resonance bell swinging from a frame on the lead rider's saddle and shrieking its bright note into the storm. Jonah stood in the middle of the road and let them see him. Hood down. Hands open. An ordinary man caught in bad weather, which was, in fairness, exactly what he appeared to be.

"Halt! In the king's name!" The lead rider hauled up ten paces away, visor streaming. "Travelers on the fen road after curfew—you'll come back to the gate for questioning. All of you. Where are the others?"

"There are no others," Jonah said. "There's just me. And the rain."

The rider frowned. The bell shrieked. And Jonah, who had spent five years teaching himself to be a man with no more power than a damp match, reached out with the thing he had spent those same five years pretending not to be, and took hold of the storm the way a musician takes hold of an instrument he built with his own hands.

He did not strike them. He wanted that remembered, afterward, by whoever asked—he did not strike them. He simply *asked* the lightning to go elsewhere, and elsewhere was everywhere except the road. Bolts came down in the fields to either side, in the fen, on the toll house roof, close enough to turn the rain to steam and light the riders' faces white as bone. The horses screamed and bunched. The bell's note shattered into discord. Thunder followed each stroke so fast and so hard that the ground itself seemed to flinch, and the six riders found themselves at the center of a ring of dancing fire with a calm, soaked charm-seller standing in the middle of it, watching them with an expression that was not anger and not threat but something far more unsettling: recognition. Pity, almost. The look of a man greeting old friends he wished had stayed strangers.

"Go back," Jonah said, and the wind carried his voice into their helmets without effort, intimate as a whisper against the ear. "Tell them whatever you like. Tell them the storm rose sudden. Tell them God was in it. But if you follow this road tonight, the next bolt won't fall in a field."

They believed him. He could see it land in them, one face at a time—the animal certainty that arrives ahead of thought. The lead rider wrenched his horse around. They went, at a gallop that quickly became a rout, bells and lanterns and polearms abandoned to the mud, and Jonah stood alone in the downpour and watched them go, and felt—

Everything.

That was the truth of it, the truth he would carry like a coal in his chest for the rest of his life: it felt like *everything*. Five years of held breath leaving his body in a single exhalation. Five years of mediocrity, of careful dullness, of selling trinkets to frightened women and calling the discipline invisibility when it was really amputation. The storm moved through him and he was not diminished by it, he was *completed* by it, every nerve singing a chord he'd been forbidden to play, and some traitorous part of him laughed out loud into the rain, wild and young and utterly undone.

And underneath the laughter, quiet as a debt collector at a funeral, another voice said: *they saw you.*

Not the riders. Riders could be doubted; riders could be bribed; riders could tell stories about weather. But behind him, sheltering under the broken wall of the shepherd's cot, four people had watched a man call lightning out of a clear intention, and there was no doubting that, and no buying it, and no forgetting it.

He turned and walked back through the rain, and the rain parted around him slightly, politely, the way a crowd parts, and he hated that most of all—that even the water knew him now.

* * *

They didn't speak until the cart was moving again, creaking south through the fen road's mud with the storm grumbling off westward like a dog settling back onto its porch. Wren had wrapped him in the dry blanket from the crate without a word. Bram drove. Pell stared at his own hands as if recalculating every document he'd ever filed. And Odessa sat across from Jonah in the cart bed, arms crossed, studying him with the expression of a merchant who has just discovered the coin in her purse is worth ten times its stamp.

"Well," she said finally.

"Well."

"You told me you were a charm-seller."

"I am a charm-seller."

"You told me your gift was *small*." Her voice stayed level, conversational, which somehow made it worse. "Your exact words, Whitlock, sitting at my table three nights ago, were 'enough to be dangerous to myself, not enough to matter to anyone else.' I build my plans on words like that. I build *routes* on words like that. And then on the first night out, you pull a storm out of the sky like drawing a knife, in front of a court bell, in front of witnesses, and now every list in Greyharrow has a new entry being written on it in a very expensive hand."

"I know."

"Do you?" She leaned forward. In the dark, lit only by Wren's shielded lantern, her eyes were the color of wet slate. "Because here is what I know now that I didn't know at my table. A man with a small gift hides. A man with *that* doesn't hide for five years unless he's hiding from something specific. And a man who can do what you did tonight isn't cargo I'm smuggling, Odessa-style, for coin or favor. He's a weapon walking around loose in my cart, and weapons change the price of everything."

"Odessa—" Wren started.

"Stay out of it, girl. This is between the weather-witch and me." But her tone gentled by a degree, and she never took her eyes off Jonah. "So. Recalculate for me. What am I carrying?"

Jonah pulled the blanket tighter. The exhilaration was still in him—he could feel it humming in his teeth, in his fingertips, a festival that refused to end—but beneath it the exposure had begun to set in like cold, and he understood that there was no path back to the small life now. The door he'd opened couldn't be closed. He had been a man-shaped shadow for five years, and shadows, once seen, become men whether they want to or not.

"You're carrying a storm-caller," he said. "Trained. Full discipline, before the purges. Master Corvane's last student to walk away." He watched the name land—Pell flinched; even Bram's shoulders shifted on the bench. "And you're carrying the reason Aldric wants the ledger read name by name. Because men like me are why he's afraid. And men like me are what he's building that mountain weapon to kill."

Silence, except for the wheels and the rain easing off.

"That's worth a great deal," Odessa said at last, slowly, "to the right buyer."

"It's worth a great deal to the wrong ones, too. That's rather the problem."

A ghost of a smile crossed her face and vanished. "Yes. It usually is." She sat back, and Jonah watched her do it—watched the recalculation happen behind her eyes, sums shifting, routes redrawing, the smuggler's eternal arithmetic adjusting itself around a new and terrible variable. When she spoke again, her voice had the flatness she reserved for decisions already made. "New terms. We move at night. We avoid every checkpoint, every garrison town, every road with a bell on it—which means the long way, the fen way, the way nobody takes because nobody sane takes it. And you—" she pointed one finger at him, "—you don't call anything else out of the sky unless we're dying. Do you understand me? Not inconvenient. Not frightened. *Dying.* Because the next time you light up that sky, Whitlock, it won't be six riders who see it. It'll be the mountain. And the mountain sends things that don't gallop away."

"The mountain," Jonah said quietly. "Mount Sereth."

Something flickered across Odessa's face—there and gone, fast, but Jonah had spent five years learning to read what people hid, and he caught it. Guilt, maybe. Or grief. Or a debt of her own, riding in the cart beside them like an unseen passenger.

"We'll get there when we get there," was all she said.

Wren shifted closer to him in the dark, shoulder touching his, and said nothing at all. But her hand found the edge of his blanket and held it, a small anchor-weight, and Jonah realized she was trembling—not from fear of him, he thought, or not only that, but from the memory of the lightning, the beauty of it, the terrible ease. From seeing, perhaps, the shape of the man behind the charm-seller, and understanding dimly that the shape explained everything: the caution, the kindness, the way he'd looked at her that first night in his shop like a man recognizing a fellow survivor of a country that no longer existed.

Ahead, the fen road bent south into darkness, toward the hills, toward the free cities, toward the mountain where a ledger held her name in letters she couldn't read and his name in letters he hadn't yet seen. The rain had stopped. The clouds were breaking apart, letting through ragged stars.

And far to the north, faint as a memory, Jonah felt—or imagined he felt—a pressure against the edges of his gift. Deliberate. Patient. Searching. Like fingers drawn along a harp string, testing which one sang.

Someone else was calling storms tonight. And they were getting closer.
