## 6. The Company of the Long Road

Odessa Vane had learned to read a company the way other people read weather. Five years of moving refugees through the Greyharrow marshes taught her that a group was never really a group until it had survived its first bad night—until hunger and fear and the dark had pressed everyone close enough to smell each other's secrets. Until then, they were only strangers walking in the same direction.

Four days out from Greyharrow, she still wasn't sure what she had.

She sat with her back against the wagon's wheel, a bowl of barley soup cooling in her hands, and watched the fire do its slow work on them all. Bram sat across the flames, mending a harness strap with the patient, wordless concentration he brought to everything. He'd spoken eleven words today. She'd counted. Her brother kept his own ledger, though his entries were silence.

Pell the scribe sat nearest the light, as if proximity to flame were a habit of ink and paper rather than flesh. He'd spread a scrap of oilcloth over his knees and was drawing on it with a stub of charcoal—the corridors beneath Mount Sereth, again, from memory. He drew them every night. Odessa suspected it was less for their benefit than for his own: a man retracing the shape of the cage he'd lived in, trying to understand how he'd ever fit inside it.

And at the far edge of the firelight, where the shadows pooled thickest, Jonah Whitlock sat sharpening a knife he did not need to sharpen, while Wren watched him with the particular stillness of someone counting another person's breaths.

Odessa drank her soup and made her assessments.

The girl was trouble. Not the loud kind—Wren hadn't raised her voice once in four days—but the kind that settled into a company like damp into plaster. She was twenty, maybe twenty-one, with ink-stained fingers and a hedge-witch's furtive competence: she could find water in dry ground, quiet a fever, charm a horse into calm. Small magics. The kind that got people taken by quiet carts. And she carried herself like someone who had already been caught once and hadn't fully escaped it.

The storm-caller was worse trouble. Odessa had seen what he could do at the checkpoint outside Harrowgate—had stood three wagons away and watched him pull lightning out of a cloudless dusk sky like a man drawing a sword he'd sworn never to draw again. It should have terrified her. It had terrified her. But underneath the terror there had been something else, something she'd spent four days refusing to name, because naming it would mean admitting she'd already decided to keep him.

*You're getting soft,* she told herself, and ate her soup.

"You're drawing it wrong," Bram said.

Everyone looked up. Eleven words, and now twelve more. Pell blinked at him. "Wrong?"

"The third corridor." Bram didn't look up from his harness strap. "It bends north before the gatehouse. You've drawn it straight."

Pell stared at the charcoal lines, and Odessa saw something move behind his eyes—not embarrassment but a colder thing, the look of a man discovering that memory is a liar. "I walked that corridor every day for six years," he said quietly.

"Memory bends too," Bram said, and went back to his strap.

That was the most her brother had said in two days, and he'd spent it on a stranger's map. Odessa filed that away with everything else. Bram trusted no one; that was doctrine. But he'd corrected Pell's map because a wrong map could get them killed, and because—though he'd deny it under torture—he'd already started keeping these people alive in his head, the way you keep a fire banked overnight.

"Here." Wren crossed the firelight suddenly, kneeling beside Pell, her braid swinging. She took the charcoal from his hand without asking. "If the corridor bends north, then the servants' gate can't be where you said. It has to be—" She sketched quickly, tongue between her teeth, and Odessa watched Pell's face change as she worked: suspicion giving way to something almost like relief. A scribe who'd defected from Aldric's court, carrying six years of guilt and a head full of forbidden architecture, and a hedge-witch who thought in lines the way he did.

"There," Wren said, sitting back. "Does that feel right?"

Pell looked at the map for a long moment. "Yes," he said. "God help me, yes."

Odessa set down her empty bowl. "Then we're agreed on the approach. We come at the mountain from the west, through the pass, and we use your gate. We don't touch the main roads, we don't touch the pilgrim trails, and we are inside and out before anyone in that court knows we were ever born." She looked around the fire. "Questions?"

"I have one." Wren hadn't moved from Pell's side. She was looking at Jonah now, and her voice was mild as milk. "Just one."

Jonah's knife stopped moving against the whetstone.

"Why does he flinch," Wren said, "every time someone mentions my name?"

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, an owl called once and went silent, as if even the night wanted to hear the answer.

Odessa kept her face still. *There it is.* Four days she'd felt the fault line running under this company, and here was the hedge-witch putting her finger directly on it, gentle as a physician palpating a wound.

"I don't flinch," Jonah said.

"You do." Wren rose, brushing off her knees, and came around the fire to stand over him. She wasn't angry. That was the unnerving part. She spoke with the flat clarity of someone stating observations about the weather. "When I introduced myself the first morning, your hands went still. When Pell asked how we knew each other, you answered before I could, and you got it wrong—you said I bought charms from you twice, when it was three times. And every time Odessa says the ledger keeps names, you look at me. Not at the fire. At me." She tilted her head. "Hedge-witches learn to notice things. It's how we stay alive. So. Why do you look at me?"

Jonah set the knife down. Slowly, deliberately, the way a man sets down a weapon to show his hands are empty. Odessa recognized the gesture; she'd used it herself in a hundred negotiations. It meant: *I am deciding right now how much truth this room can hold.*

"Wren," he began.

"Don't." Her voice cracked, just slightly, the first fracture in the milk-mild calm. "Don't do the thing where you're kind to me instead of honest. I've had kindness. Kindness is what people give you when they think you can't carry the truth." She wrapped her arms around herself. "I sold something once. Three years ago. To pay for a healing I couldn't afford, for a stranger who was dying in the reeds below the mill. I never knew his name. I signed the broker's paper and I walked home and I told myself it was worth it, whatever it cost, because a man lived." Her eyes were bright and terrible in the firelight. "And then the Audit comes, and the criers say the gifted will be registered, and Pell says the registration lists come from a ledger under Mount Sereth, and I think—I *know*—my name is in that book. Because of him. Because of the stranger I saved." She took a breath. "So when you look at me like that, Jonah Whitlock, I start wondering whether the dying man in the reeds had grey eyes. Whether he had a scar on his left hand. Whether he's been sitting at my fire for four days knowing exactly what I paid, and letting me walk toward the mountain blind."

Nobody moved. Bram had put down the harness strap. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

Odessa watched Jonah's face and saw the whole war of it: the truth rising in him like floodwater, and the five years of discipline pushing it back down. She knew that war. She'd fought it herself the day she'd decided not to ask him why a charm-seller with careful, mediocre hands had calluses shaped like nothing a charm-seller should have.

"Wren," Jonah said at last, and his voice was rough as gravel under rain. "The man in the reeds—"

"He was you." She said it first. Of course she did. She'd already done the arithmetic; she'd just needed him to confirm the sum. "Wasn't he. You were the stranger. You've known since Greyharrow. Since the shop." Her breath came out shaking. "You've known the whole time that I'm in that ledger because of you, and you let me sign on with this company, and you never said a word."

"I was going to tell you."

"When? At the mountain?" A short, broken laugh. "At the gate? 'By the way, Wren, mind the step, also your soul is in that vault'?"

"Yes," Jonah said simply. "Something like that."

And Odessa heard it then, under the absurdity—the raw honesty of it, the refusal to dress the cowardice up as anything nobler than what it was. She'd met a hundred men who lied beautifully. This one lied terribly, which meant he might be worth trusting after all.

Wren's hand came up fast, and for one heartbeat Odessa thought she'd strike him. Instead she pressed her palm flat against her own sternum, as if holding something in. "Three years," she whispered. "Three years I've been afraid of the quiet carts, and half of that fear was *bought*. My own coin. My own signature." She turned away from him, hugged herself tighter. "Do you know what the worst part is? I'd do it again. If I found a stranger dying in the reeds tomorrow, I'd do it again. That's what makes me so angry. Not you. Me. I'm the fool who pays full price for strangers."

"You're not a fool," Bram said.

Everyone turned. Fourteen words in one evening was unprecedented; the world was clearly ending. Bram set down the harness, picked up the mended strap, and held it out to Pell without looking at anyone. "Fools don't check their work," he said. "She checked hers. She just didn't like the total."

A sound escaped Wren—half laugh, half sob—and she sat down abruptly on the wagon step, all the fury going out of her at once, leaving behind something younger and more frightened. "I hate all of you," she said, without conviction. "I've known you four days."

"Five," said Pell gently. "You met Odessa at the safehouse first."

"Five days. I hate you all."

"Family," said Bram, and went back to work.

Odessa let the moment breathe. This was the alchemy she'd seen a hundred times on the long roads and never tired of watching: strangers becoming a company in the space of a single bad conversation. Not because the lies were gone—they weren't, not all of them, not yet—but because the truth had been passed around the fire like bread, and everyone had eaten, and no one had died of it.

But she was a pragmatist before she was a sentimentalist, and she could see the shape of what was still hidden. Jonah had confirmed the debt. He had not explained why he was really going to the mountain, or what he intended to do when he got there, or why a man who'd spent five years burying his gift had offered it to her in a dockside tavern as payment for passage. There was more under his ice. There always was, with men like him.

"We move at dawn," she said, standing, brushing off her skirts. "West road to the river fork, then north. Pell—your map. Redraw it tonight, all of it, and mark every corridor you're sure of and every one you're guessing. Bram, double the watch. Two hours each." She paused at the wagon's tailboard. "And Wren."

The girl looked up, eyes red-rimmed but steady.

"Whatever else is true," Odessa said, "you saved a man's life, and he's sitting at this fire because of it. Whatever it costs to fix the rest—we fix it together. That's the whole point of a company. Nobody carries their debts alone."

Wren held her gaze for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, and Odessa saw something settle in the girl's shoulders—a weight accepted rather than surrendered.

Odessa climbed into the wagon and pretended, for the next hour, to sleep.

She listened instead. She heard Pell scratching charcoal, correcting six years of memory line by line. She heard Bram take the first watch, his boots soft on the frost-hardened ground. She heard Wren lie down near the fire, and Jonah settle a blanket over her when she shivered, and Wren—awake, of course she was awake—say quietly, without opening her eyes:

"I'm not forgiving you yet."

"I know," Jonah said.

"But thank you. For the blanket."

"For the reeds," he answered.

Silence, after that. The good kind.

Near midnight, Odessa finally slept, and dreamed—as she had dreamed every night since the checkpoint—of lightning splitting a clear sky, and of the moment afterward when Jonah Whitlock had turned to look at her, smoke rising off his shoulders, his eyes lit from within like storm-glass, and she had understood with cold certainty that she hadn't hired a passenger.

She'd hired a weapon. And weapons, in her experience, always pointed somewhere eventually.

She woke before dawn to Bram's hand on her shoulder and his voice low and urgent in the grey light: "Smoke," he said. "East. A lot of it. And there's a rider coming up the road—wearing colors I haven't seen since the old king died."

Odessa rolled out of her blankets and reached for her boots. Behind her, the eastern horizon glowed a dull, wrong orange, like a bruise on the sky, and the wind carried the first faint smell of ash.

Vellmark was burning.
