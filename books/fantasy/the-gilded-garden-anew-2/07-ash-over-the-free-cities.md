## 7. Ash Over the Free Cities

The smoke reached them a full day before the city did.

Wren smelled it first—she always did, her nose sharper than Bram's, though she'd never say so to his face. It came on the wind like a warning written in cinders: woodsmoke and something sweeter underneath, something that made her think of burnt sugar and burned hair, and she knew before Odessa called the halt that Vellmark was burning.

They crested the ridge at dusk, and there it was.

Wren had heard of Vellmark all her life. The free cities were the stuff of hedge-witch gossip, of smugglers' songs—the places where a woman could hang a charm above her door without hiding it in a drawer, where the gifted walked in daylight with their hands unbandaged. In Wren's imagination it had always been golden: white walls and copper roofs, market canals bright with lanterns, spires hung with bells that rang for festivals instead of alarms.

What lay below was half of that and half of nothing. The western quarter was black. Whole streets stood as rows of charred teeth against the evening sky, and the copper roofs that remained had gone green-black with soot. Smoke still rose from a dozen places, lazy and gray now, the fire mostly fed out. But the eastern walls still stood, and along their tops moved torchlight—soldiers, or militia, or both—and beyond the walls, spread across the plain like a rash, the camps of Aldric's vanguard. Cookfires by the hundred. Horse lines. And things that caught the last light and threw it back wrong: siege engines, tall and jointed, standing among the tents like patient herons.

"Half-burned," Odessa said beside her. "And half-defended. That's Vellmark for you—never does anything by halves except survive." She spat over the ridge's edge. "We go in through the river gate. Quietly. Nobody speaks unless I speak first."

Bram grunted, which from him was a full paragraph of agreement.

Jonah said nothing at all. He'd been saying very little since Greyharrow, and Wren had begun to notice the shape of his silences—the way they weren't empty but full, packed tight like a jar sealed too soon. He stood looking down at the burning city with an expression she couldn't read, his hands loose at his sides, and when the wind shifted and brought the smell of ash again, he flinched. Not at the smell, she thought. At something under it.

She didn't ask. She was getting good at not asking, which was its own kind of wound.

* * *

The river gate was a wound in the wall that had been stitched shut with timber and desperation, and the guard who let them through took Odessa's coin without counting it and asked no questions, which told Wren everything about how the city was spending its attention. Inside, Vellmark was a held breath.

Streets that should have roared with market noise were quiet except for footsteps and the ring of hammers—carpenters shoring up doors, smiths working through the night on arrowheads. Every window had been shuttered or boarded. Charms hung openly above some doorways—real ones, Wren realized, her skin prickling as she passed them, little woven wards and sigil-boards that would have meant a pyre in Greyharrow—and beneath each one someone had chalked a fresh mark on the doorframe. A circle with a slash through it. She counted eleven before she stopped counting.

"What's the mark?" she whispered to Pell.

The scribe walked with his shoulders up around his ears, a man returning to a house where something terrible had happened. "It means *registered*," he said quietly. "Households that gave up a gifted member during the first Audit sweep. They're marked so the wagons know they've already paid."

"Paid," Wren repeated.

Pell didn't answer. He didn't have to. Ahead of them, in the mouth of an alley, a cart stood in the lamplight, and Wren's whole body went cold before her mind caught up with why.

It was a long cart, longer than a grain wagon, with high slatted sides like a livestock hauler. It was painted court-gray, and on its side, in gold leaf gone dull, was the seal of Mount Sereth—a mountain with an open eye set into its peak. Two men in the livery of the crown sat on the bench, eating bread, unhurried. The cart was empty. It waited the way a trap waits.

"The quiet carts," Pell murmured, and crossed himself with an old gesture, one hand touching throat and brow, that Wren had only ever seen done over graves.

* * *

They lodged that night in a smuggler's safehouse above a dye-shop, in rooms that smelled of mordant and wet wool, and it was there that Wren began to hear the stories.

She hadn't meant to listen. But the safehouse was full—refugees from the outlying villages, packed into the long room like pickled fish—and stories were the only cargo they'd carried with them. So she sat near the window with her knees drawn up and listened while a woman with a burn scar down one arm talked to anyone who would hold still.

"—came through Harrow Fen three weeks past, the wagons did. Set up in the square with a scale, a great brass thing, and they made every soul in the village put their hand upon it. My boy touched it and nothing happened, thank the saints, but old Marta's grandson—" The woman's voice snagged. She smoothed it flat again, the way you smooth a sheet over a body. "The scale sang for him. Just a note, low, like a bell underwater. And they wrote his name in a book and took him away in one of those carts, and his grandmother ran after it until she fell down, and do you know what the soldiers did? Nothing. They didn't even look at her. Like she was weather."

"And the families?" someone asked. "Did none of them follow?"

"Follow where?" The woman laughed, and it was the worst sound Wren had heard in weeks. "The carts go east, toward the mountain. Nobody follows the mountain. And here's the thing—" she leaned forward, and the room leaned with her, "—the carts come back. Empty. Always empty. You'd think they'd fill them going the other direction, with supplies, with anything. But no. Full leaving, empty returning. My cousin drives grain for the garrison and he swears the drivers won't take that road after dark. Won't say why. Just goes pale and drinks."

Full leaving, empty returning. Wren turned the words over and felt them catch on something inside her, some instinct older than thought, the same instinct that told her a storm was coming by the taste of copper on the air. Carts that returned empty weren't carrying people away. They were delivering them somewhere. Delivering them into something that used them up.

She looked across the room and found Jonah watching her. He sat apart, as he always did, mending a strap on his pack with careful, unnecessary stitches, and when their eyes met he looked down too quickly.

That, more than the carts, made her afraid.

* * *

She couldn't sleep. The safehouse breathed around her—strangers' snores, a baby's fretful whimper, the tick of the dye-shop's beams cooling—and finally she gave up and went down to the common room, where a single lamp burned low and Jonah sat alone at the table with his wares spread out before him.

His charms. Dozens of them, laid out on a cloth like a physician's instruments: knotted cords and painted stones, bits of sea-glass and iron nails wrapped in red thread. The stock-in-trade of a charm-seller, harmless as buttons. Except that Wren had watched him sell them in Greyharrow, watched him murmur over them with his lips barely moving, and she had begun to suspect—for weeks now, a suspicion growing like mold in a dark corner—that nothing about Jonah Whitlock was harmless.

"You should be sleeping," he said, not looking up.

"So should you."

"I don't sleep well in cities that are burning." He said it lightly, but his hands had gone still on the cloth.

Wren sat down across from him. The lamp between them guttered and steadied. And then, because she was tired and frightened and the day had scraped her raw, she did the thing she'd been not-doing since Greyharrow.

"Show me," she said. "One of them. Show me what they really are."

Jonah's head came up slowly. For a long moment he just looked at her, and she watched him decide—watched the war move behind his eyes, caution against something else, something lonelier. Then, wordlessly, he turned one of the charms toward her: a small wooden disc, palm-sized, painted with a spiral of marks she almost recognized.

"This is a warding," he said. "A simple one. It turns small ill-luck—a slipped knife, a bad step on stairs. The marks aren't decoration. They're instructions."

"Instructions for what?"

"For what the charm is allowed to do." He tapped the outermost mark, a hooked character like a bird's footprint. "This one says *turn*. This one says *small*. This one says *only what comes uninvited*." His finger moved around the spiral. "Read them in order and the charm knows its business. Break the order and it's just wood."

Wren stared at the disc. The marks were old sigils—she'd seen their cousins scratched on cottage lintels and inside witch-bottles her whole life, fragments her gran had called *the old tongue*, never explained. She'd always assumed they were inherited superstition, shapes copied without meaning, the way a parrot copies speech.

But Jonah read them. Not recited—*read*, the way she read a bill of sale, with comprehension moving behind his eyes.

"Teach me," she said.

He blinked. "Why?"

"Because I'm a hedge-witch with no hedge left, and because—" she gestured vaguely upward, toward the dark and the mountain beyond it, toward the eye set in the peak, "—because everyone in this city is paying something, and I intend to know exactly what my coin buys. Because knowledge is the only thing they haven't figured out how to put in a cart." She pushed the wooden disc back toward him. "Start with the bird-foot. What's it called?"

Something shifted in Jonah's face—something that might have been the first honest warmth she'd seen there since Greyharrow. "*Veth*," he said. "Old-tongue. It means *to turn aside*."

"*Veth*," Wren repeated, and the shape of the word settled into her mouth like a key settling into a lock she hadn't known she carried.

They worked until the lamp burned to a stub. Jonah taught the way he did everything—carefully, precisely, with a wariness that never quite relaxed—and Wren learned the way she did everything: greedily, messily, filling the margins of her mind with hooked birds and coiled serpents and the small grammar of protection. By the time the sky outside the shutters went gray, she could look at any charm on the cloth and pull out three words in ten. Turn. Small. Uninvited. Bind. Weather. *Wait*.

It was that last one that stopped her. Near the edge of the cloth, half-hidden under a knotted storm-charm she'd never seen him offer for sale, lay a flat slate pendant scored with a single sigil she now knew: *wait*. Beneath it, in a different hand—a hurried, scratched hand, not Jonah's neat painting—were two more marks she didn't recognize, and beneath those, a name.

Not her name. A name she didn't know at all, in letters that seemed to press harder than the others, as if the writer had been angry or afraid.

"Whose is this?" she asked.

Jonah went very still. It was the stillness of a man who has just heard a floorboard creak overhead in a house he thought empty. "Where did you get that?" he asked, and his voice had changed—it wasn't the charm-seller's voice, or even the road-companion's voice. It was older. Harder.

"It was in your things. On your cloth." Wren turned the slate over in her fingers and felt, faintly, a hum against her skin—not sound, not quite, but pressure, like the moment before thunder. "There's a name on it. Whose name?"

"Give it back." He reached across the table, and for the first time since she'd known him, Jonah Whitlock moved fast.

Wren pulled the pendant out of reach. Old instinct, hedge-witch reflexes—you didn't hand over a charged object just because its owner asked nicely. The hum against her palm strengthened, and the lamp flame bent sideways though there was no draft, and outside, far off toward the walls, thunder rolled across a cloudless sky.

They stared at each other across the table. Somewhere upstairs, the baby started crying. The lamp steadied itself.

"That was you," Wren said slowly. "Just now. The thunder."

"Wren—"

"You're the gifted." It wasn't a question anymore; it had stopped being one weeks ago, maybe, and she'd refused to hear it. "You're not selling charms. You're *hiding*. And this—" she lifted the slate, "—this is yours, isn't it? The name. It's your name. Someone wrote your name on this and you've been carrying it like a wound ever since."

Jonah closed his eyes. When he opened them, all the careful mediocrity of five years had drained out of his face, and what was left underneath was exhausted and young and terribly alone.

"Not on the slate," he said quietly. "In a ledger. Under a mountain. Written by the man who raised me." He held out his hand, palm up—an offering, or a surrender. "And if we don't reach it before Aldric's armies do, Wren, then your name will be in there too. Next to mine."

The hum against her palm had stopped. Or rather—it hadn't stopped; it had changed, deepened, become something she felt in her sternum instead of her skin, and she understood suddenly that the slate wasn't humming at all.

She was.

Outside, the thunder rolled again, closer now, and in the doorway behind Jonah, silent as a shadow, Odessa Vane stood watching them both with the expression of a woman recalculating everything she'd ever believed about the cargo she was carrying.

"We leave within the hour," Odessa said. "Both of you. There are crown riders asking questions at the dye-shop door, and whatever this is—" her eyes flicked to the slate, to Wren's trembling hand, to the lamp that wouldn't sit still, "—it can finish being a confession on the road. Move."

* * *

They went out through the dye-yard while the sky was still the color of dirty wool, past vats of indigo gone sour overnight, and Odessa led them not toward the river gate but down—a trapdoor beneath the drying racks, steps cut into the earth, a tunnel that smelled of roots and old water.

"Smuggler's road," Bram rumbled, holding the trapdoor for them. First full sentence he'd offered in two days. "Runs under the wall. Comes up by the millpond."

Wren climbed down into the dark with the slate pendant clenched in her fist, and the last thing she saw of Vellmark was the strip of gray sky above the yard—and against it, on the eastern horizon, the distant shape of Mount Sereth, faint as a bruise, with its single open eye.

Somewhere inside that mountain, a book held her name. She hadn't signed anything. She couldn't remember signing anything. And yet the thought of it pulled at her like a hook behind the breastbone, the way the slate had pulled at the lamp flame, the way the thunder had answered her anger.

*Full leaving, empty returning.*

She thought of the boy from Harrow Fen, sung out of his village by a brass scale. She thought of the grandmother running after the cart until she fell. She thought of the child's voice in the story, and of her own hands—hands that had healed a dying stranger once, that had spent something precious to do it, though for the life of her she couldn't remember what the price had been, only that there had been a gap afterward, a missing week, a hole in her memory shaped exactly like a bargain.

The tunnel swallowed them. Ahead, Bram's lantern swung its small yellow circle through the dark, and Jonah walked beside her in silence, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, and neither of them spoke, because there was nothing left to hide behind and everything left to say.

Behind them, muffled by earth and distance, Vellmark's alarm bells began to ring.

The vanguard was testing the walls.
