## 8. The Mentor's Letter

The fire had burned down to its red bones by the time Pell stopped pretending to sleep.

Jonah heard it in the way the scribe shifted—three small movements, deliberate as a man arranging his affairs before a hanging—and then the soft scrape of leather against stone as Pell drew his document case from beneath his bedroll. The case was court-issue, oiled and black, the kind that carried writs of execution and deeds of land and all the other papers by which a kingdom decided what people were worth.

They had made camp in the hollow of a dead river, a mile off the trade road, where the willows grew in pale thickets and the water had gone underground years ago. Odessa stood first watch on the eastern rise. Bram slept like a man dropped from a height. Wren lay curled near the fire with her cloak pulled to her chin, and Jonah had spent the last hour watching the shape of her breathing and thinking about ledgers.

About names written in books.

"Whitlock." Pell's voice was low, pitched to carry no farther than the firelight. "You should see this."

Jonah crossed the camp quietly. He sat where Pell indicated, on a flat stone worn smooth by some older river, and waited. The scribe was not a dramatic man. In the week since Vellmark he had been precise, nervous, and relentlessly useful—the sort of man who knew which corridors smelled of mold and which guards took bribes in copper rather than silver. He did not summon people at midnight for nothing.

Pell opened the case. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, lay a sheaf of documents: route schedules, garrison rosters, copies of the Audit proclamations with their seals still bright. His fingers moved past all of it and found a single sheet at the bottom, thinner than the rest, and Jonah saw that the scribe's hands were shaking.

"I wasn't going to show you," Pell said. "I told myself it would serve no purpose. That you'd already suffered enough from Corvane's arrangements." He swallowed. "But we ride into that mountain in four days, Whitlock, and I've watched you plan this theft like a man buying back a horse. You deserve to know what you're actually riding toward."

He held out the paper.

Jonah took it the way one takes a blade offered hilt-first—carefully, expecting the edge.

It was a court record. He knew the format instantly; Greyharrow's magistrates used a plainer version of it, and even disguised as a charm-seller he had sat through enough property disputes to recognize the architecture of official language. *Be it entered and witnessed.* The seal at the bottom was Mount Sereth's—a mountain crowned in ink, ringed by nine stars—and above the seal, in the fine clerk's hand that Pell himself still wrote with when he forgot to disguise it, ran the entry.

*Entry the four hundred and eleventh of the autumn reckoning.*

*Name: Jonah Whitlock, called the storm-son of Corvane.*

*Nature of gift: weather-working, storm-calling, the old line.*

*Condition of entry: sold freely by the holder of guardianship, Master Aldous Corvane of the Circle of Nine, in consideration of the following terms—*

Jonah stopped reading. Not because he wanted to. Because his eyes had begun to slide off the words the way rain slides off oiled canvas, and he understood, distantly, that some part of him was trying to refuse the page entirely.

*In consideration of the following terms.* He made himself go back.

*—namely, that the said Jonah Whitlock shall be permitted to depart the Circle's lands alive and unmolested, his exile accepted in full satisfaction of the charges brought against him, provided that his name be entered in the Ledger of Sereth and held thereafter as the property of the mountain court.*

There was more. There was always more; clerks loved their clauses. Something about succession, about the name passing to the crown upon Corvane's death, about the gift itself being held in escrow—"held," as though his storms were coins in a merchant's strongbox—as security against his ever returning north of the Ashford line.

Five years ago. The date at the top was five years and two months old.

Two months after the night the lightning had come out of him sideways and burned the granary at Corvane's hold, and killed three men who had drawn steel on an unarmed boy, and made him something the Circle could neither teach nor forgive.

"You knew," Jonah said. His voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "You filed this."

"I copied it." Pell's honesty had a terrible cleanliness to it. "I was a junior scribe in the Hall of Entries. I copied four hundred names that season alone. Yours was one of them." He paused. "I didn't know then what the ledger was for. None of us did. We thought it was accounting."

"Accounting." Jonah laughed, or something near it. The sound frightened the horses; he heard Bram shift in his bedroll and settle again. "That's what my life was. An entry. A line item."

"It was the price of your life," Pell said quietly. "Read the whole thing. It matters how it ends."

Jonah read the whole thing.

The final clause was short. It said that the transaction had been executed under the Steward's own hand, witnessed by two archivists whose names meant nothing to him now but must have meant everything once, and that the seller—Corvane—had signed not with a signature but with his mark of office, the coiled serpent of the Circle of Nine, pressed deep into the wax.

Pressed deep. Jonah stared at that impression across five years and a hundred miles and could almost feel the weight of the man's hand coming down. Corvane had always pressed hard. When he corrected a student's sigilwork he bore down until the stylus nearly cut the vellum, saying, *Ink fades, boy. What you carve is what remains.* Jonah had thought it a lesson about discipline.

It had been a lesson about ownership.

* * *

He walked away from the fire without deciding to. His legs simply took him, out past the willow thickets, up the eastern rise where Odessa stood her watch, because walking toward Odessa felt safer than standing still, and he could not have said why except that she was the only person in the world who had never pretended to be anything other than what she was.

She heard him coming—of course she did—and didn't turn. She stood with her arms folded against the cold, looking south toward the black shoulder of the hills beyond which, somewhere, Aldric's Audit wagons were rolling.

"Well," she said. "Either you're here to take the watch early, or the scribe finally showed you the letter."

Jonah stopped. "You knew too."

"I suspected. There's no version of Corvane that gives away a student for free." She turned her head just enough to look at him, one eye catching the starlight. "I've moved enough contraband to know a bill of sale when I see the buyer's face. You've had that face all week. I assumed you'd worked it out yourself and didn't want to say it aloud."

"He sold me." The words came out of Jonah like something being lanced. "Not betrayed me. Betrayal implies I was taken from him. He *sold* me. He went to the mountain, he sat down across from that hollowed-out Steward, and he traded my soul for permission to let me live. And then—" Jonah's breath caught, and he hated it, hated the wet heat behind his eyes, hated five years of grief rearranging itself around a new and uglier truth. "And then he wrote to me. After. Every year, on the anniversary of my exile. A letter. *I grieve that it came to this. The Circle is not what it was. Guard your heart, boy, if not your gift.* I kept them. Every one. I kept them because I thought—" 

He couldn't finish it. Odessa finished it for him, because she was Odessa.

"You thought he loved you and it cost him something."

"I thought he'd fought for me." Jonah's hands had closed into fists without his permission. "I built five years on it, Odessa. Five years of mediocrity, of selling tin charms to farmers, of flinching every time the sky went green before hail—I built all of it on the idea that somewhere north of the Ashford there was one man who'd paid a price to keep me breathing. That the exile was mercy. That it was the best he could do." He shook his head slowly. "It wasn't mercy. It was commerce. He got rid of the embarrassment of me, kept his seat on the Circle, and threw in my immortal soul to sweeten the terms. I wasn't his student. I was stock."

Odessa was quiet for a long moment. Below them, the fire had sunk to embers, and Wren's small shape breathed in and out beside it, and Jonah watched her and felt the full obscene symmetry of it settle over him like cold water.

"She sold herself," he said. "To save me. And he sold me to save himself. The same ledger holds us both. Her name bought my heartbeat, and his signature priced it." He laughed again, and this time there was nothing human in it. "Do you know what the worst part is? I remember that night. The granary. The men with the swords. I remember Corvane standing in the doorway with the rain behind him, and I remember thinking he looked *grieved*. I've replayed that look for five years. Grief. Regret. A mentor mourning what he had to do." 

"And?"

"And it was arithmetic. He was counting." Jonah pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "Every memory I have of him has to be reweighed now. The lessons. The rare praise—he gave so little of it, and I treasured each word like a relic, and now I wonder if scarcity was just good husbandry. Keep the animal hungry and it works harder. The day he taught me to call fog off the marsh, he put his hand on my shoulder and said, *This gift is bigger than you know, boy. Bigger than either of us.* I thought it was pride." His voice dropped. "It was appraisal."

Odessa unfolded her arms. She came closer—not touching him, Odessa did not touch people she respected, a fact Jonah had learned the way one learns any of her laws, by breach and consequence—but standing near enough that her voice needed no guard at all.

"My father sold me once," she said. "Not my soul. My labor. I was eleven, and he signed a contract with a canal company that owned me until I was nineteen, and he cried while he did it, and it changed nothing about what the paper said. I spent years deciding which was worse—that he wept, or that he signed anyway." She shrugged, a small hard motion. "Here's what I decided. The tears were real. So was the pen. Men are rarely lying when they grieve the things they sell. They just sell them regardless. Corvane may have loved you the way a miser loves gold—it doesn't make the coin spend itself any less."

"That's supposed to comfort me?"

"No. Comfort is for people whose problems can be solved by feeling better." She nodded south, toward the mountains invisible in the dark. "Yours get solved in four days, in a vault, with a knife or a storm. So feel whatever you need to feel tonight, Whitlock, and tomorrow you tell me whether knowing this changes the plan. Because it should. If your name is in that ledger—if it's been there five years—then this stopped being a rescue the moment we left Greyharrow. It's a retrieval. Two entries. One debt."

"One debt," Jonah echoed.

"Wren's. Only hers. Yours is a separate matter, and we'll discuss what we do about it when I've slept on it." She turned back to her watch, dismissing him the way she dismissed everything—completely, without malice. Then, over her shoulder: "For what it's worth. The letters. Keeping them wasn't foolish. A man needs evidence of who he was before he learns who he is. Burn them when you're ready. Not before."

* * *

He did not burn anything. He went back down through the willows and sat at the edge of the firelight, the court record still in his hand, and read it a third time because the mind keeps returning to the wound the tongue returns to the broken tooth.

Wren woke sometime past the deepest hour. He heard her breathing change, saw her sit up, saw her find him across the dying fire. In the ember-light her face was young and unguarded, and she looked at him the way she had looked at him in her doorway in Greyharrow, the day he'd sold her a charm against scrying that was nothing but glass and copper wire, and she had paid him too much and thanked him twice.

"You're reading that like it's about you," she said softly.

"It is about me."

"Mm." She crawled nearer, wrapped her blanket around her shoulders, and studied the paper upside down with the frank illiteracy she never apologized for. "I can't read the words. But I can read the sigils, a little, now. The ones in the border." Her finger traced the air above the margin, where the clerk's ornamentations ran in a fine chain. "That one means *held*. This one means *given*. And this one—" she tapped the air over the seal, the mountain crowned in ink, "—this one I've seen before. On the charm you sold me. On the inside of the wire, where nobody was supposed to look."

Jonah went very still.

"You marked it," Wren said. Not accusing. Wondering. "The charm you gave me. You put the mountain's seal inside it. Why would you do that, Jonah? Five years ago I'd never heard of Mount Sereth. Neither had you, I'd wager. So why does your work carry their mark?"

Because it isn't mine anymore, he thought. Because a man can be sold and not know it, and everything his hands make carries the new owner's stamp, the way a branded ox bears the mark whether or not it has ever seen the ranch. Because Corvane's serpent was pressed deep into the wax, and what you carve is what remains.

"I don't know," he said, and it was the truest thing he'd said all night.

Wren held his gaze a moment longer, and he saw her decide—not to believe him, exactly, but to bank the question, the way she seemed to bank everything lately, feeding some private fire he couldn't see. She settled back down into her blankets. Just before her eyes closed she said, "Whatever's written in that book about you, Jonah Whitlock—it's wrong. Names are what other people call you. You're what you do next."

She slept. Jonah sat alone with the paper and the embers, and above the dead river the sky was turning the bruised grey that comes before dawn, and far to the south, faint as memory, he felt the pressure drop—the taste of iron on the wind, the old electric itch along his forearms that meant a storm was building somewhere over the mountains, gathering itself, patient and enormous, waiting for someone to call it home.

Four days, Odessa had said.

He folded the letter along its creases, tucked it inside his coat, over his heart, where the other letters had lived for five years, and began, silently, to plan what he would do when he stood in front of the book that owned him.
