## 9. Bargaining with Ghosts

Odessa Vane had made her living reading men's faces across candlelit tables, and Jonah Whitlock's face, in the three days since Pell had laid that court document on the camp table, was the worst hand she'd ever been dealt.

He still did everything right. He still rose before dawn to help Bram check the wagon axles. He still haggled cheerfully with farmers at the crossroads for eggs and salt pork, playing the charm-seller down to his soft hands and his merchant's smile. He even laughed sometimes—a low, warm sound that seemed to cost him nothing.

But she had watched a man learn his own death once before, back when she ran refugees through the Fenmarsh and a boy named Callow discovered the fever papers listing him as dead. Jonah wore the same look. Not grief. Grief was loud and eventually burned itself out. This was quieter and worse: a man standing in the wreckage of his own history, picking up pieces of it and finding each one stamped with someone else's price.

Five years of exile, re-priced as a purchase. A mentor's mercy, revealed as an invoice. Every kindness Corvane had ever shown him, Odessa suspected, now being audited line by line in the dark behind those grey eyes.

She let it go for three days because she needed him functional, and a man mid-drowning is no use at a tiller. But by the fourth night, camped in a hollow of black pines below the free cities' last hills, with Mount Sereth a jagged absence on the horizon—visible more as a place where stars refused to settle than as any shape she could name—she decided functional wasn't enough anymore. They were nine days from the foothills, maybe less if the weather held, and she would not walk into that mountain with a man carrying an unopened wound in his chest where a plan should be.

So she waited until Wren had gone to sleep in the wagon and Pell had banked the fire to embers, and she sat down across from Jonah with two cups of Bram's terrible pine-needle tea and the particular smile she used on clients who thought they could out-stubborn her.

"Drink," she said. "You look like something the tide rejected."

"I've had worse compliments."

"You've had better lies told to you, too, but here we are." She pushed a cup at him. "We're going to talk about the ledger."

Jonah's cup stopped halfway to his mouth. "There's nothing to talk about."

"There's a dead man sitting across from me who thinks he's alive," Odessa said. "I talk to dead men all the time. It's most of my business model." She sipped her tea, grimaced—Bram truly could not be taught—and set it down. "Your name is written in a book under a mountain. Mine isn't. Wren's is. So before you decide your soul being sold is a private grief you can carry quietly all the way to Sereth, I'm going to ask you the question I should have asked the night Pell showed us that document."

Jonah said nothing. The fire popped. Somewhere up-slope, an owl asked its one question of the dark and got no answer.

"If names can be sold," Odessa said, "can they be bought back?"

* * *

For a long moment Jonah just looked at her, and she watched the charm-seller fall away from his face like a mask slipping off in the rain—the careful pleasantness, the practiced harmlessness, all of it going slack until what remained was younger than she'd expected and far more tired.

"You think I haven't turned that over?" His voice came out rough. "Every hour since Pell put that page in my hands. Buy it back. Steal it. Burn it. Bargain." He laughed, and there was no warmth in it at all. "I spent five years learning how to disappear, Vane. Do you know what I learned? That disappearing only works if nobody's looking for you. Aldric is looking for everyone now. And somewhere under that mountain is a book that tells him exactly where to find us."

"So you have thought about it," she said. "Good. Then you'll have noticed the problem."

"The problem," Jonah said, "is that I don't know anything. I was trained by a man who sold me, and everything he ever taught me about the ledger came wrapped in lessons about why we don't touch it. 'The ledger keeps itself,' he used to say. 'The ledger is patient.' I thought it was philosophy." He stared into the fire. "It was a warning."

"Then we ask someone who filed the paperwork."

They both turned. Pell sat half-shadowed beyond the fire's reach, knees drawn up, a court-issue inkstain permanently shadowing the side of his right hand like a bruise he couldn't wash off. Odessa hadn't heard him approach, which annoyed her; the man moved like a rumor.

"I was going to wait for morning," Pell said.

"You were waiting to see if I'd say it first," Odessa corrected. "Cowardice loves company. Get over here."

Pell came into the light and lowered himself onto the log beside Jonah, and Odessa saw the strange geometry of it—the scribe who had processed souls like cargo, sitting next to a man whose soul he might well have catalogued without knowing it. Pell didn't flinch from it. She'd give him that. Whatever else he was, the little defector had walked away from the warmest rooms in the kingdom with nothing but the ink on his hands.

"The ledger isn't a record," Pell began, in the flat reciting voice he used for things he'd memorized so thoroughly they'd stopped meaning anything. "That's the first mistake everyone makes. It's not a list of the gifted. It's a registry of ownership. When a soul is entered, a binding forms between the name and the mountain. Think of it as—" he searched, "—a chain. Invisible. Weightless. Until someone pulls it."

"And the Steward holds the other end," Jonah said quietly.

"The Steward *is* the other end." Pell rubbed at the inkstain, an unconscious gesture Odessa had noticed him make whenever he talked about the vault. "Every strike, every transfer, every sale has to pass through his hand. He's the only one who can break a binding or rewrite an entry. There are exactly three copies of any given page: the original in the vault, a duplicate in the court archive, and—" he hesitated.

"And the one the Steward carries in his head," Jonah finished. "Corvane mentioned that much."

"Then you know why forgery alone won't work. You can fake the paper. You can't fake the binding. If we walked into the vault tonight with a perfect copy of Wren's page marked STRUCK, the chain around her soul wouldn't notice. Paper remembers what people wrote. The ledger remembers what was *done*."

The fire had burned low enough that shadows pooled in the hollows of all their faces. Odessa found herself thinking, absurdly, of her mother's account books—her mother, who had kept a fish stall in Sallowport and believed with religious fervor that every debt in the world could be settled if both parties were honest and the arithmetic was done twice. Her mother had never met King Aldric.

"Options," Odessa said, and pulled a charred stick from the fire's edge, and drew in the cold dirt between them. "One." A circle. "We buy. We get word to whoever brokers sales for the court—I know three names, all of them snakes—and we offer gold, routes, information. Problems: the Steward answers to Aldric, which means the price of a soul these days is whatever Aldric says it is, and I doubt he takes payment in smuggled brandy."

"He doesn't take payment at all anymore," Pell said softly. "Since the Audit began, entries have only gone *in*. Nothing comes out. The clerks call it the Closing of the Book." He caught their looks. "Yes. We all pretended it was a normal administrative phrase. That's most of working for the crown—you spend years pretending phrases are normal until you believe them."

"Two." Another circle, crossed out. "We steal. We go in, we take Wren's page, we run. Problems: the binding stays. We'd have a piece of paper and she'd still belong to the mountain. Worse—we'd have told the Steward exactly who wants what, and men who keep books of souls tend to write *that* down too."

"Three," Jonah said. His voice had changed. Odessa looked up from her dirt-diagrams and found him watching the fire with an expression she recognized from her own mirror on bad nights: the arithmetic of desperation, columns not balancing, carried anyway. "Three is we don't take the page out. We change what's written on it while we're standing there. Strike the entry ourselves. In the vault. With the Steward's own instruments."

Silence. Even the owl up-slope had gone quiet, as if it too wanted to hear this madness through.

"That requires the Steward," Pell said at last. "Or his authority. Or—" he stopped.

"Or his hand," Odessa said slowly. "Forced, borrowed, or bribed." She looked at Jonah. "You knew Corvane. Men like Corvane built that office. What do we know about the Steward himself?"

Jonah was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. When he did, his voice had dropped into something almost reverent and almost afraid.

"Not much. Corvane spoke of him once, when I was young, after I'd asked too many questions about where the court's power actually came from. He said the Steward was old when the kingdom was young. He said—" Jonah's brow creased, reaching for words that had clearly been chosen to frighten a child, and landing on them anyway. "He said the Steward had once been a storm-caller. The first of them. And that the ledger kept him alive, and that keeping it kept him, and that the two of them had grown together so long ago that asking where the man ended and the book began was like asking where the river ended and the sea began."

"A storm-caller," Odessa repeated. She glanced at Jonah—at the faint restless pressure she'd learned to sense around him, like weather deciding whether to happen. "Like you."

"Like me," Jonah said. "Older. Bound longer." He turned his cup in his hands. "And answering to Aldric, which means whatever he was, he serves the usurper now. Which means either he was broken, or bought, or—" he exhaled, "—or bargained with. And I know exactly how bargains with that court go, because I'm wearing the results."

* * *

They argued until the moon crossed the pine tops and started down the other side.

Pell argued for the forgery gambit, and he argued well—not because he believed in it, Odessa suspected, but because he believed in anything that avoided the vault's inner door. Forge a Steward's writ of transfer, present it at the servants' gate with his own old credentials, walk out with Wren's page before anyone understood what had happened. Clean. Quiet. Deniable.

"It won't work," Odessa said, "because you told me yourself the clerks verify against the binding. You said the phrase. *Paper remembers what people wrote.*"

"The gate-clerks don't verify against anything," Pell shot back. "Gate-clerks are frightened men with candles who check signatures and stamps. I *was* one. The verification happens at the archive, days later, by which point we're over the border and Wren's page is in a river. Yes, the binding survives—but the Audit wagons work off the pages, not the bindings. Take her page off the active rolls and no hunter is ever sent for her. It's not freedom. It's invisibility." He spread his inkstained hands. "I am proposing we do to her what your friend Whitlock has been doing to himself for five years. It works. Mostly. Until it doesn't."

"And when it stops working," Jonah said quietly, "she's spent her whole life running from a book that still owns her."

"Better than dying in a vault!"

"Would you say that about your own name?"

The question landed harder than Jonah perhaps intended, because Pell went white and shut his mouth, and Odessa understood suddenly that the little scribe had a page of his own somewhere in that mountain—that his defection hadn't been pure principle, that some clerk's error or some informant's greed had put *Pell* on a list, and he'd run rather than ride the quiet cart. She filed it away. Not to use against him. Just to know. Knowing people was the whole trade.

"It doesn't matter whose plan is prettier," she said, before the silence curdled. "Because neither of them accounts for the actual obstacle, which is that we're four people with one wagon, and the mountain is full of hunters, wards, and a Steward who reads souls for breakfast. So let me tell you what I've decided, and then the two of you can explain to me why I'm wrong, and then I'll ignore you."

Jonah raised an eyebrow. Pell looked resigned. Good. Familiar ground.

"We do both," Odessa said. "Pell builds his forgery—the writ, the seals, the whole beautiful lie. We use it to get through the gate, because a lie is cheaper than blood at the front door. And then, inside, we aim higher than a stolen page. We go for the Steward himself."

"To do what?" Jonah asked. "Kill him? Kill the Steward and every name in that book becomes an orphan—Pell said it himself, the bindings need a holder. You'd loose them all at once. Every taken gifted in the kingdom, snapped free in a single moment." He paused, hearing his own words, and Odessa watched something kindle behind his eyes that hadn't been there in three days. "You'd free everyone."

"Or kill him wrong and shatter the bindings into the nearest strong vessel, which according to every text Pell stole is how you make a lich, and I've smuggled past enough cursed ruins to know I don't want to meet the homemade version." She jabbed the charred stick at the dirt, at her circles, at the mess of plans. "No. We don't kill the Steward. We *talk* to him. A man who's been bound to a book for five hundred years answers to Aldric because Aldric holds his chains. But chains can be renegotiated. Everything can be renegotiated. I have never once in my life met a debt that didn't have a seam somewhere, if you pressed hard enough in the right place."

"And if he won't bargain?" Pell asked. "If he's past wanting anything?"

"Then we're four people standing in a vault with a forged writ and a stolen page and nothing left to lose," Odessa said. "Which is, incidentally, my favorite negotiating position. Nobody haggles harder than a corpse."

Jonah laughed—a real laugh this time, startled out of him, brief and rough-edged. It transformed his face entirely, and Odessa thought, with a pang she didn't examine closely, that the charm-seller's smile had never once looked like that. The smile was armor. This was the man underneath, briefly visible, holding a cup of terrible tea in a freezing forest with his soul sold out from under him, laughing because the alternative was screaming.

"You're insane," Jonah said.

"I'm expensive," Odessa corrected. "Insane is free."

* * *

It was near dawn when the others stirred, and the plan was still raw clay in all their hands—shaped enough to carry, soft enough that it would need reshaping a dozen times before it reached the mountain. Odessa stood at the edge of the hollow, watching the east go grey, and felt the familiar cold arithmetic settle over her: nine days, one wagon, one forged writ to build, one storm-caller with a target on his back, one hedge-witch asleep on straw who had no idea yet what they intended to spend getting her name back.

And that was the part that sat wrong in her chest, she admitted. Not the danger. Odessa had priced risk her entire life and slept fine. What sat wrong was the ledger of a different kind—the one in her head, the one where she tallied what people were worth to her. She'd kept it scrupulously for twenty years. Everyone had a number. Routes, debts, favors owed and owing. It was how you survived when the world treated people as cargo.

But somewhere between Greyharrow and this frozen hollow, Wren had gotten under her guard—the girl's stubborn, untrained ferocity, the way she practiced sigils by firelight with singed fingers and total concentration, the way she'd started leaving extra rations on Bram's pack without saying anything because she'd noticed he gave his share away. Wren had a number now. So did Bram. So, damn it all, did Pell and his inkstained guilty hands.

And numbers, Odessa knew, were the beginning of loyalty, and loyalty was the one commodity she had sworn never to hold, because everything she'd ever loved had been priced by somebody else and taken as payment.

Behind her, Jonah was showing Pell how to draw the sigil that would anchor the forgery—his hands moving in the old patterns, patient, precise, teaching the scribe the language of a life that had been sold beneath him. Pell copied carefully, tongue between his teeth, and Odessa thought: *there's the seam. Not in the Steward's chains. In us.*

Aldric counted on people acting like property because they believed they were property. Every plan she'd ever made had assumed the same thing. But a man whose soul was sold and who was teaching forgery to the clerk who'd once filed him—that man had already stopped believing it.

She turned back toward the camp as the first light came through the pines, and froze.

At the edge of the trees, motionless, stood Wren. She'd been there long enough for frost to silver her shoulders. In her hand, half-crumpled, was one of Jonah's charm-papers—the old ones, the harmless ones—and her eyes, fixed on the fire where Jonah and Pell bent over their lesson, held an expression Odessa recognized from a hundred interrogation rooms she'd never been inside and a thousand betrayals she'd witnessed from the outside:

The look of someone who has just realized the people protecting her are lying to her—and has decided to find out why herself.
