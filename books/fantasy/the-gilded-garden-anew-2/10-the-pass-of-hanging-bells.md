## 10. The Pass of Hanging Bells

Bram Vane had carried a lot of things over a lot of passes in his life, and he had learned long ago that the mountain always told you what it wanted from you. Some passes wanted speed. Some wanted silence. Some wanted blood, and those were the ones he avoided, or used to, before his sister started collecting strays like other women collected buttons.

This pass wanted stillness.

He felt it before they crested the first rise—the way the air hung in the col between the peaks, thick and expectant, like the held breath of a man listening at a door. The wind that should have been screaming through the gap was doing nothing at all. That was wrong. Wind didn't forget its work in a place like this. Something up there was drinking it.

"The bells," Pell said quietly, and Bram watched the scribe's face go the color of old tallow as he looked up.

They hung along the whole length of the pass, hundreds of them, strung on iron wire between the rocks and the dead pines and the crooked posts the king's engineers had planted in the scree like a crop of gallows. They were small things, no bigger than a fist, cast bronze with a slit mouth and a clapper of pale bone, and they turned slowly, slowly, in a wind that wasn't there. Each one had been etched with sigils Bram couldn't read, but he knew the maker's hand anyway. It was Aldric's hand. Everything ugly in the world lately seemed to have it.

"How many?" Odessa asked. She'd come up beside him, and her voice was all business, which meant she was scared. Bram had known her thirty-one years. Business voice was just fear wearing a clean shirt.

"Counted forty from the treeline," he said. "There's more than forty."

"There's always more than forty," Odessa muttered.

"They ring for magic." Pell again, wringing his soft scribe's hands. "Not for people. For the gift. The resonance of an active working passes through them like—like a shout through a sleeping house. One bell wakes the next. By the time you're halfway across, there's a garrison at the far end waiting for you with chains and a wagon." He swallowed. "I filed the requisition orders for these. The bronze came from Vellmark. I signed for the bone."

Bram looked at him. "You signed for the bone?"

"I signed for everything," Pell said, and his voice cracked down the middle, and Bram decided not to hate him anymore, at least not this hour.

* * *

They made camp below the pass that night, in a hollow of black pines where the fire could be kept small and the horses could be kept quiet. Bram took first watch because he always did, sitting on a stone with his back to the cold and his axe across his knees, watching the pass above them catch the moonlight. From here the bells looked almost pretty. Little silver teeth in the dark jaw of the mountain. He hated them for being pretty. Ugly things you could prepare for. Pretty things got under your guard.

He heard footsteps behind him—soft ones, deliberate—and didn't turn. Only one person in their ragged company walked like that, weight rolled heel to toe like she was afraid of leaving prints even on bare stone.

"Wren," he said.

She sat down next to him without asking, pulling her knees up, and for a while neither of them said anything. That was another thing he liked about her. Most people filled silence like it was a hole they'd fall into. Wren treated it like a room she was allowed to sit in.

"You're worried about tomorrow," he said finally.

"I'm worried about Jonah."

That surprised him enough to turn his head. The girl was staring up at the pass, her chin on her knees, her hedge-witch eyes catching the starlight. "Why him? He's the one who's supposed to be able to handle himself. Man carries a storm around inside him like a flask of brandy."

"That's why." She picked at a loose thread on her sleeve. "I've been watching him since we left Greyharrow. Do you know what it costs him, not using it? Every day. Every single day, holding it down. It's like—" she searched for the word, hands moving, "—it's like watching someone hold their breath underwater. And tomorrow he has to hold it for eight miles of bells."

"He'll manage."

"Yes," she said. "And I'll be walking right beside him, and I don't know how to manage anything. Nobody ever taught me. My gift is just—it's just there, like breathing. What if I breathe wrong? What if one little working slips out of me sideways and every bell from here to the sea starts screaming?" Her voice went thin. "Bram, what if I'm the one who kills us all?"

Bram considered his answer the way he considered most things: slowly, and with his whole body. Then he reached over and put one big hand on top of hers, stopping the picking.

"My sister once told me," he said, "that courage isn't the absence of shaking. It's the shaking, and you do the thing anyway." He squeezed once and let go. "Tomorrow you shake quietly. That's all any of us can do."

Wren laughed—a small wet sound, half a sob trying to disguise itself. "You're a strange man, Bram Vane."

"Aye," he agreed. "Runs in the family."

* * *

Dawn came up grey and mean, and they stood at the mouth of the pass like pilgrims at the door of a hostile god.

Odessa laid out the rules in her flat merchant's voice, and Bram stood behind her with his arms crossed, being the thing her words leaned against. Single file. Twenty paces between each person. No speaking above a whisper. No touching the wires. No workings—not Jonah's storms, not Wren's charms, not so much as a warming cantrip for cold fingers. And above all—

"If a bell rings," Odessa said, "you do not run. Running makes noise, noise makes vibration, vibration feeds them. You freeze. You let it die. Am I understood?"

They understood. Bram watched Jonah Whitlock as the exiled storm-caller pulled off one glove and pressed his bare palm flat against the cold rock of the pass's threshold, eyes closed, and Bram saw something happen in the man's face that frightened him more than all the bells combined. It was like watching a man lock a door on something that was already inside the house.

Jonah opened his eyes. "I'm ready," he said, and his voice was steady, and Bram thought: *no, you're not, but go on then.*

They went.

The first mile was almost easy. The path was wide enough for two abreast, the footing good, the bells overhead turning in their dead wind with a patience Bram found obscene. He walked rear guard, as always, axe slung across his back, counting his sister's strays the way a shepherd counts sheep. Odessa in front, Pell behind her, sweating through his coat. Then Wren. Then Jonah, walking like a man carrying a full cup of water across a frozen lake, every muscle committed to level. Then Bram.

It was Wren who nearly ended them the first time.

She didn't do anything wrong—that was the devil of it. A bird burst from the scree near her feet, some grey mountain grouse exploding upward in a thunder of wings, and she flinched, and the flinch was nothing, pure animal reflex—but the charm at her throat, the little woven thing she'd made herself back in better days, answered her fright the way a dog answers its master's fear. Bram saw it happen. Saw the faint blue shimmer wake in the knotwork, saw the nearest bell tilt toward her like a flower finding sun.

The bell hummed.

One note, low and sweet and terrible, hanging in the still air.

Everyone froze. Odessa's hand went up. Pell went white as paper. And Jonah moved—fast, faster than Bram would have credited, closing the twenty paces between them in six strides and putting his hand flat over the charm at Wren's throat, not touching her skin, just hovering, and Bram watched the shimmer gutter and drown like a candle pinched between wet fingers.

Jonah had swallowed it. Bram realized that with a lurch. The man had reached into the girl's little working and drunk it down into himself, added it to the ocean he was already holding behind his teeth, because it was easier for him to carry than for her to stop.

The bell's note faded. The pass stayed silent.

For a long moment nobody breathed. Then Jonah stepped back, and his face was grey as the stone underfoot, and sweat stood out on his forehead in beads despite the cold.

"Thank you," Wren whispered.

"Don't thank me," Jonah whispered back, and there was something raw underneath the words, something bleeding. "Just walk."

* * *

By the third mile, Bram could see what it was costing.

Jonah walked like a man being flayed slowly by his own skin. His shoulders had crept up toward his ears. His breath came short and shallow through his nose. Twice Bram saw him stumble on ground a goat wouldn't have tripped over, and twice he caught himself, jaw clenched hard enough to crack teeth. The storm inside him wasn't sleeping. It was pressing. Bram had hauled cargo his whole life; he knew what a load looked like when it shifted against its bindings, and Jonah Whitlock was a binding close to bursting.

And Wren—Wren was getting worse, not better. Fear fed her gift whether she willed it or not. Every jangle of her nerves woke a little answering glow somewhere on her person: the charm at her throat, the stitched sigil inside her cuff, the blessed ribbon in her hair that she probably hadn't thought about in years. Small things. Flickers. Each one a lit match waved over a powder store.

Jonah kept reaching for them. That was the thing that stuck in Bram's chest afterward, the thing he'd tell Odessa later by the fire when the others slept. Every time one of the girl's charms woke, Jonah was there, hand out, drinking it down, adding it to his own unbearable weight. He never said a word about it. He never looked at her. He just did it, over and over, mile after mile, a man quietly drowning himself so a stranger wouldn't splash.

Bram had carried wounded men off battlefields. He knew sacrifice when he saw it, and he also knew what it looked like when it was about to fail.

It failed at the narrows.

The path pinched down between two walls of granite, barely wide enough for the pack mule, and the bells hung thickest there—a curtain of them, dozens deep, swaying on their wires directly overhead. Pell went through first, flattened against the rock, eyes shut, whispering what might have been a prayer or an inventory list. Odessa followed. Then Wren.

Halfway through the narrows, her foot rolled on a loose stone.

It was nothing. A stumble. She caught herself on the wall, gasped—and the gasp became a word, half a word, the start of a charm she'd been casting since she was six years old, the hedge-witch's reflexive little spell for steadying the ground beneath your feet. Bram saw it leave her like a bird leaving a cage. Saw it hit the bells.

Every bell in the narrows rang at once.

The sound was indescribable. Not loud so much as *total*, a chord of bronze and bone that went through Bram's skull and chest and bones like ice water, and above them the wires began to sing the alarm onward, bell waking bell, the note racing away up the pass toward whatever garrison waited at the far end with chains and wagons.

"Run!" Odessa roared, all discipline gone. "RUN!"

But Wren had gone rigid, rooted in the middle of the narrows, her face white, her lips moving helplessly as the alarms climbed and climbed—and Jonah turned around.

Bram would remember it the rest of his life. The man walked *back*. Into the ringing, into the noise, into the certain death of it, and took Wren's face in both his hands, and said something to her that Bram couldn't hear over the bells. And then Jonah Whitlock closed his eyes and stopped holding it in.

Bram felt it even from the edge of the narrows—a pressure drop, a taste of copper and ozone, the hairs on his arms standing up. But no lightning came. No thunder. Instead Jonah did the hardest thing Bram had ever seen anyone do with power: he *held* it. Held the storm inside his own body like a dam holds a flood, and reached out with it—not striking, not calling, but *absorbing*, pulling the alarm-song out of the air itself, drinking the resonance out of bell after bell after bell until the chord faltered, wavered, and died.

The last bell shivered into silence. The echo rolled away up the pass and didn't come back.

Then Jonah's knees buckled, and Bram caught him before he hit the stone.

* * *

They made it off the pass by nightfall, moving fast and low through the tree line on the far side, and camped in a hollow with no fire because Odessa said no fire and Odessa was right. Jonah slept like the dead, or like the drowned, flat on his back and grey-faced, and Wren sat beside him and didn't move for hours.

Bram took the watch again. Around midnight, Wren came and sat with him, same as the night before, knees up, chin down.

"He drank my charms," she said. "All of them. All day. I counted eleven times he reached for me." Her voice was rough. "He could have just told me to take them off. He could have let me learn. Instead he carried mine too, on top of his own, and he never said one word."

"No," Bram said. "He wouldn't."

"Why?" The question cracked open in the middle. "Why would he do that?"

Bram looked at her for a long time. He was not a clever man, and he knew it, but he had spent a lifetime reading weight loads and weather and faces, and he read hers now.

"Because some men," he said slowly, "would rather break themselves than watch someone else carry what they carry alone." He nodded toward the sleeping shape in the dark. "Ask him sometime what he's really hauling up that mountain. Not the storm. The other thing."

Wren looked at Jonah's still form, and Bram saw her expression change—saw something settle in her, some question becoming a decision.

"What other thing?" she asked.

Bram shrugged, slow and heavy. "That's his to tell. But I'd ask soon, girl. Before the mountain does."

Above them, far off in the dark, thunder rolled—though the sky was clear as glass, and there was no storm anywhere in the world but the one sleeping ten feet away.
