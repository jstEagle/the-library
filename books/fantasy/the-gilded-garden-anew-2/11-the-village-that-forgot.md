## 11. The Village That Forgot

The village had no name on any map Wren had ever seen, and by the time they reached it, she understood why. Names, it seemed, could be taken from places as easily as from people.

They came down out of the high ground at dusk, Bram leading with Odessa close behind him, and the road beneath their feet was strangely clean—no cart ruts, no hoof-churned mud, no droppings of beasts. A road that had been swept by something more thorough than brooms. Wren noticed it before anyone spoke of it: a road polished smooth as a river stone, as if the village had been wiped down like a table after a meal.

"Smoke," Jonah said quietly. "Old smoke. Weeks old."

"Not burning smoke," Pell added, and his voice had the flat carefulness of a man reading a document he wished he hadn't opened. "That's not how fire smells. Fire is greedy. This is—" He stopped, searching for the word, and Wren watched him fail to find it.

*Empty,* she thought. *It smells empty.*

The houses stood in neat rows along a single street, thatched roofs mended with new straw, shutters freshly painted, gardens weeded and tended. Everything was cared for. Everything was wrong. There were no dogs. Wren realized this first, because hedge-witches learned to read villages by their dogs—a place's dogs told you whether strangers were welcome, whether children ran loose, whether the night held anything to fear. This village had no dogs at all, and no cats, and when they passed the first window with lamplight glowing behind its shutter-boards, Wren saw why the light looked strange.

It was too even. Too calm. The kind of light made by people who had nothing left to be afraid of, because fear itself had been taken from them along with everything else worth fearing for.

"We shouldn't stop here," Jonah said.

"We're stopping here," Odessa said. "Bram's favoring his ankle, the horses need water, and I need sleep or I'll start making decisions with my temper instead of my head. Whatever happened here is finished happening." She swung down from her mare and looked up at the nearest house, at the woman standing in its doorway with a basket of washing on her hip. "Ho there! Travelers seeking shelter for the night. We can pay."

The woman smiled. It was a warm smile, an open smile, and it did not reach anywhere near her eyes—not because it was false, but because whatever part of a person eyes belong to had been scooped out and replaced with something smooth and pleasant. "Of course! We've room. We always have room." She paused, and her brow creased faintly, like water disturbed by a pebble and then settling again. "We have... so much room."

Wren felt the hair rise on her arms.

*So much room.* As if the village were a coat cut for more shoulders than now filled it. As if every house had doors built for families larger than the ones living behind them.

Odessa heard it too. Wren saw it land in her the way bad news lands in a smuggler—quickly, silently, filed away for later. "Kind of you," Odessa said smoothly. "We'll take the barn, if it's all the same. We keep early hours."

"As you like!" The woman beamed. "Supper's at sundown. You'll want supper. Everyone says my stew is the best in—" She stopped again. The pebble-in-water frown. Longer this time. "The best in the..." She laughed, bright and unbothered. "Well. The best around, anyway."

She turned back inside, humming.

Nobody moved for a moment. Then Pell said, very softly, "I've seen Audit wagons. I've filed the manifests. But I've never seen what comes *after*."

"Now you have," Odessa said. "Barn. Everyone in the barn. Nobody eats the stew."

* * *

The barn was clean too, which was somehow worse. Swept floor, fresh straw, stalls mended with lumber that still smelled of the saw. Someone had prepared this barn for animals that were no longer there, or for people who no longer remembered preparing it.

They ate their own hard bread and dried meat by the light of a shuttered lantern, and Wren sat near the door where she could see the village through the gap, and she watched the people come and go in the evening calm. A man splitting wood. Two women walking with baskets. Children—three of them, playing a game with stones in the last of the light, laughing the way children laugh.

Only three children. In a village this size, there should have been a dozen at least. And the game they played, Wren realized, watching closely, was a game with rules missing. They arranged the stones in patterns and then stopped, all three at once, staring at the arrangement as though waiting for something to tell them what came next. Then they'd scatter the stones and begin again, and stop again, and scatter them again.

A game someone had taught them. A game whose teacher had been removed from the middle of it, leaving the shape of her absence everywhere and no memory of her anywhere.

"Wren." Jonah's voice, low. He'd come to stand beside her without her noticing, which unsettled her more than she wanted to admit—she was getting used to him being there, and that was a dangerous thing to get used to. "You see it too."

"It's not that they took people," she said. "It's that they took *having had* people. Look at the houses. Look at the road. They didn't just remove the gifted—they sanded down the world until the holes closed over."

"The ledger," Pell said from behind them. He'd gone gray around the mouth, and he kept rubbing his ink-stained fingers together as though trying to scrub something off them. "I told you it records souls. I should have told you what recording means, in court terms. A record isn't a copy, Wren. A record is a claim. When your name goes into that book, you don't belong to yourself anymore—you belong to the page. And the page can be... edited." He swallowed. "I filed edit-orders once. I thought they were administrative. Redactions. I thought redaction meant hiding a name from the wrong eyes." His laugh came out broken. "It means cutting the name out of everyone else's eyes too. Out of mothers. Out of children."

In the doorway, the last light failed, and somewhere across the village a mother called a child home to supper—and Wren counted the names called, and there were only two names called for three children, and one of the children simply got up and followed the other child's mother home, unquestioning, as though that were where he'd always slept.

Except—he hesitated. At the threshold. One small hand on the doorframe, looking back down the darkening street toward the barn, toward the hills beyond it, toward nothing, toward everything.

He stood there long enough that the woman calling him had to call again, and then he went inside, and the door closed, and Wren found that her hands had curled into fists without her permission.

"I'm going to find out what happened here," she said. "All of it."

"Wren—" Jonah started.

"Don't." She didn't look at him. She couldn't; if she looked at him right now she'd either shout or cry, and she refused to do either in front of him, not tonight, not with the shape of what he wasn't telling her sitting between them like a third person at the fire. She'd felt it since Vellmark—the way he went quiet whenever her debt came up, the way Odessa watched him watch Wren, the way Pell's documents made him flinch. He was carrying something about her, about the mountain, about the price of her own soul, and he thought she didn't know there was anything to carry. "Don't tell me to be careful. Careful is what the people in this village were. Careful is what got them hollowed out while they slept. I'm done being careful with other people's secrets. Including yours."

She heard him breathe in, hold it, let it go. "Then take the lantern," he said finally. "And take me with you."

"No." Now she looked at him, and she let him see how much it cost her. "You stay. If what's in this village is what I think it is, I need to know it's real and not something you'd soften for my sake. You're too kind to show me the truth straight, Jonah Whitlock. So I'll go get it myself."

She picked up the lantern before he could argue, and stepped out into the forgetting.

* * *

The village at night was worse than the village at dusk, because at night the emptiness had room to move around in.

Wren walked the street slowly, holding the lantern low, and she did what hedge-witches do: she listened with more than her ears. Every place holds residue. Grief, joy, anger—all of it soaks into wood and plaster and doorstep-stone the way rain soaks into earth, and a witch with patience and a little borrowed sigil-work can press her palm against a wall and feel the shape of what lived there. Her mother had taught her that, back before her mother had a name anyone feared. *Houses remember, little wren. People forget. Houses never do.*

She pressed her palm against the first doorpost.

Grief. Old grief, worn smooth—but underneath it, stranger than grief: a *smoothness*, a place where feeling should have been and wasn't, like running a tongue over a tooth pulled years ago. The house remembered a family. Four places at the table. And now three, and the fourth place not mourned but simply *unnoticed*, the way you don't notice a sound has stopped until someone plays you the silence where it used to be.

She went door to door. Doorpost to doorpost. The same everywhere. A husband gone and a wife who set out two cups each morning without wondering why she owned two cups. A grandmother gone and grandchildren who played games with rules missing. An entire village stitched full of holes that had been sewn shut from the outside, and the thread used for the sewing was the same thread Wren had felt once before—in the sigils on Jonah's charms, in the old court-script Pell carried in his document case. Court-work. Ledger-work. The mountain's handwriting.

Near the well at the center of the village, she found the boy.

He was maybe seven, sitting on the well's stone lip in the dark with his knees drawn up, and he wasn't crying, which was the most frightening thing about him. Children who are sad cry. This boy was doing something past sadness. He was *waiting*, with the terrible patience of the very young, for something he could not name to come back.

"You should be asleep," Wren said gently.

"They say I have to sleep in Marta's house now." He said it without resentment, reciting a fact that had been handed to him like a borrowed coat. "Marta says she's always been my aunt. But Marta's hands are wrong."

Wren crouched down, keeping the lantern between them so its light wouldn't frighten him. "Wrong how?"

"Aunts' hands are soft." He turned his own small hands over, studying them. "Marta's hands are hard. She kneads bread all day. My—" He stopped. The pebble-in-water frown, the same one the washing-woman had worn, crossing his small face. Then, quieter, in a voice that seemed to come from somewhere deeper than seven years old: "My mother's hands were soft. She was a candle-maker. No. She wasn't. Nobody was. Why would a village need a candle-maker? We have lamps."

His voice cracked on the last word, and finally, finally, he cried—but he cried like someone crying over a dream upon waking, furious and confused, wiping his face with his sleeve as though the tears themselves were a mistake someone had made in him.

"She sang a song," he whispered. "About a garden. A gilded garden. With a wall around it, and nobody could get in unless she—" He pressed both fists against his eyes. "Unless she *what*. Unless she WHAT? I can't—there's a hole where the words go and it doesn't HURT, that's the worst part, it doesn't hurt, it's just GONE, like a tooth, like a—" He looked up at Wren, and his eyes in the lantern-light were wet and ancient and seven years old all at once. "Did you take her?"

"No," Wren said, and her voice came out steady, which surprised her, because nothing inside her was steady. "No, love. But I know who did. And I'm going to make them give her back."

"How?" The question was simple, unanswerable, a child's question with a child's terrible clarity. "If nobody remembers her, how will she know the way home?"

Wren had no answer. She knelt there in the dust with a crying child and a guttering lantern and the whole hollowed village breathing its smooth empty breath around her, and she understood, all at once and completely, what she was walking toward.

The ledger didn't record souls. It *ate* them. It reached out of its vault beneath the mountain and into kitchens and cradles and songs half-sung, and it unmade people not just from the world but from the *remembering* of the world, and it left behind villages of pleasant smiling hollows who set out extra cups and taught their children games with the middle missing. Aldric wasn't hunting the gifted to register them. Registration was the harvest. The taking was the point. Somewhere under Mount Sereth, pages turned, and mothers became candle-makers who never existed, and songs lost their endings, and a boy sat on a well in the dark waiting for a hole in the world to explain itself.

Fear would have sent her running. What rose in her instead was colder and harder and had no name yet, and it settled into her bones like a blade settling into a sheath.

She took off her own shawl and wrapped it around the boy's shoulders. "What's your name?" she asked.

"Tarn."

"Tarn. Listen to me. Your mother was real. Whatever they cut out of this village, they couldn't cut it out of you, because you're too stubborn to forget. So you hold onto that song. Sing it every day. Get the ending wrong if you have to—make one up. Because someday, someone's going to walk into this village and ask about a candle-maker, and you're going to be the only answer left in the whole world. Do you understand me?"

He nodded, sniffling, gripping the shawl.

"And Tarn—if anyone with a wagon and a clipboard ever comes asking questions, you run to the hills and you don't look back. Promise me."

"I promise."

She walked him to Marta's door, and Marta opened it with that same warm, vacant smile, and Wren made herself return it, and inside her the cold hard thing grew another inch.

When she got back to the barn, they were all awake, all waiting, and Jonah stood first, and the question in his face was naked and helpless and hers to refuse or answer.

She answered it by throwing Pell's document case open on the straw between them.

"Show me the mountain," she said. "Show me every corridor, every gate, every lock. And show me the page where my name is written—because I've seen what that page does, and I'm not afraid of it anymore. I'm going to Sereth, and I'm going to put my own hand on that ledger, and I am going to teach it what a hedge-witch does with things that steal children's mothers."

Pell went pale. Odessa's eyes narrowed, sharp with recalculating. And Jonah—

Jonah looked at her like a man watching a storm build over a horizon he'd been pretending was clear, and Wren thought, with a fierce and terrible tenderness, *you should talk, storm-caller. You should tell me what you're carrying. Because I'm done waiting.*

But what she said aloud was: "We leave at first light. And this time, nobody keeps secrets from anybody. Starting with whatever it is you're all not saying about Mount Sereth."

The lantern hissed. Outside, far off, faint as memory, someone in the hollowed village began to sing a song about a garden—with no ending.
