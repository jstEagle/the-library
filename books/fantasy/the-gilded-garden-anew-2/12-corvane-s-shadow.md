## 12. Corvane's Shadow

The mountain announced itself long before they could see it.

Jonah felt it first in his teeth—a low, grinding pressure, like the moment before lightning finds its ground. Then the wind changed, coming down off the high passes carrying a smell he had not encountered in five years: cold stone and old iron and something beneath both, something that had no name except *wrong*. The air of Mount Sereth did not move the way other air moved. It came at them in slow, deliberate pulses, as though the mountain itself were breathing.

"You feel it too," Odessa said. It was not a question. She had learned to read his silences the way sailors read cloud banks.

"It's like standing under a storm that never breaks," Jonah said. "Five years I've lived with weather. This isn't weather."

Bram grunted from the head of the column, where he walked with his axe across his shoulders and his eyes on the treeline. He had said perhaps forty words since Vellmark, and Jonah suspected most of them were still being held in reserve. Pell walked beside him, clutching his scribe's case to his chest like a shield, flinching at every crow-call. Wren brought up the rear, and Jonah made himself stop looking back at her—made himself, because every time he did, the guilt sat up in his chest like a animal waking, and he needed to be sharp for what lay ahead.

They had climbed for two days out of the burned country around Vellmark, following goat tracks and the ghost of an old pilgrim road that Pell swore had once carried ten thousand souls a year to the mountain's gates. Now it carried six: two smugglers, a defected scribe, a hedge-witch who kept practicing sigils in the dirt with her fingertip when she thought no one watched, and a man walking toward the place where his own name was written in a book he intended to break.

The foothills rose around them in dark ranks of pine. Somewhere above, hidden by cloud, the peak waited with its court and its ledger and its Steward, and beneath all of it—the thought Jonah kept turning over like a stone in his pocket—*Corvane*.

Master Corvane, who had taught him to call the wind by teaching him first to listen to it. Master Corvane, whose voice still lived in Jonah's hands whenever he shaped a charge of static between his palms: *Feel where the sky wants to go, boy. Your work is only to agree with it.* Master Corvane, who had exiled him with one hand and, if Pell's document told true, sold his soul with the other—and called it mercy.

"Camp here," Bram said, as the light went copper behind the western ridges. "No fire."

"No fire," Odessa agreed, and then, quieter, to Jonah: "You've gone somewhere. Where?"

"I'm trying to remember if Corvane ever taught anyone else." Jonah crouched, checking the ground on instinct, though there was nothing to check but pine needles and frost. "He kept students rarely. He said the gift thinned when it was shared, like blood in water. But he wasn't a man who worked alone. He always had someone watching."

"And you think whoever's watching is still watching."

"I think we've been too lucky since the pass road began. No patrols. No bells. Aldric's vanguard is burning cities three days south of us, and the road to his own mountain is empty." He looked up at her. "Odessa, empty roads frighten me more than full ones."

She held his gaze for a long moment. In the failing light her face was all planes and judgment, a face built for weighing cargo and men and finding both wanting. "Then we post watches. You take second with me. And Whitlock—if your past comes walking up this road tonight, you tell me before you fight it. Understood? No more private wars."

"No more private wars," he agreed, and did not entirely believe himself.

* * *

Second watch came down on him like a weight he'd been carrying all day without noticing. He sat against a boulder above the camp with the sleeping company below him—Wren curled near the dead fire ring, one hand open on the frozen ground as though she'd been writing sigils even in sleep—and he let himself do the thing he never let himself do: he reached, gently, into the sky.

Not calling. Just listening. Five years of discipline had taught him the difference the way a mute man knows the difference between speech and breath. He opened the smallest door in himself and let the night's weather speak to him.

And the night spoke back wrong.

There was no storm within fifty miles—he could feel the whole slow architecture of the region's air, the cold high pressure sitting fat and quiet over the peaks—but threaded through the stillness ran something else. A charge. Faint, disciplined, moving. Not weather at all. Weather was vast and mindless; this was small and *aimed*, and it was coming up the pilgrim road from the south, and it was close.

Jonah was on his feet before he finished understanding it.

"Bram," he breathed, not loudly—the man slept like a knife in a drawer, ready and silent, and woke the same way. One heartbeat, two, and Bram's eyes were open in the dark. "Something's coming. Gifted. Alone. From the south."

Bram didn't waste breath asking how he knew. He touched Odessa's shoulder once and she surfaced from sleep instantly, hand already on her belt-knife, and the three of them arranged themselves around the camp with the wordless economy of people who had done ugly things together before. Pell woke confused; Wren woke silently, and Jonah saw her eyes find his across the dead fire and read the truth in his face, because she had learned to read him too well these last weeks.

"What is it?" she whispered.

"Company," he said. "Stay down. Stay *quiet*—" and stopped, because the trees at the southern edge of the clearing had begun to glow.

It was not firelight. It was the light he knew better than his own heartbeat: the pale blue-white shimmer of ionized air, ozone blooming, the world's breath held before a strike. It crawled along the branches like frost given ambition, and out of the darkness between the pines stepped a figure in a traveler's grey cloak with the hood back, and the light ran along her arms like jewelry.

"Sera," Jonah said.

Her name left him before he decided to say it. Twelve years collapsed into a single syllable. She stood at the treeline with the storm-light dying slowly off her skin, and she was older—that was the first obscene fact, that time had continued without him—and harder, her face planed down to angles and watchfulness, a scar through one eyebrow that hadn't been there at nineteen. But her eyes were the same: grey-green, quick, always half-amused, the eyes of the girl who had been the best of Corvane's students until Jonah arrived, and who had hated him for exactly as long as it took to decide instead to love him like a sister.

"Sera," he said again, uselessly, because there was nothing else in his mouth.

"Hello, Jonah." Her voice was steady and cold and it cracked precisely once, on the second syllable of his name, before she mastered it. "You look terrible. Exile agrees with no one."

Odessa shifted at his left, blade low. Bram had moved, somehow, to flank the treeline. Sera noted them all with a flicker of professional contempt and dismissed them.

"Four of you armed and one of you useless," she said, nodding at Pell. "I counted you from the ridge at noon. I could have taken you asleep. I'm not going to take you at all, so everyone can stop performing bravery."

"Why are you here?" Jonah asked.

"Because Corvane sent me, and because I asked to be sent." She took another step into the clearing, and now he saw what the cloak had hidden: the collar. A thin band of dark metal at her throat, seamless, engraved with script too fine to read at distance—ledger-script, court-script, the writing of ownership. His stomach turned over. "He wanted a hunter. I wanted to see whether it was really you, walking up my mountain with a smuggler and a hedge-witch and a runaway clerk, planning to steal from a ledger that has been stealing from the world for four hundred years."

"You know what we're doing."

"I know everything you're doing. I've known since Vellmark burned. Do you imagine the court doesn't watch its own roads?" She smiled, and it was the old smile, the one that had meant *we are the only two people alive who understand this*, and it was terrible now, worn over something hollowed. "That's why I'm here, Jonah. Not to fight you. To warn you. Turn around. Go back to Greyharrow. Go back to selling your little charms to frightened women and being nobody. Because he knows. He has known since the day you crossed the river at Marlow's Ford. Every step you've taken toward this mountain, you've taken inside his palm."

The words landed like stones dropped into still water, each ripple worse than the last. Behind him he heard Wren's breath catch.

"He let us come," Jonah said slowly. "All this way. Why?"

"Because Corvane does nothing without a reason, and his reasons are always older than yours." Sera's jaw tightened. "He wants you here, Jonah. He has wanted you here for five years. You were never exiled—you were *planted*, like a seed in winter ground, and now the season's turned and he's waiting to see what grows." Her eyes went briefly, unwillingly, to Wren, and something moved through her expression that might have been pity. "And the girl. The girl with the debt. He speaks about her the way gardeners speak about weather they're expecting."

Wren stood up. Of course she did—she would not stay down, not with her name being discussed like a crop forecast. "What does that mean?" she demanded. "What am I to him?"

Sera looked at her for a long moment, and whatever she saw—the fury, the fear, the stubbornness that Jonah had watched carry her across half a kingdom—made her answer honestly. "A key. Or a lever. I don't know which, and neither does he, entirely, and that's the part that frightens him. That's the part that's made him careful. And Corvane careful is Corvane cruel." She turned back to Jonah. "So. Last kindness I have in me: walk away. All of you. There is no version of this where you win. There is no version where you even survive. He built the trap you're standing in before you were born."

"The ledger," Jonah said. "My name. Did you know? When we were students—did you know what he'd done?"

Something flickered across her face, fast as heat-lightning. "I found out three years ago. When I found my own." She lifted her chin slightly, and the collar caught the starlight. "Do you understand what this is? I fought him, Jonah. I fought the Audit when it came to our valley, I ran, I hid, I did everything right, and it didn't matter, because my name was already in the book. Sold years before, quietly, by the man I trusted more than the sky. They didn't hunt me. They just *collected* me. One morning the collar was simply there, and the Steward's voice was in my head explaining the terms, and I have been the court's hound ever since." Her voice stayed level throughout, and that was the worst thing in it—the levelness of a woman describing a wound so old it had become geography. "So yes. I knew what he did to you. I recognized the shape of it. We're the same shape, you and I. Two entries in a book, wearing skin."

"We're not the same," Jonah said. "You're wearing their collar. I'm going to burn the book."

For a moment—just a moment—the storm-light kindled along her arms again, unbidden, answering something in her that her collar couldn't quite govern, and Jonah saw the girl she had been looking out through the woman's scarred face with an expression of naked, furious longing. *Come with us,* he almost said. *We're going to free you too.* But he saw the arithmetic happen behind her eyes, the cold court arithmetic of collateral and consequence, and he understood before she spoke.

"If I don't report the exact terms of this meeting," Sera said quietly, "the collar reports it for me. If I return having killed you, I buy myself a year of quiet. If I return having let you live, I buy something else—I don't yet know what, and neither does Corvane, and that uncertainty is the only currency I have left." She rolled her shoulders, and the light died completely, and she was only a tired woman in a grey cloak standing in the frost. "So here is what happened tonight, and you will make it true by surviving it. I ambushed you. I gave you a chance to surrender. You refused. We fought." She raised one hand, and the air above the clearing shivered and *cracked*—a single thunderclap detonated directly overhead, enormous, flattening the silence, startling birds out of the pines in a black clatter of wings. Down in the camp Pell yelped. "There. Now it's true. Every patrol on this slope heard the storm-caller resist arrest. By dawn the story will be that I drove you into the high passes, wounded, scattered."

"Scattered," Odessa repeated flatly.

"Be scattered. Split up. Take the eastern goat trails, not the pilgrim road—the road is warded, has been for a month, and the wards drink magic like wine. You—" she pointed at Jonah, and her finger trembled, very slightly, the only confession she would permit herself, "—you especially must not touch your gift on the road. Not once. Not a spark. He'll be listening for exactly the music he taught you to make. Promise me."

"Why?" Jonah asked. "Why any of this? What are you buying with your year of quiet, Sera?"

She was already turning away, hood rising, the darkness folding around her shoulders like a returned garment. At the treeline she paused, half-turned, and the starlight caught the scar and the collar and the eyes that were still, underneath everything, the eyes of the girl who had shared Corvane's roof with him through six winters.

"A clear shot," she said. "When the moment comes—and it will come, because you're too stubborn to die and he's too proud to allow it—when you finally stand in front of him with the ledger burning and his whole gilded world coming down, I want to be the one holding the knife. That's what I'm buying." A pause. "Don't get yourself killed before I can spend it."

And she was gone—not running, simply withdrawing, the way weather withdraws, the pressure lifting off the clearing all at once, leaving only frost and pine-smell and the ringing aftermath of thunder.

No one moved for a long moment. Then Bram said, "Well," which from Bram was a speech.

"She's lying," Odessa said immediately. "Some of it. Maybe all of it. This is how hunters work—they give you hope with one hand and herd you with the other. Everything she told us could be a corridor built to funnel us exactly where Corvane wants us."

"Yes," Jonah said.

"She named a safe road. Hunters don't name safe roads."

"Yes."

"She told you not to use your gift, which conveniently keeps you blind and deaf to anything hunting you."

"*Yes*, Odessa." He dragged a hand down his face. His heart was still hammering with twelve years of grief and relief and rage, all of it tangled, none of it resolved. "Every word she said might be bait. And every word might also be true, because I *know* her. Knew her. And the woman I knew would rather cut off her own hand than wear that collar one day longer than she had to." He looked south, into the dark where she had vanished. "Which means either I'm a fool who trusts a hunter's story, or I'm the only person alive who can tell the difference between Sera lying and Sera surviving. And I don't know which I am anymore."

Behind him, softly, Wren spoke for the first time since the thunderclap.

"She said he talks about me like weather he's expecting." Her voice was small and very controlled, the control of someone holding a door shut against a flood. "Jonah. What did she mean? What does Corvane want with *me*?"

And Jonah, looking at her face in the starlight—at the girl who had paid for his life without knowing it, whom he had led halfway up a killer's mountain on a foundation of silence—understood that the lie he had been living was finally, inevitably, due.

"Wren," he said. "We need to talk. About the night we met. About what you actually bought, and what it cost you, and why I'm the one who owes it."

Above them, far up in the dark where the clouds hid the peak, the mountain breathed its slow, wrong breath—and somewhere inside it, he was certain, an old man smiled at news of their coming, and turned a page.
