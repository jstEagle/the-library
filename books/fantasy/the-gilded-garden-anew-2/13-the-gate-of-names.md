## 13. The Gate of Names

Pell had filed the order for this gate's construction eleven years ago, and he remembered the requisition number the way other men remembered the names of their children.

*Servants' Gate, West Face, Mount Sereth. Iron-bound oak, warded against unregistered gift-work. Keyed to scribes of the third rank and above.*

He had been twenty-two years old, proud of his fine new hand, proud that the Steward's office trusted him with a document bearing three seals. He had copied out the warding specifications in ink mixed with ash from the ledger's own candles, because that was what the specifications required, and he had not asked why a servants' door needed to be warded against magic at all. He had not asked anything, in those days. Asking was how scribes stopped being scribes.

Now he stood in the shadow of the west face with snow coming down sideways and five people watching him, waiting for him to open the door he had built.

"The keyhole is behind the third course of stone," he said. His voice came out thin against the wind. "There's a loose block. It was loose when I inspected it in—" He stopped. He had been about to say *when I inspected it in my last year*, as though the mountain were a place he had merely worked once, the way a man might speak of a harvest or a war. "It will still be loose. Stone doesn't forget."

"Stone doesn't," Bram said. "Men do."

Pell looked at him. Bram's face was half-lost under his hood, all beard and weather, but his eyes were steady, and Pell understood that this was not an accusation so much as a measurement—Bram measuring whether Pell was the kind of man who forgot, and finding the answer not yet written.

"I didn't forget," Pell said. "That's rather the problem."

* * *

The block came free easier than he expected, which frightened him more than resistance would have. Things decayed when they were abandoned; things that came away clean had been maintained. Someone had kept this door ready for use, and Pell could think of only one reason a court that hunted the gifted would keep a hidden servants' entrance into its soul-vault in working order: because the court sometimes needed to move through it quietly itself.

The key was where he had left it—not his key, but the shape of one, pressed into a hollow in the mortar by whatever hand maintained the door. A third-rank scribe's key was a plain iron thing, square-cut, worth less than a good knife. Pell had carried his on a cord around his neck for six years and had slept with it under his tongue during his final week in the court, terrified that someone would search his effects and find it, equally terrified that they wouldn't.

He fitted it now with fingers that would not quite stop shaking. The lock turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

"Words," Odessa said quietly at his shoulder. She had a knife out, though she held it low and casual, the way she did everything—like a woman who had made peace long ago with the fact that the world required tools. "You said there'd be words."

"There are." Pell wetted his lips. "A challenge and a countersign. They rotate them every season, but the servants never learn the new ones properly—they keep using the old ones because the guards can't be bothered to correct them, and eventually the old ones just... stay." He had documented that too, once, in a marginal note that had earned him a reprimand and, privately, a flicker of pride. Systems failed at their most bored points. He had built a career on knowing where boredom lived. "*Ash keeps the light.*"

"And if they've changed it?"

"Then we're standing at a locked door on the west face of Mount Sereth with an army two valleys behind us, and I'd suggest we spend our last minutes saying something honest to each other."

Nobody laughed. Wren pulled her cloak tighter. Jonah stood apart from the rest, the way he always did, looking up at the mountain as though listening to something inside it—and perhaps he was. Storm-callers heard weather before it arrived. Pell wondered what a mountain full of stolen souls sounded like to a man like that, and decided he was happier not knowing.

"*Ash keeps the light,*" Pell said to the door.

The oak shuddered. From somewhere deep in the iron, a voice like dry paper asked: "*Who files?*"

"Pell Marrow, third rank, Office of Registrations." His own name in his own mouth tasted like the ink he had mixed for eleven years. "Returned to complete an entry."

The pause went on so long that Wren's breath audibly caught. Then the bolts drew back one by one, each with a sound like a small bone breaking, and the door swung inward onto darkness and warmth and a smell Pell had hoped never to smell again: tallow, hot stone, and underneath it, faint as a memory of a memory, something sweet and wrong, like fruit going bad in a sealed room.

Soul-light. He knew the smell now. He hadn't known it then. That was the whole of his life's story in one sentence—he hadn't known it then.

* * *

Inside, the mountain hummed.

Pell had forgotten the hum, or told himself he had, but his body remembered it instantly—the low, continuous vibration in the floor and walls and teeth, felt more than heard, like standing inside a struck bell that never finished ringing. He watched Jonah feel it too, saw the storm-caller go rigid, one hand pressed flat against the corridor wall as though taking a pulse.

"What is that?" Wren whispered.

"The ledger," Pell said. "It's not a book the way you're thinking of a book. It's—" He searched for the words he had once written in other people's documents without ever writing them for himself. "Every name in it is a soul, and every soul is still *doing something*. Being kept. Being drawn on. The hum is thousands of people, all of them saying no at once, forever."

Wren had gone very pale. "They're alive in there?"

"They're *present* in there. I don't know that alive is the word." He started walking, because if he stopped walking he would have to look at her face, and he had spent six years perfecting the art of not looking at faces. "Follow me exactly. Touch nothing. The corridors are lit by the ledger—you'll see what I mean—and the light isn't light exactly. Don't let it touch your bare skin longer than you must."

"And why," Odessa said, falling into step beside him with the unhurried gait of a woman who had walked into a hundred places she shouldn't, "would a professional caution like that matter to you personally, scribe?"

"Because I've seen what happens to people who let it touch them." He kept his eyes forward. "There was a servant in the east galleries, a kitchen girl named Maret. She used to rest her hands on the rails while she waited outside the vault offices. Just leaned on them, day after day. By the time I left, she'd stopped speaking. Not mute—she'd stopped *wanting* to. She'd stand in the kitchens holding a pot and smiling at nothing, and the head cook said she was the happiest worker she'd ever had." His throat closed around the next part and he made himself say it anyway, because these five deserved the whole weight of it. "She was happy. That's what the light does when you give it time. It doesn't hurt you. It *agrees* with you until there's nothing left to disagree with."

No one spoke after that. Their footsteps made almost no sound on the stone, and the mountain's hum filled the silence like water fills a jar.

* * *

The first gallery opened ahead of them, and Pell heard Wren make a sound she immediately strangled.

The corridor was lit by niches cut into the walls, hundreds of them, each holding a flame that burned without fuel and gave off light the color of candlelight through honey. And in the light—if you looked directly, which Pell had not done in six years and forced himself to do now, for them, because they needed to understand—there were shapes. Faces, sometimes. Hands. A curve of shoulder. Impressions moving slowly in the glow like fish under ice, rising and sinking, rising and sinking.

"That's—" Wren's voice cracked. "Those are *people*."

"Fragments of people. What the ledger draws off when it takes a name." Pell heard his own voice go flat and clerical, the register he had used for eleven years, and hated it, and could not stop it. "The body walks around outside. Eats, sleeps, works, pays its taxes. But the—the *vividness*, the part that argues and grieves and wants things—it's in here, feeding the light." He gestured down the endless corridor, and his gesture took in, without either of them being able to count them, thousands of flames. "Aldric lights his court with it. The wards on the palace, the warming-stones in the winter barracks, the weapon they're building in the deep vaults. All of it burns the same fuel."

Jonah had stopped walking. He stood before one niche in particular—a flame slightly bluer than the others—and the shape in it was a young man's profile, sharp-jawed, restless, turning and turning as though looking for a window.

"Do you know them?" Wren asked softly. "Can you read them?"

"I filed some of them." The confession came out of Pell like a splinter coming out of flesh, slow and tearing. "Not the taking—scribes don't do the taking. We do the paper. A hunter brings in a name on a warrant, and I enter it in the transfer rolls, and my entry authorizes the drawing-off, and my counter-signature closes the file." He was aware of them all looking at him now, even Bram, even Odessa, and he made himself stand in it. "Eleven years. I don't know the number anymore. I stopped counting at four hundred because counting was how I thought I'd stay innocent, and then I understood that the counting was the crime—that as long as I was keeping score I could pretend I wasn't the scorekeeper."

"You were a clerk," Odessa said. There was no mercy in it and no cruelty either, only her usual flat accounting. "Clerks aren't the knife."

"No." Pell looked down the burning corridor. "But the knife doesn't work without the paper, and I knew that, and I kept the ink warm anyway. And then one day a warrant came across my desk with a name on it I recognized—a chandler's son from my own street in Vellmark, a boy who used to race paper boats in the gutters—and I sat there with my pen dipped and ready, and I discovered that I could write his name just as easily as any other. That was the day I understood what I was. Not a coward. Cowards run. I was a *function*. The mountain didn't need guards to keep me loyal. It only needed to make sure I never stopped being useful enough to feel important."

He began walking again, faster now, because the gallery was long and the faces in the walls were patient and he could feel his own name wanting to join them, wanting to lie down in the hum and agree with everything.

Behind him, softly, Wren touched his elbow. Just that. Just two fingers, brief, gone again. He did not deserve it and did not say so, because saying so would have made it about him, and for the first time in eleven years he wanted desperately for it not to be about him.

* * *

They descended. The stairs were cut wide and shallow—built, Pell explained in a whisper, for carts of supplies, though everyone knew what else had come down those stairs in the early years, before the quiet carts were moved to the roads. The hum deepened as they went, thickening from sound into pressure, and Jonah grew quieter and grayer with every turn until Wren took his arm and half-carried him, and he let her, which told Pell more about the storm-caller's state than any words would have.

"It's louder for him," Wren murmured, when they paused at a landing. "Isn't it."

"He hears what the light is made of," Pell said. "Or close enough. Gifted ears. To me it's a hum. To him it's probably—" He glanced at Jonah's white face and didn't finish.

"To him it's a room full of drowning people," Jonah said, without opening his eyes. "All of them calling for help in a language storms speak. So if you'll both forgive me, I'd like to keep moving before I do something catastrophically stupid and noble."

Odessa, at the rear with Bram, snorted softly. "First sensible thing anyone's said since the door."

They passed a junction where a secondary corridor branched toward the offices—Pell's old offices—and he felt the pull of it like a hook behind his sternum. Somewhere down there was his desk. His stool, worn to the shape of him. The shelf where he had kept his private store of good Vellmark paper, hoarded against regulation stock, because a man had to have one small excellence even in a place like this. He could almost see the dust motes in the lamplight, could almost hear the scratch of forty other pens, the cough of old Sarel in the corner who had died at his desk and been replaced before the ink on his last file dried.

*You could walk down there,* said a voice in Pell that he recognized with horror as his own, aged six years and grown very reasonable. *You could sit down. You could pick up a pen. It would be so easy, and you were so good at it.*

"Left here," he said aloud, sharply, and turned his back on the corridor, and walked.

* * *

The last stair let out onto a gallery overlooking the antechamber of the vault proper, and Pell called the halt with a raised fist, a smuggler's gesture he had learned from Odessa and performed badly.

Below, the antechamber spread out like the interior of a bell: domed, ribbed with pale stone, lit from a source none of them could see. And in the center of it, flanked by two hunters in grey lacquered armor, stood a desk.

One desk. One lamp. One figure seated behind it, small and neat, writing.

"A checkpoint," Odessa breathed. "In the middle of their own mountain?"

"Not a checkpoint." Pell's mouth had gone dry. "A *registry point*. Nothing enters the vault without being entered. Whatever comes down that stair gets a line in a book before it goes further." He swallowed. "And that's not a guard at the desk. That's a second-rank registrar. Which means the court is expecting traffic tonight."

"Why tonight?" Wren whispered.

Pell looked at the neat little figure below, scratching away under its lamp, and felt six years of nightmares assemble themselves into an answer he did not want to say aloud.

"Because Aldric's vanguard reached Vellmark eight days ago," he said. "And every prisoner taken there has to be processed somewhere. The mountain is expecting a delivery." He drew a slow breath. "Which means that in a few hours, that desk below us is going to be the busiest place in Sereth—and right now, while it's quiet, is the only chance we will ever get to walk past it."

Below them, unhurried, the registrar turned a page.

The hum of the stolen rose around them like a tide, and Pell, who had spent eleven years learning every sound the mountain made, heard for the first time something underneath it—far down, deep in the vault, thin and rhythmic and wrong.

Hammering. Someone was building something.

Jonah's hand closed slowly into a fist, and static crawled along the stone beneath their feet like frost.
