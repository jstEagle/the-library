## 14. The Steward of Sereth

The corridor to the vault was lit by light that had no source.

Jonah had noticed it first in the servants' passages—lamps of pale glass that burned without flame, without oil, without warmth—and Pell had explained it in the flat voice of a man confessing: *soul-light. Drawn from the ledger. Every lamp in this mountain is someone.* Now, walking the final stretch toward the heart of Mount Sereth, Jonah counted the lamps as they passed and made himself stop counting, because the number kept becoming people.

The walls here were older than the rest of the mountain. Rough-hewn stone gave way to something smoother, darker, veined with threads of silver that pulsed faintly, like veins under skin. The air tasted of ozone and old paper. Somewhere far below—or perhaps everywhere at once—a hum lived in the rock, low and constant, the sound a tuning fork makes long after it has been struck.

Wren walked close behind him. He could hear her breathing, quick and shallow, and feel the small heat of her fear through the space between them. Bram brought up the rear with his hand resting on the haft of his axe, not gripping it, just touching it, the way a man touches a talisman. Odessa moved at Jonah's shoulder, silent, her eyes cataloguing everything—the doors, the seams in the stone, the places where a body might hide or a blade might come from.

Pell stopped so suddenly that Wren nearly walked into him.

"Here," the scribe whispered.

Before them stood a door unlike any other in the mountain. It was not wood or iron but a slab of grey glass, thick as a man's arm is long, and set into its surface were hundreds of small brass plates, each engraved with a name. Jonah read them without wanting to. *Maren Ostwick. Tallow-bright, third choir. Ilse Vane.* His breath caught on that one—he glanced at Odessa, who had gone very still—but the plate was old, the engraving worn, and whatever relation she might have had to its bearer said nothing now.

"The Gate of Names," Pell said. "The last door. Beyond it is the ledger itself." He swallowed. "I filed documents through this door for eleven years. I never once saw what was on the other side."

"How does it open?" Odessa asked.

"It doesn't," said a voice from the door itself. "It opens you."

* * *

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, from the glass and the silver-veined stone and the humming dark beyond it. Then the brass plates began to move—not all at once but in a slow ripple, names sliding aside like scales over an eye, and where they parted the grey glass thinned to transparency, and through it Jonah saw the vault.

And he saw the Steward.

The chamber beyond was round and vast, its ceiling lost in shadow, its floor a mosaic of interlocking circles each inscribed with script too fine to read. At its center rose the ledger—not a book, not exactly, though books were part of it: thousands upon thousands of volumes stacked in spiraling towers around a central column of white stone, and above the column, suspended in nothing, burned a single point of light so bright and so still that looking at it felt like looking at a held breath.

Beneath it sat the Steward.

He had been a man once. That much remained—a frame of shoulders, a suggestion of hands folded in his lap, the ruin of a face turned toward the door. But he was hollowed, the way a tree is hollowed by centuries of wind: still standing, still shaped like himself, but emptied out and filled with something else. Light moved beneath his skin the way the silver moved in the walls. His eyes were two points of the same sourceless glow that lit the corridors, and when he looked at Jonah through the thinning glass, Jonah felt the look land on him like weather.

"Five of you," the Steward said. "A smuggler, her brother, a scribe who regrets, a hedge-witch whose name I know by heart—" his gaze touched Wren, and she flinched as if physically struck, "—and a storm-caller."

"I'm no one," Jonah said automatically. Five years of saying it. The words came out before thought.

The Steward smiled. It was the worst thing yet, that smile—gentle, patient, terribly human in the ruined face. "I have heard that sentence ten thousand times," he said. "In this room, it has never once been true."

The glass door dissolved. Not opened—dissolved, running down its own frame like frost surrendering to morning, and the hum of the mountain swelled around them, and there was nothing left between the company and the heart of Sereth.

Odessa's hand found Jonah's arm. "Trap," she breathed.

"Probably," Jonah breathed back. He stepped through anyway.

* * *

Up close, the ledger towers were worse than awe-inspiring. They were intimate. Each volume's spine bore a single name, and the leather of each cover had the particular softness of something that had been handled with love, or at least with care, for a very long time. Jonah passed within arm's reach of a book labeled *Sera of Corvane's House* and felt his stomach turn. Another: *Pellus Andric, Scribe, Third Rank.* Pell had stopped walking entirely. He stood with his fists clenched at his sides, staring at his own name the way a man stares at his own grave.

"Don't touch anything," the Steward said mildly, from his seat at the center. "The books remember being touched."

"What are you?" Wren asked. Her voice was thin but it did not break, and Jonah loved her a little for that.

The Steward considered the question with the air of a scholar who has been asked it many times and has never once grown tired of refining the answer. "I was called Aerun," he said. "Before there were kings, before there was a court, before anyone thought to write a soul down. I was the first storm-caller. Perhaps the only one; the gift is rare, and rarer every century, and it burns what carries it." He lifted one hand, and Jonah saw that the fingers ended in stumps of light, worn away like candle stubs. "I called storms for forty years. Then I learned to call smaller things. Rain for crops. Wind for mills. And then I learned that a storm is only a debt—the sky taking back what it lent—and I began to wonder what else could be borrowed against."

"You built the ledger," Jonah said.

"I built the idea of it. Others built the thing." The hollowed eyes closed briefly. "Every borrower needs a lender, child. Every lender needs collateral. I was the first collateral. I signed my name into my own book to prove it could be done, and the book has kept me alive ever since—alive the way a wick is kept alive by the candle it burns in." He gestured faintly at the towers around him. "Ten thousand names. Each one a little light. I am fed on the interest."

"That's monstrous," Wren said.

"Yes," the Steward agreed, without any defensiveness at all, and somehow that was more monstrous than denial would have been. "It is also the reason your king cannot simply burn this place, though he would like to. The ledger does not merely record souls, hedge-witch. It holds them. Strike the book, and every name in it goes out at once." He looked at her with terrible gentleness. "You would be killing everyone you came to save."

Silence. The hum filled it. Jonah watched Odessa's face and saw the pragmatist in her doing arithmetic she hated—the plan they had carried across half a kingdom, *steal the names, run*, collapsing under the weight of what the old creature had just said.

"Why tell us this?" Jonah asked. "Why open the door? You must have known why we came."

"Because I have been waiting for you for a very long time, Jonah Whitlock." The Steward unfolded himself from his seat, and the motion was wrong, joints moving like something remembering how bodies worked. He came closer, and the light in him brightened, and Jonah felt his own gift stir in answer—an awful recognition, storm answering storm across five empty years. "Do you know what I see when I look at you?"

"A man selling charms," Jonah said.

"I see a lightning bolt that has spent five years pretending to be a fence post." The Steward laughed, a dry rustling sound. "I know the discipline of it. I invented the discipline of it. You bind yourself down so tight that even you forget what's coiled inside—and then some night the sky comes over the mountain and every hair on your arms stands up, and you weep, quietly, where no one can see, because you miss it. Am I close?"

Jonah did not answer. He didn't need to. Wren had gone pale beside him; Odessa had turned to look at him with new eyes, recalculating again, the way she had at the checkpoint when the rain had obeyed him.

"I am going to offer you a trade," the Steward said. "Not the trade your usurper's court would offer—that one is bait on a hook, and you are too clever for it, and anyway it isn't mine. Mine is simple. Freely given, freely taken, no coercion, no trick. The girl's page sits in my keeping, bound by the old law: only the Steward may strike a name, and only for payment of equal weight." He spread his ruined hands. "Her name weighs almost nothing, Jonah Whitlock. A hedge-witch. A healing bought for a stranger. Small coin. But yours—" the light in his eyes deepened, hungry and reverent at once, "—yours is a storm-caller's name, freely surrendered, uncoerced, given whole. Do you understand what that would weigh? There has not been such a name entered willingly since mine. It would balance her debt a hundred times over. It would buy her freedom, and leave change enough to strike a dozen others."

"And what happens to me?" Jonah asked, though he already knew.

"You walk out of this mountain ordinary," the Steward said softly. "No gift. No storm. No name in any book—you would be, truly and finally, no one. Which I understand," he added, and his voice cracked for the first time, ancient and raw, "is what you have been practicing to become for five years."

* * *

They put him in a side alcove while the company argued—Pell pacing, Odessa interrogating the Steward about mechanics, Bram standing guard with his arms crossed like a wall. Jonah barely heard them. He stood beneath one of the soul-lamps and looked at his hands.

He thought about the checkpoint outside Greyharrow, the rain rising at his word, the sky opening like a door he'd forgotten he owned. He thought about how it had felt—like coming home, like self-destruction, both at once, and how in the months since he had dreamed of it nearly every night, waking with his fingers curled around lightning that wasn't there.

He thought about the garden he sometimes imagined, in the life after all this. Quiet. Ordinary. Known.

*You would be, truly and finally, no one.*

And here was the terrible thing, the thing he would not say aloud in front of them: part of him wanted it. Not the sacrifice—sacrifice was easy, sacrifice was just another kind of drama, and he had watched Corvane perform dramas all his youth. What he wanted was the permission. To lay the gift down and have it be done, finished, decided by something larger than his own cowardice. To stop carrying the weight of the choice every single day. The Steward was offering him not death but rest, and rest, after five years of shrinking, sounded like grace.

That was how he knew it was a trap.

Because he knew this shape. He knew it the way you know a face under a hood. Corvane's bargains had always looked like this—never cruelty offered plainly, always relief. *Give up the dangerous thing, boy, and be free of it. Sign here, and the burden becomes mine.* His master had traded in exhaustion, in the deep human wish to stop deciding, and he had dressed every hook in the language of mercy. And this creature at the center of the mountain, this hollowed first storm-caller feeding on ten thousand souls—when he spoke of weights and balances and freely given gifts, Jonah heard Corvane's cadence underneath, patient and loving and absolutely certain that the person across from him would rather pay anything than keep choosing.

"You recognize it," said a voice at his elbow.

Wren. She had slipped away from the others, and she stood looking up at him, her sharp hedge-witch's eyes narrowed—not angry, not yet, but reading him the way she was learning to read sigils, letter by letter.

"Recognize what?"

"Whatever it is you're not telling me." Her voice stayed low. "You've been carrying it since Vellmark. Since before. You look at me like I'm a debt, Jonah. Like I'm something you owe." Her chin came up. "So before you go offering yourself to that thing in there for my sake—I want to know what I'm worth, and to whom, and why."

The hum of the mountain filled the silence between them. In the vault's center, the Steward waited with the patience of a creature who had waited five hundred years and could wait five hundred more, his ruined face turned toward them, his offer hanging in the soul-lit air like a storm about to break.

"Wren," Jonah began, "there's something you need to know about the night I—"

The lamps went dark.

All of them, at once, every corridor of the mountain plunging black—and in the sudden dark, from somewhere far above, came a sound none of them had hoped to hear so soon: alarm bells, ringing down through the stone, and beneath them, faint but unmistakable, the crackle of storm-magic that was not Jonah's.

Sera had found the gate.
