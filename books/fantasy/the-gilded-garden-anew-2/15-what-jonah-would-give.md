## 15. What Jonah Would Give

The mountain had a heartbeat.

Wren felt it through the soles of her boots as they waited in the dark corridor outside the vault—a slow, patient pulse, like something enormous sleeping beneath the stone. The soul-light in the walls flickered with it, dimming and brightening, dimming and brightening, and she understood now what fed it. Names. Thousands of names, each one a small flame torn from a living person and hung here like a lamp.

She pressed her palm against the wall and the light leaned toward her touch, hungry as a cat. She snatched her hand back.

*Not mine,* she reminded herself. *Not yet.*

Pell stood at the corner of the passage, his scribe's credentials still clutched in one sweating hand, counting footsteps somewhere far above. Bram leaned against the wall with his arms crossed, a man who could sleep standing up and often did. Odessa crouched over a scrap of map she'd drawn from memory of Pell's descriptions, muttering numbers to herself—guard rotations, door widths, the geometry of escape.

And Jonah stood apart from all of them, the way he always did, watching the vault doors at the end of the corridor as if he could see through them. As if he already knew what was waiting on the other side.

Wren had known Jonah Whitlock for five years. She knew how he took his tea—too sweet, because sweetness had been scarce where he grew up, or so he'd claimed once, in a rare unguarded moment. She knew that he hummed when he worked on his charms, tuneless little fragments that stopped the instant anyone noticed. She knew that when it rained in Greyharrow, he would stand in the doorway of his shop with an expression she'd never been able to name until this journey, until she'd seen him call lightning down on a checkpoint and understood: it wasn't longing. It was grief. He looked at rain the way other people looked at graves.

She thought she knew him. She had staked her life on knowing him, more times than either of them had counted.

But he had been lying to her since the moment they met. And tonight, in a corridor lit by stolen souls, she was going to learn exactly how much.

* * *

It began, as these things did, with kindness.

They had an hour before Pell's old contact made his rounds—an hour of waiting, which was the most dangerous thing a company could do, because waiting gave people time to talk. Odessa had forbidden talking about the plan; there was nothing left to plan that hadn't been planned twice. So they sat in the dark and let their guards down by degrees, the way soldiers do, and Jonah sat beside Wren against the humming wall, close enough that she could feel the storm coiled in him like heat off a forge.

"You should know," he said quietly, "before we go in. What you're walking toward."

"I know what I'm walking toward." She kept her voice flat. "A ledger. My name in it. A Steward who can strike it."

"That's not all of it."

"Jonah."

"You sold your soul," he said. "Five years ago. To buy a healing for a dying man. You've never told me who, and I've never asked, because I—" His breath caught, and Wren felt the air pressure shift around them, the faint static taste of a storm held too long behind its clouds. "Because I already knew who. Wren. The man you saved was me."

The corridor went very quiet. Even the mountain's pulse seemed to hold its breath.

Wren stared at him. Somewhere above them, water dripped from stone to stone, each drop landing like a struck bell.

"No," she said.

"The fever. After the marsh crossing. You found me half-dead in a ditch outside Harrowgate and you couldn't heal me—not fully, not alone—and so you went to the court brokers and you signed. That's why the Audit will find you first. That's why your name is already in the ledger, waiting." His voice cracked on the last word. "I didn't know. Gods, Wren, I swear I didn't know until Pell showed me the record. If I had known—"

"If you had known," she said, and her voice came out strange and small, "what? You'd have died?"

"I'd have found another way."

"There wasn't another way!" It came out louder than she meant, and Bram's eyes opened across the corridor, and Odessa looked up from her map, but Wren didn't care. Five years of something were cracking open inside her, and she couldn't stop it. "There was nothing else. I tried everything else. I sat with you for three days while the fever ate you alive and I burned through every herb I had and every trick my grandmother taught me, and you were *dying*, Jonah, and the broker said—I remember what he said—he said the mountain takes payment in kind, and I thought, well, it can have my name, names are cheap, everyone has one—"

She was crying now. She hated that she was crying.

"—and it wasn't cheap," she finished. "Was it. It was never cheap. It's been burning in that wall this whole time, hasn't it? A little piece of me, lighting their corridors."

"Wren—"

"And now you're going to tell me you'll trade yourself for me." She laughed, and the laugh was ugly. "That's why you brought us here. That's why you offered Odessa your storm-gift for passage. That's why you've been looking at me like that since Vellmark, like I'm something being carried to an altar. You're not here to steal my name back. You're here to *buy* it. With yours."

Jonah said nothing. In the soul-light his face was pale and open and utterly without defense, and that—that was the worst part. He wasn't even going to lie to her. He was just going to stand there and let her see it.

"Yes," he said finally.

"You absolute *fool*."

"Wren, listen—"

"No. You listen." She stood, because she couldn't bear to be sitting next to him, couldn't bear the warmth of him through her sleeve when everything warm between them turned out to be built on a transaction. "Do you know what you're doing? Do you actually understand it? You're telling me that the best thing I ever did—the one good, free thing, the thing I chose, the stranger in the ditch I saved because saving people is the only prayer I've ever believed in—you're telling me that was a debt. That it's been a debt this whole time. That I didn't save you, I *purchased* you, and now the bill has come due and you mean to settle it by erasing yourself."

"That's not—"

"Isn't it?" She was shaking. "You want to give me your name. Your *soul*, Jonah. Everything you are. For me. And you think that's love, or honor, or whatever word you've dressed it in, but it isn't. It's arithmetic. It's Corvane's arithmetic. It's the same bargain, the same cold ledger-logic, just pointed in the opposite direction. You learned his lesson too well."

Something moved behind his eyes—wounded, furious, and underneath it, horribly, agreement.

"The Steward offered it to me," Jonah said quietly. "My name freely given, in exchange for yours. It's the only way. Her page is bound. Only a willing exchange can—"

"I don't *care* what's the only way." Wren's voice broke clean in half. "I didn't drag myself across half the kingdom, past bells and burnings and quiet carts full of people who used to be my neighbors, so that you could walk into that vault and *vanish*. I didn't teach myself sigils by firelight so I could watch you become a lamp."

"Wren." Odessa's voice, low, from across the corridor. A warning. Footsteps above, maybe, or just the mountain listening. Wren ignored her.

"You want to know what I would give?" Wren stepped closer, jabbed a finger at his chest, at the place where his heart hammered like trapped weather. "Nothing. That's what I'd give. Nothing, do you hear me? Because you don't get to pay for me. Not with your name, not with your gift, not with anything. I am not a debt. I was never a debt. Saving you was the freest thing I have ever done in my entire miserable, careful, hiding life, and if you turn it into a transaction I will never forgive you. Never. Do you understand? I'd rather burn in that wall forever than owe my freedom to your grave."

The words hung between them, and she watched them land, watched them go in like knives, and part of her wanted to take them back and most of her knew she never would.

Jonah closed his eyes. When he opened them, they were wet, and he didn't wipe them, and somehow that was worse than shouting.

"Then what do we do?" he asked, and his voice was the voice of a man standing in a flooded field watching the last high ground disappear. "Tell me what to do, Wren, and I'll do it. I'll do anything except let them keep you."

And that was the trap of him, she thought. That was the beautiful, unbearable trap. Because he meant it. Every reckless, self-immolating word.

So she lied to him.

"We stick to the plan," she said, and made her voice steady, and reached out and touched his cheek, once, briefly, the way you touch someone goodbye. "Together. Like we agreed. Now let me breathe."

He nodded. He believed her. That was the cruelest thing she'd ever done, and she did it while looking directly into his eyes.

* * *

She waited until the hour turned. Until Pell's contact passed overhead and the corridor's rhythm changed, guards moving, attention shifting. Until Odessa laid out the approach one final time—Bram first, then Pell with his credentials, then Jonah, then Wren, then Odessa covering the rear—and Jonah stood with his shoulders squared and his eyes fixed on the vault doors, gathering himself for the bargain he still intended to make the moment the Steward was within reach. She saw it in him. He thought he was hiding it. He hid things badly from her; he always had.

She memorized him, in that moment. The tired line of his mouth. The storm-smell of him, ozone and rain. The way his hand flexed at his side, fingers curling around power he was already planning to spend.

Then the doors opened, and the company moved, and in the confusion of the threshold—in the single heartbeat when every eye was on the vault, on the shelves of glowing names, on the ancient hollowed figure waiting among them—Wren slipped sideways into the branching passage Pell had marked on his map and marked again in her own memory, the servants' stair, the under-corridor, the way down to the ledger's roots.

She had listened. She had listened to every word Pell said for six days, and she had read the sigils on the walls when no one was watching, and she had done the arithmetic Jonah refused to do: a willing exchange required two willing parties, and she would not be one of them.

But a name, struck by its own hand—

The old texts were clear on that much. Grandmother's texts, the ones the courts had never managed to burn. *What is given cannot be taken; what is claimed cannot be kept.* A soul could refuse its own sale. It had to reach the page to do it. It had to survive the reaching.

Behind her, faint through miles of stone, she heard Jonah's voice rise—calling her name, or calling the storm, or both—and the mountain's lights shuddered, and the alarms began.

Wren ran into the dark, toward the heart of the ledger, toward her own burning name, and did not look back.
