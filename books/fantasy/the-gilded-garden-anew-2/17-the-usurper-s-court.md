## 17. The Usurper's Court

They took her boots first.

Odessa Vane had been robbed before—by cutthroats in the Salt Straits, by a harbormaster in Vellmark, once, memorably, by her own first mate—and she had learned long ago that a thief's first act was always to strip you of anything that might be a weapon or an escape. So when the gray-robed wardens pulled the laces from her boots and the pins from her hair and the little bone-handled knife from the small of her back where Bram thought she didn't know about it, she did not struggle. She catalogued instead. Six wardens, two ahead, two behind, one on each flank. Iron collars at their throats, humming faintly, the way the bells in the Pass of Hanging Bells had hummed. They were muffled men, these wardens. Deaf to magic, and deaf, she suspected, to pity.

The corridor they marched her down was lit by no torch.

That was the thing she could not stop looking at, even with her wrists bound behind her and Bram's shoulder pressed hard against hers, even with Wren sobbing quietly somewhere behind and Jonah silent as a held breath. The walls of the mountain gave off light. It came up out of the stone itself, a pale gold radiance, warm as candlelight and utterly without flicker, and it moved. Odessa had spent thirty years reading water, and light that moved was light that lived. It pooled along the floor in slow currents, eddied around their feet like something curious, and where it touched the wardens' iron collars it recoiled, hissing faintly, like steam off a hot blade.

Soul-light. Pell had told them about it in the servants' corridors, his voice shaking so badly he'd had to stop twice. *The mountain runs on them. Every lamp, every lock, every ward. They burn the names and the light comes out.*

She had thought, then, that he meant it as metaphor. Scribes loved metaphor. Odessa trusted ledgers, tide-tables, and the weight of coin in her palm, and she had privately filed Pell's horror under the exaggerations of a guilty man.

She was not filing it anywhere now. The light was alive, and it was everywhere, and every thread of it had been a person.

* * *

The hall at the heart of Mount Sereth had not been built for a king. That much was obvious from the bones of it—the low vaulted ceiling meant for dwarves or delvers or whatever ancient folk had first hollowed the mountain, the pillars carved with wheat sheaves and fishing nets, harvest-marks from a people three thousand years dead. Someone had raised the floor on a platform of black basalt, someone had hung banners of crimson and gold from pillars never meant to bear them, and someone had put a throne at the far end that looked, to Odessa's practiced eye, like it had been bought secondhand and reupholstered badly.

King Aldric sat in it anyway, and the room bent around him regardless.

He was younger than she expected. That was the first obscenity of him. She had imagined a gray tyrant, some withered schemer grown fat on stolen years, and instead there was a man perhaps forty, broad-shouldered, brown-haired, with the open weathered face of a farmer or a ship's captain—a face you'd trust with your cargo. He wore no crown. He wore a plain sword-belt over traveling clothes, dusty at the knees, as if he had ridden here himself through the ash of the free cities he was burning.

Around the throne, the court. Not courtiers—Odessa knew courtiers, their silk and their boredom—but clerks. Rows of them, dozens, seated at long slate tables on either side of the hall, quills scratching in unison, each with a single globe of soul-light hovering above their workspace like a tame star. They did not look up when the prisoners were dragged in. Quills do not look up.

And between the throne and the prisoners, filling the center of the hall like a wound fills a body—

The weapon.

Odessa had seen engines of war. She had watched trebuches throw burning pitch at the walls of Corvo Bay, had helped smuggle powder-horns past three separate blockades, had once stood close enough to a siege-ram to feel the ground shudder with its rhythm. She knew what weapons looked like: iron and timber and intent.

This was none of those things. This was a column of light.

It rose from a basin of black stone the size of a wagon bed, a pillar of soul-light thicker than her waist, climbing toward the vaulted ceiling and bending at the top, curving, pointing—she followed it with her eyes and her stomach dropped—pointing west. Pointing out of the mountain. Pointing, if her sense of direction served, at Vellmark. At the free cities. The light pulsed slowly, like a heartbeat, and with each pulse the whole hall brightened and dimmed, and the clerks' quills never stopped, and somewhere high above, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone, thunder rolled that had nothing to do with weather.

Jonah made a sound beside her. Small. She'd have missed it if she hadn't spent a week learning to read him. It was the sound of a man recognizing his own name being spoken in a language he didn't know.

"Whitlock." Aldric's voice carried easily down the length of the hall, unhurried, almost friendly. "Edmund Crane's lost student. I confess I hoped the bells would bring you. You've cost me three hunters and a very expensive pass."

"You know me," Jonah said.

"I keep accounts," Aldric said, and rose from the bad throne, and walked toward them, and Odessa noted with a professional's detachment that no guard flanked him because no guard needed to. He stopped six paces away, close enough that she could see the dust on his knees was real, road-dust, and that his eyes were the flat gray of a sky before hail. "Jonah Whitlock, storm-caller, entered into the Ledger of Sereth five years past by his own master, one entry, condition: alive but diminished. And Odessa Vane, smuggler, of no ledger at all." He turned the gray eyes on her, and she felt them go through her like a sounding-line through dark water. "You interest me more, actually. A woman with no gift, no title, no debt recorded anywhere in my mountain—and yet here you are, a thousand miles from your routes, breaking into the one place in the world you had no business entering. Why?"

"Bad navigation," Odessa said.

Someone behind her—Wren—made a sound that was half sob and half laugh, strangled instantly. One of the wardens struck her for it; Odessa heard the blow land and kept her eyes on the king.

Aldric smiled. It was a good smile. That was the second obscenity of him.

"I'm going to tell you what this is," he said, gesturing at the pillar of light without looking at it, the way a man gestures at his own hand. "Because everyone who sees it lies to themselves about what it is, and I find lying tedious. My clerks will tell you it's a registry. My priests—if I had any—would call it judgment. The Steward calls it preservation." He said the word *preservation* with a faint curl of distaste. "It is none of those things. It is fuel."

He walked toward the basin, and the light bent around him, acknowledging him, and Odessa saw now what she had refused to see at first glance: channels cut into the black stone, running from the basin outward, feeding smaller streams of light to the walls, the doors, the collars of the wardens, the hovering lamps above the clerks. Everything drank from it. Everything was drinking *them*.

"Every gifted soul in my kingdom is a power source," Aldric said conversationally, turning back to face them. "For centuries the gifted hoarded that power in their own small bodies, spent it on charms and healings and storms, wasted it, died with it. Criminal inefficiency. The ledger collects what they wasted. And collected power—" he laid one hand flat against the basin's rim, and the pillar flared, and the thunder overhead rolled again, closer, "—applied at scale, is artillery. In eleven days, that beam crosses the last hundred miles and opens the walls of Vellmark like a soft-boiled egg. Then Corvo Bay. Then the rest. No armies. No sieges. Just light, doing what light does."

"And then what?" Odessa heard herself ask. Her voice came out level, which surprised her, because inside she was screaming in a register she hadn't known she owned. "You boil the eggs. You rule a country of ash and quiet carts. What kind of king is that?"

"The last one," Aldric said simply. "That's rather the point. After me, no one needs to be king, because nothing left will need governing. I've read the histories, Mistress Vane. Every age of war, every famine, every purge—it all comes from power in the hands of the few and fear in the hands of the many. I am removing both. Permanently." He smiled again, that good honest farmer's smile. "I am not a usurper. Usurpers want thrones. I want an ending."

* * *

They were taken away after that—or Odessa assumed they were taken away; the wardens separated her from the others at a junction of corridors, and she fought then, finally, uselessly, calling Bram's name and hearing him bellow hers until an iron collar was pressed briefly against her throat and the world went gray and quiet and far away, and when it came back she was alone in a cell of raw stone with no light in it at all except the thin gold seepage around the door.

She sat in the dark and did what she always did when a run went wrong: she counted her losses.

Bram, taken. Wren, taken. Jonah, taken. Pell—she hadn't seen Pell since the ambush in the vault approach, and a scribe loose in the mountain's arteries was either their one live hope or already dead. The plan—*the* plan, the whole fragile architecture of it, forged over campfires and forged again in the Pass of Hanging Bells—had been theft. Get in, strike two names, get out. A burglary. She had burgled better-defended places than this with half the crew and none of the guilt.

But you couldn't burgle what she had just seen.

She turned it over the way she turned over a bad contract, looking for the flaw, the loophole, the clause that let her walk away whole. Strike Wren's name from the ledger? The ledger wasn't a book anymore, not really—it was a furnace, and the names weren't entries, they were *coals*. Pull one coal out and the fire went on. Free the prisoners in the vaults, the way their half-formed plan had promised? There were thousands. Tens of thousands. She had seen the soul-light currents in the walls and understood suddenly that she had been swimming in a river made of people, and rivers did not care how many buckets you carried.

Theft was a tool for taking things that could be carried.

This could not be carried. This could only be *stopped*.

And there it was, sitting in the dark of her cell, ugly and enormous and waiting for her the way debts wait: the ledger had to die. Not emptied. Not robbed. Killed. Every name in it released or destroyed, the basin cracked, the beam snuffed out eleven days before it reached Vellmark—which meant doing it from *inside*, which meant doing it while the mountain's entire garrison and its king and its Steward and whatever remained of Corvane's hunters were trying to prevent exactly that.

Her hands, she noticed distantly, were shaking. She pressed them flat against the cold stone and made them stop, because Odessa Vane did not shake, not where anyone could see, and the dark counted as someone.

She thought about her brother. Bram, who had crossed the Pass of Hanging Bells mute and sweating, who had carried Wren on his back through the ash-fields outside Vellmark, who had never once in his life asked her whether a job was worth it because he trusted her to only take jobs that were. If she died killing this thing, he would understand. That was the worst part. He would understand completely, and he would follow her into it anyway, and she would have to spend the rest of whatever time they had making sure he didn't.

She thought about profit, too—honestly, coldly, because that was her discipline. There was no profit in this. There was no route out, no cargo, no buyer. Every instinct that had kept her alive for thirty years said *cut losses, run, survive*. And every one of those instincts had been formed in a world where the quiet carts went out full and came back empty and she had told herself it wasn't her cargo, wasn't her route, wasn't her problem.

The problem had just pointed a beam of stolen souls at everything she'd ever pretended not to see.

"All right," she said aloud, to the dark, in the voice she used for closing deals. "New terms."

* * *

They came for her at what might have been morning—there was no way to know, and she suspected the mountain's masters preferred it that way—and marched her back up through the glowing corridors to the great hall, and this time the others were there too, arranged in a line before the throne: Bram, jaw set, wrists raw where he'd clearly tested the bonds; Wren, white-faced but standing straight, and Odessa marked that—the girl stood straighter than yesterday, something hardened in her since the vaults; Jonah, last, closest to the throne, and looking at King Aldric with an expression Odessa had never seen on him before. Not fear. Arithmetic. He was calculating something, and whatever it was, it frightened him more than the king did.

Pell was not among them. She let herself feel that for exactly one breath, and then put it away.

"You've had a night to consider," Aldric said, descending from the dais, hands clasped behind his back like a merchant walking his warehouse. "Here are your options, plainly stated, because I despise coercion—it's inefficient. The gifted among you may enter service. Registration, training, contribution. Your names remain in the ledger, but under new terms: yours, willingly renewed each year. The ungifted—" his gaze touched Odessa, moved on to Bram, "—may leave. Freely. On foot, unarmed, escorted to the foothills. You will speak of what you've seen here, or you won't; it changes nothing. Nothing you can say will outrun the light."

"And if we refuse?" Odessa asked.

"Then you stay," Aldric said, "and you watch. Everyone watches, in the end. It's the one ceremony all my subjects attend." He said it without cruelty, which was somehow worse. "Well, Mistress Vane? You strike me as a woman who knows a bad position when she's sold into one."

Odessa looked past him. At the basin, and the pillar of light rising out of it, pulsing with its slow stolen heartbeat. At the clerks, still writing, still feeding it. At the channels in the black stone, branching and branching, the whole mountain one great artery system around one great heart.

She had spent her life learning the difference between a cargo worth saving and a hold worth scuttling. Some holds you pumped. Some you sealed and opened to the sea, and you did it fast, and you did it grieving, and you saved the ship.

"There is another option," she said.

Aldric's eyebrows rose, genuinely curious. Behind him, she saw Jonah go very still.

"Your beam reaches Vellmark in eleven days," Odessa said. "Eleven days of feeding, of burning, of drawing on every name in that ledger at full draw. I'd wager a ship that's the most strain the old thing has ever borne. And old things under strain—" she smiled, and it was not a good smile, it was the other kind, the kind she'd learned from watching storms come in off the Salt Straits, "—they crack."

The hall went quiet. Even the quills seemed to pause.

"You're proposing sabotage," Aldric said. "In my hall. Bound."

"I'm proposing arithmetic," Odessa said. "You showed us the engine because you wanted us to despair. Consider whether you've shown it to the wrong audience. I don't despair at engines, Your Majesty. I price them. And I'm telling you the price of that one is about to come due."

Something moved at the edge of her vision—high, near the clerks' tables, a shadow where no shadow should be, a figure slipping between the pillars with the practiced silence of a man who had spent his life in corridors exactly like these. Gray robes. Ink-stained fingers. Pell, alive, moving toward the basin with something glinting in his hand, and his eyes found hers across the hall for one instant, and in that instant Odessa Vane, pragmatist, smuggler, woman with no gift and no ledger-entry and nothing in the world but her wits and her crew, understood that the plan had already changed, had maybe already begun, and that whatever happened next would happen faster than any of them were ready for.

Pell stepped out of the shadows and threw himself at the basin of black stone, and the light screamed.
