## 22. The Gilded Garden Anew

The rain came in off the Greysea at dusk, the way it always did in autumn, and Jonah Whitlock put down his trowel and let it fall on him.

He did not call it. That was the first thing he noticed every time now—the absence of the reaching, the old reflex that had lived in him since boyhood, the way a man who has carried a sword for twenty years still feels the ghost of its weight at his hip. The clouds gathered over the harbor and he felt them the way he felt weather, only: pressure behind the eyes, a damp ache in his knuckles. Nothing more. The storm passed through him like a stranger passing through an empty room, touching nothing, taking nothing.

He stood in it anyway.

The garden was small—a strip of dark soil behind the charm shop, walled on three sides by brick gone soft and green with age, maybe eight paces long and four wide. He had built the beds himself in the first weeks after they came back, when his hands shook so badly he could barely hold a hammer and Wren had to steady the boards while he drove the nails. Now there were winter cabbages under their cloth cloches, a row of late carrots going orange and sweet in the cold, rosemary gone woody and enormous along the south wall where it caught what sun Greyharrow allowed. There were flowers too, because Wren insisted a garden without flowers was just farming, and she had planted marigolds and something called gilded gardenia that she swore would not survive the frost and then coaxed through two frosts already out of pure stubbornness.

He had not used magic on any of it. He could not have, and he would not have wanted to. That was the strange arithmetic of it, the sum he turned over every morning like a coin worn smooth: everything he had lost, and how little of it he missed.

* * *

The shop fronting the street had been a cobbler's once, then empty for three years, its windows papered over with proclamations—Aldric's Great Audit, still legible beneath newer bills advertising a lost dog and a shipment of Vellmark wool. They had peeled the papers away the first day. Odessa had done most of the peeling, standing on a crate with her sleeves rolled, saying nothing, working her thumbnail under the corners of Aldric's face until the whole sheet came free in one long curl of vellum that she dropped into the gutter without ceremony.

They sold charms now. Real ones—or as real as charms ever were, which is to say mostly hope with a sigil drawn on it. But the difference, and everyone in Greyharrow knew the difference now, was that no one pretended otherwise. Wren taught the children to draw the old sigils properly, and the sigils worked the way a kind word works, the way a held hand works—not power, but attention. A charm against nightmares was a promise that someone had thought about your sleeping. A charm for safe travel meant someone wished you home. The city, which had spent five years pretending magic was a disease, was learning slowly, clumsily, the way a body learns to walk again after a long illness, that it had never actually hated the gift. It had hated being lied to about it. It had hated the taking.

There were still quiet carts sometimes. Old habits died hard in any government, and Aldric's remnant in the eastern provinces still posted bounty lists that named men who no longer existed—Jonah had checked, early on, with a thief's caution he could not shake, scanning the lists for *Whitlock* the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth. His name was not there. No one's name was anywhere anymore. The ledger was ash, and the ash was scattered somewhere over the Pass of Hanging Bells, and the bells themselves had been cut down by Bram's own hands and recast into a dinner bell for the freehold outside Vellmark, which rang now for meals instead of alarms.

But Jonah still checked. Some mornings he woke with his heart going like a drum before he remembered where he was, and lay in the narrow bed above the shop listening to Wren breathe in the next room, and told himself the same thing he always told himself: *You are known here. That is the whole of it. You are known.*

* * *

He heard the back door open—the particular creak of it, third hinge from the top—and then the quick light footsteps he would have known blindfolded, in a crowd, across a battlefield.

"You're getting soaked," Wren said.

"I'm getting rained on," Jonah said. "It's different."

She came down the two steps into the garden with her hood up and her arms full of the day's leavings: stubs of candle wax, a cracked slate, a child's mitten left behind from the morning lesson. She was nineteen now, or twenty—she had never been certain of her own birthday, and had decided last spring to celebrate all of them at once, which meant the whole street got honey cakes twice a year whether they wanted them or not. Her hair was shorter than it had been on the road; she'd cut it herself with Bram's knife outside Vellmark and declared it a new life, and it had grown back crooked and stayed that way out of principle.

She set her armload on the bench under the eave and looked at him standing in the rain with dirt to his elbows, and she smiled the way she smiled—crooked, unguarded, like a door left open on purpose.

"The children asked about you today," she said. "Little Maren wants to know if you were really a storm-caller. Tam says you're making it up because you're too ordinary-looking."

"Tam," Jonah said gravely, "is a discerning child."

"He also says you can't be a real storm-caller because real storm-callers don't grow cabbages."

"Tam has never grown anything in his life."

"No," Wren said, and her voice went softer, the way it did when the road surfaced in her voice without warning. "No, he hasn't. His mother was taken in the second Audit wave. He half-remembers her. Like the village children." She sat on the bench, pulled her knees up, watched him through the silver curtain of rain. "That's why I teach them, I think. Not the sigils. The remembering. Somebody has to hold the names."

Jonah turned his hands over, looked at them—broad, scarred, ordinary hands, gardener's hands, a seller-of-charms' hands. Five years he had hidden them inside careful mediocrity, keeping them still so they wouldn't crackle with static, keeping his voice flat so it wouldn't taste of thunder. He had been so good at being no one that he had nearly become it.

"I held mine wrong for a long time," he said. "My name. I hid it so deep I forgot what it opened."

"And now?"

"Now it opens a garden." He crouched by the carrot bed, not because the carrots needed him but because kneeling made the words easier, the way they always had. "Odessa came by this morning. Before you were up."

Wren's eyebrows went up. "Odessa Vane, voluntarily, before noon? What did the free cities do to deserve—"

"She brought the charter copy. The final one. Signed." He reached into his oilskin coat, hanging on the nail by the door, and drew out a folded document, softened already at the creases from being read too many times. "Every gifted soul in the coalition territories is a citizen. Registered—" he almost laughed at the word, tasting its old poison and finding it gone— "*recorded*, in open ledgers, in public halls, where anyone can read them and no one can sell them. Pell drafted it. Odessa bullied six councils into ratifying it. Bram signed it with an X and dared anyone to comment."

Wren took the pages and read them even though she'd heard it all before, because reading it made it true in a different way than hearing did. Rain dripped off the eave between them, patient as a metronome.

"They're talking about a school," Jonah said. "In Vellmark. A real one. For the gifted. Pell wants me to—"

"To teach?"

"To advise. To stand up in front of rooms full of people who can do what I used to do, and tell them how to hide." He shook his head slowly. "I don't know how to teach them anything except hiding, Wren. And hiding is finished. It should stay finished."

"So say no."

"I said maybe."

Wren laughed—a bright, startled sound that scattered a pigeon off the wall. "You said *maybe* to Odessa Vane? She'll have you on a barge to Vellmark by week's end. She doesn't hear *maybe*. She hears *yes with extra steps*."

"She always did." Jonah sat back on his heels. The rain was easing, thinning to that fine grey mist that Greyharrow wore like a shawl nine months of the year. "I told her I'd think about it after the harvest. After the frost. I'm not leaving the garden mid-season."

He said it lightly, but they both heard the truth underneath it, the way you hear the sea under a floorboard. He was not leaving. That was the miracle he still tested every morning, pressing his thumb against it like a bruise to see if it would hurt: *he was staying.* In the city that had exiled his kind, in the country that had hunted them, behind a shop on a street where the neighbors knew his face and his name and his history—all of it, the storm-calling, the ledger, the mountain, the price he'd paid—and knew him anyway, and called him *the charm-seller* in the friendly way, and saved him the good compost, and asked after Wren's lessons, and left bread on the sill when he'd been sick in the spring.

Belonging, it turned out, was not a place you fled to. It was a place you stayed until it grew around you like roots around a stone.

* * *

They ate supper upstairs—barley soup with the last of the garden thyme, brown bread from the baker on the corner who charged them half-price and pretended he didn't. The shop below was dark, its shutters latched, the day's charms laid out in their trays ready for tomorrow: nightmare-knots and traveler's knots, a birthing charm for the midwife on Tanner's Row, a grief charm for the widow Hollis whose son had come back from the eastern war hollowed and silent. Wren had made the grief charm herself. It was the best work in the tray.

Afterward they sat in the window seat, the lamp low, watching the street. This was their hour, the one they kept without ever agreeing to keep it. Sometimes they talked. Often they didn't. Tonight the rain had stopped entirely and the clouds were breaking apart over the rooftops, and a wash of moonlight came down the street like water finding its level.

"Do you ever miss it?" Wren asked quietly. She was looking at the sky, not at him. She asked this perhaps once a season, and always the same way—sideways, gently, giving him room to lie if he needed to.

Jonah considered it honestly, the way he always did, because lying to her had become impossible sometime on the long road and neither of them mourned it.

"I miss the *knowing*," he said at last. "Knowing a storm was coming three days off. Feeling the lightning before the sky did. It was like having a second heartbeat." He flexed his fingers, feeling only the old ache in the knuckles, the rain-ache any farmer knows. "I don't miss the rest. I don't miss being a weapon other people aimed. I don't miss Corvane's voice in my ear telling me what my gift was *for*." He paused. "I don't miss being afraid of myself."

"And when it rains?"

"When it rains," Jonah said, "I stand in it."

He rose and went down the stairs and out the back door, and she followed, because she always did, pulling her cloak over her shoulders. The garden smelled of wet earth and rosemary and the green iron smell of coming winter. Overhead the last clouds were shredding, and beyond them the stars were coming out in ones and twos, shy, like children at a lesson.

Somewhere far to the north, over the mountains where Mount Sereth stood broken and cold, a storm was building—he could feel nothing of it, and yet he knew it the way you know a friend is thinking of you, by faith alone. Sera had written once, a single line sent through a dozen hands: *The mountain is quiet. So am I, for the first time.* He hoped the storm stayed north. He hoped it didn't. Either way, it would fall where it fell, and the earth would take it, and nothing in him would reach up to meet it.

Wren slipped her hand into his. Her palm was warm, ink-stained at the fingers from the day's lessons, calloused from the garden spade they shared.

"Maren asked me something else today," she said. "She asked if we were married."

Jonah huffed a laugh. "What did you tell her?"

"I told her that some people belong to each other without paperwork." She squeezed his hand. "And then Tam said that was exactly the sort of answer a hedge-witch would give, and Maren threw chalk at him."

"Tam," Jonah said, "is going to be a diplomat."

"Tam is going to be a menace." She leaned her head against his shoulder. They stood together in the ruined garden of the world, in the small repaired garden of their street, and the night came down soft around them.

Above the shop, in the window where the lamp still burned, the light fell across the sill where tomorrow's bread would appear, and across the trays of charms that promised nothing except that someone had thought of you, and across the slate where Wren had written the evening's last lesson in her careful, crooked hand—the old sigil for *home*, the one that wasn't a ward and wasn't a spell and had never been either, the one that simply meant:

*I am seen here. I am staying.*

The stars thickened. The garden breathed. And Jonah Whitlock, who had been a storm, who had been a shadow, who had been a name in a book of stolen souls, stood in the dark with dirt under his nails and a woman's hand in his, and was—for the first time in his life, and for all the rest of it—exactly, ordinarily, completely himself.
