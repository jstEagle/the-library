## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The apple trees were dying the way old people die in the Hollow: slowly, politely, and without anyone saying it out loud.

Noor Fairweather knelt in the black soil at the base of the oldest tree in the south orchard and pressed her palm flat against its trunk. The bark was cool even under the afternoon sun, and beneath her hand she could feel it—or rather, feel the absence of it—that faint thrum of living wood that every book in her grandmother's library said should be there. *Trees sing*, the books said. *All green things sing, if you are quiet enough to hear them.*

Noor had been quiet her whole life. She had never heard a single note.

"Press harder," said Grandmother Sable from two rows over, not looking up from the basket of windfall apples she was sorting with hands that had once, allegedly, coaxed rivers to change their minds. "The sap runs shallow this time of year. You have to meet it where it lives."

Noor pressed harder. The bark bit into her palm. Nothing sang.

"Yes, Gran," she said, because that was what one said in the Hollow, where hope was rationed like sugar before a hard winter and everyone had learned to make do.

The valley spread out below her in the late light—the terraced orchards going gold and gray, the slate roofs of Hollow's End huddled together as if for warmth, the river running thin and silver through the middle of everything like a thread pulled too tight. Beyond the fields rose the boundary stones, twelve of them, moss-eaten and ancient, arranged in an arc across the mouth of the valley where the land narrowed to a single road. And beyond the stones, past the treeline, the world. The real world. The one Noor knew only from books, from her grandmother's stories, and from the occasional trader who came through twice a year with salt, iron, and news that was always a season stale.

She was nineteen years old, and she had never seen anything grow faster than it ought to, or heard anything speak that had no mouth, or felt anything in the air but weather.

In the Hollow, magic did not follow. Everyone said it like a blessing. *Safe*, they called it. *Clean*. Her grandmother called it other things when she thought no one was listening—called it by older names, in a language that made the fire gutter—and those names sounded less like blessings and more like bandages over wounds no one was allowed to look at.

"Noor." Sable's voice carried down the rows, dry as seed husks. "You're sulking at that tree again."

"I'm tending it."

"You're sulking *at* it. There's a difference, and the tree can tell." Her grandmother straightened, one hand pressed to the small of her back, and looked at Noor across the intervening rows with eyes the color of wet slate. Sable Fairweather was seventy-three years old and stood like a fence post driven into bedrock; the years had bent everyone else in the valley like grass, but they seemed to have given up on her early and moved on. "Bring your basket. We've got the vigil tonight, and I'll not have you arriving with dirt to the elbow and resentment to the eyebrows."

"I don't resent the trees."

"No," Sable agreed. "You resent everything else. Come along."

* * *

The house sat at the orchard's edge, stone-footed and timbered, older than any other building in the valley except perhaps the meeting hall. Inside it smelled of dried herbs, lamp oil, and paper—paper above all, because the Fairweather guild had once kept records the way other families kept livestock, and though the herd had dwindled to almost nothing, the smell of it lingered in every room like a ghost that refused to be evicted.

Noor washed at the basin while her grandmother laid out the vigil clothes. They were the same clothes they wore every year: dark wool, high collars, silver buttons stamped with the guild sigil—a stylized blossom inside a ring of thirteen points. Thirteen petals, thirteen points, thirteen turns of some wheel Noor had never been shown. When she'd asked about it as a child, Sable had said, *Thirteen is just our number, dear,* in the tone that meant *stop asking,* and Noor had learned, eventually, to stop asking about most things.

"You'll recite the second litany tonight," Sable said, shaking out Noor's collar. "Your diction has improved."

"The second litany is the boring one."

"The second litany is the *accurate* one. The first is for children and traders' ears. Tonight there will be neither." Sable fastened the collar herself, her fingers quick and impersonal, the way she did everything. "A hundred years since the Vigil of Sealing began. A hundred years since the guild turned its face inward. The words matter tomorrow more than they have mattered in decades."

"Why?" Noor asked. "It's the same vigil. It's always the same vigil. We light the candles, we say the trees are sleeping, we say the guild endures, we eat the honey bread, and Elder Maren—" no relation to anyone, she'd checked, "—falls asleep before the third candle. Every year. For nineteen years."

Sable's hands paused, just for a moment, on the last button.

"A centennial is different," she said. "People remember differently at a hundred years. They start asking what it was all *for*." She stepped back and looked at Noor the way she sometimes did—like she was reading something written very small, in fading ink, behind Noor's eyes. "Wear your hair covered. And Noor—if anyone asks you tonight how you find the valley, tell them you find it peaceful."

"It *is* peaceful."

"That is not what I asked you to say, which you know perfectly well." Sable turned away to the hearth, where the kettle was beginning to mutter. "Peaceful is what people say when nothing is wrong. What you would say is that it's quiet, and safe, and small, and that the orchards are dying, and that you have read every book in this house twice, and that you lie awake at night wondering if the world beyond the stones knows the Fairweathers exist at all."

Noor stood very still.

"I wasn't going to say all that," she said quietly.

"No. But you were thinking it loudly enough that the whole valley probably heard." Sable poured the tea without looking at her. "Sit. Drink. And then help me rehearse, because my memory is an old dog that wanders, and tomorrow it needs to heel."

* * *

They rehearsed in the front room by lamplight, as they had every year of Noor's life, though this year Sable went further than usual—past the founding litany, past the names of the first gardeners, into territory she usually skipped.

"In the hundredth year," Sable recited, her voice taking on the formal cadence that transformed her from a stubborn old woman into something older and stranger, "the guild gathered and chose silence over splendor. We buried the bright tools. We closed the bright gates. We let the world forget us, because remembering was costing more than the world could pay."

"What did remembering cost?" Noor asked. It was the question she'd asked since she was six. It was the question no litany ever answered.

Sable lowered the page. In the lamplight her face was all planes and shadow, and for a heartbeat she looked less like a grandmother than like a garrison commander reviewing a wall she'd held too long.

"Everything," she said simply. "And it was still the right price. That's the part you've never believed, and the part you must believe by morning." She resumed before Noor could speak. "*We let the world forget us.*"

"We let the world forget us," Noor echoed. The words came out smooth as river stones; she'd said them so many times they'd lost all texture. And yet tonight, for the first time, she heard what lived underneath them—not reverence, but a kind of grief, and beneath the grief, a question she had never dared shape into sound: *forget us from what?*

They ran the litany three times. On the third pass, Sable closed the book entirely and recited from memory, and Noor watched her grandmother's lips move around words that seemed to fit her mouth better than speech ever did, and felt the familiar ache open in her chest like a door onto a dark stairway.

Because here was the thing Noor had never told anyone, not even in the privacy of her own head until recently: she didn't want to leave the Hollow. Not exactly. Leaving implied there was somewhere else to *be*, some destination, and she had no destination. What she wanted was smaller and stranger than that. She wanted to *matter*. She wanted one thing—one single thing—to go differently because she had touched it. She wanted her hands on something that could not be tended by anyone else, wanted a task that was hers alone, wanted the world to require her presence the way a lock requires its key.

Instead she had dying orchards and a valley that forgot things and a family name that meant nothing anywhere except, apparently, nowhere at all.

"Again," said Sable. "From the sealing verse. Your emphasis drifts."

"My emphasis doesn't drift, Gran. My emphasis is honest."

"Honesty," said Sable, "is a luxury the Fairweathers gave up a hundred years ago. Again."

* * *

The vigil was held in the meeting hall, as it always was, under the great beam that ran the length of the ceiling—a single piece of heartwood, black with age, carved so long ago that the thirteen-petaled blossom at its center had worn nearly smooth. Forty-one people attended. The Hollow's total population was sixty-eight. The rest were children, or sick, or—in the case of the Tam brothers—openly contemptuous, which they expressed by staying home and being loud about it.

Elder Coram opened with the candle-lighting. Thirteen candles, thirteen points of the sigil, each flame caught from the last so that the fire itself was continuous, passed hand to hand down a century. Noor watched the little flames tremble as they traveled and thought: *a hundred years of keeping a match lit, and nobody remembers why.*

Then the litanies. Then the honey bread. Then, as predicted, Elder Maren's chin dipped toward his chest somewhere around the ninth candle, and the hall settled into the warm, drowsy murmur of people performing devotion they no longer examined.

Noor stood at the edge of the gathering with her hair covered as instructed, holding the second litany in her mouth like a stone, and looked out through the hall's open doors at the boundary stones, pale in the rising moonlight.

Tomorrow, the traders would come early for the centennial market. Tomorrow there would be strangers in the valley for the first time in half a year—strangers who had seen cities, who had seen the sea, maybe, who might carry letters or news or rumors of a world where things happened. She had already planned, with the meticulousness of someone who had nothing else to plan, exactly how she would position herself near the road. Exactly which questions she would ask that sounded casual. *Any trouble on the southern passes? Is the ferry at Vessaline running? Have you come far?* Small questions. Doors left carefully ajar.

"—and the guild endures," finished Elder Coram, and forty voices murmured the response, and Noor's mouth made the shapes while her eyes stayed fixed on the stones.

Afterward, when the candles had burned to pools and the hall emptied into the cold, Sable caught her arm at the threshold.

"You drifted during the third verse."

"I didn't."

"You recited the third verse of the *first* litany during the *second* litany. Only I noticed, because only I know both well enough to catch it." Sable's grip was gentle and absolute. "Where were you?"

Noor considered lying. It occurred to her, distantly, that she had never actually lied to her grandmother—not once in nineteen years—and that this fact said something either wonderful or terrible about her life.

"At the stones," she admitted. "Looking at the road."

Sable was quiet for a long moment. Moonlight lay across the valley floor in a broad silver sheet, and beyond it the treeline stood black and dense, and somewhere out there was everything.

"When I was your age," Sable said at last, "I used to walk up to the boundary stones and press my hand against them and imagine I could feel the other side. The hum of it. All that wild, wasteful, magnificent power, just past arm's reach." She smiled, and it was the saddest smile Noor had ever seen on her. "I never felt anything. Do you know why?"

"Because magic doesn't cross into the Hollow."

"Because I was standing on the inside of a promise," Sable said, "and promises don't hum. They hold." She released Noor's arm and drew her shawl tighter. "Go to bed, child. Tomorrow the valley fills with strangers, and you'll want your wits about you."

Noor climbed the stairs to her narrow room under the eaves, but sleep did not come. She lay listening to the house settle, to the wind moving down the valley from the peaks, and she thought about her grandmother's hand pressed against a stone, reaching for a hum that was never there.

Near midnight, unable to bear the ceiling any longer, she dressed again and slipped out.

* * *

The orchards at night were a country of shadows. Noor walked through them without a lamp, knowing every row by heart, until she reached the south wall and the gap in the hedgerow that led up the slope toward the boundary stones. She told herself she was only walking. She told herself she wasn't going to touch them.

She touched them.

The nearest stone was taller than she was, furred with moss, its carved sigil worn to a suggestion. Noor pressed her palm flat against it, the way she pressed it against the dying apple trees, the way her grandmother had confessed to pressing it forty years ago. The stone was cold. The night was cold. Somewhere below, the valley slept its dreamless, sealed, forgetting sleep, and somewhere beyond the treeline the world went on being vast without her.

"I'm here," she said softly, to no one, to the stone, to whatever promise her grandmother had spent a lifetime standing inside of. "Somebody out there—I'm here. Does anyone know that? Does anyone know we're *here*?"

The stone said nothing. The wind moved through the treeline beyond it, and the leaves whispered in a language the valley couldn't hold, and Noor Fairweather stood with her hand on the seal of her ancestors and ached, quietly and completely, in the way she had ached her whole life.

She was still standing there—so still, so long, that she later wondered whether some part of her had already known, whether some animal instinct had made her wait at that exact spot like a creature waiting at a water hole—when she heard the first sound from the darkness beyond the stones.

Not wind. Not leaves.

Footsteps. Uneven ones. Dragging.

And then, out of the black treeline, staggering into the moonlight with smoke rising faintly off his shoulders as though he were burning from the inside, a man crossed the boundary into the Hollow—and fell at Noor's feet.
