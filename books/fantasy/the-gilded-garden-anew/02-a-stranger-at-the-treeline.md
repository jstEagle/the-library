## 2. A Stranger at the Treeline

The vigil began the way all Fairweather vigils began: with candle smoke and the smell of beeswax, with Grandmother Sable's voice rising thin and certain over the assembled guild, and with Noor standing in the third row where she always stood—close enough to be counted, far enough to be forgotten.

A hundred years ago tonight, the story went, the last great working of the Fairweather guild had been sealed beneath the orchard roots, and every year since, on this same night, the survivors of that greatness gathered to remember what they had been. The meetinghouse was old timber and older grief, its rafters blackened by a century of vigil-fires, its walls hung with the tools of a craft no one living could perform: copper rods gone green with verdigris, silk banners stitched with sigils whose meanings had died with their makers, a single glass bell that had not rung in Noor's lifetime.

*We were gardeners once,* Sable said, her voice carrying the particular weariness of a woman who had told this story so many times it had worn smooth as river stone. *Not growers of apples and plums, though we grew those too. Gardeners of the world's living magic. We tended it, we pruned it, we kept it from choking itself with its own wild growth. And when the wars came—when every crown and creed wanted to seize the garden rather than tend it—we made the hardest choice any guild has ever made.*

Noor mouthed the words along with her grandmother. *We chose silence.*

Around her, the Hollow's remaining Fairweathers bowed their heads. There were forty-one of them now. Noor had counted twice, hoping she'd miscounted the first time. Forty-one people in a valley that had once sent a thousand sworn wardens out into the world. Forty-one names left on a roll that took up an entire wall, most of them struck through in faded ink. The youngest child present was six years old and already bored by the ceremony; the oldest was Old Marta, ninety-three, who slept through most of it and woke only for the singing.

Noor was nineteen, which made her neither the hope of the guild nor yet its burden, but something in between—a pair of hands good for hauling water and pruning deadwood, and nothing more. She had spent the whole day among the orchards, as she spent every day, and her arms still ached from it. The trees were dying. Everyone knew it, though no one said it above a whisper. The northern rows had stopped bearing fruit three seasons ago, and now their bark split and grayed like old skin, and no amount of tending slowed the rot. The southern rows held on, stubborn and souring, but even they grew thinner each year, as if the valley itself were slowly exhaling everything green out of its lungs.

She had asked her grandmother about it once, when she was twelve. *Why do the trees die here, when the stories say our family kept whole kingdoms blooming?*

Sable had looked at her for a long moment with those pale, unreadable eyes, and said: *Because the valley keeps us safe, and safety has a price. Now fetch the water.*

That was the answer to nearly everything in the Hollow. *Safety has a price.* The price was the trees. The price was the magic—or the lack of it. The price was Noor's whole life, arranged neatly around a hole shaped exactly like everything she might have been.

She let her gaze drift to the high window while Sable recited the names of the fallen, as she did every year, beginning with Aldous Fairweather and ending, always, with the same pause before the final name—the one that wasn't spoken aloud but that everyone felt anyway. Noor didn't know who that name belonged to. It was one of the many things the elders knew and she did not.

Outside the window, the treeline stood black against the early dark. The boundary stones were somewhere beyond it, seven of them set in a wide ring around the valley's rim, each one taller than a man and carved with symbols worn nearly smooth. Children were taught never to touch them. Adults spoke of them the way sailors speak of a reef that has sunk better ships than theirs. Beyond the stones lay the world—the real world, the humming, burning, magical world—and within them lay the Hollow, where a candle flame burned exactly as tall as physics said it should, and nothing ever whispered back.

Noor had lived nineteen years inside that silence, and lately the silence had begun to feel less like peace and more like a hand pressed gently but firmly over her mouth.

*—and may their vigil keep them,* Sable finished, and the assembly murmured the response, and the singing began.

It was during the third verse of the Warden's Hymn—the long, droning one, the one Old Marta loved best—that Noor slipped out the side door.

She told herself it was to check the lanterns along the meetinghouse path, which was technically true, in the way that many lies are technically true. She trimmed two wicks and straightened a bracket, and then, instead of going back inside, she kept walking. Past the well. Past the drying sheds. Down the lane between the hedgerows, where the night smelled of wet earth and woodsmoke and the faint sweet rot of the dying northern orchard.

The moon was a day past full and hung low and orange over the western ridge, and under its light the valley looked almost kind—soft hills, silvered fields, the dark shapes of houses with their windows glowing amber. A hundred years of vigils had happened under this same moon. A hundred years of the same songs, the same silences, the same slow fading.

Noor walked until the houses fell behind her and the path narrowed into the sheep track that climbed toward the treeline. She knew she shouldn't. The elders were strict about the boundary on vigil nights especially—*the veil is thinnest when we remember*, Sable always said, whatever that meant—but her feet carried her upward anyway, drawn by something she couldn't have named if she'd tried. Restlessness, maybe. Or the simple animal need of a caged thing to press its face against the bars.

The air changed as she climbed. It always did near the stones—cooler, somehow, and thinner, as though the valley's stillness gave way at its edges to something with more breath in it. By the time she reached the first of the boundary stones, her skin prickled with a feeling she had no word for, a sensation like standing too close to a struck bell.

The stone rose out of the bracken ahead of her, taller than her father had been, its carvings soft with age. Moss climbed its eastern face. She had seen it a thousand times. She had touched it exactly once, at fourteen, and gotten such a scolding that she'd never dared again.

Tonight, she noticed, the moss was wrong.

Not wrong, precisely. But near the stone's base, in a rough oval the size of a sleeping man, the bracken was flattened. Crushed. And the moss on the stone itself—the thick, ancient moss that had grown undisturbed for longer than anyone could say—was scorched. Blackened. As if someone had pressed a hot iron against the rock and held it there.

Noor stopped walking.

The valley behind her was quiet. The vigil-singing was too distant now to hear. Wind moved through the treeline above her with a sound like slow breathing, and beneath that breathing, she heard—nothing. That was the trouble with growing up in the Hollow. Nothing was the sound she knew best, and it told her nothing now.

She should have turned back. Later, lying awake, she would turn the moment over and over in her mind—the exact instant when turning back was still possible—and wonder why she hadn't. But the truth was simple: in nineteen years, nothing interesting had ever happened at the edge of the valley, and some starved part of her would rather meet a wolf than walk home to another hymn.

So she stepped closer instead.

The crushed bracken wasn't empty. A shape lay in it, curled on its side, and Noor's heart lurched before her eyes made sense of what they were seeing—a man, or the ruin of one. He was dressed for travel, in a long coat the color of wet slate, riding boots scuffed to nothing, and he lay with his knees drawn up and one arm flung across his face as if shielding himself from a light that wasn't there.

He was smoking.

That was the detail that froze her where she stood, that turned her blood to cold water in her veins. Not burning—there was no fire, no glow, no smell of char—but *smoking*, the way a doused torch smokes. Thin gray wisps rose off him everywhere at once: off his shoulders, his sleeves, his hair, curling up through the dark like breath in winter. Where the wisps thinned she could see his coat was marked all over with small pale scars, hundreds of them, as if the fabric itself had been seared in a thousand places by a thousand invisible brands.

And the strangest thing—the thing Noor would remember for the rest of her life—was that the grass beneath him was untouched. Green. Dewy. Whatever was burning him came from inside, or from somewhere no fire in the world could reach.

"No," the man said.

Noor startled so badly she nearly fell. His voice was cracked and thin, barely a voice at all, but his arm had dropped from his face and his eyes had found hers—dark eyes, fever-bright, wide with something worse than pain.

"You're—" He coughed, and the cough wracked him, and when it passed, smoke curled out from between his lips. Actual smoke, gray and bitter-smelling, and Noor felt her stomach turn over. "You're not them."

"I'm Noor," she heard herself say, stupidly, because her mind had emptied of everything else. "I live here. In the valley. Are you—are you hurt?"

He laughed, and the laugh broke apart into another cough. "Hurt." He said the word like a foreign coin he was testing between his teeth. "Girl. Listen to me. You have to listen, because I don't have—" Another cough seized him, harder, and his whole body arched, and for a moment the smoke pouring off him thickened until Noor could hardly see his face through it. When it cleared, he had dragged himself half upright, one trembling hand braced against the boundary stone, and the sight of his hand on that ancient moss—his scorched fingers leaving fresh black prints—made her take a step backward without deciding to.

"Please," he said. "I was followed. Do you understand me? *Followed.* They can't cross the stones—I don't think they can cross—but they'll wait. They're patient. They've waited twenty years, they can wait—" He stopped. His eyes had gone past her shoulder, down the slope, toward the valley, and something in his expression shifted from desperation to a strange, terrible wonder. "Twenty years," he whispered. "It's true. It's actually true. I'd stopped believing."

"What's true?" Noor's voice came out smaller than she wanted. "Who followed you? Who's *them*?"

But the man wasn't listening anymore. He was looking at her—really looking, his fever-bright eyes moving over her face as though reading something written there. And then he said, in a voice gone suddenly gentle and awed:

"You have her chin. You have Sable's chin exactly."

The world tilted. Noor's mouth opened and no sound came out. Her grandmother's name, in a stranger's mouth, at the edge of the boundary stones, on vigil night, in a valley where no stranger had come in living memory—

"How do you know my grandmother?"

Instead of answering, the man fumbled at his coat, at some inner pocket, and Noor saw then how bad his hands were—the fingertips blackened like the moss, the nails split—and something in her moved past fear into motion before she could think better of it. She knelt in the dew beside him. Up close, she could see he was younger than she'd first thought, thirty perhaps, with a traveler's weathered face and a week's growth of beard, and up close the smoke was worse—it rose off him steadily, endlessly, as though he were a wick burning down from the inside.

"Don't," she said. "Don't try to talk. I'll get help. My grandmother—she knows things, everyone says—just hold on, I'll be back before—"

His hand closed around her wrist. His grip was astonishingly strong for a dying man, and cold, cold as the boundary stone.

"The map," he said. "There's a map. Folded eight ways. In here." He tapped his coat with his free hand. "When I'm done—when I go—you take it. You don't show the elders. You don't show *anyone*. Do you hear me? Especially not—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening, and glanced again down the dark slope of the valley, and Noor watched a new fear move across his face, a fear with a specific shape, aimed at someone specific. "Especially not the ones who taught you the songs."

"You're not making sense," Noor whispered. "Nobody's coming for anything. Nobody comes here. This is the Hollow. Nothing happens in the Hollow."

Something like pity crossed the man's ruined face.

"Not yet," he said. "But it will. The seal is failing, girl. It's been failing for years—that's why your trees are dying, that's why the edges feel thin. Everything you were told about this valley is a story told to children, and the story is ending, and when it ends, every power in the world is going to come looking for what your family buried." His breath rattled. The smoke off his shoulders had begun to thin, and somehow that was worse, as if the burning were moving inward, toward the parts of him that mattered. "I was the Ember Court's courier. Twenty years in their service. And I am telling you, with the last honest breath I own: they know the map exists. And they are not the worst ones looking."

Noor's heart was hammering so hard she could feel it in her throat. Every instinct she owned screamed at her to run—to run home, to wake Sable, to drag the elders up the hill with lanterns and pitchforks and the whole weight of the guild's authority between herself and this smoking, raving, impossible stranger. That was what a sensible Fairweather would do. That was what the valley demanded.

Instead she heard herself ask: "What's your name?"

The question seemed to surprise him. For a moment the fever-brightness in his eyes softened into something almost like gratitude.

"Tobin," he said. "Tobin Marsh. Remember it or don't. It won't matter soon." His grip on her wrist loosened—not letting go, just weakening, a tide going out. "Listen. The thirteenth turn. When you read the map, start at the thirteenth turn. And find—" A cough tore through him, the worst yet, bending him double, and Noor caught his shoulders without thinking, steadying him, and his coat was warm as a hearthstone under her hands though no flame touched either of them. When he could speak again, the words came slower, spaced apart like a man laying stones across a stream. "Find Vessaline. Under Vessaline. There's a woman—an archivist, if she's still alive—no. No, that's too much. Too fast." He shook his head at himself. "Just the map. Take the map. Keep it hidden. Trust no one who asks about it twice."

"Twice?"

"Once is curiosity." His eyes held hers, and in them Noor saw a clarity that frightened her more than the smoke did—the terrible calm of a man who has already done all his grieving. "Twice is hunger."

Above the treeline, wind gusted through the pines, a long shivering rush of sound, and both of them went still. Noor found herself listening the way she had never listened before—for hoofbeats, for voices, for anything that was not the Hollow's familiar nothing. The valley below glowed softly with vigil-candles, forty-one households' worth of small warm lights, and for the first time in her life those lights looked fragile to her. Looked like a ring of embers that something out here in the dark had been circling for a very long time.

"They're waiting," Tobin murmured, following her gaze. "Down in the dark past the stones. One of them, at least. Maybe more. They don't sleep the way we sleep." His hand slid from her wrist at last, and he pressed his palm flat against the boundary stone as if steadying himself against it—or leaning on it the way a drowning man leans on a door. "As long as you stay inside, they can't touch you. Remember that. Whatever happens—stay inside the stones."

"But you're inside the stones," Noor said. "How did you even get in? How did you cross?"

Tobin smiled. It was a dreadful smile, gray-lipped, smoke-wreathed, and fond besides, and Noor would carry it with her a long time.

"That," he said, "is the only clever thing I have ever done. And it cost me—" The sentence ended. Not because he stopped speaking, but because the strength simply left him mid-word, like a snuffed candle, and he sagged against the stone with a soft exhalation that might have been the rest of the sentence or might have been nothing at all.

"Tobin?" Noor caught him, eased him down into the crushed bracken. "Tobin—Tobin Marsh—look at me—"

His eyes were still open. Still fixed on her face, on her grandmother's chin, seeing something or someone else entirely now. His lips moved. Noor leaned close, close enough to feel the heat radiating off his skin, and caught the last of it in fragments, a whisper worn to threads:

"...map... eight folds... thirteenth turn... tell her..." A breath. "...tell Sable... the debt is called..."

And then the smoke rising off him thinned, and thinned, and stopped, and the man on the boundary stone was only a man, still and cooling, with a week's beard and a traveler's boots and a look of terrible relief settling over his features like frost.

Noor knelt there in the dew for a long time. Long enough for her knees to go numb. Long enough for the moon to clear the ridge and pour white light down over the stone, the corpse, the scorched moss, and her own shaking hands.

Somewhere below, faint and far away, the vigil congregation had begun the closing verse of the Warden's Hymn. *Keep the garden, keep the gate, keep the silence for our sake.* Forty voices, rising sweet and steady out of a valley that believed itself safe.

Noor looked down at the dead man, and then at the shape of his inner pocket, where something folded eight ways made a hard rectangular shadow against the slate-gray cloth.

Her hands, when she finally reached out to touch it, had almost stopped shaking.

Almost.
