## 3. The Map in Unwilling Hands

The man was dying. Noor knew it the way she knew a frost-bitten branch—by the way life had already begun to withdraw from him, pooling somewhere deep and unreachable while everything at the surface grayed and slackened. He lay half against the boundary stone where she'd found him, his breath coming in shallow pulls that fogged and vanished in the cold, his eyes tracking her with an effort that seemed to cost him more than the breathing did.

"No," he said again—or tried to. The word came out wet, fraying at the edges. His hand lifted from the frost-crusted grass, trembling, fingers curled inward as though around something he could no longer hold.

Noor knelt beside him without deciding to. Her knees sank into ground that was already remembering winter, and the smell of him reached her properly for the first time: char and iron and something sweeter underneath, like fruit left too long in the sun. Burned. He smelled burned all the way through, as if whatever had wounded him hadn't stopped when it started.

"You're hurt," she said, and immediately felt foolish for it. Nineteen years in the Hollow and this was what she offered a dying man—an observation.

He laughed. It turned into a cough halfway through, and the cough brought blood to his lips, dark as spilled ink in the lantern light. "Hurt." He said it like a word in a language he was only half-remembering. "Girl. Listen. There isn't—" Another pull of breath, thin as thread. "There isn't time to be kind. Take it."

"Take what?"

His hand found hers. His skin was fever-hot where hers was valley-cold, and the contrast made her flinch, but his grip, for all its shaking, was astonishingly strong—the grip of a man who had carried something precious a very long way and intended to finish the job even now, even here, at the edge of a valley that forgot itself.

Something pressed into her palm. Folded paper, soft-worn at the creases, damp with his blood where his fingers had failed to keep it clean.

"What is it?" she whispered.

"A map." The word seemed to steady him, as though naming it gave him something to stand on. "To Vessaline. Beneath Vessaline. You have to—" His gaze slid past her shoulder toward the treeline, toward the darkness beyond the stones, and something moved through his face that Noor had never seen on a living person before: pure, undiluted fear, worn open like a wound. "They followed me. Past Corven's Ford. Maybe further. Maybe they let me run."

"Who? Who followed you?"

"The thirteenth turn," he said instead, ignoring her question entirely, or perhaps not hearing it. His voice dropped to a murmur, each word placed with the care of a man laying bricks in a wall he knew he wouldn't see finished. "The map turns thirteen times before the gate. Count them. *Count them*, do you understand? If you lose count, it will show you anything it likes."

"I don't understand any of this. I don't know what you think I—"

"Vessaline." He said the name like a key turning in a lock, and Noor felt something shift in her chest, some door she hadn't known was closed swinging ajar. "The city of brass bells. Find the gate. Or burn the map. Either is mercy." A bubble of blood rose at the corner of his mouth and burst. "But don't give it to anyone. Not the Court. Not the Thornbound. Not—" His eyes fixed on her, suddenly, terribly clear. "Not your grandmother."

The name landed in Noor like a stone dropped down a well. She heard it falling for a long time before it struck bottom.

"My grandmother?"

But he wasn't listening anymore. Or he was listening to something else—some sound inside himself, some clock running down. His grip on her hand loosened by degrees, not all at once but the way ice lets go of a branch in spring: slowly, then all together.

"Thirteenth turn," he breathed. "Count."

And then he was gone.

It didn't look like the stories said it would. There was no light, no rising, no peaceful settling of features into marble calm. He simply stopped—a whole complicated machinery of a man, breathing and bleeding and fearing, reduced between one heartbeat and the next to weight and warmth and silence. His head tipped back against the boundary stone. His open eyes caught the lantern flame and held two small reflections of it, and Noor could not make herself reach out and close the lids, because closing them would mean admitting what had happened, and some stubborn animal part of her believed that if she stayed perfectly still, the night might reconsider.

The wind moved through the treeline above the valley rim. Somewhere far off, down in the Hollow, a vigil bell rang the second hour, its tone flattened and faint the way all sounds were in the Hollow, muffled by whatever absence lived in the air there.

*They followed me.*

She looked at the treeline. Nothing looked back. That was somehow worse.

* * *

She did not remember deciding to hide him. Later, examining the hour the way one examines a bruise, pressing on it to learn its shape, Noor would find no moment of choice at all—only a sequence of actions that seemed to belong to someone wearing her body, moving with a competence she hadn't known she possessed.

First the lantern, shuttered and set behind the boundary stone where its light would read, from the valley below, as nothing but another watch-flame. Then the map, folded small and pushed deep into the inner pocket of her coat, against her ribs, where she could feel it with every breath like a second heartbeat.

Then the man.

He was heavier than he should have been. Dead weight always was, the elders said—but the elders had never said how *warm* the dead stayed, how their joints resisted, how their arms swung with a terrible loose lifelessness that made Noor's stomach climb into her throat. She dragged him by the shoulders, backward, away from the boundary stones, away from the path the vigil procession would take at dawn, toward the old drainage cut below the eastern ridge where bramble grew thick and no one went after first frost.

Twice she stopped to be sick. Twice she wiped her mouth and went back to him.

*I'm sorry,* she wanted to say. *I'm sorry I can't carry you better. I'm sorry I don't know your name. I'm sorry my hands are shaking.* What came out instead, whispered into the dark like a prayer she was inventing as she went, was nothing at all—just breath and effort and the rasp of his coat over frozen grass.

The drainage cut was a scar from before the sealing, Sable had once said, from when the Hollow had still bothered with engineering, with ambition, with a future. Bramble had claimed it for forty years. Noor laid the man in the deepest part of the tangle, arranged his limbs as decently as she could, and pulled branches down over him until only one boot showed, black leather, good quality, wrong for the Hollow. She stared at the boot for a long moment. Then she stripped off her own shawl and draped it over the boot, an absurd gesture, a child's gesture, hiding one thing with another thing that hid nothing at all.

But it was all she had.

When she straightened, her palms were scored with bramble cuts and her coat was ruined with mud and blood, and the map pressed against her ribs like a coal. She stood in the dark above the drainage cut and listened to her own breathing and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that she had just committed her first crime.

Not the lying—she hadn't lied yet. Not the disobedience—she'd committed plenty of that, small rebellions, late returns, a stolen hour here and there. This was different. This was a secret with a body attached to it. This was a hole in the world shaped exactly like a man, and she was the only one who knew it was there, and keeping it would change the shape of every day that came after.

She thought of her grandmother's hands sorting dried apples, steady and unhurried, and wondered—not for the first time tonight, but for the first time with real fear—what else those hands had done.

*Don't give it to anyone. Not your grandmother.*

"Why not her?" Noor asked the dark. The dark, being the Hollow's dark, declined to answer.

* * *

The walk back took longer than the walk out, though the distance was the same. Everything weighed more on the return: the lantern, her limbs, the knowledge folded against her ribs. The vigil fires glowed down in the valley like embers raked flat, and the songs had gone quiet—second hour meant the telling, meant Sable on her stool with the whole guild gathered at her feet, meaning the stories the way she'd always meant them, which was to say: incomplete.

Noor slipped into the longhouse through the kitchen door, the one that stuck and had to be lifted, the one nobody used because it opened onto the scullery and the smell of tallow. She made it three steps across the flagstones before the lamp behind her flared up and her grandmother's voice said, without surprise:

"There you are."

Sable sat at the worktable with a cup of willowbark tea going cold in front of her and a mending basket untouched at her elbow. She was not doing either thing. She was sitting the way she sat when she was waiting, spine straight, hands folded, a woman who had turned patience into a discipline and discipline into a fortress.

"You missed the telling," Sable said.

"I was walking." Noor kept her voice level, kept her eyes on her grandmother's face and nowhere near her own coat, her own hands, the pocket where the map sat. *Don't look at it. Don't touch it. Don't think about it so loudly.* "I couldn't sit still tonight. The centennial. It felt strange."

"Strange," Sable repeated, tasting the word. Her gaze moved over Noor slowly, unhurried, and Noor felt it like a searchlight—her mud-hemmed coat, her scratched hands, the smell of char that clung to her hair no matter how the cold had worked at it. Something flickered behind her grandmother's eyes. Not suspicion. Recognition. And that was worse, because recognition meant Sable knew what char-and-blood meant, knew it intimately, had known it sometime, somewhere, in a life she never spoke of.

"You're hurt," Sable said quietly.

"Brambles. I cut across the ridge."

A silence stretched between them, long enough for Noor to hear the vigil fire popping in the next room, long enough for her heart to count out a dozen beats that felt like accusations. Then Sable rose, crossed the kitchen, and took Noor's scraped hands in her own—and her hands were warm, always warm, impossibly warm for a woman her age—and turned them palm-up to examine the damage with a gentleness that nearly undid her.

"We'll salve these," Sable said. "And then you'll sleep."

"Yes."

"And tomorrow, when the elders ask about your evening—" Sable's thumb passed once, deliberately, over the worst of the cuts, and her eyes came up and held Noor's with a steadiness that was almost tender and almost terrible, "—you'll tell them you walked. Because you did walk. There's no lie in it at all."

Noor's mouth went dry. "Grandmother, I—"

"Go to bed, child." Sable released her hands and returned to her cold tea as though the conversation had been about weather. But as Noor reached the doorway, her grandmother spoke again, softly, to the table rather than to her:

"Some secrets keep us alive. Some secrets are just debts we haven't been billed for yet." A pause. "Learn to tell them apart before you're carrying too many to put down."

* * *

She did not sleep. She lay in her narrow bed under the eaves with the map beneath her mattress, and the house creaked its old familiar creaks, and every creak was footsteps, every footstep was *they followed me*, every shadow past the window was a rider in livery she couldn't name coming up the valley road to ask polite questions.

Sometime past midnight she gave up, lit a stub of candle, and drew the map out.

Unfolded on her quilt, it was smaller than she expected—a single sheet, dense with ink, the paper gone soft as cloth from handling. Roads she didn't know. Rivers named in a script half-familiar, like a dialect of home. And there, in the lower third, drawn in careful miniature: a walled city with towers like bells, labeled in a fine antique hand—*Vessaline*—and beneath it, beneath the very streets, a shape that might have been a garden, rendered in lines so old they had faded to whispers.

And starting at the city's edge, thirteen small marks marched inward. Thirteen turns.

She traced the first with her fingertip and felt—she would swear afterward, though she told no one—felt the faintest tremor in the paper, like a pulse. Like something patient counting along with her.

Outside, the wind shifted, and from the direction of the eastern ridge it carried, or seemed to carry, the thin bell-note of the third hour—and beneath it, so faint she might have dreamed it, another sound. Hooves. Far off. On the road.

Noor folded the map along its worn creases, exactly as the dying man had kept it, and slid it back beneath her mattress, and lay in the dark with her heart hammering, listening.

By morning, there would be a rider at the valley's mouth asking questions she had never imagined answering—and Noor Fairweather would discover what her hands were for besides tending orchards.
