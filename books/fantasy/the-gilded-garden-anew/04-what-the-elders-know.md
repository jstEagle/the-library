## 4. What the Elders Know

Noor buried the stranger before dawn.

She had chosen the spot carefully: the far corner of the orchard where the blight had taken the last of the plum trees, where the ground was soft and no one walked after the vigil ended. The shovel was old Tam's, borrowed without asking, its handle worn smooth by forty years of other people's graves. She worked in the gray half-light with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, and every time she paused she could feel the map against her ribs, folded small inside her bodice, warm as a second heartbeat.

The body had been heavier than she expected. Death made people heavy, she supposed. Made them into cargo. She had dragged him through the wet grass by his shoulders, apologizing under her breath the whole way—*I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I don't know your name*—as though words could soften what she was doing. He had given her two things in his last minutes on earth: a map that smelled of copper and smoke, and a name spoken like a key turning in a lock. *Vessaline.* And before that, *the thirteenth turn*, which meant nothing at all, or everything, depending on how long she let herself stare at the ceiling of her loft at night.

She shoveled the earth back over him and knelt a moment afterward, because it seemed wrong not to.

"I'll find out," she told the fresh-turned soil. "Whatever you were carrying. Whoever you were. I'll find out."

The valley did not answer. The valley never answered anything. That was the whole trouble with the Hollow—it took everything in and gave nothing back, not even an echo.

She smoothed the dirt, scattered fallen plum leaves across the mound until it looked like nothing more than a shallow dip in the ground, and walked back toward the house with the map pressed flat beneath her palm, as if she could hold it still by will alone.

* * *

Sable Fairweather woke before the sun, as she had for sixty years, and knew immediately that something was wrong.

It was not one thing. It was the accumulation of small wrongnesses, the way frost announces itself not in any single leaf but in the particular silence of the garden. The vigil had ended badly—she had felt it in her bones while she stood reciting the guild litany for the hundredth time, the old words about roots and remembrance, watching the young faces go glassy with boredom. Something had moved along the boundary stones. She had dismissed it as a fox. Sable was old enough to know that dismissing things was how you survived; you could not flinch at every wind that came down off the ridge, or you would spend your whole life flinching.

But this morning the wrongness had a shape.

She found it first in Noor. Her granddaughter came in late from the orchards, later than any honest errand required, and there was a quality to her stillness that Sable recognized the way one recognizes one's own handwriting in another hand. Noor stood too straight. She answered questions a half-beat too quickly. When Sable asked if the vigil had gone well, Noor said, "As well as vigils go," and smiled, and the smile was a door pulled shut.

The girl was lying. Nineteen years old and lying to her grandmother's face, and doing it badly, which meant she had never done it before—which meant whatever she was hiding was new, and heavy, and hers alone.

Sable poured the morning tea and said nothing, because that too was survival. But her hands were not quite steady on the pot, and she hated herself for it.

* * *

Noor spent the day being ordinary, which was the hardest work she had ever done.

She mended fence wire. She hauled water. She sat through the noon meal and passed the bread and laughed at her cousin Wren's impression of Elder Maren—no, not Maren, the elder was called Hollis—laughed at Wren's impression of Elder Hollis droning through the harvest accounts, and the laugh came out sounding almost real. Inside, she was turning the map over and over like a stone in a pocket. She had not dared look at it again since the treeline. In daylight, in the house, with Sable's eyes everywhere—the thought of unfolding it made her chest tight.

But at dusk, when the household drifted toward the common hall for the post-vigil meal, Noor slipped up to her loft and finally unrolled it on the floor.

By candlelight it was worse than she remembered. Not a map of roads, exactly—or not only that. It showed the kingdom in fine ink lines, but threaded through the geography ran markings she had never seen in any of the Hollow's few books: spirals and knots, dense clusters of symbols that gathered around certain places like iron filings around a lodestone. One cluster sat in the heart of a city labeled in cramped script—*Vessaline*. Another marked a mountain pass. And a third—

A third sat in the middle of nowhere she recognized. A blank stretch of high country, ringed with symbols so dense they nearly blackened the parchment. No name. No road leading to it. Just the knot of marks, and beneath them, in a different hand, older and finer:

*The seal holds while the line endures.*

Noor read it four times. *The seal.* The word snagged in her like a fishhook. Seals kept things out, or kept things in. Seals were for tombs and letters and jars of winter preserves. What kind of seal needed a map? What kind of seal held *while a line endured*?

Her family was a line. The Fairweathers were the last line of anything in the Hollow.

She pressed her thumb against the symbols ringing the unnamed place, and for one strange moment the candle flame leaned toward the map, though there was no draft.

Downstairs, someone called her name. She folded the map away so fast she creased it, and went down to be ordinary again, and was surprised to find her hands barely shaking at all.

* * *

Sable went to the records room after midnight.

It was really only a closet off her own chamber, a room no one else entered, its shelves crowded with the guild's leavings: ledgers, correspondence, genealogies, the meticulous bookkeeping of a family that had once been something and now kept accounts of its own diminishment. Forty years of dust lay over some of these boxes. Sable had not opened most of them since the night she had sealed the room with her own hands and a charm of forgetting woven so deep into the wood that even she sometimes had to fight past it.

Tonight she fought past it in three breaths, which frightened her more than anything else. The charm was weakening. Or she was. Or the world outside the valley was pressing harder against the walls than it had in decades.

She carried a lamp and the stranger's sigil.

She had seen it at the vigil's end, when the elders had walked the boundary stones in their annual circuit and found—nothing, thank the roots, nothing but scuffed grass and a smear of blood already brown. But Sable had knelt where the others did not think to kneel, and in the trampled earth beside the third stone she had found a wax seal, cracked and discarded, the kind couriers used to close dispatch cases. Pressed into the wax: a circle broken by thirteen rays, each ray tipped with a seed-shape.

She had known it instantly. That was the horror of it. Sixty years, and she had known it the way you know your own face in a dark window.

The Circle of Thirteen Rays. The courier guild that had served the Ember Court in the last years of the war—not the court's own riders, but the deniable ones, the ones who carried orders no magistrate would sign. Sable had watched their sigil burned into documents, into doors, once, terribly, into a person. They had been disbanded in the armistice. Officially. Their masters had not been.

And one of them had died at her boundary stones, smoking as if the valley itself had tried to spit him back out, and her granddaughter had been the one who found him—of that Sable was now almost certain, because Noor had come home from the vigil with river-mud on her knees and a lie ready in her mouth.

Sable set the sigil on the desk and looked at her records for a long time.

She knew what was in the boxes. She had spent forty years making sure the guild's decline looked natural—a great family fading, as families do, into farmers and fence-menders. Blight in the orchards. Bad luck with births. The slow erosion that time works on everything. It was a good story. She had told it so often, at so many centennial vigils, that she sometimes caught herself believing it.

But the decline had not been natural. Nothing about the Hollow was natural. The magiclessness was not a curse that had fallen on them; it was a wall they had built, brick by brick, generation by generation, each elder renewing the seal and each renewal costing the family a little more of itself—their craft thinning, their children born quieter, their names forgotten outside the valley on purpose. Because a guild that mattered was a guild that was hunted, and the thing beneath the garden would burn the whole world down before it let itself be shared, and so Sable's grandmother had chosen obscurity, and Sable's mother had chosen it again, and Sable had chosen it a third time with her own two hands and never once asked whether she had the right.

*The seal holds while the line endures.* She had written those words herself, fifty years ago, in a hand much steadier than the one that trembled now above the lamp.

Someone had copied them onto a map. Someone had put that map into a Thirteen Rays courier's hands and sent him toward the one place in the kingdom he should never have reached. Which meant someone outside the valley knew—not guessed, *knew*—that the seal was failing. Knew enough to send a man to die at the stones.

Or knew enough to send a man to deliver something to whoever lived behind them.

Sable thought of Noor's shut-door smile, and her blood went cold and then hot.

She did not deliberate long. Deliberation was for people with good options. She fetched the iron brazier from the corner, set it in the middle of the floor, and began feeding the records into it—box by box, decade by decade. The genealogies first, because names were leverage. Then the correspondence with the Court's heralds, forty years of polite letters that were all, in their way, reconnaissance. Then the cipher books, the ward-notes, her mother's journal with its terrible careful diagrams of the garden gate.

The firelight climbed the shelves. The paper caught with a sound like whispered applause. Sable stood in her own doorway and burned her family's history, and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that she was not destroying the truth. The truth was already loose in the world, riding in a dead man's saddlebag, folded against her granddaughter's ribs. She was only destroying the proof—and the proof had always belonged to the elders, not to Noor. That was the inheritance, wasn't it? Not the garden. Not the power. The lie. Each generation handed the next a beautiful, suffocating lie and called it protection.

"No more," she said aloud, to the fire, to her mothers, to no one. "I'm done handing it down."

The last thing she fed to the flames was the wax sigil. It curled, blackened, and split along the thirteen rays, and Sable watched it go and felt forty years of silence crack down the middle like river ice in spring.

When the brazier cooled, she raked out the ash, closed the closet, and stood a long moment in the dark of her chamber with her hand flat against the door.

Then she went to find her granddaughter.

* * *

Noor heard the footsteps on the loft ladder and had just enough time to slide the map beneath her mattress before Sable's head rose through the hatch, silver-haired and grim, holding a lamp whose flame bent toward Noor the way the candle downstairs had bent toward the map.

"Grandmother." Noor sat up. "Is something—"

"Show me what the dead man gave you."

The words landed like a dropped anvil. Noor's mouth opened and produced nothing. Sable climbed fully into the loft, set the lamp on the table, and waited with the patience of a woman who had outlasted kings' decrees and blight and her own grief, and would certainly outlast a nineteen-year-old's denial.

"How did you—" Noor started.

"You have mud on your boots from the north orchard, where nobody walks. You've lied to me twice today, badly, and you've never lied before, which means you learned it suddenly, which means you had a reason worth learning for." Sable's voice was quiet, almost gentle, and somehow that was worse than shouting. "And there is a grave in the plum corner that wasn't there yesterday. I'm old, child, not blind. Show me."

Every instinct screamed at Noor to lie a third time. But looking at her grandmother's face—really looking—she saw something she had never seen there before. Not anger. Fear. Deep, worn, familiar fear, the fear of someone meeting an old enemy across a table.

Slowly, Noor drew the map from beneath the mattress and unfolded it between them.

Sable looked at it for a long, long time. The lamp flame leaned. When she finally spoke, her voice had changed—flattened into something formal and old, a voice from the records room, from the vigils, from a version of herself Noor had never met.

"Where is the rest of it?"

"The rest?"

"He would have carried more than paper. A token. A seal-charm. Something of the blood." Sable's eyes came up, sharp as a hawk's. "Did he say anything? A word, a phrase, a name?"

"*Vessaline,*" Noor whispered. "And—the thirteenth turn."

Sable closed her eyes. For a moment she simply breathed, and Noor watched her grandmother age ten years in ten seconds, the way a tree shows storm damage all at once when the ice melts off it.

"And the sigil on the case," Sable said. "Thirteen rays. Did you see it?"

"Yes."

"Then listen to me, and listen once, because I will not say this twice." Sable sat down across from her, and took Noor's hands, and her grip was cold. "Everything I have ever told you about why we are small, why we are safe, why the valley forgets us—that story ends tonight. There is a garden beneath the world, Noor. Your blood is the lock on its door. And there are people in Vessaline who have just learned the lock is waking up."

Outside, far off at the treeline, something that was not a fox cried once and went silent.

Sable rose, crossed to the window, and stood looking north toward the boundary stones for a long moment before she spoke again.

"Pack light," she said. "We leave before the elders wake."
