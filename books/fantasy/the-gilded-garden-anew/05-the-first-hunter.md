## 5. The First Hunter

The rider came at the hour when the Hollow's light turned to honey, and Noor saw him before anyone else did, because she was the only one looking outward.

She had taken up her post on the eastern slope with a basket of late pears she had no intention of picking. It was a habit of hers, this watching—the boundary stones stood grey and patient in the middle distance, and beyond them the world began in a shimmer that was not quite heat-haze and not quite anything else. The Hollow's edge had always looked like that: as though someone had drawn a line around the valley and blurred it deliberately, the way a child smudges charcoal so no one can read what was written underneath.

The rider resolved out of that shimmer slowly, first as a dark fleck against the ambered hills, then as a horse, then as a man—and Noor's stomach dropped before her mind caught up, because no one came into the Hollow. That was the whole of the valley's history in one sentence. Traders met them at the treeline and no further; pilgrims were turned back by wards so old they had forgotten their own names; and in living memory, Noor could not recall a single stranger crossing the boundary stones.

But this man crossed them.

She watched him do it, watched his horse hesitate and stamp and then step over the invisible threshold as if stepping through a curtain of warm water. And here was the thing that made her breath go shallow and strange: where he passed, the air *rippled*. Not the way the valley always rippled at its edges—this was different, sharper, like the wake behind a boat. Magic, she thought, though she had never seen magic in her life and had no right to know its shape. Something clung to him. Something from outside.

He wore livery. Even at distance she could see it—a long coat of deep ember-red, trimmed in gold thread that caught the dying sun, and on the breast a device she recognized without knowing how: a crowned flame, worked in metallic thread that seemed almost to smolder. The Ember Court. She knew the name the way she knew the names of old kings, from Sable's stories told in the flat voice Sable used for things that were history rather than story. *They wanted what we kept, little wren. They will always want it. That is why the garden sleeps.*

Noor stood very still among the pear trees, her heart conducting some frantic private percussion, and made herself think.

He would come to the village. There was nowhere else to come. He would ask questions, because men in livery always asked questions, and the questions would be about—

Her hand went, without permission, to her bodice, where the folded map lay hidden against her ribs. Three days now it had lived there, warm from her skin, heavy as a held breath. Three days since the stranger at the treeline had died with his blood drying between her fingers and his last words still lodged in her ears like splinters. *The thirteenth turn. Vessaline.* Three days since she had buried him in the soft earth behind the old cider press, alone, at night, lying to her grandmother with her whole face while her grandmother studied her across the supper table with those pale, unreadable eyes.

*You look like a girl carrying something,* Sable had said, and Noor had laughed and said she was only tired, and it had been so easy that the ease itself frightened her more than the lie.

Now she watched the red coat descend toward the village, and understood with terrible clarity that everything depended on the next hour.

* * *

By the time she reached the green, the rider had already dismounted and was speaking with Elder Bramwell, who had come out of the meetinghouse wiping flour from his hands and wearing the expression of a man presented with a talking bear. A handful of villagers had gathered at a respectful distance—the Hollow's entire population could have fit inside a city tavern, and half of them were here now, drawn by the same gravity that pulled moths and gossips.

Up close, the rider was younger than his bearing suggested. Perhaps thirty, with the lean, weathered look of a man who spent more time in saddles than beds. His hair was cropped short in the court fashion, and his boots—Noor noticed boots, she couldn't have said why—were good ones, oiled and worn at the stirrup but carefully maintained. Everything about him was careful. That was the word that settled over her impression of him and stayed: *careful*. A careful man on a careful errand.

"—appreciate your patience," he was saying to Bramwell in a voice like smooth river stone, cultured but not arrogant. "I know my presence is unusual. I'll be gone again within the day."

"Unusual is a gentle word for it." Bramwell's chin was up, but Noor could see the tremor under it. The elders had rules for outsiders, rehearsed for generations and never once used: *be courteous, be vague, be rid of them.* "We're a quiet valley, sir. We keep to ourselves."

"So I've heard." The rider smiled—it reached his eyes, which somehow made it worse—and produced a small leather wallet from inside his coat. From it he drew a sheet of paper, and Noor's blood went cold before he'd even unfolded it, because she already knew what would be on it. "Then perhaps you can help me with something quiet. A courier of ours went missing some days past. His route should have brought him near these hills." He held the paper up—not offering it, just displaying it, the way a fisherman displays a lure. On it was a sketch in fine ink: a man's face, sharp-featured, hollow-cheeked.

The stranger's face. The face she had closed with her own two hands.

"He carried something of value to the Crown," the rider continued, folding the paper away with unhurried precision. "We believe he may have been injured. We are, of course, concerned for his welfare." A pause, delicately measured. "And for the Crown's property."

Murmurs moved through the little crowd like wind through wheat. Noor stood at its edge, the pear basket still hooked over her arm, absurdly domestic, and felt every eye-weight in the world settle onto her shoulders. *Say nothing. Be nobody. You are a girl with pears.*

Elder Bramwell was shaking his head. "We've seen no one, sir. Travelers don't come here. Ask anyone."

"I intend to." The rider turned, surveying the crowd with an expression of mild, professional regret, and Noor watched his gaze move from face to face the way a lender's eye moves down a ledger—looking for the entry that didn't balance. "I'll ask each of you the same three questions, and then I'll trouble you no further. Have you seen this man? Have you found any item he might have carried? Has anyone in your valley left recently, or behaved unusually?"

*Behaved unusually.* The phrase landed in Noor's chest like a dropped stone. She thought of the fresh-turned earth behind the cider press. Of the stranger's boots, which she had buried with him because she couldn't account for them otherwise. Of the map against her ribs, and of herself, standing here, behaving more unusually than any soul in this valley had behaved in a hundred years.

The rider began working through the crowd. She watched him question Old Marta, who babbled cheerfully and knew nothing; the Cooper twins, who stared and stammered; Tam Fletcher, who grew defensive and therefore suspicious under questioning that had been perfectly polite until his tone changed. *Careful*, she thought again, watching. He wasn't listening to answers. He was listening to *how* people answered—the pitch of fear, the texture of evasion. He was reading them like weather.

And then he was in front of her.

Up close his eyes were grey-green, unremarkable and utterly steady, and they went over her the way she imagined a customs officer goes over cargo. The basket. The dust on her skirt. Her face.

"You've been outside today," he said. Not a question.

"I tend the orchards." Her voice came out level. That surprised her. Underneath the levelness her pulse was slamming like a fist on a door, but the surface—somehow the surface held. "East slopes, mostly. Pears don't pick themselves."

"No. They don't." He glanced at the basket. "Good year?"

"Bad year. Everything's been thin since spring." True, all of it, and she heard with detached fascination how truth was the best mortar for a wall of lies. "I'm sorry—I don't know your man. We don't get travelers. Nobody comes here."

"Nobody comes here," he repeated softly. "That must make for a very small world."

"It makes for a very safe one."

Something flickered across his face—amusement, or its professional cousin. "May I ask your name?"

Here it was. The hinge. Some part of her, the part raised on Sable's stories of courts and hunters and the price of being known, screamed to give a false name—but she looked at the crowd, at Bramwell hovering anxiously nearby, at Old Marta craning her neck, and she understood that a lie there would be a stone thrown into a pond full of witnesses. Everyone in the Hollow knew everyone. One inconsistency, one villager's puzzled *that's not her name*, and the careful man would stop being polite.

"Noor Fairweather," she said.

His eyes changed. It was small—she nearly missed it—a fractional narrowing, a stillness in the lids, gone in a heartbeat and replaced by bland courtesy. But she had been watching for exactly that kind of flicker, learning his method even as he applied it to her, and she caught it. He knew the name. Or the name meant something to whatever had sent him.

"Fairweather," he said pleasantly. "A common name in these parts?"

"It's the only name in these parts." She let a smile touch her mouth, shy and slightly apologetic, the smile of a girl who has never had a secret in her life. "There's maybe forty of us. We're all cousins twice over. It's a wonder any of us have teeth."

He laughed. It was a genuine-sounding laugh, and she hated how well it worked on her, how human he suddenly seemed, and she understood that this too was technique—or perhaps it wasn't, and that was worse. "Well, Noor Fairweather of the forty cousins. Last questions, and I'll leave your valley to its pears." He held up one finger. "Have you seen this man?" Another finger. "Have you found anything he might have carried—a satchel, documents, anything out of the ordinary?" A third finger, and here his voice slowed by half a beat, here his eyes fixed on hers with the gentle, total attention of a man lowering a lantern into a well. "Has anything strange happened here lately? Anything at all. Sometimes the smallest detail matters. A light in the wrong place. A sound in the night."

The well-lantern of his gaze poured down into her, and Noor Fairweather, who had never lied, who had been raised in a valley where there was nothing worth lying about, discovered something rising in her that felt appallingly like *talent*.

Because she did not freeze. She did not flush. Instead, some clever, previously dormant chamber of her mind opened and began, calmly, to furnish itself. She let her brow crease—just slightly, the crease of honest effort, of a girl genuinely searching her memory. She let her gaze drift sideways, the way people's eyes drift when they truly reach for recollection. And she let the silence stretch one breath longer than comfort, because she had watched him question six others and she knew now that guilt answered fast and innocence took its time.

"A light," she said slowly. "Maybe. Two nights ago—I was coming back late from the orchards, and I thought I saw something near the treeline. I assumed it was marsh-gas. We get it sometimes, in the low fields." Every word of it plausible; most of it even true; the marsh-gas real, the treeline real, the lateness real. Only the shape of it bent, and the bend was invisible. "And there was a sound, I think. Like a horse that wouldn't settle. But horses won't settle near our boundary anyway—they never have. Ask anyone. The animals hate this place."

It was beautiful. She heard it come out of her mouth and marveled at it even as terror sang along her nerves: it was all *checkable*, all of it woven through with verifiable threads, and it gave him something—gave the hunter a scent trail that led nowhere, or rather, led everywhere, because marsh-gas and restless horses were the valley's oldest facts.

The rider was quiet for a moment. Then: "Where near the treeline?"

"The east stones. Toward the split oak." She gestured vaguely eastward, generously imprecise, and prayed the split oak's actual position matched her gesture. It did. She'd climbed it a hundred times.

He nodded slowly, and wrote nothing, and she understood that he memorized instead—that writing things down was for men who needed notes, and he was not such a man. "If you remember anything else," he said, producing a small card of stiff cream paper and holding it out to her, "there's an inn at the crossroads two days south. Leave word for Master Corven. There's coin in it, for accurate accounts." His smile returned, warm as banked coals. "Coin for accuracy, Mistress Fairweather. Nothing more."

"I'll remember," she said, and took the card, and their fingers did not touch, and she felt the near-miss of it like a spark jumping a gap.

* * *

He rode out an hour before dusk, pausing at the boundary stones to look back at the valley for a long, unreadable moment—as if measuring it, or memorizing it, or saying goodbye to a place he intended to see again. Then the shimmer took him, and the ripple of his passing smoothed away, and the Hollow sealed itself behind him like water closing over a stone.

The villagers exhaled collectively and immediately began to talk, the talk swelling with relief and self-congratulation. *Told him nothing. Nothing to tell. Strange business, the Court poking about.* By full dark the incident had already begun its transformation into anecdote, softened and rounded, a story with no teeth.

Noor stood alone behind the cider press in the blue hour, looking down at the patch of earth she had turned ten days ago. New grass was already threading through it, indifferent and green. She held the cream-colored card in her hand and read it again by the last light: an inn's name, a road, a false-friendly name—*Master Corven*—and beneath it, embossed, the crowned flame.

"You're getting good at this," she said aloud to herself, and the sound of her own voice in the empty dark startled her, because it didn't sound like guilt. It sounded like discovery.

She should feel sick. She had lain to a hunter of the Ember Court, to his face, with his lantern-eyes in hers, and she had *enjoyed* it—not the danger, exactly, but the craft of it, the sudden vast interior room she'd found in herself, furnished with plausible details and checkable truths. Nineteen years of being nobody, of tending dying trees in a valley that forgot the world, and it had taken a stranger's death and one afternoon of terror to reveal that she contained this: a mind quick enough to build a lie strong enough to stand on.

That was the thrilling part, and she let herself hold it for a long moment, turning it over like contraband.

Then the other part arrived, as it always did, cold and punctual: *he knew the name.* Not surprise—recognition. *Fairweather* had meant something to him before she ever opened her mouth. Which meant the Court had not lost a courier at random. Which meant somewhere in the wide world beyond the shimmer, her family's name sat in ledgers and dossiers, filed and flagged, waiting. Which meant the rider would report *a girl named Fairweather, calm, watchful, quick*, and careful men remembered girls like that.

And beneath all of it, quieter and colder still: he had asked about items carried. Documents. Which meant the Court suspected the courier had been carrying something, and had not yet found it—which meant the hunt was not over, only paused, and the pause had a name, and the name was *thirteenth turn*, and the name was *Vessaline*, and the name was pressed warm against her ribs where no lantern-eye could reach.

Noor folded the card into quarters and slid it into her pocket. Evidence, she told herself. A reminder. Though of what—her cleverness, or her peril—she honestly could not say.

Behind her, gravel crunched.

She turned, heart leaping, and found her grandmother standing at the edge of the yard with a lantern held low, its light pooling at Sable's feet and leaving her face in shadow. How long she had been standing there, Noor could not guess. Long enough, perhaps. Long enough to hear a girl talking to the ground over a dead man's grave.

"Grandmother," Noor said, and was proud—faintly, desperately proud—of how steady it came out. "You're up late."

Sable said nothing for a moment. The lantern flame leaned in a wind Noor couldn't feel. When her grandmother finally spoke, her voice was not angry, which was somehow far worse than anger.

"The red-coat man asked his questions," Sable said. "And one girl in the crowd answered better than she should have been able to." The lantern rose, slowly, until the light climbed Sable's face and showed her eyes—pale, old, and terribly awake. "Come inside, Noor. Bring the map. It's time you learned why your grandfather died with a sigil burned into his hands."

Noor's blood turned to ice water. Behind the cider press, the new grass whispered, and the buried stranger kept his silence, and her grandmother turned and walked toward the house, leaving Noor no choice at all but to follow.
