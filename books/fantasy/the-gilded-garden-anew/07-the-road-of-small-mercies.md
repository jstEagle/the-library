## 7. The Road of Small Mercies

The world outside the Hollow was louder than Noor had imagined, and stranger than she had dared.

She had walked for three days along the pilgrim road that wound down from the boundary stones, following the cart ruts and the cairns her grandmother had described in stories so old they had worn smooth with telling. Her feet ached inside boots meant for orchard mud, not stone and dust. Her pack held bread gone hard as bark, a water skin, the guild ring warm against her sternum where it hung on its cord, and—wrapped in oilcloth at the very bottom, beneath everything—the map.

She did not take it out on the road. She only touched the oilcloth through the canvas sometimes, walking, to be sure it was still there. It always was. It felt heavier every day, as though it were drinking something out of her.

On the third afternoon she crested a rise above the river town of Brindleford, and stopped, and forgot how to breathe.

Magic was everywhere.

Not the wild, terrible thing she had half expected—not lightning or dragons or anything from Sable's grimmer tales—but magic the way one might find salt in bread: invisible until you knew to taste for it, and then impossible to miss. A woman at the well drew water with a gesture, the bucket rising smooth as if pulled by an invisible hand, and she didn't even watch it come up; she was talking to a neighbor about the price of wool. Lanterns lit themselves along the main street as the light failed, each one flaring with a small amber sigh, like a row of drowsy birds settling. In a shop window, a tailor's shears cut fabric on their own, tracing a chalk line while the tailor stood with his arms crossed, arguing amiably with his wife.

Noor stood at the edge of the road with her mouth open like a child's, and no one thought anything of it, because everyone here had seen it all their lives.

*This,* she thought, and the word hurt going down. *This is what we gave up. This is what Grandmother sealed us away from.*

Because it wasn't only that the Hollow had no magic. She had understood that much since childhood—that the valley was quiet the way a room is quiet after music stops. What she hadn't understood, what no story had ever made real, was that the rest of the world *hummed*. That ordinary people lived wrapped in small mercies their whole lives without once thinking them remarkable. The Fairweather guild had not merely lost its power when the seal went up. It had exiled itself from the entire texture of the age, and then spent a hundred years telling itself that quietness was a virtue.

A boy ran past her chasing a paper bird that folded and refolded itself in the air, steering around corners with obvious delight. His laughter was completely ordinary. That was what undid her—not the wonder of the thing but the ease of it. He would grow up and never think twice about a bird made of nothing.

Noor pressed her hand flat over the guild ring beneath her shirt and walked down into Brindleford before anyone noticed her staring.

* * *

She learned quickly that wonder did not keep her fed.

Her coins were old—guild mint, decades out of circulation—and the first innkeeper turned one over in his palm with narrowed eyes and asked where exactly she'd come from that she was paying in *these*. Noor said south. The innkeeper said the south road didn't pass through anywhere that minted silver like this. Noor bought a bowl of stew with the coin anyway, because he took it in the end, and ate it in the corner with her back to the wall, listening.

Listening was something the Hollow had taught her well. Nineteen years of tending trees and mending fences left a great deal of time for listening, and Noor had catalogued every story her grandmother told, every contradiction between the versions, every name spoken too carefully. Now she sat in a tavern full of strangers and listened for the word *Fairweather*, and heard it twice in an hour.

"—court's offering a purse, they say. Courier vanished somewhere past the hill country, carrying something for the Magistrate himself."

"Couriers vanish all the time."

"Not ones carrying *this*." The speaker lowered his voice, and Noor strained across the noise of the room. "My cousin works the toll bridge at Vessaline way. Says the riders came through asking after a girl too. A girl from the valleys. They're saying the courier was headed for the Hollow."

The stew went cold in her mouth.

They were saying the courier was headed for the Hollow. Which meant whoever had followed him—whoever had burned him from the inside out at the boundary stones—knew where he'd been going. Knew, perhaps, what he'd been carrying, or would soon enough. And between Noor and that knowledge lay three hundred miles of road on which she was now, apparently, a rumor.

She paid for a room she barely used. She lay awake under the roof beams listening to the tavern empty below, doing sums: how far to Vessaline, how little money remained, how many days until the thirteenth turn of whatever-it-was that the dying man had whispered. She fell asleep with the map's corner pressing through the oilcloth into her hip, and dreamed of lanterns lighting themselves, and woke feeling poorer than she had the night before.

In the morning she sold her grandmother's second-best shawl to a cloth merchant who didn't ask questions, and took the east road out of Brindleford with her hood up and her chin down.

* * *

The road taught her other things in the days that followed. It taught her which farmhouses left water out for travelers and which dogs were for warning rather than company. It taught her that her accent marked her faster than her face—that the flattened vowels of the Hollow, where speech had fossilized a century ago, sounded antique and strange to eastern ears, and that the safest answer to *where you from, girl?* was a shrug. It taught her hunger, properly, for the first time: the hollow ache behind the ribs that made the world go sharp and slightly gray at the edges.

And on the sixth day, outside a market village called Tarn's Cross, it taught her that wonder and vulnerability travel together.

She should have seen it coming. That was the thing she hated afterward—not being robbed, but how *easy* it was. Two men detached from the shade of a grain wagon with the unhurried confidence of people who had done this often enough to have a rhythm. One stayed near the wagon, whistling. The other drifted toward her along the verge, friendly, asking if she was headed to market, whether she'd heard about the fair, whether she had far to walk. His accent was warm and local and his eyes never once met hers; they rested on the strap of her pack, on her belt, on the shape of the coin pouch under her cloak.

"No," Noor said. "I mean—I'm not going to the market."

"Shame." He smiled. His hand closed around her wrist, casual as a handshake, and twisted.

The world lurched. She was spun into the ditch with a violence that stunned the air out of her, and by the time she got her knees under herself the man had her pack open and the coin pouch in his fist and the oilcloth bundle—

"No!" She lunged. He caught her by the hair and put her down again, harder, and the sky wheeled overhead, blue and indifferent. Through a haze of dirt and ringing ears she saw him weigh the bundle in his hand, frowning at the weight, beginning to unwrap—

"Excuse me," said a voice, bright as a struck bell, "but I believe that belongs to the lady."

The whistling man by the wagon stopped whistling. Noor rolled onto her side.

A figure was approaching down the road, and even mid-misery Noor's first coherent thought was that she had never seen anyone move like that—as though the road were a stage and he had been waiting in the wings for precisely this cue. He wore a performer's motley subdued almost to respectability: a green coat with tarnished brass buttons, a scarf of improbable orange, dark curls escaping from under a hat set at an angle that suggested it had been placed there deliberately rather than fallen there naturally. Over one shoulder hung a case shaped for a stringed instrument. He was smiling at the robber with the wide, delighted smile of a man greeting an old friend he intended to ruin.

"Mind your own road," the thief said.

"Oh, I intend to. This is it." The newcomer spread his hands. "You see, I know this lady. We're cousins. Distant. Tragic family history—you wouldn't want to hear it, it involves a shipwreck and a disputed inheritance and, I'm afraid, the Magistrate's own constabulary, who are terribly bored just now and grateful for any excuse." He sighed. "Also—and I say this as a professional, with love—the wagon. Your friend by the wagon. He's been watching the two soldiers at the crossroads shrine for the last five minutes, and soldiers get so *restless* when they've nothing to do. Shall we all wave to them together? I make an excellent witness. I've testified in three provinces. I'm famous for it."

The silence stretched. Somewhere down the road, faintly, metal jingled—helmets, maybe; maybe nothing. The whistling man said something low and urgent.

The thief looked at Noor on the ground, at the bundle in his hand, at the smiling man in the orange scarf, and made the arithmetic of a careful man. He dropped the pack. He dropped the pouch beside it, after weighing it one last time with genuine regret. Then he backed away, both palms raised in mock courtesy, and the two of them were swallowed by the traffic of the road as neatly as if the road had done it on purpose.

"Thieves," said the stranger, to no one, with mild reproach, as if remarking on weather.

Noor pushed herself up. Her scalp throbbed. Her palms were scraped and bleeding thinly, and her heart was going so fast it felt less like fear now than like anger looking for a place to land. "I didn't need help."

"Of course not." He picked up her coin pouch, brushed the dust from it with two fingers, and offered it back with a small bow. "You had the situation entirely in hand. I merely wished to observe."

She snatched the pouch. "Who are you?"

"Kesh." Another bow, shallower, mocking himself even as he made it. "Kesh of the Crossroads, some call me, which sounds grander than it is—it mostly means I sleep wherever two roads argue. And you are a girl from a place with no roads at all, paying in hundred-year-old silver, wearing a sword-hand callus on a farmer's daughter's knuckles—" he tipped his hat toward her scraped palms, "—and lying badly about the market. Don't worry. I'm the only one who noticed, and I notice professionally."

Noor's hand had gone, without her deciding it, to the front of her cloak—to the ring beneath the cloth. Kesh's eyes tracked the movement, and something flickered behind the performance, quick and unreadable, there and gone like a fish under ice.

"You were robbed," he said, more gently, dropping the brightness like a mask set down on a table. "It happens on this road. It happened to me twice my first month out. The trick isn't avoiding it forever; nobody manages that. The trick is having someone nearby who owes you a favor, or wants one." He smiled again, smaller this time, and jerked his thumb back down the road. "There's a pie stall in the village ahead run by a woman named Odile who thinks I'm funnier than I am. First pie's on me. You look like a person who hasn't eaten a pie in approximately forever, and I have a professional instinct about these things."

"Why?" Noor asked. It came out rougher than she meant. "Why help me?"

Kesh considered her for a moment. Whatever he was weighing—and she could feel him weighing it, the way she'd learned to feel her grandmother choosing which version of a story to tell—he did it quickly.

"Call it an investment," he said. "People who get robbed on this road and don't cry about it afterward are usually going somewhere interesting. I like interesting. Interesting pays better than pies." He turned and began walking backward down the verge, facing her, hat brim dipping. "Coming, cousin?"

Every sensible part of Noor—the part raised on Sable's warnings, the part still bruised from the ditch—said *no*. Walk the other way. Trust no one. The map was burning a hole through six layers of cloth and oilskin, and this grinning stranger read people like broadsheets, and the last man who had carried secrets toward the Hollow had died smoking at the boundary stones.

But her coin pouch held eleven coins and a handful of clipped slivers. Her feet were blistered through. Ahead of her lay Vessaline, a city she knew only from a dead man's whisper and her grandmother's oldest, most frightened silences, and she had already been marked on the road once today by men who were merely hungry. There would be others who were worse.

"I'm not your cousin," she said.

"No," Kesh agreed cheerfully, falling into step beside her, matching her pace without seeming to. "But you'll do."

They walked toward the village together, and Noor kept her hand over the ring and told herself she was simply accepting a pie. But as the rooftops of Tarn's Cross rose gold in the late light, and Kesh began—apparently unable to help himself—to narrate the entire biography of the pie woman, complete with impressions, Noor caught him glancing at her cloak, at the place where her hand rested, and understood with a cold little clarity that she had not been rescued so much as acquired.

And the worst of it, she thought, hurrying to keep up with his easy stride, was that for the first time since the stranger had died in her arms, she was not entirely alone on the road.

That was either mercy or a trap. With her luck lately, she suspected she'd find out which before the week was out.

Behind them, unnoticed by either, one of the grain-wagon men had doubled back along the hedgerow, keeping pace, and watching the orange scarf bob through the dusk—and memorizing, for later telling, the face of the girl who clutched at her chest as though she were holding something that could burn the world.
