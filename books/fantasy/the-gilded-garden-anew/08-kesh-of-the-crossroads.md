## 8. Kesh of the Crossroads

Kesh Varrow had three rules, and he'd broken all of them by noon.

Rule one: never feed a stranger from your own purse. Rule two: never learn a stranger's name before you've learned what they're worth. Rule three—his mother's rule, the one that had kept him alive through two famines and one very bad marriage—never, under any circumstances, fall for a girl with trouble written on her like a warrant.

The girl with the valley accent and the burned map was eating his stew.

He watched her across the fire he'd built in the lee of the crossroads shrine, the little stone godling's face worn smooth by centuries of travelers' thumbs. She ate the way people eat when they've been hungry longer than they'll admit: carefully, without waste, but with a kind of astonished gratitude, as if food were a rumor she'd only just confirmed. Her hair was cropped ragged at her jaw—recently cut, badly, with something duller than a knife—and the cloak she wore was his spare, the gray wool one with the burn hole in the hem that he'd been meaning to mend for a year.

She'd taken it without ceremony when he offered. That had interested him more than anything else about her. People who refused charity were either proud or afraid. People who accepted it like it was an accounting error were usually both.

"You're staring," she said, not looking up.

"I'm assessing."

"Assessing what?"

"Whether you're worth the stew." He turned his bowl in his hands, letting the steam curl past his face. "It's venison. Well. It's venison-adjacent. There was a deer involved at some point in its history, I'm fairly confident."

That earned him half a smile—the corner of her mouth twitching before she caught it and put it away, like a coin she couldn't afford to spend. Noor Fairweather. He'd gotten the name out of her the night before, or most of it; she'd given it the way you hand over a hostage, slowly, watching to see what he'd do with it. Fairweather. He'd rolled it around his head all night while he pretended to sleep. He knew the name. Everyone past thirty knew the name, though mostly as a story grandmothers told to make children behave: *the Fairweathers sealed the garden, the Fairweathers started the war, the Fairweathers took the magic and buried it where none of us could reach.* A dead guild. A cautionary tale. And here was one of them, nineteen years old at most, sitting at his fire eating his venison-adjacent stew with a courier's bloodstain still ghosting the inside of her satchel.

He'd seen the satchel stain while she slept. She thought she'd hidden it well. She hadn't. The dark smear had soaked through the canvas in the shape of a handprint, five fingers and a palm, pressed flat as if someone had held the bag down while dying beside it.

*The thirteenth turn,* the dying man had said, according to the story she'd let slip in fragments. Kesh collected stories the way other men collected debts—which is to say relentlessly, and with interest.

"So," he said. "Vessaline."

Her spoon stopped halfway to her mouth. "I didn't say I was going to Vessaline."

"You didn't have to. You asked me three times last night how far it was. You flinched when I mentioned the Court garrison at Millbrook. And this morning you studied the signpost pointing south for so long I thought you were trying to set it on fire with your mind." He shrugged. "Also, you're wearing my cloak, which means you owe me honesty. That's the toll. Cloak-toll. Very old law. Older than the shrine."

"There's no such law."

"There absolutely is. I invented it just now, which makes it exactly as enforceable as most laws I've encountered." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and gave her the grin that had talked him out of six arrests and into twice as many bad ideas. "Here's what I know, Noor Fairweather. You're running toward something, not away from it—that's rare, most runners are all retreat and no direction. You've got a dead man's cargo and no idea who else knows he's dead. And you have never once in your life handled money, because yesterday you tried to pay for bread with a button."

"It was a silver button."

"It was a *coat* button. From a coat made within the last ten years, which means it came off a garment, not out of a mint." He spread his hands. "You are, and I say this with enormous affection, the least equipped traveler I have ever met. You'd be robbed blind by the second toll bridge. Robbed *competently* by the first."

"And you wouldn't rob me?"

"Oh, I'd rob you beautifully," Kesh said. "But I'd leave you enough for bread. Professional courtesy."

The fire popped. Somewhere down the crossroads, a cart creaked past, the driver singing something tuneless about a miller's daughter. The shrine godling smiled its blank, thumb-worn smile at them both, keeping whatever counsel stone godlings keep.

Noor set down her bowl. When she looked up at him, her eyes were the color of the Hollow must be, he decided—green gone gray with shade, the green of things growing under something heavy. "Why did you help me? Yesterday. With the cutpurse. You didn't know me."

True question, then. Underneath the wariness, a real one, and it deserved better than a grin. Kesh considered lying—it was his native language, lying, he dreamed in it—but there was something about the way she asked, straight and unadorned, that made the cheap answers feel like littering.

"Because he'd have taken the satchel," Kesh said. "And you'd have let him. You'd have fought, mind, I saw you square up—you fight like someone who's never lost a fistfight because you've never been in one—but if he'd gotten the bag and run, you'd have stood there and let him go rather than chase him into a crowd." He tapped his own temple. "I could see you deciding it. Weighing whether the bag was worth a knife in the ribs. You were going to decide no."

"It's not mine to bleed for," she said quietly. Then, catching herself: "It's—"

"A dead man's. I know. I saw the handprint." He let that sit between them, honest as a blade on the table. "Here's my problem, Noor. Everything about you says *trouble*. The cloak you can't afford, the map you won't show me, the name that gets taverns quiet when you say it too loud. My whole life, my whole *profession*, is built on walking around trouble, not through it. And yet." He gestured at her, at the fire, at the general situation. "Yet."

"Yet?"

"Yet I spent last night cataloguing everyone who might come looking for you instead of sleeping. Yet I bought you breakfast with money I owe to four separate people, two of whom have knives and one of whom has a *grudge*, which is worse. Yet I'm about to do the stupidest thing I've done since the incident with the magistrate's wife, and I want you to understand that I know it's stupid while I'm doing it. That's important to me. Self-awareness."

"What's the stupid thing?"

"I'm offering to take you to Vessaline myself." He said it lightly, the way you step onto ice you've already tested. "Guide, escort, conversationalist, occasional liar-for-hire. I know the roads, the tolls, which guards take bribes and which take offense, which inns water the ale and which water the *guests*. I know Vessaline itself—every gate, every gutter, every archivist with a price and every priest with a weakness. You need to get there alive and unnoticed. Those two requirements are, historically, my entire skill set."

"Why?" Again the straight question, no ornament. She really was from nowhere, this girl. Nowhere taught you to ask like that.

"Three reasons." He held up fingers. "One: you'll pay me. Not much, I know—I've seen your button—but you'll pay me in something better than coin eventually, and I'm patient. Two: whoever killed your courier will follow his trail, and trails lead through crossroads, and I would very much like to be standing next to the interesting part of the story when they arrive, because information about *you* is about to become the most valuable commodity on this road, and I intend to be holding the deed." He paused. "Three."

"You don't have a third."

"I have a third. I'm deciding whether to tell you." He looked into the fire for a moment. The truth was that reason three was the only real one, and real ones were expensive. "My mother used to say there are two kinds of people in the world: people who run from fires, and people who run *toward* the smoke to see if anyone needs pulling out. She was the second kind. It killed her, eventually, pulling someone out. But she died knowing which kind she was, and I've spent twenty-nine years trying to be the first kind and failing at it daily." He spread his hands. "You look like smoke, Noor Fairweather. I find myself walking toward you against my own excellent judgment."

She stared at him. For a long moment he thought she'd laugh, or throw the stew at him, or stand and walk south alone and vanish into the world that wanted her dead. Instead she said, "Your mother sounds like she'd have liked my grandmother."

"Terrifying women often travel in packs, spiritually speaking."

Something loosened in her shoulders—a single degree, barely visible, but Kesh made his living noticing single degrees. "If I say yes," she said, "there are conditions."

"Naturally."

"You don't touch the map. You don't ask what's on it. If I tell you to walk away, you walk away, and you forget my name."

"Forget your name," Kesh repeated. "The name that's currently worth more than this entire crossroads. Sure. Easy. I'll also forget my own debts while I'm at it, and possibly how to breathe."

"Kesh."

"No, no, those are fine terms, wonderfully reasonable, I accept all of them in the same spirit in which they're offered—which is to say, I accept them out loud and interpret them however survival requires." He stuck out his hand. "Counter-condition. One. Just one. When we reach Vessaline, whatever you're doing there—whatever the map leads to—you tell me before you do it. Not after. Before. I don't need to stop you. I probably won't even try. But I refuse to be the fool in the story who carries the lantern and never asks what's at the end of the tunnel."

She looked at his hand. He watched her weigh it—not the handshake itself but everything attached to it, the whole tangled ledger of trust and risk that a stranger's palm represents. He recognized the arithmetic. He did it himself, constantly, with everyone. It was strangely moving to watch someone else do it and land, against all sense, on yes.

Her grip was calloused in the wrong places for a thief and the right places for an orchard worker. Firm. Brief.

"All right," she said. "Vessaline. Together."

"Together," Kesh agreed, and felt the word settle over him like a debt coming due, which was, after all, the only kind of promise he trusted.

* * *

They walked south through the morning, and Kesh learned her the way he learned anything: sideways, by indirection, hoarding details.

She walked like someone conserving energy she didn't know she'd need—no soldier's stride, no merchant's amble, but a steady, stubborn pace that would outlast both. She flinched at birdsong the first hour, until she identified it as birdsong, and then was embarrassed about the flinching, and covered the embarrassment by asking sharp, precise questions about the road ahead. She counted things. He noticed it by midday: fence posts, crows, the coins in a beggar's cup as they passed. A woman doing sums about the world, as if she'd only recently learned the world required counting.

"You grew up somewhere small," he said, as the road bent through a stand of birches gone gold at the edges. "Small enough that the trees have names."

"Some of them," she admitted. "The old ones."

"And nobody locked their doors."

"How would you—" She stopped. Started again. "There aren't locks in the Hollow. There's nothing to steal and nowhere to run. It didn't seem remarkable until right now, saying it out loud."

"The Hollow." He tasted the name. "That's the valley. The magicless one. The place maps draw as blank space because surveyors go in and come out with nothing to report." He glanced at her sidelong. "What's it actually like? The blank space."

She was quiet long enough that he thought she wouldn't answer. Then: "Quiet. Not peaceful—quiet. Like a room where someone's stopped playing music and everyone pretends they prefer it that way." She pulled his gray cloak tighter. "Everything grows a little slower there. Everything ends a little sooner. And everyone calls it safety."

"That's not blank space," Kesh said softly. "That's a wound drawn as blank space. Someone went to a great deal of trouble to make your home look like nothing."

He felt her look at him—really look, reassessing, recalculating whatever figure she'd assigned him in her internal ledger. Good, he thought. Let her. Let her wonder whether the charming fool was a fool at all. The charm was real, but it was also armor, and armor worked best when people forgot it was there.

They camped that night in the ruins of an old waystation, its roof fallen in, its hearth still sound. Kesh built a small fire and produced, from some interior pocket of his coat, a paper packet of dried plums, which he divided with the scrupulous fairness of a man who'd once fought siblings over less.

"Tell me about Vessaline," Noor said. "Since we're walking into it."

"Ah." He settled back against the hearthstone. "Vessaline. City of Brass Bells. Built on the bones of the old capital, which was built on the bones of the one before that, which was—well, you see the pattern. The city eats its own history and sells it back to pilgrims. You can buy a Fairweather luck-charm there for three coppers. Carved bone, mass-produced, blessed by no one. They sell them in the shadow of the Archivists' Quarter, where the actual records of your family molder in sealed vaults that no one's paid to open in forty years." He watched her absorb that. "The bells ring thirteen times at midnight, except the thirteenth bell is silent—cracked, centuries ago, never recast. Superstition says the city will end when someone casts a new one. So of course every generation someone proposes casting it, and every generation the proposal dies in committee. Vessaline runs on things everyone agrees not to finish."

"Thirteen," Noor murmured. Her hand had gone, unconsciously, to the satchel strap.

Kesh noted it. Filed it. Said nothing—for now. A good broker knew when a silence was worth more than a question.

Instead he said, "Whoever you're meeting there—or whatever you're looking for—know this about the city: it watches. Every doorway is a pair of eyes that rent by the day. The Ember Court's magistrates buy information the way brewers buy grain, in bulk and without questions. The Thornbound have sympathizers in every kitchen and stable. And above it all sit the bell-towers, where anyone with a spyglass and a grudge can count who enters which gate." He fed a stick to the fire. "Getting in unnoticed is easy. I've done it eleven ways. Staying unnoticed once you're *in*—that's the craft."

"Eleven ways," she repeated. "What was the best one?"

"Funeral procession. I was the chief mourner. Nobody looks at mourners—they look at the coffin, and the coffin contained, at various points during the journey, forty pounds of smuggled saffron and one extremely annoyed pigeon." He grinned at her expression. "The pigeon wasn't part of the plan. Plans rarely survive pigeons."

And there it was again—the half-smile, quicker this time, harder to catch. She was learning to spend them. He found himself absurdly pleased, and crushed the feeling under the usual boot: *she's an asset, Varrow, a walking fortune with legs, keep the ledger clean.*

Except the ledger wasn't clean. He knew it wasn't. Somewhere between the cutpurse and the plums, a second column had opened in his books, unpriced, unlabeled, and every time she looked at him with that grave, counting gaze, the column got longer.

He was saved from examining this by the sound of hooves.

Not near—distant, up the north road, but moving fast, and moving *together*. Kesh was on his feet with his hand out for silence before his mind finished the count: four horses, maybe five, riding hard at dusk, no cart-rattle, no pack-train jingle. Riders in a hurry carried either news or warrants, and neither stopped at ruined waystations unless they were checking them.

He doused the fire with a sweep of his boot and pulled Noor back into the deep shadow behind the collapsed roof-beam. She came without resistance, breath held, body still—too still, he thought. Frightened-still. Or recognizing-still.

The hoofbeats grew. Slowed. Passed the waystation at a walk.

Through a gap in the timbers Kesh saw them: five riders, armored in the lacquered black-and-crimson of the Ember Court, their livery catching the last light like cooling coals. At their center rode a man in civilian dress whose bearing was entirely military—a narrow face, a magistrate's chain glinting at his throat, eyes that swept the ruin the way a lender sweeps a ledger.

Corvane. Kesh knew the face from a dozen whispered descriptions. The Court's best hound, this far south, this far from any court business he should have—

And then Corvane raised one gloved hand, and the column halted, and the magistrate turned his horse directly toward the waystation's broken door, and Kesh felt Noor's breath stop against his shoulder.

"Well," Kesh breathed, barely a sound at all, his heart hammering a rhythm that felt suspiciously like rule number three shattering. "Isn't *this* interesting."
