## 9. The Ember Court's Summons

The checkpoint appeared out of the morning mist the way a toothache appears—suddenly, and with a certainty that it had been waiting for you all along.

Noor saw it first: a squat stone arch straddling the road where the hills narrowed toward the river crossing, hung with banners of deep orange and black. The Ember Court's colors. Torches burned in iron brackets on either side even though the sun was well up, their flames burning a shade too steady, a shade too bright—the kind of fire that had been told to burn and would not dare do otherwise. Behind the arch, wagons queued. Behind the wagons, soldiers.

Her stomach dropped through the bottom of the cart.

"Kesh," she said, low, without turning her head. "Roadblock."

"I see it." He didn't miss a beat of his humming—a little tuneless thing he did when he was thinking, which she had learned meant he was doing arithmetic of some kind, usually involving other people's money or other people's gullibility. "Two guards at the arch, one checking papers, one collecting toll. Two more by the brazier. A sergeant under the banner. And—" his eyes flicked sideways, quick as a card leaving a sleeve, "—a magistrate's coach parked past the trees. Black lacquer, court sigil. That's not routine."

"What does that mean?"

"It means someone important wants to look at faces today." He clicked his tongue at the mule, an ancient, bad-tempered creature they'd bought two days ago from a farmer who had lied about its age with impressive conviction. Kesh had respected the lie enough to pay full price. "Now listen to me, because we're about to have very little time and no room for error. What are your papers?"

Noor touched the inside pocket of her cloak, where the folded documents sat against her ribs. Forged papers. She still could not say the word comfortably, even silently. *Forged.* As if the letters themselves might hear her and confess.

"Merchant's daughter traveling with her father's factor," she recited. "Name's Wren Calloway. You're Tam. We're carrying wool samples to my uncle in Marrowford."

"And who wrote those papers?"

"You said you knew a man."

"I know many men. This particular man owes me eleven silver and a sincere apology, which is why I checked his work twice before paying him." Kesh's humming stopped. "The seal is good. The ink is good. The handwriting is good. Do you know what isn't good?"

"No."

"*You* are. You've got a face like a woman reading her own eulogy. When he asks where you're from, don't think. Thinking is how liars die. Have an answer already living in your mouth, small and boring, and give it away like it costs you nothing. Boring is armor. Can you be boring?"

"I grew up in a valley where the most exciting event of the decade was a new fence post."

"Then dig deep, Wren Calloway." And then, infuriatingly, he smiled at her—that crooked, easy smile that had gotten them free bread in three towns and nearly arrested in a fourth—and the smile did something strange to her fear. It didn't erase it. It gave it somewhere to stand.

The cart rolled forward into the queue's slow shuffle. Noor kept her eyes down and her hands folded, the picture of a merchant's daughter enduring a tedious morning, and let the details of the checkpoint come to her one by one, because looking directly at things had a way of making them look back.

The sergeant under the banner was young, with the raw knuckles of someone who drilled more than he fought. The paper-checker wore spectacles and handled documents the way Sable handled seedlings—with a gardener's suspicion of anything that might be diseased. The magistrate's coach sat half-hidden in the tree shadow, its black lacquer catching slivers of light, and beside it stood a figure in grey robes who had not moved once since Noor first saw him. Not shifting weight. Not turning his head. Just watching the line of travelers with the patience of a heron over still water.

"That one," she breathed. "By the coach. He hasn't blinked."

"Magistrates don't blink. It's in the training." But Kesh's jaw had tightened, just slightly, and Noor had learned enough of his face by now to read the difference between his performance of ease and the real thing. "Grey robes means a circuit magistrate. They travel when the Crown wants something found. Papers, people, debts, secrets—all the same to them. Cargo in different shapes."

"We could turn around."

"The road behind us has been watched since yesterday. I paid a boy in the last village to count riders coming up from the south." He said it lightly, as if reporting the weather. "We go through. Smile at nothing. Agree with everything."

They were three carts from the arch when Noor heard the voice.

"—name and business."

A farmer ahead of them, answering in a flat drone. The spectacles man stamped something. The farmer rolled through. Simple. Mechanical. A gate that opened for anyone who fed it the right words.

Two carts. One. Then the mule was clattering under the stone arch, torchlight sliding orange across the cart boards, and the spectacles man raised one finger—not at Kesh, but at Noor—and said:

"You. Dismount, please."

*Please.* The courtesy was worse than a shout.

Kesh hopped down first, all loose limbs and affable weariness, stretching like a man whose back hated long roads. "Morning! She's my employer's daughter—I'm just the factor, the brains are all hers, gods help us both—"

"Spectacles ignored him entirely. His eyes stayed on Noor as she climbed down, and there was a quality to his attention that made her skin prickle: not lust, not suspicion exactly, but *sorting*. He was placing her in a drawer, and the drawer wasn't closing easily.

"Papers."

She handed them over. Her hands did not shake. She was distantly proud of that, the way one is proud of not screaming during a fall—one is falling regardless.

"Wren Calloway." He read it aloud as though tasting it. "Traveling to Marrowford."

"To my uncle's warehouse. Wool samples." Small. Boring. It cost her nothing. It cost her everything, but her voice didn't show the ledger.

"Where from?"

"Coppervale." The name came out smooth, and only afterward did Noor realize she had never once rehearsed saying it aloud. It lived in her mouth now. Kesh had built it for her, plank by plank: Coppervale, the wool, the uncle Aldous—no, *Ambrose*, Ambrose Calloway, who dealt in dye-grades and was famously tedious about moisture content in stored fleece. She had a whole uncle now, a whole invented life with damp wool in it, and some traitorous part of her mind noted that this was the most detailed family history she had ever possessed.

The spectacles man turned the papers over. Once. Twice. Held them closer to the torchlight.

And Noor saw it—the fractional pause, the way his thumb rested a hair too long on the wax seal. The pause of a man comparing something against something else in his memory.

"Your father's factor," he said. "And your father himself? Why does he not travel with his own daughter?"

"He's lame in the hip. Autumn roads are cruel to him." True-sounding. Warm. She even let a little fondness leak into it, because Kesh had taught her that the best lies carried a small cargo of truth. "He worries. He made Tam swear an oath on his ledgers."

Something almost human crossed the man's face—amusement, maybe, or its corpse. He looked up to call the sergeant over, and—

"Oh, for pity's sake, Master Hobb, are you interrogating the girl or courting her?" 

Kesh. Sweeping in with the timing of a man who had spent his whole life arriving precisely one breath before disaster. He plucked the papers from the examiner's hand with the cheerful entitlement of the truly shameless, held them up to the light, squinted at his own forgery, and laughed. "Look at that. Third time this trip someone's given these the full wedding-night examination. Master Hobb, was it? You've a good eye. Genuinely. See that there?" He tapped the seal. "Coppervale guild-mark. Most of the forgeries on this road use the Marrowford mark because it's simpler to draw, and then they get caught by exactly the sort of careful man I can see you are. Whoever cut our seal knew their trade. Cost my employer a fortune and he complained about it for a month, which is how I know it's genuine, because he never complains about anything cheap."

It was magnificent. It was insane. He was explaining, in loving detail, the superior craftsmanship of a forged document to the man whose job was to detect forged documents, and Noor could feel the moment the logic of it landed—not as evidence, but as *personality*. Only an honest man, the performance whispered, would be this much of an idiot.

Master Hobb blinked behind his spectacles. "The seal is... consistent with Coppervale work, yes."

"There you are!" Kesh clapped him on the shoulder with the familiarity of an old friend, and Noor watched the man decide, visibly, in real time, that being flattered was easier than being thorough. "Now, if we're done—and I only ask because my employer's daughter has the patience of a wet cat and we've still a river crossing ahead—"

"One moment." The voice came from the trees.

Grey robes. The magistrate had crossed the distance without seeming to walk, the way certain people did, as if the world simply arranged itself a step closer whenever they wished it. Up close he was older than he'd seemed, with a face like a closed ledger and eyes the color of unpolished pewter. He did not look at Kesh. He did not look at Master Hobb.

He looked at Noor.

"Your name," he said.

She had answered this question twice already. She answered it again, and heard her own voice stay level, and marveled at herself from a great distance, the way one watches a stranger perform courage on one's behalf.

"Wren Calloway, sir."

"Calloway." He turned the name over like a coin he suspected of being clipped. "Wool family."

"Dye-grades mostly, sir. My uncle says color is where the money hides."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Approval, perhaps, or the memory of approval. "Your uncle sounds like a sensible man. Where is Coppervale, precisely?"

"South and east of here. Past the slate quarries." Geography Kesh had drilled into her over two nights of cold campfire: quarries, then the forked oak, then the river bend with the drowned mill. She could draw it blindfolded. She could probably dream in it.

The magistrate studied her a moment longer. Then, to her horror, he reached out—not fast, not threatening, just inevitable—and took a strand of her hair between two fingers. Looked at it against the light.

"Unusual coloring," he said. "Fairweather gold, they used to call that, once upon a time."

The world went very quiet and very loud at once. Noor felt her heartbeat in her teeth. Somewhere behind her, Kesh had gone perfectly still, and his stillness was louder than any noise.

"My mother's side," she said. "Everyone on that side has it. There's a portrait of my great-aunt that looks like a candle flame wearing a dress."

"A candle flame wearing a dress," the magistrate repeated, without inflection. He released the strand of hair. "Charming family. Well, Mistress Calloway. Your papers are in order." He stepped back, and the gray of his robe swallowed the morning again. "Mind the river road. It's been restless lately."

They were waved through. The mule walked. The cart creaked. The arch slid past overhead, and the torches, and the banners, and the patient heron eyes, and only when the road bent and the checkpoint dropped out of sight behind a rise of gorse did Noor allow herself to breathe, and even then it came out ragged.

"You took my hair," she whispered. "He took my *hair*, Kesh. He knows that color. He named it—"

"He was fishing. Grey robes fish everywhere; they catch minnows ninety-nine times out of a hundred." Kesh's voice was light. His hands, holding the reins, were white-knuckled. "You did well. The great-aunt was inspired. I'm stealing her for future use."

"She's not *real*—"

"Neither is Wren Calloway, and yet look how hard she's working." He glanced at her sidelong, and whatever he saw in her face made him soften, a degree, the way ice softens. "Hey. You lied to a magistrate of the Ember Court and walked away. Do you understand what that makes you?"

"A criminal."

"An *artist*," he corrected. "There's a difference. Criminals get caught."

Noor laughed—it escaped before she could stop it, cracked and disbelieving—and pressed her palms against her eyes. Her heart was still hammering out some private, frantic rhythm against her ribs. *Fairweather gold.* The words sat in her chest like a swallowed coal.

* * *

They made the river crossing by late afternoon and camped that night in a hollow beneath a stand of birches, off the road far enough that the firelight wouldn't show. Kesh cooked something involving onions and optimism. Noor ate mechanically and watched the dark beyond the flames.

She couldn't stop seeing it.

Not the magistrate. Not the hair. The *other* thing—the thing she hadn't told Kesh yet, because she hadn't fully believed it herself until the fire made shadows honest.

While Master Hobb had examined her papers, while Kesh performed, while the magistrate fished—during all of it, Noor's eyes had drifted, once, involuntarily, to the wagon standing near the magistrate's coach. A prisoner wagon, barred and shuttered, guarded by two soldiers who stood with the boredom of men assigned to a formality rather than a threat. And through the gap in the shutters, for no more than two heartbeats, she had seen a hand grip the bar.

A thin hand. An old scar across the knuckles. And inked—or branded, she couldn't tell which, the angle had been cruel—into the skin of the inner wrist, half-hidden by a frayed cuff:

A crest. A flowering branch inside a ring of thirteen stars.

The Fairweather mark. Not the faded version stitched on the Hollow's vigil banners, worn soft by eighty years of reverence. This one was sharp. Recent. Kept alive deliberately, the way a prisoner keeps a knife.

She had told no one. She had walked to the cart on legs made of someone else's calm, and she had said nothing, and all day the knowledge had grown in her like a splinter working toward the bone.

"Kesh." Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "At the checkpoint. The prisoner wagon. Did you see it?"

He looked up from the fire. "I saw a wagon."

"There was a man inside. And on his wrist—" She pulled her own sleeve back, traced the shape in the air above her skin, unwilling to touch it. "Thirteen stars. A flowering branch."

The silence stretched. Kesh set down his spoon with exaggerated care, the way he did when he was buying time to think, and when he spoke, all the performance had drained out of his voice, leaving something quieter and older underneath.

"That's a guild crest. An old one. I've seen it on ruins and in ballads and on the kind of souvenirs they sell to idiots in cities." He paused. "I've also seen it on a wanted circular, once. Twenty years old, the parchment was. The reward had been withdrawn, but the paper was still nailed up in a courthouse in Dellamere, curling at the edges. *Last of the Fairweathers,* it said. *By order of the Crown.*"

"Last of the—" Noor's throat closed. "The Fairweathers aren't dead. We're right here. We're just..." *Fading*, she almost said. *Forgotten. Sealed in a valley that magic can't enter.* "Why would the Crown want us? We're orchard-keepers. My grandmother's greatest ambition is a fence post."

"I don't know." He said it plainly, and she trusted him more for not dressing it up. "But I know what I saw today, same as you. A circuit magistrate doesn't ride out to check wool merchants' seals. Someone gave him a description. Something specific enough that he took a stranger's hair in the road and turned it toward the light." Kesh poked the fire, and sparks climbed, brief and gold. "And I know that whoever sits in that wagon wears your family's stars fresh-inked on his skin, which means either he's a fool who worships dead legends..."

"Or he's one of us."

The words left her before she could weigh them, and once they were out, they refused to be taken back. One of us. The thought expanded in her chest, terrible and warm at once. All her life, *us* had meant forty aging souls in a forgotten valley, rehearsing the same stories, tending dying trees. It had never once occurred to her that *us* might extend outward—into dungeons, onto wanted circulars, into twenty years of a history the Hollow had never spoken of.

Sable had burned papers once, Noor remembered suddenly—a small memory, ordinary at the time: her grandmother feeding a box of old letters to the stove one winter night, saying only, *these are done being true.* Noor had been nine. She had smelled the smoke and thought nothing of it.

She thought of it now, and the smoke of it curled through her chest.

"Tomorrow," Kesh said quietly, "we take the eastern fork. Longer, rougher, but no checkpoints until Marrowford. And Noor—" he waited until she met his eyes across the fire, and for once there was no grin, no angle, no ledger in his face, only the bare, unguarded thing beneath, "—if that man in the wagon is what you think he is, then the past isn't behind us. It's riding the same road we are. And magistrates who carry prisoners don't carry them toward mercy."

Noor looked into the fire until the gold of it blurred.

Somewhere behind them, in a barred wagon rolling toward Vessaline, a wrist marked with thirteen stars gripped iron in the dark. And she understood, with a certainty that needed no map and no ghost-written ink, that whatever her family had buried—whatever her grandmother had burned—was not finished with any of them.

The past was not behind her.

It was ahead, waiting in the city of brass bells, wearing her own name like a crown of thorns.
