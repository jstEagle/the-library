## 11. The Thornbound Ambush

Kesh had a theory about coach roads, and it was this: every road that promised comfort was lying.

The Vessaline coach road was the worst kind of liar. It was broad and graveled and lined with poplars that threw shade like a promise of ease, and it was, in Kesh's professional estimation, the single most dangerous stretch of ground between the crossroads inn and the city walls. Comfortable roads carried rich coaches. Rich coaches carried rich people. And rich people were the reason men like Kesh learned to sleep with one eye open and their knives closer than their hearts.

He'd told Noor as much that morning, watching her fold her new cloak with the fussy precision of someone who still believed the world rewarded tidiness.

"Rich roads are robbery with better manners," he'd said. "That's all civilization is."

She'd given him one of her looks—the flat, unimpressed stare she'd been perfecting since the day he'd picked her pocket by accident, found nothing worth taking, and somehow ended up responsible for her instead. "Then why are we on it?"

"Because your map says Vessaline, and my legs say they're tired, and sometimes even liars point somewhere useful."

She hadn't laughed. She almost never laughed at his best material. But she'd climbed into the coach when he bought the tickets, and she'd sat with her back straight and her satchel—*the* satchel, the one with the map sewn into its lining—held in her lap like a sleeping child, and Kesh had spent three hours pretending not to watch the road behind them.

Now, in the amber light of late afternoon, the road stopped lying.

It happened the way these things always happened: too fast and too slow at once. First there was nothing. Then there was a crow lifting off a poplar in a hurry, which meant nothing. Then there was the driver's whistle cutting off mid-note, which meant everything.

Kesh was out of his seat before his mind finished the arithmetic. "Down," he said, and grabbed Noor's wrist, and hauled her into the aisle as the first arrow came through the window where her head had been and buried itself in the seatback with a sound like a fist hitting wet bread.

The coach lurched. Horses screamed. Somewhere ahead, a man's voice—deep, unhurried, carrying the particular calm of someone who had done this many times—called out words that turned Kesh's stomach cold:

"Thornbound! Nobody needs to die today!"

Which was a lie, because Thornbound never said *nobody needs to die today* unless somebody needed to die today.

* * *

They spilled out of the wrecked coach into chaos. The driver was down; the guard was running, sensibly, in the wrong direction. Passengers scattered into the poplar shade like startled birds—a fat merchant, two sisters in matching gray, an old man clutching a hatbox—and the rebels let them go, because the rebels weren't here for passengers.

Kesh counted them fast, the way he counted everything: six visible, probably four more in the trees. Leather armor dyed the brown of dead leaves, thorn-and-briar badges stitched at the shoulder, bows half-drawn but not loosed. Their leader stepped forward, a woman with a scarred jaw and eyes that did arithmetic of their own, and her gaze went past Kesh, past the weeping merchant, and settled—

On Noor.

On Noor, standing in the middle of the road with her satchel clutched to her chest, too frozen to run, exactly the kind of person who gets looked at twice and remembered forever.

"The courier's cargo," the woman said. Not a question. Her voice had the flat patience of a river wearing down stone. "We know it was transferred to this coach at the crossroads. Hand it over, and you walk away."

Kesh moved before he thought, sliding between them with his hands up and his smile already working, that easy crooked thing that had talked him out of a dozen nooses. "Friend, look at us. Do we look like couriers? This is a schoolteacher—" he gestured at Noor, who opened her mouth, possibly to object to *schoolteacher*, "—and I'm a performer. Juggler. Fire-eater, in the good seasons. Whatever our friend the courier was carrying, it wasn't ours."

The woman's eyes didn't move off Noor. "Search them," she said, and two rebels started forward, and Kesh's mind went white-hot and fast, running through every trick he knew—distract, charm, drop, run—but none of them were built for this, for six armed strangers and a girl who couldn't lie and a map sewn into a lining that any competent hand would find in ten seconds—

And then the world broke open.

* * *

Later, Kesh would try to describe what happened, and he would fail, and he would keep trying anyway, because some things demand witnesses even when witnesses are useless.

Noor made a sound—not a word, something smaller, the noise a person makes when a hand closes around the last thing they love—and thrust out her free arm across him, across the space between him and the advancing rebels, palm open, fingers spread.

Light came out of her.

Not firelight, not lamplight, not any light Kesh had ever begged or borrowed or stolen. It came from *underneath* her skin, gold shot through with green, the color of sap and honey and something older than both, and it didn't flash so much as *bloom*—a wave of it rolling outward through the air like heat-shimmer made solid, like the ghost of a garden growing impossibly fast.

The two advancing rebels stopped as if they'd hit glass. One dropped his bow. The other staggered backward, clawing at his own eyes, making a sound Kesh had only heard once before—from a man who'd put his hand into a snare meant for something much larger than himself.

The scarred woman went for her blade, and the light reached her too, and Kesh watched her face do something extraordinary: not pain, exactly. *Recognition.* Her mouth shaped a word he couldn't hear, and then she was shouting it, shouting to her people, her composure cracking clean through:

"*Fairweather!* It's a Fairweather—fall back, FALL BACK—"

The woods swallowed them. Bows vanished, leather shapes dissolved into the brown and green, and in the space of ten heartbeats the road was empty except for the screaming horses, the scattered luggage, the weeping merchant—and Noor, standing in the center of a circle of scorched grass, her arm still outstretched, shaking like a plucked string.

The light guttered. Died. She looked at her own hand as if it belonged to a stranger.

"Kesh," she said. Her voice was very small. "What did I just do?"

* * *

He got her off the road. That was the whole of his thinking for the next several minutes—off the road, into the trees, away from the eyes of the dying coach and its scattering passengers, because Kesh understood something in that moment that Noor clearly didn't yet: she had just become the most valuable piece of cargo in the kingdom, and cargo doesn't get to stand still.

He pulled her through the poplars by the wrist until the road was a pale ribbon far behind them, until they were deep enough in the young birch wood that the light came down broken and green, and only then did he stop and turn and really look at her.

She was still staring at her hands. Turning them over. As if the answer might be written on the palms.

"Noor."

"I've never—" She swallowed. Started again. "I've never done anything. That's the whole point of the Hollow. Nothing works there. Nothing has ever worked there. My grandmother used to hold my hands over a candle when I was small and tell me to make it bend, and nothing happened, ever, and I thought—I thought we were finished. Done. Empty." Her breath hitched. "And now I—did I hurt them? Did I *hurt* those men?"

"One of them's going to have a headache," Kesh said. "Maybe two headaches. You didn't kill anybody, valley girl. You scared the living daylights out of trained killers, which frankly took less effort than I expected."

She wasn't listening. She was somewhere else entirely, her eyes wide and wet and burning with something Kesh recognized, because he'd seen it in gamblers and fugitives and once, memorably, in a duke's daughter who'd discovered forgery: the terrible vertigo of finding out you are not who you thought you were.

"It felt like—" She pressed her fist against her sternum. "Like holding a door shut against a flood. Like it had been pushing against me my whole life and I finally leaned back. And it *knew* me, Kesh. It knew my name. It felt like it had been waiting."

Kesh crouched down slowly, putting himself below her eyeline, the way you'd approach a spooked horse. He kept his voice light, because light was the tool he trusted most, but inside, his thoughts were moving very fast indeed.

Inside the Hollow, magic didn't work. Everyone knew that—it was common knowledge, the kind of thing children learned along with the names of rivers. And everyone also knew the Fairweathers had been the greatest guild of them all, once, before the fall, before whatever had emptied them out. He'd grown up on ballads about them. Half the songs were nonsense, but songs usually grew around a seed of truth like a pearl around grit.

And here was the grit, standing in a birch wood, glowing.

"Here's what I know," he said carefully. "Those were Thornbound. Real ones, not bandits flying colors. And real Thornbound don't ambush coach roads for coin—they're hunting something specific. They saw what you did, and they ran *away* from it, and they shouted your family name like it was a warning." He paused. "You know what that means?"

"That I'm in danger?"

"You were already in danger. It means you're *known*. There's a difference. Danger is weather. Known is a price on your head." He stood, brushing bark off his knee, and forced a grin he didn't fully feel. "Good news is, you just proved you can defend yourself. Bad news is, you just proved it in front of seven witnesses, one of whom is currently reporting to whoever holds her leash that the Fairweather line isn't dead after all."

Noor sat down abruptly on a fallen log, as if her legs had resigned without notice. The satchel was still clutched against her chest, knuckles white.

"They'll come again," she said. Not a question either.

"Oh, absolutely. Both of them. Everybody, eventually." Kesh sat down beside her, close enough that their shoulders almost touched. For a moment he considered—genuinely considered—the exit. Every instinct he owned, honed over twenty-six years of looking out for exactly one person, was whispering the same lesson: *when cargo becomes treasure, drop the cargo.* He could be in Vessaline by tomorrow night with a new name and no entanglements.

Instead he heard himself say: "First rule of being hunted: they can't chase what they can't predict. So we change everything. Route, look, schedule. We walk into the city like pilgrims, not passengers. And you—" he tapped the satchel, gently, "—you learn to do that thing on purpose. Because right now it only comes out when you're terrified, and terror is a terrible teacher."

She looked at him then, really looked, and there was something in her face that made his chest ache in a way he resented deeply. Gratitude, maybe. Or trust. The two most expensive commodities on any road.

"Why are you helping me?" she asked. "Really. You could have walked away back at the crossroads. You could walk away now. I'd understand."

Kesh considered several answers. *Because you're profitable* was true and insufficient. *Because you're interesting* was true and cruel. *Because nobody should carry what you're carrying alone* was false—he'd carried everything alone his whole life and been fine, mostly, more or less.

"Because," he said finally, "I've spent my whole life watching people with power use it badly, and I want to see what happens when somebody uses it well. Call it curiosity. Curiosity's kept me alive longer than caution has."

It was mostly a lie. He was getting worryingly good at telling those around her.

Noor nodded slowly, accepting it, and looked down at her open palm—and this time, deliberately, she closed her eyes and breathed, and Kesh saw it: a faint gold-green shimmer gathering above her skin like pollen in still air, trembling, uncertain, *hers*.

It died after three seconds. But it had come when called.

"I need to get to Vessaline," she said quietly. "There's something under the city. Something about my family. And I think—" her fingers curled closed around the light that wasn't there anymore, "—I think it's been waiting for me a long time."

Kesh exhaled, long and slow, and pulled his threadbare coat tighter, and did the math on how much trouble they were both in now.

Somewhere behind them, back toward the road, a crow lifted from a poplar and flew hard south—toward Vessaline, toward the Court, toward whoever paid for news. Kesh watched it go and felt the noose begin, very quietly, to draw tight.

"We should move," he said. "Before the birds finish singing."
