## 12. Marked

The inn at Millbrook Cross had one common room, six rooms to let, and a tapster who watered his ale so thoroughly it was nearly soup. It also had, as of an hour past midnight, a fire in the hearth, two travelers huddled close enough to it that their knees nearly touched, and a silence between them so heavy it seemed to have weight, to press down on the floorboards and settle into the grain of the wood.

Noor could not stop looking at her own hands.

They looked the same as they always had—chapped at the knuckles from orchard work, a faded scar across the left palm where she'd slipped with a pruning knife at fourteen. Ordinary hands. Farmer's hands. And yet three hours ago those hands had torn the night open.

She kept replaying it, the way her tongue returns again and again to a chipped tooth. The first arrow out of the dark, thudding into the coach's door not a hand's width from Kesh's ribs. The Thornbound pouring out of the treeline in their mottled greens and greys, faces masked, blades already drawn. Kesh shouting something useless and magnificent—*gentlemen, please, we're only passengers*—and then the world going white at the edges, and heat rising up through her arms like water climbing a wick, and the air itself *tearing*.

Three men thrown backward. A cart overturned. A hedge of brambles erupting from bare road-dirt as though it had been growing there for twenty years, thick and grasping and crowned with thorns longer than her finger. She hadn't decided any of it. She hadn't *chosen* it. It had come out of her the way a scream comes out of someone falling—a reflex of the whole body, older than thought.

And now everyone who'd seen it knew what she was.

"They'll have run to their cell leaders by now," Kesh said quietly. He was turning his cup in slow circles on the table, watching the ale slosh. He hadn't drunk any of it. "Thornbound cells are small—six or eight people, scattered, so when one burns the others don't. But word travels fast between them. By tomorrow night, every rebel cell between here and Vessaline will know there was a girl on the coach road who threw magic like a stone."

"And the ones who ran the other way," Noor said. Her voice came out flat, strange. "The coachman. The guard who fled into the trees. Where do they report?"

Kesh grimaced. "Whoever pays them. Which is to say—" he tapped the table twice, "—the Ember Court. A courier goes missing carrying court cargo, rebels attack the recovery party, and a girl sets the hedgerow blooming with raw untrained power. That story will be on a magistrate's desk within two days. Possibly sooner." He finally took a drink, and made a face at it. "Congratulations. You're officially interesting. Interesting is the worst thing you can be."

Noor looked at the fire. In the Hollow, fire was for cooking and warmth and nothing else; her grandmother had a hundred small rules about flame, all of them practical, none of them superstitious, because superstition required believing in something to be superstitious *about*. Here, the firelight caught the copper edging of Kesh's rings and threw little coins of light across the ceiling, and Noor thought about how, three days ago, she would have watched those lights and felt nothing but homesickness. Now she watched them and felt hunted.

"Say it plainly," she said. "I want to hear how bad it is."

Kesh set down his cup. He was quiet a moment, and she realized he was deciding whether to spare her, and she realized further that she hated being spared more than almost anything.

"Fine. Plainly." He held up one finger. "The Thornbound want you because you're proof the old bloodlines still work. Their whole grievance is that the Court hoarded magic through guild families until the guilds died off—and if a Fairweather is walking around throwing power without training, you're either a weapon they can aim or a symbol they can parade. Possibly both." A second finger. "The Court wants you because a Fairweather alive outside the seal means the seal is weakening, which means the thing under Vessaline might be reachable, which means every ambitious person in that city is about to start dreaming very large dreams. You're not a person to them. You're a door." A third finger, and here his voice dropped, lost some of its performance, became almost gentle. "And both of them will burn down everything around you to get to you. Your grandmother. Your valley. Me, probably, though I'm cheap to replace and I'd make a point of haunting whoever did it."

"I won't let them touch the Hollow."

"You can't stop them by wanting it hard enough. I've tried wanting things hard. It's a terrible strategy." He leaned back, chair creaking. "What you can do is stop being findable. Right now, somewhere south of here, a man is drawing a picture of your face from a survivor's description. Dark hair, braided. Green eyes. Young. Traveling with a fast-talking fool in a patched coat. That description is worth money, and money makes people look closely at every girl on every road between here and the city. So we make sure the girl they're looking for stops existing tonight."

Noor's stomach turned over—not with fear, exactly, but with something adjacent to it. Grief's cousin. Because she understood immediately what he meant, and she understood that he was right, and some part of her had already known before he said it.

"You mean the hair."

"I mean everything," Kesh said. "Hair, clothes, name, walk, the way you hold your chin when someone asks you a question. Everything they described, we change. What's left over is someone they've never met."

* * *

The innkeeper's wife lent them scissors and a room upstairs for a coin Kesh produced from somewhere inside his sleeve—he had coins in an implausible number of places, Noor was learning, like a tree that fruited in secret. The room was small and low-ceilinged, lit by one candle, with a washstand whose mirror was a hand's width of polished tin, warped at the edges so that whoever looked into it was always slightly bent, slightly melted, slightly other than themselves.

Noor sat on the stool before it and pulled the braid forward over her shoulder.

Her hair had never been cut short in her life. In the Hollow, long hair was one of the quiet continuities that held the valley together—mothers teaching daughters the same braiding patterns their grandmothers had used, patterns that predated the seal itself, that had come down from the guild's glory days when Fairweather women wore silver thread woven through black braids at ceremonies no one living remembered properly. Sable had braided Noor's hair every morning of her childhood until her hands grew too stiff with age, and after that Noor had done it herself, fingers moving through the same motions, and it had been, though neither of them ever said it aloud, a way of putting on the family each morning. Of wearing the guild.

She unwound the braid slowly. Her hair fell loose past her waist, dark and heavy, smelling faintly of woodsmoke and road dust now instead of apple blossom.

"You don't have to watch," she said. Kesh was leaning against the doorframe with his arms crossed, and he shook his head.

"One of us should appreciate the occasion. Also, if you faint, I'd rather catch you than explain to the innkeeper why there's an unconscious girl in my room holding scissors."

"I'm not going to faint."

"People say that right up until they faint."

She gathered the whole length of it in one fist, held it away from her head, and brought the scissors together just below her grip.

The sound was small. That was the strange part. After everything—the tearing air, the erupting earth, the arrows—the sound of her own transformation was this: a soft snick, and then the weight leaving her neck all at once, so sudden that her head actually tipped back, unbalanced, as if the hair had been holding her upright for nineteen years and had simply decided, mid-morning, to stop.

She stared at the stranger in the warped mirror.

The girl looking back at her had a ragged crop of dark hair ending at her jaw, uneven where her grip had slipped, shorter on the left than the right. Without the braid her face looked wider. Older. Her cheekbones stood out differently; her neck looked naked and pale above her collar. She looked like someone who worked for a living and didn't apologize for it. She looked like someone who had been in fights.

She looked, Noor thought, like no one's granddaughter.

"It'll grow out crooked," Kesh observed from the doorway. "Crooked grows out honest, though. Nobody suspects a person with badly cut hair of having a destiny."

A laugh escaped her—short, startled, half-sob—and she pressed her lips together until it died. Then she picked up the severed braid from her lap. It lay there warm and heavy as a sleeping animal, and for a moment she couldn't make herself move, because this was nineteen years of mornings in her hand, her grandmother's fingers, the smell of the orchard, the vigil candles, the boundary stones, all of it coiled and silent and finished.

"What do I do with it?"

"Burn it," Kesh said, and then, gentler: "Or keep it. Burners regret it sometimes. Keepers get found out. There's no clean option—that's most of what being on the run is, choosing which regrets you can carry."

Noor carried it to the candle.

She told herself she would do it quickly, but her hand hovered a long time, the braid's end curling near the flame, heat breathing against her knuckles. *You can still go home,* whispered some voice in her, some voice that sounded like the valley wind through the apple rows. *Cut nothing. Change nothing. Ride north until the stories forget you.*

But the stories wouldn't forget. Stories about magic never forgot; that was practically their profession. And every day she remained recognizable was a day the map in her coat drew a straighter line from the coach road back to the Hollow, back to Sable's crooked house among the dying trees, back to everything the seal had spent a century keeping invisible.

"Noor Fairweather," she said quietly—to the mirror, or to the braid, or to the girl she had been until last night. "That's who they're hunting."

Then she fed the braid into the flame.

It caught with a smell like snuffed candles and summer, and she held it until the fire reached her fingers, and dropped the burning coil into the washbasin, and watched it curl and blacken and come apart into ash that had been, an hour ago, the shape of her entire childhood.

When she turned around, Kesh was holding out his spare cloak.

* * *

It was a good cloak, better than anything else he owned, which told her something about how seriously he was taking this. Heavy grey wool, hood deep enough to swallow a face, plain of ornament except for a single mend at the shoulder done in mismatched thread. He draped it over her shoulders and stepped back and considered her with his head tilted, the way he probably considered merchandise he was appraising.

"Hood up. Shoulders rounder—you walk like someone who's never once expected to be shoved, and that's a luxury we're retiring. Slouch. Look bored. Boredom is camouflage; nobody robs or recruits a bored person." He circled her once. "Name?"

"That's the problem. Every name I try sounds wrong. 'Maren.' 'Tally.' They sit on me like borrowed shoes."

"Don't pick a name. Names you pick are costumes; names you answer to are skin. Let me try something." He studied her a moment, then said, conversationally, as if asking directions: "Oi. Grey-cloak. You eating or dawdling?"

"Wren," said Noor's mouth, entirely without consulting her.

Kesh grinned. "There it is."

"Wren?" She frowned at herself in the melting mirror. "Why wren?"

"Small brown bird everyone overlooks. Loudest thing in the hedge, though—ask anyone who's stood near one. And it flew out of you just now, which means it was already yours." He shrugged. "Names are mostly true anyway. Wren suits. Better than 'the Fairweather girl,' which is what you'll be called in every report from here to the coast, and which is exactly why nobody in this room should ever say it again."

*Wren.* She tested it silently, the way she'd test a new tool—turning it over, feeling its balance. It fit strangely and then less strangely, the way new boots fit after a mile. *Wren.* A bird with no guild history, no seal, no bloodline, no bounty. A bird that was nobody, and therefore could go anywhere.

Downstairs, the common room had begun to fill with the morning traffic of the crossroads: drovers, a tinker, two women in the grey-and-green scarves that marked them as Thornbound sympathizers without making them legally rebels. Noor's heart slammed once at the sight of the scarves—but the women glanced at her slouched grey figure by the fire and looked away, finding nothing, and the nothing they found was the most valuable thing she owned now.

Kesh settled the bill with sleight-of-hand generosity and steered her toward the door, murmuring, "South road, then Vessaline. We move like we're dull. Dullness, Wren, is a discipline."

Outside, the morning was cold and rinsed-clean, the kind of light that makes even mud look intentional. Somewhere behind them, in a rebel safehouse or a magistrate's office, a sketch of a dark-haired girl was drying on paper. Somewhere ahead, beneath a city built on its own ruins, a gate waited with her name folded into its wards like a letter never sent.

Noor pulled the hood up. It smelled of Kesh—woodsmoke and cheap spice and something like ink—and it closed around her face, and the world narrowed to the strip of road visible beneath its brim.

She had shed the braid. She had taken the cloak. She had answered to a name that belonged to a bird.

And it was working—it was *working*, the innkeeper's wife had looked straight through her, the scarfed women had dismissed her—and somehow that was the most frightening part of all. Not that she was hunted, but how easily she had stopped being Noor Fairweather. How thin the skin of a self turned out to be, once you decided to step out of it.

They were halfway to the horses when Kesh stopped so abruptly she walked into his back.

He was reading a notice nailed to the crossroads post, fresh paper, fresh ink, the paste at its corners still wet. Noor followed his gaze and felt the morning tilt.

It was a warrant. Not a full likeness—just a charcoal impression, hasty but unmistakable: a young woman, dark-haired, sharp-eyed. Beneath it, in formal court script:

*WANTED FOR QUESTIONING — THE FAIRWEATHER GIRL. LAST SEEN MILLBROOK CROSS. REWARD: 500 CROWNS.*

Below that, in different ink, cruder, added sometime in the night by a different hand:

*THE THORNBOUND ALSO SEEK HER. ALIVE. REWARD NEGOTIABLE.*

Both factions. Both rewards. Both notices nailed to the same post, side by side, agreeing on nothing except her.

Kesh exhaled slowly through his teeth. "Well," he said. "We'd better get you to Vessaline before someone reads this one aloud."

Noor—*Wren*—pulled the hood lower and put the post at her back.
