## 13. The City of Brass Bells

They heard Vessaline before they saw it.

The sound came rolling down the road like weather—a thousand bells, brass and bronze and something stranger, ringing in no particular harmony. Kesh had been humming tunelessly for the last hour of their walk, but he stopped when the bells began, and his face did something complicated that Noor couldn't read.

"There she is," he said. "The city that never learned to be quiet."

They crested a hill through a stand of black pines, and Noor forgot how to breathe.

Vessaline sat in a great bowl of land like a coin dropped into a cupped palm—no, not a coin, she thought, watching the light catch it. A bell. The whole city was shaped like a bell, terraced rings descending toward a central spire, and every surface that could hold metal did hold metal: gutters, weathervanes, door-hinges, railings, the scales hanging outside shopfronts, the little charms nailed above thresholds against ill luck. The afternoon sun struck all of it at once, and the city rang with reflected gold.

And beneath the gold, if you looked—and Noor looked; she had spent nineteen years learning to look closely at things—the bones showed through. The lowest ring of the city was built on foundations too massive for any building, walls of fitted stone that no mason had raised in living memory. Roads ran straight where roads had no reason to run straight, cutting through newer streets like old scars. And here and there, rising out of the terraces like teeth from a gum, stood fragments of towers much older than anything around them, sheared off clean at strange heights, as if some enormous hand had come down and snapped them.

"The war," Noor said quietly.

Kesh glanced at her. "You've heard of it?"

"My grandmother told me about it." Every night of her childhood, more or less. *Before the Sundering,* Sable always called it, in the reverent hushed voice she saved for the guild's oldest stories. "She said the cities burned for nine years."

"Nine years, give or take." Kesh's voice had lost its performance. He was watching her with that careful look he got sometimes, the one that made her feel like a puzzle he was deciding whether to solve or sell. "Most people just call it the Old Ruin now. Easier on the conscience." He nodded down at the city. "Everything you see standing was built after. Everything you see broken was built before. Vessaline didn't rebuild so much as settle on top of the corpse, like—" he considered, "—like moss on a fallen tree."

Noor said nothing. Her grandmother's stories had never mentioned what the fallen tree looked like up close.

* * *

The road funneled them down with carts and pilgrims and a caravan of feathered lizards loaded with dye-wood, and by the time they passed under the first gate Noor's ears had adjusted to the bells enough to pick out individual voices in the chorus: a deep bell tolling the hour somewhere near the center, a cluster of small bright ones chiming over what sounded like a market, one cracked bell with a stutter in its voice that rang and rang and never quite finished its note.

The gate guards barely looked at them. Kesh had coached her on the walk in—*walk like you owe someone money and you know exactly where they live*—and it seemed to work, or else the guards were simply bored. They stamped papers without reading them, waved the lizard caravan through, and turned back to a dice game played on an overturned helmet.

Inside, the city closed around her like a fist.

Noor had never been anywhere with this many people. The Hollow's entire population could have fit into one of these market squares with room left over for the orchards. Here the streets were rivers of bodies—fishwives shouting, children weaving between legs, a woman selling candied plums off a tray balanced on her head, two men arguing over the price of a horse with the elaborate theatrical fury of people who have already agreed on the number. The smells layered themselves: roasting meat, horse dung, hot metal, incense from a shrine recessed into a wall, the green river-smell underneath everything.

And magic. Magic everywhere, casual as breathing.

A lamplighter walked the early dusk with a wand of pale wood, and flame leapt from lamp to lamp ahead of him like tame birds coming home to roost. A fruit-seller's scale corrected itself when she miscounted change, the weights sliding on their own. Above a cobbler's door, a charm shaped like a sleeping cat opened one glass eye as they passed and tracked them, unhurried, before closing again.

In the Hollow, magic couldn't follow. That was the whole point of the place. Noor had grown up thinking of magic the way you think of a wolf at the treeline—distant, dangerous, defined by its absence. But here it wasn't a wolf. It was a workhorse. It was a household tool, worn smooth by use, unremarked upon. Nobody even glanced at the lamplighter.

Something in her chest ached with a feeling she couldn't name. It took her three streets to recognize it as grief.

*This is what they gave up,* she thought. *This is what Grandmother chose for us.* The Fairweather guild had once been the greatest of them all—that much even Kesh knew, though he'd learned it from songs rather than stories. And now the name survived here only in—

She stopped walking.

They had turned onto a wider street that climbed toward the city's middle ring, lined with shops whose goods spilled out onto tables in the open air. And on the nearest table, arranged on a square of faded velvet between a tray of copper bracelets and a stack of painted masks, lay a row of small silver pins shaped like flowering branches.

Fairweather blossoms. The guild sigil, rendered in cheap pewter, each pin priced at two coppers.

"Noor?" Kesh said. "What—"

She crossed to the table without deciding to. The shopkeeper, a round woman with ink-stained fingers, beamed at her with the professional warmth of someone who has greeted ten thousand customers and remembers none of them.

"Good eye! Guild pins, authentic design, very popular this season. The historical societies can't get enough of them." She picked one up and held it to the light. "See the five-petal blossom? Old Fairweather mark. They say the guild masters wore these at the Sundering, when they held the eastern passes alone for a hundred days."

"They say," Noor repeated. Her voice came out flat.

"Oh, well—" the woman laughed, unbothered, "—who knows, after all this time? But it's a good story, isn't it? People want to wear a good story." She turned the pin. "We've got brooches too, and there's a puppet show on Bellringer's Row does the whole siege, wonderful puppets, the youngest Fairweather brother dies beautifully every evening at sundown—"

"The youngest Fairweather brother," Noor said, "was named Tammas. He was seventeen. He didn't die at the eastern passes."

The shopkeeper blinked. "Beg pardon?"

"He died at the western ford. In autumn. There were no passes to hold in autumn; the snows close them." Noor heard herself speaking from a long way away, calm and precise, her grandmother's cadence in her mouth. "He drowned carrying a wounded sapper across the water. The sapper lived. His name was Odo Bren, and his descendants run a ferry on the River Liss to this day, because the family swore a debt to ours and has never broken it."

The silence stretched. Somewhere down the street a bell rang the half-hour, bright and indifferent.

"Well," the shopkeeper said at last, cheerfully recovering, "that's a better story still! You should tell it to the puppet troupe, they'd love the detail—two coppers for the pin?"

Noor stared at her. The woman smiled back, patient, pleasant, utterly without malice, and that was somehow worse than mockery would have been. Mockery would have meant the story mattered enough to insult.

"Noor." Kesh's hand landed light on her shoulder. "Come on."

She let him steer her away. She didn't buy the pin. She thought about buying it anyway, just to take it off the velvet, just so one piece of her family wouldn't sit there being sold—but that was foolish, there were hundreds of them, there would be thousands in a city this size, and her coppers were counted down to the last few.

"You knew all that," Kesh said as they merged back into the current of the street. "The ford. The sapper. The ferry family."

"Every Fairweather child learns the roll of names. It's the first thing we're taught. Before letters, almost." She hugged her arms to herself. The evening was cooling and the crowd pressed warm around them and she felt cold anyway. "Grandmother says a guild lives as long as its debts are remembered. That's why we keep the rolls. Not the glory. The debts."

"And here they keep the glory and lose the rest."

"Yes." She looked up at the middle ring, where lanterns were kindling along the terraces one by one, lamplighters' flames hopping from post to post. "That's exactly it. They kept the part that sells."

Kesh was quiet for a dozen steps. When he spoke again, his voice had the careful lightness he used when he was about to say something he'd already decided was true. "For what it's worth, most of those people couldn't find the eastern passes on a map. The story's worn smooth because everyone rubs it. Doesn't mean the stone underneath is gone." He glanced sideways at her. "You're the stone underneath."

She wanted to laugh at that. What came out instead was closer to a sob, quickly swallowed, and Kesh had the grace to pretend not to notice, pointing instead at a food stall and asking loudly whether she wanted skewers, because the smoke smelled like heaven and he was starving, hadn't eaten since dawn, etcetera, etcetera, filling the air with ordinary words until her face worked itself back into order.

* * *

They ate skewers of spiced lamb on the steps of a dry fountain in a small square off Bellringer's Row, sitting shoulder to shoulder among students and dockhands and an old man feeding crumbs to pigeons that glowed faintly blue in the dusk—enchanted birds, Kesh explained, bred by some forgotten mage, now feral and common as sparrows. Noor fed one from her hand and its feathers hummed against her skin like a struck string.

Above the rooftops across the square rose the tower she'd been noticing all evening: one of the old sheared-off ones, broken centuries ago, its stump crowned with newer construction. But unlike the others, this one bore marks on its exposed stone—carved lines, deliberate, running in patterns too regular for erosion. As the last light faded, lamps mounted on the ruin lit up, and Noor realized the carved lines were words. Hundreds of them. Names, ranked in columns, climbing the tower's broken height.

"What is that?" she asked.

Kesh followed her gaze and chewed thoughtfully. "Roll of Honor. Oldest thing in Vessaline that isn't a wall. They put the war-dead's names on it—well, the noble dead. Common soldiers got a mass grave out past the tanneries." He wiped grease on his trousers. "There'll be Fairweathers on it. Top third, probably. Your lot were famous."

"I know." She was reading the visible columns, straining in the lamplight. "I know some of those names."

"Want to go look? We've time before I take you to my contact."

"No." The word came out faster than she meant it to. She made herself soften it. "Not tonight."

Because she suddenly understood, looking at the tower, that she couldn't bear it—not the names, but the company they kept. Carved in stone for strangers to admire. Ranked. Lit by lamps for tourists. Somewhere on that tower her ancestors' deaths hung displayed like the pewter pins on velvet, polished smooth, rubbed featureless, owned by everyone and therefore no one. And the debts—the real record, the ferry on the River Liss, the sworn names recited every vigil—none of that was here. None of it had ever left the Hollow.

*We kept the debts and they kept the glory,* she thought, *and neither half is the truth.*

The old man finished his crumbs and shuffled away, and the blue pigeons rose in a soft chiming cloud, and Noor sat with her half-eaten skewer going cold in her hand and felt the strangest sensation of standing in two places at once: in this loud, gilded, indifferent city, and in the silent valley that had sent her ancestors out to die in a war this city had turned into decoration.

"Kesh," she said. "Your contact. The one who can read the map."

"Maren. Yes." He studied her. "Why?"

"Nothing. Just—" She looked at the tower one more time, at the small distant lamps burning over other people's names. "How fast can we see her?"

His eyebrows went up. Then he smiled, slow and crooked, the smile of a man whose ledger has just shown an unexpected profit. "Maren keeps late hours," he said, standing and brushing crumbs from his cloak. "Lucky for us, so do I. Come on, valley girl. Let's go wake an archivist."

He set off across the square, threading the crowd like water finding its level, and Noor rose to follow—past the souvenir stalls, past the glowing pigeons, past the tower of borrowed names—down into streets that grew older and darker with every step, toward whatever waited beneath the gilded city and its ten thousand indifferent bells.
