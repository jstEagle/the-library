## 14. The Archivist's Price

Kesh had three rules for surviving Vessaline, and he recited them to himself every time he passed beneath the Brass Bells, whose thousand mouths rang the hour in a chord that was beautiful from a distance and maddening up close.

One: never owe anyone more than they owe you.

Two: never let a mark see you counting.

Three: never, under any circumstance, go to Maren Quill sober.

He broke the third rule within the hour, because the girl was watching him, and Noor Fairweather had a way of looking at things—straight on, unblinking, like a person who'd grown up somewhere honesty wasn't a performance—that made lying feel less like craft and more like vandalism. So he walked into the Sump District with his wits about him and his palms sweating anyway, which was, he reflected, probably the most honest he'd been in years.

The Sump was the lowest quarter of the city, where the old war's ruins hadn't been built over so much as absorbed. Streets here were the ribs of some drowned beast: archways of fused glass that had once been windows, doorframes standing alone in courtyards of moss, staircases ascending to rooms that no longer existed. Rain collected in the hollows and turned everything the color of tarnished coin. Even the bells sounded different down here—muffled, apologetic, as if the city itself were embarrassed by what it kept in its basement.

Maren lived at the bottom of one of those orphaned staircases, in a doorway that led nowhere, behind a door that led everywhere.

"You're going to want to let me do the talking," Kesh said, pausing with his knuckles an inch from the wood.

"I haven't said anything," Noor said.

"You've said plenty. You've got a face that says *I don't understand why we're skulking*, and Maren eats faces like that for breakfast." He glanced back at her. She stood in the drizzle with her hood up and her cropped hair escaping it in dark tufts, arms crossed, wearing his spare cloak like she'd been born to it. Three days since the ambush. Two days since she'd pulled fire out of empty air to save his life and then looked at her own hands like they belonged to a stranger. One day since she'd started studying the map again with an expression he could only call *hunger*.

She was trouble. Not the ordinary kind—the kind that arrived on schedule and could be paid off—but the deep, geological kind, the sort that shifted whole landscapes while everyone slept. And Kesh, who had survived nineteen years in this city precisely by knowing which troubles to walk away from, had walked directly toward this one.

"Knock," she said.

"See, that's exactly the tone that's going to get us killed."

But he knocked, because the truth was he'd already decided, days ago, that he was going to see this through whatever it cost him, and decisions made in the dark tend to announce themselves eventually.

The door opened without a sound. Beyond it lay a corridor that should not have fit inside the doorway—Kesh had stopped questioning the geometry years ago—and beyond the corridor, light: hundreds of candles, thousands, guttering in jars and bottles and broken lanterns, illuminating shelves that climbed into darkness overhead. Books, scrolls, tablets, bundles of letters tied with string gone soft as cloth. The air smelled of dust and tallow and something older underneath, something like the inside of a sealed room that had finally been opened just a crack.

And in the middle of it all, at a desk buried under strata of paper, sat Maren Quill.

She had been beautiful once, or so the story went; now she was mostly angles and ash-gray hair, with ink stains climbing both forearms like ivy and spectacles pushed up into her hair where they served no purpose except to hold it back. She did not look up when they entered. She was writing, quill moving in small furious strokes, and she finished her line before she acknowledged them, because Maren believed that sentences deserved respect and people earned it.

"Kesh Varo," she said, laying down the quill with the care of a surgeon setting aside a blade. "They told me you were dead."

"They tell me that too. Usually when they're trying to make it true."

"Mm." She removed her spectacles, polished them on a sleeve that was already gray, and put them back on. Then she looked past him at Noor, and Kesh watched something move through her face—quick, involuntary, quickly caged. Recognition. Not of the girl. Of the *type*. "You brought me a Fairweather."

Noor went very still beside him. Kesh felt the temperature of the room change.

"Now, before anyone panics—" he began.

"Sit down, both of you." Maren rose, joints cracking like green wood, and shuffled toward a kettle hanging over a candle-flame stove. "Tea first. Deciphering goes better with tea, and what you're going to ask me to decipher is going to go badly, so we may as well start comfortable."

* * *

The tea was terrible, which meant it was genuine; Maren had never learned to fake hospitality any more than she'd learned to flatter. She poured three cups with hands that trembled only slightly, and studied Noor over the rim of her own with the frank appetite of a scholar presented with a primary source.

"Do you know what your family was?" she asked.

"Guilders," Noor said carefully. "Orchard-keepers. Storytellers, my grandmother says. Once."

"Once." Maren smiled without warmth. "Child, I spent eleven years in the Court Archive before they stripped my credentials and threw me down here. Eleven years cataloguing confiscations from the wars. Do you know how many documents survive from the Fairweather guild's height? Four hundred and six. Do you know how many survive from any comparable guild of the same era? Never fewer than two thousand." She set down her cup. "Someone burned your history, and they did it thoroughly, and they did it deliberately, and the fact that you're sitting in my parlor looking like a startled deer suggests you didn't know that."

"We know some of it," Noor said. There was steel under the softness now; Kesh heard it arrive and thought, not for the first time, that the valley had done its best to sand this girl smooth and failed utterly. "That's why we're here."

"That's why *you're* here." Maren's eyes cut to Kesh. "He's here because he owes me, and because he's hoping to turn a profit, and because—" she paused, tilting her head, examining him the way she examined damaged manuscripts, reading the watermarks and the wormholes, "—because he's frightened, though he'll deny it, and fear makes people loyal in ways they don't advertise. Am I close?"

"You're showing off," Kesh said. "It's unbecoming."

"It's Tuesday." She extended one ink-stained hand, palm up. "Show me."

Noor looked at Kesh. It was a question, and they both knew it, and it stung a little that after everything—the ambush, the fire, the road, the way she'd fallen asleep against his shoulder on the last night's coach and pretended not to—he still ranked below certainty in her ledger. But he couldn't blame her. Certainty was rare currency in Vessaline, and he'd spent his whole life passing counterfeit versions of it.

"It's her map," he said. "Her call."

Noor drew it from inside her cloak.

Even now, seeing it again made the hairs on his arms rise. It was old—old the way bedrock is old, old the way the bells are old—and it was wrong in ways the eye registered before the mind could name them. The parchment seemed to drink the candlelight rather than reflect it. The lines of ink moved if you weren't quite looking at them, settling like silt in disturbed water. And there, ghosted faintly beneath the cartographer's confident strokes, other marks: handwriting, small and slanted, in a script too young for the document's age.

Maren took it with both hands, the way priests take relics, and for a long moment she didn't breathe.

Then she began to laugh—a dry, rusted sound, like a hinge that hadn't been oiled in decades.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, you stupid, magnificent children. Do you have any idea what this is?"

"We were hoping you'd tell us," Kesh said.

"This is a survey chart. Pre-war. Look at the notation system—this is Court work, first dynasty, before they prettified their numerals. Someone official drew this." Her finger traced a line across the parchment, and where it touched, the ink brightened, responding to her the way a cat responds to a familiar hand. "But the annotations—" she tapped the ghost-script, "—these are later. Much later. These are *Fairweather* corrections. Someone from your guild took an imperial survey of the city's foundations and marked it up." She looked up, and behind the smudged spectacles her eyes were bright with a fever Kesh recognized and feared: the fever of a woman who had spent twenty years among dead paper suddenly handed living proof. "They marked the way *down*."

"Down where?" Noor asked, though Kesh saw in her face that she already knew—or half-knew, the way you half-know a word on the tip of your tongue.

"Underneath." Maren was already clearing space on her desk, sweeping papers into drifts, weighting the map flat with candlesticks and a skull whose provenance Kesh had decided long ago not to ask about. "Vessaline isn't built on ruins, girl. Vessaline is built on top of Vessaline. Three cities deep, maybe four, each generation paving over the last. And at the bottom of the bottom—" her finger came to rest on a point near the map's center, where the ghost-handwriting clustered thickest around a symbol like a seed split open, "—there's a garden."

The word dropped into the room and lay there.

"The Sealed Garden," Noor whispered.

"So you *have* heard the stories." Maren glanced between them. "Everyone's heard the stories. The court calls it a fable. The Thornbound call it a promise. The archives call it a redaction—I've seen the gaps in the record where entire folios were cut out with razors, and razors leave scars, and scars can be read." She straightened, and her trembling hands went still, which Kesh knew from experience meant she was about to say something she'd rehearsed. "This map shows the catacombs beneath the Sump, the drowned levels, the old moon-shafts. And this—" the split-seed symbol, "—is a gate-mark. Ward-script. First dynasty, but older than anything the dynasties wrote. This gate doesn't open with keys, child. It opens with blood. The right blood."

The candles flickered, all together, though there was no draft.

"And you can read it," Noor said. Not a question. A plea dressed as one.

"I can read enough." Maren sat back down slowly. "Enough to get you to the threshold. Enough to get you killed, more likely. Which brings us to price."

Here it was. Kesh had been waiting for it since the tea, watching Maren the way he watched card tables—not the cards, the hands. And sure enough, her eyes went to the map, and stayed there, and the hunger in them was naked now, undisguised, a scholar's greed which was the most honest greed in the world because it wanted nothing but the thing itself.

"No," Kesh said.

"You haven't heard the price."

"I've seen your face. The answer's no."

"The answer," Maren said, with the patience of a woman who had outlasted empires' paperwork, "is one night. You leave the map with me until tomorrow's second bell. I copy nothing. I memorize nothing. I simply *work* with it—there are readings I must take that cannot be done in an hour, resonances, cross-references against texts I will not show you because you couldn't read them. In exchange, I give you the full decipherment: the route, the wards, the warnings, everything I find. And then the map goes back in your cloak and out my door, and I never speak of it, because speaking of it would bring the Ember Court down my stairs and I am far too old and far too fond of my candles to be burned twice."

"She copies nothing," Kesh repeated. "You swear it on—"

"On what? My credentials? They took those." Maren smiled thinly. "On my life's work, then. On the archive I've built down here, book by book, rescued from every fool with a torch. Burn that instead of me, if I betray you. It's the only oath I have left, and it's worth more than my life, so use it."

Kesh opened his mouth to argue—rule one, never owe anyone more than they owe you, and leaving the single most dangerous object in the kingdom overnight with a disgraced archivist was owing with interest compounded hourly—

"Noor," Maren said quietly, "ask him why he really came to me. Ask him what he owes me. Since he won't tell you himself."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere above, impossibly far away, the Brass Bells began to ring the hour, and the sound came down through the drowned streets like rain through soil.

Kesh kept his face pleasant and blank, which was his masterpiece, his life's finest work, and Noor looked at him with those unblinking valley eyes and waited, and he understood that she would wait forever, and that he had perhaps four seconds before silence became its own confession.

"A favor," he said finally. "Years ago. Before you. A man came to Maren with a debt contract bearing my name and a Court seal, and Maren lost it. Misfiled it. Terribly tragic clerical error." He shrugged. "I've owed her a blank favor ever since. That's why we're here, and that's why she'll do the work properly, because if she cheats you, she cheats herself out of collecting on me. We're collateral for each other. It's very tidy."

"It's a lie of omission," Maren said, "but the arithmetic is correct." She turned back to Noor. "So. The map stays. Or it goes, and you go into the catacombs with half a cipher and my condolences. Decide, child. The candles are expensive and the hour is late."

Noor looked at the map—at the ghost-writing beneath the ink, the handwriting that Kesh had seen her trace with one fingertip at midnight, murmuring, as if she almost remembered writing it. Then she looked at Kesh, and whatever she found in his face, it made her exhale slowly through her nose, the way people do before stepping off a ledge.

"One night," she said. "Second bell tomorrow. If the map isn't in my hand—"

"If the map isn't in your hand, burn the archive and salt the ashes. I mean it literally; there's lamp-oil under the third shelf and I'll draw you a diagram." Maren was already pulling the map toward her, and her hands had stopped trembling entirely now, steady as a surgeon's, hungry as a lover's. "Now go. Both of you. Come back at second bell and I'll either hand you a miracle or a eulogy, and frankly at my age the suspense is the best part."

* * *

They climbed out through the impossible corridor into the drizzle and the dark, and the door closed behind them without a sound, and Kesh became aware that Noor was walking beside him in a silence with edges on it.

They made it three streets before she spoke.

"You lied to me."

"I omitted," he said. "There's a difference. Omission is a survival strategy; lying is a character flaw. I'm very careful about which one I'm caught in."

"What aren't you telling me, Kesh?"

The rain ticked on the hoods of abandoned doorways. Down the street, a lamplighter worked her way along the Sump's edge, and each flame she lit made the ruins briefly look like a city that had never fallen.

"There was no contract," he said. "Not mine."

Noor stopped walking.

"Six years ago," Kesh said, staring at the lamplighter because it was easier than staring at Noor, "a courier came to Maren. Same kind of courier, I'd wager, as the one who died at your boundary stones—Court-trained, carrying something he shouldn't have been carrying, running from someone he shouldn't have been running from. He wanted her to verify a document. She refused—too hot, even for her. Three days later they pulled him out of the canal." He paused. "He was wearing a crest. A tree in a ring of stones. Your family's tree, Noor. Your family's ring."

The rain filled the silence.

"He had a daughter," Kesh went on, quieter now. "Maren found out afterward. Found out the man had a kid somewhere north, in some valley nobody could find on a map, and that the Court was asking questions about dependents, and Maren—Maren hid the file. Buried it so deep even she couldn't swear where it is anymore. That's the favor I called in tonight. Not a contract with my name. A file with yours."

Noor's face, in the lamplight, did something complicated and then went very simple.

"My father," she said.

"Maybe. Probably. The dates fit, and Fairweathers don't grow on trees—" he winced at his own words, "—poor choice. The point is: I didn't bring you to Maren because she's the best cipher-breaker in the city, though she is. I brought you because she might be the only person alive who knows what happened to him. And I didn't tell you because—" he spread his hands, honest for once and hating how it felt, "—because I was afraid you'd hope. Hope's a bad trade, Noor. I've seen it ruin better gamblers than me."

For a long moment she said nothing. Then she reached out and took his wrist—not gently, not cruelly, but firmly, the grip of someone anchoring themselves or anchoring him, he genuinely couldn't tell.

"Second bell," she said. "We come back at second bell. And Kesh? Next time you decide what I can afford to hope—" her grip tightened, "—don't."

She released him and walked on toward the rented room, hood up, spine straight, a girl-shaped storm in a borrowed cloak. Kesh stood a moment longer in the rain, watching the lamplighter's flames bloom one by one down the drowned street, and admitted to himself what he'd been refusing to admit since the treeline:

He wasn't guiding her anymore. He was following.

And behind them, down in the candle-dark, Maren Quill bent over the map with her instruments laid out like knives, and began, with great tenderness, to read.
