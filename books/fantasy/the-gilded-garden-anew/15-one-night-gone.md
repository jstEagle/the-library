## 15. One Night Gone

The night Maren kept the map was the longest of Noor's life.

She had told herself it would be fine. She had repeated it like a prayer while Kesh counted out the last of their coin for the room above the Drowned Bell, a tavern so old its beams had gone black with smoke and its floors sloped like a ship's deck. *One night. She gives it back at dawn. That was the price.* But knowing a thing and believing it were different animals entirely, and Noor spent the hours between moonset and morning learning the difference in her body—her jaw aching from clenching, her hands restless in her lap, her ears straining through the thin floorboards for footsteps that never came.

Kesh slept. Or appeared to. He lay on the narrow cot with his boots off for once, one arm folded behind his head, breathing in the slow theatrical rhythm of a man who wanted you to think he slept. Noor had learned to tell the difference. Real sleep made Kesh's face younger, softer, the charm burned away down to something almost boyish. This was performance sleep, and she resented him a little for it, because it meant he was awake too, listening to her listen.

"You'll wear a groove in the floor," he said at last, without opening his eyes.

"I'm not pacing."

"You're pacing with your shoulders. It's very disciplined."

Noor stopped anyway and sat on the windowsill, where the glass was warped and bubbled and turned the street below into a smear of lantern light. Vessaline did not quiet at night the way the Hollow did. In the valley, dark meant silence so complete you could hear the orchards breathing—or she had always imagined you could. Here, dark meant a different kind of noise: drunks singing two streets over, a watchman's bell, the creak of the brass bells that gave the city its name swinging in some tower she couldn't see, ringing a quarter hour she didn't care about into a sky she couldn't read. She missed the stars. The city's glow ate them all, and without them she felt unmoored, a boat cut loose from its mooring line, drifting in a harbor full of strangers.

"Do you trust her?" Noor asked. "Maren."

Kesh opened one eye. In the lamplight it gleamed brown and shrewd. "I trust that she wants something from me more than she wants anything bad to happen to me. That's the only kind of trust there is in this city, sweetheart. Everything else is decoration."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the best answer anyone will ever give you here." He rolled onto his side, propping his head on his hand, and studied her with an expression she'd begun to recognize—the one he wore when he was deciding how much truth a moment could hold. "Maren's been disgraced twice, exiled from the Archivists' Guild once, and banned from three private collections she could probably still walk into blindfolded. She's bitter, brilliant, and broke. People like that don't sell you out for gold. Gold's too simple." He paused. "They sell you out for relevance. For being right about something everyone else forgot. And your map—" he tapped his own temple, "—your map makes her relevant again. So no, I'm not worried about Maren. I'm worried about whoever Maren brags to."

Noor's stomach tightened. "You said this was safe."

"I said she was discreet. Discreet isn't silent. Discreet is choosing *who* you tell." He yawned, stretched until his spine popped, and swung his legs off the cot. "Come on. We may as well be miserable in motion. There's a tea house on Lantern Row that stays open all night, and if we're going to stare at nothing, we should do it somewhere with decent cardamom buns."

* * *

They walked. Vessaline at night was a city wearing a mask over its face—the grand avenues shuttered and shadowed, the real life pushed down into the low streets where steam rose from grate-grills and night markets sold fried eel and luck-charms stamped with the Fairweather crest, mass-produced, the gilt already flaking. Noor had stopped looking at those stalls. Looking at them felt like watching strangers wear her grandmother's face carved in cheap tin.

The tea house was warm and half-empty, run by a woman with forearms like dock rope who took one look at Kesh and charged him double. They took a corner table beneath a lamp with a cracked green shade, and Noor wrapped her hands around a cup she didn't drink and watched the door.

It was there, in the third hour before dawn, that the thought finally caught her—the one that had been circling her all night like a moth around a flame.

Copies.

Maren was an archivist. Copying was not merely what archivists did; it was what they *were*. A woman who had spent her life among documents did not look at a map like theirs—a map written in blood-faded ink, ghosted underneath with handwriting Noor somehow recognized as her own—and simply *memorize* it. She would have traced it. She would have measured it, noted its dimensions, its paper, its watermarks, the precise angle of every fold. Somewhere in that cluttered shop behind the Hall of Records, or in whatever bolt-hole Maren actually slept in, there was now a second map. Perhaps a third. Perhaps a tracing so exact that the original hardly mattered anymore.

And if Maren had copied it, then the secret was no longer a thing Noor carried. It was a thing that existed *outside* her, in the world, multiplying.

She thought of her grandmother's voice, dry as autumn leaves: *A secret is like a seed, Noor. You can keep one seed safe your whole life. Plant it, and you've surrendered any hope of controlling the harvest.*

"Kesh." Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "What if she doesn't give it back?"

He looked up from the bun he was dismantling with surgical precision. "Then I burn her shop down and we flee the country. Next question."

"I'm serious."

"So am I. It's a very good shop. Lots of flammable vellum." But then he set the bun down, and the humor drained out of his face the way water drains from a basin, leaving something older and more careful. "Noor. Listen to me. If Maren keeps the map, we've lost a map. If Maren copies the map, we've lost nothing yet—because a copy still needs someone who can read it, and reading it needs me, and Maren, and time, and none of those things are loyal to the Ember Court. What you need to understand—" he leaned forward, lowering his voice, and the lamplight caught the sharp planes of his face, "—is that secrets aren't objects. Everyone treats them like objects. Lock them up, hide them under floorboards. But they're not. They're more like fire. Every time you share one, you're not handing over a thing—you're lighting another wick. And you can't ever take the flame back. You can only decide how many candles you're willing to light, and pray you like what they illuminate."

Noor stared at him. "That's the most honest thing you've ever said."

"Don't get used to it. It'll ruin my reputation." He picked the bun back up, but his eyes stayed serious over the top of it. "Why do you think I asked for half the payment up front? Not because I doubt Maren. Because I doubt *everyone*. Including myself. Especially myself, on my worse days." He chewed, swallowed. "The question isn't whether the secret spreads. It's already spreading—it spread the moment the courier bled on it. The question is whether *you* are still the one holding it when it matters. Speed, sweetheart. That's all survival is, in this city. Being faster than the fire."

* * *

Maren returned the map at dawn, exactly as promised, and Noor hated how relieved that made her feel, because relief was exactly the kind of thing a clever person could use against you.

The archivist arrived at the Drowned Bell looking like she hadn't slept—which, Noor reflected, made two of them—and smelling of candle smoke and old paper and something sharper underneath, a chemical tang Noor couldn't name. She set a wrapped bundle on the table between them with both hands, almost ceremonially, and unwrapped it: the map, folded along its original creases, looking exactly as it had when it left Noor's fingers.

Exactly. Which was itself a kind of answer.

"You traced it," Noor said.

Maren's eyebrows rose—not in guilt, but in something like professional appreciation. "The girl has eyes. Yes. I traced it. I also tested the ink, weighed the paper, and photographed the under-writing by raking light, which is a technique you wouldn't know about and which confirmed something interesting, incidentally—the ghost script isn't ink at all. It's impressed into the fibers. Someone wrote that message first, pressed hard, and the map was made over it later. Years later, judging by the degradation." She folded her arms. "Whoever wrote to you, girl, wrote to you *before the map existed*. Think about what that means."

Noor thought about it. She had been thinking about little else since the night by the fire when she'd first seen the faint ghosting beneath the courier's lines—her own handwriting, or close enough to make her skin crawl, though she had not held a pen until she was seven. *Someone who knew me before I could write.* The thought was a door she kept approaching and turning away from, because behind it was a room she wasn't ready to enter.

"The map," she said, and slid it across the table toward herself, hand flat on top of it like a claim staked in new territory.

"The map," Maren agreed. Then, as she turned to go, she paused, and something flickered across her lined face that Noor would spend the rest of the day trying to classify. Pity? Warning? Guilt? "A piece of advice, free of charge, since your friend overpaid me shamefully. When you leave this city—and you should leave soon, sooner than you're planning—you should assume that everything you've done here is already known. Not by the Court, necessarily. Not yet. But known. Written down. Filed. Vessaline forgets nothing, it just misplaces things for a few years at a time." Her gaze drifted to the map under Noor's palm. "And it remembers Fairweathers most of all."

Then she was gone, swallowed by the morning crowd, and Kesh let out a long breath.

"Well," he said. "I liked her better when she was just expensive."

* * *

The first letter came two days later, and Noor nearly threw it in the river.

It arrived the way Sable's letters always had—no courier, no seal, just folded paper appearing where it shouldn't, tucked into the doorframe of their rented room as if the wind itself had decided to deliver it. Noor knew the handwriting before she touched it: the small, severe, upright script of a woman who had spent sixty years writing records no one would read.

*Noor. Stop where you are. Turn back. I know what you carry and I know what it costs, and I am telling you as plainly as I have ever told you anything: the map is not yours to follow. Burn it. Bury it. Feed it to the river fish. Do not go to Vessaline—if you are reading this, you have already gone, and I have aged ten years writing that sentence.*

Noor read it four times in the grey light of the window, standing very still, while Kesh pretended to be absorbed in re-lacing his boots.

*How does she know?* was the first thought. *How can she possibly know?* Noor had left the Hollow in secret, crossed the boundary stones in secret, traveled in secret, hidden in plain sight through checkpoints and coach yards. And yet her grandmother, forty miles and a lifetime away in a valley where magic could not follow, had written to her in Vessaline with the certainty of a woman reading tomorrow's weather.

Unless the valley's silence worked both ways. Unless the seal that kept magic out also kept watch—that the Fairweather elders had ways of knowing things that no courier carried. Or unless someone else had written. Unless the letter was bait.

Paranoia, Noor discovered, was not a sudden thing. It was a slow flood. It came up ankle-height at first, easy to dismiss, easy to laugh at—*of course Grandmother knows, she's Grandmother*—and then it rose to the knee, to the waist, and one morning you realized you were swimming and had been for some time, and you could no longer remember what solid ground felt like under your feet.

She wrote back. She had no address to send to, so she did what the letter implied she should: she left her reply tucked in the same doorframe, weighted with a pebble, feeling foolish the entire time. *I can't come home. I'm sorry. There are things you haven't told me and I deserve to know them.* It sounded childish written down. She almost went back out to retrieve it. She didn't.

* * *

The second letter came five days after the first, and this one was different.

Different in the hand—shakier, the upright script listing slightly to starboard, as though written by candlelight or by a hand that hurt. Different in length—shorter, stripped of everything except urgency. And different, most of all, in what it did not say.

*Noor. The rider came asking questions. I told him nothing, but he had your description, child, and it was accurate. Not the description you left with—accurate. Someone is describing you who has seen you recently. Trust no one who offers help freely. Trust least of all the ones who smile. I am sending this by a man I would not have trusted a year ago, which tells you what my choices have become. If the third letter reaches you, stop reading and start running.*

Noor sat down on the edge of the cot, the paper trembling in her grip, and read the middle lines again. *He had your description, and it was accurate. Someone is describing you who has seen you recently.*

Kesh? The thought arrived unbidden and ugly, and she hated herself for it even as it took root. Kesh, who had debts. Kesh, whose creditors had fingers everywhere. Kesh, who smiled more than anyone she'd ever met, and whom her grandmother had just instructed her to trust least of all.

No. No, that was the flood talking. Kesh had bled for her on the coach road. Kesh had stood between her and Thornbound steel with nothing but a wooden practice sword and a mouth full of insults. Whatever Kesh was—broker, liar, performer—he had chosen, somewhere on that road, and people didn't bleed for a mark.

But someone had described her. Recently. Accurately. The innkeeper at the Wayfarer's Rest, maybe, with her sharp eyes and her ledger of names. The magistrate's clerk who had squinted too long at her forged papers. Maren, who had traced the map and warned her so kindly and charged them triple what the work was worth. Any of them. All of them. The city was full of eyes, and every one of them, apparently, had a price.

"What is it?" Kesh was watching her from across the room, and she realized she'd been sitting motionless for a long time.

"Nothing. Grandmother."

"Liar." He said it gently, which was somehow worse. He crossed the room and held out his hand, and after a moment—because she was tired, because the flood was at her chest now and she was tired of treading water alone—she gave him the letter. He read it twice. His face did something complicated. "Your grandmother," he said slowly, "sounds like a woman who has received a visit from the Ember Court and talked her way through it."

"She's seventy-three years old."

"Corvane's men don't care if you're seven or seventy. They care what you know." He handed the letter back. "The question is what they'll do when talking stops being enough."

* * *

The third letter never came.

What came instead, nine days after the second, was a boy of perhaps eleven, breathless, barefoot, working the taverns along Lantern Row for coppers. He shoved a scrap of paper into Noor's hand outside the Drowned Bell—an old woman paid him, he said, gray-haired, traveling, didn't give a name—and was gone before Noor had unfolded it.

It was not a letter. It was a single line, torn from the edge of a larger page, the handwriting barely legible, pressed so hard the nib had nearly gone through:

*THEY HAVE FOUND THE HOLLOW. RUN, CHILD. DO NOT COME HOME. WHATEVER YOU DO —*

The rest was torn away.

Noor stood in the street with the scrap shaking in her fingers while the market roared around her—fish-sellers crying their prices, cart wheels grinding cobblestone, somewhere a child laughing, the whole indifferent machinery of a city that did not know or care that a valley was burning or bleeding or bound, that orchards she had tended since she could walk were now a place on someone's map with a circle drawn around it.

They had found the Hollow. Her grandmother's last words were torn off mid-sentence, and Noor would never know whether the messenger had lost the rest, or whether Sable had run out of time to write it, or whether the sentence had ended the way sentences end when soldiers kick in a door.

She became aware, distantly, that Kesh was saying her name. That he had taken her elbow. That the market noise had gone strange and underwater.

"Noor. Look at me. What is it?"

She looked at him—at the face she had decided, days ago, to trust—and heard her grandmother's voice: *trust least of all the ones who smile.* And beneath that, colder, the thought she had been outrunning since the night Maren kept the map, the thought that had finally caught her at last:

Everyone who knew the secret was a door, and doors could be opened from either side. The courier had died carrying it. Maren had copied it. Her grandmother had bled her last strength warning about it. And now the Hollow itself—

"We have to move," Noor heard herself say. Her voice sounded calm, which frightened her more than screaming would have. "Tonight. Before the third letter finds us."

"There is no third letter, sweetheart. That was—"

"That was the *last* letter, Kesh." She folded the scrap into her coat, over her heart, where the map's ghost-script lived, where everything that mattered seemed to end up. "There's no one left to write them."

Behind them, unnoticed by either of them, a man in a gray traveling cloak set down his cup in the tea house window, dropped coins on the table, and slipped out into the crowd—walking, unhurried, in the same direction they were going.
