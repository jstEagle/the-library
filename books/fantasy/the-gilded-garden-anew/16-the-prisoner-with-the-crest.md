## 16. The Prisoner with the Crest

The dungeon beneath the Magistrate's Hall smelled of wet stone and old iron, and Noor hated how familiar it had become.

Three nights she had watched it now—three nights of Kesh's grumbling and her own stubborn silence, three nights of counting guards by torchlight from the shadowed mouth of a drainage culvert that emptied into the Vessaline canals like a wound that never quite closed. Three nights of telling herself she would let it go.

She had not let it go.

It was the crest that kept her awake. She'd seen it only for a heartbeat, through the grate in the transport wagon as the prisoner was dragged down into the dark—a small thing, a silver pin on a ragged collar, half-hidden by filth. A tree with thirteen branches, its roots drawn as a circle, biting its own tail.

Noor had grown up tracing that shape. It was carved into the lintel of every house in the Hollow. Her grandmother wore it on a cord around her neck, tucked beneath her shawl where no one could see. It was the mark of the Fairweather guild, and there were perhaps nine people left alive who bore it, and all of them were supposed to be sleeping in the valley that forgot.

*Roots that bite their tail.* The guild motto, though her grandmother had only ever said it once, and softly, and would not explain it. *We keep what we keep because we must.*

A man wearing that crest, chained in an Ember Court dungeon, twenty years after the last Fairweather was supposed to have died or scattered.

Kesh had told her to forget it. He'd been gentle about it, which was worse than if he'd laughed. "Valley girl," he'd said, turning his cup in his fingers at the Wayfarer's Rest, "prisons are full of sad men with interesting jewelry. Half of them stole it off a corpse. You go poking down there, you'll come out in chains yourself, or not at all." Then he'd looked at her too long, the way he did when he was deciding whether to lie to her, and added: "And I'd rather you didn't."

*I'd rather you didn't.* Not *don't*. Kesh never gave orders; he gave openings and pretended they weren't invitations.

So here she was, past midnight, standing hip-deep in canal water at the mouth of the culvert, listening to the drip of the city's refuse and telling herself one more time that this was madness.

Then she went in anyway.

* * *

The culvert was older than the city above it. That was the thing about Vessaline that no one said aloud—the whole glittering place was built on bones, on the ruins of the war everyone profited from remembering and no one remembered correctly. The tunnel walls were fitted stone from some earlier age, blocks the size of coffins, and the mortar between them had crumbled to grit. Noor moved through the dark with her hand on the wall and her breath held, and the darkness pressed back against her palms like something alive.

She had planned this the way she planned everything now: obsessively, fearfully, with lists recited under her breath until they wore grooves in her mind. The guard rotation changed at the third bell. Two men on the upper gallery, one on the stair, one dozing at the desk by the gate—and the desk man was always the same one, a heavy-jawed fellow who drank from a flask he thought no one saw. The cell block proper was behind an iron door locked with a key that hung from the desk man's belt.

She had no key. She had no plan for the key, except that Kesh had taught her locks were liars—"every lock is just a door apologizing"—and had shown her, laughing, how a stiff wire and patience could make most of them take it back. She had practiced on the shutters of their rented room until her fingers bled. She was still terrible at it. But the gate lock was old, and old things in Vessaline were tired things, and tired things gave up.

The hard part came first. The culvert ended at a rusted grate set low in the foundation wall, and beyond it lay the guardroom's coal cellar, and beyond that, the guardroom itself, lit and occupied.

Noor crouched in the water and watched through the grate for what felt like an hour. The desk man drank. The stair guard paced. Somewhere above, the third bell rang, muffled and slow, and the rotation began—the shuffle of boots, the exchange of bored murmurs, the stair guard descending to relieve the gallery, the gallery men climbing up into the cold.

For ninety heartbeats, the guardroom held only the desk man, and the desk man was drunk.

Noor pulled herself through the grate. The rust bit her palms; she barely felt it. She crossed the coal cellar on catwalks of stacked crates, black dust rising around her ankles, and came up through the cellar hatch into the corner of the guardroom where the shadows pooled thickest, directly behind the desk man's chair.

He was snoring. Actually snoring, chin on chest, the flask tipped sideways in his loose grip. The key ring hung from his belt in easy reach, glinting.

*This is the part where you die,* said a calm voice in her head that sounded unpleasantly like her grandmother. *This is the part where you reach for the keys and he wakes and shouts and you hang, and the Hollow never knows what happened to you.*

Noor thought of the crest. Roots biting their tail. Nine people in the world, and one of them in chains below her feet.

She took the keys. The ring was cold and heavier than she expected, and it clinked—one soft, catastrophic note—and the man stirred, muttered something about his mother, and slept on.

Noor stood very still for a long moment, shaking so hard her teeth wanted to chatter. Then she made herself walk, quiet as a debt collector, to the iron door at the back of the room, and knelt before the lock with her wire.

It took four tries. On the fourth, something inside the mechanism surrendered with a click she felt in her spine rather than heard, and the door swung inward on hinges that had been oiled recently enough to be silent, and the dark of the cell block breathed out at her, cold and mineral and waiting.

* * *

The cells ran in two rows down a long gallery, torches burning at intervals, each flame guttering in air that had nowhere to go. Noor moved along the near row, checking each door, reading the occupants in glimpses through the barred windows: a thief asleep in straw; a thin man muttering numbers to himself; an empty cell scrawled floor to ceiling with tally marks, thousands of them, a life counted out in scratches.

Her heart was sinking with every door. Of course. Of course it was nothing. A stolen pin, a dead man's silver, some smuggler wearing a Fairweather trinket bought off a cart in the Brass Bells market where her family's history was sold by the ounce—

The last cell on the far row was different.

It was cleaner, for one thing. And the man inside was sitting up, cross-legged on the cot, facing the door, as if he had been waiting. He was gaunt in the way of people who had been thin for years rather than weeks, with grey shot through a beard that had once been carefully kept, and his hands—his hands were folded in his lap like a scholar's, like a craftsman's, calloused across the palm in a pattern Noor knew intimately, because she saw the same pattern every day in her own reflection when she worked the orchard rows.

Pruning hands. Grafting hands. Guild hands.

But it was the eyes that stopped her breath. They were pale hazel, deep-set, lined with exhaustion—and they were her grandmother's eyes. Not similar. *The same*, set in a face twenty years younger than Sable's, weathered differently but built from the same blueprint, and Noor felt the recognition land in her body before her mind allowed it, a sick lurch, a door swinging open onto a room she had spent her whole life not looking at.

The man looked at her for a long moment. Then he smiled, and it was a terrible smile, worn and wry and unbearably sad.

"You have Sable's way of walking," he said. His voice was rough from disuse, gravel over silk. "Quiet on the outside edges, heavy in the middle. Like someone carrying something they don't want dropped." He unfolded himself from the cot and stood, and even chained, even starved, he stood the way guild members stood—balanced, rooted, unhurried. "Hello, Noor."

Her name. Her name in a stranger's mouth, in a dungeon, spoken without question.

"No," she whispered. The word came out wrong, too loud, cracking. She pressed her back against the opposite wall of the gallery, putting iron bars between them, as if bars could protect her from whatever this was. "You don't—you don't know my name."

"I've known your name since before you had it." He came closer to the bars, slowly, showing her his shackled hands, and in the torchlight the crest on his collar caught the flame—thirteen branches, roots biting their tail. "I argued for *Wren*. Your grandmother wouldn't hear of it. Said the valley needed a name that meant light, since it refused to hold any." His smile flickered and died. "Sable Fairweather is many things, but she has never once been wrong about what she wants. Remember that. It may save your life."

"Who are you?" The words scraped out of her throat. She already knew. Some part of her had known since the wagon, since the crest, since the moment she'd seen those hazel eyes and felt the ground tilt. Knowing it and saying it were different countries, and she could not make herself cross.

"You know who I am." He said it gently, which somehow made it crueler. "You've known since you picked that lock. You're not here for information, Noor. You're here because you couldn't sleep unless you looked. So look."

"Aldric." The name fell out of her like a stone through ice. "Aldric Fairweather. My uncle. You're dead. You've been dead for twenty years. Grandmother said—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, hating how young her voice sounded. "Grandmother said the roads took him. That the Court burned him with the others in the purges. There's a marker. In the orchard. I used to leave flowers on it when I was small."

"I know." Something moved behind his eyes—pain, or guilt, or both wearing each other's clothes. "I've wondered, sometimes, whether she lets the children tend my marker deliberately. Whether it's penance or instruction. With Sable it's usually both."

"The purges." Noor seized the story like a rope thrown across a chasm. "The Court destroyed the guild. That's why we hid. That's why the valley—"

"The Court didn't destroy the guild." Aldric's voice went flat and careful, the tone of a man handling something sharp. "Noor. Listen to me. The Court didn't destroy the guild because the Court couldn't. Do you understand what our family was? What we *are*? Wardens of the seal. Keepers of the thirteenth garden. The Ember Court has spent two hundred years trying to find that gate, and every time they got close, a Fairweather moved it, buried it deeper, blinded their scryers, turned their maps to ash in their archives. We were the one lock they could not pick." He gripped the bars, and the chains sang softly. "They didn't kill us. They couldn't. So why do you think there are nine of us left, hiding in a hole in the world?"

The torches guttered. Somewhere far above, the city murmured in its sleep.

"Because of the seal," Noor said slowly. "Because the valley—"

"Because of Sable." Aldric's knuckles whitened on the bar. "Twenty-two years ago, the guild council voted to end it. To unseal the garden and disperse the power into the land, the way it flowed before anyone thought to own it. It was close—eleven votes to nine. Your grandmother led the eleven. She said dispersal was surrender. She said whoever held the seal held peace itself." His laugh was short and bitter and had no humor anywhere in it. "She was right, in a way. Whoever holds the seal holds everything. She just never asked whether anyone should."

"That's not true." Noor heard her own voice rise, and forced it down. "Grandmother isn't—she raised me. She's given everything for the valley. For *me*. She burns herself hollow keeping us safe, and you—" the anger found its footing now, and she leaned into it, grateful for anything solid, "you ran. You ran and let her carry it alone, and then you show up in a cage wearing her eyes and tell me she's the villain of your story? What did you do, Aldric? Why are you *here*?"

"Why do you think?" He held her gaze, and did not flinch, and that was the worst part—that he didn't flinch. "I came for the map. Before the courier ever reached the treeline. I'd spent nineteen years tracking it, buying informants, bleeding my life into the search, because I knew what would happen the moment it surfaced. Both courts would come. The Thornbound would come. And somewhere in the middle of all that hunger, a girl would be standing in an orchard holding it." His voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "I was three days too slow. Three days. If I'd reached the boundary stones before that courier died—"

"You'd have taken it from him." The realization arrived cold. "You'd have done exactly what the Court wants to do. Exactly what the Thornbound want. You'd have killed for it."

"I'd have *hidden* it." Fiercely now, leaning against the bars. "There's a difference. I'd have taken it somewhere no faction could follow and buried it so deep that no one—not Corvane, not the rebels, not Sable—could ever dig it up again. Because I am the only person alive who understands what happens if that gate opens under the wrong moon with the wrong blood in front of it." He exhaled, long and controlled, and when he spoke again the fury had drained out of him, leaving something rawer underneath. "I'm not here to steal from you, Noor. I'm here because you're carrying the single most dangerous object in the kingdom, and you're carrying it badly, and every hunter from here to the coast is going to smell it on you soon. I know how the wards read bloodline. I know what you are. And I am asking you—as the last two Fairweathers outside that valley—to get me out of this cage so I can protect you."

"Protect me." She tasted the words. "From whom?"

"Everyone." He said it simply. "Including me, if it comes to that. Including your grandmother, if it comes to *that*." He paused, and his eyes—Sable's eyes, her eyes—searched her face with an intimacy that made her skin crawl, seeing in her features the ghosts of people she'd never met. "You want to know the truth about your family, child? Here it is. We are not heroes. We are not villains. We are a lock that learned to love itself, and every generation we choose the same thing: the seal over the world, the secret over the sharing, the garden over the gardener. Sable chose it. I chose it, for nineteen years, and it cost me everything. Now it's your turn to choose." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "And I am begging you to choose differently than either of us did."

Noor's hands were trembling. She shoved them into Kesh's borrowed cloak and made herself breathe—in through the nose, out slow, the way she calmed the orchard horses.

"If I believed you," she said at last, "which I don't—not yet, not fully—if I believed even half of it, then getting you out means crossing the Court, and Kesh, and—" She shook her head. "Why should I trust you? Give me one reason that isn't your eyes."

Aldric was quiet for a moment. When he answered, he didn't raise his voice at all, and somehow that was louder than shouting.

"Because I could have lied to you," he said. "Nineteen years I've rehearsed this meeting, and in every version, I lied. Told you Sable murdered the council. Told you the Court held me unjustly. Told you whatever would send you running to break these bars fastest." He spread his chained hands. "Instead I told you the ugliest version I know—including the parts where I'm the monster. That's the only reason I have, Noor. It's the only reason I've got left."

Footsteps, above. The fourth bell, distant. The night was spending itself while she stood in a dungeon arguing with a ghost.

"I have to go," she whispered. "If they find the keys missing—"

"They won't, not till dawn shift." He stepped back from the bars, and the torchlight drew his face into planes and hollows, a portrait of everything the guild had lost. "Think about what I said. About what Sable chooses, every time. And when you're ready—when you've checked my story against hers and found where they don't fit—come back. Ask the archivist woman for the route word 'latchkey.' Maren owes me, and Maren owes you more than she knows." A ghost of the wry smile returned. "Tell her Aldric says the ledger's still open."

"How do you know about Maren? How do you know about—" But even as she said it, she understood, and the understanding chilled her. Nineteen years. Nineteen years of watching, of networks, of knowing. He had known about them before they'd ever reached the city. Perhaps before they'd ever left the road. "You've been following us. Since the ambush. Since the valley."

"Since before the valley," Aldric said softly. "Since the night you were born, and I stood in the snow outside a house I wasn't welcome in, and listened to you cry." He sat back down on the cot, folded his craftsman's hands, and became again a prisoner, still and patient as stone. "Go home, Noor. Whatever home is now. And think about whose story you're living in—because right now, you're living in hers. And she wrote it in blood."

Noor fled.

She fled up the gallery, through the iron door, past the snoring desk man whose keys she returned with hands that would not stop shaking, down through the coal cellar and the grate and the freezing black water of the culvert, and she did not stop moving until she was three districts away, huddled in the doorway of a shuttered dye-house with the canal reek in her nostrils and dawn still hours off.

*Aldric Fairweather.* Alive. Chained. Wearing her grandmother's eyes and her family's crest and a story that fit the map's ghost-ink better than any story Sable had ever told her.

*Family,* she thought, staring at the dirty gold line where the sky would eventually lighten, *is both the wound and the weapon. Grandmother taught me that without ever saying it. I just never understood which one we were using on each other.*

Somewhere across the city, a bell tolled the hour, and Noor realized she already knew what she would do next, and that she was terrified of it, and that neither feeling would change anything at all.

She was going to test her grandmother's story against a dead man's testimony.

And one of them was going to break.
