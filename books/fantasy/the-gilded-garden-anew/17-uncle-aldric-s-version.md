## 17. Uncle Aldric's Version

The cell smelled of wet stone and old iron, and Aldric Fairweather had learned to measure time by the drip of water somewhere behind the eastern wall—one drop for every breath he did not take.

Noor sat across from him on the far side of the bars, her knees drawn up, her cropped hair still rough at the edges where she'd cut it herself with a dull knife. She had not spoken for a long while. He could see the war in her face, the way her grandmother's stories and his presence were grinding against each other like millstones.

"You're supposed to be dead," she said finally. "Twenty years dead. Grandmother buried you. There's a stone."

"There's a stone," he agreed. "I've seen it. They spelled my name wrong."

"Then tell me how you're alive."

He leaned back against the wall. The chains on his wrists were court-issue, heavy enough to bruise, but he had spent two decades learning to be comfortable inside things that held him. "It's a long story, Noor. Longer than you have, maybe. But I think you already know it isn't the story you were raised on. Otherwise you wouldn't be sitting here."

"Tell me anyway."

So he began where all Fairweather stories began: with the garden.

* * *

"You know the version they teach children," he said. "The guild as gardeners. Keepers. Humble stewards who sealed away a dangerous power to protect the world." He smiled, and there was no warmth in it. "My mother told me that story too. Then one night when I was sixteen, I found her ledger—the real one, not the one the guild kept for show—and I learned that we were never gardeners at all. We were jailers. And the thing in the jail was never ours to keep."

Noor's jaw tightened. "Grandmother said—"

"I know what Sable said. Sable has been saying the same things for forty years because if she stops saying them, she'll have to hear them." He shifted forward, chains whispering against stone. "Let me tell you about the garden as it actually was. Before the seal. Before the schism. Before your grandmother decided that the whole kingdom should pay for her grief."

The catacombs beneath Vessaline were older than the city above them, older than the Ember Court, older than any crown that claimed them. In the years before the war, they had not been catacombs at all but open ways—roads under the earth where anyone might walk, lit by veins of pale light that ran through the stone like sap through bark. That light came from the garden. Everyone knew it. Everyone drew from it.

"The power wasn't hidden," Aldric said. "It was *shared*. That was the whole point of it. A farmer in the eastern marches could press his palm to a root-stone and coax his fields through a killing frost. A midwife could warm a failing child with a thread of it. It wasn't hoarded, it wasn't owned—it rose out of the ground like water rises out of a spring, and no one could dam a spring, so no one tried." He watched Noor's face carefully. "Does that sound like the dangerous thing your grandmother described?"

"It sounds like a story," Noor said quietly. "Grandmother said the guild sealed it because factions were fighting over it. Because people died."

"People did die," Aldric said. "But not because the power was wicked. Because people are. And here is the part your grandmother will never say aloud, though she has confessed it to every candle in the Hollow by now, I'd wager: the fighting wasn't over whether to share the garden. It was over whether to *own* it. And the guild—" he tapped his own chest with two chained fingers, "—the guild wanted to own it too."

* * *

He remembered the last council meeting before the schism. He had been twenty-two, newly sworn, standing at the back of the round chamber beneath the Hollow with rain coming down through the fissures overhead. His mother, Maren-the-elder—not the archivist, the other Maren, the one history forgot—had laid out three paths on the table like a hand of cards.

The first path was openness. Let the garden breathe. Widen the ways, teach the root-craft freely, trust the world with what the world had always had.

The second path was stewardship. Close the outer roads, keep the inner ones, let the guild decide who was worthy. Orderly. Comfortable. The path most of the elders favored.

The third path was the seal. Total enclosure. The garden locked behind wards keyed to Fairweather blood alone, its power drawn off in careful sips by the guild and no one else, forever.

"And do you know who argued loudest for the third?" Aldric asked. He didn't wait for an answer. "Your grandmother. Sable was thirty then, brilliant, furious—her sister had just died in the border skirmishes, caught between Thornbound irregulars and Court levies both trying to seize the northern root-roads. Sable stood up in front of the whole council with her sister's blood still under her fingernails, metaphorically speaking, and she said: *As long as this power sits in the open, someone will always be marching toward it. Seal it, and the marching stops.*"

"She was grieving," Noor said.

"She was grieving," Aldric agreed. "And she was wrong, and everyone knew she was wrong, and they voted for it anyway because grief is louder than wisdom and fear is louder than both. My mother voted against it. So did six others. They were outvoted forty-one to seven." His voice went flat. "And then, because the seal required blood to key it—Fairweather blood, willingly given, from every living member of the guild—those seven refused. Do you understand what that means, Noor? The seal didn't just close the garden. It closed the guild. We were locked out of our own inheritance by our own family, and the price of refusal was exile."

"That's not—" Noor stopped. Started again. "The records say the dissenters left voluntarily. That they couldn't accept the burden."

"The records were written by the winners." He laughed softly, without humor. "Voluntarily. Yes. My mother walked out of the Hollow with nothing but her name and her daughter, and the ward-stones burned her sigil off the guild rolls as she passed them. You could feel it from the treeline—a sound like a bell cracking. Forty-one of us stayed inside the seal. Seven of us were cast outside it. And the world outside had just lost the only spring it had ever known."

* * *

"What happened after?" Noor asked. Her voice had changed. Less challenge in it now. More of something he recognized from mirrors: the sound of a person watching their foundations move.

"What happened after is what always happens after a theft," Aldric said. "The victims got angry. The Ember Court had spent generations building legitimacy on access to the garden's power—their healing houses, their weather-craft, their blessed coronations. All of it drew from the root-ways. When the seal closed, the Court's magic didn't vanish, but it thinned, and thinning power makes frightened kings. The Thornbound weren't rebels yet—they were villages, guilds, free-towns, people whose whole lives had been braided into those shared roots. When the roots went dark, they starved. Not immediately. Slowly. Crop by crop, winter by winter." He looked at her steadily. "You've seen Ilsa's maps, haven't you? Kesh told me you sheltered with them. The villages marked in black. Ask yourself why a rebellion would bother keeping a list of the dead."

"They say the Court starved them."

"The Court finished the job," Aldric said. "But the seal started it. Every faction in this war is fighting over the same wound, Noor, and your grandmother made the wound. Not out of malice—I want to be fair to her, because she'll be fair to you someday, or she won't deserve you—but out of the oldest sin there is. She loved something small more than something large. She loved her grief, and her guild, and her certainty, more than the thousand thousand strangers whose lives depended on an open spring."

"You talk about her like you hate her."

"No." He said it simply, and meant it. "I talk about her like a man who loved her once. She was my sister, Noor. Did you know that? Not grandmother-somehow, not cousin-of-a-cousin. My sister. I held you at your naming day. You screamed the whole time and Sable said you'd inherited the family lungs." For a moment the flatness broke and something older moved behind his eyes. "She chose the seal over me. Or rather, she chose the seal, and I was what choosing cost. I've had twenty years to decide which of those sentences is true. I still can't."

* * *

"Why did you flee?" Noor asked. "If you stayed in the Hollow until twenty years ago—why leave? Why try to steal the map?"

"Because I finally read the ledger," Aldric said. "The real one. The one my mother kept until she died, and which Sable recovered—or confiscated; the verb depends on which side of the wall you stood on—and hid in the guild archive behind the false shelf in the drying room. Third board from the top. Has she moved it?"

Noor said nothing, but her silence was answer enough, and he nodded slowly.

"Sable kept everything. That's her curse—she can't destroy evidence, only bury it. And the ledger tells you what forty years inside the seal does to a caged thing." He drew a slow breath. "A spring that's dammed doesn't stay a spring. It goes bad. Stagnant. The garden's power was alive, Noor—alive the way a forest is alive, the way a river is alive—and we walled it up in the dark and took sips from it through the wards and called ourselves protectors. The ledger's last entries are in Sable's hand. Measurements. The light-veins in the deep ways dimming year over year. The root-craft souring. Guild members who tended the inner gates coming back changed—gray-eyed, quiet, wrong. And one entry, the last one, dated the winter before I left. Four words." His voice dropped. "*It dreams of doors.*"

The drip behind the wall counted out several seconds.

"So I went to Sable," Aldric continued, "and I told her the seal was killing the very thing it was built to protect, and that a poisoned spring bursting its dam would be worse than any war. And you know what she said?" He shook his head. "She said, *Then we hold it together anyway. Better a dying garden than a burning world.* Twenty years of evidence and she still reached for the same answer she'd reached for at thirty. Some people build a wall around their heart so early that they mistake it for a ribcage."

"So you stole the map."

"I tried to steal the map," he corrected. "There's a difference, and the difference is currently wearing court irons. The map isn't a record of where the garden is—that much Sable will have told you, if she told you anything. It's a key. Blood-keyed, generationally keyed, written partly in ink and partly in intention. Whoever holds it doesn't find the gate. The gate finds *them*." He flexed his chained hands. "I failed. Corvane's people took me at the boundary stones, and Sable let them believe I was dead rather than spend guild lives on a rescue, and I have spent twenty years in a series of cells thinking about the fact that my sister calculated my life against the seal and the seal won."

Noor had gone very still. "She told me you died protecting the valley."

"She would." Gently, now. He could see the exact shape of the blow landing, having taken it himself long ago. "And understand—I'm not telling you this to make you hate her. Hate is just another kind of certainty, and certainty is what built that seal in the first place. I'm telling you because you're carrying a map right now that four factions would burn kingdoms to hold, and you deserve to know that every story you've been told about it was written by someone with something to lose. Mine included. Remember that. I want the garden opened. Question everything I've said. But question her ledger too. Question the vigil stories. Question the stone with my name on it, misspelled, in a graveyard full of people who died of *quiet* while the world starved outside the walls."

* * *

Noor stood. She paced the length of the cell corridor twice, chains of her own kind clinking faintly—the stolen cloak, the borrowed boots, the new name she hadn't yet told him. When she turned back, her face had settled into something he almost recognized: the Fairweather stubbornness, the family lungs, screaming at its own naming.

"If even half of what you've said is true," she said, "then the seal has to come down. But I've met the people who want it down, Uncle. The Court wants a crown's worth of power. The Thornbound want a weapon with a righteous flag on it. And you—" she pressed her hand flat against the bars, not quite touching him, "—you want *in*. You've wanted in for twenty years. Why should I believe you want anything but the door open for yourself?"

"Because I'm in a cell," Aldric said, "telling you the truth for free."

"Truth for free." She almost laughed. "Kesh says nothing is free. He says everything has a ledger."

"Kesh is wise. It's his worst quality." Aldric rose, joints protesting, and met her eyes through the iron. "Ask me for my price, then. Go on. I'll give it honestly."

"What's your price?"

"To stand at the gate when it opens," he said. "Not to claim it. Not to bind it. Just—to see it. To see the thing we were all born to tend, once, before it goes, whatever *goes* means. I've paid twenty years for that view, Noor. It's the only thing I've ever wanted that I couldn't steal."

She studied him for a long moment, and he let her, because he had learned that the truth survives inspection better than lies do—most of the time, in most of the light.

Then footsteps sounded in the stairwell above, unhurried and official, and Noor's face went white. "That's the changing of the watch. I'm not supposed to be here. If Corvane learns I came—"

"Go," Aldric said quickly. "But listen to me first. One thing. The map you carry—look at it tonight, by firelight, and look *underneath* the ink. Not at the lines. Under them." The footsteps were closer now, echoing off wet stone. "There's writing there that no courier put there. Writing in a hand you'll know better than you think. And when you find it, ask yourself who could have written to a child who hadn't yet learned to hold a pen—and why."

"Who wrote it?" Noor whispered.

The door at the top of the stairs began to open.

"A Fairweather," Aldric said, sinking back against the wall, closing his eyes, becoming again the model prisoner. "We're the only ones who ever knew the garden well enough to leave it instructions. Now go, girl. And Noor—" his voice followed her into the dark like a hand on her shoulder, "—when you've read it, come back and tell me which of us your grandmother lied to worse. Me, or you."
