## 18. Which Truth to Carry

The letters had arrived with the dawn, and Noor had not touched them since.

They sat on the narrow table in Kesh's rented room, three of them now, each sealed with the Fairweather sigil pressed in plain wax—no gilding, no flourish, the way her grandmother did everything: deliberately, stubbornly plain. The topmost letter's seal was broken. Noor had read it twice in the grey light before Aldric came, and then she had put it down and walked to the window and watched Vessaline wake beneath its thousand brass bells until the ringing made her teeth ache.

*Trust no one who tells you the garden wants to be free,* Sable had written. *Especially not a man with my brother's face. He wanted it once. Wanting does not die in prison; it only learns patience.*

*My brother's face.* As if Aldric were an impostor wearing it. As if twenty years behind stone could be survived without becoming someone Sable would refuse to recognize.

Noor turned from the window. Across the room, Aldric sat where she had told him to sit—on the floor, back against the wall, hands visible, the posture of a man who understood exactly what he looked like to her and had decided not to argue with it. His chains were gone; Kesh had seen to that with two bribes and a forged transfer order he refused to explain. But the habit of imprisonment clung to him. He sat small. He kept his wrists loose, as though expecting weight on them at any moment.

"You've read them again," he said quietly. It wasn't a question. "I can tell by how you're standing."

"How am I standing?"

"Like someone deciding which way to run." He almost smiled. "You stand like your mother did. She used to plant her feet before she said anything difficult, as if the words needed ground to grow from."

"Don't." The word came out harder than she intended. "Don't do that. Don't hand me pieces of people I never got to have and call it proof of anything. You know what my grandmother says about you. You know what I was raised on. A man who tried to steal the map. A man whose greed broke the guild."

"And what does she say happened to the map?" Aldric asked. "After I supposedly stole it? Where did I keep it, all those years? Under my tongue in the dungeon?"

"I don't know. That's the point. I don't *know* anything—"

"No," he said. "You don't. And neither does she. That's the whole tragedy of the Fairweathers, Noor. We stopped knowing things a long time ago and started believing them instead."

She hated him a little for saying it, because it was true, and because truth spoken gently is somehow worse than truth shouted.

* * *

She took out the map.

It had traveled folded into eighths for so long that the creases had become part of its body, soft as old scars. She smoothed it flat on the table between the letters, weighting the corners with a candlestick, a cup, Kesh's spare knife. The lamplight caught the ink—that strange ink that seemed to sit *above* the parchment rather than on it, drawn by some hand working in more than two dimensions.

"Tell me what you see," Aldric said.

"A map."

"Look again. Look at the river."

She looked. The river ran down the left edge of the map, rendered in a line so fine it was nearly a hair, branching and branching into tributaries like the veins of a leaf. She had traced it a hundred times by now. She knew every bend of it.

"It bends west below the city," she said slowly. "But Maren said the Vessa runs east beneath the catacombs. She said—" Noor stopped. Her finger hovered over the fine line. "She said the river under Vessaline hadn't always run east. That it had been diverted during the war. Dammed and turned, when they flooded the lower tunnels."

"By whom?"

"The Court. To drown the rebel holds." She said it flatly, but the flatness cost her something. She had learned the war's shape from Ilsa's stories, from villages shown to her in charcoal sketches and survivor testimony. The Court had drowned its own people rather than yield ground.

"So," Aldric said. "Your map shows the river as it was. Before the diversion. Which means whoever drew it drew it *before the war ended*. Not after. Not twenty years ago, when I supposedly stole it. This map is old, Noor. Older than me. Older than Sable's story about me."

"That doesn't prove anything. Maps get copied. Someone could have copied an old one—"

"Who?" He leaned forward, and for a moment the dungeon-smallness fell away and she saw the man he might have been: sharp-eyed, certain, a scholar's fire banked but not out. "Copies carry their copiers' mistakes. Look at the marginalia. Read the notes in the corner."

She bent over the corner she had never quite been able to decipher—a cluster of tiny glyphs crowded against the border like birds on a wire. She had assumed they were archaic script. Maren had squinted at them and shrugged.

Now, in the better light, with Aldric watching, she saw what she had refused to see. The glyphs weren't writing at all. They were a *key*—a sequence of turns and distances, and beside each turn, a small notation of what the traveler should expect. *Flooded here. Watch the third arch. The water remembers.*

"The water remembers," Noor whispered. "That's—those are warnings. Someone wrote warnings for a specific person walking this route."

"For a Fairweather," Aldric said. "Read the third warning."

"*The gate knows the blood it was keyed to. Do not fear it. Fear what waits above it.*" Her mouth had gone dry. "Above the gate is—the city. The Court."

"Fear what waits above it," Aldric repeated softly. "Sable's letters say the garden is a weapon we buried. Mine says it's a wound we dressed. But look at the handwriting on those warnings—not the ink, the *hand*. Whose hand does it match?"

And there it was. The thing she had found weeks ago by firelight and had told herself meant nothing, or meant everything, or meant only coincidence: the faint ghosting beneath the main ink, a second message written first and covered over. She had matched that ghost-hand once, trembling, against a page of her own childhood copybook—her letters, her childish loops, reproduced in adult confidence years before she'd been born.

The ghost-hand and the warning-hand were the same.

Someone had written the map *for* her. Someone who knew her before she could write.

"Sable says you tried to steal the map," Noor said. Her voice sounded far away to herself. "If that's true—if you came for it—why would it carry messages in a hand that matches mine? Why would the warnings be for *me*, written before I existed?"

"Because the map wasn't stolen," Aldric said. "It was *sent*. By the last of the garden's original wardens, before the seal closed for good. Sent forward through time along the bloodline—to the one descendant who would be able to open what needed opening. The guild didn't lose the map, Noor. The guild was *given* it, generation after generation, and generation after generation the elders chose to bury it instead of follow it. Your grandmother isn't lying to you, exactly. She's repeating what she was told. That's crueler, in some ways. She has spent her whole life defending a decision she didn't make and can't take back, because admitting it was wrong would mean her whole life was spent wrong."

Noor stepped back from the table as though it had grown hot.

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't. To any of us."

* * *

She walked. That was what she did now when she couldn't think—she had learned it on the road, from Kesh, who claimed that feet unstuck thoughts. Down the stairs, out into the street, through the evening crowds of the Brass Quarter where the bell-towers clashed the hour and vendors called the day's last prices and nobody looked twice at a girl in a hooded cloak, because Vessaline was full of girls in hooded cloaks and most of them were running from something too.

Sable's voice in her head: *The garden is power caged for a reason. Some doors are locked because of what's behind them.*

Aldric's voice, quieter, more terrible: *Some doors are locked because of what locking them did.*

Both of them loved her. That was the trap of it. Sable had spent nineteen years making herself small so Noor could stay safe, and Aldric had spent twenty years in a cell refusing to give up her name, and now they stood on either side of her like two banks of a river, and the water between them was the truth, and she could not drink from both.

Kesh found her at the fountain by the tanners' gate, sitting on the rim with her boots in the water like a child, which she supposed she still was in ways that mattered.

"Well," he said, dropping beside her. "You have the face."

"What face?"

"The face you make when two people you love are both holding knives." He studied her sidelong. "Which one is it tonight? Grandmother or uncle?"

"Both. Neither. Kesh—" She pulled her feet from the water. "What if everything the Hollow taught me is wrong? Not lied-about, not wicked—just *wrong*. Built on a mistake so old nobody alive remembers making it. What if the seal didn't save us? What if it just... delayed us. And the delay cost more than the thing ever would have."

Kesh was quiet a moment. He was good at quiet, when he chose it—one of the first things she'd trusted about him.

"Then somebody has to be the one who finds out," he said finally. "And it can't be your grandmother, because she'll defend it. Can't be your uncle, because he wants it. Can't be the Court, can't be the rebels. So it's you, or it's nobody." He stood, offered his hand. "For what it's worth, valley girl—I've watched you check every story anyone's told you since the day I met you. Including mine. Twice. Whatever you decide, it won't be because someone talked you into it. That's rarer than magic, where I come from."

She took his hand and let him pull her up.

* * *

The room was dim when she returned. Aldric had not moved from his place against the wall, and the map lay where she'd left it, corners still weighted, patient as a held breath.

She sat down across from him on the floor, mirroring him, knees drawn up. For a long moment neither spoke. Somewhere above them a bell rang the eleventh hour, and the sound sank down through the floors of the old building like water through sand.

"If I help you," she said, "and you're lying—if this is all a longer, cleverer version of the theft they accused you of—"

"Then you'll have helped a liar reach the thing he wants," Aldric said simply. "And you'll stop me. Or fail to. I won't pretend there's no risk in trusting me. There's enormous risk. I'm asking you to take it anyway, knowing that I might be exactly what your grandmother says."

"Why would you tell me that? If you were what she says, wouldn't you swear you weren't?"

"Yes. And you'd believe me less for it." He smiled, tired and crooked. "I'm not asking for faith, Noor. I'm asking for a test. Come with me to the gate. Watch what happens when my blood touches the wards. If I'm a thief, the wards will know it—they were built by wardens who thought of everything. If I'm telling the truth, they'll pass me through and you'll have your answer written in light older than both our stories. Either way, you learn something true. That's all I've ever wanted. The truth, and then whatever follows from it."

"And Sable?"

He was quiet for a moment. When he answered, his voice had roughened.

"When you see her next—and you will, that woman will walk through fire to reach you, I've known her since she was smaller than you are—tell her I said the orchards were the right choice. She'll understand. Tell her the rest was wrong, but the orchards were right." He looked away, toward the shuttered window and the moonlight seeping through its slats. "We don't get to choose which parts of ourselves our families remember. Only which parts we leave them."

Noor looked at the map. At the fine-veined river, drawn before the drowning. At the warnings in a hand that predated her own and matched it perfectly. At the thirteenth turn marked in ink that seemed to glow faintly, patiently, like a lamp left burning in a window for someone expected home.

Her grandmother had given her a guild ring and a lie—or a belief, worn smooth by decades into something indistinguishable from a lie. Her uncle offered her a test and a door.

Choosing felt like betrayal. It would feel like betrayal whichever way she chose, and she understood, finally, that this too was part of her inheritance: the Fairweather gift for making children carry what the elders couldn't finish.

"All right," she said.

Aldric's breath went out of him slowly, like a man setting down a weight carried twenty years.

"We go at moonset," Noor said, before he could thank her, before gratitude could make this softer than it was. "Through the drowned tunnels. We follow the map exactly—every turn, every warning. And Kesh comes with us, and you tell him nothing you don't tell me. Those are the terms."

"Agreed."

"And Uncle—" The word tasted strange, half rust, half honey, the first time she'd used it aloud. "If the wards reject you... you walk back up those stairs and you never come near me again."

"If the wards reject me," Aldric said, "I'll deserve it."

He reached out and touched the map's edge, lightly, the way one touches a sleeping animal—and in the lamplight Noor saw the ink shift, just barely, the thirteenth turn brightening as if it had heard them. As if it had been waiting, all these years and all these miles, for the family to stop arguing and start walking.

Outside, the bells began to ring midnight, and somewhere beneath the city, past the drowned arches and the sleeping water, a gate older than grief felt its key turn over in her chest—and waited.
