## 20. Beneath the City

The entrance was a beggar's mouth in the foundation of the Tanners' Row aqueduct, half-drowned and entirely forgotten, exactly as the map promised.

Noor stood at its lip while Kesh held the lantern low, its flame bending toward the dark as though the dark were drinking it. Below, stone steps descended into black water that moved without wind—slow, patient, the motion of something breathing rather than flowing.

"You're certain this is it," she said. Not really a question. She had traced the map's lines so many times by now that she could draw them blindfolded: the crooked spine of Vessaline's old quarter, the buried river, the stair marked with a symbol she had finally understood only three nights ago—a circle with thirteen rays, one for each of the moons in the old reckoning, before the calendar was cut down to twelve by men who preferred tidy arithmetic.

"Certain enough," Kesh said. "Which in my experience means either treasure or drowning, and I've survived both." He smiled his street-corner smile, but his eyes stayed on the water. "After you, oh keeper of secrets."

She went first. Of course she did. Somewhere between the ambush on the coach road and the burned edge of the map, she had stopped being the person who waited to be led.

* * *

The water never rose above their knees, but it never left them either. It clung cold to their shins and made every step a negotiation with the unseen floor—broken tile, slick stone, things soft and nameless that shifted away from their boots. The lantern threw their shadows huge against walls that had been built by hands that believed in grandeur: vaulted ceilings furred with mineral bloom, columns carved with vines and birds, faces worn nearly smooth by centuries of seepage. Once, Noor was sure, these had been streets. A city beneath the city, drowned when the new city rose atop the ruins of the old war, layer upon layer, each generation building over what it could not bear to look at.

"It's beautiful," she said, and meant it, and hated how small her voice came out.

"Drowned things usually are," Kesh said. "From a distance."

They walked for hours, or what felt like hours; time down here was a rumor. The map guided them at each branching—left at the column with the broken bird, right where two tunnels met like cupped hands—and Noor began to notice something she couldn't yet name. The air was changing. Not warming, not cooling. *Thickening.* Each breath carried a faint sweetness, like the air inside a greenhouse in high summer, or the moment just before rain when the whole world seems to hold its breath.

And then they reached the first fissure, and she stopped pretending she didn't know why.

It split the ceiling overhead like a lightning scar, no wider than her hand, and through it came light. Not lamplight. Not the gray memory of the sun. It was silver-white and faintly green at the edges, and it fell in a single bright thread into the black water below, where it lay glowing on the surface like a fallen hairpin.

"The moon," Kesh said quietly. "But—" He looked up, though the fissure showed nothing but stone and that impossible light. "We're fifty feet under the city. There are four floors of cellars and a temple and probably a tax office between us and the sky."

Noor didn't answer. She was watching the light lie on the water, and watching the water carry it, and understanding, with a slow cold certainty, that the light was not coming *down* to them. It was coming *through*—through stone, through earth, through everything that should have stopped it—the way sound comes through a wall, muffled but unstoppable, because the wall was never meant to be the point. The fissures weren't holes. They were seams. And the whole weight of Vessaline above them was pressing down on something that glowed anyway.

"The thirteenth moon," she said. "Sable used to say the old year had a moon the calendars forgot. I thought it was a story about keeping track of harvests." Her throat tightened. "It's not a moon in the sky, is it. It's whatever's down here. It shines through everything, if you're deep enough to see it."

Kesh said nothing, which from Kesh was an act of enormous restraint.

* * *

By the third fissure, Noor had stopped being able to ignore it.

The seal was weakening around her. She could see it now—not with her eyes, or not only—but she could also see it with her eyes, which was worse. Wherever the moonlight touched her, it didn't pass over her skin the way it passed over Kesh. It gathered. It pooled in the hollows of her collarbones, ran along the lines of her palms, collected at her fingertips until they looked dipped in pale fire. And around her, in a radius of perhaps an arm's length, the water behaved strangely. It stilled. It flattened into a mirror, and in the mirror she could see not her own face but something behind it—a suggestion of green, of gold, of branches heavy with fruit that no orchard in the Hollow had borne in a hundred years.

"Noor." Kesh's voice, careful. "Your hands."

"I know."

"Do you want to talk about it?"

"No."

He nodded, and let it go, and she loved him a little for that, and hated herself a little more for needing it.

Because the truth was that she *had* talked about it—in fragments, in the safe language of the road—and every fragment had frightened her more than silence. Since the Thornbound ambush, since the burst of raw power that had come out of her without permission or shape, things had been different inside her skin. She felt fuller. That was the only word. As if all her life she had been a cup held upside down in a dry country, and someone had finally turned her over, and something was pouring in. And here, beneath the city, near the source, the pouring had become a roar she could feel in her teeth.

The seal wasn't just weakening around her. It was weakening *because* of her. She was not standing next to the key. She was not carrying the key. The wards below—she could feel them now too, vast and patient and half-asleep, strung through the stone like nerves—were responding to her footsteps the way a sleeping animal responds to its own name.

Every step she took downward, the seal frayed a little finer.

* * *

They came to a place where the drowned street ended at a wall of worked stone, and the map told them to go through it, and after an hour of searching Kesh found the seam: a door disguised as masonry, its edges so perfect that five hundred years of water had not pried them apart. In its center, worn shallow but unmistakable, was carved the crest of the Fairweather guild—an open hand cradling a blossom.

Noor put her palm against it before she decided to. The stone was warm.

Warm, in a place where the water had been trying to take the heat out of her for six hours. Warm like a living thing. And beneath her hand, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, something answered—a pulse, a pressure, a recognition that traveled up her arm and settled behind her sternum like a swallowed coal.

"It knows me," she whispered.

"Of course it does," Kesh said. "You're a Fairweather. Your family built this lock."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "Locks don't know you. Locks open for keys." She looked down at her hand, still glowing faintly where the fissure-light touched it, and heard herself say the thing she had been circling for days without landing on. "I'm not the person who has the key, Kesh. I'm not even the person who *is* the key. I think I'm the key turning."

The silence that followed had weight. Somewhere far off, water dripped into water, counting out seconds neither of them wanted.

"We can still go back," Kesh said at last. "Maren has her copy, the rebels have their rumors, the Court has its spies, and none of them have you. We walk out of here tonight, we take the coast road, we disappear. I'm very good at disappearing. It's practically my trade."

"And the Hollow?" she asked. "And Sable? And every letter she sent me warning me that the moment I stop hiding, everyone I love becomes a target?" She pressed her forehead briefly against the warm stone. "There's no walking out of this anymore. There's only choosing which way to walk through it."

"That," Kesh said softly, "is the most depressing true thing anyone's said to me all year."

Despite everything, she laughed—a short, cracked sound that echoed strangely down the drowned street, and the echo came back wrong. Came back doubled. Came back, she could have sworn, in a voice slightly deeper than her own, as if the tunnel itself were practicing her laughter, learning it, keeping it.

* * *

Beyond the false wall, the character of the dark changed.

The passages narrowed, then opened again into galleries that no flood had ever reached—the water stopped at a threshold as clean as if a glass pane divided it, and beyond the threshold the air was dry and sweet and moving, gently, in and out, like lungs. The walls here were not carved. They were *grown*—stone that had been coaxed, long ago, into shapes no chisel makes: arches like rib cages, buttresses like roots, and everywhere the recurring motif of the open hand.

And the fissures were more frequent here, and wider, and the thirteenth moon's light came through them in braids instead of threads, and in that light Noor could see what the seal was made of.

It hung in the air of the galleries like gossamer strung with dew—threads upon threads upon threads, silver-fine, woven across every passage, every arch, every crack, a web the size of a cathedral spun by hands that must have worked for years. Some strands were taut and bright. Others sagged, dulled, snapped at their anchors and drifting like cobwebs in a doorway. And wherever Noor walked, the nearest strands *leaned toward her*. Gently. Helplessly. The way grass leans toward the sun, the way iron filings lean toward a lodestone, the way, she thought with a lurch, a drowning man leans toward any hand at all.

"They're drinking from you," Kesh said. His voice had gone flat and careful, the voice he used when he was frightened and refusing to show it. "Or feeding you. I genuinely can't tell, and I hate that."

"So can I." Noor lifted her hand and watched a strand of the seal drift toward her fingers and touch them and shiver, and where it touched, the strand brightened—fed, yes, or filled, or *remembered itself*—and then, horribly, thinned. As if the contact cost it something. As if she were drawing from it without meaning to, the way she breathed.

She snatched her hand back. The strand swayed, dimmer than before.

"I'm killing it," she said. "Just by being close. Every step I take toward the gate, the seal spends itself trying to—to hold against me, or hold *me*, I don't even know which, and it's losing." Her voice climbed and she dragged it back down. "Sable spent her whole life guarding a valley with no magic in it. Her mother before her. Generations, Kesh. They poured themselves into this seal, and I'm unwinding it by *walking*."

"Hey." Kesh caught her shoulder, turned her to face him. In the braided moonlight his face was half silver, half shadow, and for once there was no performance anywhere in it. "Listen to me. Whatever you are—and I've stopped pretending I understand it—you didn't build this cage, and you didn't ask to be its lockpick. If the thing is failing, that's not your crime. That's just the hour getting late."

"And if I'm what's making it late?"

"Then we'd better hurry," he said, "and find out what it's late *for*."

She stared at him. Then, despite the coal behind her sternum, despite the leaning threads, despite the doubled echo of her own laugh waiting somewhere in the dark—despite all of it—she smiled.

* * *

The last gallery opened without warning, the way a held breath opens.

They stood at the edge of a vast round chamber, dry as bone, its ceiling lost in darkness above. Fissures radiated across it like the rays of the carved symbol on the map—thirteen of them, she counted, thirteen veins of silver-green light converging inward—and at the center of the chamber, where all the light gathered, stood a door.

Not a large door. That was the first shock of it. After miles of grandeur, of grown-stone cathedrals and drowned streets, the gate to the Sealed Garden was barely taller than a man: dark wood banded in metal gone green with age, set into a frame of living rock, utterly plain, utterly final. An ordinary garden door. The kind you'd find at the end of any walled plot in any village in the kingdom, leading to beans and bees and somebody's laundry drying on the line.

Except that the light of the thirteenth moon did not fall on it. It fell *into* it, every vein in the ceiling bending its glow toward that plain wooden face, sinking in and not coming out, so that the door seemed lit from within, seemed almost translucent, seemed—when Noor blinked—like a window onto a summer noon somewhere very far away.

And except that across its surface, in a script older than any alphabet she knew, words were beginning to surface. Rising through the grain of the wood like bruises rising through skin. Like letters written in water, appearing one by one as the water goes still.

Kesh read them aloud, haltingly, sounding them out the way he'd taught himself to sound out anything—contracts, warrants, other people's mail. "It says... 'Whose hand?'" He glanced at her. "That's all. Just that. Over and over. *Whose hand. Whose hand. Whose hand.*"

Noor stepped forward, and the whole chamber changed.

Every remaining thread of the seal in the galleries behind them leaned after her, a field of silver wheat bowing to a passing storm. The veins of moonlight brightened. The sweet moving air went still. And the words on the door surged upward all at once, dozens of them, hundreds, crowding the grain, and beneath them—beneath them, faint and fresh and unmistakable, in handwriting she knew better than anyone alive, because she had seen it on envelopes and recipe cards and the margins of her schoolbooks her entire childhood—

Her grandmother's handwriting.

*Noor,* it said. *If you are reading this, then I am sorry, and I am proud, and both of those are lies I have no right to tell.*

Behind her, at the far mouth of the gallery, faint but growing, came the sound of boots on stone. Many boots. Descending.

Kesh's hand found hers in the dark. "Tell me you hear that too."

Noor did not answer. She was looking at the door, and the door—warm, patient, lit from within—was looking at her.

And it was opening.
