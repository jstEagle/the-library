## 21. The Gate That Knows Her Name

The thirteenth moon's light came down through the fissures in silver threads, and where it touched the water of the drowned catacombs, the water glowed like spilled honey.

Noor walked at the front because the map demanded it, and because the wards demanded her, and because—though she would not say this aloud, not even to herself—the deeper they went, the more she felt as though she were walking toward something that had been waiting for her since before she was born. The air grew warmer with every step. Not the damp warmth of underground water, but a living warmth, breath-warm, garden-warm, the warmth of a greenhouse in late spring when the glass has caught the sun all day.

Behind her, Aldric moved quietly for a man who had spent twenty years in a cell. His boots made almost no sound on the ancient stone. He had changed out of his prisoner's rags two days ago, when Kesh's stolen court uniforms had finally found their way to them through Maren's last favor, and now he wore plain gray wool like any traveler, and he looked like any traveler, and that was precisely what frightened Noor about him. A man could shed twenty years of dungeon like an old coat and stand there looking reasonable.

Kesh brought up the rear, and Noor heard him the way she always did—a soft shuffle, a half-hummed note under his breath, the small restless music of a man who could not bear silence. Every few minutes he touched her elbow, just briefly, just enough to remind her he was there. She had stopped flinching at it somewhere around the third flooded gallery.

"The map says left," Aldric said.

"The map says nothing," Noor said. "The map is burned on the left side."

"Then it says left by omission." He smiled when he said it. He smiled often, her uncle, and each smile was a small knife turning, because he had Sable's mouth exactly—the same deep lines bracketing it, the same way the corners folded down before they lifted—and every time she saw it she felt the ground shift under her certainty again. *Which truth to carry.* She had chosen his. Some hours, in the dark stretches between waystations, she still wasn't sure why.

She turned left.

The passage opened.

*Opened* was the only word for it. One moment there was stone, cracked and weeping and furred with pale blind moss, and the next moment the moss fell away like a curtain drawn back and the walls widened into a vault so tall her lantern light couldn't find its ceiling. The water ended here, pooling at their feet in a shallow mirror that stretched ahead of them across black polished floor—no, not floor. Noor knelt and pressed her palm to it and felt grain. Wood. A floor of living wood, grown smooth as lake ice, and beneath her hand it pulsed once, faintly, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

"Oh," Kesh said softly behind her. All his music gone. "Oh, that's not right."

"It's the antechamber," Aldric said. His voice had changed too. The reasonableness had drained out of it, leaving something older and hungrier underneath, and Noor recognized the tone because she had heard it from Ilsa, from Corvane, from every person who had ever spoken about the garden. Reverence and appetite wearing each other's clothes. "The records called it the Threshold Hall. Thirteen pillars. Count them."

She counted. Thirteen columns of pale stone rose around the hall's edge, and each one bore carvings worn nearly smooth by time—but not quite smooth. As her lantern passed over them, the shadows in the grooves deepened, sharpened, and Noor saw figures: women and men in guild robes, hands raised, faces turned inward toward the center of the hall. Toward the far wall.

Toward the gate.

It was smaller than she had imagined. That was her first thought, and it was absurd, and she knew it was absurd, but she had carried the idea of the Sealed Garden for so many weeks now that she had somehow built it into a mountain, a cathedral, a thing that would dwarf her. Instead it was a door. A double door of dark wood banded in green metal gone black with age, set into the living rock, no taller than the door of her grandmother's house in the Hollow. Above it, carved in letters she could read without trying—as though the reading were happening inside her rather than on the stone—ran a single line:

*WE KEPT IT SO THE WORLD WOULD NOT EAT IT.*

Below that, older, in a different hand: *AND WHO WILL KEEP IT FROM US?*

Noor stood very still.

"That second line," she said. "That's not in any record."

"No," Aldric agreed. He had come up beside her, close enough that she could smell the lamp oil and prison soap still clinging to him under the clean wool. "It isn't."

"Who wrote it?"

"I don't know. It was already old when the schism happened." He reached toward the doors, and Noor caught his wrist without thinking.

"Don't."

He looked down at her hand on his arm. For a moment something moved behind his eyes—hurt, maybe, or its performance—and then he stepped back, palms open, the picture of harmlessness. "Of course. It has to be you. You know that. I've told you a dozen times." He gestured at the doors with something like tenderness. "They won't open for me. They wouldn't open for your grandmother anymore, either—the wards take the bloodline at its strongest, and that's you, Noor. Nineteen years old, born inside the seal, never touched by the power it holds. You're the key. I'm just the man who knows which way to turn you."

*Which way to turn you.* She turned the phrase over as she approached the doors alone, and found she didn't care for its shape.

Up close, the wood of the gates was not dead. She could see it now—fine veins running through the grain, green-gold, and they moved. Slowly. The way sap moves. She set her lantern on the wooden floor and it cast her shadow huge against the doors, a giantess with hollow eyes, and she thought: *this is what my family did.* Not kept safe. Kept. The way you keep a prisoner. The way Aldric had been kept. The way the Hollow kept her, all those years, wrapped in magiclessness like bandages.

Her blood was singing. There was no other word for it. Since crossing the boundary stones she had felt magic as weather, as texture, as the hum in a struck bell—but here, standing before the thing her ancestors had sealed, the hum became a chord, and the chord became a voice, and the voice was saying her name.

Not *Noor*. Something longer. Something that had her name folded inside it the way a seed folds a tree.

"Say it aloud," Aldric murmured behind her. "The wards need to hear consent. That's how the old seals work—they can't be broken, only answered."

"How do you know how the old seals work?" she asked, without turning around.

A pause. Half a heartbeat too long.

"Because I studied them," he said. "For twenty years, in the dark, it was all I studied."

She believed him. That was the terrible part. Everything Aldric told her fit together, fit the map, fit the ghost-writing beneath the ink, fit the shape of Sable's guilt in her final letters. Believing him cost her nothing except everything she'd been raised on. And yet—

And yet the pause. She filed it away, the way Kesh had taught her to file things. *Information is coin,* he liked to say. *Never spend it all at once.*

Noor drew her belt knife.

"Noor—" Kesh started forward.

"It's fine." She turned her left hand palm-up. In the lantern light the lines of her palm looked ordinary, chapped from road work, a scar across the heel of the thumb from Kesh's disastrous attempt to teach her knife-juggling. An ordinary hand. She pressed the knife's edge to it, hesitated one breath—

—and the doors breathed.

There was no other way to describe it. The great panels swelled outward and settled, swelled and settled, and the green veins in the wood brightened, and warmth rolled off them like the opening of an oven, and every hair on her arms stood up. The carved words above the door began, very slowly, to glow.

"They know you're here," Aldric whispered. He sounded young suddenly. He sounded like a boy at a vigil. "Twenty years I sat in that cell telling myself this was real. Cut, Noor. Let it read you."

She cut.

The pain was small and bright, a red thread welling along her lifeline, and before a single drop could fall, the doors took it. She felt the taking—a pull, gentle and immense, like a tide going out through her skin—and the blood rose off her palm in a fine mist, drifting to the doors, sinking into the grain. The veins drank it. The whole surface of both panels flushed green-gold, then white, then a color she had no name for, and the carved letters blazed:

*FAIRWEATHER.*

Then, beneath it, in the same burning script, as if the gate were speaking to itself, remembering:

*THE THIRTEENTH TURN. THE LAST TURN. THE TURN THAT WAS NEVER MEANT TO BE TURNED.*

The locks—she heard them rather than saw them, great tumblers of stone shifting somewhere in the rock itself, a sound like a mountain clearing its throat—and the doors swung inward an inch. Two inches. Warm golden light spilled through the gap and lay across the black wooden floor, and the smell that came out was impossible: cut grass, ripe apples, rain on hot stone, her grandmother's kitchen, every good smell of her childhood braided into one.

Noor's eyes filled with tears she didn't understand.

"There," Aldric breathed. "There it is." And he put his hand on her shoulder—not gently—and turned her to face him, and his face in the golden light was not the face of a man seeing salvation.

It was the face of a man seeing a door he intended to walk through.

"You need to step back," he said.

"What?"

"The threshold. The wards will accept you fully once you cross—it has to be you first, alive and willing, that's the seal's condition, willing passage. But once you're through—" He was moving her backward as he spoke, firm hands on her shoulders, and his voice had gone quick and low and rehearsed, and Noor understood, with a lurch like the floor dropping, that she had heard this cadence before. From Corvane. From Ilsa. From every careful man explaining why she must do the necessary thing. "Once you're through, the garden reads the whole bloodline again, and it will accept me too, kin-kin, the old law. We go in together, Noor. Together we can hold it. Together we can *use* it—"

"Use it." Her voice came out flat. "You said protect."

"I said what needed saying to get us here." He didn't even have the grace to look ashamed. That was somehow the worst of it—that he looked *relieved*, unburdened, a man setting down a heavy pack after a long climb. "You'll thank me. When you see what it can do—the Hollow, Noor. Your valley. I can make it bloom. I can make the whole kingdom bloom. We Fairweathers have hidden long enough. It's our turn to be the ones who decide."

"And everyone else?"

"Everyone else gets to live in the garden we grow." He smiled, Sable's smile, the knife-smile. "You want me to pretend that isn't mercy? After what the courts did, after the wars? Someone has to hold the power, girl. Better us than them."

Behind him, past his shoulder, the gap in the doors had widened to the width of a hand. Golden light poured through it, and in the light Noor saw movement—leaves, actual leaves, stirring in a wind that had no business existing underground—and she felt the garden reaching for her, feeling for her, the way a sleeping animal feels for warmth.

And she felt something else. Under the reach, under the welcome, a wrongness. A hunger with a familiar flavor. Because she had felt this exact sensation before, three nights ago, when the wards in the lower gallery had recognized her and begun to open—and she remembered what Kesh had said afterward, joking, shaking, lighting his pipe three times before it caught:

*Funny thing about doors that want you, sweetheart. Sometimes what wants you isn't the room. Sometimes it's the thing in the room that hasn't eaten in a hundred years.*

"Aldric," she said quietly. "Let go of my shoulder."

"Not until—"

"*Let go.*"

She did not wait for him to obey. She dropped her weight and twisted the way Kesh had drilled into her outside Vessaline—unglamorous, effective, the escape of someone who had been grabbed before and meant never to be grabbed twice—and his hands slipped, and she staggered back against the thirteenth pillar, and the knife was in her good hand with the bloodied one behind it.

For a moment none of them moved. The golden light flickered across the Threshold Hall. Somewhere far above, muffled by fathoms of stone, came a sound like thunder—or like the gates of the city being breached.

"Do you hear that?" Kesh said from the shadows by the stair. His voice was steady, which meant he was terrified; she knew his tells now. "That's the Court. That's Corvane's people coming down, and probably Ilsa's too, and in about four minutes this hall is going to be full of everyone in the world who wants what's behind that door. So." He looked at Noor. Then at Aldric. Then at the glowing gap. "Family conference later. Right now we pick a side of this doorway and defend it."

"She's not defending anything," Aldric said. "She's going through. The wards are already opening—look at them. If she doesn't complete the passage, the seal resets, and we lose another century, and the Court takes it anyway. Is that what you want, Kesh? To have climbed all this way to hand Corvane a god?"

"He's lying," Noor said.

"About which part?" Kesh asked.

And that was the question, wasn't it. That was the question she would carry out of this hall if she survived it. Because the wards *were* opening. The garden *was* reaching for her. And some traitorous part of her—some part that had spent nineteen years aching to matter—was whispering that Aldric was right, that she could go through, that she could hold it, that she could grow the Hollow green from root to ridge and never watch another orchard die.

The doors groaned wider. Three inches. Four.

And through the widening gap, clear as a bell across water, the garden spoke her name again—that long name with hers folded inside it—and this time Noor heard what she had refused to hear before.

It wasn't welcoming her.

It was calling her home the way a trap calls.

"Noor," Aldric said, and reached for her.

* * *

Later—much later, in the ruin of what happened next—she would try to remember whether she had decided, or whether the decision had simply arrived through her like lightning finding the tallest tree. What she remembered was this: the pillar at her back pulsing like a heart; the golden light stretching toward her feet across the black wood like fingers; her uncle's hand closing on her wrist; and above them all, through the fissures in the vaulted dark, the first true ray of the thirteenth moon sliding down the wall like a blade being drawn.

The ray touched the gate.

The gate *screamed*—a sound of wood and stone and something older than either—and every ward in the hall ignited white, and the doors blew wide, and the garden looked out at them.

All of it. At once.

What Noor saw in that first unbearable instant was beauty, and what she saw in the instant after that was the thing the beauty was made of, and the two sights did not agree with each other, and her mind slid off the contradiction like a boot on ice. Flowers the size of wagon wheels, blooming and rotting and blooming again in the same breath. Trees that grew upward and downward from the same trunk. Light that fell upward. And threaded through all of it, starving, coiled, patient beyond patience, a presence that had been sealed in the dark for a hundred years by people who thought they were saving the world—

—and that presence turned its attention, entire and absolute, onto Noor Fairweather, the last key, standing bleeding in the doorway.

"Come in," it said, in her own voice. "You're late. You've always been late. I've been waiting since before you were born."

Behind her, boots on the stairs. Many boots. Kesh swearing fluently in three languages. Aldric's grip tightening on her wrist—not holding her back now.

Pulling her forward.
