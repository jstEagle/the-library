## 23. Refuge Among Thorns

The Thornbound camp did not look like a rebellion. It looked like a wound that had learned to close over itself.

Noor saw it first from the back of a swaying cart, half-asleep against Kesh's shoulder, her wounded arm bound in strips of boiled linen that smelled of yarrow and smoke. The rebels had found them at dawn on the third day after the tunnel collapse—found them because, as Kesh muttered into her ear with grim humor, *they let themselves be found*. Two women in patched green cloaks and a man whose left arm ended at the elbow, standing over a burned map and a boy who could not have been older than fifteen, all of them watching the road with the patience of people who had been waiting a long time for exactly this.

Now the cart rolled down through a fold of hills where the pines grew crooked and close, and the camp unfolded below like something grown rather than built. Tents of waxed canvas and stripped bark. Cookfires smoking thin and low, damped so their light would not carry. A palisade of sharpened saplings that had been planted, she realized on second look, to look abandoned—leaning at angles no engineer would choose, gaps left where gaps would invite attack, and behind them the real defenses: pit lines, trip-cords strung with bells made from melted spoons, and archers in the trees who let the cart pass only after a whistle-code exchanged three times, each note different, each answered.

"They're good," Kesh murmured. His voice was rough; he had not slept properly since Corvane's men put him in irons, and there was a bruise gone yellow-green along his jaw that he touched absently, the way a tongue finds a broken tooth. "Better than good. I've sold information to three rebel cells and none of them were this disciplined. This is the real spine of it."

"Is that supposed to comfort me?"

"It's supposed to prepare you." He looked at her then, and his eyes had none of their usual glitter. "Noor. They didn't save us because we were bleeding in a ditch. They saved us because you're the most valuable thing in this kingdom right now, and they know it, and they want you to know they know it. Everything that happens next happens on purpose. Watch what they show you. Count what they don't."

She wanted to argue. She wanted, absurdly, to defend the people who had pulled her out of a ditch and fed her broth and set her arm—but the wanting itself told her how dangerous the ground was. Gratitude was a door, and doors opened from both sides.

* * *

They gave her a tent of her own, which should have been a kindness and felt instead like a cell with softer walls. A guard outside—not menacing, just present, a broad-shouldered woman who mended fishing nets and never once looked away from the tent flap longer than a breath. Meals arrived. Water arrived. Questions did not, and that absence was louder than any interrogation.

On the second evening, the tent flap opened without a knock, and Ilsa came in.

Noor had seen her from across the campfire the night before—a tall woman in her fifties with iron-gray hair cropped short and a face like a cliff face, weathered and unreadable. Up close she was smaller than she seemed at distance, and her hands were wrong for a war leader: scarred across the knuckles, yes, but fine-boned, a scribe's hands or a surgeon's. She carried a wooden box under one arm and set it down between them before she said a word.

"Noor Fairweather." She said the name carefully, tasting it, the way one tastes a coin to check if it's gold or gilt. "Do you know how long I've waited to say that name out loud where it wasn't a death sentence?"

"Most people manage it with a warrant attached," Noor said.

Something moved at the corner of Ilsa's mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment that the girl had teeth. "Sit, please. You'll want to be sitting."

Noor sat. Ilsa opened the box.

Inside, packed in straw, were objects that made no sense together: a child's shoe, cracked leather, small enough to fit in Noor's palm. A ledger with a swollen, water-stained cover. A tin cup dented nearly flat. A lock of hair tied with thread, brown going gray. And a loaf of bread—or what had once been bread—petrified to the color and density of river stone.

"What is this?" Noor asked, though some cold part of her already knew.

"Evidence," Ilsa said. "From Merrow's Ford. From Ashen Hollow. From Candlemere, and Duncarrow, and eleven other villages you've never heard of, because the Ember Court made very sure no one would ever hear of them again." She lifted the petrified loaf and held it in both hands, gently, the way one holds an infant or a relic. "This came out of a granary in Candlemere. Sealed by court writ. The grain inside was counted, catalogued, and locked away while the village starved around it. Do you know why?"

Noor's throat was dry. "Why?"

"Because the villages fed Thornbound sympathizers. Because one miller's son ran messages. Because a grandmother hid two deserters in her root cellar for a single night." Ilsa set the loaf down with terrible precision. "One night. The reprisal order was signed by Magistrate Corvane's own mentor, may his name rot. The seal went on the granary in autumn. By midwinter, Candlemere had four hundred souls. By spring, it had sixty-one. My mother was one of the ones who lasted until spring. She died in March, weighing less than the child's shoe in that box."

The fire in the little brazier popped. Somewhere outside, a sentry called the hour in a low voice. Noor became aware that she was gripping the edge of her cot hard enough to whiten her knuckles, and made herself let go.

"I'm sorry," she said, and heard how small the words were, how useless, how true.

"Don't be sorry." Ilsa crouched, bringing herself level with Noor's eyes, and her voice dropped into something quieter and far more frightening than anger. "Be accurate. Your family sealed the garden. The Court hoarded everything else—the ley-wells, the charm-houses, the harvest-blessings, all of it licensed and taxed and rationed. When your ancestors buried the last free source of power in the kingdom rather than share it, they didn't end the war over magic. They decided who would win it. And the people who lost were people like my mother, who never cast a spell in her life, who just wanted bread and got arithmetic instead."

"The guild didn't—" Noor started, and stopped, because Aldric's voice was in her ear: *Sable chose to seal rather than share*, and Sable's own letter, trembling across the page: *we stole it, child, we stole it and called it protection.* "I don't know what the guild did," she finished quietly. "Not really. Everyone tells me a different story and every story makes them the villain except their own."

Ilsa studied her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, she laughed—a short, harsh sound with no joy in it, but real.

"Good," she said. "That's the first honest thing anyone's said to me in weeks. Keep doing that and we might get along." She closed the box. "You're wondering why I'm showing you this. So I'll tell you plainly, because I find plainness disarms people better than charm does—I tried charm in my twenties and it cost me two fingers." She raised her right hand; the smallest finger ended at the second knuckle. "I'm showing you this because it's true, and because when the time comes for you to choose—and it will come, sooner than you'd like—I want you choosing with your eyes open. The Court will offer you safety. We're offering you justice. Neither offer is clean. But only one of us has a box full of dead children's shoes, and only one of us is asking you to help make sure no more boxes get filled."

"And what do you want me to do?"

"Open the garden," Ilsa said simply. "With us. Under our terms. The Thornbound take stewardship of the source, the Court's monopoly breaks, and the power goes where it should have gone eighty years ago—to everyone. To the millers and the midwives and the message-boys. That's all. That's the whole of it." She stood, smoothing her cloak, and at the tent flap she paused. "Rest. Eat. You're no use to anyone half-starved—we've had enough of that particular lesson to last several lifetimes."

Then she was gone, and Noor sat alone with the ghost of a petrified loaf, and understood that she had just been recruited, or begun to be, and could not point to the exact moment it happened. That was the trick of it, she thought. Nobody forced you. They just showed you the shoe.

* * *

Sleep did not come, so she walked.

The guard—Brenna, she'd learned, though Brenna offered nothing but a nod—fell into step behind her without comment, which told Noor that walking was permitted and watched in equal measure. The camp at night was a different creature: fires banked to embers, sentries moving between shadows like fish under ice, and everywhere the small sounds of a large group of people trying to be quiet—canvas breathing in the wind, a horse shifting, someone humming three notes of a lullaby before catching themselves.

She passed the mess tent and stopped, because inside it, by the light of a shielded lamp, a dozen rebels were copying documents. Not orders. Not maps. Letters—hundreds of them, stacked in crates, and the copyists worked in shifts, transcribing each one onto fresh paper in careful hands.

"What are those?" she asked Brenna.

The guard's jaw tightened. "Testimony. Every survivor of the starvations who can still write, or who can talk while someone else writes. Names, dates, orders, the magistrates who signed them. Three copies of each—one stays here, one goes east, one goes—" She stopped herself.

"Goes where?"

"Nowhere yet. It goes nowhere until there's somewhere safe enough to keep it." Brenna's voice was flat in the way voices go flat when feeling has been pressed down so long it's compacted into something harder. "The Court burned the parish records, you know. After Candlemere. Officially those villages never existed. So we write them back into existence, one page at a time, and we wait for a day when writing them down isn't treason."

Noor stood in the cold and watched the lamplight move over bent heads and moving pens, and felt something shift in her chest that she couldn't immediately name. It was not agreement. It was not quite pity. It was closer to recognition—the same ache she had known in the Hollow, watching her grandmother rehearse stories no one outside would ever hear, tending orchards that existed only because the world had agreed to forget them. These people were building a Hollow of their own, out of paper instead of valley stone. Keeping alive what the powerful wanted dead.

And they wanted to use her to win. Both things were true at once. She was beginning to understand that most important things came in pairs like that.

* * *

In the morning, Ilsa took her walking.

Not to a war council, not to a strategy table hung with maps of Vessaline's catacombs. She took her north along a ridge trail, Brenna trailing at a respectful distance, until the camp was out of sight below and the land opened up—scrubland rolling toward the horizon, stitched with old field walls gone to ruin, and in the middle distance, a cluster of roofs.

A village. Ordinary. Smoke rising from chimneys, goats in a stubbled field, laundry on a line.

"Candlemere," Ilsa said, "rebuilt. Sixty-one survivors. Now two hundred and ten. They farm the same fields their grandparents starved above." She pointed south, where a gray smudge marked another settlement. "Merrow's Ford. And beyond it, Duncarrow. There are forty settlements like them within three days' walk. The Court calls them the Reconciliation Lands. Charming phrase. What it means is: here live the descendants of the people we broke, on land we returned only because working it produced taxes."

"Why show me this?" Noor asked. "You've shown me the shoes. You've shown me the letters. Why the living village too?"

"Because the shoes are easy." Ilsa turned to face her, and the wind pulled at her cropped hair, and for a moment the cliff-face cracked and Noor saw the exhausted woman underneath—the scribe's hands, the decades of ledgers, the weight of being the one who remembers. "Anyone can weep for the dead, Fairweather. Dead people ask nothing of you. But look at that village and understand what I'm actually offering—not revenge. Continuation. Those people exist because someone survived, and they will keep existing whether or not you open any garden. But their grandchildren will either grow up under a Court that can starve them again whenever it likes, or in a kingdom where the power in the ground belongs to the ground and to everyone standing on it." Her voice hardened again, the crack sealing over. "That's the choice. Not vengeance versus mercy. Continuation versus repetition."

"You're very good at this," Noor said.

"I should hope so. I've been doing it for thirty years." Ilsa's mouth twitched. "You think I don't know what I'm doing? Girl, I've read your file. Yes, your file—the Court keeps one, and so do we, and mine's more accurate. Nineteen years old. Raised sealed and silent. First lie told to a magistrate six weeks ago, first spell cast three days after that, uncle betrayed her, grandmother lied to her, and now she's standing on a hill being lectured by a rebel commander." She shook her head slowly. "You're not a fool. You see the shape of this. You know I'm showing you exactly what serves me, arranged in exactly the order that serves me best. So here's the only thing I can give you that Corvane can't: I'll admit it to your face. I need you. You are leverage, and shelter, and a symbol, and possibly the key to everything, and I want to use you. The difference between me and the Court is that I'm telling you, and when this is done, I intend for you to still be alive to be angry about it."

The honesty landed harder than any manipulation could have. Noor looked out at Candlemere's chimney smoke and felt the trap close around her gently, almost kindly—and hated that the kindest thing anyone had said to her in weeks had come from a woman using her.

"If I say no?" she asked.

"Then you eat our bread and heal your arm and walk out the south gate with supplies and my word that no Thornbound hand touches you." Ilsa shrugged. "We're not the Court. Leverage that has to be chained stops being leverage and starts being a liability. Besides—" and now the smile came fully, sharp and tired and strangely warm, "—you won't say no. Not because I've manipulated you. Because you've seen the shoes, and you're your grandmother's granddaughter, and underneath all that Valley obedience you've got the same disease the rest of us have."

"Which is?"

"You can't stand watching people go hungry when you know where the food is buried."

* * *

That evening, Kesh found her at the campfire, tossing a strip of dried apple to the boy from the ambush—Wick, his name was, all elbows and adoration, trailing Kesh like a duckling.

"Well?" Kesh dropped onto the log beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched. "Did the great Ilsa give you the shoes? She always gives them the shoes. It's practically liturgy."

"She gave me the shoes. And the letters. And a tour of the redeemed countryside." Noor turned the apple strip Wick had given her over in her fingers without eating it. "Kesh. How much of what she showed me was true?"

He was quiet a moment, and when he answered, the flippancy was gone. "All of it. That's the horrible part. I've brokered enough secrets to know a forged document when I see one, and those ledgers are real. The starvations happened. The cover-up happened. Ilsa doesn't need to lie—she's got the truth, weaponized, and truth's the deadliest blade on the market." He plucked the apple strip from her fingers and ate it himself, which was somehow comforting in its sheer shamelessness. "Question isn't whether she's honest. Question is whether honest and right are the same thing. In my experience they're cousins who meet at funerals."

"She wants me to open the garden for them."

"Mm. And the Court wants you to open it for them. And dear Uncle Aldric wanted you to open it for him. Notice a pattern? Everyone's plan begins with 'Noor opens' and ends with 'for us.'" He glanced sideways at her. "So. What's your plan?"

And Noor opened her mouth to say *I don't have one*—and stopped.

Because she did have one. It had been forming since the ridge, since the chimney smoke, since the copyists bending over their lamps writing dead villages back into existence. A plan nobody had offered her, because nobody who wanted the garden could afford to want what she suddenly wanted. Not the Court. Not the Thornbound. Not even Sable, perhaps, who had spent a lifetime believing seals kept people safe.

She looked into the fire and said, carefully, "Ask me again tomorrow. I want to see their maps first. All of them. If I'm going to be everyone's key, I intend to know every door."

Kesh studied her with narrowed eyes, and then grinned the grin that had gotten him out of a hundred tight corners. "There she is," he said softly. "There's the Fairweather."

Across the camp, a runner emerged from Ilsa's tent at a pace that was more than urgent and less than panic, heading for the watch-fires. Noor caught fragments as he passed—*riders on the south road*—and *court colors*—and then, lower, passed hand to hand along the picket line like a spark:

*They're carrying a white banner. And a chair. They've brought a sedan chair, and whoever's inside it is asking for the Fairweather girl by name.*

Noor's hand went, without thinking, to the burned half of the map hidden against her ribs.

Someone had come to negotiate. Or to threaten. Or both—and in her experience of the last two months, those had always been the same visit wearing different clothes.
