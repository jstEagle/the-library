## 24. Ilsa's Bargain

Ilsa Vane had learned long ago that the most dangerous lies were the ones you told yourself while looking in a mirror.

She stood at the entrance of the thorn-hollowed meeting chamber and watched Noor Fairweather sleep, or pretend to sleep, on a pallet of woven bracken near the far wall. The girl had been with them three days now, and in three days Ilsa had catalogued her the way she catalogued everything: threats first, uses second, humanity third—though she was honest enough with herself to admit the order sometimes collapsed under its own weight.

The Fairweather girl slept like someone who had never learned that sleeping was dangerous. That alone told Ilsa more than a week of interrogation would have. In the camps, in the war years, they had all learned to sleep with one ear open, hands curled near weapons, backs to stone walls. This girl lay open as a book left in the rain, her breathing slow, her burned map tucked beneath her cheek like a child's doll.

*The last Fairweather,* Ilsa thought. *The key to everything we bled for.*

Outside the thorn-walls, the camp of the Thornbound stirred through its evening rituals—the clatter of cook-pots, the low murmur of sentries changing watch, somewhere a child laughing before an adult hushed it. Two hundred souls, give or take, sheltered in a fold of hills that did not appear on any Ember survey. They called it the Refuge Among Thorns because the approach was walled with blackberry bramble grown wild and thick over twenty years of deliberate neglect, and because Ilsa liked names that told the truth twice.

She crossed the chamber quietly and crouched beside the cold fire pit, feeding a few dry sticks into the ash. The girl's eyes opened before the first flame caught. Of course they did. She wasn't as unguarded as she looked.

"You're watching me again," Noor said. Her voice was rough with sleep, but steady. Three days ago she'd flinched at every raised voice. The road was teaching her fast.

"I'm watching everyone," Ilsa said. "It's my least favorite habit and my most useful one."

"And what have you decided about me?"

Ilsa let the small fire build before answering. Around them the thorn-chamber breathed; the living walls creaked faintly in the wind, and the smell of crushed bramble and woodsmoke filled the space like a benediction. She had chosen this room for this conversation deliberately. Thornbound business happened among thorns. It kept everyone honest—or at least reminded them what happened to things that struggled against the wrong grip.

"Sit," Ilsa said. "Eat first."

She passed the girl a bowl of barley porridge sweetened with a spoonful of honey so precious it might as well have been currency. Noor took it without hesitation but ate slowly, watching Ilsa over the rim. Good. A girl who checked her food even when starving was a girl who could survive the months ahead.

"The Ember Court starved my village when I was nine," Ilsa said, without preamble, because she had learned that stories delivered plainly struck deepest. "Not besieged. Not raided. Starved. There was a granary tax—they called it a levy—and when our elders couldn't pay, the magistrates sealed the storehouses and posted guards. We watched soldiers eat bread made from our wheat while our youngest died of the flux that comes when bodies go too long empty." She turned her hands over, palms up, scarred from bramble and rope and worse. "Forty-one people. I can name every one of them. I've made sure I'll never forget how."

Noor had stopped eating. "I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be precise." Ilsa leaned forward. "That's why the Thornbound exist. Not for glory. Not for crowns. Because somewhere between the Court's hunger and the guilds' hoarding, people like mine became arithmetic. Collateral in other people's wars. And I swore over forty-one graves that no power in this kingdom would ever again be held where it could not be reached by the people it crushed."

"That's why you want the garden."

"That's why we want what the garden represents." Ilsa watched the girl carefully now. "Power that answers to no throne. Magic that belongs to the soil and the river and anyone willing to work for it. Your family buried that, Noor. Sealed it away in a cage of forgetting so that no one—not the Court, not the rebels, not the starving villages—could touch it. Forty-one graves in my village, and your ancestors sat behind their seal and let the world burn rather than share what they'd stolen."

"They didn't steal—" Noor began, then stopped. Ilsa saw the doubt move through her like weather. Good. Doubt was fertile ground.

"Didn't they?" Ilsa said softly. "Ask yourself why a guild of healers and gardeners needed a seal at all. Ask yourself why the Hollow forgets, why magic cannot follow past those boundary stones. Protection, they'd say. But protection for whom? The world outside was bleeding, girl. My world. And the Fairweathers pulled the valley shut behind them like a door."

The fire crackled. Somewhere outside, a sentry called the hour. Noor set down her bowl, untouched now, and wrapped her arms around her knees.

"My grandmother says the seal saved lives."

"Your grandmother says many things. So did mine, God rest her, and half of them were lies she needed to survive." Ilsa rose and paced to the thorn-wall, pressing one hand flat against the brambles, feeling their stubborn life. "Here is my offer, and I'll make it only once, so listen with both ears."

She turned back. The girl's eyes were green-gold in the firelight, wary and bright, and Ilsa felt the old familiar ache—that treacherous flicker of wanting to be believed, wanting to be good—that she strangled every morning before it could grow into weakness.

"Help us take the garden," Ilsa said. "Guide us to the gate. Stand with us when the wards ask for blood, because they will ask for yours and no one else's. In exchange, the Thornbound will shield the Hollow forever. Not a season. Not a treaty that dies with me. Forever. No magistrate will ever ride into your valley. No courier will ever bleed out at your boundary stones. The Ember Court has wanted the Fairweathers found for twenty years—we know this, we've seen the orders. Join us, and the hunt ends. Your grandmother dies in her bed, at peace, in a valley no crown can touch."

She let the silence do its work. It was a master's silence, honed across a hundred negotiations, and she watched it settle over Noor like snowfall.

"You could protect one valley," Noor said slowly. "With two hundred rebels?"

"With what's under Vessaline?" Ilsa allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "With the source of all magic in the kingdom, yes. We wouldn't hoard it the way your guild did. We'd open it—carefully, deliberately—to the villages, the freeholds, the people who've never touched magic except as something done *to* them. The Court's power rests on monopoly. Break the monopoly and the Court breaks itself. And the Hollow—" she spread her hands, "—the Hollow becomes what it always should have been. Not a hiding place. A home."

Noor was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was careful, the voice of someone walking a frozen river, testing each step.

"And if I refuse?"

Ilsa held her gaze. "Then you leave with Kesh and your burned map, and we wish you well. I don't hold children hostage, whatever the Court's propagandists say about us." It was true, mostly, in the way that mattered. "But understand what refusal means. The Court finds you eventually—they're already closing. And when they find you, they find the gate, and they bind the garden to the Crown, and every village in this kingdom learns what it means to live downstream from a king who owns the rain. Refusal isn't neutrality, Noor. Refusal is choosing them by default."

"That's not fair."

"No," Ilsa agreed. "Nothing about this is fair. Fairness died in a granary in Corrowdale when I was nine years old. What's left is choices, and who has to live inside them."

* * *

Afterward, Ilsa climbed to the ridge above the camp, as she did every night, and stood where the wind could strip the performance off her.

Below, fires dotted the hollow like fallen stars. She counted them out of habit—cook-fire, forge-fire, watch-fires—and beyond them, far to the south, a smudge of orange on the horizon that might have been sunset and might have been Vessaline burning off its excess light into the sky. The city of brass bells, where the Court grew fat atop the ruins of the old war. Where the garden waited beneath the streets like a heart waiting for a surgeon.

Her second-in-command, Bren, joined her at the ridge, boots crunching frost. He said nothing for a while, which was why she kept him.

"She'll say yes," he said finally.

"She'll say yes."

"And does it trouble you? Using her like this?"

Isla watched the fires. "I offered her truth, Bren. The Court offers her a cell. Her uncle offers her a leash dressed as family. Of the three of us, I'm the only one whose offer survives daylight." She paused. "But yes. It troubles me. That's how you know you haven't rotted all the way through yet."

Bren grunted. "And the plan itself? When we hold the garden. What then?"

There it was. The question underneath every question, the one Ilsa asked herself at three in the morning when the wind came down off the hills carrying the smell of distant hearths. *What then.* Every rebel leader since the first rebellion had answered it with beautiful words, and every one of them had either died or become the thing they fought.

"We open it," she said. "To everyone. Carefully. With rules written in front of witnesses and carved in stone where no magistrate can burn them."

"You believe that?"

"I believe I have to," Ilsa said. "Because if I don't, then forty-one graves were just the price of making me into another magistrate with better manners." She turned her collar up against the wind. "The difference between us and them, Bren, is that we have to keep choosing it. Every day. Out loud. The moment we stop saying it, we've already become them."

He nodded slowly and left her to the dark.

Alone, Ilsa let herself admit the thing she admitted to no one: she wanted the garden's power. Not for the villages, not at first, not in the first hot second of imagining it. In that first second, she imagined herself standing at the gate with the whole kingdom's magic flowing through her hands, and it felt like justice, and justice and hunger wore the same face, and she could not always tell them apart. That was the honest ledger. That was the entry she kept hidden even from herself on most nights.

Which was exactly why she needed the Fairweather girl.

Not only for her blood. For her eyes. Noor Fairweather looked at the Thornbound and saw nobility—Ilsa had watched her see it, watched hope kindle in that valley-sheltered face. But the girl also saw the armory, the drill-yards, the way Ilsa's people talked about the Court's fall with a hunger that had teeth in it. The girl saw both, because she came from a family that had once seen both and chosen the door instead of the crown.

Maybe she'd choose better than her ancestors. Maybe she'd choose worse. Either way, Ilsa intended to be standing next to her when she did, close enough to catch the knife if it turned, close enough to learn something either way.

* * *

Noor found her at dawn, at the edge of the bramble wall, where the eastern light came through the thorns in slivers of gold.

"I'll help you," the girl said. No preamble, no negotiation. She had clearly spent the whole night arriving here, and her eyes had the bruised, resolved look of someone who had argued herself across a bridge and burned it behind her. "I'll guide you to the gate. Under the thirteenth moon. But I have conditions."

Ilsa kept her face still, though something in her chest tightened—triumph and its shadow, arriving together as always. "Name them."

"The Hollow is shielded first. Before anything else happens. Written terms, witnessed, copies in three places. If I die, the terms hold."

"Agreed."

"Kesh goes free. Whatever he owes you, whatever debts he's carrying—he walks away clean when this is done."

"He's not mine to free," Ilsa said, "but I'll spend what influence I have. Agreed."

"And—" Noor hesitated, and here her voice dropped, went young and fierce at once. "When we reach the garden. When the gate opens. Nobody claims it. Not the Court, not you, not me. I've seen what caged power becomes. I've felt it. If we do this, we do it my way at the end, or there is no deal."

There it was. The hook beneath the bait, revealed too late to matter—or perhaps not too late at all. Ilsa studied the girl standing in the thorn-light, nineteen years old and negotiating like a woman who had already buried a kingdom, and felt the strange vertigo of recognizing something in another person that you hated in yourself.

"Those are bold terms for a guest," Ilsa said.

"I learned from watching you."

A laugh escaped Ilsa before she could stop it—short, genuine, immediately regretted. She extended her hand, and Noor took it, and the bargain was sealed with a grip that was almost strong enough to hide how frightened they both were.

"One more thing," Ilsa said, as the girl turned to go. "The letters your grandmother sent. The messenger who carried the last one—did he say how the Court found the trail?"

Noor went very still. "He was dying. He said riders. Ember livery. Heading north along the old salt road."

North. Toward the hills. Toward the Refuge Among Thorns, which had been secret for twenty years and was secret for perhaps another six days.

Ilsa was already turning, voice rising into the camp's morning noise, calling for Bren, calling for saddles, calling for the war she had spent her whole life preparing for and was not, she understood suddenly and completely, prepared for at all.
