## 26. The Court's Offer

The message came the way all terrible things had come to Noor Fairweather lately: folded small, carried by someone with clean hands.

A boy of perhaps twelve stood in the doorway of the Thornbound safehouse—a tanner's back room that smelled of lye and old blood—holding out a letter sealed with a disc of black wax stamped by a crown within a ring of thorns. He wouldn't come inside. He wouldn't say who'd paid him. He only waited until Noor took the letter from his fingers, then turned and walked into the drizzle as though he'd never existed at all.

Noor stood in the doorway a long moment after he was gone, watching the rain stitch silver threads through the gray afternoon, and did not open the letter. She knew what it would say. She had known since the moment she'd woken that morning to an absence she couldn't name, a wrongness in the shape of her days—and then found Kesh's bedroll cold, his pack gone, and a single playing card left on the floor where his boots should have been. The seven of bells. His lucky card, the one he used to end every performance, the one he'd once told her he kept *for luck and for leaving*, though he'd never explained which was which.

She opened the letter anyway, because knowing and confirming were different wounds, and one of them could still be survived.

*Fairweather girl.*

No signature, but she recognized the hand from the magistrate's summons in Vessaline—that precise, patient script, each letter built like architecture. Corvane.

*You have something the Crown requires. I now have something you require. The performer is alive, unharmed, and comfortable, insofar as any guest of mine is comfortable. This state of affairs will persist exactly as long as it is useful to both of us. You will come alone to the Lantern Bridge at moonrise, three nights hence. You will bring nothing and tell no one. In exchange for your cooperation in opening what your family closed, I will return him breathing. If you run, if you bargain with the rebels, if you die uselessly before our appointment, I will return him in pieces, arranged alphabetically, so that you may appreciate my thoroughness even at a distance.*

*Consider how much easier this is than war.*

*M.C.*

Noor read it twice. Then she folded it along its original creases, walked to the room's small iron stove, and held it over the coals until the paper curled and went to ash and the ash went to nothing.

Then she sat down on the edge of her cot and put her face in her hands and let herself shake, just for a little while, in the privacy of a room where no one could see the key to the sealed garden come apart like a frightened girl.

* * *

They were watching her. She felt it within the hour—the way conversations paused a half-beat too long when she entered a room, the way Ilsa's lieutenants found reasons to be near her, the way Darrah, the scarred woman who'd been assigned as her shadow since the ambush, suddenly had business on every street Noor walked down.

Of course they were watching. Noor was leverage; that was why the Thornbound sheltered her at all. And now the Crown had made its own claim, and every faction in the kingdom understood what Noor was only beginning to grasp fully: that she had become the most valuable hostage in the world, and everyone she loved had become hostages of hers.

Because that was the shape of it now, wasn't it? The Ember Court held Kesh. The Thornbound held her—gently, with soup and shelter and soft words about freedom, but held nonetheless, their whole rebellion balanced on the possibility of her cooperation. And somewhere behind her, in a valley she could not reach, the Hollow sat inside the Ember Court's widening shadow, its safety resting entirely on whether Noor Fairweather stayed hidden or came into the light. Sable's last letter had made that brutally plain. Even her grandmother was a hostage, held by distance and threat rather than chains.

Every faction holds someone I care about, she thought, standing at the safehouse's grimy window while rain ran down the glass like the world weeping politely. And the person they're all holding me for is me.

It would have been funny if it hadn't been killing her.

Darrah appeared at her elbow with the particular silence of a woman who'd learned to move quietly around dangerous things. "Trouble?"

"No."

"You're a bad liar when your hands are shaking."

Noor looked down. Her hands weren't shaking. They were perfectly still, gripped white-knuckled around the windowsill, and somehow that was worse. "I'm fine," she said, and heard how flat her voice had gone, how much it sounded like her grandmother's voice, and hated it.

Darrah studied her with eyes the color of river mud, missing nothing. "Ilsa wants you at council tonight. There's movement in the city—court troops pulling back from the east gates, wagons going up toward the heights above Vessaline. Whatever's coming, it's coming soon." A pause. "You know something."

"I know what everyone knows," Noor said. "The thirteenth moon is four nights away."

Darrah grunted, unconvinced, and left her to the window and the rain.

Three nights. Moonrise at the Lantern Bridge. Alone.

Noor watched the street below, where a court patrol clattered past in their brass-buttoned coats, and began, very quietly, to plan.

* * *

She told Ilsa nothing. That was the hardest part—not the lying, she'd discovered she was disturbingly good at lying, but lying to people who had fed her and armed her and shown her starving villages, people whose cause she believed in even as she saw the hunger underneath it.

On the second night she sat across from Ilsa in the rebel leader's cramped map room, candlelight guttering between them, and listened to Ilsa lay out the assault plan for the garden gate with the calm of a woman reading a grocery list. Three columns. The catacombs' known entrances seized and held. Noor escorted to the gate under heavy guard—"for your protection," Ilsa said, and her eyes said *for our investment*—and the garden's power taken for the people, distributed justly, hoarded by no one except, of course, the people holding it.

"And after," Ilsa said, leaning forward, firelight catching the gray in her close-cropped hair. "After, no court can starve another village. No guild can seal away a wellspring and call it wisdom. Everything your grandmother's generation stole from the world, we give back." Her voice dropped, went almost gentle. "You could be the one who does it, Noor. Not a weapon. A door. Doors don't have to answer for what walks through them."

*A door.* Noor thought of Aldric saying nearly the same thing in the dark beneath the city, right before he'd tried to shove her through the gate. Everyone wanted her to be a door. Nobody asked whether doors got to choose which way they swung.

"I'll think on it," she said, and Ilsa smiled the smile of a woman who thought she'd already won, and Noor went to her cot and lay awake counting hours until moonrise.

She left an hour before dawn on the third day, during the change of watch, wearing Darrah's spare oilskin and carrying nothing but a knife, the half-burned map pressed flat against her ribs, and the sick certainty that she was walking into exactly the trap Corvane had built. She'd considered every alternative. Telling Ilsa meant the Thornbound turning her rendezvous into an ambush, which meant Kesh dying in the first volley. Running meant Kesh dying slowly. Going meant—

Going meant she didn't know what. But it was the only direction in which Kesh stayed alive, and Noor had long ago stopped pretending her choices were anything more complicated than that.

The rain had stopped. The streets of Vessaline gleamed under a rinsed sky, brass bells tolling the hour in their towers, and Noor moved through the waking city like smoke, hood up, head down, past market stalls being unshuttered and bakers hauling their first trays, past a knot of children playing at soldiers with stick-swords outside the barracks wall—one of them wearing a scrap of green cloth tied at her arm, a Fairweather color, sold now in souvenir shops for two coppers. Noor's chest tightened. She kept walking.

The Lantern Bridge crossed the Serpentine canal in the oldest quarter of the city, a humpbacked span of black stone hung with a hundred paper lanterns that some ancient ordinance required be lit at dusk and doused at dawn. By day it was just a bridge, wet stone and pigeon mess and a fishmonger shouting prices at the water. Noor reached it as the last lanterns were being pinched out, their smoke rising in thin gray ghosts.

Corvane was already there.

He stood at the bridge's center, alone as promised, hands clasped behind his back, watching the canal flow beneath him with the mild interest of a man inspecting a drain. He wore no armor. His magistrate's coat was dove-gray, immaculate, and utterly without ornament except the thin gold chain of office at his collar. Up close, Noor saw he was older than she'd imagined—sixty, maybe more—with a scholar's stoop beginning at the shoulders and a face like a closed book: nothing cruel in it, nothing kind either. Just attention. He had the terrifying quality of a man who had never once needed to raise his voice.

"Fairweather," he said, without turning. "Punctual. Good. Punctuality is the one virtue I've never had to torture anyone into adopting."

"Where is he?"

"Nearby. Alive. Comfortable, insofar as—" 

"—any guest of yours is comfortable. Yes. I read the letter." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "I want to see him."

"In time." Corvane turned at last, and his gaze settled on her with a weight she could feel physically, like a hand on the back of her neck. It wasn't hostile. That was the worst part. He looked at her the way Maren had looked at the map—as a problem nearly solved. "Before we proceed, I want you to understand that I bear you no ill will. Truly. What happened to your family was a tragedy of policy, not malice. Your guild made a catastrophic decision eighty years ago, and the Crown has spent eight decades paying interest on it." He gestured vaguely at the city around them, the towers, the bells. "Do you know what happens when a source of power that large is simply *removed* from the world? Scarcity. Scarcity breeds courts, courts breed wars, wars breed magistrates. I am not the disease, girl. I am the scar tissue."

"You're holding my friend hostage," Noor said. "Scar tissue doesn't do that."

Something flickered at the corner of Corvane's mouth. Approval, maybe. "He's not your friend. He's an information broker who sold your description to three separate buyers before you ever left Vessaline the first time." He let that land, watched it land. "But yes. He matters to you. That makes him useful, and usefulness is the only form of safety I offer anyone." He stepped aside, gesturing toward the far end of the bridge, where a covered canal barge waited with its lamps lit. "Come. See for yourself."

Kesh sat in the barge's stern, chained at the ankle to an iron ring, a blanket around his shoulders and a cup of something steaming in his hands. At the sight of Noor he managed—of course he managed—a crooked grin, though she could see the exhaustion carved under his eyes and the way his wrists, above the shackles, were ringed with bruising that hadn't come from the chains.

"Noor! Lovely morning. Terrible company. Have you eaten? They feed you here like you're a visiting dignitary, it's honestly embarrassing, I've had worse hospitality at festivals I was *performing* at—"

"Enough," Corvane said mildly, and Kesh's mouth shut with an audible click of teeth.

Noor's vision blurred. She blinked it clear. *Don't cry in front of him. Don't give either of them that.*

"He's unharmed," Corvane said. "As advertised. Now. Let us discuss terms, because I suspect you've spent three sleepless nights imagining I want something monstrous, and I'd like to spare us both the melodrama." He turned to face her fully, and when he spoke again his voice was almost—almost—kind. "I do not want you dead. I do not want your valley burned. I do not even particularly want the garden's power *for the Crown*, whatever my superiors believe. I want the war that is currently assembling itself above this city to not happen. Three factions are converging on a single gate in four nights, Fairweather. Tens of thousands of soldiers. When they meet in those tunnels, Vessaline will drown in its own blood, and whatever comes out of that garden will belong to whoever is left standing—which is to say, whoever is worst."

"So open it and scatter the power," Noor said. "That's what I intend. It ends every claim at once."

"Yes," Corvane said. "I know. I've read Maren's copies. I have excellent readers." He folded his hands. "And that is precisely my offer. I don't want the garden bound to the throne. I want it *gone*—dispersed, ownerless, useless to armies. A kingdom where magic rises wild from the soil is a kingdom where no one can monopolize it. It would end the Court's power too, eventually. I am aware. I find I no longer mind." A pause, dry as dust. "I am sixty-three years old, girl. I have watched what power does to the people who hold it, including the people who hold me. Call it professional disillusionment."

Noor stared at him. Every instinct screamed trap. "Then why the hostage? Why the letter full of threats?"

"Because you would not have come otherwise." He said it simply, without apology. "You are a Fairweather. Secrecy is bred into your bones. If I had written asking nicely for a meeting to discuss the peaceful dispersal of the garden, you'd have assumed ambush and run to the rebels, and the rebels would have marched you to that gate under guard, and the bloodbath would have proceeded on schedule. Fear got you here alone. Here, alone, you can actually hear me." He tilted his head. "Was I wrong?"

The honesty of it was disarming, and Noor hated how disarmed she felt. "And Kesh?"

"Goes free the moment the garden opens and the power scatters. Not before—I'm not a fool, and neither are you. Until then he stays my guest, comfortably, as insurance against you doing something heroic and fatal." Corvane's gaze sharpened. "Understand me, Fairweather. I have no intention of forcing you through that gate. It must be your choice, freely made, or the wards will reject you—we both know how your family's seals work. Which means everything depends on you *wanting* what I want. So consider: the rebels want the garden as a weapon with righteous paint. Your uncle wants it as a crown. The Court's hardliners want it as a treasury. I am offering you the only faction in this war that wants it to simply *stop mattering*." He extended no hand. Made no oath. Just stood in the gray morning light, waiting.

Behind him, Kesh caught her eye and gave the smallest shake of his head—*don't trust him*—and then, infuriatingly, a shrug—*but look at your options*—and Noor wanted to laugh and scream in equal measure.

"The thirteenth moon," she said slowly. "Tomorrow night."

"Tomorrow night," Corvane agreed. "The rebels move at dusk. Your uncle moves earlier—he always does. We will need to be beneath the city before either of them." He finally, formally, inclined his head. "Terms?"

Noor looked past him at Kesh—at the blanket, the steam, the living, breathing, insufferable shape of him—and felt the full weight of what love had become in this war: a chain with her name on one link and his on the other, and every faction in the world holding a different length of it.

"One condition," she said. "When it's done—when the power is scattered—you give me the names of every buyer he sold me to. All three. I want to know who else has been hunting me since Vessaline."

Corvane's eyebrows rose a fraction—the first genuine surprise she'd seen on him. "Why?"

"Because when this is over," Noor said, and heard her own voice go quiet and cold and entirely unlike the girl who'd tended dying orchards a lifetime ago, "I'm going to need to know who to forgive."

For a long moment the only sound was the canal slapping against the bridge pilings and the fishmonger crying his prices to the water. Then Magistrate Corvane smiled—thinly, briefly, like a crack in a closed book—and said:

"Done. Come along, then, Fairweather. We have a garden to kill, and very little night in which to do it."

And as she stepped down into the barge, past her chained friend's wide and worried eyes, Noor felt the half-burned map shift against her ribs like a heartbeat, and thought: *Grandmother, I hope I'm not becoming you. But I think I finally understand why you sealed the door.*

Above the eastern towers, pale as a scar, the last sliver of the waning moon hung in the morning sky—counting down.
