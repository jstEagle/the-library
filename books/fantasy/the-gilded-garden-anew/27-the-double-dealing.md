## 27. The Double Dealing

The cell smelled of wet stone and old iron, and Kesh had already named it. *The Brass Bells' finest accommodation,* he thought, running his thumb along the wall's damp seam. *One star. The lice are complimentary.*

He had been in worse places. He'd been in the holding cells beneath the Crossroads market for three days once, after selling a forged deed to a man who turned out to be the forger who'd made it. He'd been in a Thornbound pit for a week, back when his debts were younger and his mouth less careful. Prisons were all the same at their bones: they ran on information the way mills ran on water, and whoever controlled the flow controlled everything.

Kesh Varrow had never in his life been on the wrong side of an information flow.

So he sat cross-legged on the straw with his hands loose in his lap—his wrists still raw from the manacles, which Corvane's people had finally removed once they'd decided he was more useful talking than bleeding—and he listened.

Prisons talked. That was the first thing everyone forgot. The guards outside his door spoke in the low, bored cadence of men halfway through a long shift: *relief at midnight, the magistrate hasn't slept, the Fairweather woman is still in the city somewhere.* The boots that passed in the corridor above carried rhythm like gossip—the quick scurry of clerks, the heavy tread of soldiers, and twice now, once at dusk and once near dawn, a third kind of step. Measured. Deliberate. Boots that had learned to walk softly in places where softness wasn't expected.

Aldric Fairweather.

Kesh smiled at the wall. *Good evening to you too, uncle.*

* * *

They came for him on the second day, as he'd predicted they would. Not Corvane himself—that would have been beneath the magistrate, and besides, Corvane understood theater better than most men understood their own mothers. No, they sent a lieutenant first. A test run. A man named Hask with a jaw like a shovel and eyes that kept flicking to the door as if hoping someone more important would arrive and take over.

"Your debts," Hask said, laying a ledger on the small table between them with the reverence of a priest handling relics, "have been purchased."

"I know," Kesh said pleasantly. "I could hear the ink drying from here."

Hask blinked. "You—"

"The Ember Court bought my paper through a broker in Bellows Row, probably through two brokers, because Corvane likes distance between himself and anything that might smell bad later." Kesh leaned back against the wall and folded his arms. "Which means I'm not a prisoner anymore, Lieutenant. I'm an asset. And assets get treated differently. Ask anyone who's ever been one."

"You're in a cell."

"I'm in a cell the way a sword is in a sheath." He let that sit for a moment, watching Hask decide whether to be offended or intrigued. Intrigued won—it always did; curiosity was the cheapest lever in the world and Kesh had been pulling it since he was nine years old. "Now. You're going to go back up those stairs and tell Magistrate Corvane that the prisoner laughed when you read him the debt terms. He'll want to know why. And when he asks—and he will ask, because a man who buys debts instead of torturing prisoners is a man who thinks three moves ahead—you tell him this: *the prisoner knows what Aldric Fairweather is planning to do with the garden, and it isn't what the Court thinks.*"

Hask's shovel jaw worked once, twice. "And if he doesn't care?"

"He cares." Kesh smiled his market-day smile, the one that had sold rainwater to fishermen. "Men like Corvane always care about the knife they can't see. Go on. I'll be here. It's not as though I have appointments."

* * *

Corvane came at midnight, which told Kesh everything. Midnight was when powerful men came to say things they didn't want witnesses for, and Corvane had brought no witnesses—not even Hask. Just himself, a single lantern, and that immaculate gray composure that made him look less like a magistrate and more like a well-dressed blade.

"Mr. Varrow." He set the lantern on the table and did not sit. Powerful men never sat in cells; sitting meant staying, and staying meant the cell was winning. "My lieutenant tells me you've developed opinions about Aldric Fairweather."

"I've developed opinions about everyone. It's my defining flaw." Kesh unfolded himself and stood, slowly, so the movement couldn't be mistaken for threat. "But yes. Him especially. Shall we trade? You give me something true, I'll give you something true, and we'll both walk away feeling clever."

"You're in no position to negotiate."

"With respect, Magistrate—" and Kesh put just enough warmth into *with respect* to make it an insult, "—you bought my debts rather than putting me to the question. That means you think I'm worth more talking. Which means you've already conceded the negotiation. You're just here to find out the price."

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes—annoyance, mostly, but underneath it, the grudging attention of a man recognizing another player at the table. "The price," Corvane said, "is your cooperation. Full cooperation. Everything you know about the Fairweather girl, her movements, her allies, the map."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange, your debts are settled and you leave this city with your fingers intact."

"Fingers." Kesh clicked his tongue. "See, this is where our relationship has a problem already. Because you're offering me freedom, but freedom isn't worth anything to a dead man, and I'll be a dead man within the month whether I cooperate or not. Do you know why?" He let the silence stretch, let Corvane lean into it the way buyers leaned into a seller's pause. "Because Aldric Fairweather doesn't intend to hand the garden to the Crown. He intends to bind it to himself. And when he does—if he does—everyone who helped him get there becomes a loose end. Everyone who helped stop him becomes a martyr. There's no version of that story where the informant walks away whistling."

"You speak as though his betrayal is certain."

"It's certain the way sunrise is certain." Kesh began to pace, three steps each way, working the small cell like a stage. "Think about what he's done already. He spent twenty years in your dungeon—twenty years, Magistrate, and then suddenly he's free, cooperating, helpful? Men don't turn that fast unless they were turning the whole time. He fed you exactly enough to earn his collar and his keys, and every piece of it pointed toward the garden, toward the thirteenth moon, toward needing the girl alive. Ask yourself: why does a loyal servant of the Crown need Noor Fairweather *alive*? The map's half-burned. The wards answer blood. If all he wanted was the Crown's glory, he'd have told you to bring her dead and let your ritualists work it out. But he didn't. He wants her breathing, and willing, and standing at that gate with her hand on the latch." Kesh stopped pacing and looked Corvane full in the face. "That's not service. That's courtship. He's courting the garden, and she's the key, and you, Magistrate, are the carriage he's borrowing."

For a long moment the only sound was the lantern's flame ticking in its glass.

"You have evidence," Corvane said at last. It wasn't a question, but it wasn't quite a statement either. It was a door held open a crack.

"I have patterns," Kesh said. "Evidence is for magistrates with time. Patterns are for men who need to decide by moonrise." He held up one finger. "Pattern one: his guards change on nights when the catacomb entrances are watched—he rotates the watch to men who owe him, not men who report to you. Check it. Two days of records, that's all it takes." A second finger. "Pattern two: he's been asking the archivists about binding rites. Not opening rites—*binding*. Opening gets the garden for whoever holds the gate. Binding keeps it for whoever holds the rite. Look at what he requested from Maren's old colleagues. Requested quietly. Through intermediaries." A third finger, and here Kesh slowed down, because this was the blade under the silk. "Pattern three: the night the Thornbound ambushed the coach road, someone warned them the courier's cargo had changed hands. The Thornbound knew things only four people knew. One of them is in this cell. One of them is standing in front of me. And one of them spent twenty years learning how to pass messages through dungeon walls."

He hadn't actually confirmed any of that. He'd built it out of half-heard guard talk, the shape of Aldric's boot-steps, and the simple, load-bearing truth that ambitious men betray. But Corvane didn't know how thin the mortar was, and Kesh watched the cracks spread across that gray composure anyway—a tightening at the mouth, a fractional shift of weight, the lantern light catching sweat at the temple that hadn't been there before.

Doubt, once planted, needed no gardener. It grew on its own, all night, all week, in the dark where powerful men kept their certainties.

"I will verify your patterns," Corvane said carefully.

"Please do. Verification is free. Ignoring me is expensive." Kesh sat back down on the straw, casual as Sunday. "And Magistrate? When you check the guard rotations—do it yourself. Don't send Hask. Ask yourself why Aldric requested Hask specifically for my interrogation detail." He hadn't requested Hask at all, as far as Kesh knew. But by tomorrow morning, the question would be living in Corvane's head rent-free, and Hask would flinch at his own shadow, and flinching men confirm suspicions simply by being suspected.

Corvane picked up the lantern. Paused. "If you're lying, Mr. Varrow, no debt settlement will save you."

"If I'm lying," Kesh said comfortably, "you'll know within two days, and I'll still be in this charming cell. Whereas if I'm right and you do nothing—" he spread his hands, "—well. Sunrise."

* * *

The beautiful thing about sowing distrust between two men was that it required almost no maintenance. Kesh had learned that young, running cons in the Crossroads markets: you didn't tend the lie, you just made sure both parties kept checking on it. Every time Corvane verified something, he'd find ambiguity, and ambiguity read as confirmation to a suspicious mind. Every time Aldric felt the chill of his master's new wariness, he'd grow more guarded, and guardedness read as guilt.

By the fourth day, the guards told each other—in the bored corridor cadence, believing their prisoner asleep—that the magistrate and the Fairweather traitor had shouted at each other behind closed doors. By the fifth, Aldric's boot-steps stopped passing Kesh's corridor altogether, rerouted, avoiding. Avoiding meant aware. Aware meant off balance.

And off-balance men made mistakes with doors.

Kesh catalogued the mistakes the way other men collected coins. The night guard rotation shifted to include a boy of maybe seventeen who checked the locks by feel rather than sight because the torch bracket nearest the cell had gone out and nobody had replaced it—*a dark corner, gentlemen, really, you shouldn't have.* The kitchen delivery came an hour earlier than usual, which meant the corridor stood empty for eleven minutes between shifts instead of four. And on the sixth evening, Hask himself came down, red-eyed and jumpy, to move Kesh to a different cell—*interrogation transfer, standard procedure*—and in doing so walked him past a junction where a drainage grate sat loose on its rusted pins, and Hask's hand shook so badly turning the key that Kesh memorized the lock's resistance, its little stutter, its whole personality, in the space of three seconds.

Locks had personalities. So did routines. So did frightened lieutenants and suspicious magistrates and traitorous uncles circling each other like dogs who'd caught each other's scent.

Kesh lay in the new cell—worse accommodations, smaller, damper, *one star, would not recommend*—and assembled the window the way he'd assemble a story: piece by piece, until it held weight. Kitchen delivery, eleventh minute. Dark corner, dead torch. Drainage grate at the junction, pins rusted to lace. Boy-guard with the feel-not-look habit, due for relief at midnight, relief always late on the nights Aldric's people pulled men for the catacomb watches.

Three nights from now, if the pattern held, there would be a gap in the world roughly the width of one thin, quick, deeply indebted man.

He didn't smile about it. That was the strange part. He lay in the damp dark and found that the thought of escape arrived wearing an unexpected coat: escape *to*, not escape *from*. Escape from cells was easy; he'd done it twice before the age of twenty. Escape *to* something—that was new. Escape to a valley girl who cut her hair and lied to magistrates and threw magic around like a first snowfall, wild and blinding and hers. Escape to a cause that wasn't his, carrying a debt that wasn't written in any ledger.

*I'm getting sentimental,* he told the ceiling. *It's the damp. Definitely the damp.*

But sentiment, he'd learned, could be useful too, if you gave it a job. So he gave it this one.

* * *

On the last night before the window, they brought him writing materials. This was Corvane's move, of course—*cooperate fully, write down everything you know about the girl, her routes, her habits, her weaknesses*—and it was also, inevitably, Kesh's move, because a man who asks a liar for a document has commissioned a forgery.

He wrote for two hours. He wrote truthfully, that was the artistry of it—every word about Noor Fairweather technically accurate, her hair now short, her cloak gray, her papers forged by a man whose name he spelled slightly wrong. He wrote her habits, her courage, her terrible taste in travel companions. And then, in the final paragraph, in the middle of a dense, dutiful inventory of her supposed hiding places, he wrote four sentences that read like filler and were anything but:

*She keeps the burned half of the map folded inside her left boot, though she has taken to sleeping with it under her pillow since Vessaline. She counts things when she is afraid—stairs, bells, stars—her grandmother's habit, she says, from a valley that forgets. She will not enter a garden gate without asking permission of the ground first. She believes mercy is a plan.*

Noor would read that paragraph—or someone would read it aloud to her, or it would surface in some archive after all of this was ash—and she would understand. *Left boot* meant *look closer.* *Counts things* meant *I counted the days, I counted the minutes, I am coming.* *Asks permission of the ground* meant *the drainage grate, below, beneath, follow the water.* And *mercy is a plan*—

Well. That one was just true, and he wanted her to have it.

He signed the document with a flourish, added a postscript requesting better straw, and handed it to Hask, whose hands had stopped shaking and started trembling, which was worse.

"One more thing, Lieutenant," Kesh said pleasantly, as Hask gathered the pages. "Tell the Magistrate the prisoner cooperated fully and expects his debts honored. And tell him—" he leaned forward, dropping his voice into the register he saved for the best lies, the ones built entirely out of truth, "—tell him the girl trusts Aldric now. Uncle and niece, reconciled. Whatever happens at that gate, it'll happen with the Court's own traitor standing closest to her. Sleep well."

Hask went pale in the torchlight and hurried up the stairs with the pages clutched to his chest like contraband.

Kesh settled onto the bad straw of the worse cell and closed his eyes. Above him, faint through the stone, he could hear the city settling into night—the brass bells marking the hour, the shift-change boots beginning their slow procession, the whole vast machine of the Ember Court grinding toward the thirteenth moon with its wheels full of sand he'd poured there himself.

Two days. Maybe three. Kitchen delivery, eleventh minute. Dead torch. Rusted pins. A boy who locked by feel.

And somewhere out in the dark, a girl counting stars, waiting for a message that hadn't reached her yet—but would. Paper traveled. Paper always traveled. It was practically a religion with him.

*Hold on, Fairweather,* he thought, grinning at nothing in the damp dark like the fool everyone had always underestimated. *Your worst investment is about to pay out.*
