## 28. Sable Rides Out

The seal-charm had not left the Hollow in forty years, and neither had Sable Fairweather.

She took it down from the rafter beam above her hearth on a morning so still the smoke from her chimney rose in a single unbroken column, as if the sky itself were holding its breath. The charm hung from an iron hook she had forged herself, back when her hands were steady and her eyes did not ache in candlelight, and it was smaller than most people imagined a thing of such consequence should be—a disc of dark metal no wider than her palm, worked with the thirteen-turn knot that was the guild's oldest mark, its grooves filled with something that was not quite rust and not quite blood.

Sable held it up to the grey light and let herself look at it properly for the first time in decades. She had made a practice, over the years, of not looking. Looking invited remembering, and remembering invited the particular kind of grief that had teeth. But there was no more room in her life for careful practices. The letters she had sent to Noor had gone unanswered—or worse, answered by silence that might mean anything—and the last messenger she'd hired had come back with his horse lamed and his nerve broken, babbling about soldiers on the coach roads and rebels burning waystations. The world outside the boundary stones was coming apart, and it was coming apart along the exact seams her generation had sewn shut.

"You're a foolish old woman," she said aloud, to the empty kitchen, because someone ought to say it and there was no one left to say it for her.

The orchards were dying. That was the truth beneath everything else, the truth she had been circling for years the way a tongue circles a cracked tooth. She walked out among the bare-limbed apple trees while the frost still silvered the grass, and she put her hand against the oldest trunk—the one her mother had planted, the one that had borne fruit every autumn of Sable's childhood—and felt nothing under the bark. Not death exactly. Death would have been honest. It was absence, a hollowness, as though the tree were only pretending to be wood and had grown tired even of that. The seal kept magic out of the valley, and without magic, slowly, over generations, the land itself forgot how to be fully alive. The Fairweathers had built their sanctuary out of forgetting, and now the forgetting was eating them.

Her mother had warned her. *A seal is a wound you choose,* she had said, the night before the binding was done, when Sable was young and certain and so very proud of the cleverness of it. *And every wound you choose, you must tend forever, or it chooses you instead.*

Sable had tended it for forty years. She had burned records and buried truths and watched three generations of her family dwindle into farmers who told stories about magic the way other people told stories about dragons—fondly, from a great distance, with no expectation of ever meeting one. She had done it because the alternative had been war, because the garden's power hoarded in one place had already soaked the kingdom in blood once, because someone had to be the coward who stopped the bleeding rather than the hero who won the battle.

And now Noor had crossed the stones with a dead man's map in her pocket, and all of it—every lie, every buried record, every quiet year—was coming due at once.

* * *

She packed the way she imagined soldiers packed: quickly, ruthlessly, with no sentiment permitted past the door. Two cloaks. Bread and hard cheese wrapped in waxed cloth. Her mother's knife, which had never been used for anything but pruning and which she buckled at her hip anyway, because the world outside had teeth and she was old enough to be honest about what she could and could not fight. A satchel of dried herbs. The letters she had written and never sent—eleven of them, tied with string, each one a small confession she had composed in the sleepless hours and then lacked the courage to dispatch.

At the bottom of her pack, wrapped in three layers of linen, went the seal-charm.

There were things she needed to do before she left, and she did them in order, like a woman following a recipe she had known all her life. She banked the hearth fire so it would burn low and long. She opened the coop and scattered grain generously, letting the hens fend for themselves; they would, hens always did. She walked the length of the orchard one final time and pressed her palm to each of the thirteen oldest trees, and if her lips moved as she passed them, there was no one to hear what she said.

Last of all she went to the meeting hall, where the remaining elders sat around the cold hearth arguing about the vigil schedule as though schedules mattered anymore, as though the centennial meant anything now that the world beyond the stones had learned where the Fairweathers slept.

"I'm leaving," she told them.

Old Merrin looked up first. He had been her friend since they were children stealing apples together, and he was the only one whose face showed understanding instead of alarm. "Leaving," he repeated. "Sable. You haven't crossed the stones in forty years."

"No. And I've counted every day of it, which is its own kind of madness." She set her pack on the table between them, and after a moment's hesitation, unwrapped the charm and laid it in the center of the scarred wood. The elders drew back from it as one body, the way people draw back from a snake. Even here, even in the valley where it was inert, where it was only metal and memory, they feared it. Good, she thought. Fear was the only honest inheritance she had ever given them.

"This stays in the Hollow," she said. "Bury it under the third tree from the well. If I don't come back, dig it up in ten years and give it to whoever leads next. If no one leads, let it rot."

"And where are you going?" Merrin asked quietly.

"Out." She smiled, and knew from his expression that it was not a comforting smile. "To end what my generation began. Or to die trying, which would at least be tidy."

"Sable—" One of the younger elders, Tam, leaned forward, earnest and frightened. "The stories say the guild fell because we reached too far. If you go out there—"

"The guild didn't fall because we reached too far, boy." She heard her own voice go flat and old. "The guild fell because we reached too far and then, when it cost us, we hid. We sealed away a power that belonged to no one and called it protection, and we called our fear wisdom, and we called our shrinking virtue. Forty years I've called it that. I'm done calling it anything. I'm just going to go and see what's true."

* * *

The boundary stones stood where they had always stood, at the northern pass, seven grey monoliths worn smooth by weather and by the passage of everyone who had ever left and never come back. Sable had walked this path thousands of times in her youth, and her feet remembered it even as her lungs complained and her knees announced their grievances with every step uphill.

She stopped at the sixth stone and rested her hand against it. The stone was warm. That was new—that was wrong. Stones in the Hollow were never warm. Unless—

Unless the seal was weakening. Unless whatever Noor had done or become out there in the wide world was pulling at the threads of it, unraveling the work of three generations from the far side. Sable closed her eyes and felt, faintly, unmistakably, a hum through her palm. Like a plucked string still ringing somewhere below hearing.

"Oh, child," she murmured. "What have you gotten yourself into?"

She stepped across.

The change hit her like cold water. For forty years she had lived inside a silence so complete she had forgotten it was silence, and now the world roared back in—not sound, but presence, a thousand textures of living force brushing against skin that had forgotten how to feel them. The grass sang. The wind carried flavors. Somewhere upslope a fox watched her with eyes that held actual thought behind them, actual attention, and Sable laughed—a rusty, startled sound, half sob—and pressed both hands over her mouth.

Magic. Ordinary, casual, everywhere magic. The world she had locked her family away from. It had never gone anywhere. It had simply been waiting for her to come back to it.

She stood at the treeline for a long time, an old woman with a pack and a knife, laughing and crying into her hands while the wind moved her white hair. Then she straightened, wiped her face with her sleeve, and turned south toward the coach road, toward Vessaline, toward whatever remained of her granddaughter.

* * *

The world had changed, and it hadn't.

That was the cruelty of it. The roads were the same roads. The milestones bore the same chipped numerals. The same crows picked at the same dung heaps outside the same villages. But threaded through all of it, invisible and constant, ran the hum—the great slow pulse of the land's own power, rising wild and ownerless since the night Noor had opened the garden. Sable felt it strengthen daily as she traveled, felt it in the water she drank and the bread she bought and the ground under her feet at night. Whatever Noor had done, she had done it thoroughly. Magic belonged to everyone now, or to no one, and the kingdoms were only beginning to understand what that meant.

She saw the consequences at every crossroads. In one village, a hedgewitch who would have been burned twenty years ago now sold charms openly from a market stall, and a queue of farmers waited patiently for cures and blessings. In another, a mob had recently torn down a magistrate's office—she read it in the scorch marks and the shattered notice boards—and someone had nailed a thorn branch above the empty doorway. The Thornbound's rebellion had found new purpose, or new desperation; from the talk in the taverns, it was hard to tell which. In a third town, soldiers wearing Ember Court livery went door to door with lists, and Sable kept her hood up and her eyes down and felt her mother's knife at her hip and was grateful for it.

She was nobody. That was her armor. An old woman walking, gray and bent and unremarkable, carrying nothing worth stealing. She had spent forty years practicing invisibility, and it turned out the skill transferred.

On the ninth day she reached the waystation at Coldford Bridge, a low stone inn straddling the river, and paid for a bed with coins she had not touched in four decades. The innkeeper's wife fed her stew and asked no questions, which was the custom of the road, and Sable ate gratefully and listened.

The talk was all of Vessaline. Of armies gathering on the hills above it. Of the Magistrate Corvane, who had fallen from favor overnight when his own faction learned he had bargained away court secrets. Of the Thornbound marching openly now, no longer rebels but something almost like a pilgrim army, singing as they came. Of a woman from a green valley, some said, who had given the magic away—though others said she was dead, and others still that she had never existed at all, that the dispersal had been the garden's own will, its own escape.

Sable sat with her spoon halfway to her mouth and felt something loosen in her chest that had been clenched for nineteen years.

Alive. They said she was alive. Dead in half the tellings, but alive in the others, and Sable found she believed the others, because she had raised that girl and she knew the stubborn set of those shoulders, and because a grandmother is permitted her certainties.

She finished her stew. She went up to her narrow bed, and lay awake listening to the river, and allowed herself, for the first time since the courier died at the boundary stones, to imagine a future in which she was not too late.

In the morning she would hire a cart south. In three days, perhaps four, she would reach the outskirts of Vessaline, and then—

Then she would find her granddaughter, and say the things the eleven unsent letters had failed to say. *I lied to you. I lied to your mother, and her mother before you, and I am not sorry for the sealing but I am sorry for everything else, and none of it was ever your burden to carry, and I love you, and I am here.*

The floorboard outside her door creaked.

Sable was awake and upright in an instant, knife hand finding the handle by instinct older than thought. Forty years of quiet had not dulled that particular reflex; it had honed it, the way a stone in a river goes smooth but also sharp.

The knock, when it came, was soft. Courteous. Which frightened her more than pounding would have.

"Grandmother Sable Fairweather?" A woman's voice, young, carefully neutral. "I've ridden two days to find you. My name is Wren. I serve the Thornbound."

Sable did not answer. She weighed the window against the door and found both wanting.

"There's no ambush," the voice said, and now there was something underneath the neutrality, something tired and human. "Ilsa Thorn sent me. She wants you to know that Noor is alive. She's been wounded, but she's alive, and she's sheltering with us." A pause. "And she wants you to know that Aldric Fairweather is alive too. He's been serving the Ember Court. And grandmother—he knows you're traveling. He's coming to find you."

The river sounded very loud suddenly, filling the silence the way water fills a crack in stone.

"He's asking," the voice continued gently, "for the seal-charm."

Sable looked at her pack, where the charm lay wrapped in linen against her spine, and understood—with the terrible clarity that comes only at the ends of things—that the reckoning she had ridden out to meet had just arrived early, wearing her brother-in-law's face, and that the road ahead had narrowed to a single question:

Who would reach Noor first?
