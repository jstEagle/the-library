## 29. Reunion of Liars

The waystation at Miller's Rest had been a shrine once, before the war made shrines unfashionable and the Ember Court converted them all into tollhouses with candles. Now it was neither—a squat stone building hunched at the crossroads where the river road bent toward Vessaline, its bell tower stripped of its bell, its windows patched with oiled leather that turned the daylight the color of weak tea.

Noor arrived two hours before dawn because Kesh had taught her that anyone expecting you would come at dusk, and anyone hunting you would come at midnight, and the hour between midnight and dawn belonged to no one but ghosts and the desperate.

She had been both for weeks now.

* * *

She had found Sable's last letter three days earlier, tucked inside the lining of a coat Kesh had stolen—no, *acquired*, he'd have corrected her with that crooked grin—for her in Thornhold. The letter was written in her grandmother's hand, which was to say it was written like a blade being sharpened: short strokes, no wasted motion, every word honed to cut or hold.

*The Hollow is found. They came through the northern pass under a trade banner, and I am not so old that I missed what rode beneath it. The valley's protection lives and dies with you now, girl. Stay hidden. Stay small. Stay—*

The rest was ash. The messenger who'd carried it had been dying when he pressed the charred corner into her hands outside the walls of Thornbound camp, his lips gray, his eyes already fixed on something past her shoulder. He'd said only one word: *"Waystation."* Then he'd coughed blood onto the frozen mud and been done with the whole business of living.

Noor had spent three days telling herself she wouldn't go. Three days of Ilsa watching her with those patient, hungry eyes, of Thornbound couriers coming and going with news that grew worse by the hour—the Ember Court mobilizing along the river, Aldric's name appearing on warrants beside Corvane's own, the thirteenth moon swelling fat and pale above everything like an eye refusing to blink.

Three days of hearing her grandmother's voice in the letter's final unfinished sentence. *Stay—*

Stay what? Hidden? Small? Alive?

Or stay away, so that whatever came for the Fairweathers would find only an old woman and a valley full of dying orchards, and not the girl who was the key to everything?

She had left before moonset, taking one of Ilsa's horses without asking, which Ilsa would either forgive or add to a ledger. With Ilsa it was always both.

* * *

Miller's Rest was empty when she reached it—or seemed empty. Noor had learned the difference between *seemed* and *was* the hard way, in catacombs and courtrooms and one memorable alley in Vessaline where "seemed" had nearly cost her a kidney. She circled the building twice, noted the single horse in the lean-to (a gray mountain pony, Hollow-bred, its mane braided in the old guild style), and felt something in her chest crack sideways before she'd even seen a face.

Only one person alive still braided a horse's mane that way.

The door was unlocked. That frightened her more than anything else could have. Sable Fairweather did not leave doors unlocked; Sable Fairweather believed locks were a courtesy extended to the stupid.

Inside, the great room smelled of tallow smoke and dried herbs and, underneath it, faintly, impossibly, of the Hollow—of apple wood and cold stone and the particular mineral smell of the boundary stones after rain. A fire burned low in the hearth. Two cups sat on the table. Both were full. Both were cold.

And in the chair by the fire, wrapped in a traveling cloak that had once been fine and was now merely stubborn, sat her grandmother, hands folded in her lap, eyes closed, looking for all the world like a woman who had simply decided to stop existing for a while and might resume at any moment.

"You took the long way around the building," Sable said, without opening her eyes. "Twice. Your grandfather used to do the same thing. As if circling makes a place safer instead of just making you later."

Noor stood in the doorway with snow melting off her shoulders and her heart hammering somewhere up near her teeth. Nineteen years of rehearsed conversations collapsed in on themselves. She had imagined this meeting a hundred times since leaving the Hollow—in the first week she'd imagined apologizing; by the second month she'd imagined shouting; lately she'd imagined it not happening at all, because people who carried the things she carried did not get reunions. They got endings.

"I thought you'd be dead," she said, because she had never learned to soften things and saw no reason to start now.

Sable opened her eyes. They were the same gray-green as Noor's own, and they were tired in a way Noor had never seen before—not the tiredness of age but the tiredness of a woman who had been carrying a weight for forty years and had finally, deliberately, set it down on the ground in front of her granddaughter.

"The messenger was," Sable said. "Dead, I mean. Couriers who carry my letters seem to have short careers lately. Sit down, Noor. Your tea's cold, but the kettle isn't."

* * *

For a long while neither of them drank anything. The fire ticked and settled. Outside, the wind worried at the stripped bell tower, making a sound like a voice trying to remember a song.

Noor studied her grandmother across the table and catalogued the changes the way she'd once catalogued blights on the orchard trees—methodically, because method was how you kept from weeping. Sable had aged a year in two months. Her hands, always knotted, now trembled unless she pinned them down. There was a new scar along her jaw, thin and white. And she was here—*here*, four hundred miles from the valley she hadn't left in forty years—which told Noor more than any scar could.

"You left the Hollow," Noor said.

"I did."

"You swore you never would. You told me the valley needed its keeper. You told me—" She stopped. Swallowed. "You told me a lot of things."

"Yes," Sable said. "I did."

It was not an apology. Noor hadn't expected one. But it wasn't a defense either, and somehow that was worse, because it left the sentence hanging there between them, open on both ends, admitting everything and nothing.

"Why are you here?" Noor asked. "Really. Not the answer you'd give the elders. Not the answer you'd write in a letter that might get intercepted. Why are you actually sitting in a dead shrine four hundred miles from home, drinking cold tea at the hour of ghosts?"

Sable looked at her for a long moment. Then she laughed—a dry, rusted sound, like a gate opening after decades shut—and Noor realized she couldn't remember the last time she'd heard her grandmother laugh. Had there ever been laughter in the Hollow? Or had that too been sealed away with everything else?

"You've changed," Sable said. "You used to ask questions like you were afraid of the answers. Now you ask them like you're owed."

"I'm owed," Noor said. "I think I'm finally owed."

* * *

What Sable told her then, she told plainly, and Noor would remember afterward that plainness more than any of the words—that her grandmother, who had spent a lifetime wrapping truth in story until the two were indistinguishable, chose at last to speak without ornament, as if she knew that anything decorated would be discarded.

"There was never a curse," Sable said. "There was never a plague of magic, or a divine judgment, or any of the hundred stories I fed you and your mother and her mother before her. The Hollow is quiet because we made it quiet. Because I made it quiet. The seal over the garden extends outward, girl—it doesn't just cage the power. It cages everything that power might touch. And the Fairweather lands touch the garden's roots deeper than anywhere else in the kingdom."

Noor's hands had gone still around the cold cup. "You sealed our own valley."

"We sealed our own valley," Sable agreed. "Because the seal needed an anchor, and the anchor needed to be somewhere the Fairweathers controlled absolutely. Somewhere no one would look. What better hiding place for a locked door than a house everyone believes is cursed?" She paused. "Your mother was born inside the seal. So was I. It's why none of us could work magic. It's why the guild faded. We didn't die out, Noor. We were *spent*. Every generation poured a little more of itself into keeping the door shut, and the door took payment in exactly the coin we had least of."

"The gift," Noor whispered. The old stories said the Fairweather gift had dimmed over the centuries, worn thin like a garment handed down too many times. She had grown up grieving a loss she'd never witnessed, mourning talents she'd never had, believing it was simply the way of things—the way orchards died, the way rivers silted, the way greatness faded into anecdote.

"It was never thinning," Sable said. "It was being *drunk*. The seal drinks from the bloodline. That's what keeps it fed. That's why you—" She stopped herself, but Noor finished it anyway, because she had known it in her bones since the night the wards beneath Vessaline had sung to her blood.

"That's why I'm the key."

"That's why you're the key. The strongest vessel born to the line in six generations. The seal has been feeding on you since the day you were born, and it grew fat on you, and a fat lock is a strong lock, and I thanked the ancestors for it every morning of your childhood." Sable's voice did not waver, but her hands, pinned in her lap, had gone white-knuckled. "Every morning, Noor. I looked at a child and thanked the powers that she'd been born strong enough to be imprisoned well."

* * *

The fire had burned down to coals. Neither of them moved to feed it.

"And the garden itself?" Noor asked. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears, flat as the map's burnt edges. "Whose was it? Who built it? Who does it belong to?"

And here Sable did something Noor had never seen her do. She bowed her head.

"No one's," she said. "That's the truth I have spent forty years drowning, and I will say it once, plainly, and then I will spend the rest of my life wishing I'd said it sooner. The garden belonged to no one. It belongs to no one. It is older than the guild, older than the Court, older than the war they fought over it. It is not a treasure. It is not a weapon. It is a *source*—the source, maybe, the first spring from which all the scattered magic of this kingdom flows—and it was never ours to hold, any more than the rain is ours to hold, any more than the sun."

"Then why—"

"Because we could." Sable lifted her head, and her eyes were terrible and clear. "Because eleven generations of Fairweathers convinced themselves that we alone were wise enough, pure enough, disciplined enough to keep it safe. Because the Court wanted to spend it and the old orders wanted to hoard it and we told ourselves we were different—we were *stewards*—and then we built a lock around it and threw away the key and called the throwing-away virtue." Her breath came out ragged. "The war didn't happen because the garden was stolen, Noor. The war happened because we stole it first, and everyone else spent twenty years trying to take it back from thieves who insisted they were saints."

The words landed in Noor like stones dropped into deep water. She thought of the villages Ilsa had shown her—starved hollows, fields salted on the Court's orders, children buried in rows because grain had gone to armies instead of mouths. She thought of the Court's gilded halls, of Corvane's patient, reasonable face, of Aldric's burning sincerity. Everyone reaching for the same prize. Everyone certain of their right to it.

And underneath all of it, at the bottom of everything, an old woman's decision, dressed up as duty.

"Grandmother." Noor heard her own voice crack on the word, and let it. "Did you know? When you sealed it—did you understand what you were doing?"

"I understood that I was afraid," Sable said. "I was young. Younger than you are now. My mother had just died holding the seal-charm, and the Court's magistrates were at our borders, and the Thornbound's grandfathers were burning granaries, and everyone I loved was going to die in a war over a thing none of us deserved to own. Sealing it was the only move that ended the killing *that day*. So I made the move that saved today, and I let tomorrow pay for it." She shook her head slowly. "That's the whole history of my generation in one sentence, girl. We saved today and billed tomorrow. And tomorrow turned out to be you."

* * *

Noor got up then—not because she wanted distance, but because her body demanded it, because grief and fury had filled her to the brim and stillness would have spilled her. She went to the window and pushed back the oiled leather and looked out at the pre-dawn dark, at the road she'd ridden in on, at the mountains beyond it where three armies were even now converging on a city built over a buried garden that belonged to no one.

She thought about forgiveness. She turned it over the way Kesh turned over a marked card, looking for the flaw, the trick, the angle.

Here was the thing she kept arriving at: she could not find it in herself to absolve Sable, and she could not find it in herself to condemn her. Both truths sat in her chest at once, uncomfortable roommates, refusing to resolve. Her grandmother had imprisoned a child—had imprisoned *generations* of children, had spent the family's soul like a drunkard spending wages—to prevent a war, and the prevention had worked, and the working had cost everything, and the cost had fallen on people who never got to choose.

Including her. Especially her.

"I don't forgive you," Noor said at last, still facing the window. "I want you to know that. I can't. Maybe someday, but not now, and I won't pretend otherwise just because you finally told the truth."

Behind her, silence. Then, quietly: "Good."

Noor turned. Sable was watching her with an expression Noor had never seen on that weathered face—something raw and relieved, like a woman who had braced for a blow and been granted, instead, honesty.

"If you'd forgiven me tonight," Sable said, "I'd know you'd forgiven the wrong thing. Forgive me in ten years, if I live, and if I've earned it. Tonight, what I need from you isn't forgiveness." She leaned forward, and the firelight caught the old seal-charm at her throat—the original, the one from the plan, the one Noor had only ever heard described in whispers. "It's your anger. Aimed properly."

"Aimed at what?"

"At the lie itself. Not mine—all of them. The Court's lie, that power is property. The Thornbound's lie, that suffering earns ownership. Aldric's lie, that bloodline is destiny. Mine, that safety justifies cages." Sable's voice had gone hard again, the blade-sharpened cadence of her letters, but aimed now at something worth cutting. "In three nights the thirteenth moon rises, and every liar in this kingdom will be standing over that garden with their hand out. And you, Noor—you'll be standing there too. The question isn't whether you forgive me. The question is whether you walk into that garden carrying any of our lies, or whether you finally carry something true."

* * *

They talked until the tea was reheated twice and the light through the oiled leather went from black to iron-gray. Sable told her the mechanics of the seal—the thirteenth turn, the wards' hunger, the way the charm could hold a door open but only living Fairweather blood could unmake the lock entirely. Noor told her about the catacombs, about Aldric at the threshold, about the half-burned map and Maren's copies and the message Kesh had left that only she could read. About Kesh himself. Sable listened to all of it with her eyes half-closed, and when Noor mentioned Kesh, something flickered across her grandmother's face that Noor chose, generously, to interpret as approval.

Near dawn, hoofbeats on the road.

Both of them were on their feet before the sound resolved—but it was one rider, one horse, blowing hard, and the rider wore no livery at all, which in the current kingdom meant either a spy or a friend, and often both. He swung down at the waystation's door and pounded on it, and when Noor opened it, he thrust a packet into her hands, gasping.

"From the city—from a man with a debt-ledger and a bad limp—he paid me everything he had, said you'd know what it meant—"

The packet held a scrap of paper, folded eight times. In Kesh's quick, slanted hand:

*Corvane marches tonight. Aldric rides with him—Ilsa doesn't know yet. Moon's in three. Come to the drowned chapel. Bring the old woman. Trust no one wearing gratitude.—K*

Noor read it twice. Then she looked up at her grandmother, who had read it over her shoulder the way she'd read over shoulders for seventy years, and whose expression had settled into something grim and calm and terribly awake.

"Well," Sable said, taking the seal-charm from around her neck and closing Noor's fingers around it, its metal warm from her skin. "It seems tomorrow has arrived early. Shall we go and give it our bill?"
