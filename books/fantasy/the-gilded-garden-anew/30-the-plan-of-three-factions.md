## 30. The Plan of Three Factions

The waystation smelled of tallow and old rain, and Noor had not slept in two days.

She sat at the corner table with her back to the wall—she always sat with her back to the wall now, a habit she'd acquired somewhere between the ambush on the coach road and the collapse of the catacombs, though she could not have named the exact day it took root—and she watched Sable read Ilsa's letter for the third time.

Her grandmother's hands were steady. They were always steady; forty years of tending dying orchards in a valley that forgot itself had burned every tremor out of them long ago. But Noor had learned, in these last weeks of speaking plainly with the woman who raised her, that steadiness was not the same as calm. Sable's stillness now was the stillness of a woman reading her own death warrant written in someone else's hand.

"Three moons," Sable said at last. She set the letter down on the scarred wood and smoothed it flat, as if neatness could change what it said. "The thirteenth full moon of the year. The Ember Court has called it a convocation of the realm's faithful. The Thornbound are calling it the Reckoning. And Aldric—" her voice caught, just slightly, on the name, "—Aldric will be there, because Corvane believes he serves the Crown, and because Aldric has never once in his life served anyone but himself."

"And us," Noor said. "What do they call us?"

Sable looked at her across the table. The waystation's single lamp threw shadows into the deep lines of her face, and for a moment Noor saw not the grandmother who had taught her to graft apple stock and recite the guild's founding litany, but a woman who had made a choice sixty years ago—a terrible, necessary, unforgivable choice—and had carried it alone through every winter since.

"They don't call us anything," Sable said. "That's rather the point of you, isn't it? You're the one thing none of their ledgers account for."

Outside, the wind moved through the bare trees along the road, and somewhere a nightbird called three times and fell silent. Noor thought about the sound of it—the ordinary sound of an ordinary world that did not know what was coming for it under the next full moon.

* * *

They had reached the waystation at dusk, Sable travel-worn and dust-caked from a journey no one in the Hollow had believed she would ever make, Kesh limping but grinning, Noor herself carrying the half-burned map wrapped in oilcloth against her ribs like a second heart. Ilsa's messenger had been waiting for them—a thin Thornbound girl with frostbitten ears and eyes too old for her face—who handed over the sealed letter, saluted with a fist against her chest, and vanished back down the dark road before Kesh could offer her soup.

The letter was brief. Ilsa wasted nothing, not even words.

*The Court marches from Vessaline in force. Corvane carries writs claiming the garden for the Crown by right of conquest. Your uncle rides beside him—we have confirmation from two sources inside the column. Our own people gather at the old battlefield above the catacombs' western mouth. The moon turns full in nine days. Whatever you intend, Fairweather, intend it soon.*

And beneath that, in a different ink, added later and less formally: *I meant what I said about the Hollow. That offer stands only if you stand with us. I am not a charitable woman.*

Noor had read that last line a dozen times. She heard Ilsa's voice in it—that low, certain voice that could make righteousness sound like arithmetic. And she understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that Ilsa's offer was genuine and Ilsa's offer was also a cage, and that both things were true in exactly the same proportion.

Now, hours later, with the lamp burning low and the messenger girl long gone, Noor laid the map on the table between them.

It was worse than it had been. The fire in the collapsing tunnel had taken its lower third entirely—the rivers and roads south of Vessaline reduced to a brown scorch-edge, like a coastline eroding into nothing. But the upper portion survived, and the upper portion was what mattered: the city rendered in the Fairweather cartographers' impossibly fine line-work, and beneath the city, in strokes so faint they seemed to rise out of the parchment itself rather than sit upon it, the descent. The drowned catacombs. The fissures where the thirteenth moon's light would fall. And at the bottom, drawn not as a place but almost as a face—a rounded shape, a suggestion of petals or fingers opening—the gate.

And beneath all of it, ghosted in handwriting Noor knew better than any living person's, because it was her own:

*The garden is not a treasure. It is a prisoner. Decide what you are willing to become before you open the door.*

She had written that—or someone had written it through her, or for her, before she could hold a quill—and she still did not know which truth frightened her more.

"The thirteenth moon," Sable said quietly. "Your great-great-grandmother chose it deliberately, when she laid the seal. The year has twelve moons in its common turning; the thirteenth comes only when the calendar corrects itself, once in a generation or two. A door that only opens on a night most years don't have." She touched the scorched edge of the map, gently, the way she touched dying trees. "We built our whole lives around making sure no one stood before that door on the wrong night."

"Well," Noor said. "Everyone's coming to the wrong night."

* * *

Kesh appeared sometime past midnight, materializing out of the waystation's back rooms with the unerring instinct of a man who could smell a serious conversation the way sharks smelled blood. He poured himself the dregs of the tea, made a face at it, drank it anyway, and dropped into the third chair without asking.

"So," he said. "Three armies, one hole in the ground, nine days." He ticked them off on his fingers. "The Crown, which wants to own it. The rebels, who want to own it while calling ownership a sin. And your charming uncle, who wants to own it while calling it family." He looked up at Noor, and beneath the theatrical weariness his eyes were sharp and worried. "Have I missed anyone?"

"The garden itself," Noor said.

Kesh blinked. Sable went very still.

"I've been thinking about what we saw," Noor said. "Down there. Before the tunnel came down." She had told them both already, in the exhausted aftermath—told them about the gate reading her blood, about the wards singing open, about Aldric's hands on her shoulders and the lie underneath his love. But she had not told them everything. She had not known how to say the rest until now.

"When I stood in front of the gate, it didn't feel like a door. It felt like a throat." Her own voice sounded strange to her, low and steady, as if someone else were saying it. "Something on the other side was pressing against it. Not pushing to get out—not exactly. More like... leaning. The way a starving person leans toward a window that smells of bread." She looked down at the map, at the little drawn shape of petals or fingers. "Everyone keeps talking about the garden like it's a vault full of gold. But it isn't. It's alive, and it's been shut in the dark for sixty years, and it is *hungry*, and every faction in this kingdom is planning to walk in and feed themselves to it."

"That's cheerful," Kesh said faintly.

"It's true," Sable said. Her voice had gone very quiet. "Noor's right. I've known it was true since the night we sealed it, and I let myself pretend otherwise for sixty years because the alternative was admitting what we'd done." She folded her hands on the table, and Noor saw that for the first time since the Hollow, they were not quite steady. "We didn't imprison a treasure, child. We imprisoned a living thing that belonged to no one, because we couldn't bear for it to belong to no one. And a caged power doesn't stay sweet. It sours. It turns inward. Everything the garden touches now, it touches with sixty years of rot behind it. Whoever claims that heart won't be crowned—they'll be consumed."

The lamp flickered. Somewhere in the walls, a mouse ran its small urgent errand, indifferent to the fate of kingdoms.

"So there it is," Noor said. "Corvane thinks he's taking a crown jewel. Ilsa thinks she's taking back what the warlords stole. Aldric thinks he's taking his inheritance." She pushed the map an inch toward the center of the table, toward both of them. "And they're all going to arrive at the same door on the same night, and fight each other for the privilege of walking into something that will eat them alive. Unless someone does something else first."

Kesh set down his cup very slowly. "Noor," he said, in the careful voice he used when he was about to talk someone out of something, a voice she had heard him deploy on drunk merchants and overconfident guardsmen, "you have that look. I've seen that look. That look cost me four days in a magistrate's holding cell."

"Open the gate," Noor said. "All the way. And then let it go."

Silence. The wind pressed at the shuttered windows and withdrew.

"Not claim it," she went on, and the words came faster now, tumbling out as if they had been waiting behind her teeth for days, maybe weeks, maybe her whole life. "Not re-seal it. Not give it to the Crown or the rebellion or the guild or anyone. Open the gate under the full moon and just—let the power out. All of it. Into the soil, the water, the roots, the air. Everywhere at once. Ownerless. Unownable." She spread her hands. "You can't steal a river after you've broken the dam. You can't crown yourself king of rain. If the garden's magic disperses into the whole land, then every army camped above Vessaline wakes up the next morning to find the prize they marched for scattered into the grass under their feet. Corvane's writs claim a treasure that no longer exists. Ilsa's revolution loses the weapon she was counting on. Aldric—" her throat tightened, and she made herself finish, "—Aldric gets nothing, because there's nothing left to inherit."

"And the Hollow?" Sable asked softly.

"The seal breaks either way, Gran. It's already failing—I'm the proof of that. The wards know my blood. In nine days, three armies break it for us if we do nothing. At least this way, it breaks on our terms instead of theirs." Noor met her grandmother's eyes. "And the valley gets what it never got. Magic. Real magic, rising wild, for the first time in sixty years. The orchards might actually live."

* * *

Kesh was the first to find his voice, and being Kesh, he spent it on logistics rather than sentiment—which Noor understood, and loved him a little for, because logistics was how he said *I'm terrified and I'm staying anyway*.

"All right. Say it works. Say you crack the gate and the power floods out across half the kingdom." He counted on his fingers again, but slower now. "One: you're standing at the center of it when it goes. What does sixty years of caged magic do to the girl standing in the doorway?"

"I don't know," Noor said.

"Wonderful. Two: the dispersal doesn't care whose side anyone's on. Wild magic everywhere means wild magic in Corvane's soldiers and Ilsa's rebels and every village between here and the coast. Some of it will land well. Some of it won't. There will be chaos."

"There will be chaos anyway," Sable said. "Three armies colliding underground, beneath a city built on ruins, fighting over a hungry god. What Noor proposes is the difference between a catastrophe with a purpose and one without."

"Three," Kesh said, and here his voice lost its performance entirely, went quiet and real. "Once it's done, it's done forever. No taking it back. No re-sealing. No second thirteenth moon. Every faction in this kingdom will know exactly who did it and exactly where she sleeps." He looked at her. "You'll never be invisible again, Noor. Not anywhere. Not ever. You understand that? The valley kept you safe by forgetting you. This plan makes you the most famous person alive."

Noor sat with that. Outside, the nightbird called again—two notes this time, questioning—and the wind combed through the bare trees, and she thought of the Hollow, of the boundary stones, of the orchards she had tended row by row while her heart beat itself raw against the inside of her life. She had wanted, once, so simply and so desperately, to matter. To be seen. She had imagined it as a kind of warmth.

It was not warmth. It was exposure. It was standing in an open field with every hunter in the kingdom knowing her name.

But the alternative was letting Corvane or Ilsa or Aldric take the garden's rotten heart and remake the world around their hunger. And she had seen enough of hungers dressed as crowns, as revolutions, as family, to know that she would rather burn the prize than feed another one.

"Yes," she said. "I understand."

"She understands," Kesh said to Sable, half-laughing, half-mourning. "Of course she understands. Valley girls shouldn't understand things like that." He dragged a hand down his face. "Fine. Fine. Then we need three things, and we need them impossibly fast. We need to reach the western mouth of the catacombs before the armies close the roads. We need Ilsa not to kill us on sight for refusing her bargain. And we need—" he gestured at the map, at the scorched lower third, "—the rest of the route, which is currently ash. Does your grandmother's famous memory extend to tunnels she helped dig sixty years ago?"

Sable did not answer immediately. She was looking at Noor with an expression Noor had never seen on her before—or rather, an expression she had seen a thousand times and never understood until this moment: the look Sable wore when she walked the dead rows of the orchards in late autumn, cataloguing what could still be saved.

"You'll need the seal-charm," Sable said at last. "The original. The one your great-great-grandmother used to lay the seal." She reached into the collar of her traveling coat and drew out a cord, and on the cord a small object that Noor had glimpsed before and never asked about—a disc of pale metal, worn smooth, etched with thirteen interlocking rings. "It won't open the gate. Only your blood does that. But it will steady the wards while you work, keep them from closing on you halfway through. And it will mark you, to anything down there that remembers the guild, as..." She paused. "As the heir. As the one with the right to end what we began."

Noor reached out and took it. The metal was warm—warmer than it should have been, warmer than the room—and it hummed faintly against her palm, a vibration she felt in her teeth, in the roots of her teeth, in the place behind her sternum where the map's ghost-handwriting seemed to live.

It felt like being recognized.

"Gran," she said. "When I open it. When the power goes out. What happens to me?"

Sable held her gaze, and did not look away, and did not soften it, because that was the kind of love she had to give—love that refused to lie even now, especially now.

"I don't know," she said. "But I will be standing beside you when we find out."

* * *

Dawn came gray and thin through the shutters. Noor stood at the waystation's door with the seal-charm against her chest and the burned map rolled tight in her fist, watching the light come up over the eastern hills—the direction of Vessaline, the direction of the gathering armies, the direction of the door in the dark.

Nine days.

Behind her, Kesh was arguing cheerfully with the waystation's keeper about horses, deploying charm and counterfeit coinage in roughly equal measure. Beside her, Sable stood silent, an old woman in a borrowed coat looking at the horizon she had avoided for forty years.

"Three factions," Noor said quietly, mostly to herself. "Let them all come. Let them all watch. Let them learn what it looks like when nobody wins."

The wind rose, carrying the smell of thaw and far-off rain, and far to the east, beneath the hills, beneath the drowned catacombs, beneath the gate that knew her name, something ancient shifted in its sleep—and waited for the thirteenth moon.
