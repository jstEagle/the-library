## 31. Storm Before the Moon

The wind changed in the night, and every living thing above Vessaline felt it.

It came down off the northern ridges carrying the smell of cold iron and cut grass and something older beneath both—the scent of a door that had been shut for a hundred years beginning, very slowly, to remember it was a door. The horses screamed at it. The dogs went silent. In the tent cities spreading across the high meadows like mold across bread, forty thousand soldiers woke without knowing why and lay staring at the canvas, listening to a hum that was not thunder, not bees, not anything with a name.

Kesh woke because someone was trying to kill him.

* * *

He had spent nine days in a cell that smelled of wet stone and his own fear, and he had spent those nine days doing what he did best: talking. To the guard who brought his slop—about dice, about a horse race two summers past, about the guard's brother-in-law's failing tannery until the man forgot he was speaking to a prisoner and started speaking to a friend. To the sergeant of the watch, whose gambling debts Kesh mapped from three careless complaints and one boast. To the wall itself, some nights, when there was nothing else.

Every conversation had been a brick. Tonight, the storm of armies gathering on the ridge had pulled half the garrison up to the walls to watch the Ember Court's banners arrive by torchlight, and the bricks came down all at once.

The guard—Tamlo, with the tannery brother-in-law, with the bad knee he favored on the left—had accepted a wager earlier that evening. A friendly one. A locked-cell challenge, man against lock, because Tamlo liked Kesh the way you like a stray dog that does tricks. The pick Kesh had palmed from the tray three days ago, filed smooth against the flagstone during the hours when no one listened, slid into the mechanism like it had been born there.

The lock opened on the second try. Kesh allowed himself four seconds of trembling gratitude—he was not brave, he had never been brave, he was merely thorough—and then he was moving.

The corridor beyond was empty. Above him, through the arrow slits, the sky flickered green-white, and the hum in the air had grown teeth; he could feel it in his fillings, in the scar on his palm, in the small animal part of the brain that remembered being prey. Something vast was waking under the city, and everyone on the surface could feel it, and everyone on the surface was looking *up* instead of *down*.

Which suited Kesh fine.

He had left a message for Noor on the underside of his bunk board, scratched in the shorthand they'd invented on the road, the one built from her grandmother's guild-cipher and his own card-sharp's notation. *Thirteenth turn. Water remembers. Don't wait for me.* He didn't know if she'd see it before they moved her—Ilsa would move her soon, the rebels were marching too, anyone with eyes could see that—but the message was there, and it was true, and truth was the only currency he'd ever carried that never devalued.

He was three corridors from freedom when he heard the argument.

Two voices, low and furious, behind a door ajar. One belonged to Magistrate Corvane; Kesh knew that voice now the way you know a toothache. The other—

"You serve the Crown," Corvane was saying, "and yet you prepare a ritual in my city that binds nothing to the Crown."

"I prepare a ritual," said Aldric Fairweather, "that binds everything to *me*. The distinction should comfort you. It doesn't bind anything to you at all."

Silence. Then Corvane, very quietly: "I could have you killed."

"You could have tried. You'd fail, and then you'd have lost your only map to the gate, and the girl besides. Sit down, Magistrate. Pour yourself something. We are allies of convenience, and convenience is about to become the most valuable substance in this kingdom."

Kesh pressed himself into an alcove, heart hammering, and understood three things at once. That Aldric had turned Corvane's own leash into a collar. That the ritual Aldric meant to work would not free the garden but *own* it. And that Noor, walking willingly toward that gate with her uncle's promises in her ears, was walking toward the worst kind of trap—the kind built out of love.

He waited for the footsteps to fade, and then he ran—not away, as any sensible man would have run, but down. Toward the catacombs. Toward the message he had to get past Ilsa's pickets and into Noor's hands before the moon rose full and thirteenth and everything ended or began.

The sensible thing, he reflected as he dropped through a drainage grate into the dark, would have been to save himself.

He was getting tired of the sensible thing.

* * *

Aldric stood alone in the drowned chapel beneath the Court's temporary quarters, and let himself feel, for exactly one minute, the weight of what he was about to do.

Then the minute passed, as minutes do, and he went back to work.

The chapel had been a place of worship once, before the war, before the city grew over its own dead like a tree growing over a fence. Now it was a shell of salt-stained stone where the floodwaters of some ancient spring still crept and withdrew, and the walls wept mineral tears, and the thirteenth moon's light—when it came, in a few hours—would fall through a fissure in the vaulting onto a floor where Aldric had spent six nights drawing a circle in silver and ash and his own blood.

The circle was beautiful. He could admit that. His sister Sable had always been the scholar of the family, but Aldric had the craftsman's hand, and the geometry he had laid down was a masterwork: seven concentric rings, each inscribed with a binding drawn from the guild's oldest records—the records Sable thought she had burned, the records Aldric had copied as a boy while she slept, because even at eleven years old he had known that a woman who burns her history is a woman planning to lie about it.

At the circle's center, waiting, stood a vessel of black glass no larger than a lantern.

That was the whole secret of it. Everyone—Corvane, Ilsa, the Thornbound, the girl herself—imagined that claiming the garden's heart meant standing in it, breathing it, letting it pour through you like light through a window. Amateurs. The heart of the garden was a wellspring, and a wellspring cannot be held in cupped hands; it can only be *redirected*. Seven rings of binding, a vessel of glass quenched in the blood of the bloodline, and the entire power of the sealed world would flow—not into the land, not into the factions, not into the girl who fancied herself its key—but into the vessel. Into him. Portable. Containable. *His.*

He did not think of it as theft. That was the word Sable would use, and Sable had never once in her life used a clean mirror. What had their family done, all those generations in the Hollow? They had *hoarded*. They had sealed a wonder of the world behind a valley's worth of forgetting because they feared what men would do with it—and in fearing it, they had starved it, poisoned it, made it mad. Aldric had seen the wards' edges fraying. He had read the tremors in the deep places. The garden was dying in its cage, and when it died, its power would die with it, and the world would be poorer forever because one frightened old woman in a dying orchard couldn't imagine a future braver than a padlock.

He would be the brave one. Someone had to be.

And Noor—Noor was the tragedy in all of it, and he did not pretend otherwise. The wards wanted her. Blood of the prime line, young, unmarked by the seal's long leaching. She would open the gate as naturally as breath. And then the bindings would take her gift and route it through the glass, and she would live—he had designed it so, he had *insisted* on designing it so—she would live, hollowed of the power but alive, and in time perhaps she would understand that he had saved the garden from her mercy and saved her from her own goodness, which in this kingdom was the most fatal quality a person could possess.

"She'll hate me," he said aloud, to the weeping walls.

The walls, being walls, said nothing. But far overhead, through the fissure, the first thin edge of the rising moon touched the water at his feet, and the reflection came back to him doubled, and tripled, and wrong—a dozen Aldrics looking up out of the dark, and every one of them, he noticed, wearing the expression of a man who has already decided.

He lit the seven candles. He began to chant.

* * *

On the eastern ridge, in a camp that smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and defiance, Ilsa of the Thornbound sharpened a blade that did not need sharpening, because her hands needed something to do.

Below her, Vessaline glowed like a coal banked in ash. Beyond it, on the western heights, the Ember Court's encampment blazed with a thousand torches—Corvane had stopped pretending subtlety mattered; the moon was tomorrow night and everyone knew it. Between the two, in the valley of the river, the ordinary people were locking their doors and putting out their lamps and hoping, in the way ordinary people always hope, that history would be kind enough to happen somewhere else.

It wouldn't. Ilsa had learned that lesson in a village called Brackenmere, where the Court's quartermasters had computed that a starving district was cheaper than a fed one, and where she had buried her brother among forty-one others in ground too frozen to dig properly. Everything she had done since—the rebellion, the ambushes, the hard arithmetic of lives weighed against lives—grew out of that frozen ground. She told herself so every morning. Some mornings it helped.

"Arrows are fletched," said Doss, her second, dropping onto the rock beside her. "Pikes distributed. The sappers say the catacomb entrance off Tanner's Row can be blown wide inside an hour of the signal." He paused. "The girl's asking for you."

Noor Fairweather. Ilsa had kept her close for a month now, sheltered and watched in equal measure, and the balance between those two things grew harder to hold every day. The girl was leverage—that was the honest word, and Ilsa made herself use it. Leverage over the Court, which wanted her found. Leverage over the seal itself, which answered to her blood. But somewhere in the last weeks the leverage had developed a spine and a stubborn jaw and a way of looking at Ilsa that made her feel like a ledger being audited, and Ilsa had begun, dangerously, to *like* her.

"The Hollow," Noor had said to her, three nights ago, in the flat voice of someone reciting terms. "You promised to shield it. I want it in writing. Guild cipher, witnessed, unbreakable. If I'm going to open that gate for you, I want to know the valley survives regardless of how this ends."

Regardless of how this ends. As if she already suspected there were endings Ilsa wasn't showing her. The girl was her grandmother's granddaughter after all.

"Send her in," Ilsa said.

Noor came up the trail with her cropped hair damp from the river mist and Kesh's old cloak around her shoulders, and she looked, Ilsa thought, nothing like the frightened courier-chaser who had stumbled into their camp a month ago. She looked like weather coming.

"You felt it too," Noor said, without greeting. "The hum."

"The whole ridge feels it. My sentries are jumping at shadows."

"It's stronger than it was a week ago. The seal is failing faster now that—" Noor stopped. Chose again. "Now that I've been near it twice. I'm the key, Ilsa. Every step I take toward that gate, the lock gets looser. Which means whatever happens tomorrow night, it won't wait politely for our plan. If Aldric moves early, if Corvane moves early, if the wards simply give way—we need to be underground before moonrise. Not at moonrise. Before."

"And your uncle?"

Something crossed the girl's face, quick and painful, like a bird hitting a window. "My uncle is a man who mistakes possession for rescue," she said. "I know that now. I should have known it at the gate." She looked out at the Court's distant fires. "Ilsa. When we go down tomorrow—I go first. Not your sappers, not your vanguard. Me. The wards will let me through and grind anyone else to paste, and if we're racing Aldric, seconds matter."

"That's exactly what a spy would volunteer."

"I know." Noor almost smiled; it was a tired, old-young smile. "That's what makes it a real offer. A liar would ask to stay safe in the rear and swear loyalty. I'm asking you to put me at the point of the spear because it's the only place the spear works." She turned, and her eyes in the torchlight were steady and grief-colored. "One more thing. Have you heard anything from your people in the city? About a prisoner who escaped the Court cells tonight? Small man. Talks too much. Insufferable grin."

Ilsa went very still. "Why?"

"Because if Kesh got out," Noor said quietly, "he didn't run. Whatever he learned in that cell, he'll be trying to bring it to me. And if he brings it to me, it will be the truth, dressed in jokes, arriving late—which is the only way truth ever arrives in my life." She pulled the cloak tighter. "Find him before the Court does. Please. He's the only person in this whole war who's never wanted anything from me except my company, and I miss him like a missing rib."

She walked back down the trail. Ilsa watched her go, and felt the old arithmetic shift and slide in her chest, the numbers refusing to sit still the way they had since Brackenmere.

"Doss," she said at last. "Double the pickets on the river road. Anyone comes up from the city tonight—anyone—they're brought to me alive and unbloodied." She stood, and rolled the blade into its sheath, and looked east where the moon would rise. "And wake the smiths. If the girl goes first tomorrow, she goes armed."

* * *

Far below them all, in the deepest gallery of the drowned catacombs where the water hung suspended in midair like slow silver birds, the gate waited.

It had waited a hundred years. It could feel the moon climbing toward its thirteenth fullness; it could feel the small warm presences scurrying on the surface of the world like insects on a drumhead; and most of all it could feel *her*, the young blood, the key, drawing nearer through the dark with each passing hour, and it strained against its bindings the way a flower strains against frost—not toward freedom, for it had forgotten what freedom was, but toward the one thing it remembered:

It was starving.

And it knew, in the blind vegetable way it knew anything, that tomorrow night, someone was finally coming to feed it.

Above, the moon cleared the ridge. Below, three armies held their breath. And in a drainage tunnel beneath Tanner's Row, a small man with an insufferable grin picked himself up out of the muck, wiped the grate-mud from his eyes, and began, at a dead run, to keep a promise.
