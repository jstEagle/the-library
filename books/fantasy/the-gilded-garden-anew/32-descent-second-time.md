## 32. Descent, Second Time

The moon was not yet risen when they went down, and that was the whole of the plan: to be beneath the earth before the thirteenth moon found them.

Noor stood at the mouth of the drowned catacombs where the river Vessa slid black and patient between leaning walls of old brick, and she thought about how many times a person could descend into the same dark before it became a kind of home. Twice now for her. The first time she had come as a girl playing at being a key; this time she came knowing exactly what the lock wanted from her, and knowing, too, that she meant to break it rather than turn it.

"You're stalling," Kesh said softly at her shoulder. He was soaked to the waist already, having waded out to test the depth, and his teeth were chattering in a way he clearly hoped was charming and was not. "I've seen you face down a magistrate, an ambush, your uncle, and my creditors—gods rest their merciless souls—and you've never once looked like this."

"Like what?"

"Like someone reading the last page of a book first."

Noor almost smiled. Almost. Behind them, up the slope through the tangle of ruined gardens that had given the city its name, the sounds of gathering armies carried down on the night wind: the creak of siege lines being drawn, the low animal murmur of thousands of people who had all come to want the same impossible thing. Above the eastern ridge, the sky had gone the color of a bruise deciding whether to bloom.

"We have until moonrise," Sable said.

Her grandmother stood a little apart, wrapped in a traveler's oilcloth that did nothing to disguise how small she had become on the road. Forty years inside the Hollow, and then four days of hard riding to reach this place—and still Sable Fairweather held herself like a woman presiding over a vigil, spine straight, hands folded, as if the whole world were simply an orchard that had gotten out of hand.

"The wards will be waking," Sable went on. "Everything your ancestors built wakes with the thirteenth moon. That's why they chose tonight, all of them—the court, the rebels, Aldric. The gate can only be opened under this light. And so it can only be *unmade* under this light." She turned her eyes to Noor, and in the last grey light before darkness Noor saw something in her grandmother's face she had never seen there: fear, worn openly, like a ring too valuable to hide. "Once we go down, there is no version of this night that ends quietly. Whatever happens below, the world above will feel it."

"I know," Noor said.

"Do you?" Sable's voice gentled. "Because I have spent fifty years telling myself I understood what we did to that garden, and I was wrong every single time. Understand it now, child, while you still can: you are not going down there to win. You are going down there to *let go*. And letting go is the one thing no Fairweather has ever managed gracefully."

Kesh snorted. "Speak for yourself. I let go of money constantly."

"*Kesh.*"

"He's right to laugh," Noor said, surprising herself. She pulled the half-burned map from inside her coat—the courier's map, scorched at the corner where the tunnel collapse had taken it, its ghosted handwriting still faintly visible when the light caught it right, in a hand that was hers before she could hold a pen. "If I don't laugh I'll do what I did the first time I came down here. Freeze. Stand in front of the gate like a lamb waiting to be told which way the knife goes." She folded the map away. "Not this time. This time I know what I am. Let's go."

She waded into the river first.

* * *

The water was colder than memory. It always was. Memory softened everything except the things you needed sharp.

They moved through the flooded galleries by Kesh's lantern, hooded against the drip and echo, following the map's thirteenth turn—the spiral stair that descended past the level of any cellar, past the level of sense, into corridors where the brick gave way to living stone veined with pale mineral light. Noor remembered the route in her body now, the way she remembered the orchard rows of home: left at the weeping fissure, down the stair of ninety-one steps, along the gallery where the ceiling hung so low that even Kesh had to stoop, and he complained about it in three languages, two of which Noor was fairly sure he'd invented.

The thirteenth moon's light reached them even here. That was the miracle and the horror of these depths: thin threads of silver fell through fissures in the rock far overhead, threading down through fathoms of stone as if stone were water, pooling on the floors in coins of light. Under that light, the seal's presence was everywhere—in the faint pressure against the ears, like descending into deep water; in the way their breath fogged and hung unmoving; in the hair-thin lines of gold that ran through the walls, the veins of the wardwork, pulsing slow as a sleeping pulse.

And around Noor, the gold veins brightened as she passed.

It had been like this the first time, but she had not known what it meant. Now she walked through the catacombs trailing light like a bride trails a veil, and every step made the seal sit up a little more in its long sleep, and every step made her more certain of the thing she had proposed to Sable on the road: the gate would open for her whether she willed it or not. The only choice left to her was what happened after.

"You're doing it again," Kesh murmured. He had drifted close without seeming to move, the way he did, and his voice was stripped of all its performance. "The freezing-in-advance thing."

"I'm preparing."

"You're rehearsing grief. There's a difference." He glanced back at Sable, some twenty paces behind, walking alone in her own small circle of moonlight. "Talk to her. Before we get wherever we're going. Trust me—I've sold enough last chances to know they don't keep."

"She's my grandmother, not a debt."

"All the worse. Debts forgive easily."

But he was right, and Noor knew it, and so at the next landing—a wide chamber where an underground stream crossed their path over stepping stones worn glassy-smooth—she waited for Sable and offered her grandmother a hand across the water.

Sable took it. Her grip was stronger than her frame suggested, orchard-woman's grip, rope-and-root grip, and for a moment they simply stood together on the middle stone with the black water sliding past their ankles and the moonlight falling through the rock above them like rain that had forgotten how to land.

"When I was small," Noor said, "you told me the story of the garden. The one you always told at vigil. The guild kept the valley safe, and the valley kept the guild, and everyone lived dutifully ever after."

"I remember."

"It wasn't true. Any of it."

"No," Sable said. "None of it. I'm sorry. I've been sorry for longer than you've been alive." She looked up—not at Noor, at the faint silver threads overhead, as if she could see through them to the moon climbing toward its zenith. "Your grandfather used to say a lie told often enough becomes a kind of architecture. You build rooms in it. You live in it. You raise children in it." Her thumb pressed once, hard, against Noor's knuckles. "I built you a house of lies because it was the only house I had. Forgive me the house. Not the lies. The lies I'd tell again."

"That's the most honest thing you've ever said to me."

"Don't get used to it. Honesty is exhausting. I don't know how you young people manage it daily." But her voice cracked on the joke, just slightly, and Noor pretended not to hear, because that too was inheritance—Fairweathers protecting each other from the sound of each other breaking.

They crossed the stream. The gallery narrowed beyond it, and the golden veins in the walls thickened, converging, and Noor felt the pull begin—that gentle, insistent drawing toward the gate, like a tide taking hold of a swimmer. The wards knew she was coming. They had always known. Somewhere below, behind stone that had not been moved in a century, something vast and starving had begun, very slowly, to turn toward the sound of her footsteps.

* * *

The antechamber of the gate was a dome of black stone, and it was full of the thirteenth moon.

There was no other way to describe it. Light poured through the crown of the dome in a single broad column, impossibly, impossibly silver, and filled the round room the way water fills a cup. Dust motes hung in it unfalling. The air tasted of green things—of sap and cut grass and rain on warm soil—which was obscene, this far under the earth, and heartbreaking, because Noor knew that smell. It was the smell of the Hollow before the blight. It was the smell of everything the seal had been slowly strangling for a hundred years.

And there, across the chamber, in the wall opposite: the gate.

It was not a door, quite. It was a seam—a vertical line of gold light in the black stone, tall as ten men, thin as a blade, humming at a pitch Noor felt in her back teeth and the roots of her hair. The wardwork around it was dense beyond reckoning, spirals within spirals of engraved script, and every character of it glowed, and every glow bent subtly toward Noor as she entered, the way flowers bend toward a window.

"Oh," Kesh said quietly. All his languages seemed to have failed him at once. "Oh, that's—yes. That's worth every terrible decision I've ever made, and I've made *so many*."

"Stay near the wall," Noor said. "When it opens, stay near the wall."

"And you?"

"I won't be near the wall."

Sable stepped forward into the column of moonlight, and the change in the chamber was immediate and terrible.

The gate *sang*. There was no softer word for it. The hum rose through octaves into something almost melodic, almost joyful, and the gold script flared so bright that shadows leapt and spun across the dome, and every line of light in the room reached toward Sable Fairweather like stems toward the sun.

For one heartbeat, Noor thought: *it knows her. It will open for her instead. She can carry this, not me.*

Then the song broke.

It happened the way a note breaks when a string is wound too tight—one instant of unbearable tension, then a sound like a bell cracking, and the light around Sable curdled from gold to a sickly amber, and the great seam in the wall flickered like a guttering lamp. Sable cried out—not loudly, but from somewhere deep, somewhere old—and staggered, and Noor caught her arm as the ward-light recoiled from her grandmother's skin like a hand pulling back from a stove.

"What—" Kesh started forward. "What happened? What did it *do* to her?"

"Nothing," Sable gasped. She was laughing, actually, a thin shocked laugh, even as she leaned her weight into Noor's side. "Nothing. It only... looked at me. All the way through. Fifty years of me, in one glance." She lifted her head, and Noor saw tears cutting tracks through the dust on her grandmother's face, and saw, beneath the tears, a kind of terrible relief. "It read my blood, child. Like it read yours at the boundary stones. And it found the bloodline—but it found it *spent*. Wound down. A tree that has gone to heartwood." She touched her own sternum, wonderingly. "I aged out of it. Do you understand? The wards were keyed to the bloodline in its prime. To a Fairweather young enough to bear what the gate asks. I could have opened it forty years ago. Perhaps thirty. Now..."

"Now there's only me," Noor said.

The words came out flat, and they landed in the chamber and echoed off the dome, and the gate hummed agreement, low and patient, a lock acknowledging its key.

Sable straightened, and took Noor's face in both hands, and her palms smelled of the road and of the seal-charm she wore against her heart, the original charm, warm as a living thing.

"Yes," she said. "Only you. That is the truth of inheritance, Noor—it is never the whole estate passed down. It is always the part nobody wanted. My mother handed me the sealing and kept the guilt; I handed you the keeping and called it safety. Every generation passes the burden wrapped in prettier paper." Her thumbs brushed Noor's cheekbones, fierce and gentle at once. "I cannot do this for you. I cannot even walk beside you through that door. All I can do is what I should have done fifty years ago—stand guard behind you while you undo what I helped make."

"Sable," Kesh said, and Noor heard it, the bareness in his voice, the absence of any performance at all. He was looking at the gate, and at Noor, and at the column of moonlight that was brightening by slow degrees as the moon climbed toward zenith above the city. "How long do we have?"

Sable tilted her head, listening to something none of them could hear. "The moon crowns within the hour. When it does, the gate opens fully—for her, or for whoever stands closest in her blood. And we are not alone down here anymore." She nodded toward the gallery they had come by, where, far off, faint as a dream of sound, boots were echoing on wet stone. Many boots. More than one army's worth.

"They followed us down," Noor said.

"Everyone follows everyone down," Kesh said grimly. "That's rather the theme of the evening."

Above them, muffled through a hundred feet of stone, the first horns began to sound.

* * *

Noor walked to the gate alone.

Behind her, Sable took up position at the chamber's entrance with the seal-charm bared in her fist, an old woman planting herself in a doorway like an orchard tree planted against the wind. Kesh hovered at the edge of the moonlight, wanting to follow and knowing better, his hands opening and closing at his sides as though he could hold her there by force of wanting to.

The gold seam hummed as she approached, and the light of it lay across her skin like a hand learning her face. Closer, she could see through the seam's thin blaze—to green. A suggestion of green. Leaves, maybe, or the idea of leaves, moving behind the light the way fish move behind ice.

*You're starving,* she thought at it, at the garden, at the power coiled behind the door. *They locked you up to own you, and owning you has killed you by inches, and every faction in the kingdom is racing down here to fight over the corpse of a thing they murdered with their wanting.* She raised her hand to the seam of light. It was warm. Of course it was warm. Everything alive was warm. *I'm not here to claim you. I'm not here to re-seal you. I'm here to open the cage and let you go to seed.*

The gate pulsed once, like a heartbeat stumbling.

And then, from the gallery behind them—closer now, much closer—came a voice Noor knew, a voice that had lived in her nightmares since a tunnel collapsed, calling her name with all the warmth of family and all the coldness of ownership braided together:

"Noor! Wait—it isn't what you think—"

Aldric.

The moon crowned. The gate began, slowly, joyfully, terribly, to open.
