## 34. Aldric at the Heart

The garden's light reached Aldric before its beauty did.

He came up the last stretch of catacomb with Corvane's seal still warm in his pocket—a thing he had stolen an hour ago from a dead man's courier satchel, along with the magistrate's own marching orders—and the light came down through the fissures in pale gold threads, catching the water that dripped from the drowned arches overhead and turning each drop into a small falling star. Twenty years he had imagined this place. In Vhalen's dungeons, in the Ember Court's gilded antechambers where he had knelt and lied and smiled, in the border camps where rebels burned his name into effigy—he had carried the garden inside him like a swallowed coal, and now, at last, it was burning through his skin.

The wards parted for him without a whisper.

That was the thing no one understood, the thing Sable had never confessed and Noor could not yet know: the gate did not open for blood alone. It opened for *want*. The Fairweather blood was the key, but the lock tested what turned it—tested hunger, tested need, tested the shape of a soul against the shape of the door. His sister had walked these tunnels forty years ago and been turned back, because some buried part of her had wanted to walk away more than she wanted to enter. Aldric had spent two decades honing his wanting to a single unblunted edge.

The gate stood open ahead of him. Of course it did. Noor had passed through, and where a Fairweather of the true line went, the way stayed soft.

He stepped into the garden and stopped breathing.

* * *

It should have been beautiful. It was beautiful—the way a drowning woman is beautiful, the way fever-bright eyes are beautiful. Roses climbed trellises of white stone in colors that had no names outside these walls, blues like deep ice, violets like bruised dusk, a red so red it seemed to bleed light rather than reflect it. Fruit hung heavy on trees that bore blossom and ripe harvest in the same instant. A fountain sang somewhere he couldn't see, water over marble, water over silver, water over something older than either. The air tasted of honey and rain and, beneath both, faintly, of ash.

And everything was *straining*. That was the truth the beauty hid, the truth he felt against his skin like a change in pressure before a storm. Every vine reached. Every root gripped. Every petal strained toward the center of the garden, toward the heart of it, where the source sat caged in its lattice of old enchantment—a knot of light so dense it made his teeth ache, so bright he could not look at it directly, wound round and round with spells laid down by hands three generations dead. His mother's hands among them. Sable's hands.

They had built the cage well. They had also built it around a living thing.

Aldric walked the central path slowly, boots silent on moss that curled toward his heels as he passed, drinking at his warmth the way a starving dog drinks at a bowl. He could feel it noticing him. The whole garden noticed him—leaned toward him, a field of green faces turned to the sun of his intent. When he had imagined this moment, he had imagined triumph. What he felt instead was closer to grief, and he hated himself a little for that. Grief was a luxury. Grief was what happened to men who hesitated.

He heard her before he saw her.

She was standing at the edge of the heart-lattice with one hand raised—not touching it, not yet, but close enough that the light bent toward her fingers like iron filings toward a lodestone. Her hair was short now, hacked off at the jaw; Kesh's grey cloak hung from her shoulders; and there was blood on her sleeve, dried brown, someone else's or her own. She looked thinner than the girl in Sable's letters, harder than the girl he'd watched from across a dungeon floor. But when she turned at the sound of his step, the face that met his was his sister's face, young again, and for one unguarded heartbeat Aldric Fairweather felt twenty years fall away like scales.

"Noor," he said.

"You betrayed Corvane." Not a question. She had seen the aftermath, then—or heard it, the shouts and collapse echoing down the tunnels above, the magistrate's column tearing itself apart on the false intelligence Aldric had fed them all.

"I betrayed everyone," Aldric said. "It's the only honest thing I've done in years."

"And you came here." Her hand lowered from the lattice but did not leave it far. "To the heart."

"To you," he said, and found, to his own surprise, that it was not entirely a lie.

* * *

They faced each other across thirty feet of impossible roses, uncle and niece, the last two Fairweathers under the sky who were not old enough to remember why that mattered.

"Your grandmother sent letters," Noor said. "I've read every word she ever sent me. I know what she says you are."

"A thief. A coward. A man who tried to steal the map and got our people killed." Aldric nodded, unhurried. "All true, in its way. Do you know what else is true? She sealed this place with the power that could have ended the famine in Vessarin Province. Could have healed the blight that took the western orchards—the same blight that killed your father's father. She chose a cage over a cup, because a cage could be kept, and a cup gets drunk by strangers." He took a step forward. The vines did not flinch from him. "I have never disagreed with her about what the garden is worth, Noor. Only about who should hold it."

"Anyone," Noor said quietly. "That's my answer already. You know it is. I'm not here to hold it."

"You're here to destroy it." He said it gently, the way one corrects a child's arithmetic. "Or scatter it. Let it go to seed—I've heard you say it to the rebel woman through walls thinner than she thinks. And I understand it. Truly. You've spent your life in a valley that forgets, starving for magic, and now the first taste of it has been armies and hunters and men selling your name in taverns. Of course you want it gone. You want the world to be safe from it." Another step. Ten feet now. The heart-light pulsed between them, slow as a sleeping pulse. "But look at it, Noor. Really look."

She didn't look away from him. Stubborn. Sable's stubbornness, and their mother's before that, and his own—he recognized the set of her jaw the way a man recognizes his own reflection in warped glass.

"It's dying," he said. "Caged, it curdles. Scattered—" he spread his hands, and let the sorrow show, because the sorrow was real, that was the monstrous joke of it, every word he meant— "scattered, it thins until it's nothing. Rain on a field. Pretty stories in another hundred years. But *held*—" his voice caught, and he let it catch, because the catch was true too— "held, tended, *shared rightly*, it could remake everything. The plagues. The famines. The courts that grind the poor to powder because they've never known anything but scarcity. All of it ends. One garden, kept by people who learned what hoarding costs. Our family paid that price, Noor. Who better to hold the cure?"

"The Thornbound would say the same," she said. "So would Corvane. So would anyone."

"Yes," Aldric said. "And they're wrong, because they want it for themselves. I'm right, because I want it for *us*." He laughed, short and bitter, and gestured at the strangling splendor around them. "Do you think I don't hear it? How it sounds? I've rehearsed this speech in a cell for twenty years and it always sounded noble there. Here it sounds like what it is. But hear the bones of it, at least. Hear the part that isn't rot."

He closed the last distance between them, close enough now to see the exhaustion carved under her eyes, the burn-scar on her palm from the tunnel collapse, the map's charred corner peeking from her coat like a black tongue.

"I was nineteen when I left the Hollow," he said. "Nineteen, sneaking out past the boundary stones to see if the world hummed the way the old songs said. It does, by the way. It hums. And I thought—if we have this, if the guild once held the greatest working ever wrought, then we are not nothing, we are not a valley that forgets, we are *keepers*. And then I learned what being a keeper meant. My own mother locking the door and swallowing the key. Your grandmother letting the guild rot rather than spend one drop of what she guarded." His hands had become fists without his permission. "I did terrible things to get free of that. I'll answer for them. But I never stopped believing the same thing I believed at nineteen: that we were meant for this. Not the courts. Not the rebels. *Us.*"

"Noor," he said, softer now, and took her hand—the scarred one, the burned one, and she let him, which was either trust or trap and he could not tell which and did not care—and pressed it toward the light. "Come with me. Not behind me. Beside me. Two Fairweathers at the heart, the way it should have been from the beginning. We end the famine. We break the courts' monopoly on power—not by giving the garden to the mob, but by making scarcity itself a memory. And when we've done it, when the world is fed and healed, then—" his voice cracked clean through— "then we go home. To the Hollow. With the orchards green and the valley singing, and Sable alive long enough to see she was wrong. That's all I've ever wanted. That's the whole of it. I swear it on Mother's grave, on Father's, on every name in the guild rolls."

The garden leaned in around them, listening. The heart pulsed. Somewhere far above, muffled through fathoms of stone, the thirteenth moon was rising, and armies were moving, and none of it mattered as much as the next ten seconds.

Noor looked at their joined hands.

"Aldric," she said, and her voice was very quiet and very steady, "when you pressed my hand toward the light just now—did you feel it pull?"

He had. The lattice had tugged at her like a hook in a fish's mouth, hungry, eager, and he had felt it through her skin and called it destiny.

"It knows us," he said. "It's waited a hundred years for us."

"No." She withdrew her hand, gently, terribly gently. "It knows what it can use. There's a difference, Uncle. I've spent this whole road learning it—from Kesh, from Ilsa, from Corvane's smiling magistrates. Everything that wants me wants the door I open." Her eyes were wet. He saw that. He saw, with a lurch of horror he would carry however long he lasted, that she was crying *for him*. "You're not lying to me. That's the worst part. You believe every word. But the garden doesn't want family. It wants a keeper it can wear."

"Noor—"

"Sable told me what the sealing cost. Not the story she told the elders. The real one. The garden was never ours, Aldric. It was never anyone's. And the moment we claim it—" she stepped back, one step, toward the lattice instead of away from it, and he realized with cold dawning dread that she had been positioning herself this entire conversation, "—it claims us back."

Above them, through the fissured dark, the first light of the full thirteenth moon slid down into the garden like a blade of white fire, and the heart-lattice began, at last, to sing.

Aldric moved.
