## 36. Let It Go to Seed

The garden was dying the way a starved animal dies—beautifully, and with its teeth still sharp.

Noor stood at the center of it, where the thirteenth moon's light fell through a wound in the ceiling of the world, and she let herself look. Really look. Not the way Aldric looked, with hunger sharpening his eyes into blades. Not the way Sable had looked through forty years of guilt. She looked the way you look at something you are about to lose forever, because that was the only honest way left to look.

Roses the size of her head climbed trellises of white stone, their petals veined with gold that pulsed like slow heartbeats. Grass grew in silver waves beneath her feet, bending toward her as if she were wind. Fruit hung heavy on trees that had no business existing underground—pears of pale glass, apples that glowed ember-red from within—and every branch strained upward toward the fissure overhead, toward the moonlight it could smell but never touch. The whole garden leaned. The whole garden reached. A hundred years of caged light had turned every living thing here into a prisoner pressing its face against the bars.

And underneath the beauty, rot. She could see it now that she knew to see. The gold veins in the roses were not gold but scar tissue. The silver grass grew over mounds of what had once been other plants, grown and bloomed and burned out and died in cycles too fast, too furious, the power cycling through them like blood through a heart beating far past its natural speed. At the center of the garden, sunk into the earth like a second sun buried alive, was the source itself: a sphere of light no larger than a harvest moon, wrapped in roots, wrapped in wards, wrapped in the seal her great-grandmother had laid down with the best intentions and the worst possible wisdom.

It flickered. Even now, even with the gate open behind her and the wards unraveling thread by thread, it flickered—sputtering like a lamp running out of oil, hoarding everything it had because it did not know how else to survive.

*You've been alone down here so long,* Noor thought, *you've forgotten that light is supposed to leave.*

Above her, muffled by a hundred feet of stone and root, the war had arrived. She felt it as vibration more than sound—the catacombs shuddering, dust sifting down through the moonlight like gray snow. Corvane's soldiers and Ilsa's rebels were colliding somewhere in the drowned halls above, and Kesh was somewhere between them, holding the tunnel with nothing but his quick tongue and whatever he'd pried loose from a dead man's belt, and Sable—

Sable stood ten paces away, at the edge of the circle of wards, and the garden would not let her pass.

Noor had watched it happen. Her grandmother had walked up to the boundary of the inner ring with the seal-charm raised in both hands, an old woman carrying the weight of an old crime, and the wards had simply—refused her. Light had risen along the ground like frost spreading backward, and Sable had stopped as though she'd walked into a wall of deep water. The magic had read her blood and found it true and found it spent. Whatever right the Fairweather line had to this place, it lived now in one body only.

"It knows me," Sable had said, and there had been no bitterness in it, only a terrible clarity. "It remembers what I did. Go on, child. I'll hold the door."

That had been minutes ago. It might have been hours. Time moved strangely under the thirteenth moon.

"Noor." Aldric's voice came from her left, low and warm, and she hated how much of her still wanted to lean toward it. Twenty years of grief did not dissolve just because the man wearing it turned out to be made of ambition. "Look at me. Not at the heart. At me."

She looked at him. He stood near the buried sun with his hands open, his palms up, the posture of a man offering everything—but he stood *between* her and it, and that told her everything his voice tried to hide.

"You've seen what it can do," he said. "You felt it in yourself on the coach road, when you shielded Kesh without knowing how. That was a drop. A leak through the seal. Imagine the whole of it in you—in us. We could end the Court in an afternoon. We could end hunger. We could end *war*, Noor. Our name would stop being a curse. It would be a crown."

"I don't want a crown."

"Then wear it for a year and give it away," he said, and his sincerity was the worst thing about him, because it was real. She could see it burning in him, clean and monstrous at once. "But take it first. Hold it first. Because if you scatter it—" his voice caught, and for one moment she saw her uncle, the boy who'd fled the Hollow, the brother Sable had mourned for twenty years— "if you scatter it, it's gone. Everything it could have been. Gone into dirt and ditches and rivers, wasted, and we will have been the last two Fairweathers, and we will have thrown away the only thing that ever made our name mean anything."

Noor thought about the Hollow. About orchards that gave less every year, about a valley where nothing magical could live, about nineteen years of being a legend's leftover, a footnote in someone else's story. She thought about how badly she had wanted to matter.

And then she thought about Maren's copies of the map multiplying in the dark. About Corvane's polite, patient cruelty. About the villages Ilsa had shown her, the starved fields, the children with the gray look of people who had learned that power flows only to those who already have it. Every faction down in those tunnels tonight believed the garden was a treasure. None of them—not one—had asked what the treasure wanted.

"I used to think mattering meant being chosen," Noor said. "Being the key. Being special." She took a breath, and the garden's air went into her, sweet and rotten at once, ancient and desperate. "But keys exist for doors. Doors exist for rooms. And this room has been locked so long it's killing itself." She met her uncle's eyes. "I'm not going to claim it, Aldric. And I'm not going to re-seal it. I'm going to do the one thing nobody down here has thought to do in a hundred years."

His face changed. Slowly, like ice shifting on a river. "Noor—"

"I'm going to let it go."

* * *

The ritual—if it could be called that—was not in any book. Sable had taught her the shape of it on the road from the waystation, three nights ago, speaking plainly at last: *A seal is a hand closed around something. Your great-grandmother closed hers and never opened it again, and the thing suffocated. Unsealing isn't breaking the hand open, love. It's remembering how to let go.*

So Noor did not fight the wards. She walked to the buried heart with her hands empty and her palms open, and when the outermost ward rose against her like a wall of cold light, she did exactly what she had done at the boundary stones of the Hollow, the day she'd left home. She did not push. She *agreed*. She let the magic read her—all of her, the fear and the guilt and the longing and the lie she'd told the Ember rider and the burst of raw power that had saved Kesh's life—and the ward shivered, and sighed, and became a doorway.

One by one, the rings of the seal opened for her. Not shattered—opened, the way a fist opens, finger by finger, reluctant and grieving. Each layer of light parted around her body like water around a wading child, and each time, the buried sun below flared brighter, sensing, hoping, *daring* to hope, and Noor felt its attention settle on her like the gaze of something enormous waking from a century of nightmares.

Behind her, boots on stone. "Noor, stop—" Aldric, moving fast now, all pretense gone. "Sable! Stop her!"

"She's a Fairweather," her grandmother's voice answered, steady as bedrock, from beyond the ward-line. "This is the only thing a Fairweather has ever done right. Let her work, Aldric. If you love anything but yourself, let her *work*."

He didn't stop. Of course he didn't. Noor heard him coming and did not turn around, because turning around would be choosing him over the garden, and she had already chosen. The last ward parted. She knelt at the edge of the buried sun and pressed both hands flat against the earth above it, and the ground was warm, fever-warm, the warmth of a body burning itself up from the inside.

*I know,* she told it, the way you speak to a frightened animal, the way her grandmother spoke to dying orchards. *I know. You've held on so hard for so long. You're allowed to stop.*

The heart pulsed against her palms. And in that pulse she felt everything it was: a hundred years of caged bloom, generations of power with nowhere to go, folding inward and inward until it had begun to eat itself. She felt its terror, which was the oldest terror there was—*if I let go, I cease*—and beneath the terror, faint as a seed under snow, the other thing. The true thing. The reason sources of magic existed at all.

Magic was never meant to be kept. It was meant to move. Through soil and root and rain and blood, through everything living, the way water moves, the way light moves, asking nothing back, belonging to no one because it belonged to everyone. The garden had forgotten. A hundred years in the dark will do that. But it hadn't been destroyed. Only starved of somewhere to go.

Noor could give it somewhere to go.

"Aldric," she said quietly, without lifting her hands. "Last chance to step back."

"Don't do this." His voice had cracked wide open; the crown was gone from it, and what remained was just a man, terrified, watching his life's purpose kneel in the dirt and prepare to drown itself. "Noor. *Please.*"

"I'm sorry," she said, and meant it, and meant it in the way that hurt most: she was sorry for him, not for what she was about to do. "You came back for the wrong thing. But you came back. That counts for something. I'll remember it that way."

She opened her hands—not physically; they stayed pressed to the earth—and opened everything else.

* * *

Letting go, it turned out, was the hardest work she had ever done. Harder than lying to the rider. Harder than leaving the Hollow. Harder than learning her family's history was a crime scene.

Because the moment she stopped holding the seal shut, the power surged *up through her*, and it wanted what everything starving wants: to keep, to keep, to keep. It poured into her body like a river into a cracked jar, and for one blinding instant Noor understood exactly why her great-grandmother had sealed it instead of sharing it, why Aldric knelt in the dirt weeping and reaching, why every faction in the kingdom had bled for this. It felt like being chosen by the sun. It felt like mattering beyond all measure or end. One breath of it and she could see the whole kingdom—every river, every field, every hidden thing—one breath of it and she would never be small or overlooked or afraid again, one breath of it and she could remake the world in a Fairweather image and call it mercy—

*This is how it starts,* she thought, from very far away inside herself. *This is how Grandmother started. This is how all of them started.*

She thought of Kesh, who had debts and charm and a crooked grin, and who had stood in front of her in the tunnel anyway. She thought of Sable, who had carried a crime for forty years and finally set it down by telling the truth. She thought of the Hollow, where nothing magical could follow her, and how she had hated it her whole life—and how, all that time, the valley had been the only honest thing in the kingdom. A place that refused to hoard. A place that had given up magic entirely rather than let anyone own it.

Her family hadn't fallen from greatness. They'd paid for it, in advance, for a hundred years.

"Go," Noor whispered—to the power, to the garden, to the starving buried sun. "You don't belong to me. You don't belong to anyone. *Go.*"

And she let go of letting go, and opened all the way.

The dispersal was not an explosion. It was worse than that, and better. It was a *harvest in reverse*—the whole garden exhaling at once after a century of held breath. Light poured out of the buried heart and out of the roses and the silver grass and the glass pears and the golden veins, poured up through Noor's body and out through the fissures in the stone, out through the gate, out through a thousand hairline cracks in the roots of the world, and it did not gather anywhere. That was the miracle of it. It scattered. It scattered the way seeds scatter, the way pollen scatters, finding soil, finding water, finding root, sinking into the kingdom itself—into the drowned catacombs and the rivers above them, into the fields around Vessaline, into forests and farms and fens she would never see, into the Hollow itself, maybe, into every handful of dirt that had ever been denied. Ownerless. Wild. Everywhere and therefore no one's.

Noor felt it leave her, too—felt the borrowed infinity drain out through her open hands and keep going, and she did not clutch at it, though every cell in her body screamed to clutch. That was the victory. Not the power. The *not keeping*.

In the last of the light, she saw Aldric.

He had lunged for the heart as it faded—of course he had, her beautiful, broken uncle, reaching for the last of the gold the way a drowning man reaches for the surface—and the dispersal had taken him mid-reach. It did not strike him. Nothing so simple. It simply *included* him, unmade him gently into the general going, and for one heartbeat his eyes found hers across the collapsing brightness and there was no rage in them at all. There was relief. There was a boy from a green valley, finally putting down something too heavy to carry.

"Aldric—" she cried, reaching back, too late, always too late, and the light took even her cry and scattered it.

Then the garden was only a garden. Roses, ordinary-sized, ordinary-colored, trembling in the settling dust. Grass, merely green. The buried heart was gone—not extinguished but *distributed*, everywhere at once, invisible, and the silence it left behind was the deepest silence Noor had ever stood inside.

She knelt in the dirt of the Sealed Garden, which was now just a garden, and wept for her uncle, and for her grandmother's forty years, and for the girl who had wanted to matter, who had mattered, who had chosen the one way of mattering that left nothing behind to prove it.

Above her, through the fissures, dawn was coming. And with it—she could feel it rising through her knees, through the stone, through the whole tired bones of the world—a green, wild, ownerless hum, like a billion seeds deciding, all at once, to grow.

Somewhere far above, a tunnel collapsed with a roar, and Kesh's voice—hoarse, triumphant, unmistakably his—shouted words she couldn't make out, followed by Sable's laugh, cracked and young and free.

Noor wiped her face, stood, and began to climb toward the light.
