## 40. Epilogue: Seeds on the Wind

The road east of Vessaline had a new name now, though the old stones still remembered the old one. Travelers called it the Fairweather Road, and they said it wrong in a dozen accents along its whole length—*Fairwether*, *Farewater*, *Fairvater*—and Noor had stopped correcting them years ago. Names, she had learned, were like seeds. They did not need to be pronounced perfectly to take root.

She walked it in the season when the wild orchards bloomed, which was every season now, which was the miracle no one had quite gotten used to. The trees that lined the road had not been planted by any hand. They had come up on their own in the years after the thirteenth moon, pushing through old battlefield soil and burned foundations and the cracked flagstones of roads built by kings who believed power was something you could hold. Their blossoms were gold at the heart, white at the edge, and they opened out of season, out of turn, out of spite for every calendar ever drawn.

A carter going down to the city with a load of late pears tipped his hat to her as she passed. He did not know her face—he was young, and the stories had worn her features smooth into something more legend than woman—but he knew the way she walked, unhurried, unarmed, looking up at the branches instead of over her shoulder.

"That's her," he told his mule afterward, because everyone knew you could tell her by that. "That's the one from the green valley. The one who doesn't fear anything."

This was not true. She feared plenty. But she had made her peace with fear the way farmers make peace with weather—not by conquering it, but by planning around it, respecting it, and refusing to let it decide what got planted.

* * *

In the taverns of Vessaline, where brass bells still rang the hours from towers that had once been siege engines, the story was told differently depending on who told it and how much wine had been poured.

The dockworkers' version had her descending alone into the drowned catacombs with nothing but a burning map and her grandmother's blessing, holding back three armies with a word in a language older than the war. The scholars' version—the ones Maren had spent a decade assembling in the rebuilt archive, footnotes thick as frost—had her doing something far stranger and far less dramatic: she had simply *opened a door*, and what came through belonged to no one, and that was the whole of the politics of it, though the scholars took four hundred pages to say so.

The children's version was the best one. In it, the garden under the city had been a sleeping dragon, or a caged star, or a prisoner—and the girl from the valley had kissed it, or sung to it, or simply said *you can go now*, and it had gone, everywhere at once, into the ground and the water and the wind, and after that magic rose up through the world like spring through frozen earth, wild and free and belonging to everybody, which meant, the children understood without being told, that it belonged to nobody, and this was the point.

Kesh told his own version, of course. Kesh's version featured Kesh prominently. He performed it in the crossroads markets on market days, with a scarf trick and a false limp and a voice that dropped to a whisper at the crucial moment, and he always ended the same way: "And I said to her—I said, *Noor Fairweather, if you're going to unmake the oldest power in the world, the least you can do is let me hold the lantern.*" He would pause there, letting the crowd lean in. "And do you know what she said?"

He never told them what she said. That was the trick of it. The not-knowing filled seats better than any answer, and besides, what she had actually said was *stop talking, Kesh, the tunnel is collapsing*, which lacked a certain poetry.

But those who watched him closely—and there were always a few, other brokers, other survivors of other ledgers—noticed that he never took payment for the telling. Not coin, not favor, not information. The story of the garden was the one thing in the world Kesh gave away.

* * *

The Hollow had changed, and had not changed, and this was the thing returning travelers found hardest to describe.

The valley still sat folded between its hills like a cupped hand. The boundary stones still stood, though their script had faded to decoration, their work long done. What had changed was the air—or rather, what had changed was that the air was ordinary now, ordinary the way it was everywhere else, humming faintly with the ownerless magic that rose from every root and river in the kingdom. Wildflowers along the creek bloomed in small impossible colors. The orchards fruited twice a year and grudgingly a third. Children born in the Hollow in the last decade could make light with their fingers if they concentrated, and the elders had given up pretending to disapprove.

The guild had not died. This had been Sable's last great surprise to everyone, herself included. The Fairweathers, who had spent a century sealing and hiding and forgetting, had become teachers. Farmers came up the valley road in every season to learn what the guild had learned too late and too dearly: that power hoarded rots, that power shared disperses like seed on wind, that the only safe place to keep a miracle is in open ground. They called the school the New Garden, and its first rule, painted over the door in Sable's own handwriting, read: *Nothing here is yours.*

Sable herself had lived just long enough to see the first class graduate. She had planted the first tree of the restored orchard with hands that shook, and Noor had held the ladder, and neither of them had said anything about forgiveness, because forgiveness had already happened somewhere in the space between a waystation table and a dying letter, quietly, the way most true things happen. On the last morning, Sable had looked out at the valley—the valley she had sealed, the valley she had saved, the valley she had lied about—and said only, "Well. It kept." And then she had gone to sleep in her chair beneath the blooming tree and had not woken, and they buried her at the orchard's head where she could keep arguing with the weather.

Noor visited the grave every time she came home, which was often, though home no longer meant invisible. She would sit on the warm grass and report, the way you report to someone who cannot answer: the road tolls abolished in the western provinces; the Thornbound's reformation holding in the north, Ilsa gray at the temples now and more feared than ever for having laid down her leverage; the Ember Court dissolved into something smaller and humbler that mostly concerned itself with bridges and grain stores. Corvane she did not speak of. Some names were seeds that were better left unplanted.

* * *

The woman herself was harder to find than the stories suggested, and easier.

She carried no title. When the remnant courts sent envoys—and they still did, occasionally, men and women in fine coats arriving at the New Garden with proposals wrapped in ribbon—she received them in muddy boots, offered them cider, and listened with a patience that unnerved them more than any threat could have. She signed nothing. She claimed nothing. When they asked what she wanted in return for all she had given away, she looked genuinely puzzled, as if they had asked what she wanted in return for breathing.

"You misunderstand," she told a particularly persistent ambassador from the coastal league, a man accustomed to every gift being a hook. "I didn't give it away. It was never mine. That was the entire lesson. My family spent a hundred years learning it the hard way so that nobody else would have to."

"And yet you are famous," the ambassador pressed. "Revered. You could ask for anything."

"Yes," Noor agreed, and smiled, and poured him more cider. "It's very restful, actually. Wanting nothing is the one kingdom no one can besiege."

He left unsettled and told his colleagues she was a saint. She was not a saint. She still lied well when lying was needed, still kept Kesh's secrets and her own, still woke some nights with the taste of the catacombs in her mouth and Aldric's name caught in her throat like a fishbone. Grief for the uncle who might have been, anger at the man who chose otherwise—those had not resolved; they had simply been carried long enough to become part of her weight, distributed evenly, like ballast that keeps a ship upright.

What had changed was that she no longer hid. She walked the Fairweather Road with her face bare and her name spoken plainly, and when strangers stared, she stared back kindly until they remembered their manners. Visibility, she had discovered, was not the price she had paid. It was the harvest.

* * *

On an evening in the gold-blooming season, she sat on the low wall of the New Garden with Kesh beside her, both of them watching the students practice in the orchard below—farmers' sons and merchants' daughters and one solemn ex-soldier, all of them learning to coax light from their fingertips, failing cheerfully, trying again.

"Rumor at the last crossroads," Kesh said, in the tone he used for rumors he had verified twice. "There's a valley in the far south. Deep one. Sealed, like this one used to be. Old wards, older than ours. The people there don't know why nothing grows quite right, why their dreams are quiet. They think it's a blessing."

Noor was quiet a while. Below them, the ex-soldier got her light to hold—a small golden flame above her palm, trembling, hers and no one else's and everyone's—and laughed out loud at it, startled by her own hands.

"They'll have a courier someday," Noor said at last. "Or a map. Or a stranger dying at their treeline with a name on his lips." She turned the thought over, testing it, the way she had once tested a bloodstained map against firelight. "The world's full of sealed gardens. We just didn't know it, back then."

"So we wait for their courier to find them?"

"No." She stood, brushing off her skirt, and looked south, where the hills went blue with distance. "We go to them. That's the difference. That's everything my grandmother's generation got wrong—they waited, and hid, and let the secret curdle into a war." She smiled, and it was the smile the carter had seen, the one that made legends out of an ordinary woman in muddy boots. "Seeds don't wait to be found, Kesh. They travel."

Kesh groaned, already reaching for his pack, already calculating the road, already composing the opening lines of the story he would tell about it in every market between here and the southern sea. "You know," he said, falling into step beside her as the gold blossoms came loose on the wind behind them, drifting down the valley toward the wide world, "one of these days I'm going to stop following you into other people's legends."

"No, you won't," said Noor Fairweather, who carried no title and feared no court, and walked on toward the horizon with the seeds at her back and the road opening ahead like a page.
