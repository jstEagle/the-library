## 2. Thirteen Turns

The almanac smelled of dust and mouse, the way all old things in Warden's Valley did—like they had been left alone so long that solitude had become their primary ingredient. Felix turned its cracked leather cover with two fingers, careful not to smudge the ink, while behind her the astrologer's shop filled itself with the particular silence of a man deciding how much truth he could afford to tell.

"You see it there?" Master Corvin said at last. He was a small man made smaller by age, his spine curved like a question mark over his workbench, his eyes still sharp enough to thread a needle by candlelight. He tapped one yellowed page with a fingernail gone thick and ridged as hoof. "Thirteen turns."

Felix looked. The page was a moon-table, columns of numbers marching down in neat rows, each row a turn of the moon from new to full to dark and back again. Twelve rows. Then, squeezed between the twelfth and what should have been the first, a thirteenth—written in different ink, browner, older, as if someone had added it later and then tried very hard to make it look like it had always been there.

"There are twelve turns," Felix said. "There have always been twelve turns."

"Mm." Corvin took the almanac from her hands with the tenderness other men reserved for infants or relics. "That's what I told myself for sixty years. And yet." He turned to a shelf behind him, ran his fingers along spines until he found what he wanted: three more almanacs, older still, their covers furred with mold. He opened them one by one to the same page. In each, the thirteenth turn sat in its brown ink like a splinter in flesh.

"Every copy," he said. "Even the ones copied before my grandfather's grandfather learned his letters. The thirteenth turn is in all of them. Same dates. Same notations. *The moon swells. The tide forgets itself. Do not count the nights.*" He read that last part aloud in a voice gone thin. "Do not count the nights, Thornbury. What kind of instruction is that? Who writes that into an almanac?"

"Someone who wanted people to count them anyway," Felix said.

Corvin laughed, but it came out wrong, more cough than humor. "Aye. That's the trouble with you soldiers. You think like the enemy." He closed the oldest book gently, as if shutting a door on something sleeping. "I found this forty years ago, when I first apprenticed here. I showed my master. He went white as tallow, burned his own copy in the yard, and told me never to speak of it again. So I didn't. Sixty years, I didn't." His eyes came up to hers, and Felix saw something in them she had only ever seen in men the night before a battle. "And then last week I couldn't sleep, and I got up, and I pulled it down off the shelf, and I've been looking at it every night since. Like a man picking at a scab."

"Why tell me?" Felix asked. "I'm not your master. I can't burn anything for you except trouble."

"No," Corvin agreed. "But you walk the border every day. You touch the wardstones. And you're the only soul in this valley who'd believe me and also know what to do about it." He paused. "Also, you're the only one who won't laugh."

Felix looked at the thirteenth turn again. *The moon swells.* She thought of the moon she had seen rising these past few nights—fuller than it should have been, she'd assumed, some trick of haze on the horizon, some distortion of the eye. She had not counted the nights. She had not even thought to count the nights.

"I'll keep walking the border," she said finally. "Same as always. If anything's wrong with the wards, I'll know."

Corvin nodded slowly, and wrapped the almanacs back in their oilcloth, and put them away, and neither of them spoke again about the thing that shouldn't exist. But when Felix left the shop, the bell above the door rang once—a small, clear sound—and she stood in the street a moment longer than she needed to, listening to it fade, feeling like a woman who has just been told the floor beneath her feet is thinner than she thought.

* * *

She walked the border that afternoon because walking the border was what she did, the way rain fell and bread rose and old men complained about their knees. It was the shape of her life, and she loved the shape of it, though she would have denied loving anything under torture.

Warden's Valley ran nine miles end to end, cupped between two ridgelines like water in a folded hand. At the mouth of the valley, where the river left for the lowlands, stood the wardstones: seven of them, waist-high, gray as old teeth, set in an arc across the gap. No one remembered who had raised them. The histories in the village shrine-room began with the valley already walled, already safe, already forgotten by whatever world lay outside. Magic could not cross the stones. That was the whole of the law and the whole of the miracle. Witches burned themselves out at the border like moths against glass. Curses unraveled. Enchanted swords rusted overnight. Once, thirty years ago, a sorcerer-king's herald had ridden right up to the westernmost stone, dismounted, drawn a circle in the air with a word that made the horses scream—and nothing had happened at all. The man had stared at his own empty hand for a long time, remounted, and ridden away without a word.

Felix maintained the stones. That was her second duty, after patrolling, and she performed it without understanding it, which was the valley's way. Every seventh day she walked the arc with a jar of lamp oil and a stiff brush and cleaned the lichen from the carvings—not letters, exactly, but marks, spirals and bars and small dense knots of line that predated any alphabet she knew. She did not know what they meant. She knew only that when she cleaned them, the stones hummed faintly under her palm, a vibration felt rather than heard, like a cat purring through a wall. The other wardens did the same on their days, and none of them understood either, and the valley stayed safe, and that was enough. It had always been enough.

Today she reached the third stone—the one nearest the river, its base green with moss—and knelt, and set down her brush, and stopped.

There was a crack in it.

Hairline. A finger's width below the largest spiral, running sideways for perhaps the length of her thumb, so fine that if she hadn't known the stone's face the way she knew her own hands she would have missed it entirely. She pressed her fingertip to it. The crack was real—she could feel the seam, the minute displacement of stone against stone—but when she traced it with her eyes it seemed to vanish at both ends, as if the fracture existed only in the middle, only where her finger rested, only when observed.

And the humming was wrong. Not absent—she would have noticed absence the way you notice a missing tooth—but *thin*. The purr-through-the-wall had developed a rattle in it, a stutter, like a heart skipping beats.

Felix knelt there a long time, her brush forgotten in the grass, the river talking its usual nonsense over the rocks. She thought about fetching the mason. She thought about telling the head warden. She thought about Corvin's brown-inked thirteenth turn and the way his voice had gone thin reading *do not count the nights.*

Then she cleaned the stone. All of it, every spiral and bar and knot, working the oil into the lines with the patience of ten years' habit, and when she finished she laid her whole palm flat against the gray face and held it there until the hum steadied—or until she persuaded herself it had steadied, which may have been the same thing.

The crack did not close. But it did not widen. She marked its length with a scratch of charcoal at each end, so she would know, tomorrow, whether it had grown in the night.

It was late afternoon when she walked home, and the sun went down red and clean behind the western ridge, and the first stars came out over the valley one by one, punctual as lamplighters, and Felix looked up at them and felt—for no reason she could name—the specific loneliness of standing in a house whose foundations have begun, very quietly, to shift.

* * *

She did not sleep.

She told herself it was the ale she'd had at the Drowned Lamb, where the evening crowd had talked of nothing but the harvest and the price of wool and, briefly, of the moon—"swollen as a tick," old Marta had said, and everyone had laughed, and the subject had died there, unexamined, the way subjects die in comfortable places. Felix had laughed too. Now, lying in her narrow bed above the guardhouse, she replayed the laugh and heard how hollow it had been.

Moonrise came near midnight. She saw it through her window, climbing over the eastern ridge, and her breath caught in a way she was ashamed of even alone in the dark.

It was wrong. There was no other word. Full it should not have been—the tables said waxing gibbous, two nights short—and yet it hung swollen and nearly perfect, silver-white, too large, too bright, throwing shadows in her room as sharp as noon's. She watched it for an hour. It did not behave strangely. It simply rose, and crossed, and shone, and was beautiful, and was wrong, the way a familiar face is wrong when the smile doesn't reach the eyes.

Somewhere past two bells she slept despite herself, a shallow drifting sleep full of corridors and counting.

What woke her was not a sound.

It was the absence of one. She surfaced from the dream like a swimmer breaking water, heart hammering, straining her ears—and heard the valley breathing around her: wind in the pines, the river far off, a dog somewhere complaining at nothing. Ordinary sounds. But underneath them, so fundamental she had never once registered it in ten years of nights, a hum. The wards. The great slow chord of the seven stones singing to each other across the valley mouth, felt in the bones more than the ears, the sound that meant *safe*, the sound she had slept inside her whole life the way a child sleeps inside its mother's heartbeat.

The hum was gone.

Felix was out of bed and dressed before the thought finished forming, boots half-laced, sword belt buckled by feel. She took the stairs two at a time and shouldered out into the street—and stopped dead.

The sky had gone out.

Not clouded. Not veiled. *Out.* Every star, gone, all at once, everywhere she looked, from ridge to ridge, zenith to horizon—a blackness so total and so sudden that for one vertiginous moment she thought she had gone blind, that the night had taken her eyes as payment for something. Only the moon remained, hanging swollen and silver and alone in a sky like spilled ink, and its light fell on the rooftops and the empty street and her own raised hand, proving the world still existed, proving only the stars were gone.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. The constellations she had steered by on night patrols, the Ploughman and the Weeping Sisters and the Hunter's Belt, the fixed patient furniture of every night of her life—they had not dimmed, had not slid behind cloud. They had simply stopped. As if the sky were a lamp and someone had pinched the wick. As if the stars had never been fires at all, but holes in a curtain, and someone had drawn the curtain shut.

Felix stood in the middle of the street with her sword unbuckled at her hip and her breath smoking in the cold, and she felt awe move through her like cold water, and then, following close behind it, the awe curdled. Because awe was for things that meant nothing. This meant something. Everything that kept its schedule—tides, seasons, moons, stars—was the world keeping a promise. And the stars had just broken theirs, all together, without a word, the way an army breaks at the moment before contact.

She ran for the border.

The streets were waking around her as she went—shutters banging open, voices calling, someone ringing the chapel bell in ragged alarm—but Felix outran the noise, out along the river path, boots slamming the frozen mud, her breath tearing white behind her. The moon lit the way cruelly well. She came around the last bend and the wardstones rose up ahead of her in their arc across the valley mouth, seven gray teeth in the moonlight—

—and they were dark.

Not broken. Not toppled. They stood exactly as they had stood for centuries, mossy and carved and patient. But the hum was gone, and now, standing close, Felix could see what the hum had been holding back from her notice: the carvings were fading. The spirals and bars and knots, the marks she had oiled and tended for ten years, were draining of definition even as she watched, like ink in water, like a face forgetting itself. The third stone—the one with the crack—had already lost half its face. The charcoal scratch-marks she'd made that afternoon stood out starkly on blank gray stone, marking the place where a crack used to be, on a surface that no longer had any features left to crack.

"Felix!" One of the younger wardens, Tam, skidding up the path behind her, wild-eyed, sword drawn for no enemy he could name. "The stones—I was on watch, they just—there wasn't any sound, they just *stopped*—"

"I know."

"What do we do?"

Felix looked at the stones. She looked past them, through the gap in the arc, down the river road into the darkness of the lowlands, where the world went on without the valley's permission—loud, magical, and full of everything the walls had spent lifetimes keeping out. Nothing moved there. Yet.

She put her hand flat on the third stone. Cold. Silent. Stone, and nothing else. For the first time in her life she touched a wardstone and felt nothing looking back.

"We wake the valley," she said. "Every soul, armed, at the shrine. Send runners to the outlying farms. And send someone to Corvin's shop." She was already turning back up the path, toward the bell and the light and the people who would look to her for orders, her mind moving fast and cold. "Tell him he can stop counting the nights."

Behind her, in the gap where the wall of the world used to be, the swollen moon climbed higher, brighter, fuller than any moon had any right to be—as if it were swelling toward something. As if it were counting too.

And far down the river road, at the very edge of what the moonlight could reach, something that might have been a figure, and might have been a shadow, and might have been a memory taking its first breath of air, stepped across the line where the wards had been.

Felix didn't see it. But the night did, and the night, for the first time in living memory, said nothing at all.
