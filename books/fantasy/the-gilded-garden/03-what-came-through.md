## 3. What Came Through

The silence after the stars went out was worse than any sound Felix had ever heard.

She had stood at the border for the better part of an hour, watching the wards die. Not flicker, not gutter—*die*, all at once, like a throat cut clean. The wardstones that had hummed beneath her palms every patrol for fifteen years now sat cold and dumb in their sockets of earth, and the valley beyond them was just a valley, and the world outside was just the world, and nothing separated them anymore except the dark.

Felix Thornbury had spent her whole life inside that separation. She had never once thought to question it, the way she had never once thought to question her own heartbeat. The valley kept magic out. That was simply what it did, the way stone did hardness and water did wetness. It was the one fixed truth of her existence, older than her, older than the village, older than the shrine where she left offerings to a god who had not spoken in ten years.

And now it was gone, and she could feel the absence the way you feel a missing tooth—continually, with your tongue, unable to stop.

The moon was still there. That was somehow the worst part. One pale, swollen disc hanging over the eastern ridge, fat as a coin held too close to the eye, while every star around it had been snuffed like candles pinched between wet fingers. She had counted the constellations on this border walk a thousand times. The Warden's Crown. The Ploughman's Yoke. The Sisters, three faint points that old Marta the astrologer swore were actually four if you knew when to look. All of them gone. The sky above the valley was a smooth black lid, and under that lid, everything Felix had built her life on had quietly stopped being true.

She should go back. Wake the village council. Rouse the other wardens—what few of them remained; the company had thinned to six since the god fell silent, and two of those were older than the shrine itself. She should do all the sensible things a warden does when the border fails.

Instead she stood in the frost with her hand on the dead wardstone and listened to the valley breathe, and waited, because some animal part of her knew that waiting was also a kind of duty. You don't run from a wound. You watch it.

The first thing came through just past midnight.

She didn't hear it cross. That was the thing she would keep returning to, afterward—the *not hearing*. For fifteen years, nothing had crossed that line without the wards announcing it, a low chime through the stones that every warden felt in their back teeth. Now the border was a door standing open in a house where no one lived, and things walked through it the way neighbors walk through an unlocked gate: casually, without hurry, as if they had always been meant to come this way.

It came up the sheep track from the western ford, and by its silhouette it was a man, tall, walking with a slight hitch in his left leg, and Felix's body knew him before her mind caught up. Her heart lurched. Her mouth opened around a name.

*Aldric.*

Except Aldric Voss had died eleven years ago, at Harrow Ford, with an arrow through his eye, and Felix had carried his shield home herself, and she had stood at his burial with the rest of the company while Sergeant Bray spoke the words of remembrance over a cairn of river stones. Aldric Voss was dead. Aldric Voss had been dead for eleven years, and the man walking up the sheep track toward her moved exactly the way Aldric had moved, favoring the left leg he'd injured falling from a horse in the spring campaign, and wore the same scarred leather brigandine, and had the same way of tilting his head when he saw someone he was glad to see.

"Felix." His voice was right too. Warm, a little rough, the voice of a man who'd spent twenty years shouting across battlefields. "There you are. I've been looking everywhere."

She did not answer. Her sword was out before she had decided to draw it, which was itself wrong—her body moving on instinct older than thought—and the blade's edge caught the swollen moonlight and threw a thin line of silver between them.

Aldric stopped. And smiled. And the smile was the problem, because Aldric Voss had smiled with his whole face, crinkling at the eyes, quick and crooked, and this smile was only the shape of a smile, laid over the face like paint over wood, and underneath it something else was doing the work of expression.

"You died," Felix said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. Fifteen years of mercenary work teaches you where to keep your fear: behind the teeth, where it can't get into your hands. "I buried you."

"Did you?" The thing wearing Aldric tilted its head, and the gesture was almost right, almost warm, and that made it infinitely worse. "That sounds like something that happened. I remember rain. I remember stones. I remember you carrying my shield and crying, though you told everyone you hadn't."

"I didn't cry."

"No," it agreed pleasantly. "You didn't. But I remember that you wanted to, and couldn't, and that you've never once managed it since. Isn't that strange? Eleven years, and not one tear. You're very good at not doing things, Felix Thornbury."

Every hair on her arms rose. Not because of the words—though the words were bad enough—but because of how they arrived. They didn't come through the air. They came *up*, from somewhere below speech, surfacing in her mind already formed, the way a memory surfaces, the way you suddenly recall where you left a key. This thing wasn't talking to her. It was reminding her.

"Stay on your side of the line," she said.

"There is no line anymore." It said it gently, almost kindly, the way you tell a child the dog has died. "You felt it go. We all felt it go. Do you know what it was, Felix? That wall around your little valley? Did anyone ever tell you, or did you just grow up inside it and call it safety?"

"Last warning."

"It isn't a warning that matters." Aldric's face did something complicated, grief and hunger braided together. "It's the name. Names are what matter now. The borders are down, and everything is loose, and we are all looking for the ones who hold names worth taking."

She gave it the warning anyway, because the oath demanded warnings before steel, even now, even silent, even ten years unanswered. She spoke the old formula—*stand and be judged, cross and be refused*—and the words tasted like ash, like speaking into a well with no bottom, and the thing wearing her dead friend took one more step toward her, and Felix killed it.

It was not a good fight. It barely fought at all. It raised Aldric's arms in a guard that Aldric would have been ashamed of, slow and theatrical, and her blade took it through the chest and out the back, and it looked down at the sword in its body with mild interest, the way you look at a stain.

Then it leaned close, past the blade, past the blood that wasn't bleeding quite right—too slow, too thick, like sap—and whispered against her ear.

"*Vess.*"

One word. A woman's voice, or no voice at all but something using the idea of a woman's voice, and it went into her not through the ear but through the bone, through the marrow, through whatever part of a person is deeper than the person.

Then it came apart. Not like a body falls. Like smoke remembering it was smoke. Aldric's face dissolved last, and the last thing to go was the smile, and then there was nothing on the sheep track but frost and her own ragged breathing and a sword slick with something that evaporated before she could wipe it.

*Vess.*

She stood there a long time. Long enough for the frost to creep up her boots. Long enough for the false moon to slide a hand's width down the sky.

The word sat in her like a swallowed coal. It wasn't painful, exactly. It was *known*. The way a room you've never entered can feel known. Somewhere under her sternum, something had turned over in its sleep and murmured, yes, that's mine, I always wondered.

She had been Felix Thornbury for thirty-four years. Before that—there was no before that. That was the strange thing she had never said aloud to anyone, not to Bray, not to old Warden Cott, not to the priest at the shrine: she had no memory of being a child. Her earliest memory was seventeen years old, waking in the company's camp with a sword-callused hand shaking her shoulder and Sergeant Bray saying, *you're with us now, girl, don't ask questions the road won't answer.* The company had accepted her without papers, without history, without a family to write to. Mercenaries didn't ask. Half of them were running from something. She had assumed she was too.

But thirty-four years was what she'd estimated. Estimated. Counted backward from the lines on her own face, because there was nothing else to count from.

*Vess.*

She sheathed the sword. She turned back toward the village, and that was when she saw the others.

They were coming up the valley road in the moonlight—not running, not marching, just *walking*, in twos and threes, spaced out like mourners arriving late to a funeral. A woman in a midwife's smock. A boy of maybe twelve, barefoot in the frost, unbothered by the cold. An old man leaning on a stick. None of them were people Felix knew.

That was what stopped her heart. Not monsters. Not horrors. Strangers. Ordinary strangers, walking into a valley that had been sealed against the impossible for longer than anyone living could remember, and they walked like people coming home.

No. Not strangers. She watched the woman in the smock pass the boundary where the wardstone stood dead in its socket, and the woman turned her head, and looked directly at Felix across forty yards of frozen field, and smiled—and it was the same smile, the painted-on smile, the smile-shaped thing—and Felix understood.

They weren't memories of her past. They were memories of a past she'd never had. Faces that had never existed, wearing lives that had never been lived, assembled from the raw material of *almost*: almost-mothers, almost-neighbors, almost-children, the people who should have filled the empty rooms of her history and never had. Someone—or something—had looked at the blank spaces where Felix Thornbury's life should have had people, and had begun, patiently, lovingly, to fill them in.

The woman in the smock raised one hand. Not a wave. A greeting. The kind of greeting you give someone whose name you know.

Felix ran.

She was not proud of it. Fifteen years a soldier, and she ran, boots tearing through frozen ruts, breath sawing, past the sleeping houses with their banked fires, past the shrine where the Silent One's carved face watched the road with its empty stone eyes—all the way to Warden Cott's door, where she hammered until the old man opened it with a knife already in his fist and decades of war in his squint.

"They're crossing," she said. "The border's down. They're coming up the valley road, and they look like—" Her voice cracked, and she hated it, and pushed through. "Cott, they look like people. They look like people who know me."

The old warden stared at her. Behind him, his wife lit a candle, and the light spilled yellow across the threshold, ordinary and small and suddenly the most precious thing in the world.

"How many?" Cott asked.

"I counted nine before I ran. There'll be more. The line's open the whole way round—we're not a valley anymore, we're a doorway." She gripped the doorframe to keep her hands still. "Wake the company. Bar every door. And Cott—" she hesitated, because saying it would make it real, and it was already real, it had been real since midnight, "—don't let them speak to you. Whatever they say. However well they know you. Don't let them finish a sentence."

"Why?"

Because the one that wore Aldric's face had put a word in her bones that fit a lock she hadn't known she carried. Because she could still feel it sitting there, patient as a seed.

"Because they give you things," Felix said. "Things you didn't ask for."

Cott searched her face, found whatever he found there, and nodded once, and shut the door to wake the valley.

Felix stood alone in the lane. Above her, the swollen moon hung in its starless black, and somewhere out on the dark fields, the almost-people kept walking, unhurried, calling gentle greetings to the shuttered houses in voices like remembered weather.

*Vess,* the night seemed to whisper, from every direction at once. *Vess.*

And far to the south, past the ridge, past the fallen wards, past the whole blind width of the world—a single point of light appeared in the sky where no star had burned since midnight. Small. Cold. Deliberate.

Like an eye opening.
