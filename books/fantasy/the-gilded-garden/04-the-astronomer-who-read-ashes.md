## 4. The Astronomer Who Read Ashes

The stars had been dead for three days when Isolde Vane finally understood that she was going to die in the mountains, and the understanding came to her not as fear but as a kind of professional embarrassment.

She had spent thirty-one years reading the sky. She had charted the precessions of eleven constellations, corrected the royal ephemeris twice, and once—her proudest hour, though it had become her ruin—predicted the exact night a comet would graze the horn of the Stag. She knew the heavens the way a harpist knows her strings. And for three days now, the heavens had been a sheet of black felt stretched over the world, featureless, absolute, and she had walked away from Aldermere with nothing but her instruments and her life, and both were running out.

Her astrolabe still worked. That was the bitter joke of it. Brass did not care what it pointed at; the rings turned, the alidade swung, the sighting holes framed perfect circles of nothing. Three nights running she had stopped on the high road, set the instrument on its folding stand, and taken sightings of stars that were not there. The numbers came out as gibberish or as zeroes, depending on how she coaxed them, and zeroes, in astronomy, were not a result. Zeroes were a scream.

*They are not gone,* she told herself, packing the astrolabe with hands too cold to feel the buckles. *They are covered. There is a difference. A covered thing can be uncovered.*

It was the only sentence keeping her legs moving.

Behind her, two days' ride back down the switchbacks, lay the city where she had been born, educated, honored, and very nearly hanged. She could still hear the Grand Astronomer's voice—not old Master Corvane's voice, but the one behind him, the smooth court voice that had come to her chambers at midnight with two guards and a warrant written in ink so fresh it shone.

*"You looked where you were told the sky ends, Mistress Vane. That is not astronomy. That is treason."*

She had predicted the star-death. That was all. Eleven days before it happened, her instruments had shown her something impossible: the light of certain stars arriving late, as if the stars themselves had flinched backward from the earth, or as if something vast had slid between. She had done what an astronomer does. She had written it down, dated it, sealed it, and delivered it to the Crown, because the Crown paid her to tell the truth about the sky.

The Crown had thanked her by burning her observatory notes and naming her a witch of the foreign dark. The proclamation had used the word *harbinger*. Isolde had read it nailed to the customs house door while a fishmonger spat at her feet, and she had understood, with the clarity that comes only to people whose profession is seeing things far away, that the darkness coming was not merely in the sky. Something in Aldermere had known it was coming. Something in Aldermere had been *waiting* for it.

So she ran north and west, away from the roads the king's riders watched, into hills so old the maps gave up on them. And there, in a shepherd's hut on the third night, an old woman had fed her barley soup and drawn a line on the floor with ash and said:

"You want a place where the wrongness can't follow you, girl? Then find the valley that forgot magic. My grandmother said the wards there were older than the crown. Older than the gods, maybe." The old woman had laughed, toothless and unbothered. "Course, my grandmother also said the moon was a hole in heaven. But she lived to ninety."

Isolde had walked two more days toward that laughable, half-remembered valley, and on the morning of the fourth day, cresting a ridge of grey stone with her boots split at the seams and her hair full of pine needles, she saw it.

The Warden's Valley lay below like a bowl of green glass held up to a sun that wasn't there. Mist pooled along its southern rim. Fields in neat chevrons, a river writing itself lazily through the middle, a village of slate roofs and smoking chimneys, orchards, a bell tower, sheep like scattered chalk marks on the slopes. It should have been beautiful. It was beautiful. But Isolde's eye—the trained, cursed, unforgiving eye—caught what was wrong with it immediately, and her breath went out of her in a long, shaking sigh of relief.

There were no wards visible. No boundary-stones glowing, no hedge-magic humming, none of the thousand small enchantments that laced every other corner of the world: no charm-lights in windows, no weather-bells, no guardian sigils over doorways. The village below was lit by ordinary fire and organized by ordinary human stubbornness.

And yet the air above it was *smooth*. That was the only word she had. In a world where magic moved through everything like current through water, the valley sat in the sky's river like a stone in a stream—and everything upstream of it, everything that had followed her out of Aldermere, would break against that stone and flow around.

*The last place magic cannot reach.* The shepherd's words. Perhaps the old woman's grandmother had been right after all.

Isolde stood on the ridge a moment longer, letting herself feel hope for exactly four seconds—she counted them, because hope unmeasured was how astronomers got hanged—and then she started down.

* * *

They were waiting for her before she reached the first fence.

Not soldiers. Farmers, mostly, but farmers with spears, and the way they held the spears told her they had held them before. A dozen of them fanned across the road with the unhurried competence of people who had done this perhaps twice in their lives and remembered it perfectly. At their center stood a woman.

Isolde noticed the woman before she consciously decided to. Not because she was tall, though she was, nor because she was armed, though she carried a sword with the bored familiarity of a limb—but because she stood the way instruments stood: balanced, calibrated, aimed. Her hair was dark red, cropped short. Her eyes were the pale grey-green of sea glass, and they went over Isolde the way Isolde went over a star chart: cataloguing, weighing, filing.

"Name," the woman said.

"Isolde Vane."

"Where from?"

"Aldermere."

A ripple through the farmers. Someone shifted a spear. The red-haired woman didn't move at all.

"Aldermere," she repeated. "That's six days' hard walk. You look like you've walked twelve."

"I took the long way. Your roads—" Isolde stopped, recalibrated. Lying badly was worse than not lying. "I avoided your roads. I've been followed. Or I was. I don't know if I still am."

"Followed by whom?"

"The Crown of Aldermere. And possibly by something older than the Crown." She heard her own voice and winced inwardly. To her own ears it sounded exactly like what it was: a scholar's voice, precise, exhausted, saying things that sounded mad. "I know how that sounds."

"Do you," said the woman. It was not a question. Her eyes flicked past Isolde's shoulder, scanning the ridgeline, the mist, the empty road. Whatever she was looking for, she seemed not to find it, and this appeared to annoy her rather than reassure her. "Open your pack."

Isolde opened her pack. The farmers crowded in to look at the astrolabe, the brass tubes, the star charts covered in her own cramped notation, the little iron lamp, the books. One of them—a young man with flour to his elbows—recoiled from the charts as if the paper might bite.

"What is it?" he whispered.

"Drawings," said another. "Stars." He pronounced the word carefully, the way one pronounces the name of a dead relative.

"They're mine," Isolde said. "I made them. I'm—I was—the court astronomer of Aldermere. Before."

"Before what?" the red-haired woman asked.

"Before the stars went out." Isolde met her eyes. "Eleven days before it happened, I measured the light arriving late. I reported it. They called me a witch and tried to hang me, and then the sky went dark anyway, which I confess I found unsporting of it."

Silence. Somewhere up-slope a sheep complained monotonously. The woman studied her for a long time, and Isolde had the strange sensation of being weighed by something that did not weigh in pounds.

"You're telling me you saw it coming," the woman said at last.

"I'm telling you I saw *something* coming. I didn't understand what. I still don't. That's why I'm here." Isolde spread her hands, aware of how they trembled and deciding to let them. "Your valley has no magic in it. I've walked through half the world's magic in six days and I can feel yours the way you'd feel a missing tooth. Whatever put that emptiness here did it on purpose, and whatever emptied the sky—" she pointed upward, at the blind noon, "—is the same kind of thing. Maybe the same *thing*. I need to study it. I need somewhere it can't reach me while I do."

"And if we don't want you?"

"Then I'll keep walking, and in a week I'll be dead, and you'll have lost nothing but an hour." She paused. "Though I'd point out that your border let me find it. If your wards are failing, you may want someone who knows what failing looks like."

Something moved behind the woman's eyes—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. She turned her head slightly and spoke to an older farmer without taking her gaze off Isolde.

"Hallam. Get Mother Ostrey."

* * *

Mother Ostrey turned out to be the village astrologer, which in a valley without magic apparently meant the woman who kept the almanacs, planted by the moon, and remembered things. She was small and round and sharp-eyed, and she examined Isolde's instruments with the frank greed of a collector before examining Isolde with considerably less warmth.

"Star charts," she said. "Huh. We haven't needed those in years. Sky doesn't change here." She tapped one of Isolde's sheets with a stubby finger. "This notation's court-trained. Corvane's school?"

"Corvane's student. Before his time."

"Mm." Mother Ostrey looked up. "You said the wards are failing. What makes you say that?"

"Because I'm standing inside them," Isolde said, "and I'm not comfortable."

That got a reaction. The farmers exchanged glances. The red-haired woman's jaw tightened almost imperceptibly.

"Explain."

"Magic can't enter this valley—that seems to be the design. But a ward is a wall, and walls have thickness. Right now I can feel the edge of it like standing in a doorway in winter. Cold on my left shoulder. Faint. But ten years ago—" she nodded at Mother Ostrey, guessing, "—I'd wager nobody crossing this border felt anything at all. A wall wearing thin lets drafts through. I'm a draft."

Nobody spoke. The wind moved the orchard branches with a dry rattle that sounded, just then, like whispering.

"It's true," said the flour-dusted young man suddenly. "The shrine candle. Gran says it never used to gutter, and last month it started."

"The shrine candle gutters because you let the door stand open, Tam," said an older farmer, but without conviction.

"The wards are stone," the red-haired woman said flatly. "Wardstones. Old ones. They don't wear."

"Everything wears," Isolde said gently. "Stone most slowly, but everything. And whatever emptied the sky has been working at walls for a long time. Walls are what it hates." She looked around at their faces—suspicion, fear, and underneath both, terribly, hope—and understood that she was doing the one thing she had sworn off doing in Aldermere: being believed. It was worse than being hanged. Being believed meant being responsible. "I'll make you a bargain. Let me stay under watch—your prisoner, if you like. Give me a week and your tallest roof, and I'll take measurements at your border stones. If I find nothing, you'll know your walls are sound, and you can send me on my way with a clear conscience. If I find something—" she shrugged, "—you'll want to know before it finds you."

"And if we just turn you out now?" the red-haired woman asked.

"Then whatever is eating the world eats it with you inside it, and I will have died for nothing, which would annoy me greatly."

Someone laughed—one bark of nervous laughter, quickly smothered. Mother Ostrey snorted.

"She talks like Corvane," the old woman said. "Corvane talked like that right up until he was right about everything." She fixed Isolde with a look. "You'll lodge at the shrine. Watched. You'll touch nothing at the border without one of ours beside you. And you—" this to the red-haired woman, "—will keep an eye on her, since you're the one who found her and the one who doesn't trust her. Seems fitting."

"Fine," said the red-haired woman.

"Felix," Mother Ostrey added, to Isolde, by way of explanation. "Felix Thornbury. Best sword in the valley and worst temper. You're in trustworthy hands, which is not the same as friendly ones."

* * *

The shrine stood at the valley's heart on a low hill, a round stone building older than every other building in sight, and Isolde felt its strangeness before she reached the door. Inside: one room, one altar, one candle—the famous guttering candle—burning before an empty alcove. No statue. No image. Just a hollow in the stone shaped vaguely like a seated figure, worn smooth by centuries of hands that had touched it seeking a god who no longer answered.

Felix watched her from the doorway while Isolde set up her instruments on the altar ledge, unpacking brass and glass with the ritual care of a priestess at vestment.

"There's no god in here," Felix said. It wasn't hostile, exactly. It was the tone of someone testing a floorboard.

"No," Isolde agreed. "But there was. Recently, too. Ten years, give or take." She ran her thumb along the hollow in the alcove. "Devotion wears stone differently than weather. This wore fast, like people came daily, desperate. And then stopped all at once."

"The company swore here," Felix said. "My company. Mercenaries. We swore to a god of—of boundaries. Thresholds. Crossings. He answered, for a while. Then he didn't." Her voice stayed level, but Isolde, who had spent her life listening for the wobble in distant signals, heard the fault line under it. "We kept the oath anyway. Nothing else to do with it."

*A god of boundaries.* Isolde's hands went still over the astrolabe.

Boundaries. The valley's walls. The border between life and death that the archives whispered some old bargain had blurred. The border between the earth and the sky—which was, when you thought about it in the right light, simply the largest boundary there was, the one every astronomer spent her life looking across.

And the stars. The stars were *out there*, beyond the sky's edge, and they had gone dark all at once, like lamps behind a shuttered window. Or like—

Like prisoners seen through bars.

The thought arrived whole, uninvited, and terrible, and Isolde kept her face still and wrote nothing down, because Felix Thornbury was watching her with those calibrated sea-glass eyes, and some instincts were older than scholarship.

"You felt something," Felix said. "Just now. When I said boundaries."

"I felt a great many things just now," Isolde said. "Most of them I'd like to check against measurements before I say aloud. Superstition is how I got sentenced to death the first time."

"That was for predicting the dark."

"That was for being right too early." Isolde straightened, met her eyes. "I won't make that mistake twice. When I know, Felix Thornbury, I'll know it all the way down, and then everyone in this valley can hang me or hail me as suits them."

For a moment—just a moment—something that was almost respect crossed the mercenary's face. Then it was filed away again behind the suspicion, and she jerked her chin toward the door.

"Curfew at dusk. Don't wander. And Vane?" She paused on the threshold, backlit by the blind grey afternoon. "If you're lying about any of it—if the Crown sent you, if you're their witch after all—I'll know. I'm good at knowing."

"I believe you," Isolde said sincerely. "It's why I'm glad you're the one watching me."

Felix left. Isolde stood alone in the godless shrine with her instruments and her thoughts, and after a long moment she allowed herself the luxury of taking out her journal and writing, in cipher, the sentence she had refused to write on the road:

*The stars have not died. They have been caged. And the cage is a boundary, and the god of boundaries is missing, and these facts are the same fact.*

She blew out her lamp, and in the dark of the shrine, the altar candle guttered once—twice—and steadied, as if something in the walls had leaned closer to listen.
