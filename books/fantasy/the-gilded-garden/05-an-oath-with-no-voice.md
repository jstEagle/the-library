## 5. An Oath With No Voice

The shrine at the valley's heart had no roof, because Felix had never seen the point of one. Rain fell through it same as light, and the god had never once complained about either.

She went there first, before she brought Isolde Vane anywhere. That was habit older than thought: trouble came, you walked it past the stone, and if the stone said nothing—well, the stone always said nothing—but you'd done your part. The shrine was a single slab of grey granite set in a ring of white river stones, worn smooth by ten years of Felix's boots and thirty years of other feet before hers. No carving marked it. No inscription. The village children used to ask what god lived there, and old Mother Cassel would say, *the quiet one*, and that was the whole of the theology anyone in Warden's Valley had ever needed.

Felix stopped at the ring's edge and did not step inside. Isolde stood a pace behind her, arms crossed against the morning cold, watching with the particular stillness of someone cataloguing everything for later use.

"You want to know about my oath," Felix said to the stone. "Fine. But we do it here."

"Why here?"

"Because if I'm going to say things out loud that I've never said out loud, I'd rather say them where he can hear."

"He?" Isolde's voice sharpened. "The god has a name?"

"Not anymore." Felix crouched, touched two fingers to the cold granite, the way she had every morning since she was nine years old and Sergeant Bray first dragged her up this hill. "Not for ten years. We called him the Quiet Brother when I was young. Some of the older ones called him the Door-Keeper. The company just called him *him*." She straightened. "You wanted my story, astronomer. Sit down. It's not short."

Isolde didn't sit. She never sat when she was nervous; Felix had learned that much in three days of guarded coexistence. Instead she drifted along the ring of stones, reading them like pages, and Felix supposed that to a woman who read dead stars, a circle of river rocks might as well be an open book.

"The Thornbury Company," Felix began. "Forty-one sworn swords, when there were forty-one of us. We weren't from here originally. We came over the pass nineteen years ago, hired as caravan guard, and the valley took us in during a bad winter, and—" she shrugged "—we stayed. That's the shape of most mercenary companies' endings. They stop moving."

"And the oath?"

"The oath came first." Felix's hand drifted to her collarbone without her permission. Beneath the wool of her shirt, the mark sat where it had always sat: a thin line of silvered skin, like a scar shaped like a keyhole turned sideways. She'd stopped seeing it years ago. Now, under Isolde's gaze, she was aware of it like a splinter. "Every company swears to something. Most swear to coin, or to each other, or to whatever war-god is fashionable that decade. Ours swore to him. Kneeling on this stone, all forty-one of us, hand to hand and blade to blade. *We hold the line so others may cross. We stand at the edge so the middle stays safe.*" The words came out in the cadence they'd been drilled into, unbroken by ten years of disuse. Her mouth knew them better than she did. "*Where you set your seal, nothing passes. Where you turn your back, nothing follows.*"

She stopped. The wind moved through the bare trees above the shrine, and somewhere down in the village a dog barked twice and gave up.

"That's the whole of it? A boundary formula?"

"That's the part you'd find interesting." Felix finally turned to face her. "There's more. There's the part where he answers. Every sworn sword hears it once, the night they take the oath—a voice like a door closing somewhere very far away, and then your own name spoken back to you. Not the name you were born with. Your true name. The one that fits the lock of you." She tapped her collarbone. "This appears the next morning. And after that, you can feel him. All the time. Like standing near a large warm thing in a dark room. You don't see it, but you know exactly where it is."

Isolde had gone very still. "And ten years ago?"

"Ten years ago, midwinter night, every sworn sword in the company woke at the same hour screaming." Felix kept her voice level. It had taken years of practice to keep it level. "Not from pain. From absence. Like a limb torn off you never knew you had. We ran to the shrine and the stone was cold—colder than stone has any right to be in winter, cold enough to burn—and the warmth was gone. The presence. Whatever it was we'd been leaning against our whole careers, it wasn't there anymore."

"What happened to the company?"

"What happens to any congregation when the god stops answering." Felix laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Some decided he'd died. Some decided he'd never existed and we'd all been mad together, which was easier. Some—" her jaw tightened "—some decided we'd been abandoned, and that abandonment was an answer too. Forty-one swords became twenty within the year. Twenty became eleven. Eleven became me, and Bray, and a handful who stayed because staying was all they knew how to do."

"Bray. Your sergeant."

"My sergeant." Felix looked at the stone. "He still comes up here every dawn. Still kneels. Still says the words into the silence like a man posting letters to a house that burned down. I used to think it was faith. Lately I wonder if it's just grief wearing armor."

* * *

They walked down from the shrine as the sun climbed, taking the long path along the wardline because Felix wanted to show her—the crack, the hairline fracture she'd found on patrol, the wound in the valley's skin that had started all of this. Isolde walked beside her in silence for a long while, and Felix had begun to suspect the silence was hostile when the astronomer finally spoke.

"The mark on your collarbone. May I see it?"

Felix hesitated. Then she pulled the collar of her shirt aside.

Isolde leaned close. Her breath was cold against Felix's skin, and her eyes—grey eyes, starless-grey now that there were no stars left to reflect in anything—narrowed with an intensity that made Felix want to step back.

"A keyhole," Isolde murmured. "Turned sideways. Do you know what that means?"

"I assumed it meant I was locked. It's never opened."

"It means you're a door." Isolde straightened, and her voice had changed—lower, faster, the voice of a woman assembling something terrible at speed. "Felix. Tell me again the exact wording of the oath. All of it. Word for word."

"*We hold the line so others may cross. We stand at the edge so the middle stays safe. Where you set your seal, nothing passes. Where you turn your back, nothing follows.*"

"And the answer. The name he spoke back to you. What was it?"

Felix opened her mouth. Closed it. Something cold walked down her spine.

"I don't know," she said slowly. "I've asked myself that a hundred times over ten years. I remember hearing it. I remember it fitting. But the moment I try to recall the sound of it, it's gone—like a word in a dream. I know I have a true name. I cannot tell you what it is."

Isolde stopped walking. She turned and gripped Felix's arm hard enough to leave marks, and her face had gone the color of the ash she claimed to read.

"Say the description again. The presence. The warmth in the dark room. The door closing far away. The seal. The line that must be held so things don't pass." Each phrase landed like a hammer on a nail. "Felix, I spent six years at the Aldermere court observatory mapping the old cult-lines of the pre-dynastic faiths. There was a god the scholars couldn't classify because he had no temples, no priests, no festivals—he only had *edges*. Every border-shrine, every wardstone, every threshold-warding in the oldest records carries his signature. The Door-Keeper. The Silent One. The god of boundaries themselves—not walls, not war, but the sacred fact of *between*."

The world tilted slightly. Felix found herself looking at the wardline—the low, unremarkable line of buried stones she'd walked and maintained her entire adult life without understanding them—and seeing it for the first time.

"The wards," she said. "The valley's wards. I've tended them since I was a girl. Everyone here does—it's just chores, like mending fences. Nobody remembers who built them or how they work. They just... work."

"They work because they're his body." Isolde's grip hadn't loosened. "Or his bones, at least. A god of boundaries doesn't live in a heaven, Felix. He lives in every border ever drawn in his name. Your valley isn't warded by magic. It's warded by *him*—by the last functioning piece of a god your company swore itself to. And ten years ago, when he went silent—"

"He didn't die," Felix whispered. The cold in her spine spread outward, into her hands, her teeth. "He was taken. Caged. Something reached into every border in the world and pulled the keeper out of the door."

"And left the doors unlocked." Isolde released her arm and stepped back, pressing both hands flat against her own ribs as if holding herself together. "That's why the stars went dark. That's why the wards failed. That's why things are crossing borders that should be impassable. Felix, your god isn't missing. Your god is *imprisoned*, and whatever holds him holds the whole architecture of the world's edges in its fist."

* * *

They ended up back at Felix's cottage because neither of them could bear the shrine anymore, and because Isolde's questions had become the kind that required a table to slam fists upon. Felix built up the fire. Isolde paced. The kettle sang and nobody poured it.

"So the oath still binds," Isolde said. "Even though he's silent. Even though he's gone. You've felt it these ten years—the compulsion. Holding lines. Standing watch. Refusing to let things pass."

"It's not compulsion." Felix fed the fire harder than it needed. "It's just... who I am. I keep the border because keeping the border is mine."

"Felix." Isolde's voice was gentle in a way that was worse than sharp. "I watched you fight three days ago, at the ford, when those memory-things came through. You planted yourself between them and the village and you did not move one inch backward, even when one of them wore your own face. That isn't temperament. That's theology. You are a living clause in a contract whose other signatory has been kidnapped."

Felix stared into the flames. The truth of it settled over her like snowfall—soft, relentless, burying. Ten years of believing she'd chosen her life. Ten years of patrols and wardstone-mending and kneeling at a silent shrine, all of it perhaps nothing but the muscle-memory of a promise made to a prisoner.

"If he's alive," she said at last, "then the oath isn't broken. It's just... unanswered. Which means I'm still bound to a god who can't hear me, fighting for borders he can't hold, wearing his mark on my skin like a—" she stopped.

"Like a keyhole," Isolde finished quietly.

The fire popped. Outside, the blind sky pressed down on the valley, starless and wrong, and Felix felt the full weight of it for the first time: the sky itself was a locked door with its keeper gone.

"There's something else," Isolde said. She had stopped pacing. She stood at the window with her back to the room, and when she spoke again her voice had lost its scholar's precision and gained something rawer. "When I predicted the star-death—when I stood in front of the court with my charts and told them the sky was going to go out—they didn't arrest me immediately. First the king smiled at me. And he said—" her breath caught, restarted "—he said, *Astronomer Vane, you mistake the bars of a cage for the death of the birds.*"

Felix went cold. "The king knew."

"The king knew. Or the thing wearing the king knew." Isolde turned. In the firelight her face was hollowed and fierce. "I ran because I understood, three days too late, that the star-death wasn't a catastrophe. It was a *construction*. Someone built that darkness, Felix, and they built it around a prison, and the prison holds a god of boundaries, and the only living people still bound to that god by active oath are the scattered remains of your mercenary company." She crossed the room in four strides and stood over Felix, firelight catching the desperate geometry of her face. "Which means the key to that cage—the only key—might be sitting in a cottage in a forgotten valley, drinking tea she never poured."

Felix looked down at her hands. Callused, steady, ordinary hands. Hands that had held a sword for twenty years and a wardstone for longer, and never once wondered why the work felt like prayer.

"I didn't ask for any of this," she said.

"No. Neither did I. I asked for a sky I could map, and instead I got a sky that lied to me for six years." Isolde sank into the chair across from her, and suddenly all the ferocity drained out of her, leaving something younger and more exhausted underneath. "I've been running for a month, Felix. Sleeping in ditches. Burning my own research to stay warm. And the whole time, the only thought keeping me moving was that somewhere there might be one other person in the world who'd been abandoned by the same thing that abandoned me." She laughed, short and bitter. "The stars went out and I lost my life's work. Your god went silent and you lost your—what did you call it? The warm thing in the dark room. Two women, ten years and a thousand miles apart, both left talking to something that stopped answering."

Felix studied her across the small table. The disgraced astronomer with ash-stained cuffs and a fugitive's hollow cheeks, who had walked into a valley that hated strangers and refused to leave, because leaving meant walking back into the dark alone.

"We're not allies," Felix said. "I don't trust you. You read ashes and talk to kings in your sleep and you look at my oath-mark like it's a specimen."

"I don't trust you either. You're a mercenary who maintains magical wards she doesn't understand and kills things that wear familiar faces without hesitation."

"Good," Felix said. "Then we understand each other."

Something flickered at the corner of Isolde's mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where one might someday learn to live. "Then we're agreed. A reluctant alliance between two abandoned women, formed over mutual suspicion and cold tea."

"The tea's not cold yet." Felix rose, finally poured it, and pushed a cup across the table. "Drink. Because if your theory is right—if my oath is the key to that cage—then we have a problem neither of us has said out loud yet."

"Which is?"

Felix wrapped her hands around her own cup and felt the heat seep into palms that had gone strangely numb.

"To use a key, you have to carry it to the lock. And the lock, as far as I can tell, is sitting on a throne in Aldermere, behind the eyes of a king who already knows my face." She met Isolde's gaze. "So the real question, astronomer, is this: how does a disgraced stargazer and a half-orphaned sellsword walk into the capital of a kingdom ruled by the thing that caged a god—without it simply reaching out and closing its fist around the only key it doesn't yet control?"

Isolde lifted her cup. Her hand, Felix noticed, was not quite steady.

"Badly," she said. "We'll almost certainly do it badly. But I know a road out of the valley that the wards won't miss us on—and Felix, we need to leave before the moon finishes rising. Because I've been counting the nights since the stars went dark, and by my arithmetic, the thirteenth moon-turn begins in nine days."
