## 6. The Road Out of Nowhere

**Felix**

The wardstone at the western gate had been warm her whole life.

Felix had never thought about that until the morning she pressed her palm against it and felt nothing but cold granite. Ten days since the stars went out. Ten days since the wards fell like cut strings and the things wearing borrowed faces came through. The village council had voted—four voices for, three against, one abstention—to let Isolde Vane leave, and to let Felix go with her, because Felix was the only one among them who had ever sworn a sword to anything.

The stone should have been warm. It was the first thing she'd ever touched as a child, before she could name it: the low grey pillar at the valley's mouth, humming faintly under her small hand like a sleeping animal. Every Warden's Valley child learned its warmth the way they learned their mother's voice. Now it was just rock. Whatever had lived in it had gone with the stars, or been taken with them, and the valley stood open to the world for the first time in living memory.

"You keep touching it," Isolde said behind her. She stood with her pack already shouldered, her telescope wrapped in oilcloth across her back like a sword she didn't know how to draw. "You'll wear your fingerprints into it."

"It used to be warm."

"I know." Isolde's voice was gentle in a way that always surprised Felix, coming from a woman whose face otherwise seemed built entirely of angles and judgment. "I read the old survey records. The valley sat on a node of the boundary-god's power. When he—" she paused, corrected herself, "—when *it* went silent, the warmth should have faded over years. Not overnight. Not unless something pulled the rest of it out."

"Like a cork from a bottle."

"Worse. Like marrow from a bone." Isolde looked up the road, north and west, toward the pass. "We should move. I want the high ground by nightfall, and I want it while there's still light to see what the light looks like."

That was Isolde all over. She said things like *what the light looks like* as though everyone spent their days cataloguing such things, and somehow made you feel ignorant for not having wondered yourself.

Felix took her hand off the cold stone. She did not look back at the village—she'd said her goodbyes at dawn, awkward ones, Maren pressing a sack of dried plums into her arms and old Tammer gripping her wrist too hard and saying nothing at all—and she stepped over the threshold of the wardline for the first time in thirty-one years.

She expected it to feel like something. A shiver, a tearing, a bell tolling somewhere in her chest. The oath-mark on her forearm—a thin silver line coiling from wrist to elbow, inked into her skin the night her company swore themselves to the Silent One—gave no sign at all. It hadn't given any sign in ten years. But she had half-believed that crossing the border would wake it, that outside the valley's dampening hush the mark might remember what it was for.

Nothing. The line lay quiet on her arm, silver as ever, dumb as a scar.

The road beyond the gate was ordinary dirt, rutted by cart wheels, edged with gorse going gold in the autumn cold. Ordinary. That was almost worse than strangeness. She had imagined the outside world her whole life—built it out of travelers' tales and the almanac woodcuts Isolde now pored over every night—and here it was, just a road, walking away from everything she knew one boot-print at a time.

They climbed through the morning. The valley sank behind them, green and small, folding itself into the hills like a thing hiding. By noon they had reached the saddle of the pass, and Felix stopped, because the world opened up below her and it was wrong in a way that made her stomach drop.

She had seen the sky her entire life. She had trusted it. In the valley the stars had been constant—dimmed, perhaps, filtered through whatever quiet the wards cast, but present, wheeling overhead in their ancient orders. Isolde had told her, the night everything ended, that the stars had not dimmed but died, all of them, everywhere, at once. Felix had believed her the way you believe a physician about a tumor: intellectually, dreadfully, without feeling it in the body.

Now she felt it. From the height of the pass, under a sky the color of wet slate, the land rolled away to the horizon and there were no stars above any of it. None. The sun hung where it should—pale, milky, a coin seen through cloth—but the blue around it was empty in a way sky is never supposed to be empty. It wasn't night-dark. It was *absence*-dark, a ceiling painted the color of nothing, and looking at it too long made Felix's eyes water as though she were staring into a well.

And the moon. The moon was swollen. It rode the afternoon sky like a bruise, fat and yellow-white and faintly pulsing, or perhaps that was only her pulse interfering, her own blood arguing with what her eyes reported. It was too big. Everyone in the valley had noticed it, had muttered about it, had blamed bad air or bad eyes. From up here, with the whole naked sky laid open, Felix understood that it was not bad eyes. The moon was growing the way a wound swells before it breaks.

"That's not possible," she said. Her voice came out flat.

"No," Isolde agreed. She had unwrapped the telescope without being asked and braced it on her walking staff, squinting through the brass. "Lunar apparent diameter has increased roughly nine percent in eleven days. Orbital mechanics do not permit that. Tidal forces alone would be drowning coastal cities." She lowered the scope. Her face, when she turned, held the particular stillness Felix had learned meant fear wearing scholarship's clothes. "Which means either the moon is not obeying the laws that govern moons, or those laws have been suspended locally. Both possibilities are worse than the other."

"Say the second one again slower."

"The rules are being broken on purpose, Felix. By something with the reach to break them." Isolde wrapped the telescope away with quick, precise hands. "The Unmourned doesn't want to conquer the sky. It wants to *edit* it. Everything we're walking toward is a rough draft of the world it intends."

Felix looked south, back toward the valley, and felt the pull of it like a hook behind her sternum. Go home. Bolt the door. Live in the last honest corner of a dishonest world.

But the wardstone was cold, and the things with borrowed faces had come through anyway, and one of them had whispered her true name into the dark with her own dead friend's mouth. There was no home left that matched the home in her head. So she turned north instead, and walked.

* * *

**Isolde**

Isolde had catalogued four hundred and six nights of sky from the observatory at Aldermere before the court had stripped her title, and she had never once been afraid of the dark. Darkness was data. Darkness was the space between signals. She had taught first-year students that the astronomer's true instrument was not the telescope but the disciplined refusal to flinch.

She flinched constantly now. She hid it, because Felix watched her with those level gray eyes and Isolde's authority rested entirely on seeming like someone who understood what was happening, but the truth was that every hour outside the valley's borders scraped her nerves raw. The world was loud here. Not sound—though it was that too, wind and birdsong and the far-off clangor of some village smithy, sounds the valley smothered to a polite murmur—but *loud* underneath, a pressure against the skin, the sensation of standing too close to an enormous speaking crowd. Magic. Wild, unwarded, ambient magic, thick enough that her old scars ached and the hair on her forearms lifted when she slept. She had forgotten what unshielded country felt like. She hated how much of it she remembered.

And Felix walked through it untouched.

Isolde had noticed it the first day and told herself it was coincidence. By the third day she had stopped lying to herself. The pressure that made Isolde's teeth hum slid off Felix like rain off oiled canvas. The swollen moon's baleful weight, which gave Isolde headaches like a vise behind the eyes, seemed not to register on the woman at all. Once, crossing a stream, Felix had paused with her hand in the water and frowned slightly, and Isolde had asked what was wrong, and Felix had said, "It feels like it's paying attention to me," and then shrugged and waded on.

A vessel, Isolde thought, watching her walk ahead on the road, broad-shouldered and unhurried, sword riding easy at her hip. The archives would know. The archives always knew; that was their single virtue and their horror. Somewhere in Aldermere's sealed stacks there would be a record of what the Unmourned had made her, thirteen years ago, and Isolde found she dreaded reading it more than she dreaded the thing on the throne.

Because—and this was the thought she kept turning over at night, sleepless beside their small fire—the alternative explanations were worse. If Felix were merely lucky, merely dense to magic, that would be one thing. But nobody was dense to *that*. Which meant protection, and protection meant design, and design meant that every step Felix took toward the capital might be a step along a path someone else had drawn before she was born.

"Your turn to think out loud," Felix said, without turning around. "You've been quiet since the pass."

"I'm always quiet."

"You're never quiet. You narrate. Degrees of arc, barometric pressure, the theological implications of moss." Felix glanced back, and there it was—that dry half-smile that had begun appearing sometime around the second day, to Isolde's private alarm. "Out with it."

Isolde considered several deflections and discarded them. Lies required energy she was saving for better uses. "You don't feel it. Any of it. The ambient magic, the moon's pressure. You walk through a screaming room as if it's silent."

Felix was quiet for a dozen strides. "In the valley, I never felt anything either. No magic, no pull, nothing. Tammer used to say I had a hole where other people kept their souls." She said it lightly, but Isolde heard the old wound under the lightness, worn smooth by decades of handling. "Maybe the hole goes deeper than the valley."

"Or maybe the hole was cut to fit something."

"Cheerful."

"I'm an astronomer. We're paid to notice that the beautiful things are mostly on fire."

That earned a real laugh, short and rusty, and Isolde felt an absurd flush of triumph at it, which she examined later, by firelight, with scholarly disapproval.

They made camp that night in the lee of a ruined waystation—an old toll-house from the border wars, its roof fallen in, its walls still standing. Felix built the fire with the competence of someone who had slept outdoors for thirty years, and cooked oats and dried plums into something nearly edible, and took first watch without discussion, because that was the shape their arrangement had quietly become: Felix handled the ground, Isolde handled the sky, and neither remarked on how naturally the division had settled.

Isolde lay with her journal on her knees, sketching the swollen moon by firelight, recording its position against the starless dark. Without stars there was no fixed frame of reference; the moon drifted against pure black, and measuring its motion was like timing a swimmer in a featureless sea. She worked until her hand cramped, and then she looked up and found Felix watching the darkness with her sword across her knees, and the sight steadied her in a way she resented and relied upon in equal measure.

"Do you miss it?" Felix asked suddenly. "The capital. Home."

Isolde turned the question over honestly, because Felix could smell dishonesty the way dogs smelled fear. "I miss the observatory. The building, the instruments, the work. The city itself—" She paused. "I gave twenty-two years to that court. I predicted the star-death eight months early and they took my chair for alarmism, and then when it happened they took my freedom for knowing too much. What I miss is a version of Aldermere that may never have existed. Grief for a stage set." She closed the journal. "Do you miss the valley?"

"Yes." No hesitation. "And I don't trust missing it. The valley was a lie I lived inside. Warm floors over a cellar." Felix fed a stick to the fire. "But it was my lie. Thirty-one years of it. That counts for something, even if it was built on top of a cage."

Isolde looked at her—at the oath-mark catching firelight along her forearm, at the face of a woman who had lost her god, her home, and her history inside a fortnight and still sat straight-backed against the dark—and felt something shift in her chest that she filed, firmly, under *later*.

"Sleep," Felix said. "I'll wake you for second watch."

For once, Isolde obeyed without argument.

* * *

**Felix**

The bandits came at dusk on the fourth day, and Felix killed two of them, and the strange part—the part she would turn over for nights afterward—was how easy it was.

They had descended out of the hills where the road narrowed between old quarry cuts, six of them, ragged and desperate-looking, led by a big man with a billhook and the flat certainty of someone who had done this many times. "Coin and the woman's instruments," he said. "Keep the swords, we've no use for trouble." As if politeness made it a transaction rather than a robbery.

Felix had fought bandits before—in the old days, escorting caravans on contract, back when the company rode together and the Silent One's blessing moved in her arm like warm water. She remembered those fights clearly: the god's presence lending speed, damping fear, making her body feel less like hers and more like an instrument played by something patient and vast. She had assumed, in the ten silent years, that she'd been fighting on memory and habit alone. She had assumed the blessing was gone.

It was not gone. It was different.

The billhook man came first, swinging wide, telegraphing everything, and Felix read him the way Isolde read the sky—noticing, cataloguing, *knowing*. His weight was on his back foot. His grip was too tight. He expected her to retreat. She stepped inside the swing instead, close enough to smell his sweat, and put her pommel into his throat, and he folded with a sound like a dropped sack. The second one had a knife and more sense and less luck; he came at her low and she broke his wrist against her knee and put him down with an elbow, and the whole exchange lasted perhaps six heartbeats.

The remaining four froze. Bandits, she remembered from the old days, were mathematicians of violence—they fought only when the sums favored them, and the sums had just inverted spectacularly. One of them actually took a step backward.

"Walk away," Felix said. Her voice came out calm. Too calm. Inside, her heart hammered with something that was not fear—or not only fear. It was the feeling of a lock turning, of a door she'd believed painted shut swinging open at her touch. "Last offer."

They walked. They dragged their leader with them, groaning, and vanished into the quarry shadows, and the road was theirs again, and Felix stood in the middle of it breathing hard and staring at her own hands.

Isolde came up the track at a run, telescope abandoned, knife drawn—when had she even drawn it?—and skidded to a stop, taking in the two unconscious men, the empty road, Felix standing in the last red light of a sun setting into a starless sky.

"You're not hurt."

"No."

"You didn't call out. You didn't wait. You just—" Isolde gestured at the aftermath. "Six heartbeats, Felix. I counted."

"So did I." Felix flexed her fingers. The oath-mark lay quiet and silver, no brighter than it had been in ten years, and yet. And yet. "Something helped me. Or something woke up. I don't—I can't tell the difference anymore." She laughed, short and unsteady. "Ten years of nothing, and the moment I cross the border, it's like the silence was a door and I finally pushed hard enough."

Isolde was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, she had her scholar's voice on, the careful one, each word weighed before release. "Or the silence was never absence. In the valley, the wards dampened everything—including, perhaps, whatever connection you carry. Outside, with the wards down and the Unmourned's power rising..." She trailed off, and Felix watched her arrive at the conclusion and visibly decide whether to say it.

"Say it."

"If the god's power moves in you even now—even muzzled, even caged—then the cage is thinner than we hoped. Or closer than we feared." Isolde met her eyes. "Either way, you are not carrying a dead oath. You're carrying a live wire, and something on the other end knows you're holding it."

The wind came down the quarry cut, cold and smelling of iron and coming winter, and somewhere far off a bird cried once and got no answer, because the sky was empty and had been for eleven days.

Felix looked down the road, north and west, toward the distant smudge of the lowlands and, beyond them, the candlelit sprawl of Aldermere, where a king wore a crown that wore him back. She had left the valley to protect it, or to escape it, or because there was nothing left there to stay for—she still hadn't decided which story was true. But standing in the ruin of the evening with her blood singing and her hands steady, she felt something she had not felt since the stars went out.

Not safety. Nothing was safe. But *capability*. The plain animal knowledge that she could meet what was coming and it would cost her, and she would pay, and she would still be standing after.

"Then we'd better get to the capital," she said, "before whoever's holding the other end comes looking."

They walked on into the dark, two exiles on an empty road, and behind them the swollen moon rose fat and yellow over the hills, close enough now that Felix could see, when she looked too long, that its surface was not cratered at all.

It was veined. Like something alive, pressing against the inside of its own skin.
