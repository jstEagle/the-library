## 7. Aldermere Under a Blind Sky

The city announced itself by smell before it appeared out of the fog: woodsmoke and tallow, horse dung and wet wool, the river's green rot—and beneath all of it, faint as a lie told well, something scorched. Isolde Vane stood at the crest of the last hill on the pilgrim road, her travel cloak stiff with three days of rain, and looked down at the capital of Aldermere, which had not changed at all, which had changed completely.

Aldermere still wore its seven hills like a crown. The palace still climbed the highest one, its gilded spires catching light that did not exist—because there was no sun to catch it, no moon, no stars, only a low grey ceiling of cloud that had hung over the city for eleven days now, thick and unbroken, so that the whole capital lived beneath a lid. And yet the spires gleamed. She squinted against it, disbelieving, until she understood: they had polished them. Someone had gone up with cloths and ladders and burnished gold under a sky that gave nothing back.

*They're pretending*, she thought, and the thought went through her like a splinter finding old flesh. *The whole city is pretending.*

Below, the gates stood open. That was wrong too. In her memory—the good years, before disgrace, before she'd learned what the court did to people who said true things too loudly—the gates closed at dusk and opened at dawn, and the queue to enter stretched half a mile. Now there was no queue. There was instead a line of criers in crown livery, their voices bouncing off the walls, and even from this distance Isolde could make out the rhythm of it, the practiced cadence of official grief.

She pulled her hood lower and joined the trickle of carters and peddlers moving downhill.

---

Inside the walls, the city was lit like a wake.

Every street burned candles. Not oil lamps, not lanterns—candles, thousands upon thousands, set in windowsills and doorways and niches in the old walls, guttering behind horn panes, drowning in their own wax. The effect was beautiful in the way that funerals are beautiful: every flame a small insistence, *we are still here, we are still here,* and underneath it the whole city holding its breath. The smoke of them lay over the streets in a haze that made the daylight—such grey daylight as leaked through the cloud-lid—look like late evening always.

Isolde walked slowly, cataloguing. This was how she knew herself to be home: she could not stop reading the sky even when the sky was gone, and she could not stop reading streets either. Here the chandlers' quarter, where candle prices would have tripled overnight and someone was getting rich off terror. There the fountain where she'd thrown a stolen apple to Marta Sennet's dog twenty years ago; the dog was long dead, the fountain dry, drained—she realized after a moment—because the aqueduct ran along the high ridge and the wardens who maintained it were court appointments, and the court had other concerns now.

And everywhere, everywhere, the posters.

They were pasted over doors, nailed to posts, layered three deep on the customs house wall. Crown printers' marks in the corner. The same block of text repeated across the city in black ink going soft with damp:

*THE STARS HAVE NOT ABANDONED US. FOREIGN WITCHES HAVE STOLEN THEM. THE CROWN WILL RESTORE THE HEAVENS AND PUNISH THE THIEVES. REPORT ALL STRANGERS. REPORT ALL READERS OF SIGNS.*

Isolde stopped in front of one, close enough that her breath fogged the paper. *Readers of signs.* Her own title, her own life's work, converted into an accusation in six words. She thought of the night she'd stood in the royal observatory with her instruments trembling and told the Chamber of Petitioners that within a season the stars would go out—not dimmed, *out*, extinguished root and branch, and that the cause sat closer than any foreign shore. She remembered Lord Chancellor Merrivale's face going smooth as poured wax, and the way the guards had appeared without being summoned, and the warrant that followed her out of the city faster than her own reputation could.

*Report all readers of signs.* Someone had scrawled beneath this poster in charcoal: *the stars were never ours to lose.* The handwriting had been scrubbed at, hastily, but the words survived. Isolde felt something loosen in her chest—a knot she hadn't known she was carrying. Someone in this city was still writing true things in charcoal. Small comfort. Comfort anyway.

"Move along." A guard's hand landed on her shoulder, not hard, but with the weight of routine behind it. He was young, maybe nineteen, and his eyes had the glassy look of someone who had not slept properly since the sky ended. "No loitering near the notices."

"I was reading," Isolde said mildly. "I didn't know reading was a crime yet."

His grip tightened. Then he actually looked at her—hooded, rain-stiff, an old woman with a pilgrim's staff and a scholar's hands—and whatever he saw made him let go. Or perhaps he simply hadn't the energy for arrests today. "Move along," he repeated, quieter, and rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand, and Isolde walked on, her heart drumming a slow, cold beat against her ribs.

*Eleven days.* She counted everything in days now. Eleven days since the stars died, nine since she'd crossed into the valley that forgot magic, four since she'd left it again with a mercenary woman whose oath-mark burned cold under her sleeve and whose eyes held more sense than the entire Chamber of Petitioners. Felix Thornbury was somewhere behind her on the road, following at a distance because Isolde had insisted—if they entered the city together, two strangers traveling side by side, they'd be remembered. Memory was dangerous currency now. Everything about this city was designed to make forgetting easier.

---

By late afternoon she reached the Street of Astrolabes.

It had been renamed, of course. New sign hanging where the old one used to be: the Street of Loyal Instruments. The irony was so perfect it nearly made her laugh aloud, standing there in the drizzle with her hand pressed flat against a doorpost she had leaned against ten thousand times. The shops were shuttered—all but one, a lens-grinder's, where a single candle burned behind horn glass and a shadow moved inside, patient, working.

Her observatory stood at the top of the hill above it. She had spent thirty years climbing those steps. She knew each one intimately: the seventh, cracked, that caught your toe; the thirty-first, wider than the rest, where apprentices used to sit with wine after dark; the landing halfway up where the wind came off the river and smelled of iron. She climbed them now in the failing light with her hood up and her heart in her throat, past the bronze plaque—*ROYAL OBSERVATORY OF ALDERMERE*—which someone had defaced, carefully, with a chisel: the word ROYAL struck through, nothing added in its place. Just the absence. Just the hole.

The great doors were barred from outside. Sealed. A strip of crown wax across the seam, stamped with the sigil of the Chancellor's office. *Closed by order pending restoration of celestial harmony.* Restoration of celestial harmony—as if the stars were a leaky roof.

But Isolde had been born knowing how buildings keep secrets, and the observatory had been hers longer than it had ever been the crown's. She circled to the north side, where the ivy grew thick over the foundation stones, and found the loose block she'd discovered as a girl of fifteen, sneaking back in after curfew with a borrowed sextant. It moved exactly as it always had, grudgingly, grinding grit, and beyond it the maintenance passage breathed out air that smelled of dust and cold brass and thirty years of her own life.

She went in sideways, dragging her pack, and stood a moment in the dark letting her eyes adjust, listening to the building settle around her like an old animal recognizing its keeper.

*Homecoming as burglary*, she thought. *Well. I always did my best work at night.*

---

The dome room was untouched.

That was the cruelest part. She had expected ransack—seized instruments, smashed lenses, her papers confiscated or burned. Instead everything waited exactly where she'd left it the night she fled: the great equatorial telescope swaddled in its canvas shroud, the orrery on its pedestal with its brass planets frozen mid-orbit, her charts rolled and racked along the curved walls, her desk bearing a cold cup of tea gone to a skin of mold. As if the room itself had been arrested mid-breath. As if the crown wanted the observatory preserved precisely as evidence of her treason, ready to be shown to visitors: *here is where the witch worked; see how orderly evil can be.*

Or—or—someone else was using it, and using it gently, and had not wanted to disturb anything. Isolde turned that thought over and did not like its weight.

She uncovered the telescope first, out of pure need. Her hands shook doing it, which infuriated her; she was fifty-eight years old and her hands had not shaken at an instrument since she was a girl, and now they shook because the canvas came away and the brass tube stood gleaming in the candlelight and for one stupid moment she wanted to weep at the sheer dumb faithfulness of metal.

There was no sky to look at. She aimed the tube anyway, out of ritual, out of grief, at the patch of ceiling-cloud where Cassiopeia should have been, and saw nothing, of course—grey murk, the lid of the world. But the act of looking steadied her the way prayer is said to steady the devout. She had never prayed. She had only ever looked. Looking had been enough, until eleven days ago.

Then she crossed to her desk, knelt, and pried up the loose floorboard where she kept what mattered.

Her field journals were there, wrapped in oilcloth, all of them—including the ones the court believed destroyed. Thirty years of readings. And at the bottom, wrapped separately, the thing she had come back for above all else: her master star-chart, drawn the winter before her disgrace, when she had first noticed the anomaly. Not the stars as they appeared—but the stars as they *behaved*. Every constellation mapped with its drift, its proper motion, its pulse. And threaded through all of it, in red ink, the pattern she had seen once and then could not stop seeing: a lattice. A geometry underlying the sky, regular as masonry, as if the constellations were not scattered fires but bars—

As if the heavens were a cage viewed from outside.

She spread the chart across her desk and weighted its corners with the cold teacup, a brass weight, a stone from the riverbank that some student had given her decades ago because it had a hole worn clean through it and she'd kept it for sentiment. Candlelight moved over the red lattice. She had spent two years telling herself she was imagining it. She had spent the last eleven days knowing she wasn't.

"You're supposed to be in Vharen," said a voice behind her. "Or dead. The Chancellor prefers dead, but Vharen would do."

Isolde did not startle. Thirty years of night work teaches you that sudden voices are usually just students, and besides, she had heard footsteps on the seventh step—the cracked one—ten minutes ago and chosen to pretend otherwise. She turned.

In the doorway stood a slight man in the grey robe of a minor clerk, holding a shuttered lamp, and she knew him instantly despite the years and the fear carved newly into his face: Tam Ostell, her last apprentice, the boy who used to sit on the thirty-first step. He had aged twenty years in five.

"Tam." His name came out rougher than she intended. "You're the one keeping the room."

"Someone had to." He shut the door and dropped the bar, quick and practiced—he had done this before, many times, she realized. "They sealed it to make an example of you, but the seals needed checking monthly, and I volunteered, and nobody volunteers for clerical work, so it's mine. I've aired it. Kept the damp off the charts. I couldn't—" His voice cracked, and he mastered it the way she'd taught him to master his hands on the focuser: firmly, without apology. "Everyone thinks you're a witch. They're saying it was foreign witches took the stars. There are burnings in Tanner's Square. Two so far. Neither of them knew anything about anything. One of them was a fortune-teller who was mostly a fraud, which apparently doesn't protect you anymore."

"The stars weren't stolen by witches, Tam."

"I know." He set the lamp down and came to the desk and looked at the red lattice, and she watched him understand it all over again, watching the same vertigo cross his face that had crossed hers two winters ago. "I know because I've been up here every month for five years, reading your notes, finishing your work, because somebody had to keep looking while everyone else agreed to look away. And eleven nights ago I saw—" He stopped. Started again. "Before the clouds closed in, the last hour, the stars didn't fade. They went out *in sequence*. Along the lines of your lattice. Like lamps being snuffed room by room by someone walking through the house."

The cold that settled into Isolde then was familiar; she had carried it since the night of the prediction. But hearing it confirmed—in sequence, in sequence, someone walking through the house—turned it from dread into architecture.

"It wasn't a theft," she said quietly. "It was a closing. Bars don't get stolen, Tam. They get *shut*." She put her finger on the chart, on the point where the lattice converged—a convergence she'd marked with a small circle and, in her most private notation, a single word. "And there's exactly one thing in all the old records that gets shut behind bars this elaborate. I found the reference three weeks ago, in a valley that magic forgot, in a shrine older than the kingdom. The god of boundaries. The Silent One. Something caged it, and the cage is the sky, and the thing that built the cage has been sitting on the throne of Aldermere for thirteen years wearing our king like a glove."

Tam stared at her. Outside, somewhere below, the criers were calling the evening proclamation, their voices thin through stone: *report all strangers, report all readers of signs.* The candles in the city guttered by their thousands, insisting *we are still here* to a sky that refused to answer.

"Why have you come back?" Tam asked. "You were safe away. You must know they'll kill you if they take you."

"Because the answer isn't in the valley and it isn't in the archives." Isolde began rolling the master chart with careful, steady hands—her hands had stopped shaking, she noticed; purpose did that. "The answer is in that palace on the hill, and in thirteen years of sealed records, and in whatever's left of King Aldous behind those eyes. I've run from it once already. Running cost me everything except the truth, and the truth is worth more than everything."

She tucked the chart into her coat, against her heart like a second spine.

"And I'm not alone anymore," she added, almost to herself. "There's a woman coming up that road behind me with a dead god's oath on her arm, and I think—I think the oath is the key to the whole cage. Which means before very long, the most wanted astronomer in Aldermere and a mercenary sworn to a silenced god are going to walk into the royal palace and ask the king what he's done with the sky."

Tam laughed—one broken, terrified, exhilarated note.

"God help us," he said.

"He's not answering," Isolde said, snuffing the lamp. "We'll have to manage."

* * *

They worked through the dark hours by candle stub, Tam copying the master chart in fragments small enough to hide in a clerk's sleeve, Isolde drafting what she could only think of as a siege plan written in mathematics: which records in the palace archive would hold the bargain's terms, which sealed annals covered the year of the plague, which servant corridors ran closest to the king's chambers. She knew the palace from a decade of summoned appearances, summoned lectures, summoned humiliations. She had hated every visit. Now every humiliating detail became a route, a door, a weakness.

Near dawn, Tam went to the window slit to check the sky out of habit and went rigid.

"Isolde." His voice was strange. "The clouds. Look at the clouds."

She joined him. Below, the city slept under its thousand candles, and above—above, the grey lid had thinned at its eastern edge, and through the tear showed not dawn and not stars, but a shape. Round. Swollen far beyond any moon she had ever charted, bloated and pale and rising where no moon had any business rising, its face marked not with the familiar seas but with patterns too regular, too deliberate, like a lattice of its own.

Isolde pressed her hands flat against the cold stone and felt the truth arrive in her body before her mind consented to it.

The thirteenth turn wasn't coming.

It had begun.
