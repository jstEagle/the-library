## 8. The Thing Wearing the Crown

The palace of Aldermere did not look like a place where a king lived. It looked like a place where a king was kept.

Felix stood in the antechamber with her back to the wall, the way she'd stood in a hundred hiring halls and garrison offices, and tried not to stare at the candles. There were hundreds of them—hundreds, in the middle of the day—guttering in iron sconces and silver candelabra and cheap tallow stubs jammed into bottles, because the great windows along the eastern wall had been shuttered with black cloth. Not curtained. Shuttered. Nailed shut, from the look of the frames, with the nails driven in crooked by hands that had hurried.

*They're afraid of the sky,* she thought. *The whole city is afraid of the sky, so they've nailed it out.*

Isolde stood beside her, hands folded, wearing the borrowed dress of a merchant's widow that made her look like exactly what she claimed to be: a woman come to petition the crown for restitution of seized property. Her petition papers were forged. Her name was false. Her eyes, above the modest veil she'd pinned back, kept moving—counting guards, counting doors, counting the seconds between the changing of the watch.

"Fourteen," she murmured, barely moving her lips. "Fourteen seconds between reliefs. Sloppy. It used to be ten."

"You counted the guard rotations of your own arrest warrant," Felix said under her breath.

"I counted everything in that observatory for eleven years. Old habits." A pause. "The one by the far door favors his left leg. Don't stand near him if this goes wrong."

"And if it goes right?"

Isolde's mouth curved, just slightly, beneath the veil. "Then we bow to a dead man and walk out again."

Felix's oath-mark itched.

It had been itching since they'd passed the city gates three days ago—a low, crawling heat along the inside of her left forearm, where the sigil of the Broken Company sat inked into her skin in the old way, cut first and filled with ash and salt. Ten years of silence, and the mark had never done anything but sit there, dumb as a scar. Now it woke at odd hours, throbbed when she dreamed, and here, in this candlelit room with its nailed-shut windows, it burned like a coal wrapped in skin.

She'd told herself it was the city. The valley had no magic; the city had too much, pressing against her like deep water. That was all. That had to be all.

"Herald's coming," Isolde breathed.

Doors opened at the far end of the antechamber, tall doors of black oak banded in gold, and a herald in livery the color of dried blood stepped through and struck the floor with a staff. "Their Majesties' justice is merciful and their patience brief," he announced, in the flat recitation of a man who had said the words four thousand times. "Petitioners will approach the throne in order. Speak plainly. Kneel quickly. Depart quietly."

*Depart quietly.* Felix filed that away. Palaces that needed to tell you how to leave were palaces where people didn't leave.

They joined the line of petitioners—a grain merchant with sweat-dark armpits, two sisters in mourning gray arguing over an inheritance, an old soldier with a petition folded so many times it had gone soft as cloth. The line moved through the doors into the audience hall, and Felix got her first look at the throne of Aldermere, and understood, finally, why the windows were shuttered.

The hall was vast and gilded and wrong. Every surface that could hold gold leaf held it—pillars wrapped in it, ceiling drowned in it, the great seal of Aldermere rendered behind the throne in hammered gold twenty feet across—and yet the whole of it felt dimmed, muffled, like a beautiful room seen through dirty glass. The candlelight didn't so much illuminate the gold as fail to escape it. And at the center of it, on a throne that might once have been oak and honest workmanship before someone buried it in gilt, sat King Aldous the Third, and beside him, on a lower seat, Queen Maren, who according to Isolde had not spoken a public word in six years.

The king was smiling.

That was the first thing. He was smiling as the grain merchant knelt and stammered through his grievance about border tariffs, and it was a good smile, a warm smile, a smile that reached his eyes—and that was what made Felix's neck prickle, because she had spent thirty years reading faces in mercenary camps and court antechambers, and smiles that reached the eyes were work. Even kind men performed them. This one wasn't performed. This one was *worn*, the way a coat is worn, easy and natural and fitted to the shoulders—

And then the grain merchant finished, and the king raised his hand in benediction, and Felix saw the hand.

It trembled. Fine, constant, bird-quick trembling, the fingers twitching against the armrest like something trapped beneath them trying to get out. The sleeve hid most of it, but the light caught the king's rings, and the rings chattered softly against the gilt armrest, a tiny insect sound that nobody else in the hall seemed to hear.

*He's fighting it,* Felix thought, and didn't know where the thought came from, only that it arrived whole and certain, the way knowledge arrives in dreams. *Something in him is still fighting.*

"Next."

They moved up. The sisters in gray knelt, wept over their inheritance, were granted it with a wave of the trembling hand. The old soldier knelt, asked for his back-pay from a war Felix had never heard of, and received a promise so smooth and warm that the old man wept too, and kissed the hem of the king's robe, and was helped away by guards who would forget him by supper.

Then it was their turn.

Felix knelt when Isolde knelt, head bowed, eyes down—the posture of a servant, a bodyguard, nobody. She felt the hall's attention slide over her like water over oil. Petitioners were furniture. Furniture was safe.

"Rise, lady," said the king, and his voice was honey poured slow. "You have traveled far to bring your grief to me. Let us see it eased."

Isolde rose and began her performance. Felix had watched her rehearse it in a roadside inn, the forged seals, the invented uncle, the land seized by a corrupt magistrate, and Isolde played it beautifully—grief without whining, dignity without pride, the exact pitch of a widow the crown could afford to be generous to. The king listened. The king nodded. The king smiled his warm, worn smile, and said all the right things, and Felix let her attention drift to the guards, to the doors, to the exits, doing the work her body knew how to do even while her mind screamed at her to keep watching the throne—

The oath-mark flared white-hot.

Felix bit down on the sound that tried to come out of her. Pain lanced from her wrist to her shoulder, and through the pain, through the roaring in her ears, she heard the king say, gently:

"Your companion may look up, lady. I do not eat servants."

The hall went very quiet. Or perhaps it had always been quiet, and Felix had only now noticed.

She looked up.

King Aldous the Third was looking directly at her—not at Isolde, not at the forged papers, at *her*—and the smile was still on his face, warm and generous and perfectly correct, and behind his eyes something else was looking out.

She couldn't have described it if she'd tried. It wasn't a shape or a color or a presence. It was more like depth—as though the king's eyes were windows onto a room much larger than his skull, a dark and patient vastness that had been sitting there a long, long time, and had just now turned its attention, with the unhurried interest of something that has never once been surprised, upon a mercenary from a valley that forgot magic.

The trembling hand had gone still.

"There you are," the king said softly.

The words dropped into Felix's stomach like stones into a well. She heard them fall and fall and never hit bottom.

"Your Majesty is kind," Isolde said, smooth as poured cream, stepping half in front of Felix. "My companion is simple, sire. A valley-born mute who keeps me safe on the roads. She means no—"

"She has a name," the king said.

Not a question. And Felix's whole body went cold, because she didn't—she had never had one anyone spoke aloud, she was Thornbury, Felix Thornbury, that was the whole of it, a name like a coat worn so long it had become skin—

"It burns her, doesn't it," the king went on, conversational, almost tender, tilting his head at Felix's clenched left fist. "The mark. It has been calling to her since she crossed my borders. Like recognizes like, after all." He leaned forward on the throne, and the candlelight ran along the gilt behind him until the whole hammered seal seemed to ripple, a golden tide rising around the silhouette of the man. "Ten years she has carried an oath with no voice to answer it. Do you know what that is, little warden? That is a door with no house. A lock waiting for its key. I have been *waiting* for its key."

"Your Majesty," Isolde said, and her voice was still smooth but Felix, standing close enough, saw the fine tremor in her jaw, "we are humble petitioners. We ask only—"

"Yes. Restitution. The magistrate. The land." The king waved a hand, gracious, magnanimous, and the rings chattered once against the armrest before going still again. "Granted. All of it. Consider your grief eased, lady, and go home to it." The dark behind his eyes brightened, focused, fixed on Felix with an attention that had weight, that pressed on her like a hand laid flat against her sternum. "But you—" The smile widened by a degree. Only a degree. On any other face it would have been nothing. On this face it was the whole sky going wrong. "You will stay a while. I think. I think you will find Aldermere very hard to leave."

Somewhere in the hall, a candle guttered out. Then another. Not blown—there was no wind—but snuffed, pinched out one by one down the length of the chamber, as though the darkness behind the king's eyes were tasting the light and finding it wanting.

And then, just for a heartbeat—one heartbeat, less—the mask slipped.

Felix saw it because she was looking for it, because thirty years of reading faces had taught her where masks are thin: the mouth, always the mouth. The warm smile stayed exactly where it was, but underneath it, behind it, through it, the king's actual mouth moved—a different shape, a desperate shape, shaping words with no breath behind them, silent as a fish drowning in air.

*Run.*

The candles steadied. The warmth flooded back into the royal face, seamless, total, and King Aldous the Third settled back onto his throne and said, in tones of liquid kindness, "Next petitioner," and the herald's staff struck the floor, and guards were suddenly, politely, at Felix's elbows.

"This way, mistress," one said. Not unkindly. Which was worse.

They were escorted out—not through the doors they'd entered, but through a side passage, down a corridor hung with tapestries of hunts that had never happened, past courtyards where the sky showed only as a strip of nailed-down gray between rooftops. The guards left them at a postern gate with bows and blessings and the unmistakable weight of being watched.

The street outside was candlelit and crowded and loud, and Felix walked three streets on legs that belonged to someone else before Isolde pulled her into the shadow of an archway and gripped her arms hard enough to bruise.

"What did he say to you?" Isolde whispered. "When I stepped forward. What did he—your face, Felix, I have never seen your face do that."

"He said 'there you are.'" Felix's voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice. "Like he'd been looking for me. Like he'd been looking for me for a long time." She looked down at her left arm. Through the sleeve, faint as banked coals, the oath-mark glowed. "Isolde. He called me a key."

Isolde had gone the color of tallow. Behind her eyes, Felix could see the astronomer working, fast and frightened, fitting the pieces together—the star-death, the fallen wards, the thing in the archives, and now this, the enemy itself leaning off its throne to look at them.

"A lock waiting for its key," Isolde repeated slowly. "An oath with no voice. Felix—if the Silent One is caged, if the cage needs an oath to open it—then your oath isn't just a bond. It's a mechanism. You're not carrying a promise." She swallowed. "You're carrying the latch."

Down the street, a bell began to toll. Then another. Then a dozen, spreading outward through the blind city, and in the doorway of every shop and shrine along the candlelit street, people stopped and turned their faces upward toward a sky they could not see, and waited, as they had waited every night for thirteen years, for a darkness that never lifted.

And in the shadow of the archway, against Felix's skin, the oath-mark pulsed—once, twice, slow and deliberate, like something knocking on a door from the other side.

*There you are,* the dark behind the king's eyes had said.

Now, glowing against her wrist in letters she had never learned to read, in a language older than the valley, older than the crown, older than the silence, the mark answered:

*I am here.*
