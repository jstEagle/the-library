## 9. Thirteen Years of Silence

The archives beneath the Aldermere palace smelled of dust and tallow and something older—something like the inside of a sealed tomb, air that had not been asked to move in a generation. Isolde Vane descended the spiral stair with a stolen ring of keys in her fist and a candle guttering in her other hand, and with every step she felt the weight of what she was doing settle more firmly across her shoulders.

She had told herself, on the road from the valley, that returning to the capital would be simple. That she would slip into the city of her birth the way water slips back into a riverbed, finding its old channels. She had been wrong. The city had changed—or rather, it had been *arranged*, the way a stage is arranged, everything in its proper place and nothing alive. The lamplighters worked their rounds at precisely the same hour each night. The beggars had vanished from the Temple steps. The bells rang on time, every time, as though time itself had been wound like a clock and set upon the mantel for display.

And above it all, the sky stayed blind. No stars. Only the swollen moon, fat and wrong, hanging like a lamp over an empty room.

But the archives were below ground, and below ground the pretense thinned. Here there were no gilded halls, no courtiers smiling their rehearsed smiles. Here there was only paper, and paper did not know how to lie convincingly—not all of it, not forever. Somewhere in these shelves, Isolde was certain, lay the answer to the question that had ruined her: *what happened thirteen years ago?*

She had been young then. A junior astronomer with ink-stained fingers and a head full of orbital tables, too busy charting the moons of winter to notice that the king had fallen ill. The Grey Fever, they had called it. It had come up the river with the autumn barges and emptied whole quarters of the city, and Isolde remembered—now, with terrible clarity—the sound of the death carts, wheel-ruts frozen in the first snow, and how one morning in midwinter the carts simply stopped.

The plague ended overnight. The histories called it a miracle. The Temple called it the mercy of the gods. And King Aldous, who had lain dying in his bed with the fever blooming black in his throat, rose on the third day and walked to the balcony and waved to his people, and the people wept with joy.

Isolde had wept too. She had been twenty-two years old, and she had believed.

* * *

The archive was organized by reign, and the reign of Aldous III occupied seven shelves near the center of the long vault. Isolde set her candle down and began.

She worked the way she had once worked at her observatory: methodically, obsessively, letting the data accumulate until it forced a conclusion upon her whether she welcomed it or not. Tax ledgers first—they were dull, and therefore honest. She noted that in the plague year, expenditures on the royal physicians tripled, then stopped entirely in a single month, midwinter, with no record of payment or dismissal. Then the temple tithes: a sudden, enormous disbursement to the Order of the Quiet Vigil—a minor monastic order she had never heard of before this year—which purchased, in the space of two years, land in eleven provinces. Land that contained, if her memory of the survey maps served, old boundary stones. Cairns. Wardstones of the pre-Imperial kind.

Then the correspondence, and here the archive grew strange.

Official letters from the early plague months were preserved in copies, as law required. But beginning in midwinter of that year—the very month the fever died—the copies changed character. They became shorter. Flatter. The king's hand, which had been loose and looping in the autumn letters, grew precise, geometric, almost mechanical, as though the man writing had learned his own signature by studying it rather than by living in it. One letter, dated three days after the miracle, instructed the Master of Whispers to compile a list of every hedge-witch, cunning-man, and boundary-keeper in the kingdom. Another ordered the Quiet Vigil's new lands fenced and closed to visitors. A third, and this one made Isolde's candle flame shudder though no draft moved, read in its entirety:

*The borders hold. Proceed with the quieting.*

*The borders hold.* As though borders were something that could be made to hold or fail, like a dam or a dyke. As though someone had been worried about them.

Isolde sat back on her heels among the dust and thought of Felix Thornbury, standing in the Warden's Valley with her wardstone and her silent god, maintaining wards she didn't understand against magic that couldn't enter. Thought of the valley itself—that impossible pocket of ordinariness where stars still had names and the moon kept its proper size. She had assumed the valley was an accident, a quirk of ley-lines, a place the world had forgotten to finish.

It was not an accident. It was a *remnant*. A piece of the old order, walled off and left standing, like a pillar left supporting a house after the house around it has burned.

*Proceed with the quieting.*

* * *

She found the physician's journal two hours past midnight, misfiled among agricultural reports, and she knew at once that someone had hidden it there deliberately—hidden it badly, or hidden it hoping someday someone would look.

The handwriting belonged to Maester Corwin, Royal Physician, and Isolde remembered him vaguely: a stooped man who smelled of vinegar, retired abruptly the spring after the miracle and dead within the year. His final entries were cramped, hurried, written by lamplight in a hand that deteriorated page by page.

*The king woke on the third night and asked for water. I gave it. He drank, thanked me, and asked me the date. When I told him he went very still and said, "That cannot be. I died."*

*I assured him he had been ill but not dying. He looked at me then—I will write this plainly because no one will believe it anyway—and he looked at me the way a house looks when you realize someone else has been living in it.*

*He knows things he should not know. He signed the Vigil charter without reading it. He speaks to empty corners. Last night I heard him through the door, conversing—at length, patiently, in a voice not quite his own—with no one. The voice that answered was lower. Older. It used words like "vessel" and "lease" and "the long tenancy."*

*I have begun keeping this record where the clerks will never sort it. If I am found dead of natural causes, know that I died afraid, and that I was right to be.*

The last entry was dated nine days before Corwin's retirement.

Isolde closed the journal and sat in the dark with her hands flat on its cover, breathing. The vertigo she had been holding at bay since the star-death finally broke over her, and she let it—not fighting, the way she had learned to let a telescope's image swim before sharpening. Thirteen years. Thirteen years of a thing wearing the king, signing charters, buying wardstone lands, *quieting* borders. Thirteen years during which Isolde had charted skies that were already being unmade, and never seen it, because the sky changes slowly and lies are patient.

And the plague. She turned the thought over and felt its edges. The Unmourned—if that was truly its name, the name the old boundary-liturgies gave to the thing that eats endings—had come to a dying king and offered a bargain. *Your life for your crown. Your people saved for your throne.* And Aldous, drowning in fever, loving his city, had said yes. Of course he had said yes. Any good king would have said yes. That was the horror of it: the trap had been built out of the king's own virtue.

But why cage the god? Why silence the boundary-keeper, whose whole office was the drawing of lines?

She found the answer four hours later, in a liturgical fragment from the Quiet Vigil's founding charter, and when she understood it she had to put both palms flat against the cold stone floor to keep the world steady.

The boundary-god's jurisdiction was not only rivers and walls and valley-marks. The old liturgies listed its charges in full: *the hedge and the hearth-line, the ford and the field-mark, the threshold of the house, and the threshold of the flesh; the line between the quick and the dead.*

Death was a border. Death was the oldest border of all.

A god of boundaries stood, by definition, between the Unmourned and everything it wanted. Because the Unmourned did not want a crown. The crown was a glove, a convenience, a way of moving through the world with hands. What the Unmourned wanted was the abolition of the border itself—the dissolution of every line that separated one thing from another, self from self, living from dead, until everything ran together like wax, undifferentiated, unmourned, because nothing would ever fully end and so nothing would ever need grieving.

It had caged the god to erase the borders. All of them. Including death's.

And the extinguished stars—Isolde's life's work, her ruin, the blind sky she had fled under—were not destruction at all. They were architecture. Bars, seen from outside. The god's prison rendered visible only from beyond the cage, which was why the valley, that last unquieted remnant, had kept its stars until the night the wards failed.

Every mystery reframed itself in the space of a breath. The plague that ended too neatly. The king who came back wrong. The Quiet Vigil buying up holy ground. The propaganda blaming foreign witches. The hairline crack in a wardstone in a valley at the edge of nowhere. It was not a war that had begun with the falling stars. It was a war that had begun thirteen years ago, in a sickroom, with a bargain, and the whole kingdom had been losing it ever since without knowing there was a battle.

* * *

Near dawn, stiff and half-frozen, Isolde climbed back up the spiral stair with the physician's journal wrapped in oilcloth against her chest. She had what she had come for. More than she had come for.

At the top of the stair, in the grey light of the archive antechamber, she paused by the narrow window and looked east, where the sky was beginning to bruise toward morning. No stars to fade. Just the pale wash of a sunless-feeling dawn and the moon, still swollen, sinking westward like something gorged.

Thirteen years ago, a good man had made a bad bargain to save his people, and the price had been collected slowly, quietly, in the currency of borders and stars and silences. And now the lease was ending—she was sure of it, sure the way she had been sure about the star-death, with that awful astronomer's certainty that arrives before evidence and is later proven correct. The thirteenth turn was nearly full. Whatever the Unmourned had planned for the completion of its tenancy, it would happen soon.

Which meant Felix Thornbury—her oath, her name, whatever buried thing she carried—was not a curiosity anymore. She was the hinge.

Isolde tucked the journal beneath her cloak and slipped out into the waking streets, past the lamplighter snuffing his lamps at precisely the appointed minute, past the swept and empty square, and she did not go back to the safehouse.

Because waiting outside the safehouse, leaning against the doorframe in the grey light with his helmet under his arm and his face arranged into something almost like kindness, was Sergeant Bray of the Company of the Broken Oath.

"You've been busy," Bray said pleasantly. "Her Majesty's archivists do miss their books. Come inside, Vane. There are people who'd like to ask you about your friend."

Behind him, the door of the safehouse stood open, and the room beyond was dark, and Isolde understood with perfect clarity that somewhere in that dark, the thing wearing the crown already knew everything she had learned tonight—knew it, perhaps, before she had.

She smiled her court smile, the one that had survived disgrace and exile, and stepped forward to lie beautifully one more time.
