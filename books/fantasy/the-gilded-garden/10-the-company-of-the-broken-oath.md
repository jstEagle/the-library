## 10. The Company of the Broken Oath

The road to Marrow's Cross smelled of wet ash and old rain, and Felix walked it the way she had once walked patrol routes in the Warden's Valley—counting her steps, marking the turns, keeping the world at a soldier's distance. It was harder here. Outside the valley, the world refused to stay quiet. Magic pooled in the ditches like standing water; she could feel it now that the wards were gone, a low hum against her skin, the way a dog feels thunder before it comes. Isolde had told her this was normal. Isolde had told her a great many things were normal now, and Felix had decided to believe her because the alternative was screaming.

Three days out from Aldermere's outer farms, she found the first sign of her old company: a mercenary's mark cut into a mile-stone, the broken circle of the Ninth Banner with a slash through it—the sign they'd agreed on years ago, back when agreement still meant something. *Marrow's Cross. The shrine under the hill.* Someone had carved it recently. The stone dust was still pale in the grooves.

Felix stood in the drizzle and looked at the mark for a long time.

She had not seen any of them in ten years. Not since the night the god went silent—not since they'd all stood in the company shrine at Cold Harrow and shouted their oaths into a silence so complete it had felt physical, like shouting into wet wool, into deep water, into a grave. Oaths sworn to a god who answered were a contract. Oaths sworn to a god who didn't answer were just words you kept saying because stopping would mean admitting something had died.

She touched the mark with two fingers, the way she'd touched the wardstone crack a lifetime ago, and walked on toward Marrow's Cross.

* * *

The shrine under the hill was exactly the kind of place the Ninth Banner had always favored: half-ruined, half-forgotten, defensible from three sides and ignorable from the fourth. It had been a temple once—Felix could see the bones of it in the fallen arches, the worn steps, the empty niche where some local god's statue had stood before even the locals stopped remembering which god it was. Now it was a camp. Cook-smoke threaded up through a collapsed section of roof. A picket line of tired horses stood in the lee of the south wall. And moving among them, hauling water, arguing cheerfully with a boy about the proper way to sharpen a billhook, was a woman Felix would have known anywhere, dead or alive, blind or deaf, in this world or any other.

"Petra," Felix said.

The woman dropped the water bucket. Water spread across the flagstones, reflecting the grey light through the broken roof, and Petra Okonkwo turned around slowly, the way people turn when they've heard a ghost speak.

"Felix Thornbury," she said. "You absolute bastard. You're alive."

They collided somewhere in the middle of the shrine floor, and it was not a graceful reunion—it was two soldiers gripping each other by the shoulders hard enough to bruise, checking, the way you check a comrade after a battle, counting limbs, looking for wounds. Petra was broader than Felix remembered, grey coming into her close-cropped hair, a new scar splitting one eyebrow. But her grip was the same. Her laugh was the same, rough and startled out of her.

"Ten years," Petra said. "Ten years and you walk in off the road like you went out for bread."

"I did go out for bread. The bread took a while."

That got the laugh again, and then Petra was shouting names, and the shrine began to fill. They came out of the shadows and side chambers one by one, and Felix's chest tightened with each face: Hesk, older, bearded now, his crossbow slung across his back like an extra limb. The twins, Sefa and Ilo, no longer the gangly teenagers she'd taught to hold a shield line—they moved like veterans now, and something about that made her feel suddenly, terribly old. Old Maren, who had been old when Felix joined and was somehow still alive, sitting by the fire with her hands wrapped around a clay cup, watching Felix with eyes that missed nothing.

"You felt it too," Maren said, when the noise died down. "The stars going out."

"We all felt it," Hesk said. "Whole sky went black as a purse. Moon swelled up fat and wrong." He spat into the fire. "Wardens say it's witchcraft. King says it's foreign witches. Everyone's got someone to blame except anyone who might actually know."

"The king," Felix said carefully, "is part of the problem."

She told them. Not all of it—she kept Isolde's name out of it, and the details of the court, and the thing behind the king's eyes—but she gave them the shape of it: the entity, the thirteen years, the caged god. She watched their faces as she spoke. Most of them wore the expression she expected: skepticism fighting hope, the old soldiers' war between *that's impossible* and *I've seen worse.*

But Maren watched her differently. Maren set down her cup.

"You said the god was caged," the old woman said. "Your oath-god. The Silent One."

"That's what I'm told."

"And you came looking for us." Maren's voice was very gentle, which from Maren was more alarming than shouting. "Child, we're not all here. You know that."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a horse shifted against its picket line.

"Bray," Felix said.

Nobody answered. Petra looked at the floor. Hesk examined the ceiling as if it might offer tactical advice. The twins exchanged a glance that carried an entire conversation.

"He was at Cold Harrow that night," Felix said. "He swore the same oath I did. Louder than any of us."

"Aye, he did." Hesk sighed, long and heavy. "And when nothing answered—when we knelt there in the dark with our oaths hanging in the air like smoke—most of us grieved. Bray raged. Said a god that goes silent has either died or deserted, and either way we were fools to keep paying a debt to a corpse or a coward." He rubbed his jaw. "He left within the year. Took a handful of the younger ones. We heard he'd found work down south. Good work. Steady work."

"Work for whom?" Felix asked.

Hesk met her eyes, and she saw the answer there before he said it, saw it in the way he couldn't quite hold her gaze.

"We don't ask anymore, Felix. That's the truth of it. He came through here eight months back with thirty men wearing no colors at all, and he asked us—asked *you*, specifically, where you'd gone to ground. We told him we didn't know. He smiled like he already knew we were lying, and he left a message." Hesk hesitated. "He said: tell her the silence isn't empty. Tell her something is listening on the other side of it. And it knows her name."

The cold that went through Felix then had nothing to do with the drafty shrine. She thought of the memory-thing at the border, dying in the mud, whispering syllables she hadn't known were hers. She thought of the thing behind the king's eyes, smiling at her oath-mark across a throne room.

"It knows my name," she repeated.

"So Bray claimed." Maren leaned forward. "Now you tell me, girl—and think before you answer, because I've buried enough of this company to know how to read a lie—you came all this way, past the fallen wards, through a country gone mad, to find us. Why? What is it you think we can do?"

Felix looked around the fire at the faces of the only family she'd ever chosen, and told them the truth.

"I need soldiers who remember how to fight without a god's blessing. Because that's all any of us have ever been—and I think the thing wearing the crown has forgotten that ordinary people can still put a knife in it."

Silence. Then Petra started laughing—that same rough, startled laugh—and stuck out her hand, palm open, the old gesture, the mustering gesture, the one that meant *count me in.*

One by one, the hands came out. Hesk. The twins. Maren last, slowest, her palm dry and warm and steady.

All but one shadow in the corner, where a man named Corvin sat with his arms crossed, staring at the fire, and said nothing at all.

* * *

Corvin found her at midnight, on the shrine's eastern wall, where she'd taken the watch out of habit more than necessity.

"You should know," he said quietly, "before you march everyone off to die for your god. Bray didn't leave because he stopped believing."

Felix didn't turn. "Then why?"

"Because he believed too much." Corvin climbed up beside her, settling his back against the crumbling parapet. He was a lean, careful man, the kind of soldier who survived by watching. "You were the captain's favorite, Felix. The one the Silent One marked. You had the oath-mark burned into your skin, and the rest of us just had words. When the god went silent, do you know what Bray said? He said, *it wouldn't abandon her.* He built his whole faith on you. If the god was real, it would never let anything happen to its marked one. So when the silence came and stayed—" Corvin shrugged. "For him, the silence wasn't proof the god was dead. It was proof the god had been a lie all along, and you were the lie's centerpiece. He hated you for a while. Then he found something else to believe in. Something that answered."

Felix's stomach turned over slowly, like a body rolling in shallow water. "What answers?"

"I don't know. But the men who ride with him don't dream anymore. Did you know that? Soldiers who don't dream. Who don't fear. Who stand watch all night and never nod off." Corvin's voice dropped. "There's a word for men like that where I come from. It isn't *blessed.*"

Below them, in the dark, a horse screamed.

It was a short, terrible sound, cut off mid-note, and both of them were moving before it finished—Corvin sliding down the wall, Felix dropping straight over the edge and landing in a crouch among the picket lines. The horses were stamping, wild-eyed, pulling at their lines. Beyond them, the shrine yard lay in pools of darkness between the fires, and in one of those pools, where the light didn't reach, something was standing.

Not something that had crept in. Something that had simply *arrived*, the way weather arrives, the way night arrives.

"Company!" Felix bellowed. "To arms! To arms!"

Torches bloomed. And in the spreading light, Felix saw him clearly for the first time in ten years.

Sergeant Doss Bray had always been built like a door, wide and flat and immovable, and he was built that way still, but everything else had changed. His hair was gone—not shaved but *absent*, the scalp smooth as river stone. His skin had a waxy, unlined quality, the quality of a face that had forgotten how to make expressions and only remembered where they used to go. He stood in the torchlight with thirty armed men fanning out behind him along the shrine walls, and he looked at Felix with eyes that held no hatred at all, which was somehow far worse.

"Thornbury," he said. His voice was level and pleasant and completely wrong, like a song played at the wrong speed. "You look well. The valley agreed with you."

"Bray." Felix drew her sword. Around her, the Ninth Banner was assembling in loose formation, shields locking, the twins flanking her, Hesk's crossbow coming up. "Thirty against twelve. You always did hate fair odds."

"These aren't odds. This is an invitation." Bray clasped his hands behind his back, a parade-ground stance, a sergeant addressing a formation. "It sent me to find you, and I've found you, and now I'm going to give you the courtesy nobody gave me. A choice." He tilted his head. "You felt the silence for ten years and kept kneeling anyway. I used to think that was loyalty. Now I know better. It was *waiting*. You were waiting for permission to stop pretending. Well. Here it is. The thing on the other side of the silence isn't silent, Felix. It's patient. And it wants to talk to you."

"I've talked to it," Felix said. "It wears kings."

"It *wears what consents to be worn.*" For the first time, something flickered across Bray's ruined face—not anger, but grief, ancient and fossilized. "That's what you've never understood. All of you, kneeling in the dark, waiting for a voice. It offered me a voice. Do you know what that's worth? Ten years I shouted into nothing and got nothing back, and then something finally, *finally* answered—"

"It's using you, Doss."

"It *remembered my name.*" The words cracked out of him, and beneath the crack, briefly, Felix heard the old Bray, the man who'd taught her to hold a shield line, who'd carried her three miles with an arrow in his shoulder, who'd wept openly at Cold Harrow when the god went quiet. "Do you understand what that means? After ten years of being nobody's? Something reached across all that dark and said *Doss Bray, I know you, I have use for you.*" He steadied himself. The wax smoothed over. "You'll understand soon. You especially will understand soon. But not tonight. Tonight I only came to count you."

"Counting's done," Felix said. "Go."

Bray looked at her for a long moment. Then he raised one hand, and his thirty men melted backward into the darkness, unhurried, unafraid, silent as men who had nothing left inside them to make noise about. Bray himself turned last. At the edge of the torchlight he paused, and looked back over his shoulder, and gave her the old salute—fist to breastplate, the Ninth Banner's salute, the one they'd all learned together in better years—and the gesture, performed by that waxen thing wearing her sergeant, was the cruelest thing she'd seen since the stars went out.

"One piece of advice, Thornbury. Free, from an old friend." His smile was small and terrible. "You keep asking how it knows your true name. How it whispered it to you at the border. How it knew your oath-mark on sight." He stepped backward into the dark, and his voice followed him like smoke. "*It remembers your name because it gave it.*"

Then the yard was empty, and the horses were screaming, and Felix stood in the torchlight with her sword shaking in her hand and the ground gone soft beneath her boots.

Behind her, Maren's voice, quiet and old: "Girl. What did he mean by that?"

Felix didn't answer. She was looking east, toward Aldermere, toward the swollen moon climbing fat and yellow over the hills, and in her mind a single sentence was repeating itself, over and over, in a voice that sounded horribly like her own:

*If it named me—then what am I?*
