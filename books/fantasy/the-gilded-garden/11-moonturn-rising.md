## 11. Moonturn Rising

The thirteenth turn began on a Tuesday, though Felix would remember it later as beginning with the smell of hot metal.

She noticed it first in the smithy district, where the air hung thick with coal smoke and the ringing of hammers. The moon had risen swollen and strange over Aldermere's rooftops, larger than it had any right to be, its face mottled like old cheese gone soft. She'd seen moons before—had spent ten years watching them from the Warden's Valley where the sky was the only thing that never lied—and this one was wrong in every particular.

"It's closer," Isolde had said that morning, spreading her instruments across the table of their rented room above a chandler's shop. "Or it's being pulled closer. Either way, the almanacs agree on nothing anymore." She'd tapped her finger against a page of calculations so dense they looked like spider silk given ink. "The false turn has begun."

Felix hadn't understood what that meant then. She understood now.

The heat came off the palace walls like breath from an open furnace mouth. The guards at the gate had waved her through without question—her forged credentials as a royal courier were good enough for the outer courts—but inside the second wall, the temperature climbed until sweat beaded at her temples and her oath-mark prickled beneath her sleeve like a thing waking.

She'd come because Bray had sent word. A scrap of paper passed through three hands to reach her: *Palace armory. Come alone. Come armed.*

Bray. Who served the thing wearing King Aldous's face, who had told her *it remembers your name because it gave it*, who had still, somehow, found a way to summon her into the heart of the enemy's territory.

The armory doors stood open. Inside, racks of halberds and pikestaffs stretched into shadow, and between them moved a single figure in half-plate, polishing a breastplate with the mechanical devotion of a man who needed his hands busy or they might do something else entirely.

"You came," Sergeant Denna Bray said without turning. Her voice was flat as slate.

"You asked."

"I did worse than ask." She set down the breastplate and turned. In the lamplight, Felix could see the change in her face—the hollows under her eyes had deepened since the shrine, since the knife-fight, since Bray had pressed a blade against her ribs and whispered warnings instead of killing her clean. Something behind those eyes was eating itself alive. "I need you to see something. And I need you to understand that I don't know why I'm showing you. Maybe because it's the last honest thing I can do."

"Bray—"

"Don't." She held up a gauntleted hand. The steel caught the light wrong, Felix thought—there was a darkness in it that didn't belong to any forge she knew. "Don't make this harder by being kind. Just look."

She held out the gauntlet. It was one of the king's own—a ceremonial piece, silver chased with gold filigree, the kind Aldous wore at audiences when he wanted to seem approachable. But the fingers had been pried open recently, the leather lining torn back, and scratched into the metal of the palm-guard, in letters small and desperate and unmistakably human, were four words:

*CUT IT OUT OF ME.*

Felix felt the world tilt. The oath-mark on her forearm flared hot, and for one vertiginous moment she saw double: the gauntlet in Bray's hand, and beneath it, ghosted over it like an afterimage, the same gauntlet on a different hand—a thinner hand, trembling, working by candlelight in some hidden hour while the thing that wore him slept or pretended to sleep.

"He's still in there," Felix breathed.

"Barely." Bray's jaw worked. "Some nights more than others. Some nights the king comes up like a man drowning, gets maybe an hour, does what he can before—" She made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh. "Before the tide comes back in. I've served that man for thirteen years, Thornbury. I've watched him eat dinner with his children. I've watched him weep at his wife's funeral. And I've watched the other thing use his mouth to order executions with the same voice, the same little smile, like it's all the same performance and he knows his lines." Her fist closed around the gauntlet. "I told myself the silence meant abandonment. That our god left us and something better filled the space. That was easier than believing we'd been robbed."

"And now?"

"Now I've held a dying king's hand while he begged me to kill him." The words came out raw. "Now I know the difference."

* * *

Isolde Vane had not slept properly in six days, and the moon was to blame.

It hung outside her window like an accusation, and each night it was larger. Not metaphorically—she had measured it, night after night, with the quadrant she'd smuggled out of the observatory wrapped in her spare shift. On the first night of the false turn, the lunar diameter had exceeded every recorded measurement in two centuries of Aldermere astronomy by a full degree. By the fourth night, the tides along the capital's riverfront had begun behaving like animals in a burning stable. Fishermen spoke of water standing up in columns. The river had flooded three streets that no flood had touched in living memory.

Tonight she sat in the chandler's back room surrounded by charts, and the numbers told her a story she wished she could unread.

"The moon isn't growing," she said aloud, to the room, to Felix, who was cleaning her sword with the methodical calm that Isolde had learned meant the woman was deeply unsettled. "It's approaching. The Unmourned is drawing it in—or drawing power through its proximity. Every night of this turn, the veil between what it is and what it wants to be grows thinner. Thirteen nights, thirteen degrees of approach. At zenith—"

"At zenith it takes the god," Felix finished. "If the cage opens."

"If the cage is opened *for* it," Isolde corrected. She rose and crossed to the window, where the swollen moonlight lay across the floorboards like spilled milk gone grey. "There's a difference. The cage doesn't break on its own—it's been holding for thirteen years. Something has to unmake it from within. An oath broken. A boundary willingly surrendered." She turned. "Which is why it needs you, Felix. Why it named you. You're not just a vessel. You're a key it grew inside a person, hoping the person would never notice the shape of the lock."

Felix's blade paused mid-stroke. "You say that like it's settled."

"Nothing is settled. That's rather the point of the next nine days." Isolde came back to the table and spread her hands over the charts—moon-paths, star-deaths, the careful geometry of catastrophe. "Listen to me. The king flickers through. Bray confirmed it. Which means the Unmourned's grip is not absolute; it spends power holding him down, the way you'd spend strength holding a door shut against wind. Every hour the king surfaces is an hour it's weaker. Every night the moon swells is a night it's stronger. We are racing a tide, and I intend to find the low-water mark."

"How?"

"Tomorrow night there's a court function. The Unmourned loves its rituals—it wears ceremony the way the king used to wear velvet. And ritual means pattern, and pattern means predictability." Her eyes, red-rimmed from sleeplessness, glittered anyway. "I'm going to walk into that court and read the thing's schedule right off its face. You're going to hate my plan."

"I already do," Felix said. "What's my part?"

Isolde smiled, tired and fierce. "Standing next to me looking like a bodyguard. And keeping your oath exactly, precisely intact until we know which breaking frees us and which one damns us. Can you do that? Hold the lock closed even when everything in you wants to throw it open?"

Felix looked at her a long moment—at the exhaustion and the stubbornness braided together, at the woman who had walked into exile carrying nothing but instruments and certainty. Something in her chest that had been clenched since the stars went dark loosened, fractionally.

"For you?" she said. "I can hold anything."

* * *

On the sixth night of the false turn, the moon filled half the sky.

Felix stood on the roof of the chandler's shop and watched it rise, and it was like watching a wound open overhead. Its light had changed too—not silver anymore but a bruised, meaty color, the color of the underside of a tongue, and where it fell, shadows pooled thick as oil. Below, the city had gone quiet in a way cities never should. Dogs would not bark at it. Moths would not fly toward it. The whole of Aldermere lay under that swollen face pretending to sleep, the way a child pretends under a parent's angry gaze.

She heard Isolde climb the ladder behind her and didn't turn.

"The tides reached the temple district today," Isolde said, settling beside her. "They're saying it's a curse from Vellmark. The criers are calling for war against people whose borders we can't even find anymore."

"People will believe anything if it keeps the dark explainable."

"Yes." Isolde hugged her cloak around her shoulders. "I used to think that was their weakness. Now I think it's the most human thing about them. The Unmourned runs on explanation—it wants a world with no mysteries left, no thresholds, no *between*. And people are so tired of the between. So tired of not-knowing." She was quiet a moment. "My father used to say the stars were God's way of leaving the door open a crack. I thought it was sentiment. Now I think he was more right than any astronomer alive."

Felix glanced at her. In the terrible moonlight, Isolde's face looked carved, all planes and shadow, but her eyes were steady, and Felix found—as she had been finding with increasing frequency over these weeks—that steadiness was the thing she oriented by. North. True north.

"Bray sent another message," Felix said. "Same words. *Armory. Alone.*"

"Again?"

"She says the king surfaced again. Longer this time. Long enough to write more." Felix hesitated. "He remembered my name, Bray says. My *real* one. And he said—I'm supposed to tell you this exactly—he said: 'Tell the astronomer the garden still grows. Tell her to count the roots.'"

Isolde went very still. When she spoke, her voice had dropped to almost nothing. "*Count the roots.* That's—that's an observation log phrase. From the observatory. There was a survey, decades ago, of deep structures under the palace—old foundations, older wells. The survey team logged a chamber beneath the east wing that appeared on no blueprint, ringed with tree roots grown *upward* through solid stone." She looked at Felix, and the exhaustion in her face had burned away into something bright and terrible. "Thirteen years I've been reading dead stars for an answer, and the answer has been under the throne room the entire time. The cage. The garden. It's real, and the king remembers where it is, and he's spending whatever hours he steals to hand us the map."

"Then we go tonight."

"No." Isolde caught her wrist. Above them the moon swelled, patient and vast, and below them the city held its false breath. "Not tonight. Tonight it watches him closest—he'll have paid for every word already. We go at zenith's edge, when it must spend everything on the moon and has nothing left for the cellar. Nine days, Felix. We hold the lock nine more days." Her grip tightened. "Promise me. However bad it gets—however much it shows you, however loud it gets in your blood—we do this *our* way, on *our* night, or not at all."

Felix looked up at the bruised and swelling face of the thirteenth moon, and felt the oath in her arm pulse once, twice, like a second heartbeat answering something far away. Somewhere beneath her feet, past gilded halls and lying ceremonies, a leafless tree stood guard over a buried god, and a drowning man was scratching maps into metal with the last strength of a stolen life.

"Nine days," she said. "Then we cut it out of him."

Above them, silent as a held breath, the moon continued to rise.
