## 12. What the Stars Were For

The observatory had not been used for astronomy in thirteen years, and it showed in the dust.

Isolde Vane swept her sleeve across the great brass orrery at the room's center and watched the grime come away in gray ribbons. Beneath it, the mechanism gleamed—her mechanism, built over nine years of apprenticeship and obsession, its nested rings still turning with a whisper of oiled precision when she cranked the drive-wheel. The court had sealed this room after her disgrace. They had posted guards at the door, confiscated her charts, declared her work heresy. But they had never thought to change the lock on a door they believed no one would want to open again.

Fools. Or perhaps just frightened people, which amounted to the same thing.

She worked by candlelight now, as everyone in Aldermere did. The great dome above her remained shuttered; opening it would announce her presence to the whole palace quarter, and she was not yet ready to be announced. So she had carried her instruments down from the hidden loft where she'd slept these past two nights—the astrolabe with its cracked horizon plate, the star-glass that had once been the finest in the kingdom, the bundle of almanacs she'd smuggled out of the valley in Felix Thornbury's pack—and she spread them across the observatory's long table like a surgeon laying out knives.

Felix sat across from her, sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening. She had been doing it for an hour. The whetstone made a slow, patient sound against the blade, *shhh, shhh*, like waves on some shore Isolde had never seen. It should have been irritating. Instead it had become, over these weeks of flight and hiding and shared bread, something almost companionable—a rhythm to think against.

"You've turned that page four times," Felix said without looking up.

"I'm not reading it. I'm arguing with it."

"Who's winning?"

"The page." Isolde tapped the almanac—the valley's own, the one with the impossible thirteenth turn inked into its oldest tables. "Because it keeps being right, and I keep wishing it weren't."

* * *

Here was the problem that had eaten three days of her life:

Stars did not go out.

Isolde had spent her whole career learning what stars were—not the village priest's silver nails hammered into heaven's roof, but vast burning things at terrible distances, governed by laws as strict as any king's. And those laws said stars dimmed, yes, over centuries. They shifted. They could even die, in the old texts' language, collapsing into points of devouring dark. But they did not—all of them, every one, in a single night—simply stop.

Unless they were not stars.

That was the thought that had woken her before dawn, heart hammering, and dragged her here through the servant corridors with her arms full of instruments. Unless the lights in the sky were not what she had always measured, but something else wearing starlight the way the thing in the palace wore a king.

She set the astrolabe down and began, again, the calculation she had begun a dozen times. Not the positions of the extinguished stars—that was trivial, that was first-year apprentice work—but their *pattern*. Their arrangement against the dark. She had memorized the sky at fifteen; she could draw it blindfolded still, every constellation of the northern wheel, and so she drew it now, from memory, in charcoal on the back of a discarded chart: not the familiar figures of the Hunter and the Weeping Sister and the Old Ox, but only the raw points, the coordinates, stripped of all the stories humans had hung on them.

Then she rotated the drawing ninety degrees.

Then she folded it—mentally, then actually, creasing the paper with a bone folder until her hands shook.

And there it was. Again. The same thing she had seen at midnight two nights past and refused to believe, because believing it would mean everything she had ever studied was a window onto a prison wall.

The pattern of the extinguished stars was not random. It was not even, properly speaking, astronomical. Folded through the fourth dimension of the old astronomers' geometry—the dimension the ancients called the *vein*, the one along which prayers traveled—it resolved into structure. Bars. Parallel, evenly spaced, radiating outward from a single point below the horizon of the world, a point she could fix within a degree if she had better instruments and the nerve to use them.

A cage. Seen from inside.

"We are looking," she said aloud, because saying it made it more real and less like madness, "at the inside of a lock."

Felix's whetstone stopped. "Say that again, slower."

Isolde turned the charcoal drawing toward her. "Every star we ever named. Every constellation your grandmother pointed out to you. Every fixed light that sailors steered by and priests prayed under and I gave eleven years of my life to measuring." Her finger traced the radiating lines. "They're bars. They have always been bars. The sky is the far wall of a cell, and whatever is locked behind it has been pressing its face against the bars since before humans learned to look up."

"And the god?" Felix asked quietly. "My god?"

"That's the part I didn't want to believe." Isolde pulled the second chart toward her—the one she'd made from the valley's almanacs, correlating the thirteenth moon-turn across two hundred years of records. "Look. The thirteenth turn doesn't appear randomly. It appears when the cage is stressed. When something outside pushes against the bars hard enough that the whole structure flexes, and the flexing shows up down here as an extra turning of the moon—a seam in time, a place where the calendar itself buckles. Two hundred years of records, and every recorded thirteenth turn coincides with... events. Plagues that end too suddenly. Wars that pause for no reason. A comet that appeared in the year the old temple burned."

"So the Unmourned has been pushing at the cage for centuries."

"For centuries. And thirteen years ago, it stopped pushing." She met Felix's eyes. "It found another way in. It came through the king. And once it was inside—once it had a hand on this side of the bars—it did the one thing that would guarantee no rescue could ever arrive. It put out the lights."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below them, the palace clocks marked the hour, muffled and wrong-feeling, as all time felt wrong now.

"It imprisoned my god," Felix said. "The Silent One. The boundary-keeper. That's why the wards fell, why magic can't enter the valley—no, wait." She frowned, working it through, and Isolde watched her do it with the particular intensity she brought to tactics, to terrain, to anything that could be mapped. "If the god of boundaries is caged, then boundaries themselves are orphaned. The valley's wards didn't fail because something broke them. They failed because the principle that sustained them went silent."

"Yes." Isolde felt the grim satisfaction of a theory confirmed, and hated herself for feeling it. "Your valley wasn't protected by magic, Felix. It was protected by the absence of it—a hole in the world where the boundary-god's power pooled so thickly that nothing else could cross. And when the god was caged, the pool began to drain. The wards held for ten years on residue. On memory. Until the night the stars went out, when the Unmourned finished the job."

"Then relighting them—" Felix started.

"Would free the god. Yes. In theory." Isolde stood and walked to the orrery, needing to move, needing her hands busy while her mouth said terrible things. "In theory, the bars are made of covenant. That's what the oldest texts say, the ones the court burned as heresy—the sky isn't stone, it's *agreement*. An oath sworn at such scale that it became physical. Which means it can be unmade the way any oath is unmade: broken, freely, by someone bound to it."

"I swore an oath to the Silent One."

"You did. Ten years ago, you and your company, and the god accepted it, and then went silent—which means the oath is still live, still *binding*, suspended like a note held past the end of a song." Isolde turned to face her. "Break it freely, and the covenant cracks. Break it, and one bar fails. One bar failing might be enough. The cage is old; it has been stressed for centuries; the Unmourned has been hammering at it since before either of us was born. One crack, and the whole structure could come down."

Felix set down the knife. Very carefully, the way she did everything that mattered. "But."

"But." Isolde's throat was dry. "There are two outcomes written in the texts, and I cannot tell you which one we get. The first: the cage breaks, the god pours out, the Unmourned is caught between a freed prisoner and the walls of its own stolen kingdom, and we win. The second—" She made herself say it. "The second is that the cage doesn't break cleanly. It *tears*. And what comes through the tear isn't the god. It's the pressure behind the god. The Unmourned has been pushing from outside for centuries, Felix. If you break the bar, you may simply be opening the door for the thing that's been knocking."

* * *

They did not speak for a long while after that.

Felix rose and went to the shuttered dome-window, the one narrow slit of glass that faced east, and stood looking out at the blind sky. Isolde watched her back—the set of her shoulders, the mercenary's stillness that was not calm but control, control worn like armor over something that had been bleeding for weeks now. Since the border fell. Since the thing wearing her dead friend's face had whispered her true name to her in the dark.

"If breaking the oath kills me," Felix said at last, conversationally, terribly calmly, "does it matter which outcome we get?"

"Felix—"

"No, I want to understand the mechanics. You know I do. I plan around casualties." She turned. In the candlelight her face was unreadable, which Isolde knew by now meant it was very readable indeed, to anyone who had learned her. "I'm bound. The oath is in me like a splinter. What happens to the splinter when the wound closes?"

"I don't know." Isolde made herself hold those gray eyes. "I genuinely don't know. The texts speak of oath-breakers being unmade—nameless, formless, scattered. But those were oaths broken in betrayal. Yours would be broken in fulfillment, in a sense—you'd be doing the thing the oath was always for. That might save you. It might also make no difference at all. Oaths don't care about intentions. That's rather the definition of them."

"Wonderful." Felix smiled, and it was the worst smile Isolde had ever seen, bright and brittle as frost. "So the options are: I die, the god dies, the Unmourned wins. I die, the god lives, we win. Or I survive, the cage tears, and the Unmourned wins anyway. Three doors, astronomer, and behind two of them is the monster."

"There's a third variable." Isolde heard her own voice drop, steadier than she felt. "The texts are clear that the breaking must be *free*. Freely chosen, fully understood, unwitnessed by coercion. Which means whoever holds the oath gets to choose the terms of its ending. Not the god. Not me. You."

"And if I choose wrong?"

"Then we learn what wrong sounds like."

Felix laughed—one short, sharp note, surprised out of her—and crossed back to the table. She looked down at the charcoal sky, the folded pattern, the bars of a prison older than nations. "You realize what you're describing. You're telling me the entire history of human religion, every prayer anyone ever said to the night sky, was prisoners talking to the jailer's architecture. That we've been worshipping the bars."

"I'm telling you worse than that." Isolde gathered the almanacs, squared their edges, bought herself a moment with the small orderliness of it. "I'm telling you that the person who finds a way to open that cage will be remembered as either the savior of the world or the architect of its ending, and there is no way to know which until the moment the bar snaps. History doesn't grade on intention."

"History," Felix said, "can wait its turn."

* * *

Later—much later, when the candles had burned down to stubs and the calculations were copied and recopied into Isolde's neatest hand—Felix fell asleep in the chair by the cold hearth, sitting up, the way soldiers sleep, knife-hand resting loose on her thigh.

Isolde should have slept too. There would be perhaps four hours before the palace stirred, before they needed to move, before Bray's warning and the king's scratched message and the swelling moon demanded action instead of arithmetic. She knew this. She catalogued it, the way she catalogued everything, and then she ignored it, because Felix was asleep and Isolde was awake and the observatory was quiet, and quiet was the rarest substance in her life.

She looked at Felix Thornbury and tried to name what she felt, because naming was her profession, her sickness, her single talent. Not gratitude—gratitude was too small. Not the fierce camaraderie of shared exile, though that was in it. It was closer to what she felt for the sky itself, before: that specific ache of attending to something vast and silent and utterly real, something that did not perform for her, something whose truth existed whether or not she was brave enough to measure it.

She had lost the sky. She understood that now, finally, fully. Whatever happened—if the cage broke, if the world ended, if they both died in a garden beneath a palace—the sky she had loved was already gone, revealed as a wall. She would never again look up and feel the old vertigo of distance, the sweet humility of smallness. That innocence was spent.

But she could love what replaced it. She could love the woman who had walked out of a valley that forgot magic carrying an oath like a coal in her chest, who fought without blessing and won anyway, who planned around casualties and included herself among them without hesitation. She could love the person Felix was choosing to become, minute by minute, decision by decision—because that was the whole discovery of these terrible weeks, wasn't it? That a self was not given. That it was made, in the dark, out of choices.

"Felix," she said softly, to the sleeping woman, practicing the sentence she could not yet say awake. "Whatever you decide, decide it as yours. Not the god's. Not mine. Not the cage's. Yours."

Felix stirred but did not wake. Outside the shuttered dome, the swollen moon ground its way westward, fat and wrong, dragging the false thirteenth turn behind it like a hem.

Isolde bent back over her charts—and froze.

There, in the margin of the oldest almanac, in the cramped hand of some valley scribe two hundred years dead, was a notation she had read a dozen times and never truly *seen*: a tiny diagram beside the record of the last thirteenth turn. A circle of bars. And beneath it, in the old liturgical script the temples had used before the courts burned it:

*One bar may be lit anew by an oath freely broken—but beware the vessel. The cage remembers its shape, and seeks a body to pour into.*

The vessel.

Her eyes went, unwilling, to Felix—sleeping, trusting, carrying a name she hadn't known she had.

The ink was old. The warning was older. And somewhere below them, in the gilded halls of the palace, something ancient was smiling.
