## 13. The Garden Beneath the Palace

The gauntlet had led her here.

Felix had carried it for three days now, wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of her pack like a relic she was afraid to be caught holding. *Cut it out of me*, the king had scratched inside the cuff—four words in a hand that shook, pressed into steel by a man who had perhaps an hour of himself left when he wrote them. She had turned them over so many times they had worn grooves in her own mind to match. But it was not the words that had brought her down into the palace's bones tonight. It was the map.

Isolde had found it folded inside a surveyor's folio from thirteen years ago, the year of the plague and the bargain: a plan of the palace foundations with one chamber inked in and then struck through, the strike-through so hard it had torn the paper. Beneath it, in the same shaking hand as the gauntlet's message, someone had written a single word. *Garden.*

A garden beneath a palace. Buried under six floors of marble and ceremony, deeper than the wine cellars, deeper than the old cisterns. Felix had spent ten years tending wards around a shrine to a god who never answered, and she knew what people built over things they could not bear to look at.

So she had waited for the changing of the guard, and she had gone down.

* * *

The way was not hidden so much as forgotten. A service stair behind the kitchens, its door swollen shut until Felix put her shoulder to it; then a corridor where the torch brackets held no torches and had not for years; then a second stair, spiral, descending past the level where the cistern-keepers kept their records and continuing on into dark that smelled of stone and water and something else—something green. That was the wrongness of it. Everything down here should have smelled of rot or dust. Instead there was a faint living sweetness, like sap, like crushed leaves, threading up through the cold.

Her oath-mark prickled. It always did that now, near anything of the Silent One—a heat along the inside of her left wrist where the brand sat, the shape of a threshold, a doorway seen from either side. Ten years it had been only scar tissue. Since the stars went out it had become a compass with exactly one direction, and right now that direction was *down*.

At the bottom of the stair stood a door of black oak, iron-banded, unmarked by any sigil. No lock. No bar. Just a threshold, and the sense—the old, familiar valley-sense—that crossing it would mean something.

Felix put her palm flat against the wood. The mark on her wrist flared warm enough to feel through her glove.

"Hello," she said quietly, to whatever was listening. "I'm coming in. I'm sorry I took so long."

She pushed, and the door swung open without a sound, and she stepped into the garden.

* * *

It was vast. That was the first impossibility—the stair had let her down maybe forty feet below the streets, yet the space before her opened like a cathedral's nave, vaulted overhead with rough-hewn rock that no mason had cut, because no mason had needed to. The chamber had been *found*, not built. And filling it, rising from a floor of pale root-woven earth, stood the tree.

It was leafless. It was also, unmistakably, alive.

The trunk was wide as a watchtower, silver-grey bark shot through with veins of faint gold that pulsed—slowly, slowly, slower than any heartbeat she had ever counted, but pulsing nonetheless. Its branches spread across the whole ceiling of the cavern, bare and intricate as lightning frozen mid-strike, and from their tips hung not leaves but small glassy buds, each one dark, each one holding a point of light that was not quite light. Stars, Felix thought, and her stomach turned. Buds full of buried stars. The prison-bars Isolde had described, seen from the inside.

The roots were worse, or better. They ran everywhere—up the walls, across the floor, vanishing into the rock in every direction, and Felix understood with a lurch that they did not stop here. This tree's roots went out under all of Aldermere, under the valley, under everything, holding the borders of the world the way a dying man holds a door shut with his body. Thirteen years of holding. No wonder the god had nothing left to say.

"You shouldn't have come."

The voice came from the shadow of the roots, and Felix did not reach for her sword, because she already knew it. She had known it since she heard the first syllable, the way you know a voice you learned to sleep beside on campaign ground.

"Sergeant Bray."

He stepped out of the dark with a lantern raised, and the light caught him, and he was thinner than she remembered, greyer, but his stance was the same—weight balanced, hands free, a man who had decided long ago how he would stand and saw no reason to change it. Except his eyes. His eyes were wrong. Not cruel, not mad. *Tired*, in a way that went all the way down to the marrow, the tiredness of someone who has been awake for years watching something he cannot look away from.

"I said," Bray repeated, "you shouldn't have come, Thornbury."

"The king told me to." She kept her hands loose and visible. "Inside his gauntlet. *Cut it out of me.*"

Something moved behind Bray's face—pain, quickly mastered. "I know what he wrote. I'm the one who let him write it." He set the lantern down on a root as thick as a table. "One hour a month, sometimes two, when the moon-turn turns and the thing in him has to spend itself keeping the sky dark. I stand guard while he comes back. Then it takes him again." His jaw worked. "Thirteen years of that. You want to know why I serve it? Look at him. Look at what resisting costs. I stopped believing resistance was mercy."

"And instead you became a doorman for the thing that caged your god."

"My god abandoned us first." It came out flat, rehearsed, worn smooth by repetition. "Ten years of silence, Felix. We bled for it. Ossory died with the oath-mark still fresh on him and it didn't so much as whisper. When the Unmourned came to me and offered me work that *mattered*—" He shook his head. "Don't look at me like that. You weren't there. You were safe in your valley behind walls I helped build."

"I was there for every year of the silence too, Sergeant. I just didn't run from it."

"No." Something almost like a smile. "You never ran from anything. That was always the problem with you."

They faced each other across the root-lit floor, twenty feet of pale earth between them, and Felix measured it the old way, the company way—distance, footing, the lantern's reach, the angle of his hip where the knife would sit. Bray was fifty and slow in the knees. Bray was also the best blade she had ever trained beside, and he had been guarding this place alone for years, and he would not be slow tonight.

But she had not come to fight him. She had come because of what the tree had done when she entered—because the moment her boot crossed the threshold, the gold veins in the bark had brightened, just slightly, the way a fire brightens when someone sits down beside it. The god knew she was here. Her god, who had never answered, was answering now in the only language it had left.

"Why do you think it sent *me*?" she asked.

Bray frowned. "Sent you?"

"The Unmourned. It named me at birth—it told me so. Made me a vessel, a failsafe, in case this cage ever opened. It let me live my whole life as bait for its own trap." She took one step forward, deliberately, letting him see it. "So ask yourself, Sergeant. If I'm the failsafe—if killing me or using me is the whole game—why am I standing in front of you right now with no escort, no army, and a message from the king in my pack? Why does the thing that planned thirteen years of this keep leaving doors open for me?"

"Because it's arrogant."

"It's careful. Arrogance is a luxury for things that don't plan." Another step. Fifteen feet. "You've watched it for years, Bray. Closer than anyone. Has it ever once done something wasteful? Something random? Or has every cruelty had a purpose, every move a reason?"

His hand had drifted toward his knife without him seeming to notice. "Every move a reason," he said reluctantly.

"Then reason it out. If it wanted me dead, I'd be dead. If it wanted me delivered here as a vessel, fine—but then why leave the king an hour a month to scratch warnings into his armor? Why let you stand guard, the one man in Aldermere who might hesitate? Why build a trap with a hinge in it?" She stopped, close enough now that the lantern-light reached her face, and made her voice gentle, which was harder than making it hard. "Unless it isn't a hinge. Unless it's a hope. Unless some part of the design—the oldest part, the part the god itself laid down before it was ever caged—wants to be found."

Bray stared at her. Above them, deep in the canopy, one of the glassy buds flickered—once, twice—like a star remembering how.

"That's madness," he said hoarsely.

"It's the only reading of the board that fits every piece. You taught me that. First winter, the ambush at Cold Ford—you said, *when the enemy's plan makes no sense, you've misread whose plan it is.*" She held his eyes. "Whose plan is this, Sergeant? His? Or the thing wearing him?"

For a long moment there was no sound but the slow pulse of the gold veins, and the drip of water somewhere far off in the dark, and Felix's own heartbeat counting time against the tree's.

Then Bray laughed—one short, broken note, nothing of humor in it. "God's teeth, Thornbury. Thirteen years I've stood in this hole telling myself I was the last honest soldier in Aldermere, and you walk in and take my whole argument apart in five minutes." He looked up at the tree, and his voice dropped. "I used to pray here, you know. First year. Before I switched sides. I'd stand where you're standing and ask it to say *anything*. One word. A sign. Silence." His hand fell away from the knife. "And then one night I realized the silence wasn't absence. It was effort. Like listening to a man trying to shout through six feet of stone. All that strength going into just—holding. Holding the borders. Holding the cage. Holding on." He looked back at her, and his eyes were wet, and he did not seem ashamed of it. "I mistook endurance for abandonment. That was my sin. Not betrayal. Misreading."

"Then read it right now," Felix said. "It's not too late. Nothing's too late until the moon turns."

* * *

He came around the great root slowly, the way a man approaches a horse that has thrown him, and stopped an arm's length away. Up close he smelled of lamp-oil and cold stone and, faintly, of green.

"If I do this," he said, "if I show you the root-door—they'll know within a day. Maybe less. The Unmourned feels everything that happens near the tree; that's half of why it posted me here, a man it could trust because I'd already given up. When I break, it will know I broke, and it will know why, and it will come for everyone I've ever—" He stopped. Started again, soldier-flat. "There are twelve men garrisoned above us who follow me because I tell them to. Some of them are good. Some of them are Bray's boys, mine, from the old company, who followed me into this the way I followed the silence into it. If I turn, I owe them the chance to turn with me."

"Then turn loud," Felix said. "We'll need soldiers for what's coming anyway. Isolde has a plan, and the plan has a hole shaped exactly like a garrison."

"Vane." He grimaced. "The astronomer. The court thinks she's a traitor and a witch."

"The court thinks the stars went out because of foreign witches. The court thinks the king is the king. The court believes whatever keeps the candles lit." She shrugged. "Isolde read the truth out of dead stars when every other learned head in Aldermere was writing propaganda. I'd trust her before I trusted the court's opinion of anyone."

Bray huffed—almost a laugh—and then his face went serious, and he turned to the eastern wall of the cavern, where the roots ran thickest, woven into the living rock like fingers laced into a fist.

"The root-door," he said. "Watch."

He knelt and pressed both palms flat against the largest root, and spoke—not a word Felix recognized, not any word at all, more a sound, a low hum pitched to match the pulse of the gold veins. For three heartbeats nothing happened. Then the root *moved*. It drew back from the rock the way a hand opens, unwinding, revealing a gap in the cavern wall—a low archway beyond, breathing air that tasted of night and rain and open country. A passage running out under the city, out past the walls, out to somewhere the Unmourned's gaze grew thin.

"It goes to the river-caves below the Mill Quarter," Bray said, rising. "Half a mile of crawl, then daylight. The Unmourned watches the tree, but it watches *through* the tree, and the tree—" he hesitated, choosing the word with care, "—the tree doesn't want to watch this door. I think it leaves this door. I think it's been leaving this door for thirteen years, the way a prisoner leaves a window unlatched."

Felix looked at the archway, and then up—up the impossible height of the leafless tree, at the thousands of dark buds each cradling its stolen spark, at the gold pulse that had brightened since she arrived and had not dimmed since.

"Gilded," she said softly. "That's what it is. Everyone talks about the Gilded Court upstairs—all that gold leaf and borrowed glory. But this is the gilded garden. The real one. Gold running under the bark of a god that gave everything it had to hold the world's edges together, and got buried for its trouble." She shook her head. "They named the palace after the wrong garden."

"They name everything after the wrong garden," Bray said. "That's what courts are for." He straightened, and something in his bearing shifted—the old sergeant surfacing through the tired man, shoulders settling into a weight he seemed relieved to carry. "Right. Here's what happens next. You go back up the way you came, quiet, and you take Vane and whatever ragged plan she's built to the river-caves entrance—I'll mark it for you. Tomorrow at dusk I march my twelve down here on rotation, same as every third day. Any of them who turn with me, turn. Any who don't—" his mouth twisted, "—any who don't get tied up and apologized to later. By midnight tomorrow you'll have your garrison, your door, and your path to the tree. And then, Thornbury, you'd better have the rest of it figured out. Because once we move, the Unmourned stops being patient, and I have seen what it does when it stops being patient. I've been standing in it for thirteen years."

Felix put out her hand. He looked at it for a moment—at the oath-mark showing white through the torn glove, the threshold-shape, the brand of a god who had endured instead of answering—and then he took it, and his grip was the grip of the old company, hard and certain and home.

"One condition," Bray said, as they parted at the black oak door. "When this is done—whatever's left of it, whoever's left of us—we bury Ossory properly. With the rites. Even if the god never says a word over the grave."

"He'll say a word," Felix said. She glanced back once at the tree, at the single bud high in the canopy that had flickered like a remembered star. "I think he's been trying to talk for thirteen years. He's just been saying it very, very slowly."

She climbed the spiral stair into the candlelit lie of the palace above, and behind her, far below, the gilded garden pulsed its slow gold pulse, and the root-door stood open, and the thirteenth moon turned one night closer to full.
