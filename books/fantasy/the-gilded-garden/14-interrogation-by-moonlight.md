## 14. Interrogation by Moonlight

The summons came the way frost comes: silently, and all at once, and everywhere.

Isolde Vane woke in her rented room above the chandler's shop on Vellum Street to find her breath hanging in the air like smoke, though it was high summer. The candle she'd left burning had gone out. Not guttered—*out*, its wick cold, its wax unwarmed. And on the inside of the window glass, written in a rime that should not have existed in Aldermere's sweltering July, were four words:

**THE OBSERVATORY. MOONRISE. COME ALONE.**

She sat up slowly, heart hammering against her ribs like a bird trapped in a shuttered room. The ink of the frost caught the light of the swollen moon through the window—that obscene, bloated thing that had grown fatter every night since the thirteenth turn began—and she saw that beneath the words, delicate as lacework, the frost had formed a second pattern: a star chart. Her own star chart. The one she had drawn at court eleven years ago, the night before her disgrace, mapping the constellation called the Shepherd's Crook down to the last faint star.

A chart no one else alive had ever seen.

"They're watching me," she whispered, and then, because terror makes honest women of us all, "they've *always* been watching me."

She thought about not going. She spent perhaps ten full minutes thinking about not going, lying in the dark with her hands pressed flat against the mattress as if the world might pitch and roll beneath her. But the mathematics of the situation were simple and merciless. If the Unmourned could reach into her room, past the wards she'd chalked on the sill, past the iron nail she'd driven into the doorframe, then it could reach her anywhere. Better to be summoned than hunted. Better to walk into the trap with her eyes open and her lies pre-loaded than to be dragged there by the hair.

And besides—she was an astronomer. She had spent her whole life studying things that were beautiful and impossibly far away and utterly indifferent to human suffering. She knew how to look at something terrible without flinching. It was, in fact, the only skill she had ever truly mastered.

She dressed in the dark. She left a message for Felix under the loose floorboard—the agreed signal, three scratches and a charcoal mark meaning *gone to the observatory, do not follow*—and she took nothing with her but her father's brass astrolabe, worn smooth at the pivot from forty years of anxious thumbs, and the little knife she'd bought from a fishmonger's widow who swore it had once gutted a tax collector.

The streets of Aldermere at midnight were a stage set after the audience has gone home. Candlelit, yes—the whole city burned candles now, thousands upon thousands of them, because the crown had declared darkness patriotic—but the flames made everything worse rather than better. They threw shadows that moved wrong. They turned faces yellow and unfamiliar. And above it all hung the moon, vast and pale and swelling, so large now that it seemed less like an object in the sky than a hole in it, a wound through which something on the other side was pressing its face closer to see.

Isolde walked with her head down and her mind working. That was the trick of fear, she had learned: you could not stop it, but you could give it a job. So she gave it the job of rehearsing lies.

It would ask about Felix. Of course it would. It had named Felix at birth; it had built her like an architect builds a hidden room; and now that the vessel had slipped its leash and come to the capital, the Unmourned would want her back. Isolde rehearsed the lie until it sat comfortably in her mouth: *Felix never came to Aldermere. We parted at the crossroads below Harrow Fell. She went north, toward the coast.* A good lie has a spine of truth in it—they *had* planned, once, to part at Harrow Fell—and a body of detail around it, and most importantly it is boring. No one interrogates a boring answer twice.

By the time she reached Observatory Hill, the moon stood directly overhead, and she understood why the summons had specified moonrise. Under that light, the old observatory did not look abandoned. It looked *expectant*. The great dome, which she had last seen rusted and bird-spattered, gleamed as if freshly polished. The doors—oak, banded in bronze, carved with the signs of the zodiac by craftsmen two centuries dead—stood open.

Inside, the dome chamber was lit by a single source: moonlight, falling through the open shutter at the top of the dome, pooling on the marble floor in a perfect silver circle. And standing in the center of that circle, wearing the body of King Aldous the way a man wears a coat he has long since outgrown, was the thing that had eaten thirteen years of Aldermere's sky.

"Magistra Vane." Its voice was the king's voice, warm and resonant, the voice of a man who had given a thousand speeches and enjoyed all of them. "You honor me. I feared you would make me come to you, and my knees are not what they were."

"The knees are his," Isolde said. "I doubt yours ache at all."

Something flickered behind the king's eyes—not anger. *Interest*. The pupils widened slightly, the way a cat's do when something small moves in the grass.

"Oh, I like you," the thing said. "I remember why I let you live, that night at court. You looked at me across the throne room—you looked right at me, through him—and you said nothing. Do you know how rare that is? Most people, when they glimpse me, scream or kneel or run mad. You simply *took notes*."

"I take notes on everything," Isolde said. "It's a failing."

"It's a *profession*. Sit." It gestured, and though there was no chair a moment before, there was one now—a high-backed observatory chair, the kind astronomers used for long nights of watching, upholstered in green velvet gone gray with age. Her father's chair. It had been sold at auction after the disgrace, along with everything else. "You'll want to sit for what comes next."

Isolde sat. She kept the astrolabe in her lap, both hands around it, and told herself the trembling in her fingers was only the cold.

"You've been busy," the king-thing said pleasantly, beginning to circle her, slow as a planet. "The archives. Section Nine. The sealed ledgers from the plague years. You read them by candlelight over four nights, and you copied certain passages into a notebook you keep sewn into your coat lining. Page forty-one interested you particularly. Shall I recite it?"

"No need," Isolde said. "I wrote it."

"*'On the third of Frostmonth, in the year of the Grey Fever, His Majesty did bargain with a power unnamed, trading the borders of his kingdom for the lives of his subjects, and the price was accepted, and the price was paid, and the price continues to be paid.'*" It stopped circling. "Your archivists were poets. I ate two of them."

"I know," Isolde said. "Their ledgers stop mid-sentence."

The thing smiled with the king's mouth, and it was horrible precisely because the smile was *good*—warm, crinkled at the eyes, the smile of a beloved grandfather. Thirteen years of practice, Isolde thought. It has had thirteen years to learn how to wear joy.

"But we are not here to discuss the past," it said. "We are here to discuss the future. Specifically—" and here it leaned close, and the moonlight filled its eyes until they seemed to have no bottoms, "—we are here to discuss the woman you've been hiding. The mercenary. The vessel. My *Thornbury*."

"She's not yours."

"She is the finest thing I ever made." There was real pride in the voice, and that was somehow worse than menace. "Do you understand what I did, Magistra? When I caged the boundary-god, I knew the cage would need a key. And keys must be carried by someone who does not know they carry them. So I reached into the valley where magic cannot follow, into a family of border-farmers, and I *wrote* a child. Gave her a name that is also a lock. Raised her in silence, fed her on duty, taught her to love walls. And for twenty-six years she performed beautifully. Until she met *you*."

It said the word *you* the way other men said *plague*.

"So here is my offer." The king-thing straightened, folded its hands, became for a moment the very portrait of reasonable sovereignty. "You are an astronomer. A reader of stars. And I—I am a collector of them. Every star I extinguished, I kept. They are not dead, Isolde. They are *mine*, hanging in a vault beyond the thirteenth moon, still burning, still beautiful, waiting only to be rehung. All of them. The Shepherd's Crook. The River. The Crown of the South, which your father named you under." It paused, delicately. "Give me the vessel's location, and I will relight the sky. Every star. Tonight. You will watch them return from this hilltop, from your father's observatory, and your name will be cleared, and your city will see again, and history will record that Magistra Isolde Vane ended the Long Dark."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below them, a night-watchman's bell rang the half hour, absurdly ordinary.

"And if I refuse?" Isolde asked quietly.

"Then the stars stay dark, the moon keeps growing, and when it reaches its fullness—three turns hence, perhaps four—the vessel becomes irrelevant anyway, because I will no longer need a key. I will simply break the lock. And I will begin, Magistra, with everyone whose name appears in your little notebook."

Isolde looked down at the astrolabe in her lap. Her father's thumb-wear, smooth as river stone. *He offered me the stars,* she thought. *He offered me exactly the thing I have wanted since I was six years old and he first held me up to the shutter-hole to see the Crook.*

That was the obscenity of it. Not the threat—the *gift*. It knew precisely what to offer because it had been reading her the way she read the sky: patiently, nightly, for years.

"You misunderstand me," she said at last, and heard her own voice go calm and clear, the voice she used for lectures, the voice that had once held a hall of two hundred students silent. "You think I want the stars back. I don't. I want them *earned*. A sky handed to me by a thing wearing a dead man's face wouldn't be a sky—it would be a ceiling. And I have spent my life under ceilings pretending to be skies. I won't thank you for another one."

The temperature in the dome dropped. The moonlight thickened, went from silver to the white of scalded milk, and the king's face did something it had never done in thirteen years of public appearances:

It stopped smiling.

"You are lying to protect her," it said. "Where is she?"

"Gone," Isolde said. "We parted at Harrow Fell, as planned. She went north. Toward the coast. Toward the sea, where your writ runs thin and your borrowed eyes grow dim. You'll find nothing in this city but me."

The silence that followed lasted five heartbeats. Isolde counted them, because counting was what she did instead of screaming.

Then the thing laughed—a short, genuine bark of amusement, which frightened her more than any rage could have.

"There it is," it said softly. "The spine of truth. You *did* plan to part at Harrow Fell. Which means you planned this lie days ago, which means you expected this conversation, which means—" and it tilted the king's head, birdlike, studying her, "—you are stalling. Right now. In this room. For something."

The door of the dome chamber was thirty feet away. Isolde measured it without moving her eyes, the way she measured everything: parallax, angle, distance. Thirty feet. The thing between her and it wore a king's body and could probably cross the distance faster than she could finish a prayer.

So she did the only thing left. She stood up, set the astrolabe on the chair arm with exaggerated care, and said, "If you're going to kill me, do it quickly. I'm fifty-one years old and I have been tired since 1791."

"I'm not going to kill you." The king-thing stepped aside, courteous as a host. "Killing you teaches me nothing. Sending you away teaches me everything. Go home, Magistra. Walk slowly. Let me watch which streets you avoid."

*Damn it.* It had seen through the stall, and now it meant to use her as a compass needle, reading her route home for the telltale bend toward wherever Felix was hidden. Isolde bowed—because one bows to kings, even hollow ones—and walked toward the doors with her pulse roaring in her ears.

At the threshold, the voice followed her, gentle as snowfall:

"One more thing. Tell Thornbury—" and the king's voice cracked, just for a syllable, into something older and rawer and *pleading*, the real man surfacing like a drowning swimmer breaking water—"*tell her the gauntlet. The left one. Hurry.*"

Then the mask closed over the face again, and the thing wearing it smiled, and said, "Goodnight, Magistra Vane. Enjoy the stars while they're gone. You'll want to remember the dark fondly, soon."

* * *

She did not go home.

She went the way she had come, down Observatory Hill, and then she broke pattern deliberately—turned east instead of west, doubled back through the tannery quarter, took the covered stair behind the dye-works that smelled of ammonia and old blood. If the thing was watching, let it watch a woman wandering lost. Behind her, the moonlight lay on the cobbles like spilled milk, and more than once she had the crawling certainty that her shadow was a half-step behind her body.

The safehouse was a cellar beneath a disused print-shop in the Inkworkers' Ward, reachable through a grate in an alley that smelled of lead type and lamp oil. She descended the ladder with her hands shaking so badly she nearly fell, and found Felix waiting at the bottom, sword already drawn, having read the signal and disobeyed it within the hour.

"You went alone," Felix said. It was not a question. Her face in the lamplight was grim and pale and the most welcome sight Isolde had ever seen.

"I went alone," Isolde agreed, "and I lied beautifully, and none of it mattered, because it already knew. Felix—it's been watching me for years. It quoted my own notebook. It knew about Harrow Fell. It knows we're both in the capital now; it let me leave specifically to confirm it." She laughed, a thin, cracked sound. "I stalled it for an hour and bought us nothing."

"You bought us this." Felix crossed the cellar in three strides and gripped her shoulders. "You walked into its den and walked out. Nobody's done that in thirteen years. What did it say? Everything. Word for word."

So Isolde told her—all of it, the offer, the stolen stars, the vault beyond the thirteenth moon, the threat against the names in her notebook. And when she reached the end, she hesitated, because the last part felt different from the rest. Heavier. Private.

"What?" Felix said. "What aren't you telling me?"

"It spoke to you. At the end. Through him." Isolde took a breath. "The king. The real one, underneath—he surfaced for maybe two seconds. He said: *tell her the gauntlet. The left one. Hurry.*"

Felix went very still. Then, slowly, she raised her left arm, and Isolde saw her expression change as memory arrived—the royal guard's ceremonial gauntlet, the one the king himself had buckled onto her arm at the audience, the one she had kept, meaning to throw away, meaning to study, meaning—

"He's been trying to reach us," Felix whispered. "All this time. Through the cracks."

They pried the gauntlet apart there in the cellar, by lamplight, with a knife-tip and shaking hands. Inside the wrist-plate, scratched deep into the metal in a hand that had trembled with effort, were six words:

***CUT IT OUT OF ME.***

Below them, deeper in the cellars, the garden waited. Above them, the swollen moon pressed against the shutters like a face against glass. And somewhere between the two, in a palace full of gilded halls and hollow laughter, a clock had started running that neither of them could hear—but both of them, now, could feel.

"We move tomorrow," Felix said. "Before the moon finishes."

Isolde nodded, and reached for her notebook, and began to write.
