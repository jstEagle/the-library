## 15. The Name I Was Given

The palace at night was a different animal than the palace by day.

By day it roared—servants with armloads of linen, courtiers trailing perfume and suspicion, guards whose armor sang against their hips. By night it breathed. Felix moved through that breathing, down a service stair that smelled of tallow and old stone, following the route Bray had scratched into her memory before he died: *the under-gallery, past the tapestry of the Hunt, third door on the left where the hinge is broken.* She had the king's gauntlet inside her coat, pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat, the words inside it worn nearly smooth by her thumb's anxious circling. *Cut it out of me.*

She had promised Isolde she would not go alone.

She had also promised herself she would not let Isolde walk into the observatory relay tonight without knowing whether the garden door below could be opened from the inside. Both promises could not survive the night. Felix had chosen, in the end, the way she always chose—alone, quickly, and without telling anyone who might try to stop her.

The under-gallery was long and cold and lit by lamps that burned without flickering, which should have been her first warning. In the Warden's Valley, nothing burned steady. Everything living guttered.

*Third door on the left.*

It stood open.

Felix stopped in the dark beyond the lamplight and let her hand find the knife at her hip out of pure habit, though she knew—had known since the audience hall, since those eyes behind the king's eyes had found her oath-mark across forty feet of marble—that a knife would be about as useful here as a prayer to a silent god. Which is to say: she carried both anyway.

"You came," said the king's voice, warm as mulled wine. "I confess I hoped you would. There is so little honesty left in this house."

He sat in a chair beneath the broken-hinged door's archway, an old man in a nightrobe, silver-haired, unguarded. No crown. No guards. His hands were folded in his lap like a man waiting for a physician's verdict, and for one lurching moment Felix thought: *Aldous. It's Aldous, it's really him, Isolde's message worked, he's cut free—*

Then he looked up, and the eyes were wrong.

Not monstrous. That was the horror of it. They were simply *full*, the way a well is full—dark, still, and very deep, with something at the bottom that had been there longer than the water.

"Sit," the thing wearing the king said gently. "You've walked a long way tonight, and you'll want your legs under you for what I have to tell you."

"I'll stand."

"Of course you will." The king's mouth curved. "You always did prefer standing. Even as a child. Your mother despaired of it."

The cold went through Felix like a blade finding the gap between ribs. "You don't know anything about my mother."

"No," it agreed pleasantly. "But *you* don't either. And that, Felix Thornbury—or may I use the name? It isn't yours, but it fits you so neatly—is precisely what we need to discuss."

* * *

She did not sit. But she did not run either, and some traitorous part of her registered that this was exactly what it wanted, and she stayed anyway, because there are questions a person will walk into fire to ask.

"The valley," Felix said. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it. "The wards. You put them there."

"I *maintained* them. For a time. Do you know how much work it is, keeping a valley blind?" The king leaned back, and the lamplight slid across his face without warming it. "Thirteen years ago I needed a place where the Silent One's remnants could not reach—a cradle, if you like. A sealed room. Magic cannot follow into the Warden's Valley; neither can memory, nor prophecy, nor the god's slow attention. Anything kept there stays *unfound*. I needed somewhere to keep something I could not afford to have discovered."

"And that something was me."

"That something was *you*." He said it with something that sounded horribly like tenderness. "Do you understand what you are, child? No—of course you don't. You've spent your whole life being told you're nothing special. A mercenary. A ward-tender. A woman with a scarred knuckle and a dull sword and an oath no one answers. You thought the silence meant abandonment." His head tilted. "It meant *protection*. Mine."

"Say it plainly."

"Gladly. Plainness is such a rarity these days." The king's folded hands unfolded, palms up, an old man's gesture of reasonableness. "When I caged the boundary-god—when I buried its heart beneath this palace and hung its prison-bars across your sky—I knew the cage would not hold forever. Cages never do. So I built a failsafe. If the cage ever opens—if the god ever rises—it must have somewhere to *go*. A vessel. A shape prepared, warded, hidden, grown in perfect blindness so that even the god itself could not see its own escape route until the moment it poured in."

The lamp flames did not flicker. Felix's whole body did.

"A vessel," she repeated.

"Flesh and blood, sworn by oath to the caged god—the oath is the lock's key turned sideways, a channel pre-cut through the soul. Raised in the one place its attention cannot reach, so it could never sense you coming. Trained to fight, to endure, to walk toward danger on command." The king smiled, small and terrible. "You thought you chose mercenary work, Felix Thornbury. You thought you chose to come to Aldermere. Every road you have ever walked was paved by me before you were born."

The floor seemed to tilt. Felix locked her knees and hated herself for it.

"My name," she said. "Bray said you gave it to me."

"I named you the hour you were born. I whispered it through the wards myself—through the crack in the wardstone, if you recall the one; I left that crack deliberately, a little breathing-hole, so the name could get in and nothing else could get out." The thing in the king's eyes regarded her with what might have been genuine admiration. "A name is a hook, child. Yours has been set in you since your first breath. *Thornbury.* Thorn and bury. What grows from a thorn and ends in the ground. I do enjoy the craft of it."

"You're lying." Her voice cracked on the second word. "You're wearing a dead man's face and you lie for a living—"

"Am I?" Softly. "Then tell me, Felix. Tell me one true thing about yourself that I haven't just handed you. One memory from before the valley. One face. One place. Can you?"

She reached for them. She had reached for them all her life, in the quiet after battles, in the dark before dawn—the missing years, the fog where a childhood should be, smoothed over so long ago that she'd learned to call the smoothness peace. The village had raised her. The shrine had shaped her. Before that—

Before that there was nothing but white, like snow-blindness, like a page before the writing.

"There," the king said, gentle as a grave-digger. "There it is. You feel it, don't you? The shape of the hollow. I carved it myself. You are the finest thing I have ever made, Felix Thornbury, and you walked three hundred miles to deliver yourself to me, exactly as designed."

* * *

Later—much later, running—she would try to remember leaving the room. She could not. There was a gap where the leaving should be, another smooth white stretch, and when her mind touched it, it skated off.

What she remembered was the street.

Aldermere at night, blind-sky night, the streets lit by candles in every window because the city had learned to fear the dark it could no longer read. Candlelight everywhere, thousands of small trembling flames, and above them nothing—not clouds, not stars, just a black so total it felt less like absence than like a lid. Felix walked through it fast, faster than the curfew deserved, and the city blurred around her, and every face under every candle looked briefly like a stranger wearing a stranger's face wearing hers.

*A constructed thing.*

She ducked down a cooper's alley between two shuttered warehouses and put her back against the wall and slid down it until she was sitting in the muck, knees up, breath sawing. Somewhere a dog barked itself hoarse and gave up. The candle in the nearest window gutted and steadied, gutted and steadied, the only honest thing on the street.

Her hands were shaking. She made fists of them. It didn't help; now the shaking was just contained, rattling around inside her knuckles like coins in a purse.

*Every road paved before you were born.*

She tried to argue with it, because arguing was what she had. *The valley wasn't a cage, it was home. The company wasn't a leash, they were family. Isolde—*

Isolde.

And there the argument collapsed, because Isolde was the one thing in thirteen years that the design hadn't predicted. Surely. Surely. Isolde had stumbled into the valley by accident, half-dead, hunted, carrying instruments and grief. Unless—unless even that was paved. Unless the disgraced astronomer fleeing Aldermere with her dead-star charts was another road laid down in advance, another corridor in the maze, leading Felix exactly where the maze-wanted her to go.

No. She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until sparks bloomed—real sparks, pain-made, hers. *Hers.* That was the only currency she had left. This ache was hers. This fear was hers. Whatever else had been built into her, nobody had built the way her chest hurt right now, and she would die before she let it take that too.

But oh, it was thin. It was so thin. A single splinter of self, held up against thirteen years of architecture.

She thought about the oath. Ten years of silence, ten years of kneeling at a shrine that never answered, ten years of keeping faith with a god who could not hear her—and all of it, all of it, a channel. A groove cut into her soul so that when the cage opened, the flood would have somewhere to run. She had thought her constancy was a virtue. It was plumbing.

A laugh came out of her, ugly and small, and scared a cat off the alley wall.

And underneath the laugh, quieter, worse: *the valley.* The contentment she'd felt patrolling the border, the rightness of the wards under her hands, the way she'd told herself for years that she was happy there, simple and safe and unseen. Had that been happiness? Or had it been the seal holding? Had she loved her life, or had she been *kept*—like a tool wrapped in oil cloth, like a letter sealed against reading?

Her mother. She didn't have a mother. She had a story about a mother, a village midwife's tale of a woman who died in childbirth, and now even that was suspect—was there ever a woman at all, or had the thing in the crown written her in for verisimilitude, the way a forger adds a smudge of age to make the ink believable?

She was crying. She noticed it the way you notice rain—gradually, then all at once. She let it happen, because there was no one to see, and because if she was a constructed thing then at least the construction was leaking, and leaks were hers.

*If the cage opens, the god pours into you.*

Which meant—her mind caught on it, snagged like a nail on wool—which meant that everything Isolde had decoded, everything they'd planned, the ritual, the relighting, the breaking of the oath to free the Silent One—all of it ended with Felix as a glove. A glove for a god instead of a glove for a monster. She would break her oath, the cage would crack, and the vast wordless presence she'd sworn to for ten years would pour down the pre-cut channel and fill her to the brim, and whatever remained afterward would wear her face and speak with her voice and call itself free.

Both gods wanted her body. The monster had built it for exactly that purpose.

She got up. She didn't remember deciding to get up. Her legs simply reported for duty, old soldiers, and started walking, and she let them, because walking was the one skill that had never lied to her. Out of the alley. Along the candlelit street. Past a shrine niche where some faithful soul had set a stub of candle before a chipped statue of a boundary-stone—the old religion, the true one, surviving in cracks the way weeds survive in pavement.

She stopped. Looked at it. The little stone god with its blank face.

*I named you,* the thing had said. *Your name is a hook.*

Fine. Then she would think about names. Names were the battlefield now, and she had fought on worse ground with worse odds.

*Felix Thornbury.* Lucky thorn-bury. Except luck was a joke and thorns were a weapon and burial was the plan, and none of it was hers, none of it—

None of it was hers, and yet.

For ten years she had answered to it. For ten years she had signed patrol ledgers with it, shouted it across training yards, heard it in the mouths of people she'd loved—Bray saying it with grudging respect, old Corporal Maren saying it with her pipe clenched in her teeth, young Tam saying it like a prayer before his first fight. The name had been given by a monster, yes. But it had been *lived in* by her. Worn soft at the edges like a coin passed through a thousand hands. Whatever it had been at mint, it was not that anymore. It had her sweat in it. Her blood. Bray's death was in it now too, and the king's message scratched inside a gauntlet, and Isolde's voice saying her name in the dark of the observatory like it was worth saying.

They couldn't take that back. They could build her, name her, pave her roads—but they could not unlive the living.

Could they?

Her feet had carried her, without consulting her, to the foot of the observatory hill. Of course they had. Some roads you pave yourself.

Above her, at the top of the long stair, the observatory dome stood black against the blacker sky, and in its slit window a single light burned—not candle-yellow but the strange blue-white of Isolde's star-lamps, the ones she'd built to mimic light that no longer existed. Felix stood in the street and looked up at that light for a long time, and the shaking in her hands slowly stilled, and something assembled itself in her chest—not hope, nothing so generous, but its stubborn cousin: *direction.*

She could not go up there. Not yet. Not carrying this—not carrying the knowledge that touching her might be touching the enemy's design, that Isolde's whole plan might be a corridor with a door at the end shaped exactly like Felix's heart. She would not bring the maze to the one person who might still be outside it.

But she could not run either. Running was a road too, and all roads were paved.

So she stood at the bottom of the hill, in the blind city, under the lidless sky, and did the only thing that had never once been done to her or for her or planned for her in thirteen years of architecture.

She made a choice with no good options in it.

Tomorrow—no, tonight, before the moon climbed higher, before the Unmourned finished whatever it had begun in that lamplit room—she would go back. Through the under-gallery. To the buried garden. To the leafless tree with the god's heart in its roots. Not to open the cage. Not to break the oath. But to *see* it—to stand in front of the thing her whole life had been built around, the lock her name was the key to, and look at it with her own eyes, on her own errand, for reasons no one had written for her.

And if the thing in the crown was watching—if the whole city was a palm it had closed around her—then let it watch. Let it see what its constructed thing did next.

Felix Thornbury turned away from the observatory light and walked into the dark, and for the first time in her life, she could not have said whose road she was on.

That, she thought grimly, wiping her face with the back of her hand, was probably the most honest thing about her.

Behind her, high on the hill, the blue-white light in the observatory window went out—and a heartbeat later, far across the sleeping city, every candle in every window guttered at once, as if the night had drawn a single long breath.
