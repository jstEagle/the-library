## 16. Belonging to Myself

Isolde Vane had spent her life learning to read light, and so she knew, in the way astronomers know things—by absence, by the shape of what was missing—that Felix Thornbury had not come back to the safehouse.

The candle she'd left burning in the window had guttered to a stub hours ago. The room they'd shared for three nights now—the narrow attic above a chandler's shop in the Tallow Quarter, where the smell of tallow and beeswax masked the smell of fear that had settled over Aldermere like frost—was half hers and half Felix's, and the half that was Felix's was arranged with a soldier's economy: sword oiled and sheathed against the wall, boots set together beneath the cot, the oath-mark on her forearm never once discussed but always, somehow, the center of the room.

All of it still there. All of it untouched since dusk.

Isolde stood in the doorway with her cloak still dripping river-damp from her escape across the city, her heart still hammering with the memory of the Unmourned's voice pouring out of a mirror in her old observatory—*give me the vessel, astronomer, and I will give you back your stars*—and she felt the two fears collide inside her like bodies in a dark corridor: the thing that had hunted her, and the woman it wanted, both loose in the same blind city.

She did not let herself think. Thinking was for people who had time. She banked the candle, took her coat, and went down into the streets.

* * *

Aldermere under a starless sky was a city pretending to be asleep. The curfew bells had rung at ninth hour, and the watch moved through the candlelit lanes in pairs, their lanterns throwing long nervous shadows against shuttered fronts. Propaganda posters curled off the walls where the paste had failed in the damp: THE WITCHES OF THE EAST HAVE STOLEN OUR SKY, they said, above a woodcut of a weeping king. Isolde passed one and felt the old grief move through her, familiar as an ache in an amputated limb. She had loved this city. She had loved its observatory most of all, its brass and glass and patient arithmetic, and now every instrument in it pointed at nothing, and the man who had funded them all wore something ancient like a glove.

She went first to the places Felix might go if Felix were trying to disappear. The shrine of the Silent One in the Ropewalk—a bare alcove behind a cooper's yard, where the god of boundaries had been honored with a simple threshold stone and no words at all. Empty, though the stone was warm when Isolde laid her palm on it, which it should not have been, and which she chose not to think about. The stable yard where Bray's defected soldiers were quartered. Empty too, or nearly—one sentry, half-asleep, who shook his head before she'd finished asking.

It was the third place, the one she almost didn't try, that held her.

The Garden District had been the fashionable quarter once, before the dark. Now its mansions stood empty, their owners fled to country estates or simply *gone*, the way people had been going for thirteen years—quietly, unremarked, the way things go when borders stop holding and no one notices what crosses them. Isolde climbed the wall of the Marlowe estate because it was the lowest wall and because, thirteen years ago, she had watched a meteor shower from its roof terrace with a man whose name she could no longer say without tasting ash.

Felix was sitting on that roof terrace, knees drawn up, sword across her thighs, looking up at a sky with nothing in it.

Isolde pulled herself over the parapet, breathless, her palms scraped, and Felix's head came around fast—soldier-fast, blade-half-drawn—and then stopped, and the whole rigid architecture of her went slack.

"You shouldn't have come looking," Felix said.

"You shouldn't have gone walking." Isolde crossed the terrace and sat down beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, because she had learned in three nights that Felix flinched from comfort offered at a distance but leaned into it given plainly. "I escaped a god tonight, Felix. I would like very much not to spend the rest of it hunting you through empty houses."

"I wasn't hiding."

"No?"

"No." A pause. "Yes. I don't know." Felix's laugh was a dry, terrible sound. "You found me on a roof staring at nothing. Draw your own conclusions, astronomer. You're supposed to be good at reading the sky."

"The sky is empty."

"Then read the emptiness."

Isolde looked up. It never stopped being wrong, that emptiness—she had studied stars for thirty years and their absence still struck her like a slap, a page torn out of a book she'd memorized. But she made herself look, because Felix had asked, and because somewhere in the black she knew the bars were there, invisible, the cage of a god rendered in the negative space of heaven.

"It says someone turned the lights off," Isolde said quietly. "That's all it has ever said. Everything else—" She turned. "Everything else is being said by you, right now, in a voice I don't recognize. So tell me. What happened after I left for the archives this morning? And don't tell me nothing, because I know what the Unmourned is, and I know it summoned me tonight asking for *you*, and I know you well enough by now to know you'd have found some way to hear about it."

Felix's jaw worked. For a long moment the only sound was the wind moving dead leaves along the terrace flags.

"It found me," Felix said at last. "An hour ago. In the street outside the shrine. Not wearing anyone—it just *was*, standing in the middle of the lane like it had always been there, like the lane had been built around it." Her hand drifted to her forearm, to the mark beneath the sleeve. "It talked to me, Isolde. It told me—" The word caught. She had to try twice. "It told me it named me. At my birth. That my mother carried me to a crossroads shrine and prayed to something she thought was a saint, and it answered, and it put a name into me like a coin into a well. A failsafe. If the cage ever opens—if the god ever rises—the god pours into me. Into the vessel it made. Me."

"Felix—"

"My whole life." Felix's voice cracked down the middle, and she pressed on through the break like a soldier pressing through a gate, because that was how Felix did everything. "My whole life I thought I was hidden. The valley hid me, the silence hid me, I hid myself—I thought I was *lucky*. I was a lockbox. I was a thing it built and shelved and waited for. Every choice I ever made—" She stopped. Pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "It even let me love the valley. It *designed* that. It designed that so I'd stay where the god couldn't reach me until it needed me."

The words fell into the dark between them and lay there.

Isolde had spent her life among men who believed the heavens were a machine and human beings were weather—incidental, unaccountable. She had heard kings speak of people as instruments. She had watched the court of Aldermere learn, over thirteen years, to call a cage a kingdom. And none of it, none of it, had prepared her for the sound of Felix Thornbury describing herself as a constructed thing, in the flat voice of someone reading an inventory.

She reached over and took Felix's wrist—not the marked arm, the other one, the one that was only flesh and history and the calluses of a life actually lived—and turned it until Felix had to look at her.

"Listen to me," Isolde said. "I'm going to tell you something, and then I'm going to prove it, and you're going to hate both parts, and you're going to listen anyway."

Felix's mouth twisted. "That sounds like you."

"A name given isn't a self decided." Isolde said it slowly, the way she would state a theorem, because she needed it to land like one—with weight, with proof pending. "Do you understand? I was named Isolde for my grandmother, whom I never met, by parents who wanted a court appointment. My whole name is an inheritance I never asked for. Your name is worse than that, yes. Yours is a trap. But a name is a word, Felix. A word is not a self. A self is—" she gestured, helplessly, at everything—at the scraped parapet, the empty sky, the woman beside her—"a self is what you've *done*. And I have watched you do a great many things these past weeks, and not one of them was written by whatever whispered at a crossroads thirteen years ago."

"You can't know that."

"I can know it better than you can, because I'm not the one drowning." Isolde kept her grip on Felix's wrist, firm, anchoring, the way she'd anchor an instrument against tremor. "You fought bandits on the road without your god's blessing and won. You walked into a royal audience knowing what sat behind the crown and you didn't blink. You talked Bray out of a fight that Bray had every reason to win, and Bray defected, and Bray is *alive* because of how you are. None of that is a vessel. Vessels don't choose. You have done nothing but choose, at every turn, usually the harder way. So when the Unmourned tells you that you are a thing it built, you may believe that it believes it. You may even believe that it planned it. But plans, Felix—" and here Isolde smiled, thin and fierce, the smile she'd worn the day they stripped her observatory title from her, "—plans are made by creatures who assume the world obeys them. I have spent my life watching the world refuse."

Felix stared at her. In the dark her eyes were wet and furious and, underneath the fury, desperately hopeful, the way a starving animal looks at a closed hand.

"And the ritual?" Felix said hoarsely. "If what it said is true—if breaking the oath frees the god and the god pours into me—then what does any of it matter? What I choose doesn't matter. The moment that cage cracks, I stop."

"That," Isolde said, "is the part I'm going to prove."

* * *

They worked by candlelight in the attic afterward, the way they had worked every night this week, except that tonight neither of them pretended it was only work.

Isolde spread her papers across the floorboards: the archive transcriptions, the fragments of the old covenant, her own charts of the extinguished constellations, each one annotated in her cramped hand. The theory had come to her in the archives days ago and she had hated it then, had buried it under caution, because it required an assumption she hadn't dared make. Tonight, sitting on a cold roof listening to Felix describe herself as furniture, she had dug it up again and found it was the only thing left.

"The stars are the bars," she said, tapping the chart. "Seen from outside. The god's prison is built of boundary itself—the oldest boundary, the line between divine and mortal. To relight even one star requires an oath freely broken. That part we knew. That's why the Unmourned needs *you*: your oath is the key fitted to the lock. Break it, and the cage opens."

"And I become a door," Felix said flatly.

"You become a door *if the opening is aimed at the god*." Isolde pulled a fresh sheet toward her. "But think about what an oath actually is. It's a boundary. A line drawn around a self: inside this promise, I am this person; outside it, I am not. Your oath binds you to a silent god—but it also, all these years, has bound *you* to *you*. It's the frame of the person you've been. The Unmourned wants you to break it *toward* the god—to shatter the frame so the god can pour through the gap into the space it prepared. But a boundary broken isn't only an opening, Felix. It's also a *definition ended*. And what fills the space afterward depends on what stands there claiming it."

Felix frowned. "Say it plainly. You know I drown in your astronomy."

Isolde laughed despite everything—a short, startled sound—and then made herself be plain.

"Don't break the oath to free the god," she said. "Break it to claim yourself. Renounce the given name. Speak the unbinding not as the vessel releasing the prisoner, but as Felix Thornbury—the self you made, the one I just finished listing—declaring that no name but your own has authority over you. If the ritual's logic holds, the act breaks the oath, which cracks the cage, which frees the god. But the vessel—the channel the Unmourned built—breaks with it. Because a vessel is only a vessel while it belongs to something else. The moment you belong entirely to yourself, there is no hollow left to pour into. No one inherits the cage. Not the Unmourned. Not the god. No one."

The candle flame bent in a draft from the window. Neither of them moved.

"And if your logic doesn't hold?" Felix asked quietly.

"Then I have misread the sky for thirty years and deserved my disgrace." Isolde held her gaze. "There's risk. I won't lie to you—there's risk in every branch of this tree, and I'd be lying if I said I could calculate them all. But the Unmourned's plan requires you passive. It requires you to believe you are a thing. Every step of this—every single one—requires your despair more than your blood. So we take the one path it hasn't guarded, because it cannot imagine it: the path where you decide what you are."

Felix looked down at her forearm. Slowly, deliberately, she pushed back the sleeve, and the oath-mark showed in the candlelight—the interlocking lines, the threshold-sign of the god of boundaries, inked into her skin at nineteen by a company that had believed in something silent ever since.

"When I swore," Felix said, "we swore on our names. That was the form. *By my name, I keep this faith.*" Her thumb traced the mark. "So the name and the oath are braided. If I claim the name—"

"—the oath unravels with it," Isolde finished softly. "And the god comes free. And you stay *you*."

Silence. Then Felix laughed—really laughed, ragged and disbelieving, wiping her face with the heel of her hand like a soldier clearing rain.

"Thirteen years of silence," she said, "and the answer is a grammar lesson."

"The best answers usually are." Isolde gathered the papers, squared their edges, and found her hands were shaking now that the plan was spoken—now that it was real, and dangerous, and theirs. "We'll need the garden. The god's heart, the tree, the root-door Bray showed you. We'll need Bray's soldiers at the walls and the moon near zenith, when the Unmourned's attention is thinnest. And we'll need—" She stopped.

Felix looked up. "We'll need what?"

Isolde had rehearsed nothing. She had faced down the Unmourned in a hall of mirrors with steadier hands than this. But she had watched Felix sit on a cold roof and call herself a constructed thing, and she had decided, somewhere on the climb over that wall, that if they were all possibly going to die in a buried garden in three nights' time, she was not going to leave the truest thing she knew unsaid.

"You," she said. "We'll need you. Whole. Choosing. And I need you to know that when I say I believe you belong to yourself, I am not speaking as your strategist." She laid her hand over Felix's, over the mark, over the braid of name and oath and everything the Unmourned had tried to own. "I love you, Felix Thornbury. Whatever your name turns out to be worth, whoever gave it, whatever it was meant to trap—you are the person I crossed a hostile city tonight to find, and I would cross it again tomorrow, and I am telling you plainly because you deserve plainness, and because I refuse to let that thing in the crown be the only one who knows what you are."

Felix went very still. Outside, far off, the false moon was climbing over the rooftops, swollen and wrong, and the city below them held its breath the way it had held it for thirteen years.

Then Felix turned her hand palm-up beneath Isolde's and laced their fingers together, and her voice, when it came, was rough and steady and entirely her own.

"Three nights," she said. "Then we go down into the garden, and I introduce myself."

Above the Tallow Quarter, unseen behind the blind sky, the first bar of the cage began—very faintly, very far away—to glow.
