## 17. Storming the Gilded Court

The moon reached its zenith at midnight, and it was wrong.

Felix stood in the shadow of the palace's eastern wall and looked up at it, because she could not help looking. It filled a quarter of the sky now, swollen and pale as a drowned face, and its light did not fall so much as press—pressing down on the gilded rooftops of Aldermere, on the cobbled squares, on the three hundred soldiers crouched in the drainage cut behind Bray, waiting for a signal that would set them against their own king. The light had weight. Felix could feel it on her shoulders like a hand.

*It knows we're coming,* she thought. *It has known since Isolde walked out of that observatory with lies caught between her teeth like fishhooks.*

She looked at Bray instead. Her old sergeant stood at the head of the column in borrowed armor, and there was something almost peaceful in her face—the peace of a woman who had already died once, ten years ago, when the god went silent, and who had been walking around since waiting for the rest of her to catch up.

"The servants' gate," Bray said quietly. "Rotating watch changes at the half-hour. We go in on the change, before the new shift arrives. Forty heartbeats of open door." She glanced sideways at Felix. "You remember the old signals?"

"I remember everything," Felix said. "That was always your complaint."

A ghost of a smile crossed Bray's scarred mouth. Then it faded into something else, something heavier, and she put one gauntleted hand on Felix's shoulder plate.

"If it goes badly in there—if I don't come out—" She stopped. Started again. "The thing that spoke to me in the shrine. It said the Unmourned remembers your name because it gave it. I've thought about nothing else for nine days." Her grip tightened. "Whatever you hear in those halls, whatever it shows you—it gave you a name, girl. It didn't give you a self. Don't let it confuse the two."

Felix's throat worked. "You sound like Isolde."

"Then she's smarter than both of us, which is no great achievement." Bray turned back to the column and raised her fist. "On my mark."

Above them, at the top of the palace hill, the old royal observatory caught the bloated moonlight and threw it back in a long silver blade. Isolde was up there. Isolde, alone, with brass instruments and chalk circles and the relay that had to hold—or none of this mattered.

Felix made herself stop thinking about that.

Bray dropped her fist. They moved.

* * *

Isolde Vane had spent thirteen years learning to be afraid in beautiful rooms, and the observatory was the most beautiful room she had ever feared in.

They had not dismantled it. That was the horror of it. After her disgrace—after the trial, after they burned her charts in the courtyard below while the court watched from balconies—they had simply locked the doors and left everything as it was. Her orrery still stood in the center of the dome, its bronze rings frozen mid-turn. Her star-charts still hung from their rods along the curved walls, hundreds of them, each one a night sky drawn by her own hand in the years before the stars went out. Dust lay over everything like gray snow.

And through the great eye of the dome above, the swollen moon stared down at her like an iris.

"You're early," she said aloud, to the room, to whatever listened. "I know you're watching. Let's not pretend otherwise."

No answer came, but the candle flames all leaned east at once, toward the palace's heart, and straightened. A courtesy. A taunt. With the Unmourned, it was impossible to tell the difference, and Isolde suspected the entity enjoyed that ambiguity the way a cat enjoys a cornered mouse's confusion.

She got to work.

The relay was a circle of salt and ground star-iron laid across the floor in a pattern she had redrawn forty times in the margins of her flight from the capital—each line a memory of a real constellation, each junction anchored to a moment she had actually lived: the night she first found Saturn's rings through her father's spyglass; the morning she defended her thesis before men who sneered; the evening, three weeks ago, when Felix Thornbury had laughed at her over a campfire and Isolde had understood, with astronomical certainty, that she was no longer capable of leaving. The circle ran on true things. That was the whole secret of the old astronomy that the court had called heresy: magic could counterfeit anything except a life honestly remembered.

When the moon hit zenith, the thirteenth turn would reach its peak, and the boundary between the world and whatever lay beyond it would thin to a membrane. Somewhere beneath the palace, in a buried garden, a leafless tree held the heart of a caged god. And somewhere above that garden, in the gilded halls, an ancient hunger wore a crown. The relay's purpose was simple and terrible: to hold open a channel of *true sky* through the false night—to give Felix a thread of the real world to climb, when everything else had been cut away.

If Isolde faltered, even for a breath, the thread would snap, and Felix would fall into whatever waited on the other side of the membrane.

Isolde knelt at the circle's edge, chalk in her hand, and began the final inscription. Her fingers were steady. She noticed that they were steady, and was quietly astonished by it.

Below her, faint through the stone, she heard the first sounds of the assault—a bell struck once, then shouting—and she bent her head over the chalk and did not look up.

*Hold the sky,* she told herself. *Let her climb.*

* * *

The servants' gate opened onto the under-halls, and the under-halls opened onto slaughter.

Not the soldiers' doing—at least, not at first. The first corridor they cleared was empty except for the scuff-marks of running feet and a single courtier's shoe, silk slipper and all, lying on its side. The second corridor held guards, but they fought strangely, eyes flat and unfocused, blades swinging in patterns too old for any living drillmaster—as though the Unmourned were puppeting them from patterns it remembered from some other war, in some other age, fought by bodies shaped differently than human ones. Felix cut through a spear-thrust that should have been impossible and drove her sword through her opponent's chest, and the man's face never changed. He simply stopped, like a lamp going out.

"They're not defending the palace," Bray panted, dragging a wounded soldier behind a pillar. "They're delaying us. Buying time for *it.*"

"Then we don't give it time."

They climbed. The palace of Aldermere was built upward as much as inward—great stairways spiraling through its core, each landing opening onto halls more gilded than the last. Felix had grown up in the Warden's Valley, where the only gold was autumn and the only marble was river ice, and the wealth of these halls struck her now as obscene, a hoard piled over a grave. Every mirror they passed showed her something slightly wrong: her reflection a half-second late, or smiling when she wasn't, or—once, only once—wearing the oath-mark on her bare arm, glowing, though she had covered it with wrappings hours ago.

"Don't look at the mirrors," she told the soldiers near her.

"Ma'am," one of them whispered, "the mirrors are looking at us."

"I know. Keep moving."

At the fourth landing they met real resistance: a shield-wall of the Royal Guard, forty strong, filling the grand stairway from rail to rail, and behind them, standing calm as a chess player, a figure in black robes with no insignia that Felix recognized until she saw the way the shadows pooled around his feet, flowing uphill against the torchlight.

One of the Unmourned's priests. Or its mouths. Its hands.

"Hold the stair," Bray ordered, and then, to Felix, in a different voice entirely, the voice of ten years ago, of a hundred campaigns: "Thornbury. Take the left flank through the gallery. I'll fix them here. Go."

"Bray—"

"That's an order, soldier." The sergeant's grin was the old one, the terrible one, the one that had made green recruits feel immortal. "Ten years I served silence. Ten years I thought my god abandoned me because it went quiet, and I let something whisper in that quiet until I couldn't tell its voice from mine." She drew her axe. "Tonight I get to choose loudly. Go make your choice too."

Felix went.

What followed, she would remember afterward in fragments, the way one remembers a storm: the gallery's windows shattering inward under pressure that was not wind; the shield-wall breaking around Bray's axe like water around a stone; the priest in black turning to face her, opening its mouth, and speaking—not words but her name, her true name, the given one, spoken in a voice like her mother's might have been, and the pain of it doubling her over, because the name was a hook and it caught in something deep beneath her ribs—

—and then Isolde's voice, impossibly, everywhere, ringing off the gilded walls from the relay far above, clear as a bell over water:

*"Felix. Look at me. Not the name. Me. You are the woman who shared her rations with a stranger on the road out of nowhere. You are the woman who talked a traitor into coming home. You are the woman who is climbing. Hold the thread."*

Felix held the thread. She rose. She killed the priest with a thrust so clean that it seemed almost merciful, and she did not hear what the dying mouth tried to say next, because she chose not to listen, and choosing not to listen, she discovered, was also a kind of freedom.

Behind her, on the stair, the shield-wall re-formed around Bray, cutting the sergeant off from the gallery route.

"GO!" Bray roared, laughing, axe rising and falling. "We're buying time! Spend it!"

* * *

The zenith came at last, and the moon filled the dome of the observatory like a lid sliding shut over the world.

Isolde stood in the center of her circle of true things, arms raised, and spoke the constellations aloud—one by one, by their oldest names, the names from before the crown, before the court, before anyone decided the sky belonged to kings. The Salt Road. The Lantern-Bearer. The Warden's Blade, low on the horizon where the valley lay. Each name she spoke lit a corresponding point in the salt-and-iron ring, and the ring became a map, and the map became a window, and through the window poured a thin, fierce current of ordinary starlight—starlight from nights that had really happened—which fell down through the palace like a shaft through deep water, seeking the garden below.

Her nose bled freely. Her legs shook. The circle drank from her, because truth always costs the speaker, and she fed it memories like coins into a ferryman's palm: her father's hands over hers on the spyglass. The smell of ink. The sound of Felix saying her name for the first time, careful, as if testing whether it would hold.

The door of the observatory burst open.

Three shapes entered, wearing the bodies of palace servants, moving with the wrongness she had learned to dread. Behind them, filling the doorway, came a knight in full blackened plate, and where the visor should have shown a face there was only the bloated moon reflected, huge and close.

Isolde did not stop speaking. There was a knife on her belt. There was a heavy brass armillary sphere within reach of her foot. Neither would be enough.

But the shapes slowed at the circle's edge, hesitating, as though the starlight stung—and in that hesitation, boots sounded on the stair, and Bray's surviving soldiers crashed into the room with axes and fury, and the battle for the observatory began around the small, burning circle of a kneeling astronomer who never once broke cadence.

*"The Warden's Blade rises,"* she recited, blood on her lips, chalk dust in her hair, as steel rang on steel an arm's length away. *"The Salt Road endures. The Lantern-Bearer holds."*

The thread held.

* * *

Below, in the dark beneath the palace, Felix walked alone into the buried garden.

The descent had taken her through corridors where the gilding gave way to raw stone, where the air smelled of earth and old rain, where the swollen moon's pressing light could not follow—and yet she felt it anyway, felt the whole thirteenth turn bearing down on this place like a wave gathering to break. At the bottom, past the root-door Bray had shown her, the garden opened: a walled courtyard under the roots of the palace itself, and at its center the tree.

Leafless. Enormous. Older than the stones around it. Its branches spread against the cavern ceiling like veins, and beneath its bark, faintly, something glowed and dimmed, glowed and dimmed, in a rhythm like breathing. Like a heart. Like someone asleep who could not wake.

The throne of a god, buried alive.

Felix crossed the dead grass to stand beneath it. Above her, muffled by fathoms of stone, she could hear the battle—the clash from the observatory, Isolde's voice threading through it, steady as a lighthouse beam. Below her feet, through the roots, she could feel the other thing: the Unmourned, vast and coiling, pouring down through the palace's bones toward this place, toward the cage, toward *her*, drawn by the opening of the thirteenth turn like a shark to blood in water.

She had perhaps a hundred heartbeats. Maybe fewer.

Felix Thornbury—who was not certain any longer that she was Felix Thornbury, who had learned that her name was a lock built by her enemy, who had spent her whole life inside a valley designed to hide her—drew her sword, knelt at the tree's roots, and pressed her wrapped forearm against the bark.

The oath-mark burned through the wrappings.

"I'm here," she said aloud, to the sleeping thing in the wood, to the silent god of boundaries who had never once answered her in ten years of faithful service. "I don't know if you can hear me. I don't know if you ever could. But I'm going to open the cage, and I need you to know that whoever I am—the name I was given, the name I'll choose—I'm doing this as *myself*. No one else's design. No one else's vessel. Mine."

The tree's glow stuttered.

And then the temperature of the garden dropped, all at once, as though winter had stepped through a door, and Felix knew without turning that the Unmourned had arrived—that the darkness pooling at the edges of the courtyard was not shadow but presence, that the voice when it came would come from everywhere, from the walls, from the roots, from inside her own skull where her given name sat like a splinter working toward the heart.

*"Little vessel,"* the dark said, tender as rot. *"You brought the key to the lock. How kind. How very kind."*

Above, faint and unbroken, Isolde's voice rang down through the stone:

*"The Lantern-Bearer holds."*

Felix rose, and turned, and faced it.
