## 19. Two Gods, One Grave

**FELIX**

The garden was burning and there was no fire.

Felix knew this the way she knew her own hands—by the absence of heat, by the smell of ozone where smoke should be. The leafless tree at the garden's heart had split along its trunk when the cage cracked, and out of that split had come something too large for the world: a presence without edges, a pressure like deep water, a silence so profound it rang.

Her god. Her silent god, rising after ten years of nothing, filling the buried air until the roots themselves seemed to kneel.

And then the crown had screamed.

She felt it happen inside her own ribs before she saw it with her eyes—the Unmourned tearing free of King Aldous's body like a hand pulled from a glove worn thirteen years too long. It did not pour out; it *erupted*, black and starless, a shape made of every border that had ever been erased. And it came for the rising god the way floodwater comes for a candle.

It came through her.

That was the part she had not understood until it was happening. She was the vessel. The failsafe. The name given in a cradle by a thing that planned its own contingencies across decades. The cage was open, and the god had nowhere to go but into the shape prepared for it—and the Unmourned wanted that shape for itself.

They met inside her.

There was no pain, exactly. There was something worse: *fullness*. Two vastnesses crammed into the small room of her skull, each one trying to be the whole of her. The god moved through her like a tide—wordless, boundary-shaped, all edges and thresholds, the feeling of a door closing against winter. The Unmourned moved through her like rot—all doors opened at once, every wall dissolved, death's border trampled flat so that nothing could ever end, and therefore nothing could ever begin.

She was on her knees. She didn't remember falling. The garden soil pressed cold against her palms, and above her the tree's split trunk wept light and shadow in equal measure, and somewhere far away Isolde was screaming her name—

No. Not her name. A name. One of them. And that distinction, that thin knife-edge of distinction, was the only thing keeping Felix Thornbury from dissolving entirely.

Because both of them were calling her. The god called her by the oath she'd sworn, ten years of faithful silence, *warden, warden, warden*. The Unmourned called her by the name it had given her mother's ear before Felix ever drew breath—a name like a hook, like a leash, like a door built into her from the inside.

And if she answered either one, she would stop being herself.

* * *

**ISOLDE**

Isolde Vane had spent her life learning to speak to things that could not answer back.

Dead stars. Distant planets. The ash-cold mathematics of orbits that would outlast every kingdom. She had learned that the way you keep yourself real in the presence of the incomprehensibly vast is to keep talking—to narrate, to name, to insist upon the small human scale of your own voice against the enormity of what you study.

So when she saw Felix go down beneath two gods, Isolde did the only thing she knew how to do.

She talked.

"Felix." She dropped to her knees in the ruined garden, close enough to touch, not touching yet because the air around Felix's skin *moved wrong*, rippling like heat-haze in January. "Felix, listen to me. Don't listen to them. Listen to me."

The thing wearing Felix's face turned toward her—or rather, the face turned and the eyes didn't, because the eyes were full of stars going dark in reverse, constellations collapsing inward, and behind them something ancient was doing arithmetic with her soul.

Isolde kept talking.

"The first time I saw you, you were standing at the valley border with your thumb on a crack in the wardstone, checking it the way a woman checks a wound on someone she loves. You didn't know why the wards mattered. You just knew they mattered to *you*." Her voice shook. She let it shake. Honesty was an instrument; she had learned that too, later than she should have. "You gave me water when the whole village wanted to run me off. You said—I remember exactly—you said, 'Everyone who walks out of somewhere deserves a drink.' That wasn't an oath. Nobody made you say it. You just *did*."

The ripple around Felix stuttered.

Inside that stuttering, Isolde saw her—really saw her, the way you glimpse a swimmer under white water. Felix's awareness, small and stubborn and *herself*, drowning between two tides.

"You were afraid of me," Isolde went on, faster now, leaning close, her words falling into Felix like stones into a well. "You admitted it. You said I looked at the sky like it owed me money. You laughed when you said it—that ugly little snort-laugh you think is undignified, the one I—" Her throat closed. She forced it open. "The one I love. I love that laugh. I love that you check the wardstone twice. I love that you pretended to hate my star charts and then stayed up three nights learning to read them. Those are mine. *You* made those. Not the god. Not the Unmourned. You."

Something in the air shifted. The tide paused.

"Your memories," Isolde whispered. "The ones they can't touch. The ones you made yourself. Hold onto them, Felix. They're the only borders that matter."

And Felix's hand—her hand, chapped and scarred and ordinary—closed around Isolde's wrist.

* * *

**FELIX**

Isolde's voice came down to her like a rope thrown into deep water, and Felix grabbed it with everything she had.

The trick—she understood it suddenly, the way you understand a lock mechanism all at once or never—was that the gods could claim her oaths and her given name, but they could not claim what she had chosen freely. The oath to the Silent One had been sworn at nineteen, yes, but the reasons she'd kept it alive through ten years of silence were hers alone. The habit of walking the border at dawn. The way she'd memorized Isolde's constellation charts out of spite and then out of wonder. The night in the blind streets of Aldermare when she'd decided, alone, in the dark, that she was not a constructed thing—no matter who had built her cradle.

Those were borders no god could cross, because she had drawn them herself.

The war went on inside her regardless. She felt it as weather: the god pressing outward through her bones like spring sap, patient, immense, wanting only *out*; the Unmourned coiling tighter, wanting *in*, wanting the vessel sealed and owned, wanting to wear Felix the way it had worn a king. Each of them speaking to her in languages older than speech. Each of them promising.

The god promised rest. Boundaries restored. A world with edges again, where things could end and therefore mean something.

The Unmourned promised forever. No endings, no losses, Bray un-killed, the king un-dead, every grief unwritten.

And God forgive her—God, whichever of them that was—it was the second promise that made her sway.

"No," Isolde said, reading her face the way she read dead stars, from traces and deviations. "Felix. Look at me. Forever is just a wall you can't see over. You told me that."

Had she? She must have. Some night on the road, some campfire. It sounded like her. It sounded like *them*, the shared country they'd built out of talk between disasters.

She held on.

* * *

**ISOLDE**

The king was moving.

Isolde saw him from the corner of her eye and nearly cried out, because King Aldous XIII had been a puppet for thirteen years and everyone knew it, everyone had seen the hollow shine of him at his coronation audiences—but now he was dragging himself across the broken garden on hands that shook like an old man's, and his eyes were his own.

Briefly. Terribly. His own.

"Aldous," she breathed, forgetting herself, using the name of a king as if he were a man.

"I heard—" His voice came out rusted, a hinge unused for thirteen years. He was looking at Felix, at the war inside her, and his ruined face was wet. "I heard her. In the dark. All these years, in the dark, I heard everything it did wearing me." He coughed, and the cough had blood in it; whatever the Unmourned's departure had left of him, it was leaving fast. "I bargained for my people. The plague. My daughter was dying, do you understand, my *daughter*—"

"I know," Isolde said. "I read the archives. I know what you traded."

"And it took everything else." He hauled himself upright against the split tree, and where his hand touched the bark the light flared. "Thirteen years. Thirteen turns. It thinks in turns, astronomer—it counted them like coins." His eyes, fever-bright, found hers. "It has to go back in the tree. The cage isn't broken. The cage was never the god's prison alone. Read your own stars—it was a *grave*. Two things buried in one grave, and it dug itself out first, and it left the other one to be blamed for the darkness."

The truth of it landed in Isolde like a bell struck. Of course. Of course—the prison-bars seen from outside, the extinguished stars, the thirteenth turn. The tree was not merely a cage. It was a tomb, and a tomb needs a body, and the Unmourned had spent thirteen years making sure the body it left behind would be a god no one mourned.

"It won't go willingly," Isolde said.

"No." Aldous smiled, and it was a terrible, wonderful smile, the smile of a man setting down an unbearable weight. "But it will go if something holds the door. And I have been its door for thirteen years. I know the hinges."

* * *

**FELIX**

Later—much later, years later—Felix would try to describe what it was like when the king walked into the war inside her, and she would fail every time.

He came in the way dawn comes: not as force but as permission. He put his hands—not gloved now, bare, trembling—on either side of her face, and he said, in a voice almost worn through:

"I named the bargain. Let me name its ending."

And the Unmourned, which had been coiling through her veins like ink through water, *stopped*. Because here was the thing it had feared for thirteen years, the flaw in its perfect design: it had worn a man so long that the man knew every seam of it. Every hinge. Every place where the mask fit loose.

Aldous closed his eyes. "For my daughter," he said. "For every year I watched through glass. Take me back."

And he *pulled*.

The Unmourned came out of Felix like a splinter drawn from flesh—vast, shrieking in frequencies that bent the roots overhead, fighting every inch. Felix felt it tear free of the hooks it had set in her given name, felt the name itself shred like old cloth, and she screamed, and the scream was hers, only hers, belonging to nobody.

Then the king turned, holding the writhing darkness against his chest the way you hold something you have carried so long it has grown into you, and walked into the tree.

Into the split. Into the grave.

The bark closed over him slowly, lovingly, the way snow closes over a footprint. The last thing visible was his hand, pressed flat against the inner wood, and then even that was gone, and the tree stood whole again—scarred, leafless, but whole—and the silence that filled the garden was not the silence of a god withheld.

It was the silence of an ending.

* * *

**ISOLDE**

Isolde held Felix while the shaking came.

It always came after—for astronomers after a long night at the eyepiece, for soldiers after battle, for anyone who had stood at the edge of the incomprehensible and lived to need warmth. She wrapped her arms around Felix and felt the vessel-ness draining out of her, felt the body under her hands becoming simply a body: tired, human, breathing too fast.

"It offered me forever," Felix whispered into her shoulder. "Bray alive. Everything undone. And some part of me wanted it, Iz. Some part of me still wants it."

"Of course it does. That part is honest. That's how you know it's yours." Isolde drew back far enough to look at her face—the face she had come to know in blind streets and borrowed observatories, a face that belonged to no god and no design, a face she loved past all reason or astronomy. "Grief that wants to undo itself is just love with nowhere to go. We'll find it somewhere to go."

Above them, the tree shivered.

A single bud formed on a branch that had been bare for centuries—no, longer, Isolde corrected herself; the tree predated the palace, perhaps the kingdom. The bud swelled, hesitated, and opened into a flower the color of starlight remembered rather than seen.

And then another. And another.

From somewhere beyond the garden walls, muffled by earth and stone, came a sound neither of them had heard in more than a season: a gasp running through the city above, street by street, window by window, as the sky over Aldermere began—faintly, one point at a time—to burn.

"They're coming back," Felix breathed. "The stars."

"Not all of them. Not yet." Isolde tilted her head, counting by instinct the points of light she could feel kindling through the ceiling of the world. "One constellation. Maybe two. He's choosing them, I think—the god. Starting small." She laughed, and it broke in the middle, grief and relief in equal measure. "Starting the way anything worth building starts. At the edge."

Felix's hand found hers in the root-shadow. Her grip was steady. Her name—whatever names remained to her, whatever she would choose when the world finished ending—was her own.

But below their feet, deep in the grave-tree's heartwood, something knocked.

Once. Twice. Soft as a knuckle on a door.

And the flowers on every branch turned, all together, toward the sound.
