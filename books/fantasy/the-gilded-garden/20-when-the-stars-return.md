## 20. When the Stars Return

Dawn came to Aldermere the way a confession comes—slowly, painfully, and with no guarantee of forgiveness.

Felix felt it before she saw it. She was sitting in the buried garden with her back against the tree, or what had been the tree, her hands still trembling in her lap, when the first change arrived not as light but as *weight*. The air itself seemed to settle, the way a house settles after a storm passes, timbers easing back into their joints. For thirteen years—and longer than that, if Isolde's arithmetic was right—the sky above them had been a held breath.

Now it was being let out.

She looked up through the garden's open crown, past the branches that were no longer bare, and watched the darkness overhead begin to thin.

* * *

She did not remember falling asleep. She remembered Isolde's voice, though—that was stitched into her now, thread through every hour of the night. Isolde had knelt beside her while the two gods warred through her body, while something vast and wordless clawed at the inside of her skull looking for purchase, and Isolde had spoken to her. Not prayers. Not spells. Memories.

*The day you crossed the border out of the valley and complained the whole road smelled like someone else's cooking.*

*The night in the observatory when you fell asleep against the armillary sphere and woke up with brass rings printed on your cheek.*

*The way you say my name when you think I'm not listening.*

Each one a rope thrown across a chasm. Each one hers—not given, not designed, not planted in her by an ancient thing with a king's face, but made, accumulated, *chosen*, one ordinary moment at a time. Felix had gripped those memories the way a drowning sailor grips flotsam, and somewhere in the black interior of the struggle, the vast thing inside her had stopped clawing and started listening. It had no name for her anymore. She had unbound herself from it in the garden, spoken her renunciation aloud with the dead king's hand in hers, and what remained of her was nothing anyone had built.

Just a woman. Just Felix—no, not even that, not by law, not on any roll or registry in Aldermere. A nameless woman who belonged entirely to herself, holding the hand of a dying king while a god poured through her like water through a broken dam.

The king had died smiling. She would remember that too, whether she wanted to or not.

* * *

"You're awake." Isolde's voice came from the root-door, rough with exhaustion, and Felix turned to see her picking her way down the garden path with two chipped clay cups steaming in her hands. Her hair had escaped its braid entirely; there was soot on her jaw and what might have been dried blood on her sleeve, none of it hers. "Bray's people are holding the upper halls. What's left of the palace guard surrendered about an hour ago. Apparently half of them only kept fighting because they thought the thing wearing the king would kill their families if they stopped."

"And now?"

"Now they're standing around in the throne room looking at each other like men waking from a long fever." Isolde handed her a cup. Some bitter herbal sludge, valley-grown, the kind Felix had drunk ten thousand mornings on patrol. Her throat closed around the first swallow. "There's no king to arrest and no traitor to hang. Bray made sure of that before—" She stopped.

"Before it killed him," Felix said. The words came out level, which surprised her. Grief, she was learning, did not always arrive as tears. Sometimes it arrived as a strange flat calm, a clearing where a person used to stand.

"Before it killed him," Isolde agreed softly, and sat down beside her against the tree.

They drank in silence. Above them, the sky went on thinning.

* * *

The tree was budding.

Felix had noticed it dimly during the night, in the intervals between agonies, and had assumed she was hallucinating. But morning confirmed it: along every branch of the great leafless heart-tree, green was unfurling. Small tight buds first, then leaves, unfurling with an audible softness, like whispers, like pages turning. By full daylight the whole canopy would be in leaf, the first foliage this tree had worn in thirteen years—or perhaps ever; Isolde suspected the tree had been merely alive, all this time, the way a held note is merely sound.

"It could have gone back," Felix said. "When the cage broke. It could have taken everything back—all of it. Boundaries, thresholds, the whole architecture of the world. It was free. It was *out*."

"It chose not to."

"Why?"

Isolde considered the question with the seriousness she gave all questions, turning her cup slowly in her hands. "I asked it. Or—I stood very close to it and asked, and I don't know that words are its language anymore, but I felt an answer." She paused. "It spent thirteen years as a cage, Felix. And before that, eons as a boundary—a wall, a line, a definition. Always the edge of things. Never the middle. When it finally had the choice of what to be..." She gestured upward, at the tender green. "It wanted to be the middle of something. A garden. Growing things. Roots that hold soil rather than lines that divide it."

Felix looked at the tree a long time.

"That's not how gods behave," she said finally. "In the old stories. They don't get small."

"No," Isolde said. "But then, the stories were written by people who needed gods to be large. Someone has to be blamed. Someone has to be bargained with. A god who just wants to be a tree is terribly inconvenient for everyone." A tired ghost of a smile. "I find I like it anyway."

* * *

They climbed out through the root-door as the sun cleared the eastern towers, and the city of Aldermere came apart around them like a knot loosening.

Not literally—though Felix's nerves were raw enough that she half expected the streets themselves to unravel. No: the city simply stopped pretending. All through the night, while gods fought inside her body and soldiers bled in gilded corridors, the capital had maintained its thirteen-year performance. Lamps lit at proper hours. Bells rung on schedule. Criers announcing the king's continued good health in voices that cracked only slightly. Now, with the sun up and the moon—ordinary, shrunken, honest—fading pale into the west, the pretense collapsed everywhere at once. Doors opened. People came out into the streets blinking, weeping, laughing, arguing, some of them still calling for the king until someone told them, and then the crying changed key entirely.

Felix walked through it feeling like a ghost at her own funeral.

Exhaustion sat on her like chainmail. Every joint ached. There was blood under her fingernails that she was fairly sure was her own, and a bruise blooming across her ribs where the Unmourned, in its last eruption from the crown, had struck her with a force no human arm possessed. She should have been in a bed. Instead she stood in the palace forecourt with Isolde, watching the crowd gather, because something was happening overhead and she could not have looked away if the world ended again first.

The stars were coming back.

* * *

It began, as Isolde would later determine, with the constellation the astronomers called the Ploughman—the same one whose extinguishing Isolde had charted thirteen years ago from her observatory window, the first bar of the cage to go dark. A single point of light kindled low on the northeastern horizon, faint as an ember blown upon, then steady, then unmistakably *there*. A gasp moved through the crowd like wind through wheat.

Then another star. Then three more, in a rough arc, tracing the old shape that every farmhand in Aldermere knew without knowing they knew it.

"They're not all returning at once," Isolde murmured. Her eyes were wet; she didn't seem aware of it. "Of course they're not. Thirteen years of ash doesn't burn off in a moment. Each one has to be—" She searched for the word. "*Re-lit.* From the inside. Whatever the stars actually were—his bars, seen from outside—they were still his, even caged. Even dimmed. Now he can tend them again. One constellation at a time, the way you'd relight lamps down a long dark street."

Felix watched the Ploughman assemble itself, star by patient star, and felt something in her chest crack open—not painfully, or not only painfully. The sky had been a lie for thirteen years, a ceiling painted over a prison, and she had lived beneath it without ever seeing it, the way she had lived inside the valley's wards without understanding them, the way she had carried a name she never chose. She was done living inside other things' designs. And the sky, apparently, was done lying.

By mid-morning the Ploughman blazed complete, absurd and beautiful against the blue, visible even in daylight, and the crowd in the forecourt had stopped being a crowd and become something else—a congregation, maybe, or just a few thousand exhausted people standing together in the sun, pointing upward, letting themselves hope.

"The Hunter next," Isolde predicted quietly. "He always followed the Ploughman."

She was right. He rose that afternoon.

* * *

They found Bray where he had fallen, at the top of the grand stair, and they brought him down with honors that no one organized and everyone obeyed.

His soldiers—Bray's irregulars, the ones who had climbed the palace with them through fire and moonlight—carried him shoulder-high through the halls, and servants lined the corridors and bowed as he passed, these strangers bowing to a mercenary sergeant from nowhere, because everyone understood without discussion that he had bought the whole city's morning with the only coin he had. Felix walked behind the bier. She had known Bray for nineteen years. He had taught her how to hold a shield so it became part of your arm; he had been the one who swore the company's oath loudest at the shrine, back when the Silent One still answered; and he had broken first, not from cowardice but from the unbearable weight of ten years of silence, ten years of praying into a well that gave back only his own voice. The Unmourned had found him at his loneliest and told him exactly the lie he needed: *you were abandoned.* It had almost worked. In the end, at the root-door, he had chosen differently. He had shown her the way in. He had stood between her and the thing in the crown for exactly as long as his body allowed, which was longer than it should have, which was pure Bray.

At the shrine of the Silent One—the little walled chapel near the barracks, dust-sheeted and neglected these thirteen years—they laid him out beneath the empty altar niche.

And the altar niche was not empty anymore.

Felix saw it as they set him down: a single object resting in the stone recess where the god's icon had stood before it was removed, or hidden, or destroyed. It was a branch. Green, budding, freshly cut, still beaded with sap—impossibly fresh, impossibly out of place, as if it had grown there overnight through solid stone. She stared at it, and Isolde stared at it, and old Corporal Vess, who had served with the company since before Felix joined, took off his helmet with shaking hands.

"He's tending us," Vess whispered. "Even now. Even like this. He's—"

Felix laid her palm flat against the cold stone of the altar and felt, distantly, the way she'd once felt the wardstones humming under the valley frost: presence. Diminished, diffuse, spread thin through roots and soil and stone and every threshold in the world—but present. Attentive. The god who had gone silent for ten years had not been absent. He had been *compressed*, folded down into a seed, waiting in the dark the way seeds wait. And now he answered not with thunder or command but with a branch on an altar and a warmth under her hand, small as a candle, real as bread.

"We'll bury him properly," Felix said. Her voice held. "Under the garden. Where the roots are."

No one argued. Somehow, she hadn't expected them to.

* * *

Evening found her back in the garden, alone at last, or nearly.

The king lay in state in the great hall above, and the arguments had already begun—she could hear them even down here, muffled through the earth, the regency factions circling already, hungry voices sorting themselves over the body of the man who had been eaten alive for their sake. Let them argue. Tomorrow, or the day after, someone would come looking for the woman who had unbound everything, the nameless mercenary with no lineage and no legal existence, and there would be questions she had no answers for and offers she didn't want and blame, probably, because there was always blame. That was tomorrow's weather.

Tonight there was only the tree.

She stood beneath its new canopy as true dark fell, and she watched the sky finish its work. The Hunter burned whole above the eastern wall. To the south, fainter, the Sisters were kindling one by one, five points, six. Somewhere beyond the horizon, over the Warden's Valley—her valley, the valley that forgot magic, the valley where she had hidden for thirty years inside walls built to keep her safe from a truth she couldn't have survived knowing—somewhere over that quiet country too, she knew, the stars were relighting, lamp by lamp down the long dark street. The wards had fallen. Magic could follow her home now, anywhere, anytime. The valley would never be invisible again.

She found that she wasn't afraid.

A leaf detached from the lowest branch and spiraled down, and she caught it without thinking—an old soldier's reflex, snatching things out of the air—and turned it over in her fingers. New growth. Bright green, veined with silver, warm to the touch the way a sleeping animal is warm. She looked up into the branches and spoke aloud, the way she'd once spoken at the shrine, the way she'd spoken every night for ten years into silence.

"Thank you," she said. "For staying."

The garden breathed. That was the only word for it—a slow exhalation through root and leaf and soil, stirring her hair, carrying the smell of rain that hadn't fallen yet. Not an answer, exactly. Not a voice. Just acknowledgment, the nod of one survivor to another across a battlefield at dusk.

She tucked the leaf into her breast pocket, over her heart, where the oath-mark had been before she unbound it. The skin there was smooth and blank now. Nothing written on it. Nothing written *on* her at all anymore.

From the root-door behind her came footsteps she knew better than her own heartbeat, and Isolde's voice, gentle and wry and utterly exhausted: "You're going to miss the council session. The one where they decide whether you're a hero or a heretic. Bray's corporals have already started betting."

Felix smiled at the tree, at the sky going bright with returned stars, at the whole improbable, reopened world.

"Let them bet," she said. "I've got a name to choose first."
