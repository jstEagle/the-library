## 21. The Wardstone Rewritten

### Isolde

The regency council met in the Hall of Mirrors, which was either a joke or a penance—Isolde had never decided which. Thirteen years of Aldous's reign had been conducted in that room, beneath a ceiling of silvered glass that multiplied every candle into a false constellation, and now the men and women who would govern in the dead king's name sat beneath it arguing about grain tariffs while the real stars, outside, were still learning how to burn.

She had been reinstated by proclamation on the ninth day after the Unbinding. *Royal Astronomer, restored to office and honor*, the herald had read, and then, because the clerk drafting it had no idea what else to do with her, *and charged with the restoration of the heavens*. As if the sky were a roof she could re-tile. She had signed the document with a hand that shook, not from humility but from the specific fury of a woman who had been right all along and been exiled for it.

Now she stood at the long table with her charts spread out where the tariff ledgers should have been, and let them look at her.

"The thirteenth turn ends in eleven days," she said. "You've all seen the moon. It is shrinking back to its proper size, night by night, as the Unmourned's grip rots out of it. When the turn closes, the calendar will have to be amended—every almanac in the kingdom carries a month that shouldn't exist, and we can no longer pretend it doesn't. I want authority over the observatory, funding for glass and ink and assistants who can actually read an ephemeris, and I want the archives of the last thirteen years opened to me. All of them. Including the ones sealed under the privy seal."

Lord Chancellor Merrick—a narrow man with the complexion of someone who had spent thirteen years agreeing—steepled his fingers. "The archives are sensitive, Mistress Vane."

"Of course they're sensitive. They contain the record of how this court failed to notice its king had been hollowed out and worn like a festival mask." She kept her voice level, almost gentle, which made it worse for them. "I am not asking to publish them. I am asking to read them, so that when the histories are written—and they will be written, my lords, whether you commission them or not—they contain at least one true sentence."

Silence, of the particular quality that meant negotiation. Isolde had learned to wait inside silences the way other people waited inside rooms.

It was Lady Serah Coll who broke it—the youngest voice at the table, elevated to the council because half the old guard had served the crown-thing too closely to survive scrutiny. "And the valley?" Serah asked. "The Warden's Valley. The regency has to decide what to do about the walls. There are petitions. Some of the border villages want the wards rebuilt exactly as they were. Others—" she hesitated, "—others have heard what happened there. That magic came back. That the god lives in a tree."

"The god doesn't live in a tree," Isolde said. "The god *chose* to become one. There's a difference, and the difference is the entire lesson." She gathered her charts. "But yes. The valley. You should send word to Felix Thornbury—or whatever name she's calling herself this week—and ask her, not tell her. If you issue an edict about those walls without asking, I promise you the regency's first official act will be its first mistake."

"Thornbury is legally dead," Merrick said primly. "The rolls were checked. No such person exists."

"No," Isolde agreed, rolling her charts closed. "Which is rather the point."

### Felix

They gave her a room in the palace, which she hated, and then a house near the garden gate, which she hated slightly less, and finally she gave both back and took a cot in the barracks of Bray's old company—which was no longer Bray's company, because Bray was buried under the garden-tree with a sword across his chest and a lie across his heart, and the company had voted, awkwardly and tearfully, to call itself something new.

Felix had stood at that vote with her arms crossed, listening to forty mercenaries fail to name themselves. *The Company of the Broken Oath* had been proposed and rejected as too bitter. *The King's Own* rejected as too much like licking boots. Someone suggested *Thornbury's Company*, and Felix said, flatly, "Don't," and the word came out with enough iron in it that nobody asked why.

In the end they'd gone with *the Garden Guard*, because they'd be stationed at the palace's buried gate anyway, and because their sergeant—a scarred, patient woman named Odile who had once held Felix's hair back while she vomited after the fight with Bray—said, "We guarded a god we couldn't hear for ten years. Might as well guard a tree we can."

So Felix slept in a barracks again, among people who knew her, and woke before dawn out of ten years' habit, and walked the palace grounds until the sun came up, and tried to figure out what a person did with herself when the war was over and the oath was ash and the name was gone.

That was the strange part. Not grief—grief she knew, grief for Bray and for the king who had died as himself for exactly ninety seconds, and even, in some tangled way, grief for the Unmourned, which had only ever wanted what everything wanted: to not be alone, to not end, and had pursued it with the patience of a thing that had never once been told *no*. Grief she could carry. What she couldn't carry was the paperwork.

Because the law, it turned out, had opinions about names.

"You understand the difficulty," said the registrar, a small precise man named Quillon who had survived thirteen years of the crown-thing by being too boring to possess. He laid out documents on the table of the little administrative annex like a surgeon laying out instruments. "Your legal identity was constructed. 'Felix Thornbury' was entered into the rolls sixteen years ago under a charter of wardship that traces back—" he cleared his throat, "—back to instruments we have since burned. The name was a vessel's handle. Legally speaking, it never belonged to anyone, because the person it belonged to didn't exist."

"So I don't exist."

"You exist magnificently," Quillon said. "You just don't exist *legally*. Which means you cannot hold property, draw pay, sign a lease, marry, testify in court, or be hanged, though I'd advise against testing that last one. The remedy is simple. You take a new name. Freely chosen, freely declared, witnessed. The law writes you fresh." He slid a blank form across the table and a pen after it. "Whenever you're ready."

Felix looked at the form for a long time.

Isolde had told her, that first night in the blind streets of the capital, when Felix had felt like a puppet discovering its strings: *A name given isn't a self decided.* And Isolde had been right, the way she was right about orbits and wrong about how much wine constituted "one glass." But knowing a thing and doing it were different countries, and Felix had spent her whole life crossing borders she didn't choose.

"I'll come back," she said, and left the form blank on the table, and walked out into the morning.

She walked, without quite deciding to, down through the palace's lower levels, past the guards who saluted her now—she still hadn't gotten used to that—to the root-door, and out into the garden.

The tree had buds on it.

Ten days since the Unbinding, and the leafless thing that had been the boundary-god's buried heart was putting out buds along every branch, tight green fists of them, thousands, as if the whole tree were holding its breath before a shout. The grass around the roots had come in thick and absurdly green. A wren had moved in. The wren sang constantly, unbothered, in the way of creatures that had never heard of gods.

Felix sat down against the trunk, where the bark was warm in a way bark shouldn't be, and put her palm flat against it.

*I'm going to need a name,* she thought at it. *Any advice?*

The tree didn't answer. It never answered—not in words, not anymore. But warmth moved under her hand, slow as sap, patient as stone, and she had learned to read it the way Isolde read starlight: not a message, but a presence. *Here. Still here. Take your time.*

She sat there until the light came slanting gold through the budding branches, and somewhere in that light she stopped trying to think of a name and started remembering instead.

Not the given memories—the ones the Unmourned had planted, the false childhood it had confessed to her in the dark, designed to make her a comfortable vessel. Those were gone, or worse than gone, dissolved into the general ash of the broken covenant. What remained was hers. The first time she'd stood watch on the valley border and understood, without words, that she would defend it. The taste of river water after a two-day march. Odile laughing so hard at a bad joke that she fell off a fence. Isolde's face lit by a single candle in the observatory, saying *there*, pointing at a star that wasn't there yet, and then—it coming back, one point of fire in the black, and Isolde crying without any dignity whatsoever.

None of those moments had a name in them. All of them were hers.

She went back up the stairs and found Quillon's annex and picked up the pen.

She wrote: *Wren.*

Just that. No surname. Quillon looked at it, looked at her, opened his mouth, and—wisely, for a bureaucrat—closed it again.

"Witnessed and declared," he said, stamping it. "Welcome into existence, Mistress Wren."

It fit strangely, like a boot not yet broken in. But it was hers. Nobody had handed it to her. She'd taken it off a bird that sang in a dead god's garden, and if that wasn't the most honest provenance a name ever had, she'd eat the form.

### Isolde

The observatory smelled of dust and possibility.

Thirteen years of neglect had not been kind to it—Isolde had found her old instruments corroded, her star-charts used as drawer-lining by some functionary, and one of the great lenses cracked by a servant using it to crack nuts—but the bones of the place were sound, and bones were what mattered. She had hired four assistants in the first week: two disgraced academy students, one defrocked monk with a gift for grinding glass, and a girl of perhaps fifteen who had climbed in through a window because women weren't admitted and who turned out to calculate lunar perturbations faster than Isolde did. Isolde hired her on the spot and dared the academy to say anything.

Every clear night they climbed the tower and watched the sky heal.

It happened constellation by constellation, exactly as it had begun—the stars relighting in patterns that followed no astronomy Isolde knew, but which she slowly, painstakingly, began to map. The Silent One was returning itself to heaven piece by piece, and each relit star was a bar of the old prison, reforged—but open now, free-standing, bars of a gate standing wide rather than bars of a cage. She wrote it all down. Someday it would matter. Someday someone would need to know what the sky looked like while it forgave itself.

On the eleventh night, Felix—*Wren*, she was calling herself now, and Isolde practiced the name silently until it stopped catching in her chest—came up the tower stairs carrying two cups of wine and the particular expression of someone avoiding a decision.

"Quillon's form," Isolde guessed.

"Signed it. Yesterday." Wren set the cups down and leaned on the parapet beside her, shoulder touching shoulder, watching the city below go candlelit and gold. "You're looking at me like you already know."

"I know you. Knowing things is my profession." Isolde sipped her wine. "What did you take?"

"Wren."

Isolde laughed—really laughed, the undignified kind—and Wren glared at her with tremendous dignity until she subsided. "It suits you," Isolde managed. "Small, ordinary, sings wherever it lands, entirely unimpressed by divinity."

"That's the idea." Wren was quiet a moment. Below them, a cart rattled through the gate; somewhere a fiddle played; the city sounded like a city again, instead of a stage waiting for its cue. "The valley sent word today. Through the regency. They've been offered a choice about the walls—keep the wards, drop them, or rebuild them different. Open ones. And they're asking—" she exhaled, "—they're asking me to come home and help them decide. Me. The mercenary who legally didn't exist until Tuesday."

"What will you tell them?"

"I told them I'd come. After the turn ends." Wren turned, and in the light of the half-healed sky Isolde watched her decide something. "Come with me. The observatory can spare you for a season—you said yourself the stars won't finish relighting till spring. Come see the valley. Come stand on a border that isn't a wall anymore." A pause, and then, plainer, the way Isolde liked best: "Come home with me. Or come make a new one. Whichever it turns out to be."

Isolde looked north, past the city's glow, to where the mountains would be if she could see them, and the valley behind them, and the shrine where a silent god had once kept a lonely mercenary company safe by keeping it ignorant. She had spent her life reading distances—measuring how far away things were, how long their light took to arrive, how much of what we see is already gone.

"One condition," she said. "I get to survey the valley's sky from inside it. First astronomical observations ever recorded from within the wards. My name goes in the ledger."

"Deal," said Wren, and grinned, and it was the grin of someone belonging to herself.

Above them, in the northeast, another star caught fire—then a second beside it, then a third, a slow bright stitch closing a wound in the dark. The girl-assistant shouted down the tower stair for everyone to come look, and Isolde stayed where she was, shoulder to shoulder with the woman she loved, under a sky that remembered how to burn.

Eleven days until the thirteenth turn ended. Eleven days until the extra month that shouldn't exist closed its book forever—and Isolde, watching the moon rise small and pale and shrinking, felt the first cold thread of doubt she had allowed herself since the Unbinding.

Because the moon was shrinking.

And it was shrinking *too fast*.
