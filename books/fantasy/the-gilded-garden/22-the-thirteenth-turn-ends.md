## 22. The Thirteenth Turn Ends

The frost had come early that year, and it lay along the wardstone like a seam of silver thread, stitching the valley to the mountains on either side.

Felix walked the border at dawn because she liked it best then—liked the way the light came down the eastern ridge in a single long blade and set every frozen blade of grass burning, liked the hush of a world not yet awake enough to need her. A year ago she would have called this patrol. Now there was no one to report to, no company ledger, no sergeant's bark at her heels. She walked the border because it was hers, in the way a garden is yours when you have knelt in its dirt long enough. The valley did not belong to her. But this path—the worn groove between the thirteenth stone and the river ford, the places where the wards breathed in and out like something sleeping—this was hers, and she kept it.

The stones themselves had changed. That was the thing visitors never quite believed, when they came up from Aldermere with their ledgers and their questions about the regency and the observatory and the strange little valley where magic pooled now like rainwater in a cellar. The wardstones used to be silent things, dead granite that Felix had maintained without understanding, oiling their iron fittings and clearing their moss because the shrine-keeper before her had done the same, and the keeper before that, all the way back into a history no one had written down. Now they hummed. Not loudly—you had to press your palm flat against them, as Felix did now, kneeling at the base of the fourth stone with her breath fogging in the cold—and feel it: a low, patient vibration, like a cat purring in its sleep, like a heart that had remembered what it was for.

"Good morning," she told it, because she talked to them now. Everyone did. It felt foolish for exactly one week after the garden, after the tree, after the god rose out of the earth and chose, in its vast and wordless way, to stay—chose roots over dominion, soil over sky—and then it stopped feeling foolish forever.

The stone hummed back against her palm. Or perhaps it was only the frost melting under her hand. Isolde said it was impossible to say, and that the impossibility was rather the point.

Felix stood, brushed the wet from her knees, and looked east along the line of stones toward the place where the path bent and a second figure had just come around the shoulder of the hill, wrapped in a grey traveling cloak, carrying two tin cups and an expression of profound disapproval directed at the cold.

"You're up early," Isolde called, "for someone who claims to value sleep."

"I value *your* sleep," Felix said. "Mine I've given up on."

Isolde Vane, reinstated Astronomer Royal by decree of a regency council that had very little choice in the matter, picked her way down the frozen slope with the careful indignation of a woman who had spent thirty years indoors and eleven months learning otherwise. Her hair was more silver than black now, or perhaps Felix simply noticed it more. She pressed one of the cups into Felix's hands—tea, oversteeped, slightly too sweet, made exactly wrong in exactly the way Felix had come to prefer it—and stood beside her, shoulder almost touching shoulder, and looked out over the valley.

Below them, Thornbury village was waking. Smoke rose from forty chimneys in straight grey columns. Someone was already arguing with a mule near the millpond; the sound carried thin and bright across the frost. On the shrine hill, the old stone circle where Felix had once sworn an oath to a god who could not answer stood open-doored, tended now by anyone who wished to tend it, which meant mostly children, which meant mostly chaos, which was fine.

"It's tonight," Isolde said. It wasn't a question. She'd been counting down for a month, marking almanac pages with charcoal ticks, and Felix had pretended not to notice because pretending not to notice was a kindness they had both learned to give each other.

"The last night of the turn," Felix agreed.

"Thirteen years," Isolde said. "Thirteen turns of the false moon, if you count the way the old calendars counted—which, incidentally, no one should, the whole system was a mess, I've written a monograph—"

"I know. You've shown me the monograph."

"—and tonight it ends. The moon sets ordinary." Isolde said it the way she said most important things: quickly, precisely, looking slightly away, as though the feeling were too large to examine directly and must be approached from the side. "Small. Just a moon. I keep trying to imagine it and I can't. My whole life the sky has been either blind or swollen. An ordinary moon feels like a rumor."

Felix drank her terrible tea and let the silence sit between them, because some silences were wounds and some were blankets, and she had gotten good at telling the difference.

* * *

They walked the border together, the way they did most mornings now, and Felix showed her the changes the way she always did, even though Isolde had seen them all a hundred times.

At the seventh stone, where the hairline crack had once run—a crack so fine Felix had needed a full day of bad weather and worse premonition to find it—the granite had healed itself into a pale scar, smooth as river glass. Felix touched it out of habit. Under her fingers, the hum deepened for a moment, the way a sleeping person murmurs when you say their name.

"It remembers you," Isolde observed.

"It remembers everyone who touches it. Bray's soldiers come through twice a season and the whole line sings for an hour afterward. Half the village complains they can't sleep."

"And the other half?"

"The other half leaves offerings. Mostly bread. Once, inexplicably, a boot."

Isolde laughed—that short, surprised laugh she still seemed startled by herself, as though laughter were an instrument she'd been taught was broken and kept finding in working order. Felix had decided, somewhere in the past year, that she would spend whatever it took to keep hearing it.

They passed the ford, where the water ran black and quick under its lace of ice. They passed the shrine hill and climbed it, because the tree was there—or rather, the tree was *under* there, its roots wound through the hill's heart the way the god's heart had once been buried beneath the palace garden, but its branches had found their way up through the old stone circle over the course of the year, bursting through the flagstones in three places, growing with the unhurried confidence of something that had waited thirteen years underground and saw no reason to rush now.

In autumn it had gone gold, then bare. This morning, on the last day of the thirteenth turn, the first buds had come.

Felix stood beneath the branches and looked up at them: small, tight, green-flecked knots of future leaf, dozens of them along every twig, catching the early light. The villagers had begun calling it the Gilded Garden, half-joking, and the name had stuck the way names do—not because anyone planned it but because it was true. In summer the bark caught the sun and threw it back warm as brass. Beneath it, the air was always a degree kinder than the air outside.

"Do you think it knows?" Isolde asked quietly. "That today is the last day?"

Felix considered. She had learned to think about the god the way you thought about weather, or a very large animal, or grief—not as a person exactly, but as a presence with moods and seasons and a patience beyond human accounting. It had never spoken. It never would. But it had ways of being clear.

"I think," she said, "that it waited thirteen years in a hole under a palace while something wore its absence like a coat. I think it can wait one more night for spring." She put her hand against the trunk. The bark was cold, and under the cold, faintly, unmistakably, it hummed—the same note as the wardstones, the same note as everything the god touched now, because the valley itself had become its body, its boundary, its chosen shape. "But I'll tell it anyway. Later. When we're alone."

Isolde glanced at her sidelong. "You talk to it when we're alone?"

"You talk to your instruments when you're alone. I've heard you. You apologize to the telescope."

"That telescope has earned apologies."

They descended the hill together, and the morning went on.

* * *

Midday brought visitors, as midday often did.

A cart came up the valley road from the south with two of Bray's former soldiers riding escort—Maren and Oskar, who had taken the regency's offer of honest wages and discovered, to their visible bewilderment, that they liked them. They carried letters from Aldermere: one for the village council, thick with seal and ceremony, and one for Felix, which was a single sheet in Maren's blunt handwriting and began *Boss* and ended *come visit sometime, the city's weird now but the pie shop on Candle Lane survived everything somehow.*

The official letter Felix handed off unread to Councilwoman Dree, who had become, over twelve months, the kind of woman who read official letters the way other people breathed. The unofficial one Felix read standing in the road, and it made her chest do the complicated thing it still did sometimes when the world reminded her that she had people—scattered, imperfect, alive.

Sergeant Bray's name was in it, the way it always was now: the memorial wall in the palace courtyard, the regency pension paid to his sister in Harrowmere, the small stubborn fact that he had died buying them time and that no one, ever, would be allowed to forget it. Felix touched the letter once, folded it, and put it inside her coat, over her heart, where she kept such things.

"He'd have hated the memorial," she told Maren.

"He'd have hated the pension more. Said being paid for dying was the one contract he'd never sign." Maren shrugged. "His sister says thank you anyway. Every time."

The afternoon went to small work. There was always small work now, and Felix had discovered she loved it—not the desperate labor of survival she'd known as a mercenary, but the ordinary maintenance of a life: splitting wood for Widow Carrick whose shoulder was bad, re-hanging the shrine hill gate that the children had knocked crooked again, sitting with old Tam the astrologer's successor—no, not successor, Tam had insisted, *colleague*, though the boy was fourteen and terrified of him—while they argued happily about whether the returning stars matched the old charts. They didn't, quite. Isolde said the sky had been rearranged, gently, like furniture in a room after a long illness, and that mapping the differences would take a generation. The boy said that sounded like the best job in the world. Tam said he'd have his work cut out keeping the boy humble. Nobody believed him.

Toward dusk, Felix went up to the shrine hill alone, as she'd promised, and stood under the budding tree with her hand on its trunk.

"Tonight," she said. "It ends tonight. I don't know what happens after. I don't suppose you do either, and I find I don't mind." The hum rose through her palm, up her arm, settled somewhere behind her ribs where the oath-mark had once burned and now, most days, was simply quiet skin. "I wanted to say—" She stopped. Started again. Talking to gods was easier than talking to people in some ways and much harder in others. "Thank you. For staying small. I know what it cost you. I know what it would have been to take it all back, the godhood, the whole sky, and you chose a garden instead. Somebody should say it out loud. So: somebody said it."

She stayed a while longer. The tree budded overhead in the failing light, patient as arithmetic.

* * *

Night came down clean and cloudless, the first truly clear night in a week, as though the sky itself had arranged to be presentable.

Felix and Isolde took blankets and the last of the too-sweet tea up to the eastern ridge, above the wardstone line, where they could see the whole bowl of the valley below and the whole sweep of the sky above, and they sat close together in the cold and waited for the moon to set.

It was already low when they arrived—a fat, pale, unremarkable moon, waning toward nothing, hanging over the western mountains like a lamp someone had forgotten to trim. Around it, the stars were out. All of them. A year ago Felix had not known how many stars there were; she had grown up under a blind sky and then a swollen one, and the first night the constellations had reignited—one by one across the face of Aldermere, over weeks, like lamps relit in the windows of a house whose owner had finally come home—she had sat outside until dawn and cried without entirely knowing why. Even now, a year later, she sometimes caught herself looking up mid-task, arrested, astonished, a farmwife staring at rain after drought.

"There," Isolde said softly. She was pointing west, where the moon's lower edge had touched the mountain. "Watch."

They watched.

The moon went down the way moons are supposed to go down: slowly, gracelessly, flattening against the ridgeline, dimming from white to gold to the color of lamplight through cloth, until it was a sliver, a smudge, a memory, and then the western mountains stood black and complete against the deepening blue and there was no moon at all. No thirteenth turn. No swelling. No power gathering in its phases like water behind a dam. Just an ordinary satellite setting over an ordinary world, on schedule, asking nothing of anyone.

Felix realized she had been holding her breath and let it out in a long white plume.

"Well," she said.

"Well," Isolde agreed.

And then, because neither of them could help it, they both started laughing—quietly at first, then properly, leaning into each other under the blankets, laughing at nothing, at everything, at the sheer anticlimactic mercy of a sky that had simply, finally, stopped making demands. Thirteen years of dread ending not with thunder but with a moonset. Somewhere below, a dog barked at the dark and gave up. The valley lights glimmered. The wardstones hummed their low contented note along the border, and above them the stars turned in their slow ancient wheel, rearranged but whole, and none of it required anything of Felix Thornbury except that she sit here, in the cold, next to the person she loved, and witness it.

She reached over and found Isolde's hand without looking. Isolde laced their fingers together with the ease of long practice, and neither of them said anything about it, because it had long since passed beyond needing words.

"Tomorrow's the first day of the new calendar," Isolde said eventually, her voice roughened from laughing and cold. "Turn One. Year One, if the council ratifies my proposal, which they will, because my proposal is correct."

"Naturally."

"I'm going to spend the rest of my life mapping a sky nobody's seen in thirteen years. It's the greatest gift anyone's ever given me and it was left here by a monster." Isolde was quiet a moment. "I keep waiting to feel guilty about loving it. The work. This. I haven't managed it yet."

"Don't," Felix said. "Guilt's just another cage. We've torn down enough of those."

Isolde turned to look at her then, really look, the way she did maybe once a season—full attention, astronomer's precision, as though Felix were a phenomenon worth charting. "Do you ever miss it?" she asked. "Your name? Thornbury?"

Felix considered the question honestly, because Isolde deserved honesty and because she had spent a year asking herself the same thing. The name had been a leash, a failsafe, a vessel's label scrawled on her by something ancient before she drew breath. She had renounced it aloud in a buried garden while reality cracked around her, and the law had obliged her by striking it from every register, leaving her—by decree, by choice, by fire—a woman with no legal name at all. The village had solved this the way villages solve things: they called her *Keeper*, because she kept the border, and the name fit like a coat cut to measure.

"No," she said at last. "I miss my company, some days. I miss Bray every day. But the name?" She shook her head. "It was never mine. It was a hook someone left in the water. I'm glad it's gone." She smiled, small and real. "Besides, I'm getting a new one. Dree says the council will ratify any name I choose by spring. I've been thinking."

"Thinking what?"

"*Vane.*"

Isolde went very still. In the starlight, Felix watched her work through it—the surprise, then the understanding, then something warmer underneath that she tried, and failed, to make look casual.

"That's—" Isolde cleared her throat. "That's a surname. People will assume things."

"People already assume things. Have for months. Let them assume correctly."

Isolde laughed again, that startled instrument finding its voice, and pulled Felix's blanket-wrapped shoulder against her own, and for a long time they simply sat, two women on a frozen ridge above a humming valley, watching the stars wheel through the first hour of a world with no false moon in it.

Far below, the Gilded Garden stood budding in the dark, patient as spring. Along the border, the wardstones sang their one low note. And high overhead, ordinary and vast and asking nothing, the sky went on turning—open, lit, and free—for the first time in thirteen years, for the first time in Felix's remembered life, for all the nights to come.
