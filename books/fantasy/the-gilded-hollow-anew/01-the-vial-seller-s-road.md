## 1. The Vial Seller's Road

The road into Thornfell smelled of pine resin and old snow, and beneath both — faint as a lie told well — of copper. Freya Eastwick had learned to read that smell the way other smugglers read weather. Copper meant wards. Wards meant wardens, and wardens meant that somewhere ahead, someone was about to pay an obscene sum of coin for the one thing money couldn't buy anywhere else in the borderlands: yesterday.

She rode with her hood up and her satchel strapped tight against her ribs, where the glass could not knock against itself. Forty-one vials rode there, each no longer than her thumb, each stoppered with wax the color of dried blood, each holding a sliver of somebody's life. A first kiss. A mother's funeral. The particular green of a garden that had burned down twenty years ago and existed now only in this glass and nowhere else on earth.

Freya had stolen all of them herself.

Not the way thieves stole silverware — prying, clumsy, leaving fingerprints. Memory-work was gentler than that, and crueler. You sat across from your mark at some dim tavern table, you let them talk, you let them *want* to talk, and when they leaned into the good part of the story — the part that glowed — you touched two fingers to their temple and drew it out like thread from a spool. They never felt it leave. That was the trick of it, and the shame of it. She'd bottled a hundred lives and never once left a scar anyone could see.

The scars were hers instead. She'd stopped counting those around her twenty-third winter.

* * *

Thornfell's lower quarter woke late and ugly, all shuttered windows and breath steaming in doorways. Freya moved through it like smoke through a keyhole: three streets east, down the cobbler's stair, along the drainage channel where the ice grew thick enough to walk on if you didn't think too hard about what was frozen inside it. The warden patrol passed above her on the high road — six of them in slate-grey cloaks, lanterns swinging, their boots ringing a rhythm she knew better than any lullaby. *Left, left, pause. Left, left, pause.* She counted them without looking up. Counting was habit. Habit was survival.

Her client tonight was Lady Vessane Corrow, widow, collector, a woman whose house smelled of lilies rotting politely in silver bowls. Vessane bought memories the way other widows bought jewels — compulsively, competitively, and always for occasions that hadn't happened yet. Tonight's occasion was her own birthday, which she intended to spend weeping over a stranger's grief because her own had gone stale.

"You're late," Vessane said, not turning from the window. Her reflection watched Freya in the dark glass, sharp-eyed despite the powder. "I said moonrise."

"Your moonrise," Freya said, setting her satchel on the table. "My moonrise is whenever I say it is."

That earned her the ghost of a smile. Vessane liked that Freya didn't grovel; it made the transaction feel less like commerce and more like conspiracy, and conspiracy was the closest thing to intimacy either of them could afford. She swept to the table and lifted vials one by one to the lamplight, tilting them so the memories caught — a shimmer, a color, a mood made visible. Grief came up grey-blue, like deep water. Joy ran gold. Love, when Freya had ever managed to bottle it clean, went the color of embers under ash.

"This one." Vessane held up a vial of sea-green light. "Whose?"

"A fisherman's daughter in Saltmere. Last day before the storm took her father. She remembers the whole morning — mending nets, his hands, him singing off-key."

"And she sold it? Willingly?"

Freya shrugged. "Grief is heavy. Some people would rather carry coin."

It wasn't entirely true. Nobody sold willingly, not really; they sold hungry, or frightened, or desperate, and Freya had gotten very good at not letting her face move while they did it. But Vessane wanted the fiction, so Freya sold her that too. Everything got sold, in the end. That was the whole architecture of Thornfell.

Vessane paid in warden-minted marks, crisp and cold, and then — because she was lonely in the specific way of women who owned everything except company — she kept talking. About the stars, actually. About how strange the sky had looked lately, how the astrologers muttered, how her late husband had once sworn the constellations used to *watch* you back.

"The stars are stones," Freya said, packing her satchel. "They don't watch anything."

"Mm." Vessane's eyes lingered on her a moment too long. "You say that like a woman who's checked."

Freya let herself out through the servant's stair and did not check the sky until she was three streets away. When she finally glanced up between the rooftops, the stars were there — dim, distant, ordinary. Watching nothing. She told herself the small cold feeling under her breastbone was just hunger, and bought roasted chestnuts from a cart to prove it.

* * *

The second job of the night was supposed to be simple.

A merchant named Aldous Pell, new money, terrified of forgetting — that was all she knew, and it was enough. New-money men who feared forgetting were the easiest marks in the trade: they paid double, wept prettily, and never asked where the memories came from. He'd requested something specific this time. *Something grand,* his note had said. *Something with fire in it.*

She met him in the back room of a dyer's shop, where the air hung thick with mordant and the walls were stained every color a thing could be ruined into. Pell was sweating even though the room was cold. He had the look of a man who had recently done something he needed help not remembering, or something he needed help pretending to remember. Either way, coin was coin.

"I want to feel it again," he said, before she'd even unpacked. "Whatever you have. Something big. My life has been ledgers. I want one night of — of *scale.*"

Scale. She almost laughed. Men like Pell always wanted scale, and never understood what it cost the person who'd lived it.

Freya laid out her selection on the dye-stained table. Pell hovered, breathing too fast, touching nothing. His fingers were ink-stained. His eyes kept darting to the door.

And that should have been her first warning — the darting eyes, the sweat, the locked door he insisted on. But she was tired, and the chestnuts had only half-fed the hollow, and she wanted this job done so she could get back to the room she rented and the thin wall she stared at and the nameless dark she slept in. So she let him choose.

He chose the vial at the far end. Of course he did. It was the brightest thing on the table — a memory she'd taken three weeks ago from a dying old woman up past the high passes, a woman who had babbled of things that made no sense, whose last coherent hour had burned so hot that the glass still seemed warm to the touch. Freya had never sold it. She'd told herself it was because the memory was unstable. Really it was because every time she held it up to the light, something in her chest pulled tight, like a wire tuned to a note she couldn't hear.

"Forget it," she heard herself say. "Pick another."

But Pell's hand was already closing around it, greedy and certain, and pride — stupid, restless pride — stopped her from wrestling it back. Fine. Let him have his fire. Let him weep over someone else's glory and sleep soundly after, the way only buyers could.

He unstopped it against the table's edge, the way clients did, tipping the mouth of the vial toward himself so the memory could pour out into his waiting mind —

— and the vial cracked.

Not broke. *Cracked.* A single hairline fracture, running crooked from lip to base, and through that fracture the memory *leaked* — not into Pell, but out, into the room, into the air, into Freya, who was standing closest and had no defenses up and no time to raise any.

The world went white and gold.

She was standing on a ridge of black stone under a sky full of wings. Not birds — *wings*, vast and slow-beating, casting shadows the size of valleys. Heat rolled over her face from below, from a throat of living flame, and the fire was not red the way fire was supposed to be red but white-gold at its heart, blue at its edges, burning with a light that had *weight*, that pressed against her skin like a hand. And the sky — the sky above the burning was crowded with stars, more stars than she had ever seen, stars in rivers and whorls, and every one of them was *awake*, wheeling slowly, watching, watching, watching—

Then it snapped shut. The crack sealed itself — glass knitting glass, impossibly, the fracture vanishing as though ashamed — and Freya was standing in a dyer's back room with frost on the window and a merchant screaming and forty vials of ordinary human sorrow rattling in her satchel.

Pell had fallen out of his chair. He was clawing at his own arms, babbling — "*what was that, what WAS that*" — and Freya didn't answer him because she couldn't. Her mouth wouldn't work. Her heart was slamming against her ribs like something trying to get out.

Because dragon-fire didn't exist anymore. Everyone knew that. It was the first lesson children learned and the last thing the wardens enforced: the dragons were gone, the war was over, the stars were only stones. Three hundred years of quiet, built on that fact, patrolled by men in slate-grey cloaks.

And she had just seen one. Felt its heat on her face. Heard — gods, she'd nearly forgotten until this instant — heard it *speak*, a voice like mountains settling, and the words it had said were still ringing in the hollow of her skull where the leaked memory had lodged and refused to leave:

*Find the keeper's daughter.*

Freya snatched the vial off the table. It was warm. It had always been warm. She had told herself it was her imagination for three weeks, the same way she'd told herself the hollow in her chest was hunger, the same way she'd told herself the stars didn't watch.

Outside the dyer's shop, far off across the sleeping city, a warden's horn sounded — one long note, then another, then a third, a pattern she had never once heard in eleven years of smuggling.

Behind her, Pell whimpered. Above her, the sky was very dark and very still.

In her hand, the little vial pulsed once, gently, like a heartbeat. Like a summons.

Like a beginning.
