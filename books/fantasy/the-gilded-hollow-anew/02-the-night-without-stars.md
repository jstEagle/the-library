## 2. The Night Without Stars

The house on Vellamont Row had too many windows and not enough locks, which was exactly how Freya Eastwick liked her marks.

She crouched in the study of Lord Aldous Merren, third of his name and second-worst insomniac in Thornfell, with a lockpick between her teeth and the taste of candlewax in the back of her throat. Below her, the household slept the heavy, dreamless sleep of people who had paid for their dreams to be taken. She'd bottled the lord's nightmares herself, three months back — sold them to his wife's rival at a scandalous markup, then sold the wife a soothing draught of someone else's wedding day to fill the space they left. The Merrens slept like the dead because Freya had made sure of it. There was a certain poetry in robbing a house you had already hollowed.

On the desk before her lay the prize: a lacquered box lined with velvet, holding eleven vials of memory glass. Each one caught the moonlight through the tall windows and scattered it into little trembling rainbows across the ceiling, like trapped weather. Memories always looked lovelier than they were worth. A vial of a child's first snowfall could buy bread for a month. A vial of a mother's dying words could buy silence from the right constable. And a vial of whatever Lord Merren had been hoarding in this box — she'd heard whispers that it was something old, something from before the border wars — might buy her a year free of Thornfell entirely.

Freya worked fast. Her fingers knew the choreography: lift the box, swap in the decoy set she'd bought off a glassblower in Lowmarsh who never asked what he was blowing, ease the drawer shut, breathe. Ten years on the road had worn the fear down to a fine grain of sand somewhere behind her sternum, present but ignorable, like an old injury that ached only when it rained.

It was raining now, faintly, against the eastern windows.

She paused. Rain was forecast for tomorrow, not tonight. The sky through the glass was clear as a held breath — she could see the stars over the rooftops, sharp and white and countless, the way they always were above Thornfell, where the wards kept the air scoured clean. She counted without meaning to, out of habit. Seven bright ones in the Hunter's line. The pale smear of the Sisters. All there.

All there, until they weren't.

It did not happen all at once. That was the terrible part, she would think later. It happened politely, one star at a time, like candles being pinched by a hand moving down a long table. The Hunter's brightest eye went first. Then the Sisters dimmed to smudges. Then the whole eastern sky simply *ended*, a curtain drawn across the dark, and the darkness came westward toward her like water finding its level, and Freya stood in a stranger's study with stolen memories in her hands and watched every light in heaven go out.

The last thing visible was the ward-fires along the Thornfell border, far to the north — a thin gold seam along the mountains, the color of dawn that never arrived. Then that seam *shivered*.

Then it shattered.

There was no sound. That was wrong; everyone knew the wards sang if you listened hard enough, a note below hearing, the reason travelers felt lighter crossing into Thornfell and heavier leaving it. But the shatter came silent as a thought, and yet Freya felt it pass through her body like a bell struck inside her own ribs. The vials in the box rattled. One of them cracked — a hairline fracture, singing faintly — and a smell rolled out of it that stopped her heart mid-beat.

Smoke. Not hearth-smoke, not torch-smoke. Something older. Hot metal and scorched feathers and the deep green scent of a forest burning at its roots. It rose from the cracked glass and coiled around her wrist like a living thing, and for half a heartbeat Freya saw — *saw* — fire. Gold-shot crimson, vast, wheeling. Wings like storm-fronts. A heat that did not burn so much as *recognize*, that reached into her chest and pressed against something behind her breastbone as though checking whether a door still stood there.

Then the vision snapped shut and she was kneeling on Aldous Merren's carpet, gasping, her eyes streaming, the cracked vial cold in her palm.

Dragon-fire. The word arrived in her mind uninvited and absolute, the way certain truths arrive: *dragons are dead, dragon-fire is ash, the First War ended two hundred years ago.* And yet the smell clung to her skin, and the door behind her breastbone ached like a bruise.

Below her, the house woke screaming — not the household, but the house itself. Every mirror in Vellamont Row had gone black. She could hear the servants' bells tolling in panic, dogs howling across the district, the great city of Thornfell groaning awake into a darkness deeper than any it had known in living memory, because no one alive had ever seen Thornfell's true night sky. The wards had kept even the stars gentle.

Now the wards were gone, and so were the stars, and the dark that remained was not empty. It was *full*. It pressed against the windows like a tide against glass.

Freya did the only sensible thing. She took the box, left the decoys, and ran.

* * *

She made six streets before the city closed around her.

Vellamont Row had dissolved into bedlam — nobles spilling out of doors in dressing gowns, clutching vials of their own purchased memories to their chests as though the past could light the way. Somewhere a man was weeping that his son's face had gone out of his head, and Freya understood with a lurch that it wasn't grief talking. When the wards fell, everything they touched had trembled, including the delicate craftwork of bottling and keeping. Memories were leaking everywhere tonight, seeping out of cracked glass and anxious minds alike, pooling in the streets like spilled wine. The whole city reeked of other people's childhoods.

She cut south, toward the river, hood up, box under her arm. The alleys were hers; she had run them since she was nine years old and hungry enough to steal a memory from a sleeping drunk just to feel, for one borrowed hour, that someone had loved him once. She knew which walls had handholds, which gates the watch forgot, which shadows were honest and which lied.

So it was a particular insult to her professional pride when the hand closed on her arm out of a doorway she *knew* was empty.

She reacted the way ten years had trained her: elbow back, heel down, weight low, the little blade from her sleeve already sliding into her palm. The grip released her exactly when it needed to — practiced, precise — and by the time she turned, a lantern was lit and a face was looking at her from inside its circle of light.

He was older than her by a decade or more, gray coming in at his temples like frost claiming high ground. Warden's coat, but wrong — the silver braid torn off at the shoulder where the sigil should have been, the fabric faded, patched at the elbows. His eyes were the pale, washed-out gray of things seen through fog, and they were fixed on her with an intensity that made her want to step backward off the edge of the world.

"You're the vial-seller," he said. "Eastwick."

"Never heard of her." The blade stayed low and friendly.

"I've been following your route for three weeks." He said it without pride, which somehow made it worse. "Corvin Ashgrave. Formerly of the Thornfell Wardens, Third Circle." He touched the torn shoulder almost absently, the way a man touches an amputation scar. "Formerly," he repeated, as if the word needed practicing.

"Congratulations on your retirement." She edged sideways, calculating. Three streets to the river. Two if she dropped the box. "If you're here to arrest me, warden, you'll notice the entire city has decided tonight is a festival of crime. You're welcome to queue."

"The wards fell from inside."

The words landed strangely, in the flat voice of a man saying a thing for the hundredth time to make himself believe it. Freya stopped edging.

"What?"

"The wards." He glanced up, involuntarily, at the starless dark — and she saw, with a small cold shock, that his hands were shaking. A warden's hands. Hands trained to hold steady while binding spells that could crack stone. They shook like a drunkard's. "I was on the wall at Coldharbor Gate when it happened. I felt the pattern break. Wardens are taught the wards the way priests are taught their god — every knot, every anchor-point, every name in the lattice. And what broke tonight wasn't attacked, Eastwick. It was *unpicked*. From within. By someone who knew every knot, every anchor-point—" He stopped himself. Swallowed. "By a warden."

The alley seemed to lean closer. Somewhere above them, a shutter banged and banged and no one went to close it.

"That's not my problem," Freya said. "I sell the past. I don't fix futures."

"It will be your problem." He stepped toward her, and she raised the blade, and he stopped — raised both hands, palms out, the universal grammar of *not a threat*, though his eyes never lost their terrible focus. "Because in four hours, when the Wardens' Council finishes panicking and starts hunting, they are going to hunt two kinds of people. Wardens who deserted their posts tonight." His mouth twisted. "And anyone carrying memory-glass that predates the fall. Because the Council believes — I heard them believe it, before I walked out — that the wards weren't broken to destroy anything. They were broken to *find* something. Something hidden in the memories of this city, or under it, or in the mountains. Something the wards were built to keep buried."

"And you know this how?"

"Because Serevan told me." The name dropped between them like a stone down a well. High Warden Serevan. Even Freya, who kept her ear to every door in Thornfell, had only heard that name whispered, usually followed by *don't repeat that.* "He stood at Coldharbor Gate tonight and watched the wards fall and he *smiled*, Ashgrave. I've served under him eleven years. I have never seen him smile."

The lantern guttered. In its stuttering light, Corvin Ashgrave looked suddenly very tired and very frightened and very much alone, and Freya recognized the look because she shaved it off her own face every morning with a straight razor and steady hands.

"Why me?" she asked. "You've got a whole Circle of loyal hounds—"

"Had." Flat. Final. "And every one of them will be searching houses by dawn, cracking open strongboxes, confiscating glass." He nodded at the box under her arm. "Eleven vials of pre-fall memory, bought from a collector with more money than sense. If even one of them holds what they're looking for, you're not a smuggler anymore, Eastwick. You're a weapon. And weapons get aimed."

The cracked vial in her pocket — she had transferred it without deciding to, some instinct tucking it against her body like a coal — pulsed faintly warm against her hip. She thought of the wheeling fire, the wings like storm-fronts, the door behind her breastbone. She thought of the way the heat had *recognized* her, and the way she had not recognized it back, and how that asymmetry sat in her chest now like a splinter working inward.

Every instinct she owned said: walk away. Give him nothing. Disappear into the river country and resurface next spring under a new name in a town where nobody had ever heard the word *warden*.

Instead she heard herself say: "If I help you, I'm not helping *you*. I'm protecting my inventory. That's the whole arrangement. You don't touch my stock, you don't ask where I got it, and when this is done we never speak again."

Corvin Ashgrave exhaled, long and slow, like a man setting down a weight he'd carried for miles. For one moment something moved behind the fog-gray eyes — relief, maybe, or grief, or gratitude, the three of them tangled past separating.

"Done," he said.

Above them, the starless dark pressed down on Thornfell like a held breath, and far to the north, along the broken seam of the border, something in the mountains began — very softly, very far away — to glow.

Freya tightened her grip on the box of other people's pasts and started walking, and the disgraced warden fell into step beside her, and behind them the city rang with ten thousand voices asking each other the same question:

*Where did the sky go?*

She didn't answer. But the cracked vial rode warm against her hip, all the way into the dark, like something patient waiting to be remembered.
