## 3. What the Glass Remembers

Corvin Ashgrave had catalogued a thousand confiscated memories in his years with the Wardens, and he had learned early that glass was never silent.

It hummed. It shifted its weight in the hand like a sleeping bird. Each vial carried the temperature of the life inside it — grief ran cold, first love ran warm, and terror, always, ran just below the heat of blood. He had been trained to read them blindfolded, to sort contraband by touch alone in the dark of an evidence vault. That training had not saved him from disgrace. But it had never left him either, and so it was that on the second night after the stars went out, sitting cross-legged on the floor of a smuggler's abandoned waystation with Freya Eastwick asleep three feet away and a starless child curled against her side, he held her inventory in his cupped hands and listened to it sing.

There were eleven vials. He had counted them twice while she slept, then a third time because the number felt wrong — too few for a woman who made her living off other people's yesterdays, too many for someone running light through a collapsing border. She kept them wrapped in wool in a leather case stitched with false seams, and she had not let the case out of arm's reach even while unconscious. He had waited until her breathing went long and even, until Wren's small fingers uncurled from her sleeve, before he eased it free.

He told himself it was duty. The word fit poorly, like a uniform tailored for another man.

* * *

The waystation had been a warden post once. He recognized the architecture of it — the arrow-slit windows cut at warden height, the iron bracket above the cold hearth where a signal lantern used to hang, the faint scorched sigil on the lintel where someone had chiseled away the Thornfell crest when the order fell out of favor. Someone had lived here keeping watch over the pass for decades, and now dust owned the place, and Corvin sat in the ruin of his own former life holding a thief's livelihood and telling himself stories about necessity.

Outside, the mountains were blacker than mountains should be. No stars. No moon. The dark pressed against the windows like water against the hull of a drowned ship, and every hour or so the broken wards shivered somewhere far to the north — a sound less heard than felt, a deep subsonic ache in the teeth, like the mountain clearing its throat.

Freya slept badly. Even in sleep she looked like a woman mid-flight: jaw tight, one hand half-curled as if around something she intended to keep. When she'd finally surrendered to exhaustion near midnight, she had done it sitting upright against the wall, chin dropping to her chest, the way soldiers and fugitives sleep — ready to be gone again in four breaths.

Wren did not sleep at all. The child sat by the window with knees drawn up, watching the starless sky with an expression Corvin could not parse — not longing, exactly. Recognition. As if the empty dark were a room the child had once lived in and was now revisiting. Occasionally Wren's lips moved without sound, shaping words in voices that were not the child's own. Once, clearly, in a cracked old man's cadence: *"the third seal holds, the third seal holds,"* and then silence again.

Corvin did not ask. He was growing accustomed to the things he did not ask.

* * *

The first vial he opened over a scrap of mirror, angling it toward the meager light of their single candle so the memory swam up in the glass like smoke in water.

A wedding. Ordinary, golden, forgettable. A bride's laughter caught mid-note; the smell of beeswax and crushed grass. Someone had paid good coin to keep this? He set it aside. The second was a funeral — rain on black cloth, a coffin's wet grain under grieving fingertips. The third, a childhood summer, all heat-haze and river water. The fourth was stranger: a man's memory of standing in a warden induction hall, swearing oaths, and Corvin flinched at the familiarity of the vaulted ceiling, the rows of grey cloaks. One of his own kind had sold a piece of himself. Or had it taken from him. There was no way to know which, and that ambiguity sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone.

He worked quickly, quietly, wrapping each vial back in its wool with the care of a man defusing something. This was theft, technically. Examination of seized goods without writ or witness. Every rule he had ever sworn to — and been cast out for failing — said he should wake her, demand answers, take the inventory into custody.

Every rule he had ever sworn to had also, three nights ago, been revealed as theater. High Warden Serevan smiling his patient smile while the wards came down like felled trees. *From inside,* Freya had said, and Corvin had spent two days not letting himself finish the thought, because finishing it meant admitting that everything he'd given his life to had been hollowed out long ago, a beautiful shell with nothing loyal left inside it.

So: duty. He turned the word over again. It still fit poorly.

The seventh vial was different before he even touched it.

Warm. Not memory-warm, not the gentle animal heat of happiness — this was warmth like a forge seen from across a valley, warmth with weight behind it, warmth that made the glass feel faintly alive in his palm. The liquid inside did not swirl like bottled recollection. It *moved*, slow and deliberate, gold shot through with veins of white, and when he tilted it toward the candle the flame bent toward the vial. Leaned. Like a plant toward sun.

Corvin had seen dragon-fire exactly once, from a ridge eleven miles distant, the year the last of the great wyrms fell in the Ashen Reaches. He had been nineteen. The whole horizon had turned the color of an open wound, and every warden on that ridge had gone silent, and their commanding officer had wept without appearing to notice he was doing it. Dragons had been dead for sixty years since. Their fire existed now only in ledgers, in sealed evidence vaults, in the classified annexes Corvin no longer had clearance to read.

This was not a memory *of* fire. He knew the difference the way he knew the difference between a portrait and a person. A memory of flame would sit inert in the glass, a picture of heat. This was heat. This was a living coal wearing the shape of a memory, folded down and down into itself until it fit inside a thumb of glass, and it was burning still, patiently, the way coals burn at the heart of a banked fire — waiting.

His hands were shaking. He set the vial down on the floorboards between his knees and made himself breathe.

*Whose?*

He examined the casing the way he'd been taught. The stopper bore a harvest-mark — the fine triple ring of a sanctioned extraction, warden-licensed, which meant this hadn't been stolen from some noble's memory-cellar. This had been taken officially. Taken *by* the order, or by someone using its instruments. And beneath the harvest-mark, half worn away, a registry sigil: the old Thornfell border script, the script they'd stopped using twenty years ago, denoting a harvest performed on a minor.

A child's memory. Sanctioned. Sealed. Sold or stored or lost, and eventually finding its way into the inventory of a memory-thief who guarded it more fiercely than any of her others — who had, he realized now, kept this vial separate in the case, nested deeper, wrapped in a different weave of wool. Not merchandise. Something else.

He should have stopped there. He wants, later, in the long accounting he will keep of this night, to believe he simply didn't know how to read what came next. But that is a kindness he will refuse himself. He knew how to read it. He had read a hundred harvest-registries in the archive at Greywatch, and his eyes went to the notation line the way a soldier's eyes go to a wound.

*Subject: female child, approx. age six. Region: Eastwick holdings, Thornfell lowlands.*

Eastwick.

The word landed in him like a dropped lantern.

He looked up at her — at the thief asleep against the wall with her chin on her chest and her fists half closed, this sharp-edged, guarded, maddening woman who lied the way other people breathed and trusted no one further than a knife's throw — and he saw, suddenly and horribly, a six-year-old girl in a lowland farmhouse, sitting in a warden's chair while sanctioned hands lifted six years of her life out of her skull and sealed them in glass. He saw the shape of what remained afterward: a child with a hollow where her childhood should have been, learning to smile around the absence, learning to call the absence *normal*. Learning, perhaps, to do to others what had been done to her — because it was the only trade whose mechanics she understood from the inside.

And he saw why the vial burned. Dragon-fire did not survive outside a living vessel. Every text agreed on that; it was the first fact of the First War, the reason the war had ended at all — the fire died with its keepers, and the wardens' great victory had been simply outlasting the last of them. Unless a keeper's line persisted. Unless the fire had been hidden the only place it could survive: inside a living mind, folded into a child's memory, where it would grow as she grew, banked and secret and alive.

She wasn't carrying stolen goods.

She *was* the goods. She had been walking around for twenty years with the last dragon-fire sleeping behind her eyes, and someone had written it down, sealed it, and lost track of it — and Serevan had torn the sky open to find it.

* * *

Corvin sat motionless for a long time. The candle guttered. Outside, the wards groaned again, that deep tectonic complaint, and Wren turned from the window and looked at him — directly, knowingly, with those eyes that saw extinguished stars — and the child's gaze dropped to the vial between his knees, then rose to his face, and Wren shook their head slowly. Once. *No.* In a voice like Freya's own, soft and precise, the child said: *"Don't tell her."*

Then Wren turned back to the window, and the borrowed voice was gone, and the child was only a child again.

Corvin rewrapped the vial. His hands were steady now, which felt like a betrayal of its own. He nested it back in the case exactly as he'd found it, deep in the false seam, beneath the ordinary memories of weddings and funerals and rivers in summertime. He closed the leather. He slid it back to within reach of her sleeping hand, and watched, feeling like a grave-robber of a wholly new kind, until her fingers found it in her sleep and curled around it.

He had spent his career confiscating secrets. Now he was keeping one — from the person to whom it mattered most, for reasons he could barely justify even to himself. Because if he told her, she would run. She would run tonight, alone, straight into the dark the wardens had made, and Serevan would take the fire out of her the way it had been taken once before, and this time there would be no losing track of the paperwork. Because if he told her, he would have to tell her what he was — what the order was — and he was not yet certain she would not put a knife in him for it, and less certain he would deserve anything else.

Because, some quieter voice added, she deserved one night more of being only a thief. Of not knowing she was a treasure, a weapon, a target, the last ember of a dead age. Everyone should get to be small sometimes. He had never been allowed to be small in his entire life, and look what it had made of him.

*Protective,* he told himself. The word fit worst of all.

Freya stirred. Her eyes opened halfway, flat and assessing even in the depths of exhaustion, and fixed on him, on the case, on the distance between them.

"Dreaming of inventory, warden?" she murmured.

"Counting stars," he said. "There aren't any."

Something flickered across her face — amusement, or its careful counterfeit — and she drifted under again. Wren, at the window, did not turn around, but Corvin had the distinct impression of being watched anyway, judged and found forgivable, provisionally.

He fed the candle another inch of wick and settled in to keep watch, and it was near dawn when the sound came — faint, far down the pass, unmistakable to a man who had marched to it for fifteen years: the rhythmic ring of hobnails on stone. Many boots. Moving in step.

Moving toward the waystation.
