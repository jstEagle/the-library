## 4. The Starless Child

The pass above Thornfell had a name once, before the wards fell and the maps went dark. The old-timers called it the Shepherd's Throat, because it narrowed to a strangle of rock where the wind came howling down from the peaks like something being chased. Freya had crossed it forty times with her satchel of bottled lives strapped tight against her spine, and forty times she had thought nothing of the dark.

Now the dark was different. Now the dark was total.

She walked ahead, lantern hooded to a knife-thin slit of light, listening for the thing that had followed them out of Thornfell: the soft iron rhythm of warden boots on stone. Corvin moved behind her with a hunter's economy, all long limbs and held breath, and neither of them spoke. Speech carried in the Throat. Speech got you found.

They had been running since dawn — or what passed for dawn now, a gray bruise along the horizon where the sun rose without any stars to hand it off to. Twice they'd heard the hunters' signal-horns, low and mournful, sweeping the valley below like hands searching through drawers. The wardens weren't shouting anymore. That was worse. Shouting meant they were behind. Silence meant they were everywhere.

Freya paused at a bend where the cliff leaned close enough to touch both walls, and pressed her palm flat against the stone. Cold seeped up through her glove, cold as well-water, cold as the vials in her pack. She counted her own heartbeat the way she'd taught herself years ago, in another life: one beat per step, three steps per breath, breath shallow enough that even the night couldn't taste it.

*You're good at this,* she told herself. *You've been invisible your whole life. This is just more of the same.*

But invisibility had always been a trick she performed, not a truth she lived. And tricks failed when your hands started shaking.

Corvin touched her shoulder — two fingers, light as falling ash. She felt him tense against her back, his attention snagging on something ahead, and she turned her head just enough to catch it too.

A sound. Small. Wrong.

Not boots. Not horns. A sound like a bird trying to remember how its song went.

* * *

They found her half a mile on, where the pass opened into a bowl of scree and dead pine, huddled in the lee of a fallen tree as though the tree might still be persuaded to protect things.

A child. A girl, maybe seven years old, maybe younger — it was hard to tell, because she was so still. She sat with her knees drawn up and her arms wrapped around them, wearing a coat far too big for her, wool gone gray with road dust, and she was staring straight up at the sky.

Straight up at nothing.

"Stars," the girl said, in a voice that wasn't hers.

Freya's skin crawled. It wasn't the word itself; plenty of people had been muttering about the stars for two days now, praying at them, cursing them. It was the voice. It was an old man's voice — cracked, wheezing, the voice of someone who'd spent decades breathing coal smoke — coming out of a child's small mouth with perfect, terrible ease.

"Stars," the girl said again, softer. "I can see them."

"There are no stars," Corvin said quietly. His hand rested on the hilt of his blade without drawing it. "Child. Can you hear me?"

The girl's head turned. Her eyes found Freya first — not Corvin, not the drawn steel, but Freya, standing in her sliver of hooded lantern-light — and something in the child's face cracked open like river ice in spring.

"You kept it," she said.

Still the old man's voice. Still that awful borrowed gravel. But the words were aimed at Freya alone, delivered with the certainty of someone reading from a page only she could see.

"Kept what?" Freya said. Her voice came out flatter than she intended.

The girl didn't answer. She simply looked at her, and looked, and looked — the way Freya had seen desperate nobles look at their vials, hungry and grieving at once — until Freya had to turn away first.

"She's frozen," Corvin murmured. He crouched, slow, keeping his movements wide and obvious the way you would with a spooked animal. "Look at her lips. She's half-dead of cold." He shrugged off his cloak and held it out. "We can't leave her here. The hunters will find her, and if she's seen us—"

"If she's seen us, we're already seen." Freya swept the darkness behind them with her eyes. The pass lay empty. The silence lay thick as snowfall. "Wardens don't leave witnesses, Corvin. You know that better than anyone."

"I know." His jaw worked. "That's why I'm asking instead of deciding."

It was, she realized, the first time anyone had asked her anything in longer than she could comfortably count. Freya Eastwick decided things. Freya Eastwick took memories and left no forwarding address. Nobody consulted her about children found in passes speaking in dead men's voices.

The girl shivered then — one full-body shudder that seemed to break some spell — and her teeth began to chatter, and the borrowed voice dissolved into a small raw sound that needed no translation at all.

"Oh, hells," Freya said, and took the cloak from Corvin's hands herself.

* * *

They made camp in a hollow beneath an overhang, a fire no larger than two cupped palms, fed with dry pine-knots that smoked less than they crackled. The girl sat wrapped in the cloak like a moth in a cocoon, holding her hands toward the flame, and did not speak at all.

"She hasn't said a word since," Corvin said. They'd retreated a few paces, close enough to watch her, far enough for whispers. "Since the voices."

"Voices. Plural."

"The second time, when she drank the water you gave her — you were checking the fire, you missed it. She said 'thank you' in a woman's voice. Young. Then she said—" he hesitated, and Freya watched the reluctance move across his face like weather, "—she said, 'the hollow is waking.' In yet another voice. Deeper. Like something that lives underground."

Freya's hand drifted, without her permission, to the strap of her satchel. Somewhere in there, among the stolen summers and first kisses and dying fathers she carried for other people's comfort, sat the vial she'd glimpsed two nights ago — the one full of impossible golden fire. *Dragon-fire,* her traitor mind whispered, and she shut the thought away.

"The Hollow," she said instead. "That's miners' talk. There are old workings under these mountains. My grandmother used to say the mountain was hollow as a drum and one day it'd beat."

"And you believe in grandmothers' tales now?"

"I believe a child just quoted one in three different voices. Belief is adjusting." She rubbed her arms against the cold. "Where did she come from? There's nothing up here. No villages, no shrines, nothing since the fire-keepers—"

She stopped. The sentence ended like a stair missing its last step.

"Since the fire-keepers," Corvin finished quietly.

Neither of them said anything else about that. Some silences are doors you decide, together, not to open.

They returned to the fire. The girl watched them come, and Freya noticed for the first time the details the dark had hidden: a burn scar on the inside of her wrist, old and silver, shaped almost exactly like a starburst. A thin cord around her neck holding a pendant of black glass. And her eyes — her eyes were wrong, or rather right, in a way that made Freya's chest ache. They reflected the firelight the way eyes should, but underneath the reflection there was a faint luminescence, like the memory of light rather than light itself.

Like eyes that had spent too long looking at stars.

"What's your name?" Freya asked. She crouched at the edge of the cloak-wrapped bundle, close enough to offer a strip of dried apple. "Mine's Freya. The gloomy one is Corvin."

Something flickered at the corner of the girl's mouth. Almost, nearly, a smile.

She took the apple but didn't eat it. She held it in both hands and stared into the fire, and when she finally spoke, it was in a new voice — a young woman's voice, warm and clear and heartbreakingly familiar in a way Freya couldn't place, like a song heard once in childhood:

"Wren."

Then, softer, in the same woman's voice, with the same warmth, aimed at Freya like an arrow loosed years ago and only now landing:

"You always gave away the bigger half."

The world tilted.

Freya sat down hard on the cold stone. It wasn't dizziness exactly; it was recognition arriving without any memory attached, a key fitting a lock whose door had been bricked over. For one blinding instant she was five years old, or six, and someone was breaking bread with her by a hearth that burned gold instead of orange, and someone was laughing and saying *you'll give away everything one day, little ember, mark me*—

Gone. Nothing. Just the fire and the pass and her own ragged breathing.

"Freya?" Corvin's hand hovered near her shoulder, not quite touching. He'd learned that much about her in two days.

"I'm fine." Her voice shook. She hated it. "Did you hear—"

"I heard."

"That voice. I know that voice." She pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "I don't know where from. That's the hell of it, Corvin. I know it the way you know your own name — and I can't tell you a single thing about her. Not her face. Not her name. Just—" her hand made a helpless fist, "—just that she was kind. Just that she loved me. How do I know that? How does a person know that with no reason to?"

Corvin was silent for a long moment. Firelight moved across his scarred face, and in it she saw the war she was beginning to recognize: duty pulling one way, something newer and more dangerous pulling the other.

"There are arts," he said carefully, "that take memories whole. Wardens use them. Worse people use them." He glanced at Wren. "And there are children, in the old stories, who carry what's taken. Vessels. The order called them remembrancers, before the order decided the stories were embarrassing." He paused. "I always assumed the stories were embellished."

"You're saying she's carrying someone's memories."

"I'm saying she may be carrying many someones'. And that she looked at you in the pass like she'd been looking for you a very long time."

Across the fire, Wren had finally eaten the apple. She was watching them over her knees, and in the flicker of the flames her strange eyes caught the light and held it, twin coals banked in a small pale face.

Then, deliberately, she raised one hand and pointed — not at Freya, but at Freya's satchel, at the shape of the vials beneath the oilcloth.

In the young woman's warm, lost, beloved voice, she said:

"It's time to give it back."

* * *

They broke camp before the false dawn, moving fast and quiet, the girl between them with her small hand tucked into Freya's — because she had reached for it, simply reached, the way children do, and Freya had discovered that her own hand, traitorous thing, had reached back.

Freya told herself it was practicality. A mute child in a hunted pass needed guiding. That was all.

She told herself that, and then Wren stumbled on the scree, and Freya caught her without thinking, scooping her up onto her hip as naturally as breathing, and the girl wrapped her arms around Freya's neck and sighed against her shoulder — and the sigh came out in a chorus, faintly, several voices layered like rings in a tree, and every one of them sounded relieved.

Freya stood very still in the starless dark, holding a stranger's weight, feeling her own guarded heart develop hairline fractures like glass under heat.

*This is how it happens,* she thought wildly. *This is how they get you. Not with blades. With small hands.*

Behind them, far down the pass, a horn sounded. Then a second, closer. Then — cutting through both like a knife through silk — a third horn, deeper, older, blown from somewhere beneath the mountains themselves.

Corvin went rigid. "That's no warden call."

"No," Freya said. She tightened her hold on the child and started to run. "It's answering them."

And Wren, pressed against her shoulder, spoke in the deep underground voice one final time, clear as a bell over the thunder of their flight:

"The hollow remembers its keeper. Come home, little ember. Before they make you choose."
