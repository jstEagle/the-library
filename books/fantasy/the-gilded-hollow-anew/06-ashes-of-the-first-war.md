## 6. Ashes of the First War

The ruins did not look like a battlefield. That was the first thing Freya noticed, and the thing that unsettled her most.

She had imagined the First War as it lived in every stolen memory she'd ever handled — fire on the horizon, dragons wheeling like storm-birds, cities burning in long golden sheets. But what remained was quieter than that. Softer. The valley they descended into at dusk was carpeted in ash-grey grass that had never quite learned to grow again, and the bones of the old city rose from it like ribs: arches without walls, staircases climbing to nothing, doorways that opened onto open sky. Everything was the color of old ivory, bleached by two hundred years of starlight — except there had been no starlight for six nights now, and still the ruins held their pale glow, faintly luminous, as if the stone itself remembered burning.

"Fire-keeper's city," Corvin said behind her. His voice had gone strange since the shrine, since he'd turned his back on his oaths with Serevan's blade an arm's length from Wren's throat. He spoke now like a man reading his own eulogy. "They built everything out of hearthstone. It holds warmth for centuries. My grandmother used to say you could put your palm against a wall here and feel the last dragon pass by."

Freya didn't answer. She was watching Wren.

The child walked ahead of them through the ruined street as though walking home. Small boots stepping over fallen lintels, small hands trailing along the glowing stone, and everywhere Wren went, the walls brightened — just slightly, just at the edges, like coals breathed upon. The child had not spoken since the shrine. Had not spoken in any borrowed voice at all, which frightened Freya more than screaming would have. A child who spoke only in other people's voices, gone silent, was a house with all its lamps blown out.

They sheltered in what had clearly been a great hall once — its roof half-collapsed into a lacework of shadow, its floor scattered with shards of colored glass that might have been windows or might have been something older. Corvin built no fire; there was nothing dry enough to burn, and besides, Freya caught him glancing at the sky between the broken rafters every few minutes, checking for warden lanterns, for wings, for anything. They ate cold travel bread and harder cheese. Wren curled up against Freya's side without asking, the way children do when they have decided something about you that you have not yet decided about yourself, and was asleep within minutes, breathing in that deep, rattling, exhausted rhythm of someone who has run too far for too long.

Freya should have slept too. She was bone-tired, hollowed out, her hands still shaking occasionally from the shrine — from the moment Serevan had looked at her and said *the vial-seller*, said it like a name he'd been waiting years to say.

Instead she found herself sorting through her satchel by touch alone.

Her inventory was ruined. She'd known it since the shrine, but knowing and seeing were different things. Vial after vial came out dark, their silver stoppers tarnished black, the memories inside gone to smoke — a duchess's wedding day, a soldier's last morning, a lullaby some mother had sold to buy medicine. All of it dead glass now. Whatever Serevan's people had done to shatter the wards had scorched the memory-stuff itself, and Freya sat in the ruins of the First War holding the ruins of her own trade, and felt — nothing. That was the terrible part. Not grief. Just a vast, echoing nothing, like standing in one of these doorways that opened onto sky.

Except one vial.

It came out of the inner pocket warm. Not scorch-warm — *alive* warm, blood-warm, the way a bird is warm when you cup it too tightly. The glass caught what little light the hearthstone gave off and threw it back gold, and inside, instead of the grey drift of ordinary memory, something moved. Something that moved like fire moves when it is thinking.

Freya knew this vial. Of course she did. It was the first one she'd ever owned, the one that had been pressed into her hands at eight years old by a woman with kind eyes who told her, *this is yours, keep it close, never sell it, never let anyone see it.* She had carried it through fifteen years of thieving and smuggling and sleeping in ditches, and she had never once looked into it, because looking into your own bottled memory was supposed to be death for the mind — everyone knew that, every thief learned it before they learned to pick a lock.

But everyone also knew dragons were dead, and the stars had gone out, and the wards had fallen from the inside. Everyone's knowledge was ash this week.

"You're going to open it."

Corvin's voice, low, from across the hall. He hadn't been asleep either. In the dimness his face was all angles and regret.

"I'm going to open it," Freya agreed.

"It could unmake you. A memory that size, going back into a mind that—"

"I know what it could do." She turned the vial in her fingers. "I've spent my whole life selling people their own pasts back. Do you know how many of them wept? Not from joy. From *recognition*. Like they'd been carrying a weight so long they'd forgotten it was a weight until someone took it off." Her thumb traced the stopper. "I want to know what I've been carrying."

* * *

She broke the seal with her thumbnail, and the world went gold.

Not light — *heat*. Memory poured into her the way fire pours into a room when a door is thrown open onto a furnace, and Freya was eight years old again, and she was not in Thornfell, and she had never been in Thornfell—

—she was on a mountainside at dawn, and the mountain was breathing.

That was the only way to describe it. The whole slope rose and fell, slow, vast, and she understood with a child's simple certainty that the mountain was her father — no, not her father, *her father's charge* — because her father was a fire-keeper, and fire-keepers did not keep hearths. Fire-keepers kept the last dragon, coiled in the deep of the Hollow beneath the mountain, its flame banked down to an ember no bigger than a heart, and every keeper's family for nine generations had fed that ember with their own remembered joy, because dragon-fire burned brightest on happiness and would gutter on anything less.

She saw her mother's kitchen carved into living rock. Saw herself learning the feeding-song, a tuneless hum that made the ember pulse. Saw the wardens come — not Serevan, but his predecessors, their faces hidden under hoods, their voices gentle and terrible — telling her father the war was truly over now, the last dragon must be hidden beyond even hiding, and the safest place in all the world to keep a flame was inside a memory, because no one suspects a memory, no one hunts a memory, a memory can be carried in a child's pocket through any border in creation.

And then she saw the taking. She was eight. There was a chair, and straps, and a hooded warden murmuring *it won't hurt, it won't hurt,* and it did hurt, it hurt like being peeled, and her parents stood at the door and did not stop it because they had sworn the oath, because the ember had to be hidden, because *someone's* childhood was a small price for the world's last fire—

—and the worst of it, the part that made her scream aloud in the ruin, eight years old and twenty-three years old at once, was that after, they smiled at her. Her mother knelt and brushed her hair and said, *you're so brave, my darling, you won't remember a bit of it,* and Freya-the-child had smiled back at a woman whose face she would forget by morning, and the woman knew it, and smiled anyway, and that smile—

The vial fell from her hand. She was on her knees on the hearthstone floor, and Corvin was holding her shoulders, saying her name, and somewhere above them Wren was awake and standing very straight in the broken doorway with the starless sky behind her like a hole cut out of the world.

"They took it," Freya heard herself say. Her voice sounded scraped. "My whole — they didn't take *a* memory, they took the *shape* of me. Everything before that chair. My mother's face. The song. I've been walking around for fifteen years with a hole where I should be, and I thought the hole was *me*. I thought I was just — built wrong. Hollow." She laughed, and it came out ugly. "I became a memory-thief because I had nothing of my own to steal. Do you understand? Every vial I ever bottled, I was robbing other people's houses because mine had already been robbed."

"I understand," Corvin said quietly.

"No, you don't, because yours is all still *there*—" She shoved his hands off and stood, and the rage came up through her like the fire in the vial, like the ember under the mountain, huge and old and suddenly hers. "Fifteen years. Fifteen years I've been a ghost wearing my own skin, and the people who did it tucked me into bed afterward and called me *brave*. And Serevan wants it back. Wants the fire. Wants to put it in a weapon and start the war again, and he'll peel another eight-year-old if he has to, and another, because that's what they do, that's what they've *always* done—"

Wren crossed the hall. Small bare feet on the glass shards, not flinching. And the child took Freya's face in both hands — the same gesture, Freya realized with a jolt, the same gesture her mother had used in the memory, and how did Wren know that, how could Wren possibly know that —

And Wren opened her mouth, and spoke. Not in a borrowed voice. In her own — a voice like a struck bell, high and clear and utterly new:

"*Feed the ember with remembered joy.*"

The feeding-song. Freya's song. The one taken from her before she could remember owning it.

"How," Freya whispered.

Wren's eyes were enormous and reflected gold, though there was no gold anywhere in the hall. When she spoke again it was barely sound at all.

"The flame remembers its keepers. It's been calling you your whole life, Freya Eastwick. That's why the vial stayed warm. That's why you could never sell it." The child's small thumbs wiped the tears off Freya's cheeks, matter-of-fact, the way you'd wipe dust from a window. "It wasn't hiding from you. It was waiting."

Freya looked down at the vial lying in the ash-grey grass-spill of glass, glowing softly, patiently, like a heart in an open chest. Fifteen years she'd carried it as a curiosity, a talisman, a thing she couldn't name. It had never been merchandise. It had never even been hers, exactly. It was a *charge*. She was the ninth generation of a line of keepers, and the keeping had simply been waiting for her to grow into it.

The rage didn't leave her. That surprised her — she'd expected grief to wash it away, the way stories said. Instead the grief sank down through the anger and hardened it, tempered it, the way you temper steel in cold water. She picked up the vial and closed her hand around its warmth, and when she spoke, her voice was steady in a way it had never been.

"Serevan thinks the fire is a treasure to be seized." She looked at Corvin, and then at Wren. "He's wrong. It's a *duty*. And duties don't get surrendered. They get kept."

Corvin studied her for a long moment. Then, slowly, he reached into his coat and drew out the thing he'd taken from the shrine — a warden's map, inked on skin-pale vellum, showing the passes below the mountain. Showing the way down.

"To keep it," he said, "we have to reach it first. And Serevan's already ahead of us. There's a gate into the Hollow three days' march from here — and Freya, the map shows something else." His finger stopped on a mark near the mountain's root, a symbol she somehow, impossibly, recognized from the memory: the keeper's sigil, a flame inside a circle. Beneath it, fresh ink, recent handwriting.

Someone had written: *the ember gutters.*

Wren made a small sound, and for the first time since Freya had known her, the child looked afraid.

"We have three days," Corvin said. "Maybe fewer."
