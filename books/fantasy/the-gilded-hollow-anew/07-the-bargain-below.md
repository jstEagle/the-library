## 7. The Bargain Below

The Hollow did not open so much as admit them.

Corvin felt it in his teeth first — a low, grinding hum that lived in the stone itself, older than the wards he had sworn to, older than the order that had cast him out. The stairway wound down through the mountain's root like a throat, and the walls sweated a faint amber light, as if the rock remembered fire the way scar tissue remembers the blade.

Freya went first. She always did now, though she pretended it was only because she knew how to read a smuggler's passage — the false floors, the pressure-plates disguised as worn flagstones, the deadfalls rigged by hands two centuries gone. But Corvin had watched her long enough to know the truth beneath the competence: she walked ahead so no one could walk behind her.

Wren walked between them, small hand tucked into Corvin's, cold as riverstone and just as unyielding. The child had not spoken since the shrine — not in the borrowed voice of a dead warden, not in the cracked whisper of Freya's mother that had made them both flinch. Wren simply looked up at the dark where stars should have been, visible even here, even through miles of mountain, and held on tighter.

*They can still see them,* Corvin thought. *The extinguished ones. Like scars on the inside of their eyelids.*

The hum deepened. The amber light thickened until it moved like honey along the walls, and Corvin realized with a lurch that it wasn't light at all. It was warmth made visible. The last dragon-fire, breathing beneath the world, and everything within a mile of it leaning toward that breath like flowers toward a sun.

"Stop," Freya said suddenly, arm out.

She crouched over a seam in the floor where the flagstones met in a pattern too regular to be natural. Her fingers hovered above it without touching. "Warden work," she murmured. "New. Weeks old, maybe less." She glanced back at him, and in the amber glow her eyes were the color of struck flint. "They knew we'd come this way."

"They didn't know *we'd* come this way," Corvin said quietly. "They knew *I* would. Serevan wrote the routes into my training when I was nineteen. He knows I think in these passages the way you think in lock-picks."

Something flickered across her face — not quite forgiveness, not yet, but the shape of its possibility. Since the shrine, since he had put himself between her and six drawn ward-blades and said the words that ended whatever remained of his career, she had stopped looking at him like a jailer. She hadn't started looking at him like anything else either. He found he was starving for whatever came next.

"Wren," Freya said softly. "Can you tell if it's trapped?"

The child knelt beside her with grave ceremony, laid one palm flat against the stone, and closed their eyes. For three heartbeats nothing. Then Wren's mouth opened and a voice came out that was not Wren's — an old man's voice, dry as ash, speaking words that seemed to rise from the stone rather than the child:

*"Not trapped. Invited."*

Freya snatched her hand back. Corvin's sword was half-drawn before he caught himself.

"It's true, though," he said slowly. "Look at the pattern. That's not a trigger-seal. That's a threshold-greeting. Old rite. You use it when you want someone to walk through knowing they're walking into your parlor." His stomach turned. "*He* wants us to come. All of it — the collapsed wards, the hunters, the shrine — it was a funnel. We were never being chased away from the Hollow. We were being herded toward it."

Freya stood. She looked down the stairway into the swelling gold light for a long moment, and Corvin watched her do the arithmetic every smuggler does — risk against reward, exit against prize — and watched something else override it. Grief, maybe. Or fury wearing grief's face.

"Then let's not keep him waiting," she said.

* * *

The Hollow's heart opened around them like the inside of a bell.

The chamber was vast beyond sense — a cathedral of black glass where the mountain had melted and refrozen in some ancient agony, and at its center, suspended in a lattice of iron and prayer-chains, burned the flame.

It was small. That was the horror of it. After all of it — the stolen vials, the vanished stars, the war that had scorched the sky — the last dragon-fire was no larger than a candleflame, curled in on itself like a sleeping animal, pulsing slow and patient as a heartbeat. And from it, threads of gold ran outward through the black glass in every direction, veins of warmth feeding the whole mountain, feeding perhaps the whole world, and above the world the stars that had gone dark the night the wards fell.

Wren made a sound Corvin had never heard from the child before — a small, broken note, half sob, half song — and pulled free of his hand and ran toward the flame.

"No—" Freya lunged, caught the child around the middle, swung them up against her hip. Wren clung to her, face buried in her neck, shoulders shaking, and pointed one small finger at the fire and then at the ceiling, at the invisible stars, at the connection none of them needed explained anymore.

"She's bound to it," Corvin said. The knowledge settled into him like cold water. "When the flame dies—"

"I know what happens when it dies." Freya's voice was flat. She was staring at the fire with an expression Corvin couldn't read, and he understood a moment later why: she was looking at it the way you look at a mirror.

Because the flame was hers. Had been hers, once, before men in gray coats had pried it out of a fire-keeper's daughter and bottled the memory of it in glass, leaving a child who grew into a woman with a hollow where her childhood should have been. He had known since the night he examined her inventory. He had said nothing. He was still saying nothing, and the silence had grown teeth.

"You always were slow on stairs, Ashgrave."

The voice rolled down from a gallery of black glass overhead, and Corvin's hand found his hilt out of pure reflex, though he already knew.

High Warden Serevan descended alone. No honor guard. No drawn blades. Just the old man himself, silver-braided, immaculate despite weeks in the field, his boots silent on the glass steps, his hands folded behind his back like a scholar approaching a particularly fine manuscript. Behind him, at the gallery's rail, wardens appeared one by one — a dozen, two dozen — crossbows lowered, almost casual. An audience, not an ambush. The distinction was the message.

"Serevan." Corvin stepped forward, putting himself between the High Warden and the others. Old habit. It tasted like ash now. "You collapsed the wards. You turned the sky off like a lamp. Do you have any idea what you've—"

"I have an idea of what I've *saved*," Serevan said mildly. "Which is more than I can say for you, traitor." He said the word without heat, the way he'd say *apprentice*. "But we needn't relitigate your failures. I'm here to conclude business."

His gaze slid past Corvin to Freya, and Corvin saw the trap close with terrible gentleness.

"Freya Eastwick," Serevan said. "Memory-thief. Vial-seller. Orphan of no record, which has always interested me — children don't simply appear, they're *made* to disappear." He began to walk, slow, circling, and the amber light gilded him like a saint in an old painting. "You've spent your whole life selling other people's pasts. Bottled mothers. Bottled first loves. Bottled homes. And never once asked yourself why you couldn't remember your own."

"Don't," Corvin said.

Serevan ignored him. From his coat he drew a case — plain gray steel, warden-issue — and opened it. Inside, nested in velvet, lay vials. A dozen of them, each glowing faintly, each labeled in the precise hand of the Order's memory-clerks.

Freya went very still.

"EASTWICK, F. — EXTRACTION, AGE FOUR THROUGH ELEVEN," Serevan read aloud, lifting the first vial to the light. "SEVEN YEARS OF CHILDHOOD. INTACT. UNDEGRADED." Another vial. "EASTWICK, F. — MOTHER, FIRE-KEEPER'S RITE, FULL SEQUENCE." Another. "EASTWICK, F. — THE NIGHT OF THE HARVEST." He smiled, and it was the kindest smile Corvin had ever seen on him, which made it the worst. "Every piece of you that was taken. Every year they carved out and left you hollow. We kept them safe all this time, girl. Waiting for the day you'd be worth the return."

The chamber was silent except for the flame's slow pulse and Wren's ragged breathing against Freya's neck.

"What do you want," Freya said. Not a question. She already knew.

"The fire," Serevan said simply. "Given freely, into warden keeping. In exchange — your memories, restored by our clerks, clean and complete. Your childhood. Your mother's face. Your name's whole history." He paused, and Corvin watched him aim the second blade with surgical care. "And for the disgraced warden standing so bravely in front of you — reinstatement. Full rank. Your oaths restored. Your name cleared. Everything you lost at the tribunal, returned."

Corvin's breath stopped.

He hadn't let himself want it. Seven years he hadn't let himself want it — the gray coat back on his shoulders, the Order's gates opening, the word *traitor* scoured from every ledger. And here it was, offered in a single sentence, cheap as bread, if only he stood aside and let them take the last dragon-fire and the starless child and the woman who—

Who what? Who tolerated him? Who walked ahead of him? Who had, three nights ago, slept sitting up against a cave wall with Wren in her lap and her stolen vials cradled like an infant, guarding other people's pasts because she had none of her own?

"Freya," Corvin said hoarsely. "Don't listen to—"

"Quiet, Ashgrave." But there was no command in it. There was something worse. There was longing.

She set Wren down. The child grabbed at her sleeve and she gently, gently unwound the small fingers, one by one, and Corvin's chest seized because he had seen that gesture before — in the tribunal hall, in his own hands, the day they took his oath.

She walked toward the case of vials.

Each step rang on the black glass. Serevan held the case out like a priest offering communion. Freya lifted the first vial — EASTWICK, F., AGE FOUR — and held it up to the firelight, and Corvin saw the memory swirl inside it, gold and warm, a little girl's laughter made liquid, and he saw Freya's face break open with a naked hunger that terrified him more than any blade in the chamber.

"I could have it back," she whispered. "All of it. My mother. My name. Why I'm—" Her voice cracked. "Do you know what it's like, selling souls all your life and not owning one?"

"I imagine," Serevan said softly, "it's like being a locked door with nothing behind it. Give us the fire, Freya Eastwick. Be whole again."

Her thumb moved over the vial's stopper.

Wren made a sound. Small. Not a word — Wren didn't waste words — but a sound like a struck bell going quiet, and Freya's hand stilled.

She looked at the child. At the flame. At the dark ceiling where the dead stars hung invisible, and Corvin watched the war move through her face like weather — wanting and knowing, hunger and grief, the self she'd lost and the self she'd become — and he understood, with sickening clarity, that Serevan had built this bargain perfectly, that it was seductive precisely because it was fair, because every word of it was true.

Freya closed her eyes.

"Corvin," she said quietly. "If I give it to them — the fire — will the stars come back?"

"No," he said. "They'll bottle it. Bury it deeper. They'll keep the sky dark forever rather than spend their weapon lighting it."

"And Wren?"

He didn't answer. He didn't have to.

Freya opened her eyes, and Corvin saw the moment the wanting lost — saw it die in her face not easily but *expensively*, like a city paying ransom — and she set the vial back into the velvet with a tenderness that made Serevan's smile falter for the first time.

"No," she said.

"Think carefully—"

"I've thought about nothing else my whole life," Freya said, and her voice rose, and the flame behind her pulsed brighter as if it heard. "You took seven years from me and called it protection. You kept them in a box and called it safekeeping. And now you stand there offering me back my own soul like it's *yours to give*." She stepped forward, and the amber light bent toward her, and Corvin saw the veins of gold in the black glass begin to run, to quicken, to climb the walls like dawn. "You don't get to sell me myself, Serevan. That trade's been mine all along."

The High Warden's kindness fell away like a mask slipping its cord. He raised one hand.

"Then we take it," he said, "and take the memories anyway. Wardens — *fire her*."

Crossbows came up along the gallery. The flame roared once, soundless, like a held breath.

And in the space between heartbeats, Wren stepped in front of Freya, spread both small arms wide, and spoke — in a voice like the sky itself, vast and ancient and terribly sad:

*"Touch her, and I put them out for good."*
