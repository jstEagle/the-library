## 8. Guttering Light

The flame was dying.

Freya had known it the way you know a fever coming on — not by any single sign, but by the slow wrongness of everything. The vial she carried against her breastbone, wrapped in three layers of wool and one of Corvin's old warden cloak, no longer hummed. For days it had sung to her, a thread of heat beneath her ribs, a whisper of wings and burning gold. Now it only ticked, faint and irregular, like a heart deciding whether to bother.

She sat at the mouth of the ruined watchtower with the vial cradled in her palms, watching the starless dark press down on Thornfell like a lid. Below, in the tower's gutted heart, Wren slept — or something like sleep. The child lay curled on a bedroll of moth-eaten banners, breathing shallow and quick, and even in the dark Freya could see it: the faint light under Wren's skin, guttering.

It had been there since the shrine. Since Serevan's wardens had cornered them among the bones of the first war and Corvin had burned his oaths to ash to buy them an hour's running. A thread of dragon-fire lived in the child — had always lived there, Wren's borrowed voices finally admitted, spoken through the mouths of dead fire-keepers. The child was a wick. And the wick was going out because the candle was.

"Tell me what to do," Freya murmured to the vial. It didn't answer. It never had, except once — that first night on the road, when it had cracked itself open in her hands and shown her a girl standing in a doorway of flame, and Freya had known, with a certainty that still terrified her, that the girl was herself.

Behind her, boots on stone. She didn't startle anymore; her body had learned Corvin's tread the way it had once learned patrol routes and client schedules.

"You should sleep," he said. He lowered himself beside her, careful of his bruised ribs, and looked out at the blank sky as if he could shame it into lighting. "I'll take the watch."

"She's worse."

"I know." His jaw worked. In the grey half-light from the ember-lamp below, his face looked carved and tired, a man who had spent his whole life holding a wall up with his shoulders. "The fire-keepers' records said the flame could last centuries in glass. They didn't say anything about—"

"About what happens when it starts starving." Freya turned the vial over. Inside, the dragon-fire had shrunk to a bead no larger than a currant, sullen orange shot through with veins of black. When she'd first stolen it back from her own past, it had filled the glass like a captive sunrise. "Glass doesn't feed it. That's the point of glass. It keeps things *safe*." She laughed, and the laugh came out broken. "Safe and slowly suffocating. I've been carrying a dying thing inside a tomb for weeks and calling it a rescue."

Corvin said nothing for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was low and rough. "You didn't know."

"I knew enough. I'm a memory-thief, Corvin. I know what bottling does to a thing. I've watched nobles open vials of their mothers' voices and weep because the voice came out *flat*, wrong, like a portrait instead of a person. I sold them those portraits. I told myself the memories were better off kept than lost." Her thumb traced the wax seal. "And all that time I was doing exactly that to the last living fire in the world. To my own—"

She stopped. The word wouldn't come. *Childhood* was too small. *Self* was too large.

* * *

Wren woke near dawn, if dawn could be said to exist anymore — the hour when the sky went from black to a bruised, starless grey. Freya heard the child before she saw her: a voice drifting up the stairwell, thin and borrowed, speaking in a cadence that belonged to a woman fifty years dead.

"*The flame must be fed where it was kindled,*" Wren said, in the fire-keeper's daughter's voice — in *Freya's mother's* voice, though the child had never met her, though the memory had been cut out of Freya before she could walk. "*Only at the Hollow's heart. Only in the place of the first burning.*"

Freya took the stairs two at a time.

Wren was sitting up, and the sight of her stopped Freya cold. The light beneath the child's skin had gone from gold to a sickly amber, flickering at the edges like a lamp out of oil. Her eyes, when they found Freya's, were clear and terribly calm — too calm for a child of seven or eight, but then, Wren had never been only a child. Wren remembered everything. Every extinguished star, every name of every fire-keeper, every voice the world had forgotten. Remembering everything, Freya was beginning to understand, was its own kind of dying.

"There you are," Wren said — in her own voice this time, small and hoarse. She held up one hand, and Freya saw that her fingertips had gone translucent, faint shapes moving in them like fish under ice. "It's cold now. It used to be warm all the time, even when I was scared. Even in the shrine, when the bad warden came, I was warm." She frowned, considering. "Mostly I liked being warm."

Freya crossed the room and gathered the child against her before she could think better of it. Wren came willingly, tucking her head beneath Freya's chin, and Freya felt the truth of it against her own skin: the warmth was leaving. Fading the way a hearth fades overnight, coals going grey one by one while everyone sleeps.

"How long?" she asked, and hated how steady she made her voice.

Wren was quiet a moment. Then, in her mother's voice again — gently, as if lending it to soften the blow — "*Three days. Perhaps four.*"

* * *

They argued about it the way exhausted people argue: quietly, in fragments, around the practical business of packing and water and checking the passes for warden patrols.

"The Hollow's heart is two days' hard climb," Corvin said, spreading the old survey map across a fallen door. "Maybe three, with the child slowing us. If the flame dies before we reach the kindling-place—"

"Then the stars stay dead forever, and Wren—" Freya couldn't finish it.

"And if we're caught," Corvin said flatly, "Serevan takes the flame and puts it in a weapon, and we trade a slow death for a fast one. You saw what he built at the shrine. You saw what one drop did to that gate."

Freya had seen. She dreamed of it. The warden-cabal's engine, drinking a single bead of dragon-fire and turning it into a beam that unmade stone — and Serevan's face above it, serene as a priest's, saying *the wards fell from inside, Ashgrave; someone had to make the sky honest again.* As if extinguishing the stars were honesty. As if theft were restoration.

"So we don't get caught," she said.

"That's your plan?"

"That's always my plan. It's worked for eleven years." She began rolling the bedrolls with hands that wouldn't quite stop shaking. "Slip the patrols, take the high route through the ash-fields, come down into the Hollow from the north face where the wards are oldest and thinnest. I've smuggled through worse."

"With a dying star-child and a disgraced warden whose face is on every post between here and the border."

"Yes," Freya said. "Exactly that."

Corvin studied her across the map, and something in his expression shifted — that reluctant, grudging tenderness he thought he hid so badly. "You know what he'll offer you. Down there, at the bottom. Your memories. All of them. The childhood they cut out of you, whole and warm, in exchange for the vial." He tapped the map, over the deep mark that meant the Hollow's heart. "He knows what you are now, Freya. He'll make the offer sweet."

"Then I'll refuse it."

"You don't know that. You can't know that until you're standing in front of the thing you've wanted your whole life without knowing you wanted it." His voice cracked slightly on the last words. "I stood in front of mine. My oath. My reinstatement. And I nearly — Freya, I *nearly*—"

"But you didn't." She came around the door and put her hand over his where it pressed the map flat. His knuckles were scarred; a warden's hands, a traitor's hands now. "That's the difference between us and Serevan. We nearly. He simply takes."

Corvin turned his hand palm-up and held hers a moment, brief and hard, and neither of them said anything else, because there was nothing left to say that the grip hadn't already said.

* * *

They climbed through the ash-fields as the second day died, and that was where the flame truly began to fail.

It happened between one step and the next. One moment the vial against Freya's breastbone was ticking its failing heartbeat; the next, it went silent, and the cold that flooded her was so total she staggered, caught herself on a ridge of fused glass — sand melted to mirror by some ancient burning — and tore the wrappings away with numb fingers.

Inside, the bead of fire had gone almost black. Only a red seam remained, pulsing slower and slower, like a door closing.

"No," Freya whispered. "No, no — not yet, we're *close*—"

Behind her, Wren made a sound.

It was not a word. It was barely a sound at all — a small, airless exhale — and Freya spun to find the child on her knees in the ash, Corvin catching her shoulders, and the light under Wren's skin was *out*. Not dimmed. Out. Her lips had gone grey-blue, and when her eyes opened they were fixed on some middle distance where perhaps the extinguished stars hung, visible only to her.

In a chorus of borrowed voices — the fire-keeper, the daughter, a dozen keepers stretching back into the first war, all speaking at once, layered and fading — Wren said:

"*We are going out. We have been going out for a hundred years. Do not let it end in glass.*"

Then the voices stopped, all of them, and the child slumped against Corvin's chest, breathing in tiny sips.

Freya knelt in the ash with the vial in her hands and understood, finally, completely, with a clarity that felt like falling: there was no choice left. There had never been a choice. She had spent her whole life believing she could keep things — bottle them, hide them, hold them safe behind wax seals and locked doors and walls around her heart — and every single thing she'd ever kept had only grown quieter in the keeping. Memories. Fire. Herself.

The stars would not come back because she carried a secret well. The child would not live because Freya was good at hiding. The only thing left that she could do — the only honest thing, the first honest thing in eleven years of theft — was to give it away. All of it. At the Hollow's heart, in the place of the first burning, in front of Serevan and his engines and his sweet terrible offers if it came to that.

She looked down at the vial, at the red seam pulsing its last, and she spoke to it the way she'd once spoken to clients, soft and persuasive, thief's cadence, seller's charm — except this time she was selling nothing, and the bargain ran the other way.

"Hold on," she breathed. "Just hold on a little longer. I'm taking you home."

She rose, tucked the vial against her heart, and lifted Wren onto her back, the child's cold arms looped loose around her neck. Corvin fell in beside her without a word, taking the weight of the pack and the watch and whatever else she couldn't carry.

Below them, down through the shattered northern face, the Hollow opened its throat: a vast darkness ringed with the ruins of the first war, and at its bottom, faint as a dying coal, the kindling-place waiting.

And somewhere in that darkness, she knew, Serevan was waiting too — with his offer already polished, already sweet, already shaped exactly like everything she had ever lost.

Freya tightened her arms beneath Wren's knees and started down.
