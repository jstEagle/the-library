## 9. The Last Dragon-Fire

The Hollow's heart was not a place so much as a wound.

Freya felt it before she saw it — a heat rising through the black stone underfoot, through the soles of her boots, through the marrow of her shins, as if the mountain itself had swallowed an ember centuries ago and never learned to digest it. The tunnel walls wept mineral light, thin veins of silver-green that pulsed in time with something vast and slow beneath the rock. Wren's small hand was ice in hers. The child had stopped speaking in borrowed voices hours ago; now she only breathed, shallow and quick, like a bird cupped too tightly in two hands.

*It knows you're coming,* Wren had said that morning, in the voice of a woman long dead — the fire-keeper's wife, Corvin believed, though none of them could prove it. *It has always known you.*

The tunnel opened without warning.

One moment there was stone, close and breathing; the next, a cavern so immense that Freya's lantern-light simply gave up, swallowed by a dark that was not empty but *attentive*. The floor sloped down toward a basin of glassy obsidian, and at its center, sunk into the mirror-black surface like a coin at the bottom of a well, burned a single point of gold.

Small. That was the first obscene thing about it. After all the ruins they had walked through, all the scorched bones of the First War, all the murals of dragons wheeling across ceilings tall as cathedrals — after all of that, the last dragon-fire in the world was no larger than a candle flame.

And yet the whole cavern leaned toward it. The air bent. The darkness gathered around it like a congregation, and Freya understood, with a lurch, that the stars above Thornfell had not gone out on their own. They had been *called home*. Every lost light in the sky had come down here to huddle around this one surviving spark, waiting for it to decide whether the world would be worth burning for again.

"Beautiful, isn't it."

Serevan stood on the far rim of the basin, and Freya hated how ordinary he looked — a lean man in grey wool, hands folded behind his back, patient as a tax collector. No horns, no shadow-crown, no grandeur. Just a warden who had spent thirty years learning exactly how much ruin one careful man could arrange.

"You're wondering why I didn't take it while you slept," he said, gesturing mildly at the flame. "I could have. I've known where it rested since before you were born, girl. I let your mother hide it. I let them cut it out of you." His voice did not rise or fall; it simply proceeded, like water finding its level. "Because a flame this old doesn't answer to force. It answers to *choice*. It had to be carried here willingly. It had to be *offered*."

Wren made a sound — small, cracked, and entirely her own. The first sound Freya had ever heard the child make that belonged to no one else.

"I know," Serevan said, and for one flickering instant something almost human crossed his face. "I know what it costs her. She is the vessel's key, the last of the keeper line's blood-bond. She weakens because the flame weakens, and the flame weakens because it is dying in the dark." He turned his grey eyes on Freya at last, and they were not cruel. That was the worst part. They were tired. "Give it to me, Eastwick. Let me feed it properly, cage it properly, and I can hold it alive for another thousand years. The wards rebuilt. The borders sealed. Order restored. Or keep your precious defiance, and watch the last light in the world gutter out in a child's arms."

Corvin moved beside her — she heard the whisper of his hand leaving his sword-hilt, deliberately empty, deliberately visible. "Serevan. You fell the wards yourself. You let raiders through the pass. You let people die—"

"I let a *city* remain possible," Serevan said. "You would understand that, Ashgrave, if you had ever been trusted with anything larger than a patrol."

Freya barely heard him.

She was looking at the flame. And the flame, impossibly, was looking back.

Not with eyes. With recognition. The same recognition she had felt in the cracked vial on the Vial Seller's Road, the night everything began — the glimpse of gold wings against a burning sky, a woman's voice singing a lullaby in a language of sparks. Her mother's voice. Her mother's fire. Cut from her when she was six years old and sold into silence, bottled and traded and forgotten, until the only proof that Freya Eastwick had ever been anyone at all sat drowning in a well of black glass.

*I stole memories for a living,* she thought, and something between laughter and grief broke open in her chest. *And the whole time, the greatest theft ever done was done to me.*

"Freya." Corvin's voice, low. "Whatever you're going to do — do it knowing I'll stand in front of whatever comes."

She reached into her coat.

Her vials. Forty-one of them, wrapped in wool and leather, each one a stolen life: the miller's first snowfall, the duchess's dead son laughing, a soldier's wedding, a thief's mother's kitchen. Years of coin. Years of survival. Years of being *someone*, even if the someone she was had been assembled from other people's leavings.

And beneath them, sewn into the lining where she'd kept it since the shrine, the vial that was not stolen at all. Hers. The one Corvin had found and hidden and never told her about — she had known for three days now, had watched him carry the guilt like a stone in his boot, and had loved him a little for it, and had said nothing, because some secrets are held gently or not at all.

The vial was warm. It had been warm for days.

"Serevan," she said. "You want it offered."

He inclined his head, one degree. Suspicion flickered behind his patience. "Yes."

"Then watch."

She stepped off the rim of the basin.

The obsidian took her weight like frozen water, slick and singing, and she slid — half-falling, half-running — down the slope toward the center, toward the gold point, toward the wound in the world. Behind her she heard Corvin shout, heard Serevan's composure crack at last into something raw and commanding, heard boots on glass. None of it mattered. Nothing had ever mattered less.

At the basin's heart she knelt, and the flame rose to meet her — stretched up from its drowning like a hand from deep water, trembling, starving, *knowing her*. Heat washed over her face and it smelled of woodsmoke and summer and a kitchen she had never consciously remembered and had missed her entire hollow life.

"Hello," Freya whispered. "I'm late."

She unstoppered the vial.

She expected the memory to pour out — the golden image, the winged shapes, her mother's song. Instead the vial simply *opened*, the way a fist opens, the way a door opens when the person behind it has finally decided to stop hiding. And the fire came home.

It went into her.

There was no other way to describe it. Light poured up her arm and filled her chest and she was six years old again and ninety years old and every age between, standing in a high mountain hall while dragons wheeled overhead and her mother sang and the stars were so thick in the sky they seemed less like lights than like *company*. She remembered the harvest — cold hands, a warden's apologetic murmur, the sensation of being scooped hollow like a gourd. She remembered growing up around the absence, leaning on it, building herself out of other people's discarded joy because her own had been taken and sold.

She remembered everything.

And then she understood what the flame wanted, and began, quietly, to weep.

"It's not enough to burn," she said aloud, and her voice came out layered, doubled, hers and something older speaking through the same throat. "It never was. It has to be *given*. All the way. Everything it's kept safe inside itself — everything it hid in me—" She looked up at the rim of the basin, at Corvin's stricken face, at Wren's wide dark eyes, already brightening as the flame's attention swept over her like sunrise. "It keeps its hoard the way I kept mine. Bottled. Hidden. And to fly, it has to spend it all."

"No," Corvin said. He was sliding down the slope toward her, graceless, desperate. "Freya. *Freya.* There's another way, we'll find—"

"There isn't." She smiled at him, and knew it was the truest thing her face had ever done. "You should have told me about the vial weeks ago, warden. Would have saved us both a lot of guilt."

She opened her coat.

Forty-two vials. Every stolen memory she had ever carried, every life she had ever bottled and bartered, and woven through them now, waking, the flame's own hoard — the memory of the First War, the names of the dead dragons, the shape of the old sky, the faces of everyone who had ever tended fire in the dark. A lifetime of theft. An age of keeping.

The flame rose around her like a cloak.

"Tell me a story afterward," she said to Corvin, and to Wren, whose mouth was opening around words that were finally, entirely her own. "Any story. I won't know which parts are true. Make the good ones loud."

Then Freya Eastwick, memory-thief, hollow woman, fire-keeper's daughter, threw back her head and *let go*.

The world went white.

Not light — *voice*. The flame left her the way a cathedral bell leaves its tower, and the sound of it was every star that had ever gone out crying out at once. It roared up through the Hollow's throat, through miles of living rock, and the mountain did not break; it *sang*. The obsidian basin ran like dawn. The tunnels they had crawled through lit gold from within, every vein of mineral blazing, and far above — through stone, through soil, through the shattered wards and the frozen pass and the clouds that had hung starless for a season — the sky tore open.

Freya saw it even from the deeps, seen through the flame's own departing eyes: the stars coming back. Not kindled one by one but in a great rushing tide, constellations reassembling themselves like scattered embers blown home, until the whole vault of night burned as it had in her mother's song, thick as company, endless as forgiveness.

And the fire spent everything doing it.

She felt it go — felt the hoard pour out of her, vial by vial, year by year. The miller's snowfall, gone. The duchess's laughing son, gone. The soldier's wedding, the thief's kitchen, the golden wings, the lullaby, her mother's face, her own name and the shape of the girl who had once owned it — all of it rising off her like sparks off a bonfire, streaming upward, feeding the sky. She clung to nothing. There was nothing left to cling with. She knelt in a cooling puddle of glass in the dark heart of the world and grew lighter and lighter and emptier and emptier, and it did not feel like loss.

It felt like the moment a held breath finally ends.

The last thing to leave was very small. A single warm point behind her ribs, lingering like a kiss on the forehead — the flame saying goodbye to the only hearth it had ever truly had.

Then that, too, rose away, and Freya Eastwick looked up at a sky full of returned stars, and knew nothing at all.

Not her name. Not the man shouting it as he slid down the slope toward her. Not the small figure limping after him, upright now, strong, *lit from within*, calling out in a clear new voice that cracked with tears:

"*Freya! Freya, look — look, they came back, you did it, you — please, please remember—*"

But she didn't. She only smiled up at the strangers running toward her through the gold-lit dark, at their faces wet and shining, and thought, with the last unfenced corner of her mind:

*What a beautiful night. I wonder whose story this is.*

* * *

Above them, in the ruined mouth of the Hollow, High Warden Serevan stood alone in the returned starlight, his life's work ash and his orders meaningless — and did not run. He drew his warden's blade and laid it flat across both palms, and waited, head bowed, for the disgraced man and the burning child to climb up out of the earth and decide what was to be done with him.

Behind him, far below, a woman with no past laughed at something a warden and a starless child were telling her — some story about a vial seller on a road, they said, a really remarkable woman — and asked them, eagerly, to start again from the beginning.
