## 10. Anew

She woke the way dawn does — slowly, and all at once.

Corvin had been watching her for hours by then, though he would never admit it aloud. He sat with his back against a fallen pillar of the old shrine, Wren asleep against his shoulder, and he watched the woman on the bedroll breathe, and waited, and feared.

The stars were back. That was the miracle no one had asked for. All night they had burned overhead in their ancient constellations, fierce and unashamed, as if the sky had been holding its breath for three hundred years and finally remembered how to exhale. The light of them pooled silver in the ruined courtyard of the Hollow's mouth, silvered the broken dragon-carvings, silvered Freya Eastwick's face — a face that was, at this moment, entirely unguarded, because there was nothing behind it left to guard.

Her eyes opened. Grey, he thought. He had never noticed before that they were grey. There had always been too much else in them — calculation, wariness, that quick merchant's flicker that priced everything it saw. Now they were just grey, and open, and looking at him without a single scrap of recognition.

"Where am I?" she said. Her voice was rough with sleep and something older than sleep. "Who are you?"

The question landed in his chest like a stone dropped down a well. He heard it fall a long way before it stopped.

"You're safe," he said, because it was the only true thing he could reach quickly enough. "You're in the mountains. Above Thornfell. My name is Corvin Ashgrave."

"Freya," she said slowly, testing it, and his heart lurched — but she shook her head. "No. Someone said that name to me. In the dark. I don't remember it being mine."

Wren stirred against his shoulder. The child had not truly slept since the fire went up; even now their eyes opened halfway, starlight caught in them like coins at the bottom of clear water, and fixed on Freya with an attention that was almost painful to witness. Wren did not speak — Wren never spoke in their own voice, only in borrowed ones, voices gathered like fallen leaves — but when they looked at her now, their small hands curled closed around something invisible and held it to their chest.

*Come back,* Wren had whispered in Serevan's voice, days ago, in the dark below the world. *Come back, come back.* And then, in a voice Corvin had not recognized until too late — a girl's voice, bright and sing-song, a fire-keeper's daughter's voice — *I'll keep it for you.*

He understood now what Wren had been keeping. Not the flame. The flame had gone where flames go. Wren had been keeping *her* — the shape of her, the truth of her, held safe in a child's borrowed memory while the fire burned everything else away.

"It's yours," Corvin said quietly. "Freya. It's your name. You gave up more than anyone should have to give, and if you want, we can give some of it back. Piece by piece. As fast or as slow as you like."

She pushed herself up on one elbow. Her hair had come loose from its braid and fell across her face, and she did not push it away with the practiced, impatient gesture he knew so well; she just let it hang there, unfamiliar even to her own hands.

"What did I give up?"

Corvin looked at the sky. It seemed suddenly important to have somewhere honest to look.

"Everything you ever kept," he said. "And everything that kept you."

* * *

They told her the story over three days, in pieces, the way you feed a fire that must not be smothered.

The first day, Corvin told her the parts that were facts: Thornfell, the border wards, the night the stars went out. He told her about the wardens' cabal, about High Warden Serevan, who had pulled the wards down from inside like a man unravelling his own coat to find the thread of gold sewn into the lining. He told her about the last dragon-fire, hidden for centuries in a stolen child's memory, and how Serevan had wanted it for a weapon, and how she — Freya — had chosen instead to give it back to the sky.

She listened with her hands wrapped around a tin cup of tea, and she interrupted often, and her questions were sharp and precise, and Corvin found himself smiling at them despite everything, because precision like that did not burn away. You could strip a person of every memory they owned and the shape of their mind remained, the way a riverbed remains when the river is taken elsewhere.

"So I was a thief," she said, on the first evening. Not accusing. Testing the word's weight.

"A smuggler," Corvin said. "Of memories. Bottled ones. You sold people their own pasts back, mostly. Or sold other people's pasts to those who'd pay." He hesitated. "It sounds worse than it was. Or better than it was. I honestly don't know which."

"And you were going to arrest me."

"The first night we met? Yes."

"What changed?"

He turned his cup in his hands. Outside, the returned stars threw clean light through the shrine's broken roof, laying pale bars across the floor like the ghost of a cage neither of them was in anymore.

"I watched you choose," he said. "That was the change. Everything else followed from it."

* * *

On the second day, Wren told her the parts that were hers.

The child sat cross-legged in front of her in the courtyard, grave as a small magistrate, and spoke in the voices of everyone Freya had ever been to them. They used Corvin's voice to describe the mountain pass and the ambush. They used Serevan's cold drawl to describe the bargain offered in the deep places, and then — this was the part that made Corvin look away, that made his throat close — they used a little girl's voice, high and bright and full of woodsmoke, to say:

*"My mother says the fire remembers who keeps it. She says that's why they take us young. So we won't remember either."*

Freya went very still.

"That's me," she said. "That's — I said that?"

Wren nodded. Then, in their own silence, they reached into the collar of their shirt and drew out a cord, and on the cord, a single glass vial, empty now, its stopper fused shut with heat. They pressed it into her hands.

"She said you'd want proof," Wren said, in Corvin's voice, gently mocking him, and he felt his ears go hot. "She said people like her never believe good things about themselves without evidence."

Freya turned the vial over. The glass was clouded, fire-scarred, ordinary. She held it the way you hold a key to a house you no longer remember living in.

"Why do you talk like that?" she asked Wren softly. "In other people's voices?"

Wren considered the question with the seriousness it deserved. When they answered, it was in a voice none of them had heard before — an old voice, dry and warm, like pages turning.

*"Because someone has to remember everyone. And I was made before anyone decided I shouldn't."*

Freya looked at the child for a long moment. Then she did something Corvin had never seen her do in all the weeks of flight and fear and fire: she opened her arms, and Wren folded into them without hesitation, as if they had done it a hundred times, as if some part of the child remembered being held by her even when she could not remember holding anything at all.

Over Wren's head, her grey eyes found Corvin's. There was a question in them. He didn't know yet what it was. But he nodded anyway, because whatever it was, the answer was yes.

* * *

On the third day, she asked the question he had been dreading.

They were walking, the three of them, down through the pass toward Thornfell — toward the town, the roads, the life waiting to be either resumed or refused. The stars were still there, still burning, still new enough that travelers on the road below had begun to gather at dusk just to watch them, strangers standing shoulder to shoulder in the cold, pointing upward, weeping without shame. Word had come up the valley with a tinker two days ago: the wards were rebuilding themselves, thread by golden thread, and no one knew why, and no one much cared. The sky was lit. That was enough.

"They're saying a dragon flew out of the Hollow," Freya said, kicking a stone off the path. "Half the valley swears they saw it. Wings like storm-fronts. Fire in its wake."

"People say a lot of things."

"Were they there? Did you see it?"

Corvin walked a few steps before answering. "I saw light go up," he said. "More light than I've ever seen. Whether it had wings — " He shrugged. "Some things deserve to be more than what one disgraced warden can swear to."

She glanced sideways at him. "Disgraced."

"Serevan's cabal didn't appreciate my defection. I'm officially stripped of rank, warrant outstanding, name struck from the rolls." He smiled thinly. "It's the best thing that's ever happened to me."

"And me?" she said. "What am I, officially?"

The question hung between them. Below, Thornfell's rooftops smoked gently in the morning cold, ordinary and golden and utterly unaware of what had been paid above them.

"That depends," Corvin said carefully, "on whether you want the old answer or a new one."

She stopped walking. Wren, a few paces ahead, stopped too, and turned, patient as stone.

"The vials are gone," Freya said. "All of them. Every memory I ever stole, every memory I ever sold, every memory anyone ever stole from me. I've been trying to feel grief about that, and I can't find any. Isn't that strange? I should be mourning myself. Instead I just keep thinking — " She frowned, searching. "I keep thinking the shelf was crowded. And now it isn't. And the room behind it is very large, and empty, and mine."

"Yes," Corvin said. "That's exactly it."

"So." She faced him fully, and the morning light came over the ridge behind her, and for a moment she stood outlined in it, a woman made new against a sky made new, and Corvin understood that he was looking at something rarer than dragon-fire: a person with no past, choosing. "Tell me the old answer. Quickly. And then tell me the new one. Slowly."

"The old answer," Corvin said, "is that you were Freya Eastwick, the Vial Seller, and you trusted no one, and you slept in a different inn every week, and the loneliest thing about you was how good you'd gotten at not noticing it."

Something flickered across her face — not recognition, but its shadow, the groove worn where the wheel used to run.

"And the new one?"

He took a breath. Beside them, Wren slipped a small hand into hers, and she looked down at it, startled, and did not let go.

"The new one," Corvin said, "is that you're Freya, and you freed the sky, and you have a child who keeps your voice safe and a fool of an ex-warden who'd follow you anywhere, and you're standing at the top of the road into Thornfell with absolutely nothing behind you and three whole lives ahead, and — "

He stopped. She was watching him with an expression he'd never seen on the old face, the guarded face: open wonder, unpracticed and unhidden, like sunrise on water.

"— and," he finished, quieter, "some things aren't worth remembering. Some things are worth being remembered *into*. If you'll let us."

Freya Eastwick — or whoever she was now, whoever she was becoming — looked down the long road, at the smoking chimneys and the waking town and the wide lit sky above it all. Then she looked at the hand holding hers, and the man beside her, and the vial hanging empty at her throat like a promise already kept.

"Well," she said, and her voice cracked on it, and she laughed, and the laugh was brand-new too, never used before this moment. "Then we'd better start. I have a great deal to catch up on."

She took the first step down the mountain, and they went with her — all of them, together, into the story they would spend the rest of their lives telling.
