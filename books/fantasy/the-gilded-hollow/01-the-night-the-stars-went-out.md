## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The mule was named Duchess, and she was a liar.

Jude Dravenmoor had known this for three years—since the night she'd stood in the rain outside a Bellharrow stable and looked at him with those enormous liquid eyes until he'd given her his cloak, at which point she'd eaten it. Cloaks, hats, rope ends, once an entire ledger of debts that Jude had been quietly hoping would burn anyway. Duchess ate paper the way priests ate sin: constantly, and with an air of doing everyone a favor.

So when she stopped dead on the switchback above the Kettle Gap and refused to move one step farther, Jude did not assume spirits. He assumed supper.

"Duchess." He scratched behind her long gray ear. "There's nothing in that scrub but thornbush and regret."

Duchess looked at the thornbush. Then she looked back at him, with the particular patience of a creature who has outlived several owners.

Behind them, the caravan waited—a dozen souls and nineteen animals strung along the mountain road like beads on a fraying cord. Old Marta with her cart of untaxed brandy. The Fenwick brothers, who were not brothers, or possibly were; even they had stopped being sure. A widow named Corla with two children asleep in her wagon among bolts of smuggled silk. And Tam, sixteen years old, all elbows and questions, leading the spare mules and asking, as he had been asking since sundown:

"Jude. Why do we pray to Hollow? Nobody's ever *seen* Hollow."

"Nobody's ever seen the bottom of a well either," Jude said, still addressing the mule. "Doesn't mean the water isn't there."

"That's not an answer."

"It's a better answer than most gods get." He gave Duchess's lead rope a gentle tug. She planted her feet. "Fine. Starve up here. See if I care."

He cared. That was the trouble with Jude Dravenmoor, the thing the smugglers' guild had never quite managed to beat out of him: he cared about mules and orphans and widows' cargo and roads that existed only because men like him remembered them, and every one of those affections was a hook some god could hang a man by.

Hollow had hung him by all of them at once, eleven years ago, in a cellar in Graysink, over a crate of stolen candles. But that was a story for campfires, and the light was going strange.

Because the light *was* going strange.

Jude noticed it the way you notice a room has gone quiet—not the change itself, but the absence of something that had been there your whole life. He looked up from the mule, past the black teeth of the peaks, to where the stars should have been.

They weren't.

Not clouded. Not dimmed. *Gone.* Every star above the Thornfell passes, extinguished at once, as if a hand had passed over the sky and closed it like a book. The dark up there wasn't night-dark. It was page-dark. Blank-dark. The dark of a thing that had never been written.

Jude's mouth went dry. "Tam," he said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him, because inside he felt the way a house feels when its foundation shifts—one long low groan through everything. "Get the lanterns lit. All of them."

"It's not that late—"

"*All* of them."

And then he felt it.

He had no word for what it felt like, then or ever after. The closest he ever came was this: imagine a sound you've heard every day since birth—the river under the floorboards, say, or your mother humming while she worked—and then imagine waking to find it gone, and understanding in your bones that it hadn't stopped. It had never started. That the silence went backward through your whole life, and everything you'd thought was music was just the shape the noise left behind.

Hollow went silent.

Not far away. Not sleeping. *Silent*, the way a voice is silent when the throat that made it is unmade. Jude sat down hard on the cold stone of the switchback, right there in front of Duchess and God and everybody, because his legs had decided the matter without consulting him.

Eleven years of prayers. Eleven years of the quiet presence at the back of his skull, warm as a banked fire, patient as debt—that vast, unhurried attention that smugglers swore by and honest folk pretended didn't exist. Hollow, who kept the hidden ways hidden. Hollow, whose whole nature was the space between things: the gap in the hedge, the blind turn in the pass, the moment the guard looks the other way. Hollow, who had never once spoken aloud, and had never once needed to.

Gone. And worse than gone—Jude groped for the feeling and found the hole where the feeling should be, smooth-edged, like a tooth socket. Something had eaten his god, and it had eaten the memory of the taste.

"No," he said, out loud, to nobody, to the blank sky. "No, you don't."

"Jude?" Tam had come back up the line, lantern in hand, and the boy's face in the lanternlight was pale as tallow. "Jude, look behind us."

Jude looked.

South, down the long spine of the pass they'd climbed since dawn, the border wards of Thornfell were dying.

He'd seen wards his whole life and never seen them, the way you don't see glass. They'd hung over the passes like heat-shimmer, like the faint gold ring around a candle flame—Sera Vey's people called it the Gilding, though Jude had learned that word later, and learned what it cost, much later than that. Every child in Thornfell grew up under it without knowing. It was the reason maps of the country stayed drawn. It was the reason a Bellharrow man knew he was a Bellharrow man, and a Graysink miner knew his own name on the worst morning of his life.

Now the Gilding was shattering.

It went the way a window goes when the frame rots through—first a single bright crack, spidering across the southern sky with a sound like a bell struck by a corpse, then another, then dozens, the whole golden lattice crazing into shards that fell upward, or sideways, or simply *away*, winking out before they finished falling. The sound reached them a heartbeat later, rolling up the pass: not thunder. Glass. Miles upon miles of glass, breaking all at once behind them, and under the glass-sound something else, something low and vast and *hungry*, a note Jude felt in his back teeth and in the old scar on his ribs and in the place where Hollow used to live.

Duchess screamed. Mules do scream, when they've a mind to; people forget that. She reared against the lead rope and Jude held on, boots skidding on frost-slick stone, because if he let go of the rope he was fairly sure he'd let go of everything else too.

"Hold," he told her. "Hold, you great ugly liar, *hold*—"

She held. Animals know things. Whatever Duchess had smelled in the thornbush, whatever had stopped her on that switchback, she'd known before the sky did. Jude filed that away in the part of his mind that was still working, the small cold clerk's office at the center of him where panic was not permitted entry: *the beasts knew first. Remember that.*

The last shard of the Gilding fell somewhere south of the Kettle Gap, and the dark came down like a lid.

For a moment nobody in the caravan spoke. Nineteen animals and twelve human beings, breathing steam in the lantern-light, staring south at a horizon that now simply *stopped*—not faded, not shadowed, but ended, the way a sentence ends when the rest of the page is torn out.

Then Corla's little girl woke up in the wagon and said, clear as a bell across the frozen air:

"Mama, I can't remember the words to the evening song."

And Corla said, "That's all right, love, neither can I," in a voice that meant she could, she always could, she'd sung it every night of her life, and it was gone.

* * *

They made camp in the lee of an old toll-house that hadn't collected a toll in sixty years, which made it, in the smugglers' catechism, holy ground. Jude got fires going—three of them, small, shielded—and set watches, and did all the hundred small competent things that kept twelve frightened people alive, and the whole time the clerk's office in the center of him kept taking inventory:

*Lantern oil: nine days. Flour: eleven. Brandi: sufficient, thank the—*

Thank the what.

The thought hit him like a fall on ice. He'd reached for the phrase the way you reach for a stair in the dark, the prayer every smuggler muttered over contraband since before his grandfather's grandfather—*thank the Hollow for the hidden way*—and the space where it lived was smooth and empty. He knew the words had been there. He could feel the shape of their absence, the way your tongue finds a missing tooth. But when he tried to speak them, nothing came.

Old Marta found him sitting apart from the fire, hands wrapped around a tin cup he wasn't drinking.

"You felt it too." She lowered herself onto the stone beside him with the groan of a woman whose knees had opinions. Marta was seventy if she was a day, and had run brandy through these passes since before Jude was born, and her face in the firelight was grim as a gravestone. "Halfway up the scree. Like someone walked over my grave, except it wasn't my grave. It was—" She waved a hand vaguely at her own chest. "The middle of me."

"I know."

"Fifty years I've run this road. Fifty years, and I've never once gotten lost, and I'll tell you why." She tapped her temple. "Because Hollow keeps the map in here. Every smuggler's the same. We don't carry charts, boy. We *are* charts." Her hand, he noticed, had begun to shake. "And tonight somebody burned the library."

"Marta." Jude turned to look at her. "Say the evening blessing. The one over the first cup."

She frowned. "Why?"

"Please."

She said it. Or she tried to. Her lips moved through the opening cadence—*from the hidden road, to the hidden hand*—and then stopped, and stopped again, and her frown deepened into something terrible, because the middle of the blessing wouldn't come. She knew there was a middle. She could hear the ghost of its rhythm. The words themselves had been eaten.

"That's not forgetting," Marta whispered. "I'm old, but I'm not *that* old. Forgetting feels like fog. This feels like—" She searched for it. "Like surgery."

Down by the second fire, Tam was teaching Corla's children a counting rhyme to keep them calm, and losing count. The Fenwicks—or the Fenwick, singular, depending on the hour—were arguing in low urgent voices about whether they'd loaded six crates of silk or five, and being unable to settle it, and being frightened by their inability in a way that had nothing to do with money.

Jude drank his cold tea and watched the blank sky and thought about Duchess standing stubborn on the switchback, and about the hole in his chest where eleven years of quiet companionship used to be, and about the sound the wards had made coming down.

Glass. The wards had shattered like glass.

Which meant the wards had been *there*. All along, over every pass, over every town where he'd slept and stolen and laughed and buried friends. A country inside a lamp. And tonight somebody had broken the lamp, and whatever had been kept out—or kept *in*—was loose in the dark with them now, and it was hungry, and its appetite was memory.

"First watch is mine," he said, and got up, because if he sat still any longer the dread would climb out of the clerk's office and start making decisions.

He walked the picket line, checking girths and water, and Duchess lipped his sleeve for salt and he let her, and when he reached the southern edge of camp he stopped and looked down the pass, the way you look at a wound.

Far below, where the road bent toward the valley and the town of Bellharrow with its lamplit market square, there should have been lights. There were always lights. Bellharrow never slept entirely; there was always a tavern window burning, always the ward-tower's beacon turning its slow gold wheel above the rooftops.

The valley was black. Completely, perfectly black, from edge to edge, as if the town had been blotted off the world.

Except—

Jude narrowed his eyes. Leaned against the picket post. Told himself it was a trick of the dark, and knew it wasn't.

One light. Down in all that darkness, one small light was moving. Flickering, hurrying, climbing the valley road toward the pass. Toward *them*. A lantern carried fast by someone who knew exactly where they were camped, in a country where nobody should have been able to find anything anymore.

Beside him, Duchess raised her head, ears forward, and blew out a long breath through her nostrils.

"Yeah," Jude said quietly. "I see it too."

He stood a long moment, hand on the mule's warm neck, watching the little light come up out of the dead valley like a spark floating on a flooded river. Behind him the fires crackled and Tam's counting rhyme stumbled and started over. Above him, where the stars had been, the sky waited, blank as an unwritten page.

His god was dead—or eaten, or worse—and the country was coming apart at its edges, and tomorrow, one way or another, he was going to find out who was carrying that lantern.

"Get some sleep, all of you," he called back to the camp, in the voice he used for lies that needed to hold weight. "We move at first light."

There was no first light coming. But nobody had thought that far yet, and Jude intended to keep it that way until he'd figured out what in all the hollow hells he was going to tell them.
