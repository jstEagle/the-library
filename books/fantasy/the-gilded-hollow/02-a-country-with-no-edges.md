## 2. A Country With No Edges

The road down into Bellharrow was the first thing to go wrong.

Jude had walked it a dozen times in his years running goods through the Thornfell passes—salt one way, wool and smuggled brandy the other—and he knew it the way a man knows the back of his own hand: every switchback, every leaning pine, every place where the mountain wept a thin spring across the stones. He knew that at the third switchback you could see the whole valley spread out below like a coin dropped on green felt, and that Bellharrow sat in its middle with its slate roofs and its crooked bell tower and its smoke rising straight up in still weather.

He knew all of that. What he saw instead was a valley with no town in it.

He stopped so suddenly that the mule behind him walked into his back and complained about it.

"That's not right," said Tam, the youngest of the caravan hands, peering past Jude's shoulder. "Bellharrow's down there. I can smell the tanneries."

Jude could smell them too—leather and lime and the sweet rot of hides—which was somehow worse, because the smell was arriving from a place that wasn't there. The valley floor lay green and empty. No roofs. No bell tower. No smoke. Just fields going to seed and a river stitching silver through the middle of it all.

Then, between one blink and the next, the town was back. Slate roofs, crooked tower, smoke rising straight as a plumb line. It didn't fade in or build itself; it simply *was*, as though it had never been anywhere else, and Jude's memory of the empty valley began already to feel like a trick of the light.

"Did you see that?" Tam asked.

"I saw it."

"Should we be worried?"

"We should keep walking," Jude said, "and you should hold onto my sleeve if you start feeling sure about anything."

They came down into Bellharrow by late afternoon, and the town was solid enough—doors, dogs, a woman beating a rug off a balcony with the rhythm of someone who had done it ten thousand times—but it was solid the way a dream is solid, and Jude had spent enough nights around campfires listening to veterans talk about shell-shock to know the difference. The streets were too quiet. Not empty-quiet. *Listening*-quiet.

The first wrongness he could put his finger on was the map.

There was a map of the region painted on the wall of the waystation where caravans paid their road-tolls, a grand old thing, two generations of merchants' thumbprints ground into its border. Jude had studied it often enough to draw most of it from memory. Now he stood in front of it while the toll-clerk waited behind him, and the map looked like it had been washed.

Not damaged. *Washed.* The mountains were still there, faintly, like pencil under erasure. The rivers remained, because water apparently insisted on existing even when nothing else did. But the roads were gone. The towns were gone. And across the entire center of the map, where the name of the country should have been, there was only smooth blank parchment, pale as an eggshell, without even a ghost of ink.

"Bellharrow," Jude said slowly, pointing at the empty space where Bellharrow should have been. "Where's this?"

The clerk—a round, comfortable man named Ostler, whom Jude had known for nine years and who owed him eleven coppers and a grudge—looked at the map, then at Jude, then at the map again, with the expression of a man being asked to count his own teeth.

"That's... here," Ostler said. "I think. That's us." He frowned. "It doesn't look like us."

"It's not anything," Jude said. "Look at the edges. Look at the borders."

Ostler leaned in. His frown deepened into something frightened that he didn't yet have a word for. "The borders are..." He ran a finger along where Thornfell met its northern neighbor, and his finger slid across unmarked parchment without catching on anything. "There were lines here. Red ones. I've traced them myself, a hundred times, when I was bored." He looked up. "Have they been moved?"

"No," Jude said quietly. "They've been *eaten*."

* * *

The boundary stone confirmed it.

Every market town in Thornfell kept one at its edge—a knee-high pillar of gray granite carved with the country's sigil, a thorn-crowned star, and the words THORNFELL KEEPS ITS OWN. You could find them half-buried in hedgerows, worn smooth by weather, but always legible. Always. The stones were older than the kingdom that commissioned them, and stubborn as sin.

Jude found Bellharrow's stone in a pasture outside the east gate, and it was smooth.

Not worn. Not defaced. Smooth as a river pebble, top to bottom, front to back, as though the granite had been poured yesterday and polished by patient hands. He knelt and put his palm flat against it. Cold. Ordinary. Real stone doing real stonework, holding a fence post upright, and completely, perfectly silent about what it was for.

A shepherd girl watched him from atop the fence, maybe twelve, chewing a stem of grass with the insolence of her profession.

"You looking for something, mister?"

"The name on this stone."

"There's no name on that stone." She said it easily, without malice—the plain certainty of a child reporting the color of the sky.

"There was. A star with a crown of thorns, and words underneath."

She considered this with genuine interest, the way she might consider a story about dragons. "What words?"

"*Thornfell keeps its own.*"

"What's Thornfell?" the girl asked, and the world tilted very slightly on its axis.

Jude stood up slowly. Around him the pasture went on being a pasture, the sheep went on being sheep, the evening light fell gold and ordinary across everything, and none of it knew what country it belonged to. He thought of the stars going out over the passes. He thought of the wards shattering behind him like a chandelier coming down. He thought of Hollow's silence—not absence, not distance, but *silence*, the silence of a room where a voice used to live.

Something was eating Thornfell. Not its land. Not its people. Its *definition*. Its edges. The sum of facts that made it this place and not some other.

"Mister?" the girl said. "You've gone funny-colored."

"What's your name?" he asked.

"Maren."

"And where do you live, Maren?"

"Here." She gestured at the pasture, the town, the world, with magnificent vagueness.

"Which is what? What nation? What province? Who collects your taxes? Whose law does your father answer to?"

She chewed her grass stem. She genuinely tried—he could see her trying, reaching for something the way you reach for a word on the tip of your tongue—and what came back, again and again, was a soft confused nothing. Her brow furrowed. "Here," she said finally, with the first flicker of fear. "I live *here*. Why don't I know?"

"It's all right," Jude said, and hated how hollow it sounded. "Go home. Hold onto your name. Say it to yourself tonight before you sleep—Maren, Maren, Maren—and don't let anyone tell you different."

She fled toward the farmhouse, and Jude stood alone with a smooth stone and understood, with the cold clarity that had kept him alive through nineteen years of smuggling and three small wars, that the girl's fear was the healthiest thing he'd seen all day. Everyone else in Bellharrow was adjusting. Everyone else was smoothing over the hole in their minds like a cat pawing sand over its mess, and the horror of it was how *well* it worked. How easy forgetting was. How gentle.

He'd felt it himself, walking in from the pass. Little gaps. He couldn't remember which side of the river the toll-house was on, and he'd slept in it twice. He couldn't remember the name of the innkeeper at the Drowned Goose, though he could remember the innkeeper's dog, her face, the scar on her ear. Names first. Then places. Then—

Then whatever came after *then*.

* * *

He found the ward-scribe because she found him, which told him she was either very good or very desperate, and it turned out she was both.

He was sitting in the common room of the Drowned Goose—it was called the Drowned Goose; he was nearly certain; the sign above the door showed a bird and some water, which he chose to take as corroboration—working through his third cup of wine and a list of his caravan's losses written on the back of a toll receipt. The list was short, because the list was everything: the cargo was ash, the route was gone, the client in Lowmark would wait exactly as long as clients do, and the god he'd sworn his oaths to had gone quiet as a dead hearth. He was contemplating whether wine counted as dinner when a woman dropped into the chair across from him without asking.

She was perhaps forty, sharp-faced, ink-stained to the elbows, with the particular rumpled ferocity of someone who had been arguing with bureaucracy since childhood and winning more often than losing. She set a leather satchel on the table like a duelist laying down a gauntlet.

"Jude Dravenmoor," she said. "Caravan-master. God-sworn to Hollow. Currently unemployed, I'd guess, along with everyone else whose trade depended on the passes staying real."

"And you are?"

"Sera Vey. Ward-scribe, third circle, formerly of the Border Office at Cael Sarrow." She pronounced *formerly* the way other people pronounced a curse. "Which means I helped maintain the wards that came down two nights ago, which means I'm either the person you want to talk to or the person you want to hang, depending on your temperament."

"Depends," Jude said, "on what you know."

She glanced around the common room. Half a dozen locals drank in corners with the determined cheerfulness of people refusing to notice something. The innkeeper's dog—gray-muzzled now, but Jude remembered the scar—slept by the fire. Sera lowered her voice anyway.

"I know the wards didn't fail. I know they were *breached*, deliberately, by something that has spent the last two nights eating this country from the edges inward. I know the maps are blank and the stones are smooth and half the people in this town can't tell me who their king is—" She stopped. Her jaw tightened. "Who their king *was*. I know that by midwinter there won't be a Thornfell to remember, and I know that you, Master Dravenmoor, walked out of the high passes last night with thirty head and a full crew, which means you may be the last living person who remembers the smuggler's roads in working order."

Jude turned his cup slowly on the table. "You've been asking after me."

"I've been asking after anyone who came down out of the mountains. You're the first. Possibly the last." She leaned forward. "How much of the road do you still remember?"

"All of it," he said, and then, honestly: "Most of it. Some of it's... slippery. Like trying to recall a dream by noon."

"Describe the slipperiness."

"Why?"

"Because I need to know what it eats *first*." She pulled a battered notebook from her satchel, opened it to a page dense with her own cramped handwriting, and turned it toward him. Diagrams. Ward-lattices. Margins full of crossed-out theories. "Names go first—that's consistent everywhere I've checked. Then boundaries, then distances, then the reasons things are where they are. It works inward from the definition. The land stays. The *meaning* goes." She tapped the page. "And here's the part that should keep you up at night, Master Dravenmoor: meaning doesn't vanish. It gets *consumed*. Something is drinking this country, and it's growing fat on it, and every hour we sit here it takes another swallow."

The fire popped. The dog snored. Somewhere upstairs a bed creaked under an ordinary sleeper having ordinary dreams, and Jude felt the strangeness of it all press down on him like deep water.

"You said the wards were breached deliberately," he said. "By whom?"

"I don't know. Yet." Her eyes were dark and steady and more afraid than she wanted them to be. "But I intend to find out, and I can't do it from here, because here—" she gestured at the common room, the town, the soft blank evening pressing against the windows "—is dissolving faster than I can write it down. I need to move. North, toward Cael Sarrow, toward the heart of the ward-network, toward whatever did this. And I need someone to get me there along roads that still exist."

"And you think mine do."

"I think yours exist because they were never written down anywhere official. Never mapped, never recorded, never entered into any ledger the state kept." She tapped her temple. "Smugglers' roads live in *heads*, Master Dravenmoor. In memory. In oath and habit and the shape of a man's feet on stone. If this thing eats records and definitions and official truth—" her voice dropped further "—then the only roads left in Thornfell are the ones nobody honest ever knew about. Yours."

Jude looked at his wine. He thought about the third switchback, and the town that wasn't there, and the shepherd girl who didn't know her own country's name, and the silence where Hollow's voice used to be—a silence that had weight, that had *shape*, that pressed against the inside of his skull like a palm laid flat over a wound.

His god was gone. His trade was gone. His country was being drunk down to the dregs by something patient and vast, and the sensible response—the response any sane man would give—was to take what remained of his crew and his coin and walk south, out of the passes, into some neighboring land where maps still had ink on them, and start over.

He heard himself say: "What's in it for my crew?"

Sera blinked. "Payment. Whatever's left in the Border Office strongboxes at Cael Sarrow, split fair."

"And for you? What's your price?"

"Redemption," she said, too fast, and then, catching herself, "Answers. I built those wards. I want to know what got through them and why." There was something underneath the words, some guilt with old roots, but Jude had been reading liars for twenty years and she wasn't lying—she was just not telling everything, which was a different animal entirely, and one he could respect.

He drained his cup and stood.

"Be at the east gate at dawn," he said. "Bring food, ink, and that notebook. And Mistress Vey—if you slow my caravan down, I'll leave you at the first crossroads, even if the crossroads has forgotten it's a crossroads."

"Fair terms." She was already packing her satchel, already halfway into motion, a woman who had clearly been waiting days for someone to say yes. Then she paused, and looked up at him with an expression he couldn't quite read—pity, maybe, or recognition.

"One more thing, Master Dravenmoor. Your god. Hollow. When did the silence start?"

"The same moment the stars went out."

She nodded slowly, as if ticking a box on a list she very much wished she didn't have. "That's what I was afraid of. The wards didn't come down first, you see. I've reconstructed the sequence. The wards came down *second*." She slung the satchel over her shoulder. "Whatever ate your god opened the door from the inside."

She left him standing in the common room with his blood gone cold and the fire crackling on, and outside the window the last light failed over Bellharrow—a town that no longer knew its own name, in a country that was learning, swallow by swallow, what it felt like to be forgotten.
