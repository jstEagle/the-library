## 3. The Smuggler's Catechism

The road out of Bellharrow was called the Cooper's Cut, and for the first mile it behaved like a road. For the second mile it behaved like a rumor.

Jude felt the difference through his boots before he saw it with his eyes. A true road has a certain confidence underfoot—a compacted certainty, a memory of a thousand cartwheels pressing the earth into agreement. The Cooper's Cut had lost that. Each step landed slightly later than he expected, as though the ground were being invented just in time to catch him and couldn't quite hide its surprise.

Behind him, the survivors from Bellharrow strung out along the track: eleven souls, two mules, one goat of strong opinions, and Sera Vey, who walked like a woman reading a book only she could see and occasionally frowned at the horizon as if it had misspelled something.

"Your road is doing something," she said, catching up.

"It's thinking," Jude said. "Roads do that when they're nervous."

"Roads don't think."

"Then we're both about to be very disappointed."

He said it lightly, because they needed lightness the way lungs need air, but his hands were not light at all. They remembered what had happened at the pass two nights ago—the stars going out all at once, like candles pinched by a single enormous hand, and behind them the border wards shattering with a sound he still heard when he closed his eyes. Glass singing itself apart. And underneath it, deeper than sound: silence. The place where Hollow had always been, warm as a banked fire at the base of Jude's skull, gone cold.

He had been god-sworn since he was nineteen years old. He knew what it was to have a god's attention resting on you—weightless, constant, like sunlight through a window you stop noticing until someone boards it up.

Someone had boarded it up.

* * *

The first time Hollow spoke to him, Jude was twenty-two, crouched in a snowdrift above the Vell Pass with forty bolts of Kettering silk strapped to a mule that hated him personally, waiting for the toll-wardens' lanterns to swing away from the eastern path.

The man who'd hired him—an old smuggler named Doss Marrow, all knuckles and beard, smelling permanently of pipe smoke and mule—had made him swear the oath first. Not to Doss. To the goods.

"Kneel," Doss had said, in the middle of a snowdrift, at midnight, in the wind.

"I'm not kneeling in a snowdrift."

"You'll kneel or you'll walk back down and find honest work, and we both know you can't, so kneel."

Jude knelt. Doss put one hand flat on the silk bales, which were colder than the snow, and made him recite:

*"I swear by the road I know and no other. I swear by the weight carried and the weight owed. What passes my hands, I remember; what I remember, holds. If I forget this cargo, may the cargo forget me."*

It sounded like nonsense. It sounded exactly like the kind of thing old men invent to make young men feel that theft is a sacrament. Jude recited it with the sincerity of a man whistling past a graveyard, took his cut, and thought no more about it.

Except that night, coming down the far side of the Vell Pass in fog so thick it seemed less like weather than like the mountain closing its eyes, he had known—known, the way you know where your own hands are in the dark—exactly where the path bent around the scree field, exactly which of the three forks was real and which two were tricks of the slope. He had never taken that route before. Doss had described it once, badly, over ale. But the knowledge sat in him whole and certain, like something set down carefully by careful hands.

And beneath the knowing, faint as heat from a hearth in another room, something had noticed him. Something patient. Something that did not speak in words but in *certainties*, laying them into his mind the way a mason lays stone—one at a time, each one fitting.

Doss had grinned at him the next morning, toothless and terrible. "Felt him, didn't you."

"Felt who?"

"Hollow. God of the unmarked roads, patron of everything that crosses a border without permission." Doss had spat, ceremonially. "Toll-men pray to the border gods. Kings pray to the crown gods. We pray to the space between. Every oath you swore last night was a brick, boy. You're building yourself into the wall whether you like it or not."

"What wall?"

"The world, mostly." Doss had shrugged. "Somebody's got to hold it up. Might as well be paid."

Jude had laughed then. Twenty-two-year-olds laugh at everything that matters.

He wasn't laughing now.

* * *

Because here was the thing he had spent two sleepless nights turning over, the way you turn a stone looking for what's underneath: the catechism wasn't decoration. It had never been decoration.

*I swear by the road I know and no other.* Not "the road that exists." The road he *knew*. 

*What passes my hands, I remember; what I remember, holds.*

Every prayer Doss had ever taught him—and there had been dozens, one for river crossings, one for gates, one absurdly specific litany for moving contraband through a town during a funeral (the god apparently appreciated irony)—every one of them was built on the same joint: memory. Remember the road and the road stays real. Name the pass and the pass keeps its shape. The smuggler's prayers weren't requests. They were maintenance.

They were how the unmarked roads of Thornfell had stayed marked for four hundred years. Not warded from above. *Remembered* from below, by generations of thieves muttering oaths over stolen goods, each oath a stitch holding the country's hem together.

And now the stars were out, the wards were broken glass, and somewhere above him something vast was eating Thornfell's definition bite by bite—which meant the stitches were all that was left.

Which meant it was his turn to hold the needle.

"Dravenmoor." Sera again, sharper. "The road is doing more something."

She was right. Ahead, the Cooper's Cut simply stopped—not washed out, not blocked, but *unconvinced*. The last thirty paces of it had gone soft and gray, like a word on the tip of the tongue. Beyond it the hillside continued, grassy and indifferent, offering no opinion on where a road might go. The mules stopped without being told. Even the goat went quiet, which Jude found more alarming than anything else.

One of the Bellharrow survivors, a cooper named Anselm with forearms like hams, peered past Jude at the fading stretch and said, in the careful voice people use near cliff edges, "Was there ever a road there?"

"There's a road there," Jude said.

"Only it doesn't look—"

"There's a road there, Anselm. Say it with me if it helps."

Anselm did not say it with him. But he also didn't argue, which in Jude's experience was half of leadership.

Jude walked to the edge of the gray and stood on the last solid ground. Up close, the faded section wasn't empty. It was *almost*—almost ruts, almost wheel-worn stone, almost the memory of a road wearing the ghost of its own shape. He could feel it trying. That was the strange part, the part that lodged under his ribs like a fishbone: the road hadn't given up. It was reaching for itself and couldn't quite close its hand.

Because nobody was remembering it hard enough.

"All right," Jude said quietly. "Old friend. Let's see if you're still listening, even if you can't answer."

He knelt. In front of witnesses. He would deny later that his knees complained; they had not, in fact, waited for later.

He put one palm flat against the ground, the way Doss had put his hand on the silk, and began.

*"I swear by the road I know and no other."*

Nothing. Wind. The goat chewed something it shouldn't have.

*"This is the Cooper's Cut. It runs from Bellharrow to the Tarnish ford, nine miles, give or take a lie. It bends east at the split oak to dodge the sink-mire—I've walked it twice, I've run it once, I once slept on it drunk and woke up with a bruise shaped like Bellharrow's entire tax code."*

A murmur from the survivors. Sera had gone very still.

*"What passes my hands, I remember; what I remember, holds. Hold, then. Hold, you stubborn ditch. I know your every rut. I know which stones roll and which ones only pretend to. I know the pothole at the third mile that broke an axle in the spring of—"*

He faltered. The year slipped sideways in his mind, the way everything had been slipping since the stars went out, dates going soft as overripe fruit. He grabbed the memory instead of the date.

"—the spring the axle broke. I know it. So it knows itself. *Hold.*"

Silence.

Then, under his palm: a tremor. Small, grudging, like an old dog deciding to get up because the voice calling it was familiar. The grayness shivered. The almost-ruts deepened into ruts. The almost-stones firmed into stones with opinions about ankles. Color came back into the ground the way blood comes back into a hand you've been sitting on—painfully, gradually, all at once.

Nine miles of road, remembering itself one oath at a time.

Jude stayed kneeling a moment longer than dignity required, because his eyes were doing something he preferred not to have witnessed. Then he stood, brushed off his knee, and said, "There. Told you it was thinking."

Sera was staring at him with an expression he'd learn to dread over the following weeks: the look of a scholar watching her entire framework get picked up and used as a crowbar.

"That's not possible," she said.

"It just happened."

"That's not—wards are geometry, Dravenmoor. Wards are syllables of power laid in lattice patterns by trained scribes over decades. What you just did was *talk to dirt*."

"I talked to dirt *confidently*. There's a difference."

"And it worked?" She crouched, pressed her own hand to the restored roadbed, and Jude watched her feel the same tremor he had—that residual warmth, the sense of something recently alive. Her face did something complicated. "The catechism. The smugglers' oaths. You're telling me those were functional."

"They were never anything else. We just didn't know what function." He looked north, up the valley, toward Graysink and the passes beyond, all of it hanging by threads now that the wards were gone. "Doss used to say somebody had to hold the world up. I thought he was being poetic. Doss was many things but poetic wasn't one of them. He was being *literal*. Four hundred years of thieves swearing oaths over stolen goods, and every oath was a brick."

"In a wall."

"In the world, mostly." He smiled, and it hurt. "Might as well be paid."

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of the split oak, which had obligingly continued to exist, and Jude lay awake listening to the sounds a dying country makes: wind, mules, Anselm snoring in a key usually reserved for shipwrecks, and underneath it all a faint wrongness, like a song missing every fourth note.

Hollow was silent. Had been silent since the stars went out. But the silence had a texture he kept examining, the way you probe a tooth. It didn't feel like absence. Absence is smooth. This silence had edges, corners, a held breath. It felt less like a dead god than a god standing very still behind a door, listening.

Or it felt like nothing at all, and he was a grieving man hearing shapes in the dark. Both possibilities were unbearable, so he alternated.

Near midnight, Sera sat down across the low fire without asking, which he was learning was her way.

"The road held," she said.

"For now. Nine miles. Ask me about mile ten tomorrow."

"How long does it last? The remembering."

"Doss claimed his oaths held passes open for a generation after he died. But Doss also claimed he'd stolen the moon and returned it out of pity, so." Jude fed a stick to the fire. "Here's what worries me, Vey. One road, one oath, one night—it held fine. But there are hundreds of roads. Thousands. And I'm one man with one memory, and I could feel it tonight, just a little, just at the edges—when I gave the road my remembering, it took some. Like paying a toll."

"Took some of what?"

"Doesn't matter yet."

"Dravenmoor."

"It matters when it matters." He rolled onto his side, facing the dark. "Get some sleep. Tomorrow we walk nine remembered miles to the Tarnish ford, and after that we go north to Graysink, because if anyone else out there is still holding stitches, it'll be the mining towns. Miners swear worse oaths than smugglers. It's practically their religion already."

Sera looked at him for a long moment across the fire. When she finally spoke, her voice had dropped its scholarly armor entirely, and what was underneath was younger and much more frightened.

"When I find whatever is eating this country," she said, "I'm going to need someone who knows the roads it hasn't eaten yet."

"Lucky for you," Jude said, "I know the roads it hasn't eaten yet."

"Do you? All of them?"

"No." He closed his eyes. "But I know where the list is written."

It was, he reflected as the fire burned low, technically true. The list was written in the only archive that had never failed a smuggler: the catechism itself, every oath, every verse, every ridiculous funeral-day litany Doss had beaten into him over ten years of profitable crime. Somewhere in those profane old prayers was the map of everything worth keeping.

He just had to hope he could read faster than the dark could erase.

And he hoped it with the particular intensity of a man who had not yet noticed the small blank spot in his memory where the year of the broken axle used to be—or the smaller one beside it, where his mother's birthday had lived, quiet and unremarked, for thirty-one years.

The road held. The toll had begun.
