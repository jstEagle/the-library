## 4. What Sera Buried

Sera Vey had rehearsed the confession eleven times.

She knew this because she had written it out—eleven drafts, each one shorter than the last, each one less true. The first draft ran four pages and blamed the weather, the Wardens' College, her dead mentor, and a childhood fever that had allegedly damaged her sense of civic duty. The eleventh draft was a single line: *I did it on purpose.*

She had burned all eleven in the Bellharrow chapel's brazier, which no longer warmed anyone because the chapel no longer believed in fire, exactly—it believed in the memory of fire, which gave off a light like late afternoon in autumn and no heat at all. Sera had watched her confessions curl and vanish and thought: *that's what Thornfell is doing to itself.* Then she had felt sick, and then she had gone to find the mercenary.

Jude Dravenmoor was in the tavern's back room, sharpening a knife with the patience of a man who had nowhere better to be and no intention of admitting it. The tavern keeper had given him the room for free, mostly because nobody could remember whether they'd ever charged for rooms, and charging now seemed presumptuous.

"You look," Jude said without looking up, "like someone about to ruin my evening."

"I'm going to ruin considerably more than your evening." Sera sat down across from him, uninvited, because if she waited to be invited she would lose her nerve, and she had already lost everything else this week. "I need to tell you something before you go any further north with those people. And I need you to not kill me, because I'm told I'm useful."

Jude set down the knife. He had the kind of face that made silence feel like an interrogation lamp. "Talk."

* * *

Here is what Sera Vey did, nine years ago, when she was twenty-four and brilliant and furious:

She built wards. That was her whole life, then—the Warden's College in Corveth, the capital that may or may not still exist, where she had been the youngest ward-scribe admitted in a generation. She was good at it in the way that some people are good at music: she could hear the flaws. A boundary stone would hum a certain sour note under her palm and she'd know, before any inspection, that its anchoring verse had been copied wrong in 1603.

The border wards of Thornfell were her masterpiece. Forty-one anchor points, a lattice of spoken law running from the Saltmere cliffs to the Greymarch passes, woven so tight that nothing without a name could cross it. She had worked three years on the final harmonics. She had slept in the anchor vaults. She had, on one memorable occasion, argued the Arch-Warden into apoplexy over a syllable.

And then, on the night the lattice was sealed, she hid a flaw in it.

Not a door. Not a gap. Nothing so crude. She introduced a single dissonance—a half-tone off, buried in the seventh harmonic, invisible to every audit because she had designed the audits too. It was small. It was elegant. It was, she had told herself at the time, *harmless*. A pressure valve. A private joke between her and the lattice.

She had done it because three weeks earlier, the College had passed her over. The chief wardenship of the southern march went to Aldous Renk, a man whose sole qualifications were a famous father and the ability to laugh at the right volume during the Arch-Warden's anecdotes. Sera had built the thing. Renk would get to stand on it. So she left her little dissonance inside the great work, the way a mason leaves a thumbprint in wet mortar—not to break anything, only to prove the wall was hers.

"It wasn't even sabotage," she said to Jude now, and heard how pathetic it sounded, and kept talking anyway, because the alternative was stopping. "It was spite the way a cat scratches furniture. I wanted them to never quite be able to prove the wards were perfect. I wanted there to be one note in the song that was mine."

"And the devourer came through that note," Jude said.

"Through my note." Her hands were flat on the table, pressing down as though the room might slide. "Nine years it sat there doing nothing. Nine years I forgot it existed—I made myself forget, do you understand? I was so good at hiding it that I hid it from myself. And then something found it. Something patient enough to spend nine years listening to a country sing until it heard the one wrong note, and it pressed on that note like a bruise, and the whole lattice—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "The stars didn't go out, mercenary. They were *unwritten*. Everything since has come through the crack I made to annoy a man with a loud laugh."

The brazier-light flickered in the memory-of-fire way. Somewhere upstairs, a child was crying, or had been crying; in Bellharrow lately it was hard to tell if sounds were happening or merely recurring.

Jude picked up his knife and resumed sharpening it, which Sera realized, after a moment of outrage, was a kindness. It gave her somewhere to look that wasn't his eyes.

"Why tell me?" he asked.

"Because you're going north. Because everyone who goes north is walking deeper into the wound, and I am the reason there's a wound." She swallowed. "Because when the choir of Graysink prays tonight—and they will pray, they pray constantly, it's very wearing—you should know that the god they're mourning got in through me. If you're going to lead people, you deserve to know your guide is standing in a hole of her own digging."

"I'm not leading anyone yet."

"You will be. Men like you always are. It's a curse of the competent." She almost smiled. It didn't take. "There's more. There's a thing I have to give you, and after I give it to you, you're allowed to hate me properly."

* * *

The shard of ward-glass lived in a lead-lined case against Sera's sternum, on a cord, the way other people wore relics of saints. She had carried it out of the College the night the wards fell—had run, in fact, through corridors that were actively forgetting themselves, bookshelves going blank mid-title, portraits smoothing into anonymous faces, while behind her the great lattice sang its last chord with one note bent, one note hers, and broke.

She set the case on the table between them and opened it.

Inside lay a sliver of glass the size of a finger joint, greenish, uneven, with a hairline vein of gold running through it like a fault line through rock. Unremarkable. A piece of a broken window, you'd say, if you were being generous, or a broken bottle if you weren't.

"Hold it up to anything," Sera said. "Anything that's fading. Anything that's already gone."

Jude lifted it between two fingers, the way he handled everything—like it might be a trap, and like he'd walk into the trap anyway if it was interesting enough. He turned it toward the tavern wall.

The wall filled in.

Not all at once—in layers, like frost forming in reverse. First the plaster remembered its color, a deep Bellharrow blue that hadn't been fashionable in forty years. Then the whitewash over it, then the smoke stains above the hearth, then a shelf, then bottles on the shelf with labels in a hand Sera recognized as old Tam Wicker's, who had died in the Year of the Wet Harvest, and then—this was the part that made Jude go very still—a notch cut into the mantel beam, and beside the notch, scratched small: *J.D., aged 9, tall as this.*

Sera watched the mercenary read his own childhood off a tavern wall.

"That's what the wards were made of," she said softly. "Ward-glass. We grew it in the anchor vaults—crystal fed on recorded memory, census-verse, land-deeds, the testimony of the oldest living residents. Every boundary stone had a heart of it. The glass held what a place *was*, officially, so the borders could hold what a place was *legally*. When the lattice broke, most of the glass shattered with it. This is a piece of the southern march anchor. One of maybe six shards left in the country that I know of."

Jude lowered the shard slowly. The wall dimmed back to its uncertain present tense. "It shows what used to be."

"It shows what was *recorded*. Which isn't the same thing." She tapped the table. "That's important, mercenary, so listen. The glass doesn't show truth. It shows the version of truth that got written down. My grandmother's kitchen doesn't exist in it anywhere. Neither does the smell of rain on the Greymarch in summer, or whatever your mother sang to you. The glass holds the country's *official* memory. The ledgers. The maps. The names in the parish books." Her voice caught on the next part and she pushed through it. "Which means the devourer isn't eating Thornfell's soul. It's eating Thornfell's paperwork first. And a country that loses its paperwork loses the argument that it exists."

"Charming," Jude said. "You've brought me a haunted mirror and a lecture on bureaucracy."

"I've brought you the only tool in Thornfell that remembers where the roads went." She leaned forward. "You said you know smuggler's roads. Roads that aren't on any map—which means, mercenary, that they aren't in the glass either, and aren't in the devourer's appetite either, at least not yet. But the *old* roads, the king's roads, the post roads, the pilgrim ways—those are recorded. Those are in here." She touched the shard. "When we travel, I can hold this up and show you where the road used to run, even when the road itself has forgotten. Even when the road is currently a sheep pasture, or a rumor, or nothing at all."

Jude looked at the shard for a long time. Then at her.

"And what does it cost?"

There it was. The question of a man who had survived by assuming everything had a price. Sera respected it enormously and hated it completely.

"Nothing obvious," she admitted. "Though nothing in this country costs nothing anymore. Maybe it weighs something. Maybe remembering a place out loud makes the devourer notice you. I don't know. I'm telling you honestly: I built the greatest work of wardcraft in a century and I put a crack in it out of pettiness, so my judgment is perhaps not the instrument it once was."

For the first time that evening, Jude smiled. It was a small smile, crooked, gone quickly, but real. "You rehearsed this confession."

"Eleven times."

"The early drafts blame the weather?"

"How did you—"

"You've got the look of a woman who edited herself down to the truth. It's a rare look. Most people edit the other direction." He slid the shard across the table toward himself, then paused, fingers on it. "Why give it to me? You could keep it. Sell it, even, if there's still such a thing as selling."

Sera looked at the brazier, at its heatless faithful light.

"Because I can't carry it," she said. "Every time I hold it up, I see the southern march, and the southern march anchor was mine, and I see it holding, holding, holding for nine years, and then—" She made herself stop, breathe, finish. "A penance you enjoy isn't a penance. Carrying that glass would be my way of feeling punished, which is a luxury. Giving it to someone who'll *use* it to save people—that's actually unpleasant. That's the one I want."

"That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me in this country."

"Don't get used to it. I'm told honesty is going extinct here."

* * *

They left Bellharrow two days later, northward, toward Graysink, because the mining towns had gone silent in a way that frightened even the smugglers, and because Sera had a theory she hadn't shared yet: that places full of repetitive labor—mines, mills, saltworks—held their memories longer, worn into the workers' hands like grooves into stair-stones. If anything of Thornfell was going to survive in muscle rather than paper, it would be there.

On the second morning out, the road simply stopped.

Not blocked. Not washed out. Stopped—one step it was packed earth and wheel-ruts, and the next step it was meadow, unbroken green, with no seam, as though the road had never been and the grass had always been having this particular day. Behind them, the carters muttered. Ahead, the scouts stood in the grass looking foolish.

Sera climbed down from the wagon, took out the shard, and held it up.

The road came back—but only as an image, a ghost laid over the meadow: two ruts and a drainage ditch and a milestone reading GREYSINK, 14 LEAGUES, in lettering she recognized from the survey of 1587. The actual road stayed absent. The meadow stayed smug.

"There," she said, pointing along the ghost-line. "Fourteen leagues, bearing north-northeast. The road's been eaten but the record of it hasn't. Yet."

Jude studied the shard's projection, then the meadow, then her. "Can you keep that up for fourteen leagues?"

"No. The glass draws on what's stored in it, and it's a sliver, not a stone. An hour at a stretch, maybe two, and then it needs to rest—or rather, I do. Holding a place's shape in your head is heavy work. Ask any exile." She was quiet a moment. "Ask me."

He nodded, and started giving orders in that low carrying voice of his, and the caravan turned to follow a road that existed only because a guilty woman remembered it out of a piece of broken glass.

It was working. That was the terrible thing. Her spite-note had let the wolf in, and now her stolen sheepdog was helping herd the flock out the other side, and Sera Vey did not know what to do with a universe that permitted that kind of arithmetic.

That night, camped under a sky with no stars in it—just a smooth dark, like a page with the writing scraped off—she took out her journal and wrote a twelfth draft of the confession. Not to burn. To keep. One line:

*I broke it. Now I help carry it.*

She was blowing out the candle when Jude appeared at the edge of her firelight, expression odd.

"You should sleep," she said.

"In Graysink," he said, "there are people singing. The scouts heard it from the ridge—dozens of voices, hymns, all night, every night. Singing to keep their names, the priest says." He looked at her. "You built the wards, Vey. Is singing a ward?"

Sera thought of the lattice, of harmonics, of one bent note hidden in a beautiful chord, and felt the guilt turn over in her chest like a stone in a streambed.

"No," she said slowly. "But it might be the same material. Memory, set to rhythm, spoken together." She met his eyes. "Or it might be bait. I genuinely don't know which, and I think we're about to find out."

Above the black hills, in the direction of Graysink, the darkness seemed—very faintly—to be keeping time.
