## 5. The Hollow Choir

The road down into Graysink was the first thing in three days that Jude trusted.

Not because it was good — it was a scar of slag and frozen mud switchbacking down the throat of a valley that smelled of old coal fires and older despair — but because he *knew* it. He had walked it eleven times in his smuggling years, hauling uncut garnet and untaxed brandy past the assessor's station at the ridge, and his feet remembered every loose stone before his eyes found them. In a country where the maps had gone blank and the boundary stones had gone smooth, a road that lived in your own two boots was worth more than any charter.

"You're smiling," said Doss, walking beside him with a sack of turnips over one shoulder and an expression of permanent grievance on his face. "I'd like to note for the record that you're smiling."

"I'm not."

"Your mouth is doing a thing. A smug thing."

"It's called relief, Doss. You might try it sometime."

"Relief." Doss shifted the turnips. "We're thirty-one people walking into a mining town at the bottom of a hole, following a man who talks to a god that's stopped answering, and you're relieved."

Jude didn't answer, because the honest answer was *yes*, and the honest answer would have sounded mad even to him. But it was true. For three days they had been crossing country that was coming apart at the seams — a village whose name nobody could recall, a river that flowed uphill until Sera Vey made them all look away from it — and here, at last, was something solid underfoot. Something his memory could vouch for.

He should have known better than to trust that feeling. Everything else he'd trusted lately had turned out to be a lie wearing comfortable boots.

* * *

Graysink announced itself by sound before sight: a low, rolling hum rising out of the valley floor like a hive thinking hard thoughts. Not machinery. The mine had been silent since the war, everyone knew that. It took Jude a while, picking his way down the last switchback with the survivors strung out behind him like beads on a fraying cord, to place what he was hearing.

Singing. Dozens of voices, unhurried, unlovely, singing the same few notes over and over the way water wears a groove in stone.

The town itself sat in the valley's cup like a dropped coin: slate roofs, soot-blackened walls, a headframe looming over everything with its cable cut and dangling. And filling the square below the headframe — packed shoulder to shoulder, breath steaming in the cold — were people. Hundreds of them. More people than Jude had seen since the night the stars went out, all of them standing very still and singing.

They weren't singing *to* anything. That was the first thing he noticed, and it raised the hair on his arms. There was no altar. No idol, no icon, no painted eye of Hollow watching from a wall the way it watched in every shrine-house from here to the coast. They sang into the empty air above the square as if the emptiness itself were the god, and — Jude's stomach turned slowly over — perhaps it was. Perhaps that was exactly what they were doing.

"They're keeping their names," Sera Vey said quietly, beside him. She had her ward-scribe's satchel clutched to her chest like a shield. "Listen. Listen to the verses."

He listened. Under the drone there were words, worn nearly smooth by repetition:

*I am Marta, I am Marta, I have hands and I have kin,*
*I am Osk, I am Osk, I remember where I've been...*

Each singer was singing their own verse. Their own name, stitched into the same simple tune, over and over, a thread of self repeated until it couldn't fray.

"They're afraid they'll forget themselves," Sera said. "Like the roads. Like the borders. It's not just places it eats. It's—" she stopped, jaw tight. "It's whatever makes a thing that thing."

Jude stood at the edge of the square with thirty-one hungry, frightened people behind him, and listened to a town sing itself into existence over and over, and felt the weight of the silence above them settle onto his own shoulders like wet wool. Because he knew that silence. He carried it everywhere now, behind his sternum, where a god used to live.

Hollow had never asked for hymns. Hollow had been the god of smugglers, of oath-keepers, of things carried quietly through the dark — a god you spoke to by remembering, not by singing. But these people had lost something too, and they had built this out of the wreckage, and looking at their upturned faces Jude thought: *they are praying to the same absence I carry.* They just didn't know whose absence it was.

A child saw them first. A girl of maybe eight, perched on a barrel at the square's edge, who stopped mid-verse — *I am Pell, I am Pell* — and stared at the ragged column coming down off the mountain. Her song died. Then her mother's died. Then, verse by verse, voice by voice, the whole choir unraveled into silence, hundreds of faces turning toward the strangers with the slow, wary gravity of a field of sunflowers tracking something that might be either sun or storm.

Into that silence, Doss leaned close to Jude and whispered, "I want it noted that I suggested we go around."

"Noted," Jude said, and stepped forward into the square.

* * *

They came at the strangers the way miners do everything — carefully, in shifts, with someone always watching the roof. First a delegation of four with pick-handles held loosely, apologetically, the way you hold a tool you hope not to use. Then more, circling. Questions thrown like stones:

"Where from?" — "Up the pass." — "What pass?" — and here Jude hesitated, because when he reached for the pass's name he found a smooth blank place where the name should be, and had to say only, "North. We came from the north," and watch the questioners exchange looks that said *the north is gone, then.*

But they let the survivors into the square. They shared what they had — barley broth, black bread dense enough to caulk a boat, a kind of preserved cabbage that Doss ate with the expression of a man settling a personal score. The Graysink folk asked little and volunteered less, but their eyes kept going to Jude. To his hands, specifically. He couldn't work out why until an old woman grabbed his wrist without asking, turned his palm up to the grey light, and looked at the faded ink there — the small coiled mark between thumb and forefinger that every sworn carrier of Hollow wore, the oath-sign, the hollow circle open at the top.

She let go of his arm as if it had burned her and shouted across the square, in a voice cracked as river ice:

"He's marked! He's god-sworn!"

The word went around the square faster than fire through a hayrick. *God-sworn. God-sworn.* People pressed in — not hostile, which was somehow worse — touching his sleeves, his shoulders, holding their children up to see him. And above the crowd, from the steps of the chapel-house, came a new voice, deep and calm as a bell struck underwater:

"Let him breathe, all of you. He'll not vanish for your looking. Though stars know," a dry pause, "we've less cause for confidence in that than we once had."

Laughter, thin but real, moved through the crowd. And the crowd opened, and the priest came down the steps.

He was blind — Jude saw it at once, the eyes gone white as unmarked paper — a big man, heavyset, moving with a staff and the total unhesitating certainty of someone who has mapped his world by echo and refuses to apologize for it. Grey beard, chapel robes gone the color of coal dust, and around his neck, on a leather cord, a small stone pendant carved with the open circle.

He stopped in front of Jude, tilted his head, and did something no one had done since the night the stars went out.

He looked *at* him. Straight at him, blind eyes and all, with such fierce attention that Jude felt seen down to the studs.

"There you are," the priest said softly. "I wondered when you'd come."

"You have me mistaken for someone else, father. I'm nobody. Just a caravan master."

"Mm." The priest smiled. "And I'm just a man who's never seen a sunrise. We're both lying, but mine's politer." He extended a hand; Jude took it; the grip was a smith's. "Father Anselm. And you are the god-sworn, whether you claim it or not. I can hear it in how you stand. A man who's spent years listening for footsteps that aren't there stands a particular way. You stand that way."

"That's called being a smuggler."

"That's called being *chosen*," Anselm said, gentle and immovable as bedrock, "and we will argue about it inside, over tea, because you look like a man who hasn't had tea since the sky broke. Bring your people. All of them. Even the one eating our entire store of cabbage."

Doss froze mid-bite. "It was offered."

"It was *defended*," Anselm said, "which is different," and the crowd laughed again, and Jude understood that this man ran Graysink the way a keel runs a boat — invisibly, structurally, without ever raising his voice.

* * *

The chapel-house was warm and smelled of tallow and slate dust. Its interior told the story of the town's grief better than any sermon: the shrine alcove at the far end stood empty. Not stripped — *empty*, swept clean, a pedestal bare except for a ring of pale dust where something round had rested for generations. Someone had set a single candle before the nothing, and it burned steadily, patiently, illuminating the absence the way a lighthouse illuminates a reef.

"We woke the morning after the stars went out," Anselm said, pouring tea into cups that had seen better decades, "and the icon was gone. Not stolen — the door was barred from inside, and old Toma slept across the threshold with his dog, and neither heard a thing. Just gone, and the dust ring left behind, like a coin lifted off a table." He handed Jude a cup. "The others wanted to wail and rend. I told them: no. If the god has left the building, then the building was never the point. So we sing instead. Every morning, every dusk. Names in, names out. As long as we can say who we are, we are."

"It's working?" Sera asked. She'd taken the chair nearest the empty alcove, and hadn't stopped looking at it.

Anselm's face did something complicated. "Mostly. We've lost three. Old Brannick woke one day and answered to nothing, sat by the window waiting for a wife none of us can find any record of. Little Tam — a boy — forgot his own name in a single afternoon, and no amount of singing put it back. And Widow Corren walked out the north gate ten days ago saying she was going home, and there is no home north of here anymore, and she knew it, and she went anyway." He set down the teapot with great care, as if it might break, or as if he might. "So mostly. Mostly is the best anyone's offering these days."

The tea was terrible. Jude drank it anyway, and felt it do battle on his behalf.

Then Anselm turned those white eyes on him fully, and the warmth in the room seemed to lean back and give them space.

"Now," the priest said. "Tell me what happened to him. To Hollow. And don't tell me 'nothing,' because I've been his priest for forty years and I know the sound of a full silence and the sound of an empty one, and the silence over this whole country since that night is *empty*. Something's eaten, or something's fled, or something's dead. Which?"

Jude looked into his cup. Around the room, his thirty-one survivors had gone quiet, listening. Even Doss had stopped defending himself against the cabbage.

"I felt it happen," Jude said finally. "Midnight, five nights back. I was guiding a caravan through the high passes, and I felt him go quiet — not sleeping, not distant. *Gone*. Like a hand letting go. And at the same moment every star over Thornfell went out at once, and the border wards behind us shattered, and I have spent every hour since trying not to think about what those two facts mean together." He looked up. "I don't know if he's dead, father. I know he's silent. In my trade, those are usually the same thing."

Anselm absorbed this in silence. His big hands lay flat on his knees, and Jude watched the knuckles whiten slowly, the only crack in the man's composure, grief showing through like ore in a seam.

"Dead," Anselm said at last, testing the word. "Or devoured. Or—" he shook his head. "It doesn't matter. Here's what matters." He leaned forward. "You felt him die, and you're still standing, still marked, still *here*. Do you understand what that means? Everyone else who was sworn — the carriers, the oath-keepers — we've heard nothing from any of them. The roads are forgetting themselves. The towns are forgetting their names. But you walked out of the mountains carrying thirty-one souls, and the road let you. The dark let you." His voice dropped, went rough. "Whatever Hollow was, some part of him stayed with you. Enough. Enough to steer by."

"Father—"

"Lead us out."

The words landed in the room like a dropped ingot.

"Graysink has food for six weeks and a mine shaft we can't eat," Anselm went on, relentless, quiet. "The south road's already gone soft — men walk it and arrive somewhere that isn't anywhere. The choir sings, and it holds us together, but it won't hold the *town* together much longer, and it won't hold at all come deep winter. You know the old ways. The smuggler's roads, the ones that run under the skin of the map. If anyone alive can take three hundred souls out of a country that's being unwritten, it's the man its god loved enough to leave his mark on."

Three hundred. Jude's mouth had gone dry. Behind Anselm, the candle before the empty alcove guttered and steadied, guttered and steadied, as if something were breathing near it.

"Father Anselm," Jude said carefully, "I need you to hear me. I'm not salvation. I'm a man who knows some roads, and the roads are failing, and I don't know why mine still hold. I'm not — I'm not what you think I am."

"No," Anselm agreed placidly. "You're almost certainly not. People never are. Moses was a murderer with a stammer. My predecessor was a drunkard who could recite the liturgy flawlessly only when drunk. Holiness arrives in whatever vessel happens to be standing nearby." He smiled, and it was a kind smile, and that made it unbearable. "I'm not asking you to be what I think you are. I'm asking you to be what you actually are: the only man in Thornfell the darkness still answers. Lead us out, god-sworn. That's all. Just that."

The silence stretched. Sera was watching Jude with an expression he couldn't read — pity, or warning, or both. Doss had developed a sudden fascination with the floor.

And Jude felt it settle onto him, the weight of three hundred upturned faces in a square below, the weight of a dead god's open circle inked into his hand, the weight of a title he hadn't asked for and couldn't honestly refuse — because the terrible truth, the truth he'd admit to no one, was that part of him *wanted* it. Part of him had been walking since the stars went out hoping someone would mistake him for salvation, just so he'd have a reason to keep going.

"One condition," Jude said at last.

"Name it."

"We tell them the truth about what I am. Not the legend. The man. If they follow me knowing I'm only a smuggler with a good memory, they follow freely. I won't build a church on top of a lie. I've seen where that road goes."

Something flickered across Anselm's blind face — quick, unreadable, gone — and then he laughed, a great rolling laugh that shook the teacups.

"A church built on truth! What a novel heresy." He gripped Jude's forearm, hard, sealing it. "Done. We leave when?"

Jude opened his mouth to say *give me a day to plan the route* — and stopped. Because he had reached, automatically, for the map in his head, the smuggler's atlas he'd carried for twenty years, the network of hidden ways radiating out of Graysink like veins from a heart —

— and found that he could only see one of them clearly. The Garnet Road, south-by-west, the one he'd walked eleven times. Every other way out of the valley had gone soft in his mind, edges blurred, distances uncertain, like paths seen through frost.

One road. Three hundred souls. And a cold voice in the back of his skull, quiet as the space where a god used to be, whispering the arithmetic he didn't yet understand: *every step you remember costs you something. The question is what.*

"Tomorrow," Jude said. "At dawn. And Father — pray it stays cold. We'll need the frost to hold the road."

"The frost," Anselm said, "is the least of what's coming. But yes. I'll pray."
