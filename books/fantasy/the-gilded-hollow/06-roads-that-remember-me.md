## 6. Roads That Remember Me

The first thing you learned on the smuggler's roads—and Hollow had made sure Jude learned it early, back when he was all elbows and bad arithmetic—was that a road is not a thing. A road is an agreement. The mountain agrees to have a path worn into it because enough feet have insisted, over enough years, that the path exists. Take away the insistence and the mountain goes back to being merely a mountain, indifferent as a sleeping ox.

Jude had never thought about it in those words before Graysink. Before the choir.

Now he had thirty-one people behind him—thirty-two counting himself, though some mornings he wasn't sure he counted—and every one of them was watching his boots to see where reality ended. Old Marta with her hymnbook missing half its pages. The twins, Pip and Perrin, who had been miners for eleven years and could no longer agree on which of them was older. Brother Callow, blind since birth, who navigated by sound and by faith in roughly equal measure, and who had appointed himself the choir's official optimist, a job nobody had asked him to do and nobody had the heart to take away.

"Left at the split birch," Jude said, and stopped.

There was no split birch. There was a birch, whole and smug about it, standing in the middle of what should have been a fork. He remembered the fork. He remembered it the way you remember your own teeth: without effort, without gratitude. Two trunks grown together at the base and splitting like a pair of arguing brothers, one leaning east over the drop, one leaning west into the rock. He'd hidden a bolt of stolen velvet under its roots when he was nineteen. He'd pissed against it more times than he could count, which was the truest form of devotion a smuggler ever paid a landmark.

But there was only one trunk now, and the road beyond it was soft at the edges, the way bread goes when it's been left out too long. Not gone. *Unfinished.* The kind of road you could walk down if you believed hard enough, and fall off of if you didn't.

"Brother Dravenmoor?" Marta's voice, careful. She'd taken to calling him *brother* after Graysink, and he'd given up correcting her. Correcting her would have meant explaining what he actually was—a mercenary who'd sworn oaths over stolen goods to a god that had recently been eaten—and explanations cost something these days. Everything cost something these days.

"Road's tired," he said. "We'll wake it up."

He did what he'd done at the fading stretch outside Bellharrow, what Sera Vey had watched him do with her scribe's eyes narrowed like she was taking dictation from the universe: he walked to the head of the column, closed his eyes, and *remembered*.

Not prayed. Hollow was silent; praying to silence was like posting letters into a fire. But the old catechism had never really been prayer anyway. That was the discovery that had kept him up two nights running, staring at the chapel ceiling while the choir slept around him like survivors of a shipwreck—which, he supposed, they were. The smuggler's rites were memory-rituals. You named the road. You named who'd cut it, who'd died on it, who'd kissed whom behind which boulder. You gave the road its history back, and a road with a history was a road that existed. Hollow hadn't been listening to those prayers so much as *archiving* them. And now the archive was ash, and the only archive left was the one between Jude's ears.

"The Split Birch," he said aloud, voice steady, hands not. "Cut in the year the Grey Duke taxed candlelight. Old Fen Harrow widened the east path with a mule and a grudge. Tam Osker fell here in '61—broke his ankle, cursed the tree, married the herb-woman who set it. There's velvet under the roots worth forty crowns and I never told a soul."

A ripple went through the choir—not laughter exactly, but the shape of it, the ghost of laughter, which was more than any of them had managed in days. Pip whispered *forty crowns* to Perrin with reverence.

And the birch split.

It didn't grow. It *remembered*, the way a scar remembers being a wound—one moment single-trunked, the next forked and leaning east and west, roots knuckling up through the frost-hard ground, and beneath them, faintly, absurdly, the smell of old velvet. The east path firmed up like a muscle flexing. The soft edges pulled taut.

"There," Jude said, and hated how pleased he sounded. "Left at the split birch."

They walked. The road held.

* * *

It held for four days. Then it started costing.

He noticed it first with small things. The name of the innkeeper at the Drowned Lamb—he'd known that man's name the way he knew his own, had drunk with him, had helped bury him—slipped away somewhere between the second and third mile of the Millward track. Gone clean, no loose thread to pull. Then the taste of a particular plum brandy, the good stuff, the stuff old Vess used to water for tourists and keep honest for friends. He could remember that there had been a taste. He couldn't remember the taste. It was like reaching into your pocket for a coin and finding the pocket sewn shut.

On the fifth day he sat down across from Sera Vey at the evening fire and said, "Ask me something I should know."

Sera looked up from the ward-glass shard she carried everywhere now, turning it in her fingers like a worry-stone. "What?"

"Anything. Something small. Something I'd know."

She considered him. She had a scribe's face, all angles and ink stains, and she wore guilt the way other people wore cloaks—constantly, and slightly wrong for the weather. "What color were the doors of the Graysink mine office?"

"Green." Easy. "Peeling green, brass fittings, someone had scratched *MAGGIE WAS HERE* into the left one at shoulder height." He paused. "What color were the doors of the Graysink mine office?"

"You just said green."

"I know what I just said. Say it again."

"Green," she said slowly. "Jude—"

"Ask me tomorrow." He held up a hand before she could speak. "No. Don't ask me anything tomorrow. Or the day after. Just—" He looked into the fire, and the fire looked back with the particular indifference of fire. "I think I'm spending myself. Like coin. Every time I make a road hold, I pay for it out of my own pocket, and I don't get to choose which coins."

Sera was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had the careful flatness of someone trying very hard not to be right about something terrible. "Show me your hands."

"They're fine."

"*Show me.*"

He showed her. Nothing. Of course nothing. But she turned them over anyway, palms up, a scribe looking for ink, and then she did something worse: she took out her little book—the one she'd been keeping since Bellharrow, the ledger of what Thornfell was losing—and wrote something in it without letting him see.

"What are you writing?"

"A list," she said. "Of things you've mentioned twice."

* * *

The arithmetic got worse before it got better, because arithmetic always does.

By the eighth day he understood the exchange rate. A minor road—a goat track, a shortcut, a path he'd used a handful of times—cost trivia. An innkeeper's name. A card game's rules. The second verse of a song he'd pretended to hate and secretly loved. A major road, one of the deep arteries of the smuggler's country, the ones worn smooth by three generations of quiet crime, cost something with weight to it. The smell of his first campfire as a free boy. The sound of his father laughing, which had been rare enough to be precious and was now, he realized with a lurch, entirely gone—he could remember that his father had laughed, once, at a wedding, but the sound itself had been spent somewhere on the High Fells without his permission.

"That's theft," Marta said, when he finally told the choir's inner circle what was happening. They'd gathered in the lee of a ruined tollhouse, and he'd laid it out plainly because lying to these people had begun to feel like urinating in a well. "That's robbery, is what it is. You can't spend yourself for us."

"It's already happening," Jude said. "The question is whether it happens on purpose or whether I dribble away piecemeal making bad decisions. I'd rather be robbed deliberately. At least then I pick the thief."

"You could stop," said Perrin—or Pip; the twins' faces had begun to trade features in Jude's memory, another small leak he chose not to examine. "Rest. Let the roads go. We find another way."

"There is no other way. The king's roads are soup. The wards are broken. Everything south of here is being *edited*." He heard his own voice climb and brought it back down. "Look. I'm not asking for sympathy. I'm telling you the price of bread so nobody's surprised at the till."

Brother Callow, who had been sitting with his face tilted toward the fire the way he did, listening to it the way sighted people listened to music, spoke up. "What have you lost so far? Truly lost, not misplaced."

Jude started to deflect—it was reflex, older than his oath—and then stopped, because Callow was blind and therefore the one person alive who couldn't be lied to with a face. "My father's laugh. The taste of plum brandy. The name of the best innkeeper I ever knew. The second verse of *The Miller's Daughter*, which I'm honestly less upset about than I expected." He rubbed his jaw. "And I think part of a winter. My twelfth winter, maybe. There's a hole where it should be, like a tooth knocked out of a row."

"And you'd pay more," Callow said. Not a question.

"To keep thirty-one people walking," Jude said. "Yes."

Callow nodded slowly, and then smiled, and it was the worst thing, because Callow's smile was entirely without pity. "You know what we call that, in the choir? We call that a tithe."

"I don't want a title."

"A *tithe*. T-I-T-H-E. What you give the god." His smile widened. "Except you're the god now, brother. Congratulations. Worst promotion in the world."

Pip snorted. Even Marta's mouth twitched. And Jude felt something in his chest loosen half a turn—not hope exactly, but the scaffolding hope stands on. Humor. As long as they could still laugh at him, they weren't beaten.

"All right," he said. "All right. Here's how we do this. From now on, I don't spend anything without saying it out loud first. If I'm going to lose a memory, this choir is going to hear the price quoted, like honest merchants. No surprises. And if anyone ever thinks a road isn't worth what it costs, you say so, and we argue about it like civilized criminals."

"And if you refuse to quote a price?" Sera asked quietly. "If you decide something's worth it and just... pay?"

Jude looked at her. She was watching him with those ink-stained eyes, and he understood suddenly that she wasn't asking about him. She was asking about herself, about the crack she'd hidden in the wards, about the price she'd paid in secret and what it had bought. Guilt seeking redemption, wearing a practical coat.

"Then you write it down," he said. "In your book. So somebody remembers I had it, even if I don't."

* * *

The Coldmarch road was the one that broke the bank.

It came on the eleventh day, at the pass above the frozen lake, where the map in Jude's head showed a switchback trail descending through a notch in the ridge—the only way north that didn't cross open country, and open country, these days, was where the Gilded Hollow liked to stand and smile at people. The notch was real. He could feel it in his knees, the old familiar ache of that descent, stone steps cut by nobody official, steep enough that you rode your pack down it sideways.

But when he stood at the top and reached for the memory—the actual texture of it, the sequence of turns, the places where the footing lied—it came up thin. Worn. A road he'd traveled maybe six times, twenty years ago, and never bothered to love. He'd been young and in a hurry and he'd treated the Coldmarch like a tool instead of a place, and tools don't leave impressions. Places do.

Behind him, thirty-one people waited in the wind. Ahead, the light was doing something wrong at the edges of the valley, a gilded shimmer, patient as a spider. Below, the notch was a suggestion, a rumor, a road that existed only because a younger Jude had once hurried down it with stolen lace in his pack and hadn't thought to memorize the sky.

He could feel what it would cost to force it. That was the horrible new clarity of it—now that he'd started quoting prices, the market had gotten honest. To hold the Coldmarch, he'd have to feed it something dense. Something load-bearing. Trivia wouldn't cover it. The road wanted a foundation stone.

He stood there a long time. Long enough that Marta started forward and Sera caught her arm.

His mother's face.

He hadn't thought of it as currency. Nobody does. It was just *there*, the way the sun is there, the oldest fixed point in his personal sky. Her face in the kitchen doorway, flour on her knuckles, one eyebrow up—the eyebrow that meant *I know it was you, Jude, and I'm deciding whether to be angry or amused, and you should pray for amused.* He could see it now, vivid as the fire last night. He could see it, and he could feel the road below pulling at it like a tide pulling at a sandcastle, and he understood with perfect, sickening clarity that it was the only coin in his pocket heavy enough.

Thirty-one people. One face.

"Do the sums," he muttered to himself. "You're good at sums."

He wasn't. He'd never been good at sums. That was why he'd become a smuggler instead of a merchant.

"Brother Dravenmoor." Callow's voice, close—he hadn't heard the blind man come up beside him, or maybe Callow had simply always been there. "The road's asking, isn't it. I can hear it in your breathing."

"Callow—"

"Don't tell me what it costs." The priest's milky eyes found him with unnerving accuracy. "Or do. Whichever keeps you honest. But hear an old fool first." He tapped his own chest. "I have never seen my mother's face. Born without the eyes for it. And I have missed it every day of my life, the way you miss a country you were never allowed to enter." He smiled, gentle as snowfall. "So I know both sides of this bargain better than anyone living. And I'm telling you: a face held in the heart is worth more than a road held in the feet. But a heart that lets thirty-one people freeze to protect one picture—that heart becomes a museum. Beautiful. Uninhabited."

"That's a terrible argument," Jude said. His voice cracked in the middle of it. "That's a genuinely terrible argument and I hate it."

"Yes," Callow agreed placidly. "The worst ones usually are."

Jude closed his eyes.

He did it properly, the way he'd done everything since the stars went out—out loud, at market rates, no secrets. "Coldmarch road," he said, and the wind hushed to listen. "Cut by nobody, kept by everybody. Six times I went down you, hurrying, ungrateful. Young Jude Dravenmoor, nineteen years old, lace in his pack, fear in his boots. Here is what I remember, and here is what it costs."

He gave the road the kitchen doorway. The flour on her knuckles. The raised eyebrow. The way she'd said his name—*Jude*—with the *d* softened almost to nothing, a word she'd worn smooth as a river stone from saying it ten thousand times, mostly in exasperation. He fed it all into the notch below, every detail, holding nothing back because holding something back would just mean paying interest later, and he poured it down the mountainside like a man emptying his purse into a beggar's bowl, and the beggar was a road, and the road *drank*.

The switchback bloomed. Stone steps hardened out of scree, the notch deepened into shelter, the treacherous third turn grew a handhold of living root exactly where a hand would want it. The road remembered itself because he had remembered it, and the bill cleared the instant the service rendered.

He opened his eyes.

He could still see the kitchen. He could still see the doorway, the flour, the light. What was gone was—her. The face inside the scene had gone soft-focus, a portrait left out in the rain. He knew the eyebrow had gone up. He could not, for the life of him, make it go up again. He knew her name was Brenna. He could not find the mouth that had said it.

"Well?" Sera had come up beside him. In her hand, unasked, was her little book, already open, already waiting. "Quote the price."

"My mother's face," Jude said. It came out level, which surprised him. Maybe the shock was still in transit. "Scene intact. Face redacted."

Sera wrote it down. Her pen shook, and he loved her a little for letting it shake instead of hiding it.

Then he turned, raised his voice to carry down the column of thirty-one ragged, freezing, stubborn survivors, and pointed his staff at the notch.

"Single file!" he called. "Hand on the root at the third turn! And somebody sing—something with a second verse, I won't know the difference."

The laughter that came back up the pass was thin and cold-breathed and absolutely genuine, and it followed them down the switchback like a lantern.

* * *

They made the valley floor by dusk, and camped in the ruins of a shepherd's bothy that still, mercifully, believed in having a roof. Fires were lit. Barley was boiled. Pip and Perrin argued about whose turn it was to stir, a dispute with no possible resolution since neither of them could prove which twin he was, and everyone let it run purely for the entertainment.

Jude sat apart, the way he'd started sitting apart, and took inventory. It was becoming a nightly ritual, this audit of the self, and he performed it with a trader's discipline: what's in the warehouse, what's been sold, what's been stolen. Father's laugh—gone. Plum brandy—gone. Innkeeper's name—gone. Second verse—gone, and good riddance. Twelfth winter—holed. Mother's face—redacted, scene preserved.

He tried, one last time, in the privacy of his own skull, to raise the eyebrow.

Nothing. Just the doorway, and the flour, and a warmth where a woman should be, shaped exactly like a woman, detailed exactly like a woman, and utterly, catastrophically anonymous. He had traded the face and kept the love, and he could not decide if that was the cruelest possible outcome or the kindest.

"Jude."

Sera, at his elbow, wrapped in two blankets and holding out a tin cup of barley tea like a peace offering to a war criminal. He took it.

"You should sleep," she said.

"In a minute." He drank. The tea tasted like hot disappointment, which meant it was normal tea. "Sera. Question. Professional to professional."

"Mm."

"When you built the wards—you told me you hid a flaw. Your crack. Your spite." He kept his eyes on the fire. "Did you know the price? When you did it? Did you know what it would cost, someday, and pay it anyway in secret?"

She was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice. When she answered, her voice was very small for such a large confession.

"I didn't even know there'd be a bill," she said. "I just signed my name and walked away."

"Ah," said Jude. "Well. Between the two of us, I'm starting to think that's how every disaster in this country got built."

He meant it as a joke. Mostly. But Sera didn't laugh, and when he glanced over, she was staring past the fire into the darkening north with an expression he'd learn, in the coming days, to dread—the look of a woman doing arithmetic of her own, and getting a number she didn't like.

Because far up the valley, where the starlight should have been pooling silver on the frozen fields, the darkness had a shine to it. A gold shine. And it was closer tonight than it had been last night, and it was, he could have sworn, *waiting*—the way a generous host waits, just inside the door, smiling, with gifts in both hands.
