## 7. The Gilded Man

Sera Vey had spent her life around wards, and wards had a grammar. A ward under strain hummed. A ward failing sang a thin, high note like a wet finger on crystal. A ward about to break went quiet in a way that made your teeth ache.

The road north of Graysink had gone quiet three days ago, and her teeth hadn't stopped aching since.

She walked at the rear of the column, which was where she preferred to be. At the front, Jude Dravenmoor set the pace and the priest—Brother Anselm, blind as a winter turnip and twice as stubborn—sang the names of the choir in a voice that carried like smoke. Behind him came forty-odd survivors of Graysink: miners mostly, their faces gray with dust that no longer washed off because, Sera suspected, the concept of *clean* was wearing thin this far into the Hollow. Then the children, then the carts, then her.

It was a good place to think, the back of a doomed procession. Nobody expected anything of you there except that you keep walking.

"You're doing the face again," said Tam Wicker, falling into step beside her. He was a choir member, a broad, cheerful man who had been a bellows-mender before Graysink stopped needing bellows mended, and he had appointed himself her shadow sometime on the second day out of town. "The face where you look like you're doing sums nobody asked for."

"I'm always doing sums nobody asked for."

"Aye, but usually you enjoy it." He nodded ahead, to where the mountains should have been. Should have been peaks; what they got instead was a suggestion of peaks, the way you might sketch a mountain from memory while very drunk. The stone was still there. It simply couldn't be bothered to commit to any particular shape. "What's it saying today?"

"That we've lost another mile of definition since noon," Sera said. "That the road is holding because Jude remembers it, and Jude is tired, and tired men forget things."

"And that's bad."

"That's catastrophic," she said, "but let's start with bad and work our way up."

Tam laughed, easy as breathing. That was the thing about the Graysink folk that had unsettled her at first and now steadied her: they had already lost their god, their town's name was half-gone from every tongue that spoke it, and they had responded by organizing a walking choir with a rotation for carrying the children and a strict rule about singing after sundown. Grief, in these people, had been processed into something useful, like slag into road-metal.

They made camp that evening in the lee of a hill that was only mostly a hill. Sera took first watch, partly because she didn't sleep well anymore and partly because she liked watching the choir settle—the way Brother Anselm moved among them touching each shoulder, the way the children were fed first from the carts, the way Jude sat apart with his map that wasn't a map, more a memory wearing paper's clothing, and marked roads onto it that appeared as his pen touched them.

She was watching him do exactly that when the light changed.

Not dimmed. Changed. The dusk had been the bruised purple of a bruise that wouldn't heal, and then it turned gold—not sunset gold, not lamplight gold, but the gold of things that are worth too much to be touched. Every rock, every cart-wheel, every sleeping child's cheek took on a faint gilding, as if the whole world had been dipped by a careful hand.

Sera stood up so fast she knocked over her own pack.

"Jude," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. Years of lying to ward-inspectors will do that. "Jude. Something's coming."

He was already on his feet. She would learn, in time, that the man slept like a cat sleeps—one ear open, one eye half-shut, ready to be fully awake between one heartbeat and the next. "Which direction?"

"That's the problem," she said. "All of them."

Because it was true. The gold was arriving from everywhere at once, without wind, without sound, without the pressure-in-the-ears that preceded most manifestations she'd studied. It simply *condensed*, down the road ahead of them, the way frost condenses on glass: patiently, beautifully, and against every natural law.

It took the shape of a man.

Or rather, it took the shape of what a sculptor with infinite patience and no understanding of human beings might produce if asked for *a man, idealized*. Tall. Broad. Draped in robes that flowed like poured metal. Its face—

Sera heard Jude make a sound beside her. Small. Involuntary. The sound a man makes when he sees a dead friend across a crowded market and knows it can't be him.

The face was Hollow's.

She had never seen Hollow herself—few living people had; gods of silence don't sit for portraits—but she had seen the old shrine-carvings in Graysink before the town began forgetting them, and she knew the shape: the smooth oval, the closed eyes, the mouth that was not so much absent as *uninterested*. The gilded figure wore that face the way a thief wears a stolen coat. Correctly. Warmly. As if it had always belonged to him.

"It's wearing him," Jude said. His voice was flat, which Sera had learned meant the opposite of calm. "It's wearing my god's face."

"It's wearing what it ate," Sera said quietly. "We're probably lucky it kept the face. It could have worn the whole religion."

* * *

The figure did not advance. That was the first thing Sera noticed as a warden, the thing that made the hair on her arms rise: everything in the Hollow *advanced*. The forgetting crept, seeped, dissolved its way outward like ink through wet paper. This stood still, at perhaps fifty paces, hands open at its sides, and waited. Waiting was a courtesy. Predators don't extend courtesies. Hosts do.

And then it spoke, and its voice was the second courtesy, and both courtesies together frightened her more than any roar could have.

It did not boom. It did not echo. It spoke *to each of them individually*, Sera realized, the way a mother speaks to each child at table—same words, tailored attention. She heard it clearly from fifty paces away, and when she glanced at Tam Wicker beside her, his lips were moving along with it, hearing it just as close.

*"Good evening, travelers,"* said the Gilded Man, in a voice like warm honey poured over bells. *"You've come far, and I know how far. I know every mile, because I have eaten the miles behind you. Forgive me—that sounds frightening, and I mean nothing frightening tonight. Tonight I have come to give."*

"Nobody gives," Jude said, loud enough for the front ranks to hear. "Not out here. Not dressed like that."

*"Ah. Jude Dravenmoor."* The golden head tilted, and the closed eyes somehow managed to convey fondness. *"God-sworn. Oath-keeper. You have carried thirty-nine souls on roads made of your own remembering, and each mile has cost you a piece of yourself. I have watched you pay. I confess—I admire you. Which is why I begin with you an offer, though I suspect you'll refuse it rudely."*

The figure raised one gleaming hand, and in its palm, light gathered—not harsh, not blinding, but the particular amber glow of a hearth seen through a window on a cold night. And inside the light, images formed.

Sera saw them. Everyone saw them, and everyone saw *different ones*.

She saw a kitchen. A small one, copper pots, steam, a woman laughing—her own mother, young, younger than Sera was now, flour on her hands, turning to say something Sera couldn't hear but felt in her chest like a struck tuning fork. The memory hit her with the force of recognition: she had forgotten this. No—worse. She had never forgotten it, because she had never known it was gone. The Hollow had taken it so gently she hadn't noticed the absence, and here it was, handed back whole, warm, *steaming*.

Her eyes stung. She hated that. She hated the figure for knowing exactly which window to knock at.

*"This is what I offer,"* the Gilded Man said, and now its voice was softer, pitched for intimacy, a confessor's voice, a lover's. *"Your happiest memories. Returned. All of them—every one I hold, and I hold so many, children. Your mothers' kitchens. Your first loves. The day the mine paid out double and old Harrow bought the whole tavern a round. The faces of your dead, clear as morning. I will give them back to you, freely, tonight."*

A pause. Somewhere in the choir, a woman was crying.

*"There is a price, and I will speak it plainly, because I am honest in a way your god never was."* The golden face did not change—it couldn't, it was beaten metal wearing an expression of gentle sorrow—but somehow the sorrow deepened. *"Give me the rest. The dull days. The grievances. The long gray stretches between joys. The fear, the hunger, the cold road, the memory of how to hate. Keep only what shone. Walk into me carrying your brightest hours, and live inside them, lit forever. No more losing. No more forgetting. Nothing else in this dying country can promise you that. I can. I am the only thing left here that keeps what it takes."*

Silence. The fire crackled. Somewhere a child whimpered and was hushed.

Sera found she was gripping Tam's arm hard enough to bruise. He didn't seem to notice. He was staring at the figure with an expression she'd last seen on men looking into a mine shaft they'd been told held gold.

"Don't," she said. "Tam. Whatever you're thinking—"

"I'm not thinking anything," Tam said, which was a lie so transparent it was almost honest. "I'm just... listening. A man can listen."

"It's a trap."

"'Course it's a trap." He said it cheerfully, terribly. "But Sera—my girl died two winters back. Fever. And I can't—" His voice caught, and he pushed through it the way he'd push through a stuck door. "I can't remember her laugh. I remember that I loved it. I remember people said it was like water over stones. But the laugh itself is *gone*, and there's a hole in me shaped exactly like it, and that thing says it has my daughter's laugh in its pocket and it'll hand it back for free."

"For free," Sera repeated. "Tam. Nothing in Thornfell is free anymore. Nothing anywhere is."

Down the line, others were stirring. A miner named Corvin Hale—Sera knew him slightly, a dour man with a bad knee and a good heart buried under ash—had stepped forward. Beside him, a young widow called Merrin Ash, who had joined the choir holding her baby and, Sera now noticed with a lurch, was not holding her baby, because there was no baby, there had been no baby for some time, and the absence had finally grown teeth.

The Gilded Man spread its hands. Patient. Generous. *Waiting.*

*"No one must choose in haste,"* it said. *"I will wait as long as you walk. I ask only honesty. Look at what I show you. If what I hold means nothing to you, refuse me and sleep well. But if it means everything—"*

Corvin Hale stepped out of the firelight.

"Corvin!" Brother Anselm's voice cracked across the camp like a whip, and the blind priest turned toward the sound of the man's boots on frozen ground. "Corvin Hale, you stay behind the line!"

But Corvin was walking, slow, limping on the bad knee, straight toward the gold. And the terrible thing, the thing Sera would carry out of Thornfell if she carried nothing else, was that Corvin was *smiling*. She had never seen him smile. Three years of shared hardship and she hadn't known he could.

The Gilded Man opened its arms.

What happened next happened quickly and quietly, which somehow made it worse. Corvin reached the figure. The gold folded around him—not violently, not hungrily, but the way a coat folds around shoulders—and for one moment Sera saw his face above the golden collar, eyes shining with returned light, mouth shaping a word that might have been a daughter's name or a wife's or his own. Then the light closed over him like water, and the Gilded Man stood alone again, a little brighter than before. Noticeably brighter. Sera's warden's eye measured it and wished it hadn't: the figure had gained mass. Corvin Hale's mass. Corvin Hale's *everything*.

Merrin Ash followed before anyone could move. She went quickly, almost running, and at the last step she laughed—a bright, unburdened laugh—and then the gold took her too, and the night was darker by two souls and brighter by two souls' worth of stolen shine.

"No!" Anselm was shouting now, stumbling forward, hands out. "In the name of the silence—in the name of what *was*—"

"He has no name to invoke," Sera heard herself say, and the cruelty of it hit her a beat after she said it. "I'm sorry. Brother. I'm sorry."

The choir stood in a ring around the cooling fire, and Sera watched the arithmetic move behind their eyes, because she was doing the same arithmetic herself. Thirty-seven remained. Two had walked into the light smiling. The offer stood. The offer would always stand. And every one of them had a hole somewhere inside them shaped like something they'd lost, and the enemy knew the exact dimensions of every hole, because the enemy had dug them.

* * *

The Gilded Man did not press. That was the worst part. It simply inclined its borrowed head—Hollow's serene, unbothered face, now lit from within by two additional lives—and said, in the tone of a shopkeeper closing up for the evening:

*"I will be nearby. I am always nearby. Think of what you want back. Sleep on it. There is no hurry. Hurry is a thing I ate long ago."*

And it poured itself backward into the dark like molten metal returning to a mold, leaving the road gilded faintly where it had stood, and the stars—or the holes where stars used to be—seemed, for a moment, almost to shine.

Nobody slept. Sera sat by the fire with her shard of ward-glass in her fist, digging the edges into her palm to give herself an honest pain to hold against all the dishonest sweetness. Across the flames, Jude Dravenmoor stared at the spot where the figure had vanished, and his face was the face of a man doing terrible sums.

"She called it generous," Tam Wicker said softly, beside her. He hadn't left her side since Merrin Ash walked. His big hands were shaking. "The widow. Right before she went. She turned around and looked at us all and she said, 'It's generous,' like that settled it. Like generosity was proof."

"It is proof," Sera said. "Of exactly what it wants us to think."

Tam huffed something that wasn't quite a laugh. "Say it plainer, warden. My head's full of holes."

Sera turned the shard over in her bleeding palm. "Everything else in the Hollow takes and leaves nothing. It's mindless. Hunger doesn't negotiate. But that thing *negotiated*. It showed us the goods first. It let two of us sample. Do you know why?"

"Why?"

"Because a trap you're forced into is a cage," she said, "and cages get broken. But a trap you *choose*—" She looked up at the starless dark, at the faint gold residue on the road, at the choir's hollow faces ringed around the fire. "A trap you choose, you defend. Tomorrow someone else will want to go, and tonight's griefers will argue for it. We'll do its recruiting for it. That's what generosity buys."

Across the fire, Jude rose. He picked up his map-memory, rolled it, tucked it away, and looked down the gilded road toward whatever waited for them in the morning.

"We break camp at first light," he said. "Earlier. And we put distance between us and this stretch of road."

"And if it follows?" someone asked.

"It will follow," Jude said. "It told us it would. It's honest that way." He paused, and Sera saw his jaw work, saw the words come out ground fine. "So tomorrow we reach the bridge. The Oathbreaker's Bridge. And I need everyone to hear me now, before we sleep, because I won't be able to say it there."

He looked at them, all thirty-seven, and the firelight caught the exhaustion on him like gilt.

"The bridge only holds for those who've never broken an oath," he said. "Every one of you needs to decide tonight what you'll swear to when we stand on it. Because that thing back there isn't the only one on this road who asks questions with prices attached."

Sera's stomach dropped. She thought of the flaw she'd hidden in the wards years ago, the spiteful little crack she'd carved out of pride, the crack that had let the end of the world in. She thought of oaths. She thought of bridges.

*Tonight,* she thought, *of all nights, I should have learned to lie better.*
