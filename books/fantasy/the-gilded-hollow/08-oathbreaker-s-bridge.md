## 8. Oathbreaker's Bridge

The river appeared on the third morning out of Graysink, which was strange, because rivers in Thornfell had lately taken to disappearing rather than arriving.

It ran fast and silver through a gorge too deep for its width, the kind of water that sounded like it had somewhere important to be. And across it, impossibly, hung a bridge — a single span of pale stone, no railings, no pillars, arcing from cliff to cliff like something drawn by a child who hadn't yet learned about engineering.

"That's not on any map," said Tam Wicker, the choir's youngest survivor, a boy of perhaps fifteen who still had most of his memories and was loudly proud of them.

"Maps stopped being useful about two weeks ago," Jude said. "Try to keep up."

He knew the bridge, though. That was the trouble. He knew it the way you know a scar — not because anyone had shown it to him, but because he'd crossed it twice in his smuggling years, and both times he'd been alone, and both times he'd sworn quietly to himself never to speak of it. The Oathbreaker's Bridge, the old passes called it. It didn't hold weight. It held *truth*. It existed only for those who had never broken a sworn word, and everyone else found themselves standing at the near cliff wondering why they'd ever thought there was a bridge there at all.

Jude looked at it now and felt his stomach do something complicated.

Behind him, thirty-odd survivors of the Hollow Choir were making camp with the weary competence of people who had learned, in one terrible autumn, that routine was a form of prayer. They sang their name-hymns as they worked. Old Mother Cass chanted her own full name over the cookpot like a blessing on the stew. Two sisters held hands and recited each other's birthdays back and forth so neither would lose hers.

Sera Vey stood apart, as she usually did, turning the shard of ward-glass over in her fingers. She'd been quieter since Graysink. Quieter than a woman whose whole personality was already quiet had any right to be.

"We can't go around," Jude told her, low. "The gorge runs east into territory I don't remember anymore. West, it just... stops. The maps end mid-line."

"You're sure it's the bridge?" Sera asked. "The real one? Not something the Hollow is wearing?"

"The Hollow doesn't build," Jude said. "It only takes. If this were a trap, it would look better."

* * *

He told them at dawn, because lying to desperate people about a bridge seemed like the sort of thing that came back around.

"It's called the Oathbreaker's Bridge," he said, standing where all could see him, arms crossed in what he hoped read as authority rather than discomfort. "It holds for those who've kept their oaths. For the rest—" He shrugged. "There's no bridge. You'll walk off a cliff and die embarrassed."

A few nervous laughs. Good. Nervous laughs meant they were still people and not yet soup.

"I'll cross first," Jude went on. "Then you come in ones and twos. Anyone who finds nothing under their feet steps back, no shame in it, and we'll find another way if we have to."

"And how many of us will find another way?" asked Dagny Holt, a broad-shouldered woman who had been a quarry foreman before the quarry stopped remembering it had ever employed her. She wasn't hostile. She was just tired, and tired people ask direct questions.

"One way to find out," Jude said, and hated himself a little for how smuggler-ish it sounded.

He turned toward the bridge before anyone could study his face too closely. Because here was the arithmetic he'd done in the dark hours, alone, staring at the stars-that-weren't: he had broken oaths. Plenty. A smuggler's life was a long ledger of small betrayals — oaths to magistrates he'd lied to, oaths to partners he'd cut loose, oaths to gods he'd promised things to and then gotten distracted. If the bridge tested honestly, it would drop him into the gorge like a sack of turnips, and then thirty people would be stranded on the wrong side of a river with a fading country closing in behind them.

So he did what he'd spent his whole life doing. He arranged the truth until it fit through a narrow space.

He would tell them he could cross because he was god-sworn. Sworn to Hollow, bound by rites, an oath so total it outweighed every petty perjury of his former trade. And technically — *technically* — that was even true. Hollow had claimed him. The rites were binding. Whatever else he'd broken, that vow had held.

What he would not tell them was that he had no idea whether it would hold *enough*. What he would not tell them was that he was about to bet thirty lives on a technicality. What he would not tell them was that the moment his boot touched stone, he intended to find out the answer to a question he was afraid of, and he wanted witnesses far enough away that they couldn't see it in his face.

Small dishonesty. Smuggler's arithmetic. He'd been doing it so long it felt almost like breathing.

It felt different now. Everything did, lately. When memory itself was being eaten, a lie stopped being a private little sin and started being a hole in the boat.

* * *

The bridge took his weight.

He heard the collective exhale behind him — thirty people breathing at once, a sound like wind through a doorway — and he allowed himself three steps of pure relief before the guilt arrived, punctual as taxes.

Because here was the thing he discovered with each step: the bridge didn't feel solid. It felt *attentive*. Each flagstone hummed faintly under his boots, and the humming changed. When he thought of Hollow's last command — *keep them moving, keep them remembered* — the stones rang clear as bells. When he thought of the lie he'd just told, the humming went sour, a note slightly flat, like a choir member who'd learned the hymn wrong.

The bridge knew. Of course the bridge knew. Bridges built on truth are not fooled by adjacent truths.

But it held. Whether because the god-sworn oath genuinely outweighed the rest, or because the bridge was itself half-starved in this dying country and couldn't afford to be picky, Jude couldn't say. He reached the far side and turned and waved, and the wave was honest at least, and the crossing began.

Dagny came second, and the bridge held for her, and she laughed — actually laughed, a great bark of sound — and shouted back, "Thirty-one years married to the same stubborn man and I never once lied to him about money!" which told Jude more about Dagny's marriage than he needed but made the whole choir cheer.

Tam crossed holding Mother Cass's hand, both of them chanting their names, and the stones rang so sweetly under them that several people wept. The twin sisters crossed arm in arm. A one-eyed tinker named Pell crossed while apologizing aloud, unprompted, to a horse he'd stolen in 4— some year or other, and the bridge let him pass anyway, which suggested either mercy or a sense of humor.

And then Sera Vey stepped up to the near edge, and the bridge was not there.

She didn't fall — she'd only put one foot forward, testing, the way you test ice. But her foot met air where air should have been stone, and she staggered back, and the whole column went quiet in that awful way crowds go quiet when everyone understands at once.

"Oh," Sera said. Just that. Quietly. As though confirming a suspicion she'd been carrying around like a stone in her pocket.

"Sera." Jude was already halfway back across. He didn't remember deciding to move. "Hey. Look at me. It's fine. We'll rig a line, we'll—"

"Don't." She held up a hand, ward-scribe precise, and gave him a small smile that was worse than crying. "Jude. I helped build the wards. I hid a flaw in them. I have broken approximately every oath I ever swore to my order, my superiors, my city, and myself, in roughly that order." She shrugged. "I'm surprised the bridge didn't reach up and slap me."

"There are ways across gorges that don't involve honesty," Jude said. "I've used most of them."

"With what rope? With what anchors? On cliffs that stop existing when you look away?" She shook her head. "Go. Get them across. All of them. Then figure out your next problem. That's what you do. It's infuriating, but it works."

"Sera—"

"*Go.*" And then, softer, with the ghost of the gentle humor she rationed out like sugar in a siege: "Besides. Someone has to stay behind and write down what you all forget. I'm the only one with a shard that remembers. Consider me your archive."

He wanted to argue. He was good at arguing; it was most of his skill set. But the gorge wind was rising, and behind him thirty frightened people were watching their leader and their scribe have a conversation they couldn't hear, and every minute the Hollow closed a little tighter around the world.

So he did the cowardly brave thing, which is to say the practical thing, which is to say he left her.

He told himself it wasn't abandonment. He told himself he'd find another crossing, double back, rig something clever. He told himself a great many things, standing on a bridge that hummed flat notes under his feet, and the bridge judged every single one of them.

* * *

By late afternoon the whole company was across except Sera, who sat on the far cliff wrapped in her cloak, writing in a little book, occasionally waving with the brisk cheerfulness of someone pretending very hard. The choir had begun setting up camp on the north side, and Jude had begun doing the thing he was best at besides lying, which was organizing frightened people into useful shapes.

That was when Tam Wicker came to find him, wearing the particular expression of a boy who has noticed something and wishes desperately he hadn't.

"Master Dravenmoor? Can I ask you something?"

"You can ask. No promises on answers."

"How come the bridge held for you?" Tam's voice was careful, the way voices get when they're carrying something fragile. "I mean — Dagny says it's because you're god-sworn. But Pell stole a horse and it held for him. And old Ferro used to run contraband for the Bellharrow guilds, everybody knows it, and it held for him too. So it's not really about... being good. Is it. It's about oaths specifically." Tam looked down at his own boots. "And I keep thinking, a man like you, in your line of work, before — well. You must have sworn *some* things you didn't keep. Everybody has. So how—"

"TAM." Jude put a hand on the boy's shoulder, gentle, and steered him firmly toward the cookfire. "Why don't you go see if Mother Cass needs help with the stew. I suspect she does. She always suspects she does."

Tam went, but he went looking back over his shoulder twice, and Jude knew the question hadn't drowned. Questions like that didn't drown. They floated. They waited downstream.

He stood at the edge of camp and looked north, and his stomach finally finished the complicated thing it had been doing all day and settled on dread.

Because the far side of the gorge — the side they were now committed to — was wrong.

Not dramatically wrong. That was the horror of it. The pines were still pines, the road was still a road, the mountains still wore their snow like reluctant hats. But the light fell strangely, as though the sun weren't quite sure this place deserved it. A farmhouse stood by the road with its windows glowing warm and gold, smoke curling from its chimney, and no door. Not a locked door, not a boarded door — no door, and no frame where a door had been, just wall, smooth as an egg. Further up, a milestone read DISTANCE TO: and then nothing, the letters scooped out like flesh from a fruit.

And when Jude walked fifty yards up the road and looked back, the bridge was fading.

Not collapsing. Fading — going translucent at the edges, the pale stone thinning to gauze, the way everything in Thornfell thinned when it stopped being believed in. A bridge that existed only for the unbroken needed, apparently, to be *used* by the unbroken to stay fed, and the choir had crossed, and now it was starving with Sera Vey alone on the wrong side of it.

"No," Jude said aloud. "No, no, no—"

He ran. He got maybe ten strides before Dagny caught his arm, and she was strong the way quarry women are strong, and he might have fought her except that part of him was grateful.

"Jude. Jude! Look at me. What is it?"

"The bridge — Sera — it's going, I have to—"

"She knew," Dagny said, not unkindly, which made it worse. "Whatever that bridge is, whatever it costs — she looked at it, and she knew, and she sent us across anyway. That wasn't a woman being left behind, lad. That was a woman choosing her post."

"She can't choose a post on the wrong side of a river in a country that's being *eaten*, Dagny!"

"Why not?" Dagny's grip didn't loosen. Her voice dropped. "You crossed first. Alone. Before any of us knew if there was anything under our feet. You want to talk about choosing posts?"

"That's different."

"Is it." She let him go, slowly. "Because here's what I saw today. I saw a man walk onto empty air like he already knew it would hold. Not hoping. Knowing. And I've been a foreman twenty years, and I know the difference between a man who trusts his rope and a man who tied it himself." Her eyes were steady and sad. "You knew something about that bridge you didn't tell us. Maybe it was nothing. Maybe it was everything. But you lied to us about *something*, Jude, even if it was only about how scared you were, and now you're shocked — shocked — that the world's gone thin on the far side of it."

Jude opened his mouth. Closed it. The flat-note hum of the bridge was still in his ears, and it seemed suddenly to be everywhere, in the wind, in the road, in Dagny's quiet voice.

"I'm trying to keep them alive," he said finally. "All of them. That's the whole of it. That's the oath."

"I believe you," Dagny said. "That's the problem. I believe you completely, and I still watched you lie today, and I'm starting to understand that in this country those two things can both be true at once." She turned back toward camp. "Come eat. The stew's terrible. Mother Cass made it, so it's also somehow holy."

* * *

He didn't sleep. Around midnight he walked back to the gorge edge, and the bridge was still there — thinner, gauze-pale, but there — and on the far cliff a small fire burned, and a small figure sat beside it, writing.

He raised his lantern. After a moment, she raised hers. Neither waved. Some conversations are better conducted entirely in light.

*I'll come back for you,* he thought at her, as hard as he could. *I'll find a crossing. I'll break every rule I have left.*

The bridge hummed, faint and flat, and Jude Dravenmoor — god-sworn, oath-keeper, liar — stood in the dark between two halves of a dying country and understood, at last, exactly what kind of currency he'd been spending all his life, and exactly why the toll collector had finally come to collect.

Behind him, from the direction of the farmhouse with no door, someone began to sing.

It was a hymn he knew. It was one of theirs — a Hollow Choir name-hymn, Mother Cass's melody, note-perfect.

And there was no one behind him.
