## 9. Letters to a Silent God

The chapel had no name anymore.

Jude knew this because he asked it—asked the building itself, in the way old smugglers sometimes talked to walls when they thought no one was listening—and the stones gave back nothing but cold and the smell of wet ash. Once, he was fairly sure, it had belonged to Hollow. There were signs: the alcove behind the altar where a god-statue should have stood held only a smooth hollow in the rock, as if someone had scooped the deity out like the flesh of a fruit. The pews were carved with the seven-knotted cord that smugglers used to mark safe roads. And the donation box by the door still contained three copper coins that nobody had stolen, which in Jude's experience was the surest sign of divine provenance there was.

But the chapel did not answer. Worse—it did not *insist*. A living shrine pushed back against you. It had opinions about your boots on its floor. This one just sat there in the mountain fold like an abandoned coat, and Jude understood, with a lurch, that the building had forgotten what it was for. It no longer believed in itself. It was only waiting, politely, to be weather.

"Cozy," said Brannick, dropping his pack in the aisle with the delicacy of a landslide. He was the biggest of the Graysink choir, a former mine-boss with forearms like ship's cable and a voice that could be heard through stone. "Roof holds. There's a hearth. Somebody left candles." He held one up. It was half-melted and had a tooth mark in it. "Somebody *bit* this candle."

"Somebody was hungry," said Maren, the weaver, who had appointed herself the choir's quartermaster sometime around the second day of the march and defended the title like a border town. "Don't eat candles, Brannick."

"I wasn't going to eat the candle, Mare. I was making conversation."

"You were looking at it the way you look at sausage."

"That's slander," Brannick said, wounded, "and also accurate."

Jude let them argue. It was good for them, the arguing. Two weeks since Graysink, two weeks of walking north through a country that kept forgetting its own bones, and he'd learned that a band of survivors went quiet in one of two ways: the way before despair or the way before sleep. You wanted the second kind. Arguing about candle-eating got you there.

Sera Vey came in last, shaking frost off her ward-scribe's cloak, and paused just inside the door the way she always did—one heartbeat, head tilted, reading the room for whatever the room had stopped being able to say. Then she saw the hollow in the altar and her face did something complicated.

"This was his," she said quietly. "Wasn't it. Hollow's."

"Maybe." Jude set his own pack down by the hearth. "Or maybe some other quiet god. Thornfell had a few. Most of them didn't talk much either. That was sort of the local style."

"Hollow never talked at all," Maren said. She'd grown up in the mining towns; everyone down there had been god-sworn to the silence in one fashion or another. "That was the whole point of him. My gran said you could tell your worst secrets to Hollow because he couldn't repeat them even if he wanted to."

"He can't repeat them now either," Brannick said, and then heard himself, and winced. "Sorry, Captain. Bad joke."

"It was a fine joke," Jude said. "It was almost a good joke. Don't apologize it into a bad one."

But after they'd eaten—barley and salt pork, the last honest meal before Maren started rationing in earnest—and after the choir had wrapped themselves in blankets along the pews and gone into the heavy breathing of people sleeping somewhere safer than they actually were, Jude sat alone by the hearth with a candle that had not been bitten, a stub of charcoal, and a sheaf of paper he'd taken from a dead courier's satchel outside Bellharrow.

He looked at the hollow in the altar for a long time.

Then he wrote:

*To Hollow,*

*You're dead. I've been operating on that assumption for two weeks and it has been extremely inconvenient, so if you're not dead, I'd appreciate a sign. Any sign. A dream. A shiver. One of those coins falling out of the donation box. I'm not proud. I'll take a coin.*

*Here is what I want to know. When the stars went out, I felt you go. Not nearby-gone. Not asleep-gone. *Gone*-gone, the way a road goes when the country forgets it. I have carried you since I was nine years old, when you first spoke to me over a crate of stolen brandy, and in all that time you never once answered me directly, and I accepted that, because that was the covenant. Silence for silence. Smugglers don't kiss and tell.*

*But here's my problem, and I want you to know I've thought about it carefully and soberly, mostly soberly: the wards didn't break on their own. Wards don't. Glass doesn't shatter from the inside unless something inside wants out—or something outside was invited. And you were the god of the borders, Hollow. Every ward-stone from here to the coast had your knot on it. If something ate you, it ate you through a door you owned.*

*So. Did you die badly? Or did you die conveniently?*

*I'll burn this either way. That's how the rite works. But I'd burn it happier with an answer.*

*—Jude Dravenmoor, god-sworn, currently unemployed*

He read it over twice. Then he touched it to the candle flame and held it until the fire reached his fingers, and dropped the curling black scrap into the hearth, and watched the words come apart.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. The smoke went up the flue like smoke does, indifferent, and the chapel sat around him being unbothered, and somewhere in the dark of the mountains the thing that wore beaten gold went on eating the edges off the world.

Jude took another sheet.

*All right,* he wrote. *Second letter. Less polite.*

*You taught me the catechism yourself. Well—you didn't teach it, obviously, you being silent and all; Old Tammerick taught it, the ferryman, may he rest in whatever's left of peace. But you ratified it. Every oath I ever swore over stolen goods, you witnessed. Do you remember the words? Of course you don't remember, you're dead, that's the entire premise of this correspondence. Fine. I'll say them for both of us.*

*"What is taken is kept. What is kept is known. What is known cannot be unmade."*

*That's the whole thing. Three lines. We used to laugh at the temple-folk with their forty-page liturgies, and here's why it matters tonight: those three lines are memory-rituals. Sera figured it out—the road held when I swore it. The passes stay real where I remember them. Which means every prayer I ever made to you wasn't worship, Hollow. It was maintenance. I was a bricklayer and didn't know it. Thirty years of oaths, and each one was mortar in a wall.*

*A wall around what?*

*I keep coming back to that. A wall around what?*

*Because here's what keeps me up while the choir snores and Brannick dreams of sausage: walls aren't built to protect things from getting out. Sometimes. Occasionally. But mostly, in my professional experience—and I am a professional, I have built more walls than most masons, usually at night, usually without permission—walls are built to keep things IN.*

*And you were the god of the borders. And the borders fell the same night you did. Almost like the lock and the key went into the fire together.*

*Burn this too. Burn all of it. I'm not accusing you. I'm asking. There's a difference, and the difference is that I loved you, you silent bastard, and love makes a man check his arithmetic twice.*

*—Jude*

*P.S. If you're not dead, the coin thing stands.*

He burned that one slower than the first. Partly for ceremony. Mostly because his hands weren't quite steady, and he didn't want to drop it before it caught.

* * *

He wrote four more letters before dawn.

The third was short and vicious, written in the white heat of remembering that Hollow had chosen *him*—a nine-year-old orphan running contraband for a man who beat him—out of every soul in the Thornfell passes. *You picked a child*, he wrote. *You picked a child and made him your bricklayer and never told him what the building was.* He burned it fast, almost glad to see it go.

The fourth was an apology for the third, which he also burned, feeling foolish.

The fifth was a list. This was Maren's influence; she'd gotten him keeping lists ever since the supply inventory near Graysink, and now his mind did it automatically under stress. *Things I know*, the list began. *1. The stars went out. 2. The wards shattered. 3. Maps blank themselves within days. 4. Boundary stones go smooth. 5. People forget their nation before they forget their names—names go last. 6. Roads hold where I remember them. 7. Each use costs me a memory. 8. The devourer wears gold and offers trades. 9. Sera broke something, or thinks she did. 10. You are silent.*

Then, underneath, in smaller letters: *Things I suspect. 1–10 are connected. 11. So are you.*

He burned the list and immediately wished he hadn't, because it had been useful, and then wrote it again from memory, which was becoming the theme of his entire life.

The sixth letter he wrote sitting in the altar hollow itself, legs dangling over the edge like a boy on a dock, because the floor was cold and the stone was marginally less cold and he was tired of pretending he had dignity left. From there he could see the whole chapel: the choir breathing in their blankets, Sera awake at the far end, pretending to study her shard of ward-glass by candlelight and clearly listening to every scratch of his charcoal.

*Sera knows I'm writing to you,* he wrote. *She hasn't said anything. She does that thing where she looks at the glass instead of at me, which is her version of giving a man privacy, and I appreciate it, but I also notice everything, it's the trade. Here's what she doesn't know: I'm not writing to grieve. Grief I can do walking. I'm writing because grief that doesn't get asked questions turns into something worse. It turns into suspicion wearing grief's coat.*

*So ask me the question, Hollow. The real one. The one I've been circling for six letters like a mule around a bridge it doesn't trust.*

*Did you know?*

*When you spoke to me over that brandy crate thirty years ago—when you put your silence into me like a coal into a paper bag—did you already know how this would end? Was I a son to you, or was I a hinge? Because hinges don't get told things. Hinges just swing.*

*Answer me. Coin, dream, shiver, sign. I'll take a draft of cold air. I'll take Brannick's snoring changing key.*

*Anything.*

He burned the sixth letter in the altar hollow itself, letting the ash gather in the cup where the god should have been. It seemed right. Ash was honest stuff. It didn't pretend to be anything but what it used to be.

The ash lay there in a little grey mound, and Jude looked at it, and waited, the way you wait for a knock you've convinced yourself won't come.

The ash did nothing. The chapel did nothing. Outside, the wind moved through a pass that existed because he remembered it, and would stop existing the day he didn't, and the stars—there were no stars, there hadn't been stars for two weeks, and he was so tired of the sky being a closed eye—

"Captain?" Sera's voice, low, from the far end. "You should sleep."

"In a minute."

"You've said 'in a minute' four times. I counted. Counting is also part of my trade."

He huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Go back to your glass, Vey."

She didn't. She sat there, and he sat there, and eventually she said, very quietly, "What do you ask him?"

"The truth."

"And if the truth is bad?"

Jude turned the charcoal stub over in his fingers. "Then it's still the truth. Bad truth holds weight better than good lies. Any smuggler knows that. It's why we don't lie to each other about the roads. A lie about a road kills you mid-stride."

Sera considered that. "Most people prefer the good lies."

"Most people haven't walked out of a country that's being erased." He nodded at the ash. "He preferred them too, I think. Good lies. Comfortable ones. Maybe that's all a god is—a really well-organized good lie." He said it to wound himself a little, to see if it bled. It bled.

Sera gathered her glass and her blanket and moved to a pew nearer the hearth, closer to him, saying nothing, which from Sera was a whole paragraph.

Jude took the seventh sheet. Last one. His hand ached; the charcoal was down to a nub he had to pinch like a seed.

*Seventh letter,* he wrote. *Last one tonight, I promise the dead man's inbox some peace.*

*I've decided something. If you don't answer by morning, I stop writing to you and start writing about you. There's a difference. Letters to a god assume he's listening. Notes about a corpse assume he isn't. I've been doing the first because it's easier, because talking to you—even one-sided—is still a kind of company, and company is scarce out here. But easy isn't true. Easy is what the gilded thing sells. Happy memories for the rest of your life. Two of ours took the deal, and they smiled the whole way in, and I think about those smiles more than I want to.*

*So: morning. One night's grace. That's generous, for a god who's given me nothing but silence since the stars died. More generous than you deserve, probably. But you picked me when I was nine and worth nothing, and some part of me—a stupid, stubborn, brick-laying part—still wants to believe you picked me for a reason you'd have told me about, if you'd had time.*

*If you had time.*

*Huh. Funny word, "if." Keeps showing up in these letters. IF the wards were a cage. IF you died conveniently. IF you knew. IF you had time.*

*Old Tammerick used to say: a smuggler who says "if" too many times is building himself a gallows out of small words. So. Morning, Hollow. Then I start digging without you.*

*—Jude*

*P.S. The coin offer expires at dawn.*

He burned it. Watched the ash join the other six letters in the god-shaped cup. Seven letters. A week's worth of nights compressed into one, because he didn't know how many nights the country had left in it, and neither, he suspected, did the country.

Then he banked the fire, folded himself onto a pew with his cloak around him, and slept the thin, armed sleep of a man who expects nothing.

* * *

He woke to grey light and the smell of Maren attempting porridge, which meant waking to the smell of scorched barley and hope. Brannick was arguing with the chapel door, which had swollen shut overnight—"it's not locked, it's just decided against us"—and Sera was already up, standing at the altar.

Standing very still at the altar.

Jude crossed the floor before he fully decided to. "Vey?"

She didn't turn around. Her voice came out flat and careful, the way voices go when they're carrying something fragile across ice. "Captain. Did you take the ash out of here during the night?"

"No. Why would I—"

"Come look."

He came and looked.

The ash was gone. All of it—seven letters' worth, a week of grief and accusation and love ground to grey powder—gone, and in its place, laid neatly in the hollow where the god should have been, was a single sheet of paper. Unburnt. Uncreased. As if it had grown there overnight, the way frost grows, the way forgetting grows.

And the handwriting was his own.

Not similar. Not close. *His*—the cramped rightward slant, the way he crossed his sevens, the little hook he put on the tail of a J that Old Tammerick had once laughed at and called "a fisherman's J, boy, you write like you're hauling something in." No one else alive wrote like that. No one else alive had ever seen him write enough to copy it.

Sera stepped back to give him room. From the doorway, Brannick had gone quiet, which was somehow louder than his arguing. Maren set down the porridge pot. Even the chapel seemed to lean in, the first opinion it had offered since they arrived.

Jude picked up the page with hands that did not shake, which surprised him, and read it aloud, because some things need air to be real:

*"Dear Jude,*

*You asked if I knew. Yes.*

*You asked if you were a son or a hinge. Both. I'm sorry. Those were the only choices the plan allowed, and I chose you for both reasons at once, which is the coward's arithmetic, and I have had a long time to get comfortable with coward's arithmetic, and I never did.*

*You asked what the wall was built around. Come to the Undercroft. Ask the warden. He remembers what I helped bury, and he is required by his own charter to answer one honest question per visitor, and you have never in your life asked an dishonest question about a road.*

*One more thing, and then I will stop taking up space in your head, gods know it's crowded: the seventh letter was the one that woke me. Not the prayers. Not the oaths. The anger. Anger is a kind of remembering, Jude. Keep some.*

*You'll know my name soon. I'm sorry you have to learn it the hard way, at the end, out loud.*

*—H"* 

The page ended there. Just the letter. Not signed, not sealed, not explained—as if whoever wrote it had run out of something halfway through a name, ink or courage or existence.

Nobody spoke. The porridge burned. Brannick's stomach growled into the silence like distant thunder, and even that sounded reverent.

Sera found her voice first. "The Undercroft," she said. "Jude. I know where the Undercroft is."

He was still staring at the page—at the fisherman's J, hauling something in. "Of course you do," he said. "You know where everything buried is. That's your whole tragedy, Vey." He folded the letter once, precisely, and tucked it inside his shirt, over his heart, where thirty years of prayers had been filed and never once answered—until now, when the answer raised worse questions than the silence ever had.

Because here was the thing he didn't say out loud, the thing that curdled slow and cold beneath the wonder:

A dead god had written to him in his own hand.

And the devourer out there in the dark wore the faces of the things it ate.

He looked at Sera, and managed something that was almost a smile. "Pack up," he said. "We're going to the Undercroft. And somebody wake Brannick gently—he's going to want breakfast for the road, and I have a feeling we're going to need every calm man we've got."

Behind them, unnoticed by anyone, the last of the ash stirred in the altar hollow, arranged itself into a shape like a seven-knotted cord, and held—for now—against the wind coming down from the pass.
