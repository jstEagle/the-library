## 10. The Archive Beneath

The entrance to the Undercroft was hidden behind a cheese shop.

Sera had always found this fitting, though she'd never had occasion to say so aloud until now. She stood in the cold-monger's alley behind Ferro's Fine Cheeses of Bellharrow—formerly of Bellharrow, formerly fine, formerly cheeses—and pressed her palm against a wall that looked like any other wall, except that it remembered being a door.

"Behind the cheese," said Tam, the choir's youngest survivor, a boy of perhaps fourteen who had attached himself to Jude's march with the stubbornness of a burr. "Thornfell kept its entire memory behind *cheese*."

"Cheese keeps," Sera said. "That was rather the point. The founders wanted the archive somewhere no one would look twice and somewhere damp-proof besides." She paused. "Also, I believe there was a bribe involved in 312. The Undercroft's founding charter mentions 'considerations rendered to the Guild of Dairymen.' We were never able to determine what those considerations were."

"Cheese," said Tam, with the certainty of a prophet.

"Probably cheese."

Her palm tingled where it met the stone. The ward-glass shard she carried on its cord around her neck had begun to hum three days ago, softly, like a kettle deciding whether to commit to boiling. It hummed now in earnest, and beneath her hand the wall grew warm, then honest. Bricks slid apart without sound, revealing stairs descending into a darkness that smelled of dust and lamp oil and, faintly, impossibly, of wax rind.

"Well," said Marta the weaver, peering past Sera's shoulder into the dark. "Is it safe?"

"No," Sera said honestly. "But it's intact."

Marta considered this. Behind her, the ragged column of survivors—thirty-one souls when they'd left Graysink, twenty-six now, after the Gilded Man had smiled at them in the pass—shifted and muttered like weather. Old Fenwick cracked his knuckles. The blind priest, Anselm, tilted his head as though listening to something far below, which, knowing him, he probably was.

"Intact is more than anything else in this country can claim," Anselm said mildly. "I'll go first if you like. I've been walking into things all week. One more won't change my average."

"You'll do no such thing," Sera said, and went first herself, because guilt is a kind of courage, or at least wears its coat.

* * *

The stairs went down farther than stairs should.

Sera counted them out of habit—one hundred and forty steps, the same number as the wards' original anchor-points, because the architects of Thornfell had been the sort of people who believed symmetry was a moral position—and with each step the humming of the ward-glass deepened into something almost like speech. She had worn the shard for years before the collapse, back when it was merely evidence of her crime, a flaw she'd carved into the border wards out of spite and never found the nerve to remove. Now it showed her what places used to be, ghost-images layered over ruin: here a market square bustling, there a chapel whole, there a road unbroken. Down here, the ghosts and the reality were nearly the same thing. That frightened her more than any ruin could have.

Because down here, Thornfell still existed.

The stairs opened into a hall so vast that their single lantern seemed to apologize for itself. Shelves rose into darkness on every side—not wooden shelves but shelves of pale stone grown from the floor like stalagmites tamed by committee, each one stacked with ledgers, scrolls, tablets of pressed clay, bundles of letters tied with ribbon gone brittle as autumn leaves. Treaties. Censuses. Land grants. Tax rolls. Marriage registers. The final statements of executed prisoners, recorded verbatim by law, filed under the year of their deaths. Everything the state of Thornfell had ever decided to remember about itself, arranged in an order so perfect it looked less like organization than like prayer.

"Oh," said Marta, very quietly.

"I know," Sera said.

"It's *all* here?"

"All of it. Every name the state ever wrote down. Every boundary it ever drew. Every—" She stopped, because her throat had closed, and finished instead: "Every person it ever erased."

Tam ran ahead, boy-fashion, footsteps echoing absurdly loud, and came back breathless. "There's a shelf with my village's census! All of them! My mum's handwriting's in the margin, she corrected the tally-man, he'd counted our goats wrong—" His voice cracked. "It's real. It says we were real."

"You were real," Sera said, and meant it as comfort, and heard only too late how it sounded, past tense clinging to the sentence like a burr. Tam's face did something complicated and settled on determined, and he marched off toward the census shelf again as if to stand guard over it.

Jude appeared at her elbow. He had a way of doing that—arriving without arriving, a smuggler's trick she suspected he practiced even when no one was watching. He looked out over the archive with the expression of a man appraising a vault he intended to rob, which, Sera reflected, was not entirely inaccurate; they were here to steal a country's memory back from oblivion, and theft was theft regardless of the righteousness of the cause.

"The Gilded Man didn't find this place," he said.

"No."

"Why not?"

Sera turned the ward-glass shard over in her fingers. In its depths, ghost-light swam. "Because this isn't part of Thornfell, exactly. It's underneath it. Before it. The founders built the Undercroft first and the country second—the charter predates the oldest border stone by eleven years. Whatever is eating Thornfell's edges is eating a drawing. This is the table the drawing sits on." She hesitated. "At least, that's my theory. I helped build the outer wards. I never saw the foundations."

"You built the wards," Jude said. Not accusing. Just placing the fact on the table between them, the way he placed everything, plainly, letting others pick it up or not.

"I did," Sera said. "And I broke one. And everything since has been me trying to decide whether I'm leading you to salvation or deeper into my mistake." She managed something that was mostly a smile. "The archive will tell us which. If the flaw I hid is written here—in the ward-plans, the maintenance logs—then maybe I can undo it properly instead of just apologizing at it."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then I'll apologize at it louder."

Something that might have been amusement moved through Jude's eyes and was gone. "Anselm wants to hold a service," he said. "He says the archive is the closest thing to a living god left in Thornfell, and he refuses to let the opportunity pass."

"He's not wrong," Sera said. "A country's memory is a kind of divinity. We just never worshipped it properly. We filed it."

"That," said Jude, "sounds like Thornfell."

It did. It sounded exactly like Thornfell, and Sera felt the ache of that, the specific homesickness for a thing currently in the process of ceasing to have ever existed.

* * *

They made camp in the reading alcoves, small stone rooms off the main hall where clerks had once sat copying and cross-referencing. There were desks. There were inkwells, dry as old grief. There was, to Tam's ecstatic disbelief, a surviving jar of pickled plums in a clerk's footlocker, which he shared out with the solemnity of a man distributing relics, and which Fenwick declared "past praying for" and ate six of.

Sera did not rest. She took a lantern into the stacks and began the work she had been dreading since Bellharrow: finding herself in the record.

She found the ward-plans on the third shelf of the eastern range, in a folio bound in grey leather stamped with the sigil of the Office of Boundaries—a compass rose impaled on a quill, because the founders had believed even their heraldry should be slightly threatening. Her own signature was in the maintenance log. *Wards inspected, anchors confirmed, no anomalies noted.* Signed in her younger hand, crisp and confident and lying.

Because there it was, in the structural diagrams, the crack she'd hidden. Not drawn—that would have been too dangerous—but implied, a load-bearing assumption left quietly unstated, a keystone specified in a way that would fail in forty years instead of four hundred. She had done it with arithmetic, which was the coward's chisel. No one had caught it. No one had ever caught it. She had told herself for decades that it was harmless, a private spite against the Director who'd denied her a chair, a little flaw in a great machine, a splinter in a giant's boot.

And then the devourer had come, and found the one door left ajar, and walked through it into a country.

"There you are," she said to her own signature. Her voice echoed strangely in the stacks, and the echo sounded less like her than like the young woman who'd signed the log, smug and small and certain that institutions deserved whatever cracks their servants felt like adding. "You absolute fool."

She was still standing there, hating herself in period-appropriate script, when the light changed.

It wasn't the lantern. The lantern burned steady. But the shadows between the shelves had begun to move—not flickering, not dancing, but *organizing*, the way crows organize, with intent. Sera turned slowly, and the ward-glass at her throat sang one clear warning note.

Between her and the reading alcoves stood a figure made of text.

Not wearing text. Made of it. Lines of dense black script hung in the shape of a person—tall, robed, featureless where a face should be, because where the face should have been the words were struck through. Heavy black bars, row upon row of them, redactions laid over redactions, so that the creature's face was a wall of censor's ink. Where its hands should have been, sentences trailed off mid-clause. When it moved, Sera heard the sound of pages turning, thousands of them, all at once.

It had been a warden once. She knew the type—the Undercroft had employed living keepers in the early centuries, archivists sworn to lifelong service, their oaths woven into the fabric of the place. Somewhere in the last thousand years the oaths had outlived the people, or absorbed them, or replaced them, and this was what remained: duty with no one left inside it, a guard dog grown into the shape of the gate.

"Warden," Sera said carefully. Her mouth was dry. "Keeper of the Record. I am Sera Vey, formerly of the Office of Boundaries, and I come under the old right of inspection—"

The warden raised one trailing hand.

Words peeled off its body and hung in the air between them, assembling themselves into a line of script that glowed faintly, then faded, then reassembled:

**STATE YOUR ██████████ AND ████.**

The blanks were not missing words. They were *removed* ones, and looking at the gaps made Sera's head ache, the way a missing stair makes the leg ache—it kept reaching for footing that wasn't there.

"My name is Sera Vey," she said. "And my purpose is to correct an error in the record."

The warden regarded her. It had no eyes, but she felt examined anyway, thoroughly, the way she imagined a document felt under a censor's pen. Then more words flaked free:

**ALL RECORDS OF THE OFFICE OF BOUNDARIES ARE ████████ PENDING REVIEW.**

"Pending review," Sera repeated. "Pending review by whom? The state hasn't existed for weeks. The state is being *eaten*. You're guarding a vault for a government that no longer—"

The warden took one step forward, and the temperature of the hall dropped, and every ledger on every shelf rustled at once like a field of wheat deciding to be afraid. New words assembled, and these were larger, older, cut in a heavier hand:

**THE STATE IS ITS RECORD. WHILE ONE PAGE REMAINS, THORNFELL REMAINS.**

Sera stared at it. And understood, with a lurch, that the warden was not mad, and not wrong, and not rotting—except in the way that all institutions rot, which is by continuing perfectly after their purpose has died. It was the last loyal servant of Thornfell, guarding the body of the country the way a soldier guards a fallen flag. It could not help them. It could barely *see* them; she doubted it had perceived anything in centuries except filings and infractions. But it was here, and it was intact, and it was the most Thornfell thing left in Thornfell.

Somewhere behind her, Tam's voice floated up from the alcoves, asking Marta whether plums counted as supper or dessert, and Marta answering that in her house they'd counted as both, and the ordinary human noise of it made Sera's eyes sting.

"One page remains," she said softly. "Yes. I believe you."

The warden's redacted face inclined, perhaps a degree. Perhaps in acknowledgment. Perhaps simply settling, the way old buildings settle.

**STATE YOUR ██████████,** it said again, patient as stone, patient as procedure, **AND ████.**

And Sera Vey, who had spent thirty years hiding one flaw in a perfect system, drew a breath, and prepared—for the first time in her life—to file a completely honest report.

Behind the warden, deep in the eastern stacks, something answered before she could speak: a sound like a page turning itself, and then another, and another, coming closer.
