## 11. The Prisoner's Name

The warden did not have a face. It had a filing system.

Jude understood this the way you understand a trap in a smuggler's tunnel—too late to turn back, just early enough to keep your hands steady. The thing stood at the center of the Undercroft's great reading hall, where it had stood, presumably, since before anyone alive was born: a shape assembled from redacted text, thousands of black bars of ink hovering in the rough suggestion of a man, tall and narrow as a door left ajar. Where its eyes should have been there were two small rectangles of censor's black, and when it turned them on you, you felt *filed*.

"State your business," it said. Its voice was the sound of a stamp coming down on paper. "In triplicate."

"We don't have triplicate," Sera said beside him. She had her ward-glass shard cupped in both hands like a candle she was afraid of the wind snuffing. Behind her, huddled among the shelves that held Thornfell's treaties and censuses and last words, the choir waited in a nervous clump. Old Marta had brought her hymnbook. Tam Wicker had brought his dog. Nobody had brought anything useful.

"Then state your business three times," said the warden. "Once for the record. Once for the archive. Once for posterity."

Jude considered this. "We're here because our country is being eaten."

"That," said the warden, "is not my department."

* * *

It had taken two days to get here, down through Graysink's dead mineshafts and along a service road that existed only because Jude remembered falling down it drunk at nineteen—a memory he was now spending like a coin he couldn't get back. He could already feel the edges of that night going soft. There had been a woman. He was almost sure there had been a woman. Her name was gone, and the taste of whatever they'd been drinking was gone, and what remained was mostly the sensation of gravel against his cheek and a distant, cheerful conviction that tomorrow would sort itself out.

A fair trade, probably. Thirty-one people were alive because of it.

Now they stood in the one room in all of Thornfell that the Gilded Hollow had not yet reached—the Undercroft, buried deep beneath what had once been the Hall of Records in the capital, back when the capital had a name and the Hall had windows. It was vast and dry and lit by lamps that burned without oil, and every shelf was full, and everything in it was true, and none of it was helping.

Because here was the thing about an archive: it kept records, but it didn't keep *people*. Jude had spent the morning walking the stacks while Sera negotiated—or tried to negotiate—with the warden, running his fingers along spines labeled CENSUS 311, TREATY OF THE TWO FORKS, EXECUTIONS: FINAL STATEMENTS, VOL. IX. He'd found his own village listed in a census from his boyhood. Found his father's name. Found the number of goats they'd owned. (Four. He'd forgotten the fourth goat entirely; her name had been Duchess, and she had bitten him, and now even the bite was fading.) But finding a record wasn't the same as remembering. The census knew more about Duchess than he did now, and the census didn't care about her at all.

That was the horror of the place, if you let yourself look at it straight. Thornfell had written everything down so it wouldn't have to remember anything. And now something was eating the country, and the country's whole memory sat here underground, complete and catalogued and utterly useless, guarded by a creature made of censorship.

"You must have some department," Sera was saying to the warden. She had the particular patience of a woman who had spent years arguing with bureaucratic wards and had learned to treat them like weather. "Something ate the border wards. Something is eating the roads, the names, the stars. That has to fall under someone's jurisdiction."

"The dissolution of national identity," said the warden, "is handled by the Office of Dissolution of National Identity."

"Where is that office?"

"It was dissolved." A pause, in which Jude could have sworn the black bars rearranged themselves into something like embarrassment. "By the Office of Dissolution of National Identity. Before their own dissolution. They were thorough. It was their only virtue."

Tam Wicker, from the back, made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled by fear. His dog, Bristle, sneezed.

Jude stepped forward. He'd learned something in thirty years of smuggling, and it was this: every gatekeeper, no matter how stone-faced, wants something. Wardens want order. Priests want worship. Toll-keepers want coin. Even gods want something—Hollow had wanted to be remembered, though it had never once said so out loud, being a god of silence and therefore contractually incapable of honesty.

"What do you want?" he asked the warden directly. "Not your department. You. What does a warden of redacted text want?"

The black rectangles fixed on him. For a long moment the thing said nothing, and the lamps hummed, and somewhere far above them the world continued to be unmade.

"No one has asked me that," the warden said finally, "in four hundred years."

"Well," said Jude. "I've been told I'm rude."

"I want," said the warden, and the stamp-sound of its voice went strange, went almost soft, "a complete file."

"A what?"

"A complete file. Every document in this archive is incomplete. Treaties with pages torn out. Censuses with lines struck through. Executions—" it paused, and the pause had weight, like a body settling, "—executions with names removed. I am a warden of records that have been *edited*. Do you understand? I guard a house where every room has a wall missing. I cannot make a single complete account of anything. Not one life. Not one crime. Not one death." The bars of it shivered. "I want one complete file. Then I could die."

"Wardens can die?"

"Everything in Thornfell can die," said the warden. "That is rather the present difficulty."

* * *

They sat down among the shelves to think, which is to say Sera thought, Jude chewed the inside of his cheek, and the choir ate the last of the hard cheese and argued quietly about whether the warden counted as a person or a piece of furniture. Old Marta held that anything that wanted something was a person. Tam held that by that logic his dog was a person, and Marta said yes, obviously, and Tam had no answer prepared for that.

Jude drifted back to the execution volumes. He didn't know why. Instinct, maybe—the same instinct that told him which passes were watched and which bridges were rotten. If the warden wanted a complete file, then somewhere in this building was an incomplete one that mattered enough to bargain with. And if something had eaten a god, eaten a country, eaten the stars—

He pulled Volume IX.

EXECUTIONS: FINAL STATEMENTS. The spine was intact. The contents were not. Page after page of condemned men and women, each entry the same: date, crime, sentence—and then, where the prisoner's name should be, a black bar. Not ink. Worse than ink. A bar of *absence*, the same black that floated in the warden's face. The state hadn't crossed the names out. It had removed them so thoroughly that even the record of removal had been removed. You could see the shape of the hole. You could not see what had been holed.

And beside each blacked-out name, in a clerk's careful hand, a note:

*Sentence ratified under the Edict of Unremembering. Name struck from all registers, rolls, stones, and hearts. Let the condemned be neither mourned nor cursed. Let them be nothing.*

There were eleven entries. Eleven people, over two centuries, whose names Thornfell had decided to erase so completely that they would stop existing—not die, which leaves a grave, but *stop*. Jude read the crimes. Desertion. Treason. One man had stolen grain during a famine; the magistrate had called it treason against the harvest. Another had been a priest of a rival god who'd refused to stop saying her god's name aloud.

And the eleventh entry—the most recent, dated sixty years ago—had a longer note appended, in a different hand. Not a clerk's hand. This handwriting was old and slanted and sure of itself, the writing of someone who had signed important things for a very long time and enjoyed it.

*Special ratification. Sentence composed in consultation with the Silent God, who attests that unremembering is the only punishment proportionate to the crime. Crime: the prisoner attempted to speak a true name into the foundations of the capital. Sentence carried out in full.*

Hollow.

Jude sat down on the floor between the shelves, because his legs had opinions about standing.

His god. His silent, patient, beloved god, who had spoken to him once over a crate of stolen brandy and never raised its voice again. Hollow, who taught him that memory was the only honest currency. Hollow, whose last command had been *remember the roads*. Hollow had helped write this. Had sat across a table from magistrates and helped them design a punishment worse than hanging: the unmaking of a man so complete that hunger was all that was left of him.

He did the arithmetic the way you do it when you don't want to, slowly, hoping the numbers come out wrong.

Eleven erased names. One devourer wearing beaten gold and a stolen face, offering people their happiest memories back in exchange for the rest. Generous. Patient. *Hungry.* A thing that had been a person, whose personhood had been removed so thoroughly that only appetite remained—an appetite shaped exactly like the hole the state had cut.

The Gilded Hollow wasn't a monster that had come from outside.

It was a sentence, still being carried out.

"Oh," said Sera, behind him. She'd followed him into the stacks and was reading over his shoulder, and her voice came out flat and small. "Oh, we did this. We—*they* did this. We built the machine ourselves and fed it a man."

"Not we," Jude said. His voice sounded fine, which seemed obscene. "Them. The clerks. The magistrates." He tapped the second handwriting. "And him."

Sera looked at the signature block. There was no signature. Of course there wasn't. But the attestation line said *the Silent God*, and Sera Vey was a ward-scribe, and ward-scribes know what divine handwriting looks like when it's been pressed into mortal ink.

She sat down next to him on the cold floor. For a while neither of them said anything, and the lamps hummed, and somewhere above them the stars stayed gone.

"My grandmother was executed under the third edict," Sera said at last. "I found her entry when I was nineteen. Black bar where her name should be. I used to come down here and talk at the bar. Tell it things. 'You were called Anwen,' I'd say. 'You liked honey. You sang badly.' Like I could fill the hole with my voice." She laughed, without any humor in it at all. "I told myself the edicts were necessary. Dangerous names, dangerous knowledge. Somebody has to hold the shears. And then I put my flaw in the wards partly to spite the people who held them, and the thing that crawled through my crack turned out to be—" she gestured at the volume, at the black bars, at everything, "—this. A man we erased, coming back to erase us right back. Justice's machinery, running exactly as designed."

"Don't," Jude said.

"Don't what?"

"Don't take the blame alone. It spreads it too thin." He closed the volume. "There's plenty to go around. The clerks. The magistrates. The god I swore to. Me—I prayed to Hollow every night for twenty years. Every prayer was a brick, apparently. I built the cage too, one *hollow keep me* at a time."

The warden appeared at the end of the aisle. It moved without moving, the way text doesn't move but a paragraph ends up somewhere else anyway.

"You have found the file," it said.

"We've found a file," Jude said. "It's not complete. That's rather the point."

"Yes." The black rectangles regarded them. "Volume Nine, Entry Eleven. The most incomplete file in the archive. The name was removed from every register, every roll, every stone, every heart. Even I cannot restore it. I do not know what the black bar covers. I have guarded it for sixty years and I do not know what it says." The bars shivered again, and Jude realized the warden was doing the equivalent of wringing its hands. "But you are god-sworn. The choir's blind priest named you so, and archives honor titles. God-sworn means the Silent God holds a copy of your memories. Which means—"

"Which means somewhere in me might be a piece of the name," Jude finished. "If Hollow ever mentioned it."

"Hollow wrote the sentence," Sera said. "Gods don't write sentences without knowing the name they're erasing. You can't unremember a thing you haven't fully remembered first."

The warden inclined its bar-shaped head. "Bring me the name, god-sworn, and I will give you what the archive holds: the trial record, the prisoner's effects, the founding charter of this vault, and safe passage for your people through the deep stacks. A complete file, freely given, in exchange for the one word that completes it."

"And if I bring you the name?" Jude asked. "What happens to it? To him?"

The warden was quiet for a long time. When it answered, the stamp-sound was gone from its voice entirely, and what remained was older and tireder and terribly, bureaucratically kind.

"When a file is completed, it is closed. Closed files rest." A pause. "I would like to rest. So, I think, would he."

* * *

They told the choir around the evening meal, such as it was—barley porridge made with lamp-warmed water, eaten off archive ledgers because nobody had plates. Jude laid it out plainly, because he'd learned that frightened people can carry almost any truth if you don't dress it up first.

"The thing chasing us was a man. The state erased his name. My god helped. We're going to find the name and give it back."

Silence. Then Tam Wicker said, "Give it *back*? Won't that—won't that finish the job? Make it stronger?"

"Or end it," said Old Marta. "A debt unpaid keeps a ghost walking. Pay the debt, the ghost lies down." She licked her spoon. "My mother said that about my father. She was wrong about most things but right about debts."

"And how do we find a name that an entire country was engineered to forget?" asked a choirwoman named Pell, who had lost her husband to the Gilded Man's offer and spoke of it the way you speak of weather.

Jude looked at the black bar in his mind, the one where the eleventh entry's name should be, and felt the shape of the hole the way you feel a missing tooth with your tongue.

"There's one place it might still live," he said. "Inside the god that erased it. And the god is dead, or swallowed, or playing a game I don't understand yet. But I swore oaths over stolen goods for twenty years, and oath-magic runs backward as well as forward. If the name passed through Hollow, then Hollow's shrine, Hollow's rites, Hollow's old altars—they're not empty. They're *echoes*." He turned his spoon over. "I go back through the catechism. Backward. Feed the silence my own memories until it gives me back what it took."

"Your memories," Sera said sharply. "Jude. You've seen what spending them costs. You gave up a night of your youth for one road. The name of a deliberately unmade man—that's not one memory. That could be years. That could be—"

"Everything before a certain day," he agreed. "Maybe everything, period."

"Then I'll do it. I'm a scribe. Memory is practically my trade—"

"You hid a flaw in the wards," Jude said, not unkindly. "Your memory of those years is compromised and you know it. The rite needs clean vessels." He looked around the lamplit circle of faces—Marta, Tam, Pell, Bristle the dog, thirty-odd souls who called him god-sworn because they needed the title to mean something. "Besides. It's my god that did this. My prayers in the walls. My account to settle."

Old Marta snorted. "Men," she said, "always volunteer for the martyrdom with the best view."

"It's not martyrdom," Jude said. "It's smuggling. I'm going into the biggest vault there is to lift one name out from under a god's floorboards." He managed half a smile. "Worst case, I come back not knowing my own birthday. Best case, we all walk out of this country alive and I never have to hear another hymn about silence as long as I live."

Tam raised his tin cup. "To worst cases."

"To worst cases," the choir echoed, and drank lamp-water, and it was almost, for one moment, a warm thing to be part of.

Later, when the others slept among the shelves wrapped in every coat they owned, Jude sat alone with Volume IX open on his knees and the black bar in front of him. He pressed his thumb against it. It was cold, colder than the page, colder than the floor, a cold with intention behind it.

"Whoever you were," he said quietly, to the bar, to the man, to the hunger wearing gold in the dark above them, "you liked something once. You had a mother who said your name wrong on purpose to make you laugh. You had a favorite season." His thumb ached against the cold. "I'm going to find it. Not for the warden. Not even for my god. For you. Because somebody in this whole rotten machine ought to say it plain: what they did to you was not justice. It was just machinery."

The bar did not answer. Bars don't.

But when he finally slept, he dreamed of a courtroom, and in the courtroom a clerk leaned toward him across the desk, and the clerk's face was a page of black bars, and it whispered in the stamp-voice of the warden and the slanted confident hand of a god at once:

*"Sixty years I have waited for someone to read the file all the way to the end. Be careful, god-sworn. The last page is written in your handwriting too."*

Above ground, the first snow of the year began to fall—weeks early, fat and silent and wrong, covering the roads Jude remembered before he could forget them properly.
