## 12. Winter Falls Early

The first snow came on the eve of Harvestmoon, which was wrong, because Harvestmoon was supposed to smell of woodsmoke and cider and the particular sweetness of apples rotting in the ditches, not of cold that bit through wool like it had a grudge.

Marta Colloway knew this because she had lived in Thornfell for sixty-one years, and she had baked the festival loaves for forty of them, and she was not going to let some weather tell her what season it was.

"It's winter," said Tam, her grandson, who was nine and had the family gift for stating the obvious at the worst possible moment.

"It is *early*," Marta corrected, hauling the tent flap shut against a wind that tasted of iron. "There's a difference. Early means it can be argued with."

She had been arguing with things all month. She'd argued with the road out of Graysink when it started going soft underfoot, like bread left in milk. She'd argued with the sky when the stars went missing. She'd argued most vigorously with the priest, Old Anselm, when he'd declared that blind Jude Dravenmoor—well, not blind, but god-blind, which amounted to the same thing in Marta's experience—was "the god-sworn" and therefore their salvation.

Salvation, as far as she could tell, looked like a man walking at the front of a column of ninety-odd frightened people, muttering to himself about roads, occasionally stopping to touch the ground with his palm as though checking whether the world still counted as real.

Mostly it didn't.

But here was the thing Marta would never say aloud, because saying it aloud felt like inviting trouble in for supper: the roads *did* hold where he walked. She'd watched them do it. A stretch of track outside Graysink had gone thin as onion skin, translucent, showing the grey nothing underneath—and then Jude had stood on it and remembered it, jaw tight, eyes shut, and the road had firmed up beneath his boots like dough rising.

He'd paid for it too. She'd seen him afterward, patting his pockets for something, frowning, asking Tam whether he carried a whetstone. He didn't carry a whetstone. He'd never carried a whetstone. Something had gone out of him to make that road solid, and whatever it was, he missed it the way you miss a tooth.

So Marta kept her arguments to herself and did what she did best, which was feed people. The supply wagons were down to hardtack, salt pork gone grey, and a sack of dried plums that Tam kept sneaking from despite three separate warnings and one memorable incident involving a switched backside. She stretched everything. She made soup out of snow and bones and stubbornness. When the choir sang their evening hymns—the ones that kept their names from slipping, each person singing their own name woven into the verse like a thread through cloth—Marta sang hers loudest, because if oblivion was coming for her name, it was going to have to work for it.

That night, huddled in the tent with Tam asleep against her side, she heard the wind change its mind.

It didn't howl anymore. It *hummed*. Low and golden and sweet, like a hymn sung by someone who had never known grief, and underneath the humming, faint as a coin dropped in another room, a voice said: *you look tired, grandmother. wouldn't you like to rest?*

Marta pulled Tam closer and sang her own name into the dark until morning.

* * *

Sera Vey had never been warm a day in her life, and now the universe appeared to be making up for lost time by freezing her specifically.

She crouched at the edge of the road with the ward-glass shard held up to the grey light, watching frost crawl across its surface in patterns that were almost, almost legible. The glass showed what places used to be—that was its whole trick, its one small honest magic—and lately what it mostly showed was absence. Hold it over a village and you saw the shape of streets nobody could name. Hold it over a well and you saw the faces of people who'd drawn water there for two hundred years, smiling up out of the past like fish under ice.

Hold it over herself, sometimes, at night, when no one was looking, and see the young scribe she'd been, ink-stained and arrogant, carving her little spiteful flaw into the border wards while her superiors drank themselves stupid in the ward-house above.

*I did this,* she thought, for perhaps the ten-thousandth time. *I opened the door.*

The cold made the guilt cleaner, somehow. Sharper-edged. Easier to hold without flinching.

"Sera." Jude's voice behind her. He moved quietly for a big man; she suspected he'd learned it smuggling, or grieving, or both. "You've been out here an hour. Your fingers are going grey."

"They're always grey. I'm a scribe. Ink gets everywhere."

"That's not ink."

She tucked the shard away and stood, joints crackling like green firewood. Behind them the choir's camp sprawled along the frozen road—ninety-three souls now, after the Vellum twins wandered off last week and were found two days later standing in a field, unable to say their own names, wearing expressions of polite blankness. They'd recovered, mostly. Mostly.

"The roads are done," Sera said. No point softening it. "The one south of here went solid overnight—not memory-solid, *ice*-solid. It froze mid-fade. You can see the nothing through it, like ice over a pond. We can walk on it, but every step sounds wrong, and I don't trust it past noon."

Jude nodded slowly. He'd been doing that more—nodding slowly, like a man agreeing to terms he hadn't read. "And the supplies?"

"Marta says eight days. Ten if we eat the mules, and before you ask, yes, she has already named the mules, which means we will not be eating the mules."

Something that was almost a smile crossed his face and died there. "Then we move today."

"There's something else." Sera hesitated. She'd heard it at dawn, moving through the camp: the talk, low and constant as meltwater. Words like *curse* and *ward-witch* and *she brought it with her*. Words aimed, always, in her direction, by people careful not to aim them where she could hear. Except she could hear. Guilt sharpened the ears. "They're going to vote on me, Jude."

"I know."

"You *know*?"

"I've got ears too." He turned to face her fully, and even half-frozen and hollowed out by weeks of burning his own memories into the road, he had a way of looking at a person that made them feel audited. "Anselm came to me at first light. He tried to make it sound gentle. He's bad at gentle. Then Hesk and the ferryman's widow, and they weren't even trying."

"And?"

"And I'm going to tell them no."

Sera laughed—a short, ugly sound, steam curling from her mouth. "You can't just *tell them no*. It's a vote. Ninety people, Jude. They're starving and scared and I'm the reason, and they know it, or some folk-tale version of it, and fear does arithmetic faster than mercy does."

"Fear does arithmetic wrong," Jude said. "It always rounds up."

* * *

They held the vote at midday, in the lee of a frozen waystation whose walls had gone translucent at the corners, so that the assembly seemed to gather inside a soap bubble.

Hesk the carter spoke first, because Hesk always spoke first; he had the courage of a man who had never once reconsidered a sentence. "We say it plain," he announced, breath steaming. "The ward-witch built the wards. The wards broke. Everything since—the stars, the roads, my brother's name, which I have to sing twenty times a night now to keep hold of—all of it comes through *her*. She's marked. Bad luck walks behind her like a dog. We leave her."

A rumble of assent. Not universal—Marta Colloway stood with her arms crossed and her chin out, radiating dissent—but enough. More than enough.

Old Anselm rose, sightless eyes fixed on some middle distance where perhaps gods used to be. "I don't say cast her out to die," he said, which was the kind of sentence that should have warned everyone involved. "I say she walks apart. Behind us. Her own fire, her own food. If the curse follows her, let it follow her and not us."

"She'll freeze," said Marta flatly.

"Better her than—"

"Than *you*?" Marta's voice cracked across the assembly like a whip. Sixty-one years of scolding bakers' apprentices had given her a delivery that could curdle cream at thirty paces. "Which of you is volunteering to be next? Because that's the arithmetic, isn't it? Today it's the stranger, the scribe, the one who did the thing. Tomorrow it's whoever dreamed wrong, or ate the last plum, or—" she jabbed a finger at Tam, who was attempting to look innocent with plum juice on his collar, "—or stole from the stores. I've seen a hungry crowd decide things. It doesn't stop deciding."

"We're not hungry crowds," Hesk began. "We're the faithful—"

"You're a hungry crowd with hymns."

Into the silence that followed, Jude Dravenmoor stood up.

He didn't raise his voice. That was the thing about him that unnerved Sera most, more than the god-silence, more than the way he touched the ground to check if it was real: he never raised his voice, and somehow everyone heard him anyway.

"No," he said.

"It's been voted," said Hesk.

"I'm vetoing it."

"You can't *veto* a vote!"

"I'm the god-sworn." Jude's tone was mild, almost apologetic, and absolutely immovable. "That's what it means. Anselm named me so, and you all agreed, and I've spent every day since paying for your roads out of my own head. So here's my ruling, and it's the last ruling I've got in me: Sera Vey walks with us. In front, where I can see her. And anyone who wants to leave her behind is welcome to try navigating the fading country on their own, and I will personally remember the road for them as far as the first crossroads, and after that they're on their own memory, and we all know how that's holding up."

Silence. Somewhere in the bubble-walled waystation, ice ticked and settled.

"The roads are yours?" asked the ferryman's widow, in a small voice. "Only yours?"

"Only mine." Jude let that sit. "Which means the question isn't whether Sera stays. The question is whether you'd rather be cold and together or warm and lost. I've seen lost. Lost looks like the Vellum twins in that field, smiling at nothing. I'd choose cold."

Nobody left. That was the miracle of it, Sera thought later—nobody left. Hesk grumbled and sat back down. Anselm prayed, loudly, in a direction that might have been toward Jude or might have been toward the absent Hollow, hard to say. Marta distributed soup with the air of a general resupplying a garrison that had performed adequately.

And Sera stood at the edge of the assembly, cleared of the charge, acquitted by fiat, and found that acquittal sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Because they were right about the facts, every one of them. She *had* built the wards. She *had* broken them. Being forgiven by frightened people didn't change the ledger; it only meant the debt had changed hands.

That night she took out the ward-glass shard and held it over the frozen road, and for the first time in weeks, the glass showed her something new.

Not the road. Not the past. Frost crawled across the surface and formed, line by line, a pattern she recognized with a sick lurch of the stomach: ward-script. Her own ward-script, the notation she'd invented, the flaw she'd carved—

But written *backward*. As if the wards were meant to be read from the other side.

Sera stared at it until her fingers numbed, and a thought began to assemble itself in her mind, slow and terrible, like something rising through dark water: *the wards were never a wall.*

*They were a cage.*

And if they were a cage—then Thornfell wasn't a country that had been invaded.

It was a prison that had been breached. From either side.

Behind her, far off across the frozen fields, the golden humming started again, sweeter than ever, and this time Sera could have sworn she heard words in it—words in ward-script, her own notation, sung in a voice like struck brass:

*thank you for the door, little scribe. thank you for the door.*
