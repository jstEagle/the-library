## 13. What I Forgot at Bellharrow

The shard of ward-glass had been in Sera Vey's pocket for so long that she'd stopped thinking of it as an object and started thinking of it as a companion. It was the size of a thumbnail, warm as a held coin, and it showed her things that no longer existed: a bridge over the Alderwater that had been eaten in autumn, her mother's kitchen with its crooked blue shelf, the boundary stone at Harrow's Edge before someone—before *she*—had sanded it smooth.

She sat by the fire in the ruined shepherd's hut where the choir had sheltered since the vote. The vote she'd lost. The vote Jude had vetoed with three words and a look that could have frozen the Alderwater all on its own.

*She stays,* he'd said. *That's final.*

And now half the choir slept facing the wall rather than face her, and the other half slept too close to the fire out of some superstitious belief that proximity to warmth was proximity to goodness, and Sera sat apart with her guilt and her glass and did what ward-scribes do when they cannot sleep.

She checked her work.

"You're muttering again," said Tam Broadfoot, the old drover who had appointed himself her unofficial keeper sometime around the second week of winter. He settled onto the bench beside her with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions about everything. "Muttering and rubbing that bit of glass like it's a wart you're fond of."

"It's not a wart."

"Didn't say you were fond of warts. Said you rubbed it like one." He produced, from somewhere in his enormous coat, two roasted turnips and handed her one without ceremony. "Eat. You've got the look of a woman arguing with herself and losing."

"I'm not arguing. I'm reading." She turned the shard over in her fingers. "Ward-glass remembers what it was cast to hold. That's the whole principle. A ward is a sentence spoken over a place, and the glass keeps the grammar. If I can read the grammar, I can read the sentence."

"And?"

"And the sentence doesn't make sense." She said it quietly, because saying it loudly would make it real, and she wasn't ready for it to be real. "I built these wards, Tam. Well—I helped. I was junior under Warden-Cantor Ossel, who believed that complexity was a form of piety. And there's a flaw in the lattice. My flaw. I hid it years ago because Ossel refused to credit my work unless I made it worse first, so I made it worse in a way only I could find, out of spite, which I have since learned is not one of the seven virtues."

Tam chewed his turnip thoughtfully. "Sounds like Ossel had it coming."

"Ossel had it coming," Sera agreed. "But the country didn't." She looked into the fire. "Except here's the thing that's been keeping me awake. The flaw works. The devourer came through my crack like water through a bad seam. But when I finally read the full grammar tonight—the whole sentence, every clause—the flaw isn't a seam at all. It's a hinge."

"A hinge."

"A door, Tam. A deliberate, load-bearing door, set into the structure by whoever designed the lattice in the first place. And I know the hand that cut it, because I've spent twenty years imitating it." Her voice went strange and small. "It's not Ossel's hand. It's older. It's *liturgical*. It's the same cadence the smugglers use in their road-prayers—the ones Jude swears by. The cadence of Hollow."

The fire popped. Somewhere across the hut, a child of the choir whimpered in sleep and was hushed by its mother.

"That's a grim thing to say about a god," Tam offered.

"Yes," said Sera. "It gets grimmer."

* * *

She should have seen it from the beginning. That was the worst part—not the betrayal itself but the evidence of how long she'd been walking past it, whistling.

Wards keep things out. Every apprentice learned it with their first chalk line: the circle faces outward, the intent faces outward, the whole architecture of a border-ward is a wall with its face turned away from the country it protects. Thornfell's wards faced inward.

All of them. Every boundary stone, every pass-lattice, every river-anchor from the Alderwater to the icefields—they were inscribed on the inner faces. She had catalogued them herself, in her first year, and noted the anomaly, and been told by Ossel that it was "traditional orientation," and she had written *traditional orientation* in her notes like a sensible person instead of doing what she now wanted to travel back in time and do, which was shake her young self by the shoulders and shout.

A wall that faces inward isn't a wall. It's a cell.

She worked through the night with the shard held close to the firelight, translating clause after clause of the founding grammar. The lattice wasn't keyed to Thornfell's borders at all. The borders were drawn *around* the lattice. The wards predated the nation—she'd always assumed the reverse, because everyone assumed the reverse, because the alternative was unthinkable—and they weren't shaped like a country. They were shaped like a fist.

At the center of the geometry, at the exact point where every line of force converged, there was a name. Not written—wards never wrote names, names were handles and handles were dangerous—but *implied*, the way a footprint implies a foot. A absence shaped exactly like a person. A person-shaped hole in the middle of Thornfell, wrapped in gold-colored light, bound by clauses that read less like protection and more like sentencing.

*Sentence to be served within these bounds. Term to be measured in forgetting. Release conditional upon nothing, for there is no condition under which this prisoner becomes safe.*

Sera read that last clause four times. Then she put the shard down, walked outside into the freezing dark, and was sick behind the woodpile, with dignity, mostly.

When she came back in, Tam was still awake, still feeding the fire, watching her with the patient eyes of a man who had driven livestock through forty winters and knew that sometimes the animal needed to panic before it could be led anywhere useful.

"Well?" he said.

"The wards are a prison," she said. "Thornfell isn't being destroyed, Tam. Thornfell is the prison. The roads, the records, the memories dissolving—that's not the devourer eating us. That's the walls wearing thin. We live inside the sentence. We've always lived inside the sentence. Every harvest festival, every census, every child born in Bellharrow—we were all born inside somebody else's punishment."

"Whose punishment?"

"That's the question, isn't it." She wiped her mouth on her sleeve. "There's a shape at the center. A person-shaped hole. Someone was put here, long ago, and erased so thoroughly that even the prison forgot it was holding a person and started thinking it was holding a hunger. And the architect—" her voice caught, and she hated it, and pushed through—"the architect of the whole thing signed it in Hollow's own cadence. Our god built the cage. Our god has been sitting inside our prayers for centuries, and we thought we were worshipping a protector."

Tam was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, "My wife used to say the kindest people build the strongest fences."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"Dunno, entirely. She said a lot of things. But I think it means folk don't lock things up unless they're afraid of them, and folk don't get afraid unless they've got something worth losing." He poked the fire. "Maybe your god was scared. Maybe scared gods do ugly arithmetic."

"Maybe," said Sera. "Or maybe it just didn't consider us worth counting."

* * *

Morning came gray and late, the way winter mornings did now, as if the sun were reporting reluctantly for duty. The choir stirred. Jude appeared at her elbow while she was packing the shard away, moving without sound the way he always did, a big man who walked like an apology.

"You found something," he said. Not a question. He'd watched her come back in the night; of course he had. The man noticed everything except, apparently, that people found him alarming.

"I found something." She hesitated. This was the part she'd rehearsed, and the rehearsal fell apart the moment she looked at him—at the hollows under his eyes, at the way his hand drifted to his chest where the god-sworn mark sat under his shirt. "Jude. Before I say it, I need you to understand that I'm telling you because you deserve the truth, not because I'm certain of it. Certainty left me around the time I realized I'd personally opened the door for all of this."

"Say it."

"The wards face inward. All of them. They always have. Thornfell isn't a country with defenses—it's a cell with furniture. The devourer isn't breaking in. It's pressing against the bars from the inside, and we're standing between it and the door." She made herself go on. "And the lattice was laid down in Hollow's cadence. The god didn't fail to stop this. The god *built* this."

She braced for anger. What she got was worse: stillness. Jude stood in the gray light with his breath steaming, and his face did something complicated and quiet, like a man hearing a diagnosis he'd already suspected and hoped against.

"How long," he said finally.

"The lattice predates the border stones. Centuries. Maybe more."

"No." His voice was very level. "How long have *you* known? Tonight? Or"—and here his eyes came up, and they were terrible and gentle at once—"or did you suspect at Bellharrow, when you recruited me, and decide the mercenary didn't need to know he was working for the jailer?"

The question landed like a dropped anvil. Because the truth was that she had known *something* at Bellharrow—she'd felt the wrongness in the wards the moment the stars went out, the specific wrongness of a mechanism reversing—and she had recruited him anyway, quickly, before she finished understanding why, because a god-sworn smuggler was useful and questions were slow.

"I suspected the wards were compromised," she said. "I didn't know they were a cage. I swear it on the glass."

He studied her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he almost smiled—a thin, cold, tired thing. "You know what the funny part is, Sera? I believe you. Because lying is a craft, and you're terrible at it. Your hands shake."

"My hands always shake. I'm a scribe. We drink too much tea."

"There it is." He turned to look north, toward the Undercroft, toward whatever answer waited under the ice. "Hollow gave me one command. One. *Keep them remembered.* I've been carrying it like scripture. And if the god that spoke it spent centuries building a machine that unmakes memory—if every prayer I ever said was mortar in this wall—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "Then either everything I believe is a lie, or the lie goes deeper than I can reach. Either way, I want the architect's answer. Face to face."

"The gilded figure wears Hollow's face," Sera said carefully. "If you meet it—"

"Then I'll ask it what my god did." He said it simply, the way men say things that will break them later. "And then I'll decide what to do with the answer."

He walked off to rouse the choir, calling names aloud as he went—each survivor's name, spoken clearly, twice, the ritual he performed every morning since Graysink—and Sera stood alone in the gray light with the shard warm in her pocket, thinking about hinges.

Because there was one more thing she hadn't told him. One more thing she'd found in the founding grammar, in the margin of the sentencing clauses, in handwriting so old it was barely handwriting anymore:

*A door may be opened only by the prisoner's name, spoken by one who remembers.*

The state had erased the name. The archives would know what the state had erased. Which meant the answer wasn't in the mountains or the mines or the gilded thing's open hand.

It was underneath. In the vault. In the official memory of a country that had never been a country at all.

"We're going to the Undercroft," she said to no one, to the frost, to the ghost of a god she no longer knew how to pray to. "And I think we're going to find out who you locked up, Hollow. And I think you're going to wish we hadn't."
