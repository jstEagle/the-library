## 14. The God Who Lied First

Jude had faced down border reavers, tax collectors, and one memorable bear with a taste for dried plums, but nothing in his long career of bad decisions had prepared him for the moment the Gilded Man sat down across from him and asked for tea.

They had made camp in the shell of a waystation inn above the frozen Ashfall road—a place Jude remembered well enough to keep real, though lately "remembering" felt less like holding a rope and more like feeding a fire with the floorboards of your own house. The choir slept in shifts around a peat fire that burned green and sullen. Sera Vey sat apart, turning her shard of ward-glass over and over like a woman trying to read a letter through the back of the envelope.

And at midnight, without wind or warning, the Gilded Man walked out of the dark and knocked on the doorframe. Politely. Twice.

Jude had his knife out before the second knock finished. It didn't help. Knives never helped with things made of gold; they just left you holding a knife while something patient looked at you.

"You're awake," the figure said. Its voice was Hollow's voice—the low, dry warmth of it, the voice that had lived in the back of Jude's skull since he was nineteen years old and swearing oaths over other people's property. But the face was wrong. Not wrong the way a mask is wrong. Wrong the way a bell is wrong when the crack has changed its note forever. Hollow's face, beaten into gold, hammered thin, dented along the jaw where—Jude would have sworn on anything you liked—where *he* had once pressed his thumb, years ago, in some dream he'd told no one about.

"You wear my god's face," Jude said.

"I wear what's left of him." The Gilded Man folded itself down onto the bench opposite, joints moving with a sound like coins counted slowly. "He's very quiet these days. You'd know. You've been talking to a silence for months and calling it faith."

Sera was on her feet, shard hidden in her sleeve, eyes wide. Jude put out one hand without looking at her. *Stay.* She stayed. That was the thing about being god-sworn: even when the god was gone, the swearing kept working on people.

"There's tea," Jude said, because his mouth did stupid things when he was frightened, and because the alternative was violence, and violence against this creature was like punching a ledger. "If you drink."

"I don't. But I'll watch you drink yours. It helps me remember what thirst was." The golden head tilted. "You want to know why I'm here. Everyone always wants to know why I'm here. The answer is: because you prayed me here. All of you. Every prayer in Thornfell for three hundred years was a brick, and I am the house they built, and now the house is hungry, and you are standing in the pantry."

"That's not an answer," Jude said. "That's a threat wearing poetry."

The Gilded Man smiled, and the smile was Hollow's smile, and Jude hated how much it hurt to see it.

* * *

Here is what Jude knew about Hollow, going into that night:

He knew the god of smugglers and forgotten roads. He knew the silence that answered prayers—not with words, never with words, but with *arrangements*. A bridge that held when it should have fallen. A patrol that turned down the wrong valley, twice, on the same night. A guard dog that decided Jude smelled like family. Hollow did not speak. Hollow *adjusted*. And in exchange, Jude kept the roads in his memory, spoke the catechism over stolen goods, and never, ever asked what the god wanted beyond the keeping of ways.

That last part—that was the prayer. Every smuggler knew it. *Keep the roads, keep the names, ask nothing.* Jude had said it ten thousand times. He had taught it to children in Graysink. He had built his whole life inside that sentence like a snail builds inside its shell, and it had never once occurred to him that a shell is also a wall, and walls are built by someone, for reasons, and the someone rarely asks the snail's opinion.

"Ask me the question," the Gilded Man said now, watching him across the cold hearth. "The one you've been carrying since Bellharrow. Since the stars went out. Ask it, and I'll answer true. That's my nature, unfortunately. Truth is the only currency I can't counterfeit."

"Why did Hollow go silent?" Jude said. "Why did the wards fall? Why Thornfell?"

"Ah." The Gilded Man settled deeper, gold creaking. "You're asking three questions and pretending they're one. Very smuggler. Very *him*. Fine. One answer, then: because your god planned all of it."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the loft, one of the choir murmured a name in their sleep—Marta, maybe, or Marta's mother—and the sound of it was so small and so human that Jude wanted to weep and laugh at once.

"Explain," he said.

"Hollow found me first, you understand. Before the wards. Before Thornfell had edges worth defending. I was small then. A hunger with a man's shape, wandering a country that hadn't learned to be one yet. And Hollow—clever, patient, *terrified* Hollow—looked at me and saw what I would become if I fed freely. So it didn't fight me. Fighting me would have been honest, and gods are very bad at honest. Instead it built a country around me like a fist closing. Wards for knuckles. Borders for bone. And it called the whole arrangement *protection*, and the people of Thornfell thanked it, and paid it in prayer, and every prayer was mortar."

"No," Jude said. It came out flat and automatic, the word you say when the world tilts. "The wards were to keep *you out*."

"The wards were to keep me *in*." The Gilded Man spread its hands, and the lamplight slid across them like water off a roof. "Ask your scribe. She helped build the cage. She thinks she hid a flaw in it out of spite. She hid a *door*, and I walked through it, and do you know why the flaw existed at all? Because a perfect cage needs a perfect jailer, and Hollow was already planning to be somewhere else."

Jude turned his head, slowly, and looked at Sera. Her face in the firelight was gray as old ash. She shook her head—not denial; she didn't know yet, he could see that—but the shape of her not-knowing had just changed forever.

"And Hollow?" His voice came out rough. "Where was your jailer?"

"Inside me." For the first time, the Gilded Man's voice lost its smoothness. Something under the gold moved, the way something moves under ice. "When the wards cracked, Hollow didn't fall. Hollow *dove*. Let itself be swallowed whole, down my throat, into the dark at the center of me, carrying poison. Its own death, brewed slow. It has been dissolving me from within ever since. Every star that went out, every road that faded, every name the choir sings to keep—those aren't me eating Thornfell, mercenary. Those are the two of us killing each other, and Thornfell is the table we're bleeding on."

"Acceptable loss," Jude said. The phrase tasted like copper.

"His words. Not mine." The Gilded Man leaned forward, and up close its face was worse than any monster's, because it was *familiar*. "I offer people their memories back. Their happiest hours, their dead mothers' voices, their childhoods entire. I am generous. It's the one virtue hunger ever taught me. But your god offered nothing. Your god spent a country like a coin and called it strategy, and the worst part—" the golden face split into that cracked-bell smile again— "the worst part is that it worked. I am dying, Jude Dravenmoor. Slowly, from the inside, in agony you cannot imagine. And when I die, everything I've eaten dies with me. Every memory. Every name. Every road your catechism holds together. Hollow wins by making sure there is no one left to lose."

* * *

Jude got up. He didn't decide to get up; his legs simply went, carrying him to the window, where the night stood black and total—not one star, not the memory of one—and the mountains were shapes the eye refused to confirm.

Ten thousand prayers. He tried to count them and couldn't; the number dissolved like sugar in wet hands. Ten thousand times he had bowed his head over contraband and whispered *keep the roads, keep the names, ask nothing,* and he had thought he was speaking to a friend. A quiet friend. A god who adjusted bridges and turned patrols and never asked for anything except the one thing that cost nothing: don't ask.

Except it wasn't nothing. It had never been nothing. *Ask nothing* meant *see nothing*. It meant every smuggler in Thornfell walking the cage walls blindfolded and grateful for the blindfold. Every catechism a brick. Every hymn in Graysink a bar in the window. He had sung those hymns with dying people who thought they were singing to salvation, and they had been singing *mortar*.

"You're lying," he said to the glass. To the reflection. To himself. "This is what you do. You take truth and bend it until it cuts."

"I cannot lie," the Gilded Man said behind him. "It's the cruelest thing about me. Ask the scribe. Ask her what she found in the ward-records, if she ever works up the courage to look past her own guilt. Ask her who signed the founding charter of the Undercroft. Whose seal is on it. Whose *silence*."

Behind him, Sera made a small sound, like a stitch pulling loose.

Jude pressed his forehead against the cold windowpane. In the black glass, faintly, his own reflection stared back, and beside it—because the glass was old and the night was strange and the Gilded Hollow was closing like a fist—there was a second shape. Not gold. Not anything. Just an absence shaped like a friend, the exact size and posture of the presence that had ridden in his skull for twenty years.

*You knew,* he said to it, silently, in the old way, the wordless way. *You knew when I swore my first oath. You knew when I taught the catechism to children. You let me build your prison and called it worship.*

The absence said nothing. Absences were good at that. Twenty years of practice.

And that was the worst of it, Jude realized. Not the betrayal. Betrayal required trust, and trust required surprise, and there was no surprise here—only recognition, slow and sickening, like finding out the kind uncle was the thief all along and realizing you'd always half-known, had always noticed the extra coins, had always chosen not to count them. *Ask nothing.* He had taken the bargain gladly. He had *liked* not asking. Not asking was easy, and easy had always been his favorite price.

"Every prayer I ever made," he said aloud, "was a brick in your cage."

"Yes," said the Gilded Man, gently, which was somehow more obscene than cruelty. "And every brick was laid by a man who thought he was building a road home."

* * *

He came back from the window and sat down, because running was pointless and fighting was theater and there were thirty sleeping people upstairs whose names depended on him staying calm. Sera had not moved. Her knuckles were white around the shard of ward-glass.

"It's still dying," Jude said. It wasn't a question. "Hollow. Inside you. It's still poisoning you."

"Dying," the Gilded Man agreed. "Killing. Both. We are two gods in one grave, mercenary, and the grave is your country, and the funeral is scheduled for everyone you've ever met."

"Then there's time." Jude heard his own voice go flat and practical, the caravan-master's voice, the voice that measured loads and distances and odds. It surprised him. It shouldn't have. It was the only voice he had left that wasn't built on lies. "A god doesn't dissolve overnight. If you're both still killing each other, there's a middle of it. A center. Wherever Hollow is, down in the dark of you—there's a there, and a way to it."

The Gilded Man went very still. Gold does not breathe, but for a moment it seemed to forget even that.

"You want to go *in*."

"I want answers. From the source. Not from its digestion." Jude looked at Sera. "You said the Undercroft holds Thornfell's official memory. Treaties. Censuses. Final words of executed men." He watched her flinch at that last one—she knew something about executed men, something she hadn't told him yet, something sitting behind her eyes like a stone in a boot. "Then it holds the beginning. Whatever Hollow did, whatever was done to *you*—" he turned back to the golden figure— "it started somewhere, and it was written down, because states write everything down. Even their sins. Especially their sins."

"You would walk into the heart of the thing that ate your god," the Gilded Man said, "to interrogate a corpse."

"To interrogate a *liar*," Jude corrected. "Corpse is a promotion."

Something happened to the golden face then. It was almost too fast to catch—a flicker, a hairline shift, like a crack propagating through glaze—and for one unguarded instant the thing wearing Hollow's face looked not triumphant, not hungry, not generous, but *afraid*.

"You should rest," it said, rising, coins counting themselves back into the dark. "The roads are longest for the sleepless. And mercenary—when you find what you find in the Undercroft, remember that I offered you kindness first. Remember that I sat and drank nothing and told you true. When you learn what your god helped them do, remember which of us was honest."

It stepped backward into the night and was simply not there anymore, the way a word is not there after you swallow it.

* * *

The fire burned down. Sera hadn't spoken in an hour. Finally she said, to the embers rather than to him: "The charter. Of the Undercroft. There's a seal on it. I've seen it a hundred times and never—"

"Never asked," Jude said.

"Never asked." She laughed, and it came out broken. "Gods. We were all so *good* at never asking."

Jude looked at the black window, at the starless sky beyond it, at the country-sized grave he'd spent his life mistaking for a home. Somewhere in the dark at the center of everything, two gods were killing each other, and one of them had used his prayers as bricks, and the other was afraid of what the Undercroft held.

Afraid. He turned that over. Hunger with a man's shape, generous and honest and *afraid*—of a room full of old paper.

"Sera," he said quietly. "Get your boots. We leave for the Undercroft at first light. And Sera—whatever's written down there, whoever's name is on whatever sentence—I need you to promise me you won't soften it. No matter whose fault it turns out to be."

She looked at him then, and her eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "Whose do you think it'll be?"

Jude flexed his hand, the one that had once pressed a thumbprint into a dreaming god's jaw, back when he'd thought dreams were gifts.

"Mine," he said. "Somehow, I think it's going to be mine."
