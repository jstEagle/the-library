## 15. Deserter's Road

The fork in the road announced itself the way all bad news does: quietly, and with a great deal of confidence.

We came down out of the high pass on the ninth morning of the early winter, forty-one souls and one mule—the mule being, by common agreement, the most valuable member of the party—and there it was. The road split beneath a leaning cairn stacked by hands long forgotten. To the north, the frozen track wound down toward the Undercroft country, gray and narrow and honest about its misery. To the south, the ground fell away in long white slopes toward the lowlands, and someone—someone had built a marker there. Fresh. Carved into a slab of slate that had no business standing upright in wind like this.

*THE GATE STANDS OPEN. THE SOUTH IS SAFE.*

"That," said Sera Vey, "is either wonderful news or the worst sentence ever chiseled."

"It's the worst sentence ever chiseled," I said. "I've eaten worse sentences, but I've never read one."

She gave me the look she reserved for my smuggler's poetry, which is to say the look of a woman doing arithmetic about how many rations she'd have to share with me before it stopped being worth it.

But the choir had already gathered around the stone. And I watched them read it—I watched hope go through them like brandy through a starving man, warming everything and fooling everyone—and I knew, before anyone said a word, that this was where we would break.

* * *

Let me tell you what the southern gate was, since I was the only one among us who knew, and knowing it sat in my chest like a swallowed coal.

There is no southern gate.

There *was* a southern gate once—Thornfell's old customs post at Vale's Mouth, back when Thornfell had edges enough to need gates. I'd run silk past it eleven times in my smuggling years, bribing the same three guards with the same three bottles. It was a shabby little archway with a roof that leaked and a guard dog named Duchess who could be bought for sausage.

And two weeks ago, from the shoulder of Mount Carness, I had watched the Gilded Hollow close over Vale's Mouth like a hand closing over a candle. I'd seen the light go out of it. I'd seen the gold spread across the valley floor, patient as spilled honey, and I'd heard—not with my ears, some other place entirely—the sound it made when it finished eating a thing.

It sounded grateful.

So the stone lied. Or rather, the stone told the truth in the only language the Hollow speaks fluently, which is the language of what you want most.

"They'll want to go south," Sera murmured beside me. She'd gone very still, the ward-glass shard wrapped in her scarf against the cold, her breath steaming. "Jude. They're going to want to go south."

"I know."

"What do we do?"

I looked out at them. Old Tamms, who'd carried a lame girl on his back for six days and complained about his knees the entire time in a voice you could set your watch by. The Widow Corrinne and her two grandchildren, who sang loudest in the nightly hymns because their names were the youngest and needed the most holding onto. Brother Anselm, blind as a cellar, who navigated by gripping the sleeve of whoever walked nearest and had somehow chosen my sleeve more often than chance allowed. Young Petch, sixteen and furious at the world, which at the moment was a reasonable position.

Forty-one people. Half of whom were about to walk into a mouth.

"Jude?" Sera said again.

"We tell them the truth," I said. "All of it. And then we let them choose."

She stared at me. "They'll die."

"Some will. Maybe most." I found my hands had gone cold inside my gloves, colder than the morning accounted for. "But if I lie to keep them—if I stand here and swear the gate's a trap and forbid it—they'll go anyway, and they'll hate me, and worse than that, they'll be right to. You can't command faith, Sera. I've had a god try it on me. It doesn't take."

"You don't know they'll go anyway."

"I was a smuggler for fifteen years." I almost smiled. "Show a smuggler a closed road and he starts planning around it before you finish the sentence. Fear doesn't stop people. It just makes them quiet about their plans."

* * *

I called them together in the lee of the cairn, out of the wind, and I told them.

I told them about Vale's Mouth. About watching the gold come over the pass like dawn that had gotten ideas above its station. I told them about the Gilded Man—about the face he wore, which was my dead god's face, and the offer he makes, which is always the same offer: *your happiest memory back, in exchange for all the rest.* I told them about Dagny and Little Moss, the two who'd taken the bargain outside Graysink, how they'd walked into the shine smiling, and how I still sometimes heard Dagny humming her wedding song in the dark when the wind came off the ice wrong.

Brother Anselm stood near the front, his sightless eyes aimed at my voice, his hand resting on the shoulder of a Graysink miner named Hobb. When I finished, the silence went on so long I could hear the frost forming on the cairn stones, a faint ticking like a slow clock.

Then Tamms cleared his throat.

"How do we know you're not lying now?" he asked. Not cruelly. Tamms had never been cruel in his life; he simply asked questions the way other men swung hammers. "You say the south is eaten. But you also told us our god was dead, and then it turned out he wasn't dead, exactly, he was—" he waved a mittened hand, "—doing something complicated. How do we know this isn't more complicated?"

"You don't," I said. "That's the whole of it. You don't know. I'm a man who has lied professionally for most of his life, telling you the one story I've got no way to prove. All I can give you is this: I gain nothing from the lie. If you all go south and I go north, I go alone. A liar would keep you close. Liars need audiences."

That got a few grim laughs. Good. Grim laughs are worth ten prayers in weather like this.

Petch stepped forward, chin up, snow in his lashes. "My mum's sister lives past Vale's Mouth. In Lowmark. If there's even a chance—"

"There isn't," I said gently. "Petch. There's no Lowmark anymore. There may not be a *south* anymore. The Hollow doesn't leave neighbors. It leaves bait."

"But you don't *know*—"

"No. I don't know. Same answer as before, and it stays true no matter how many times you ask it. That's what the truth is like, lad. It's mostly gaps."

He turned to the others, arms wide. "So we follow him north on his word alone? Into the deep country? Toward whatever's under the Undercroft? Because he says so?"

"No," said a new voice.

It was Corrinne. She came forward slowly, one grandchild's mittened hand in each of hers, and she looked at me with an expression I couldn't read until she spoke.

"Not on his word alone," she said. "On his *road*. Jude Dravenmoor has kept us alive on roads that stopped existing behind us. Every morning I wake up and yesterday's path is gone—gone, mind, not snowed over, *gone*, like it never was—and every evening we're somehow still alive, and it's because this man remembers hard enough for all of us. I don't care what he claims about gods or gates. I've watched him pay for our lives out of his own head." Her jaw tightened. "He forgot his mother's face for us. I heard him say so. So when he tells me the south is a mouth, I believe him, because he has never once spent a memory cheaply."

I hadn't known they knew. About my mother. I'd said it once, half-delirious, on the night the road through the Whisper Col burned itself out of my skull and I'd traded the last of her to keep thirty feet of switchback real. Apparently the night keeps no secrets from people walking in it together.

Something in my chest did a slow, painful fold, like paper creased by a careful hand.

"Thank you," I managed.

"Don't thank me yet," Corrinne said. "Because I'm asking you the question that matters, and I want the truth, whatever it costs: if we go south, will you stop us?"

And there it was. The hammer under the question.

I took my time answering, because the answer deserved it.

"No," I said. "I won't stop you. I'll stand here and tell you you're walking into the Hollow until my voice gives out, and then I'll write it in the snow, and then I'll probably follow far enough behind to bury whoever I can reach. But I won't hold you. I won't lie to you, and I won't own you. Nobody owns anybody out here. That's the Hollow's whole business model."

* * *

They decided by firelight, which took until well past dark, and I sat apart with the mule—who is called Duchess, after the guard dog, a joke that had seemed funnier in autumn—and let them argue without me.

Sera found me there eventually, two bowls of barley soup in hand, and handed me one. It was terrible. We had reached the stage of the journey where terrible soup is a sacrament.

"Twenty-two are going south," she said.

I'd guessed eighteen. The number landed harder than I expected. "Who?"

"Tamms. Petch, of course. The Hollis brothers. Nine of the Graysink miners. The ferryman's widow—" She stopped, corrected herself. "Not the widow. Corrinne's staying. It's the Kettering family, and..." She trailed off, reading my face. "Jude."

"It's fine," I said, which was a lie, and she knew it was a lie, and mercifully she let it lie there in the snow between us, unburied.

"They think you're leading them to safety," she said quietly. "That's why the rest stay. They've made you the reason. Do you understand what that means? After the Undercroft—after whatever we find down there—you won't be able to fail. Not in front of them."

"I've been mistaken for salvation before," I said. "Chapter five of my life. It didn't stick then either."

"That's not funny."

"It's a little funny."

She sighed and sat down on the ice beside me, which was foolish, and I shifted over to make room on my bedroll, which was equally foolish, and for a while we ate our terrible soup and listened to the southern party singing on the far side of the fire—quietly, so as not to wake those staying, but singing nonetheless, holding each other's names up into the dark like lanterns.

"They're not fools," Sera said at last. "That's what I keep coming back to. Fools chase the gold. These ones are chasing their families. There's a difference, and the difference is everything, and it still might kill them."

"I know."

"And you're really letting them go."

"I'm really letting them go." I turned the bowl in my hands. "You know what Hollow taught me, back when I thought Hollow was worth learning from? Every oath I ever swore over stolen goods, every prayer—it was all memory-ritual, keeping the passes real. And I used to think that meant faith was a kind of grip. You hold on. You hold the road, the god, the people. You squeeze." I shook my head. "But you can't remember a thing by clutching it. Ask my mother's face. Things stay real because you carry them open-handed. Loyalty's the same. The moment it's chained, it stops being loyalty and starts being cargo."

"When did you get wise?"

"Somewhere around the time I lost everything worth being stupid about."

* * *

Dawn came gray and late, the way it did now, the sun rising somewhere behind clouds that had stopped pretending to be temporary. The southern party stood packed at the fork with their bundles, twenty-two of them, breath steaming, faces pink with cold and resolve.

Petch came to me first. He stuck out his hand, and I took it, and he held on longer than a boy his age usually holds on to anything.

"If I see a gate," he said, "and it's open, and it's real—I'm going through it. And someday, when this is over, I'm going to find you and tell you you were wrong."

"Do that," I said. "I'll put soup on."

Tamms embraced me like a collapsing building, which is to say thoroughly and with damage. The Ketterings bowed, awkwardly, as though I were something official, and I hated that, and let it happen, because taking it away from them would have cost more than my pride.

Anselm came last. The blind priest felt his way along the cairn to where I stood, and found my sleeve with unerring accuracy, and gripped it.

"God-sworn," he said. His voice was soft. Everyone else had stepped back; the whole fork had gone quiet enough to hear the frost tick. "I cannot see the stone. I cannot see the road. Tell me truly, and mind you, I will hear the lie in your voice as plain as a cracked bell: is the south death?"

I looked at his ruined, gentle eyes. I looked at the twenty-one behind him.

"Yes," I said. "As far as I can know anything, brother. Yes."

He nodded slowly. Then he released my sleeve, patted my arm twice—the way you'd pat a horse you trusted—and turned south.

"Anselm," I said. He paused. "Why go, if you believe me?"

He smiled. He had a beautiful smile; it was the only part of his face the years had left entirely undamaged. "Because you let me ask," he said. "Fifty years I served a church that answered questions with locks. You answered with a door. Even a door that opens onto dying is holy, next to a lock." He raised one hand in blessing, aimed slightly to the left of me, which somehow made it land true. "Walk in memory, Jude Dravenmoor. And forgive us for wanting our families more than our fear."

And they went. Twenty-two small figures in a white world, down the long southern slope, singing as they went, their names passing hand to hand along the line so none of them would arrive at the gate unnamed. I stood and watched until the weather folded them away, and then I stood a while longer, because someone had to witness it, and witnessing is the last job left when every other job is gone.

Nineteen stayed. Nineteen, and one mule.

Corrinne's grandchildren waved at the empty slope for a long time. Hobb the miner wept without noise, the way miners do, as though grief were another seam to work quietly. And Sera stood beside me with her shard of ward-glass held up toward the south, and I saw her lips move—reading whatever the glass remembered of Vale's Mouth, naming it, keeping it, doing the small stubborn work of authorship that was becoming her whole religion.

"Well," I said finally, and my voice came out rougher than I intended. "Nineteen is a number. It's not a good number, but it's a number."

"Nineteen and a mule," Sera corrected.

"Duchess outranks most of you."

Behind us, the northern road waited, running down out of the pass into the deep country where the Undercroft slept beneath its redacted warden. The wind came down it carrying a smell I didn't like—cold iron and old paper and, underneath, faintly, horribly sweet, like honey left too long in a sealed jar.

Sera noticed it too. I saw her go still, the shard lowering slowly in her hand.

"Jude," she said. "Last night, while you were brooding at the mule, I went through the founding charter fragments again. The ones we salvaged from the archive shelf before the warden sealed the vault." Her breath was coming fast now, fogging in quick bursts. "There's a clause. A door, written into existence *before* Thornfell drew its first border. Before the cage. Before any of it. It opens on something older than the Hollow."

I turned to look at her, and the hope on her face was so fierce it frightened me more than the smell on the wind did.

"But there's a price," she said. "To open it, someone has to speak a name the state erased. A name nobody living remembers." She met my eyes. "A name that has to be *remembered*, Jude. Out loud. By someone."

The wind gusted down the northern road, sweet as rotting honey, and somewhere far below us in the frozen heart of the country, I felt something turn over in its sleep.

"Then somebody had better start remembering," I said.
