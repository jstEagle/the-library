## 17. Remembering the Unremembered

The shrine of Hollow sat in a fold of the mountains like a coin dropped in a beggar's cup and forgotten there.

Jude had known it existed for twenty years and had never once gone inside. That was not unusual among Hollow-sworn. The god of smugglers did not want temples; temples were where authorities looked. Hollow wanted back rooms, cellar doors, the space beneath floorboards where you hid the good brandy from the tax man. A shrine was a contradiction the god had tolerated only once, early on, before it learned better—and then abandoned, the way you abandon a coat that no longer fits.

It was a low stone building squatting against the hillside, its door hanging by one hinge, its roof open to the sky in three places. Snow had drifted through the gaps and lay on the floor in pale sheets, undisturbed except by fox tracks. There had been an altar once. Now there was a flat stone with a shallow depression worn into it, the kind of hollow—Jude appreciated the irony even now, cold as he was—that rain makes when it has all the time in the world.

Sera stood at the threshold, arms crossed against the wind, ward-glass shard hanging at her throat. "You're sure about this?"

"No," Jude said honestly.

"Good. People who are sure frighten me."

He almost smiled. Almost. His face had been doing very little of anything for two days, since he'd come out of the Undercroft with a name half-formed in his mouth and a horror fully formed behind his eyes. *Merrick Vale.* Or possibly *Merrick Vael*. The warden of redacted text had shown him the shape of the name without the substance, the way a glove shows you the hand but isn't the hand. Somewhere in Thornfell—or somewhere in what had been Thornfell—the rest of it waited. And if Sera's theory held, if the wards were a cage and Hollow the cage-smith, then the place to find a god's buried secrets was the god's own house.

Even an abandoned one. Especially an abandoned one. Gods left things behind in their empty houses the way people did: old letters, spare keys, the truth they'd stopped being able to look at.

"Everyone else?" he asked.

"Camped in the lea of the hill. Tam is arguing with Marta about whether the goats can be taught to keep watch." She paused. "Marta thinks yes. I'd give the goats until sundown before they unionize."

That time he did smile, briefly, and was grateful to her for it. Then he stepped over the threshold, and the smile went where smiles go when nobody needs them.

* * *

Inside, the cold had a different quality than the cold outside. Outside, cold was weather. Inside, it was older than that. It was the cold of a room that had been waiting.

Jude set his lantern on the altar stone and took inventory the way he took inventory of any building he intended to work in. Walls: sound enough. Floor: flagstones, cracked, one missing near the north wall—a gap into darkness that smelled of earth and, faintly, of incense that had given up decades ago. In the corner, a wooden rack that had once held candles held instead a single stub, fused to its holder by age. On the wall above the altar, someone had carved words into the stone long ago, and someone else—later, angrier—had tried to chisel them out. The chisel marks were deep, but stone remembers better than chisels do. Jude brushed away the dust and read the ghost of the letters:

**WHAT IS GIVEN IN DARKNESS IS KEPT IN DARKNESS.**

The old catechism. First line of it, anyway. He knew the rest by heart, which was the whole point of him standing in this freezing ruin instead of sitting by a fire with sensible people.

Hollow's rites had never been written down. That was doctrine. Writing could be seized, copied, used against you. So the god of smugglers lived entirely in memory, passed mouth to mouth along the passes, and every rite was a remembering. When you swore an oath over stolen goods, you named the road you'd first run contraband on. When you lit the candle for a partner who hadn't come home, you described his laugh, his limp, the way he cheated at cards. Every prayer was a small act of reconstruction. The faithful didn't ask Hollow for favors. They gave the god their memories, piece by piece, so that the roads would stay real in somebody's mind, and therefore real at all.

Jude had thought, for most of his life, that this was piety.

Then the stars went out, and the wards shattered, and he had learned that the prayers were bricks. He had built a prison out of his own devotion and called it faith. And now he intended to take those bricks apart one by one, because somewhere in the mortar was a name.

The rite was simple, which was why it had survived. You knelt. You spoke aloud something you remembered—truly, specifically, with the weight of having been there. You gave it away. Not "I remember my mother's kitchen." That was a fact. You said: *the smell of bread burning while she argued with my father about money, and how she laughed when the smoke made them both stop shouting.* A moment. A whole moment, edges included. You spoke it into the dark, and then you let it go—not suppressed, not shoved down, but released, the way you release a bird you've been carrying in your hands. It flew out of you and did not come back.

Forward, the rite fed memories to the god.

Backward, Jude hoped, it would do the opposite. Give enough away, hollow yourself out enough, and whatever the god had hidden in the space where your memories used to live would have nowhere to hide. You drained the cup to see what was painted on the bottom.

"You'll want to sit down for this part," he told Sera, who had followed him in despite everything.

"I'm staying."

"It's going to be ugly."

"Jude." She looked at him with the particular patience of a woman who had spent thirty years correcting her own catastrophic mistakes. "I built the flaw that ended the world. Whatever you do to your head in here, I promise you I have done worse to mine."

So she sat on a fallen roof beam, and he knelt at the altar, and he began.

* * *

The first memories went easily, the way the first coins go easiest from a purse.

He gave away the taste of river water in high summer, cold and green, drunk from cupped hands above Grayford. Gone. He gave away the sound of dice rattling in a tin cup in a Bellharrow cellar, and old Fenwick's voice saying *seven's a liar's number, boy*, and the specific warmth of brandy stolen from a merchant who deserved it. Gone, gone, gone. Each one he spoke aloud in full, lovingly, completely—the whole point was completeness—and each one lifted out of him like a splinter drawn clean, leaving behind not pain but absence, a smooth socket where something used to fit.

Sera watched him with her hands pressed together, saying nothing. Good. He would have hated commentary.

Ten memories. Twenty. He ran out of easy ones and moved into the middle drawer: his first oath, sworn at fourteen over a crate of untaxed silk, kneeling in the dark with three grown men and feeling enormous. The name of the man who'd taught him to pick a lock with a knife tip and a prayer. The face of the innkeeper at the Drowned Bell who always watered the beer but never the whiskey, because "whiskey's honest work, watering it is a sin."

Somewhere around the thirtieth, the room changed.

He couldn't have said how. The temperature didn't drop. The light didn't move. But the darkness between the lantern and the walls got *thicker*, the way water gets thicker just before it freezes, and the silence got a texture to it, and Jude understood that he was no longer alone in the shrine. Something had noticed him. Something that lived in the spaces where Hollow used to be.

He kept going. You don't stop a rite halfway through. Half-emptied cups spill.

He gave away his mother's hands—flour to the wrist, the burn scar on her thumb from the oven door, the way she snapped dish towels at cats. He gave away his father's voice singing off-key at harvest, and the fight they'd had when Jude left home, and the worse silence after it, and the letter that came too late. He gave away the winter he'd nearly died on the Kettle Pass and the stranger who'd dug him out and refused to give his name—"names are debts, boy"—and the shape of that stranger's shadow on the snow.

And with each gift, the thick dark leaned closer, and fragments began to surface.

Not memories. These were other people's. They came up like bodies in a flooded field, face-down, and he had to turn them over.

A courtroom. Stone floors, high windows, dust turning in the light. A judge whose face kept sliding sideways like a portrait hung crooked. A clerk reading charges in a voice like dry paper: *sedition, heresy, the crime of remembering what the state has ordered forgotten.* And in the dock, a man.

Jude saw him clearly, which frightened him more than anything else, because everything else in the vision blurred and this one face stayed sharp, as though the erasure had missed it, or as though the man had held onto his own face with both hands all the way down.

Younger than Jude had expected. Maybe thirty. Thin, with the look of someone who had been hungry for years and had gotten philosophical about it. Dark hair cut short by someone in a hurry. And eyes—not fierce, exactly. Tired, and amused, and unbowed, all three at once, which is a rare combination in any century. He stood in the dock the way men stand in weather: accepting it, unimpressed by it.

He was smiling. At the judge. At the clerk. At the gallery full of citizens who had been brought in to watch justice happen and mostly looked bored. He smiled like a man sharing a joke with everyone in the room, including the ones who couldn't hear it yet.

The clerk read the sentence. Jude heard it in the vision the way you hear things underwater—muffled, distorted—but the shape of it was clear: the state would not kill him. Killing him would make him a martyr, and martyrs have names, and names are dangerous. Instead they would erase him. Every record burned. Every witness sworn to forget under penalty of the same. Every friend, every lover, every enemy made to unmake him. He would be unwritten so thoroughly that the hunger of it—the sheer *appetite* of a hole that size punched in the world—would be the only thing left.

And beside the judge, taking notes in a fair hand, dressed in plain gray robes with no insignia, sat a figure Jude knew.

He knew it the way you know a face in a crowd you've never met—you know it anyway. Quiet features. Patient hands. An expression of profound, professional regret. The figure looked up once, directly at the man in the dock, and the man in the dock smiled wider, and mouthed something.

Jude couldn't read it. The vision wouldn't let him. Some things even a hollowed-out man can't be shown.

But the gray-robed figure flinched. Just slightly. Just enough.

"Hollow," Jude said aloud, and his voice cracked in the empty shrine like ice on a pond.

* * *

He lost count after that. The memories came faster because he was digging now, deliberately, tearing them loose rather than letting them go. He gave away his first kiss, behind the tannery, tasting of smoke and nerve. The map of the Undercroft passages, which hurt more than he expected—he felt the knowledge leave him like a tooth coming out, and Sera made a small noise across the room, because she understood what he'd just surrendered and what it might cost them later. Too late. Given is given. WHAT IS GIVEN IN DARKNESS IS KEPT IN DARKNESS, and the darkness keeps its receipts.

He gave away the color of the sky over Thornfell on the morning before the stars went out. He gave away the smell of his own tent, his own horse, his own sweat after a hard climb. He gave away the joke Tam tells badly, the one about the goat and the customs officer, and realized as it left him that he could no longer remember why it was funny, or that it was funny, or that Tam told jokes at all. A thin man with a loud voice remained, attached to no anecdotes whatsoever.

The dark in the shrine was close enough now to touch. It wasn't hostile. That was the worst part. It was *attentive*, the way a dog is attentive, waiting to see what you'll do next, hoping it involves food.

And the fragments kept surfacing, and he kept catching them, and the picture assembled itself piece by terrible piece:

The condemned man had friends in low places. Smugglers, naturally—who else does the state forget but the people who carry things across lines? And one of those friends had prayed to a quiet god, and the quiet god had listened, and the quiet god had done something no god should do: it had taken the erased man's name and *hidden it inside itself*, tucked into the deepest fold of its own divinity, the way a smuggler hides the true cargo beneath the false. Not saved. Hidden. Hollow could not restore Merrick Vale to the world—the erasure was law, was machinery, was bigger than one god—but it could keep the name safe. A seed in a vault. Insurance.

Or a weapon. Depending on whose hand found it.

And then, later—how much later? Years? Centuries?—the devourer had come, hungry beyond hunger, the accumulated appetite of an entire nation's forced forgetting given legs and a golden face. And Hollow, the patient god, the god of cellars and false bottoms, had looked at the thing wearing its rival's hunger and had done the arithmetic that gods do and men cannot forgive.

*Let it swallow me,* Hollow had decided. *And I will be the poison in its belly.*

And Thornfell—the roads, the records, the recollections, the townsfolk who couldn't say what country they lived in—all of it, all of it, was the trap's casing. Acceptable loss. Jude's mother's flour-dusted hands: acceptable loss. The choir singing hymns to hold their names together: acceptable loss. Thirty years of Jude's prayers, every candle, every oath, every memory given in darkness—bricks. Mortar. Cage-wall.

He had known it intellectually since the Undercroft. Knowing it here, in the god's own house, kneeling on the god's own stone, was different. This was knowing it in the knees.

* * *

The last fragment surfaced at dawn, or what the ruined roof claimed was dawn—gray light falling through the broken slates in dusty columns, landing on the altar like an offering nobody had asked for.

Jude knelt in the wreckage of his own past and turned the final piece over.

The trial again. The end of it. The clerk asking, one last time, per procedure, per the machinery's terrible thoroughness: *"Does the condemned have any final statement for the record?"*

And Merrick Vale—thin, tired, amused, unbowed—had leaned toward the clerk with the smile still on his face and said, clearly, distinctly, in a voice that the vision finally permitted Jude to hear whole:

*"You can burn every page. You can't burn the road. Somebody always remembers the road."*

And then the name. Spoken by the clerk, entered into the record precisely so that it could be struck from the record, the state's signature act of self-defeating thoroughness:

*Merrick Vale.*

Jude held it. He held it the way you hold a coal in bare hands—carefully, knowing it will cost you, refusing to drop it.

Because the price had come due. He had known it would. The rite takes payment in kind, and he had fed it everything shallow and then everything deep, and now it stood at the door of the last room with its hand out, attentive, patient, and the last room was his childhood. All of it. The kitchen and the burnt bread and the snapping dish towels. The harvest song sung off-key. The fight, the silence, the letter too late. The whole small country of being eight years old, twelve, sixteen, before the roads and the oaths and the god.

Without it, he would be a man made entirely of trade routes and tactics. He would remember how to survive and nothing about why surviving had ever seemed worth the trouble.

He thought about the choir camped on the far side of the hill. About Sera on the fallen beam, watching him with her guilt-sharpened eyes. About the frozen march ahead, into the closing fist of the Gilded Hollow, toward a door that would only open for a name spoken by someone who remembered it.

Somebody always remembers the road.

"All right," Jude said to the dark, and his voice was steady, which surprised him. "Take it. But you take all of it. No picking. You don't get the bad parts and leave me the sweet ones like some merchant shorting a scale. The whole childhood, start to finish, or nothing."

The dark considered. The dark agreed.

It didn't hurt. That was the obscenity of it. It simply *left*, gently, thoroughly, like snow melting off a roof in spring—one moment the weight was there, and then it was water, and then it was gone, and the eaves stood bare. He watched it go. He tried to wave to it. He found he could no longer quite remember what he was waving at, only that waving had seemed important.

When it was done, Jude Dravenmoor knelt in an abandoned shrine holding a single name in his emptied hands, and the name was Merrick Vale, and it was warm, and it was the only thing left in him that had ever been a child.

Sera helped him stand. Her face, when he looked at her, was a stranger's face—not literally, he still knew her, knew her name and her crimes and her courage, but the easy recognition was gone, the sense of shared history, because history was one of the things he'd paid with. She saw him seeing her and something in her expression broke and reassembled itself into resolve.

"Do you have it?" she asked.

"I have it."

"And the rest?"

"The rest is spent." He flexed his fingers. They worked. Everything worked. He was a functioning adult human being with no childhood and one impossible name, and outside, faintly, he could hear Tam losing the argument with the goats.

Sera looked at the gray light, then at him. "Then we march today. While you still remember the way."

Jude opened his mouth to say he remembered the way fine—

—and discovered, with a lurch, that the road north out of the shrine valley was gone from him. Burned in the rite. Spent like all the rest. He knew there *was* a road. He could not, for the life of him, remember where.

He looked at Sera, and Sera looked at him, and somewhere below the hill the goats began to scream.
