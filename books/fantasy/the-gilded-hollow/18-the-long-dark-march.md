## 18. The Long Dark March

**Jude**

The snow began an hour before dawn, which was wrong, because the season was wrong, because everything in Thornfell was wrong now, and Jude had stopped expecting the world to apologize for it.

He stood at the head of the column and counted them the way he counted cargo on a night run: by breath. Forty-one plumes of white rising into the dark where stars should have been. Forty-one people who had voted to stay with him after Deserter's Road, when twenty-three of their friends had turned south toward a gate he knew was already eaten. He hadn't told them that. He'd told them the truth about everything else, and they'd stayed anyway, and somehow that was worse and better at once.

"Names," Sera said beside him, her voice rough from cold. She had taken up the practice without anyone ordering it, the way she took up most things—stubbornly, and slightly too loud.

Jude nodded and began. "Jude Dravenmoor. Sera Vey. Father Ossen. Marta Cull. The twins, Bren and Benna Halloway..."

It was the ritual now. Every morning, every dusk, and whenever the dark pressed too close between: they spoke each other's names aloud, down the whole line, hand to hand to hand. It sounded like a children's game. It was not a children's game. In Thornfell, a name spoken by someone who loved you was the last rope holding you above the Hollow.

"...and Tam Wicker," he finished, "who snores like a rockslide."

"I do not," said Tam Wicker.

"You do," said nine voices at once, and Bren Halloway laughed so hard he had to sit down in the snow, and for one bright moment forty-one people were just people, freezing together on a road that only half believed in itself.

Then the moment passed, because moments did now, and Jude raised his hand, and they walked.

* * *

**Sera**

Sera Vey had spent thirty years believing that wards were built of ink and silver and correct procedure. She had been wrong. They were built of attention. That was the lesson of her life, learned far too late and paid for in a country's worth of forgetting, and she thought about it constantly as she walked, because thinking about it kept her from thinking about the crack.

Her crack. Her little spiteful flaw, stitched into the border wards a decade ago because Archivist Dolembraight had denied her a research posting. One loose thread in a tapestry the size of a nation. She had told herself it was harmless. She had told herself a great many things.

Now she walked at the rear of the column with the shard of ward-glass in her mitten, and she watched the country die in its reflection.

The glass showed what places used to be. Right now it showed a farmstead sliding past on their left—the actual snow showed nothing but a suggestion of fences, a rumor of a barn—but the shard held a picture: wheat fields, a red door, a woman hanging wash. As the column passed, the picture dimmed. By the time they'd gone a hundred paces, the red door was gray, then pale, then a blankness shaped like a door, and then not even that.

"Keep walking," Sera said quietly, to no one, to everyone. "Don't look back at it. Looking back is how it gets your name."

That wasn't strictly true. Nothing was strictly true anymore. But the choir had adopted her sayings the way soldiers adopt superstitions, and she had decided that a useful lie was a load-bearing wall, and she would prop up as many as this march required.

Father Ossen fell into step beside her, feeling his way along the rope that linked him to Marta Cull. His eyes had gone white years before Thornfell began to vanish, which meant, he liked to say, that the Hollow owed him nothing new. "You're limping," he said.

"I'm walking with dignity."

"You're limping with dignity. There's a difference, and my knees know it." He smiled. He was the only person Sera had ever met who could smile while walking through the end of the world, and she suspected it was because he had made his peace with darkness long before it got organized. "Tell me about the Undercroft again. The part you always skip."

She always skipped the part about the warden. Made of redacted text, a thing of black bars and sealed sentences, guarding treaties and censuses and the last words of executed men. "The part I always skip is that I don't know if it will let us in," she said. "I helped build its doors. Doors remember their builders. Sometimes fondly. Sometimes the way a trap remembers the foot."

"And sometimes," Ossen said, "the foot is carrying something worth catching." He squeezed her arm through three layers of wool. "Walk, ward-scribe. Dignified limp or otherwise."

* * *

**Marta Cull**

Marta had been a baker in Bellharrow before Bellharrow forgot it was a town. She was fifty-one years old, wide as a doorway, and she carried the choir's entire food supply in a pack that should have required a mule, except all the mules had forgotten how to be mules somewhere outside Graysink, and had stood in the road looking confused until the road gave up on them.

So: Marta. And her bread, which was less bread now and more a philosophical position—flour, water, ash, hope—and which she doled out each noon with the stern generosity of a woman who has fed people through worse than this.

The Gilded Man came to her at noon on the fourth day.

He didn't arrive. He was simply *there*, standing in the snow ahead of the column, beaten gold from crown to boot, wearing Hollow's face the way a thief wears a stolen coat. The temperature didn't drop. The light didn't change. That was the horror of him—that he fit into the world so politely.

"Marta Cull," he said, and his voice was warm bread, was a kitchen in winter, was every good smell she had ever loved. "Baker of Bellharrow. I have something of yours."

"I don't want anything of yours."

"It isn't mine. It never was." He opened one golden hand, and in it lay a morning. Not a memory *of* a morning—the morning itself, whole and steaming: her husband Aldous at the table, arguing cheerfully with the newspaper, flour on his nose because he'd tried to help and helped the way men help, which is to say adorably and uselessly. Aldous, who had died six years ago of a fever, whose face she had already lost once to grief's slow erosion and was now losing twice, faster.

"One morning," the Gilded Man said. "Yours forever, perfect, unlosing. All I ask is the rest. The dull days. The tired evenings. The arguments you lost. Give me the chaff, Marta, and keep the wheat."

And here was the thing Marta Cull discovered about herself, there in the snow with her arms full of ash-bread: the offer was *good*. It was a genuinely good offer. That was the trap's genius—not that it lied, but that it didn't need to.

"Aldous wouldn't want me," she said slowly, working it out aloud, "to have one morning of him instead of all the true ones. Even the bad ones. Especially—" her voice cracked, and she let it, because the choir knew her voice cracked, that was allowed, that was what names spoken aloud were *for*—"especially the bad ones. The fever week. I hated it and I'm keeping it."

The Gilded Man looked at her for a long moment with Hollow's borrowed face, and something in that face—something deep, something buried under the gold—looked almost sad.

"As you wish," he said, and was not there anymore.

Marta stood shaking until small hands took hers—Benna Halloway, eight years old and fierce as a wren. "Was he scary?" Benna asked.

"No, love. That's what's scary." Marta knelt in the snow and pulled the girl close. "Now. Say my name."

"Marta Cull. You're our baker. You burned the first loaf and we ate it anyway."

"That's right." Marta laughed wetly. "That's exactly right. Say it again."

* * *

**Tam Wicker**

Tam Wicker was nineteen, a former chicken thief, current carrier of the rope-coil, and privately of the opinion that he was going to die out here, and had made his peace with it mostly, except during the nights.

The nights were when the Hollow whispered. Not words—it didn't bother with words anymore, not with him. It offered *feelings*. On the fifth night, crouched over a fire that burned without heat because heat was another thing Thornfell was running out of, the whisper came as a sensation: the specific pride of his father's hand on his shoulder, the year before his father died, the year before Tam stole the chickens and ruined everything and ran.

*Come be that boy again,* said the dark. *Before the chickens. Before the running.*

"Tam." Jude Dravenmoor sat down across the fire from him, uninvited, the way he did. "Say your name."

"Tam Wicker."

"Louder."

"TAM WICKER." A few heads turned. Tam didn't care. Saying it louder helped. Nobody knew why it helped. It just did, the way salt helps, the way a joke helps at a funeral.

"Good." Jude fed a stick to the heatless fire, which accepted it graciously and did nothing whatsoever with it. "Want to hear what it offered me last night?"

Tam blinked. The god-sworn, the man the priest had named salvation, admitting to— "It offers *you* things?"

"Every night. Last night it was my mother's face." Jude's voice was level, but his hands weren't, and Tam noticed, and was strangely comforted that he noticed. "I already spent that one. Back on the north roads, to save thirty lives. So the Hollow offered me a copy. A forgery. My mother's face, but smiling the way I wanted her to smile instead of the way she did." He looked into the useless fire. "I nearly took it, Tam. A forged face from a lying god, and I nearly took it, because missing someone is a hunger and hunger doesn't check provenance."

"What did you do?"

"What we do." Jude reached across the fire and gripped Tam's wrist, hard, callused, real. "Said your name. Said Sera's. Said old Ossen's. Built myself out of other people, the way you build a bridge out of whatever's lying around." He released Tam's wrist and stood. "Get some sleep. Dawn in four hours. We speak names at first light."

"Jude?" Tam said, before he could stop himself. "Does it ever get easier?"

"No," Jude said, with the honesty that had cost him half his choir and saved the rest. "But you get better at it. Same as anything."

* * *

**Bren Halloway**

On the seventh day the road ended.

Not faded—ended, cleanly, like a sentence cut off mid-word, and beyond it lay the frozen heart of Thornfell: a plain of white going out further than sight, featureless as an unwritten page. Somewhere out there, three days' walk, was the Undercroft. Somewhere out there, closer, was the thing that wore a dead god's face.

"We can't cross that holding hands in a line," Sera said. "A line is one rope. If the Hollow takes one of us, the shock runs down the whole chain."

"So we cross in circles," said Father Ossen.

Everyone looked at him.

"Circles," he repeated, delighted with himself, turning his white eyes toward their silence. "Small ones. Six or seven apiece, holding hands, facing inward. Each circle speaks its own names. If one circle falters, the others keep singing. You can't unravel a circle from one point, ward-scribe—you of all people should know that. It's the oldest ward-shape there is."

There was a silence, and then Sera Vey laughed—a short, startled, genuine laugh, the first anyone had heard from her since Bellharrow. "The old smuggler's rites are circles," she said. "Jude. Your catechism. The oath-ring over stolen goods. He's right. He's *right*, the blind old—"

"I heard that," said Ossen serenely.

They formed the circles in the blue shadow of late afternoon. Jude took the first: himself, Sera, Ossen, Marta, the twins, Tam. Benna Halloway insisted on holding her brother's hand and Marta's, and announced that her circle-name-count included the bread, because the bread had earned it.

They stepped onto the blank page together.

* * *

**Jude**

Three days across the heart of Thornfell, and Jude learned that the Hollow had stopped pretending to be patient.

It came at them in weather, now. Shapes in the whiteout: doorways standing alone in the snow, open, lit from within by kitchens and childhoods and beds where nobody had died yet. Voices calling from the circles behind—voices of the twenty-three who'd gone south, cheerful, insisting they'd found the gate, come see, come see. Once, horribly, it came as a second Jude Dravenmoor, walking alongside the column at a distance, waving.

Nobody waved back. Tam threw a snowball at it. It caught the snowball, examined it with Hollow's face, and appeared—for just a moment—almost impressed.

But the circles held.

That was the whole miracle, and it was such a small one. Seven circles of freezing, exhausted, terrified people, walking hand in hand across a country being unmade, speaking each other's names into the wind. Bren Halloway sang the counting songs his grandmother taught him. Marta distributed ash-bread and dignity in equal measure. Sera navigated by a shard of glass and pure spite. Ossen told jokes so bad that Benna laughed and Bren groaned and the circle tightened around both sounds like hands around a candleflame.

On the third night, huddled in the lee of a boundary stone that was smooth as an egg—they were all smooth as eggs now—Jude sat awake and felt it: the Hollow closing. Not attacking. *Closing.* Like a fist around something small. Like a country deciding, gently, to be finished.

Tomorrow, the Undercroft. Tomorrow, the warden of redacted text, and the name Sera said might open a door older than borders. Tomorrow, Merrick Vale—if Jude still had the name by then, if the void he'd fed his childhood into hadn't crept back in the night to take the payment back.

"Jude." Sera, wrapped in wool, watching him watch the dark. "Sleep."

"In a minute. I'm memorizing you."

"What?"

"All of you. Everyone. In case tomorrow costs more than I've got left." He said it lightly, but his voice betrayed him at the end, and Sera, mercifully, pretended it didn't. "Forty-one faces. Forty-one names. It's the largest haul I've ever carried, and I've never once stolen any of it."

Sera was quiet a moment. Then: "Jude Dravenmoor," she said, clear and deliberate, into the closing dark. "Mercenary. God-sworn. Road-keeper. The man who tells the truth even when it empties the camp."

"Sera Vey," he answered. "Ward-scribe. Crack-maker. The woman fixing what she broke."

Around the dying fire, sleepily, one by one, the circles took it up—names passing hand to hand to hand, forty-one voices braided against the fist of the world—and Jude Dravenmoor closed his eyes inside the sound, and let it hold him until morning.

Morning came gray and hard. They broke camp, formed the circles, and walked the last league to the Undercroft—

—and found the doors already open, and the Gilded Man standing on the threshold, waiting, with Hollow's face and an expression like a man about to make his final, sincerest offer.
