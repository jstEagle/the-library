## 21. Exiles From a Map

The door did not open so much as admit them, the way a tired innkeeper admits travelers after midnight—reluctantly, and with the distinct impression that it would rather have stayed shut.

Sera Vey went through last, because someone had to count.

That had been her job for three days now, ever since the long dark march began: counting. Thirty-one souls at the start of the frozen crossing. Twenty-nine when the Gilded Hollow's fist closed around the pass at Coldrow and took the Kettle twins, who walked into the gold smiling and holding hands, which was somehow worse than screaming. Twenty-eight when old Brother Anselm sat down in the snow two miles from the door and said he'd like to rest his eyes, just for a moment, and no amount of Jude Dravenmoor's profane eloquence could persuade him otherwise.

Twenty-seven when they stepped through into starlight that was merely starlight—ordinary, indifferent, beautifully unremarkable—and Sera turned around on the threshold and counted one more time, because she did not trust arithmetic anymore, and got twenty-seven, and let out a breath she felt she'd been holding since the night the wards shattered.

"Twenty-seven," she said aloud, because saying things aloud was going to matter now. She could feel that already, the way you can feel weather coming in your teeth.

The land on the other side was a hillside of dry grass going gold in late autumn, sloping down toward a river valley where somebody's sheep were grazing with the placid stupidity of sheep who had never once worried about their own definition. There were lights down there—a village, maybe a town, chimney smoke standing straight up in the still air. Ordinary smoke. Smoke from ordinary fires in ordinary houses in a country that existed.

Behind her, the door was already gone. Not closed—gone. Where it had been there was simply hillside, grass, a granite boulder wearing a hat of moss, as though the hillside had never contained anything else and resented the implication.

Jude stood beside her with his hands on his hips, looking at the boulder. "It could have waited until we were all through."

"It waited exactly as long as it was written to." Sera heard her own voice come out flat and scholarly and hated it a little. "The charter predates Thornfell's borders. It was never a door *out* of Thornfell. It was a door from before there was anywhere to be out of."

"So we're not refugees."

"We're—" She stopped. Tried again. "We're something that doesn't have a word yet."

* * *

The village at the bottom of the valley was called Marlow's Ford, according to a sign that hung crooked over its single street, and the people of Marlow's Ford had never heard of Thornfell.

This should not have surprised Sera. She had helped write the wards; she knew better than anyone how thoroughly Thornfell had been cut off, sealed, made into a sentence with no outside referent. But knowing a thing and standing in a warm kitchen while a farmwife squints at you and says *"Thornfell? Is that up past Harrowdale?"* are different experiences, and Sera found the second one lodged in her chest like a fishbone.

"No," she said patiently. "North. Far north. Past the mountains."

"There's nothing past the mountains, love. There's the mountains, and then there's sky, and then there's more mountains, and my cousin swears there's a valley full of ghosts beyond those, but my cousin also swears he once wrestled a river spirit, so."

Jude, beside her, made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't died somewhere back on the frozen march. He leaned against the farmwife's doorframe with the boneless ease of a man who had learned to look harmless in a hundred border towns, and said, "Your cousin sounds like a reliable source."

"He's an idiot, but he's family." The farmwife—her name was Dagny, and she had the forearms of a woman who kneaded her own bread and brooked no argument from dough or strangers—looked between them with the shrewdness of the rural poor everywhere, which is to say she saw everything and priced it fairly. "You lot came over the high passes this late? In what?"

"In what?" Sera asked.

"What did you come *in*. Carts. Horses. You're carrying everything you own in sacks, half of you are barefoot, and that man there has frostbite scars on his ears that say he walked through winter in autumn." Dagny jerked her chin at the crowd of twenty-six survivors huddled in her yard, dripping meltwater onto her flagstones. "Nobody walks out of the high country in autumn unless something behind them is worse than the cold."

Sera opened her mouth to deliver the truth—that behind them was a country being eaten by a god made of hunger wearing the face of the god who had built its cage, and that the country itself had been the cage all along, and that every treaty and census and executed prisoner's last word in the Undercroft archive had been both wall and prisoner—and then she looked at Dagny's face, kind and tired and utterly unequipped, and closed her mouth again.

"There was a plague," she said. "In the high valleys. We're the ones who left in time."

It was not even a lie, exactly. It was a translation. Sera had spent thirty years translating bureaucratic language into human language for petitioners and prisoners' families, and she found, distantly, that the skill held even when the bureaucracy in question had ceased to exist.

Dagny looked at her for a long moment. Then she stepped back from her doorway and raised her voice across the yard: "Well, don't stand there dripping. Barn's got hay loft space, there's a well, and if any of you can mend a roof I'll trade work for supper. And *you*—" this to Sera, with a pointing finger that brooked no appeal, "—you look like you haven't slept since the moon was young. Kitchen. Now. There's soup."

There was, in fact, soup. It was barley and onion and a suspicious quantity of pepper, and it was the best thing Sera had ever tasted, and she told Dagny so, and Dagny said "of course it is, I made it," and Sera felt something in her chest crack—not painfully, the way ice cracks before a thaw.

* * *

They stayed in Marlow's Ford nine days, because roofs needed mending and frostbite needed tending and because none of them, Sera suspected, could bear yet to be in motion. The villagers asked few questions, which Sera understood was less discretion than a settled people's immunity to other people's catastrophes. You could survive almost anything if the harvest was in and the roof didn't leak, and the misfortunes of strangers were weather from another country.

On the third day, the trouble started.

Not the dramatic kind. The quiet kind. Tam Wicker—one of the choir, a Graysink miner with hands like root vegetables and a singing voice that could strip paint—came to Sera in the barn loft where she'd claimed a corner, holding his cap in both hands.

"Begging pardon, mistress Vey. But I've gone and lost my street."

Sera set down the ward-glass shard she'd been turning over in her fingers for nine days. "Lost your—"

"My street. Copperlane, in Graysink, where I was born. I know it *was* called that. I remember knowing it. But I can't—I can't see it anymore, if you follow. Like a room with the shutter closed. And my mum's name is doing the same thing. I know she *had* one. I keep reaching for it and it's—" His jaw worked. "It's like the word's there but the shape of it's gone."

Sera knew exactly what it was like. She had spent the previous night dreaming of Bellharrow, and in the dream the town had been perfect—the crooked clock tower, the smell of the tanneries, the specific way rain came off the hills in slanting gray sheets—but every face in it had been smooth as an egg, and when she woke she could not remember whether the ward-scribes' hall had faced east or west, and she had helped *build* the thing.

"The Hollow doesn't stop at the border," she said quietly. "It ate the records. We were the records."

Tam's face did something terrible and brave. "So it's just going to keep going? All of us, bit by bit, till we're—" He didn't finish. He didn't have to. They had all watched the Kettle twins walk into the gold, trading everything for one shining moment, and they had all understood the offer.

"No," Sera said, and heard her own voice come out harder than she expected, harder than it had ever come out in thirty years of filing and indexing and being politely ignored by her superiors. "No, it isn't. Sit down, Tam. Get your slate."

He blinked. "My—"

"You kept tally in the mines. I've seen you do sums faster than the overseers. Get your slate and chalk and sit *down*."

He sat. And Sera picked up the shard of ward-glass—Sera's shard, the flaw she'd hidden in the wards years ago out of spite, which had become the only surviving lens through which Thornfell could be seen as it had been—and held it up, and looked through it at Tam Wicker.

The glass showed what a place used to be. That was its nature, its small treacherous gift. Through it she saw Graysink whole: the minehead gantries, the slag heaps steaming in cold air, and running down the hillside, a lane lined with coppersmiths' workshops, roofs green with age, and—

"Copperlane," she read aloud, and her voice shook, because the name was *there*, in the glass, written in the memory of a place that no longer existed anywhere else in the world. "Copperlane runs from the mine gate down to the Graysink bridge. Third house on the left, going down, has a blue door, because the coppersmith who built it lost a bet and had to paint it whatever color the winner chose."

Tam made a sound like a man surfacing from deep water. "Aye. Aye, that's—it's the Pell house. Old man Pell lost the bet to his wife." He laughed, wetly. "She never let him hear the end of it."

"And your mother's name," Sera said, and looked deeper into the glass, and found it waiting, patient as a pressed flower, "is Maret Wicker. Born Maret Coble. She sang second voice in the Graysink choir, below the priest, and everyone said she carried the tune and the priest took the credit."

Tam Wicker put his head in his hands and cried like a child, and Sera sat with him in the hay dust and held the shard steady, and thought: *this. This is the work now.*

* * *

She started writing that night, by tallow candle, in the back of Dagny's oldest ledger book, purchased honestly with roof-mending labor.

She began with a title, crossed it out, tried another, crossed that out too. *A History of Thornfell* was wrong—too grand, too finished, too much like the sort of thing her superiors in the ward-scribes' hall would have commissioned and then buried in the Undercroft. *An Account of the Loss* was worse. Finally she wrote, in her small precise scribe's hand:

**What Was There. Being a record of the nation of Thornfell, which no longer appears on any map, compiled by Sera Vey, formerly Second Indexer of the Ward-Scribes' Hall, Bellharrow, from the testimony of survivors and the witness of the ward-glass, so that it will have been true.**

*So that it will have been true.* She stared at the phrase a long time. It was grammatically strange and she decided she liked it that way. The past tense of a country that had been unwritten needed new grammar.

Then she began at the beginning, which was not treaties or kings. Her superiors would have begun with treaties and kings. Sera had spent thirty years watching what actually got remembered in a country—the things people scratched on doorposts and sang in mines and told children to frighten or comfort them—and she began there instead.

*"Bellharrow has forty-one streets,"* she wrote. *"I can currently account for thirty-three. I will get the rest or die trying, and I record here that I am ashamed of the eight I have already lost, and that shame is itself a form of keeping."*

She named streets aloud as she wrote them, because she had learned in the barn loft that the naming mattered, that a name spoken over a survivor worked into them like salve into a wound. Copperlane. Tanners' Row. The Stair (nobody remembered why it was called the Stair; it was a street, not stairs, and the mystery was part of the record). Weepers' Wynd, named either for a famous funeral or a famously leaky aqueduct, sources disagreed, and Sera wrote down both versions and noted that disagreement was also history.

By the fourth night she had filled eleven pages and acquired a routine, and the routine had acquired an audience.

It happened without planning. She wrote in the barn loft by candlelight, and the first night Tam came back with two others. The second night there were six. By the fifth night there were fifteen survivors crowded into the hayloft, sitting on feed chests and overturned buckets, and when Sera read aloud—street names, dead men's names, the recipe for Bellharrow plum brandy that Widow Anser had guarded jealously for sixty years—they listened the way starving people watch bread being broken.

And they corrected her. That was the miracle of it, the thing that made Sera's throat tight every evening: they *corrected* her.

"Tanners' Row wasn't forty houses, mistress, it was thirty-eight, the Anser place burned and was never rebuilt."

"The clock tower leaned *south*, not east—you're thinking of the chapel."

"Widow Anser's brandy had *cloves* in it, my gran bought some at the autumn fair the year the fair flooded, cost her three days' washing wages and she said it was worth every copper."

And Sera would strike the error, write the correction, and say aloud: "Amended by testimony of [name], who remembers." Every entry witnessed. Every fact anchored to a living person. She was building, she realized somewhere around the twelfth page, a new kind of archive—not stone and redaction and warden-text, but a distributed one, written into twenty-seven heads, each of them holding a piece, none of them holding enough to lose alone.

Jude came on the seventh night. He stood at the bottom of the loft ladder with his arms folded, listening to her read the names of the executed prisoners whose final words she had catalogued in the Undercroft—she gave each one their full name, their crime (most crimes, she noted acidly in the margin, were poverty wearing a uniform), and their last words, several of which were funnier than they had any right to be—and when she finished, he climbed halfway up the ladder and stopped.

"You sleep at all?" he asked.

"Some."

"You eat?"

"Dagny feeds me like a siege victim."

"Good." He looked at the candle, the ledger, the crowd of hollow-eyed people hanging on the names of streets that no longer existed anywhere but here. Something moved behind his face—something old and tired and, lately, strangely light, the lightness of a man who had set down a weight so heavy he'd forgotten he was carrying it. "Keep going, then," he said. "It's good work."

From Jude Dravenpoor—god-sworn, god-unsworn, the man who had spoken two true names and unmade heaven—that was a canonization. Sera nodded, and he went back down into the dark, and she picked up her pen.

* * *

On the ninth day, Dagny's cousin arrived from over the eastern ridge, riding hard, and brought the first news of the world beyond Marlow's Ford that any of them had heard.

Most of it was local—weddings, a bad beet harvest, a feud over a millpond—which Sera recorded anyway, because a history that begins nowhere begins with whatever is at hand. But the last item made the cousin lower his voice and glance at the northern horizon, where the mountains stood under a sky that had, in the nine days since the door, remained stubbornly, ordinarily full of stars.

"Folk in Harrowdale say the north country's gone strange," he said. "Lights in the high passes some nights, gold-colored, moving like it's alive. And the caravans that used to come down from over the mountains—" he shrugged. "They've stopped coming. Been near a month now. Merchants are grumbling. Say the roads up there aren't where the maps say they are anymore. Some of the old drivers swear the passes have just... shut. Like a door."

Sera felt the words go through her like a blade finding the gap in armor. Beside her, she heard Jude exhale slowly through his nose.

"A month," he said quietly, when the cousin had moved on to complaining about the price of nails. "The Hollow's done with Thornfell, then. Eaten down to the roots. It'll be looking for new ground."

"Or it died with its meal," Sera said. "We don't know. That's the truth of it—we don't know, and nobody left alive can find out except—"

She stopped. They looked at each other. Between them lay the entire unsaid sentence: *except the man who remembers the roads, and every road he uses costs him a memory he can't replace, and he has already paid his mother's face and his childhood and a god he can no longer even grieve properly because he cannot remember why he loved it.*

"Well," said Jude, after a while, in the tone of a man reading his own death warrant and noting the handwriting is nice. "I always did hate leaving a road unfinished."

That night, Sera sat late over the ledger. She had reached the letter M in her index of the dead, and she paused there, pen hovering, over the entry she had been circling for nine days without letting herself write it.

Then she wrote:

**Vale, Merrick. Condemned man. Name erased by the state; hunger remained. Wronged beyond the capacity of any record to repair, including this one. I did not know him. None of us did—that was the point of what was done to him. I record his name here because it was spoken aloud once, at great cost, by a man who carries the cost still, and because a name that is written and read and said is a name that cannot be fully erased. If there is anything left of him anywhere—in the gold, in the dark, in the grammar of the world—I hope it hears this.**

**Merrick Vale. Merrick Vale. Merrick Vale.**

She blotted the page, closed the ledger, and looked out the loft window at the northern mountains, black against a sky scattered with honest stars.

Somewhere up there, the lights were moving like something alive. And tomorrow, she suspected, the man downstairs who slept without dreams for the first time in years was going to start asking Dagny's cousin questions about the high passes, and Sera was going to have to decide whether the history she was writing was going to end with Thornfell—or continue.

She dipped her pen one more time and wrote, at the top of a fresh page, in letters larger than her usual hand:

**Book Two: What Comes After.**

Below it, after a moment's thought:

**Recorded by witnesses. Corrected by the living. Ongoing.**
