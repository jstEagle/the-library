## 3. Sera of the Crown's Church

The missing pages were the color of absence.

Sera Vane had catalogued forty thousand documents in the Royal Archive beneath the Cathedral of the Radiant Throne, and she could feel a gap in a shelf the way a blind woman felt a missing stair. This one was on the third tier of the western stacks, among the fiscal annals nobody had touched since the reign of Aldric's grandfather. A ledger spine, cracked leather, gilt lettering gone dull: *Tithes and Offerings, Consolidated, Years 411–437.* She had pulled it eleven days ago to verify a footnote. It had been there. She had returned it herself.

It was not there now.

In its place stood a receipt slip in her own handwriting—her own, because she had written it, because the archive's rules required that any volume removed be logged by the removing hand—and she stared at it for a long time before understanding what it meant. She had taken the book out. She simply could not remember doing so.

That was the thing about the crown's memory. Everyone assumed an archivist forgot nothing. The truth was worse: Sera remembered everything except what was taken from her, because you cannot miss a hole unless someone shows you its edges.

She pressed her thumb to the slip. The ink was dry. Old dry. Weeks old, maybe more.

*How many times have I stood here before?*

She went back to her desk in the gallery above the reading floor—a desk of black oak with a brass lamp and a view of the cathedral's eastern rose window, which at this hour poured red light across the tiles like something bleeding—and she began, quietly, methodically, to do the one thing the archive had never trained her for.

She looked for other holes.

* * *

She found seven.

Not books missing—gaps wearing books like costumes. Volumes still shelved where their contents should have been elsewhere; folios whose page counts no longer matched their own tables of contents; marginalia in a chancellor's hand referring to entries that existed nowhere. And threaded through all seven, the same word recurring across two centuries of fiscal record like a vein of ore through worthless stone:

*Tithe.*

Not tithe as the church used the word—not candles, not coin, not the tenth part of a harvest given up to the Radiant Throne. These tithes were denominated in stranger currency. *One year of answered rain, rendered.* *The hearing of ten thousand prayers, transferred.* *Divine attention, apportioned quarterly, per contract.* The clerks who wrote them had treated godhood as a commodity with the flat diligence of men counting sacks of barley, and every entry bore the same counterparty notation:

*Received by the Office of the Crown, per instrument executed through the Gilded Oath.*

Sera sat very still in the red light of the rose window.

She knew the Gilded Oath the way everyone in the capital knew it: a brokerage of impossible trades, licensed, taxed, tolerated. You could sell your luck there. You could buy a stranger's courage, borrow a dead man's last hour, pawn a year of your life against future fortune. The church called it blasphemy dressed as commerce; the crown called it revenue; the common people called it the only bank that never lied. Its keepers were famous for their discretion. Its ledgers were famous for being absolute.

And now someone—or something—was reaching into the royal archive and taking the memory of those ledgers out of Sera's head, one visitation at a time, leaving receipts signed in her own hand.

There were only two explanations. Either the Gilded Oath had found a way to trade in the crown's memory itself—which would be a crime beyond any charter—or someone inside the cathedral did not want the tithes remembered, and had the power to make forgetting look like routine.

Both explanations ended in the same place. Both explanations meant she needed to see the guild's books.

She also knew, with a clarity that had nothing to do with archives, exactly whom she would find behind them.

* * *

The Gilded Oath's counting house stood in the Saltquarter where the merchant streets gave way to the tide-flats, a building of grey stone and green bronze that looked less like a bank than a held breath. Sera dressed for the part: the plain grey traveling habit of a church assessor rather than her archivist's robes, papers of negotiation forged with a skill she chose not to examine too closely, and the seal of the Chancery's trade office, borrowed through channels that would cost her three favors to repay.

At the door, a keeper with a face like a closed book asked the nature of her business.

"Reconciliation of cross-holdings," she said. "The Chancery wishes to regularize certain instruments held between the crown and private parties."

"Which parties?"

"The kind," she said, "whose names appear in your master ledger."

Something moved behind the keeper's eyes—the flicker of a man deciding whether to laugh or send for knives—and then he stepped aside. "Third door on the left. Someone will attend you."

The corridors smelled of ink and tallow and, faintly, of salt water, as though the sea were kept somewhere below as collateral. Clerks passed her with ledger-chains at their belts, keys of green bronze, faces professionally blank. And then the third door opened onto a small room with a scarred table, two chairs, one high barred window letting in a blade of morning light—

—and Ada Coldharbour rose from behind the table as if he'd been struck.

For a moment neither of them spoke. Ten years compressed itself into the space between the doorframe and his face.

He hadn't changed much. That was the first cruelty of it. The same lean frame, the same ink-stained fingers, the same careful stillness of a man who had learned young that stillness was safer than motion. Only his eyes were different—older, and guarded in a way she remembered them never being, even at the worst. Even the night she left.

"Sera." Her name in his mouth sounded like something he'd practiced saying neutrally and failed.

"Master Coldharbour." She let the title do its work, cold and official, and watched him flinch almost imperceptibly at the distance it put between them. "I'm here on behalf of the Chancery's trade office."

"You're here in a church assessor's habit carrying a Chancery seal that was issued nine days ago to a man named Corwin Hale, who is currently in Vellamere negotiating grain tariffs." He said it without heat, without triumph, just the flat precision she remembered from watching him work a ledger line. "Sit down, Sera. Please. Before someone less sentimental than me checks your papers."

She sat. Her heart was hammering and she hated it.

"You checked my credentials."

"I check everyone's credentials. It's my function." He sat too, slowly, folding his hands on the scarred wood. Up close she could see new lines at his eyes, a scar along his jaw she didn't know. Ten years of a life she'd had no part in. "You're good. Better than most. But the seal wax is Vellamere stock and the countersignature is a quarter-turn off true. Whoever forged it respects the original. They just don't love it."

"I didn't come here to be caught."

"No," he agreed quietly. "You came here because you couldn't stay away. Those are different crimes."

* * *

They talked around it for an hour. Trade reconciliations, hypothetically; cross-holdings, in the abstract; the guild's obligations to the crown, in the careful language of two people fencing with words they both knew how to use. And underneath it, like a second conversation conducted in glances and pauses, ran everything unsaid.

She had loved him once—loved him with the whole reckless breadth of her twenty-three years, before duty had shaped itself into something with edges. She had left him for the archive, for the order and permanence of it, for the promise that some things could be kept safe forever if only someone devoted their life to keeping. He had let her go without argument, which at the time she'd read as indifference and now recognized, looking at the careful wreckage of his face, as the discipline of a man who had decided he wasn't worth fighting for.

"You look well," he said at one point, and it came out wrong, too heavy, and they both pretended it hadn't.

"The archive agrees with me."

"Nothing agrees with anyone in this city. They just stop arguing loudly enough to be ignored." He almost smiled. Almost. "Why the tithe records, Sera?"

There it was. No preamble, no deflection. He'd been waiting for it, and she realized with a cold slide in her stomach that he'd known why she came before she'd walked through the door—that perhaps the guild had known, that perhaps the forged seal had been permitted past the threshold precisely so that this meeting could happen under a roof the guild controlled.

"Why does an archivist ask?" she countered.

"Because I'm the one who answers questions here. It's my function." His hands were perfectly still on the table, but she saw the tension in his wrists. "So ask."

"The royal archive has holes in it." She kept her voice level, professional, and hated how much effort that took with his eyes on hers. "Records relating to tithes paid by the crown—tithes not of coin but of—" she paused, choosing the word with care— "*attention*. Divine attention. Answered prayers. Rendered to the Office of the Crown through instruments executed by this guild. And someone has been removing not just the records but the memory of them, from me, personally, repeatedly, over what I now understand to be years."

Silence. The blade of light from the window had crawled across the table and now lay between them like a drawn line.

"And you think we took them."

"I think whoever took them wanted me to suspect you. Or wanted you to know I suspected you. One of those."

"That's paranoid."

"I'm an archivist. Paranoia is literacy."

Something in him broke then—not visibly, nothing so dramatic, just a settling, like snow sliding from a branch. He looked older suddenly, and tired, and she thought with a strange pang: *he's been carrying something alone for a long time.*

"If I told you," he said carefully, "that the guild keeps its records absolutely—every trade, every instrument, every party—that our ledgers are complete and incorruptible and have been for two hundred years—"

"I'd believe you. That's what frightens me."

He frowned. "Explain."

"A perfect record is only innocent if the trades themselves are innocent." She leaned forward. "Ada. I've spent two weeks finding the shape of a hole. Do you understand what that means? Not the contents—the shape. Whatever the crown has been paying for, it's been paying for a long time, in amounts large enough to require annual consolidation, through brokers discreet enough to leave no trace in any memory they can reach. Your guild is the only institution in this city that deals in what cannot otherwise be owned." She let that sit. "What did the crown buy?"

And Ada Coldharbour—who had lied to superiors, hidden a body, decrypted forbidden pages, and carried the word of a silent god for ten years—looked at the woman he had loved for a decade and said, with the flawless calm of a man spending his last honest coin:

"I don't know."

* * *

She believed him. That was the worst part. She read him the way she read documents—for structure, for the tells of alteration—and the statement was genuine, which meant either he truly didn't know, or whatever he knew was buried deeper than knowledge, in a place even he couldn't reach.

Or he was better at this than she was.

She gathered her papers. He rose when she did, and for a moment they stood on opposite sides of the scarred table with the bar of sunlight between them, two people who had once shared a bed and now shared only suspicion, each holding a piece of a truth that would not fit together.

"The Chancery will expect a report," she said.

"I'll write you one. Full reconciliation. Every figure accurate." A pause. "Every figure useless. You know how these things are done."

"I know how these things are done." She moved toward the door, then stopped, hand on the frame, not turning around. "Ada. If you find out what the crown bought—if it ever crosses your desk—"

"It won't."

"But if it did."

The silence stretched long enough that she heard, distantly, the sound of surf against the tide-flats, the guild's strange borrowed ocean breathing below them.

"If it did," he said finally, "I'd want to tell someone who reads holes for a living."

She nodded, once, and left.

In the corridor, the keeper with the closed-book face fell into step beside her. "Shall I show you out, Assessor Hale?"

"Please."

But at the great green-bronze door, the man paused, and his voice dropped to something almost gentle. "A message arrived for you while you were upstairs. From within the guild, unsigned." He pressed a folded scrap into her palm. "We weren't certain it was intended for you. But it uses your name, and the old honorific."

Her stomach turned over. There was only one person alive who still called her by the honorific from the university, from the years before the church, before the archive, before everything hardened.

She waited until she was three streets away, in the shadow of a chandler's awning, before she unfolded it. The handwriting was Ada's—she knew it instantly, the precise downstrokes, the archivist's economy—but the words were not his style at all. Too plain. Too urgent.

*The hole you're looking for isn't in the archive. Ask yourself who benefits when a god stops answering prayers. Then count how long it's been since one did.*

*And Sera—the courier who died last week on the Vellamere road prayed to Veyl before the end.*

*Veyl answered.*

She read it twice. Around her, the Saltquarter went about its morning—fishwives crying their catch, cart wheels grinding, the ordinary machinery of a city that did not know its king had been buying pieces of heaven for two hundred years.

Sera Vane, keeper of the crown's memory, folded the note into her sleeve beside her forged seal, and understood that she had just stopped working for one side of this question and started working for the truth instead—and that the truth, whichever way it cut, ran straight through the heart of the man she had never stopped loving, and might destroy them both.
