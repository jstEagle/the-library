## 6. Ashes on the King's Road

Sera Vane had been taught that prayer was a door, and the gods stood behind it.

She had believed it the way she had believed in the dawn: without examination, without effort, because it had always simply been so. The Church of the Crown taught that the old gods answered only the faithful, and that the crown's church was faithful above all others. Ten years of silence from those gods had not shaken her faith; it had refined it, the way fire refines ore. Silence was a test. Silence was the long dark before the answering.

Now she rode at the rear of a column of two hundred soldiers and forty wagons, and ahead of her the sky over the shrine of Mereth was the wrong color.

Not the orange of ordinary burning—she knew that color well enough from hearth-fires and candle-flames and the great bronze lamps of the cathedral. This was a white-gold blaze, too bright for its distance, flickering with something that moved against the wind rather than with it. The light pulsed. It kept time, she realized with a lurch, like a heartbeat.

"Archivist." Captain Drenna reined her horse back alongside Sera's mule. The captain's face was grimy, her voice pitched low so the men wouldn't hear. "You don't have to see this."

"I'm here under the king's writ," Sera said. "I have to see everything."

Drenna studied her a moment. There was something almost like pity in the woman's eyes, and Sera hated it. "The inquisitors said you'd say that."

* * *

They had left the capital eleven days ago, and Sera had spent every one of them lying by omission.

Her orders were simple enough to recite in her sleep: accompany the punitive column to the free-city borderlands; document the destruction of unauthorized shrines; recover any texts, relics, or records of pre-Crown worship. She was the crown's archivist. Records were her jurisdiction. No one questioned why an archivist rode with soldiers instead of staying among her shelves.

No one but herself, at three in the morning, staring at the ceiling of whatever waystation they'd claimed, turning over the thing she could not stop turning over.

For three months, pages had been vanishing from the royal archives. Not stolen in any way she could prove—no broken locks, no disturbed dust, no gaps in the catalogues. The entries were simply gone, as though they had never been written, and only her memory insisted otherwise. She remembered a ledger of annual disbursements from the royal treasury to an unnamed recipient. She remembered auditing it twice. She remembered the strange, heavy quality of the vellum, and the way the ink seemed to sit *on* the page rather than in it, like something that did not wish to be absorbed.

And she remembered where such vellum was made, because she had once held a book bound in it across a counting-house table, while a man with ink-stained fingers and a smile he thought he hid well explained the Gilded Oath's rules of trade.

*Ada.*

She had told herself the guild connection was professional suspicion. She had requested the trade negotiation with the Gilded Oath because the archives' losses pointed there, and because an archivist followed evidence. All true. None of it the whole truth. The whole truth was that she wanted to see him again, wanted it with an ache that embarrassed her, and that when she'd sat across from him in the counting house and watched his face go carefully blank at the sight of her, she had felt both the old warmth and a new, colder question:

What else was he hiding?

The column crested the last hill before the shrine, and Sera stopped asking questions, because the answer was spread out below her like an opened wound.

* * *

The shrine of Mereth had stood for six hundred years. She knew this from the archives—the very archives now missing their pages. A wayside sanctuary at the crossroads of two pilgrim routes, built around a spring that never froze. The god Mereth was a small god, a local god, a god of travelers and safe crossings. Children left copper coins at the spring. Farmers left bread. There was nothing at the shrine worth a single soldier, let alone two hundred.

The inquisitors had burned it anyway.

From the hilltop, Sera could see the white-gold fire consuming the sanctuary's cedar roof, and she could see the figures standing in a ring around it—not fighting, not fleeing, just *standing*, seven robed figures of the Inquisition arranged with liturgical precision, their arms raised, their mouths moving in unison. And between them and the burning shrine, kneeling at the edge of the spring, was a priest.

An old man. She could see that much even at this distance—the stoop of his shoulders, the whiteness of his hair. He wore no armor. He held no weapon. He was praying.

She knew he was praying the way you know a person is weeping from across a room: by the shape of them. He knelt with his forehead nearly touching the water, his hands open at his sides, and even over the roar of the fire and the wind carrying ash up the hillside, Sera heard his voice.

It should not have carried. Two hundred yards, a burning building, a column of armed men breathing and shifting and jingling on the ridge above. But his voice reached her as clearly as if he knelt beside her, and it was not loud—it was *everywhere*, the way a bell is everywhere inside itself.

"*Mereth, keeper of crossings, I have kept your spring. I have fed your pilgrims. Sixty years I have swept your stones and I ask one crossing only—carry my people past this fire. Carry them.*"

The fire went out.

Not died down. Not smothered. *Went out*, all at once, the way a lamp goes out when pinched, and in the sudden dark Sera heard a sound she would spend the rest of her life trying to describe: a vast indrawn breath, as though the whole valley inhaled.

Then the spring blazed with light.

It came up out of the water like a pillar, white and gold and impossibly gentle, and in its glow Sera saw the pilgrims—there must have been thirty of them, huddled in the shrine's stone undercroft—rise up unharmed, untouched, their faces lifted and shining. She saw the old priest rise with them, laughing, laughing through tears, his arms spread wide.

"*Go,*" the god said—or the light said, or the spring said; the voice came from everywhere and nowhere and it was warm, warm as a hearth, warm as the faith she had built her whole life inside. "*My crossing is open. Go, little ones. Go—*"

The inquisitors moved.

Seven figures stepped forward in perfect unison, and each one spoke a single word—a word Sera did not know, a word that hurt to hear, that scraped across her mind like a nail across slate—and the pillar of light *bent*.

That was the only word for it. Light should not bend. But the pillar bowed toward the seven robed men like a tree in a gale, and the warmth in the god's voice curdled into something else, something Sera had never imagined a divine voice could hold.

Confusion. Then fear.

"*What—what is this—what are you doing—I am small, I am only Mereth, take nothing, I have given, I have always given—*"

"Tithe," said the lead inquisitor, in a voice flat as a ledger line. "In the name of the Crown, render what is owed."

The light screamed.

Sera had heard men scream in battle drills, had heard horses scream when they broke their legs, had once heard a woman scream for three days while fever burned her mind empty. Nothing had prepared her for this. The sound was not loud so much as *total*—it filled the valley, filled her skull, filled the space behind her eyes, and it was the scream of something ancient and innocent being pulled apart at the roots of itself. The pillar of light thrashed, thinned, unraveled into threads, and the threads streamed toward the seven inquisitors, toward the rings they wore, toward—

Toward the capital. Toward the crown. She understood it suddenly, completely, the way you understand a fall already in progress: the threads were flowing *through* the inquisitors, through some channel she couldn't see, pouring away east along the king's road like water down a drain.

The old priest lunged for the spring. "Stop—stop, please, he kept us, sixty years he kept us—"

An inquisitor struck him aside without looking at him. The priest crumpled against the shrine's scorched wall and did not get up.

The threads thinned. The scream faded—not ended, *faded*, dropping octave by octave until it was a whimper, then a whisper, then a silence worse than either. The spring's water ran clear and cold and utterly ordinary. The light was gone.

Where the shrine had stood there was a roofless ring of blackened cedar and a handful of stunned pilgrims, and a dead god's spring, and an old man who had prayed for sixty years and been answered once, at the end, in the worst possible way.

Sera found she was gripping her mule's mane hard enough to tear out tufts of hair. Her lips were moving. She didn't remember starting. She was saying, over and over, barely aloud: "*He answered. He answered. He answered.*"

* * *

They made camp that night in the shrine's own courtyard, because the inquisitors declared it cleansed ground and the captain preferred walls to open fields. Sera sat apart, near the dead spring, and tried to pray, and found she did not know how.

The words were still there. That was the horror of it—the forms rose in her mind exactly as trained, the invocations, the praises, the ordered architecture of a lifetime's devotion. But every phrase now had a second meaning laid over it like a palimpsest. *Blessed be the Crown, vessel of divine favor.* Favor. Was that what she had seen poured down the king's road? *The gods honor the throne above all altars.* Honor. Was that what a scream was, when the screaming thing was small and old and generous?

She thought about the archives again. The missing pages. Disbursements from the treasury to an unnamed recipient, in ink that sat on the vellum like it didn't want to soak in.

Annual payments. Annual tithes.

Ten years ago, the gods had gone silent. Ten years ago, give or take, the first of those entries would have been written.

She had assumed the Gilded Oath was stealing from the crown. Sitting in the dark beside a murdered god's spring, Sera Vane admitted to herself, with the precision she brought to everything, that she had gotten the direction of the theft backwards. Someone in the capital was buying divinity. Someone wearing the crown was *eating*.

And somewhere in the guild's ledgers—in ledgers kept by a man whose hands she had once held, whose silences she had once learned to read like scripture—was a record of every meal.

"Archivist."

She looked up. One of the inquisitors stood over her, his hood back, his face mild and forgettable. She had heard the others call him Brother Cassian.

"You witnessed the rite today," he said. It was not a question.

"I witnessed it."

"And your report will reflect that the god Mereth rendered its lawful tithe to the Crown?"

Sera looked at the spring. Clear water. Cold. Empty.

"My report," she said slowly, "will reflect what I observed."

Something flickered behind the inquisitor's mildness—satisfaction, perhaps, or appetite. "Good. Because we ride for the next shrine tomorrow. And after that, the border temples. There is a great deal of work remaining, Archivist, and the Crown values thorough record-keepers." He smiled faintly. "Especially ones with friends in unusual places. We understand you've corresponded with the Gilded Oath."

The night air went very still around her.

"I conducted a trade negotiation," she said. "On the Crown's behalf."

"Of course." He turned to go, then paused. "Their keepers maintain remarkable records, we're told. Ledgers going back a century or more. When our work here is done, the Crown may have need of a thorough archivist to examine them." His eyes caught the firelight. "You'll want to be the one who does, I think. Before someone else burns them."

He walked away, robes whispering, and Sera sat alone by the dead spring with her heart hammering and her duty in pieces on the ground around her, and understood two things with perfect clarity.

The first was that the crown intended to destroy the Gilded Oath's ledgers—and everyone who had read them.

The second was that she had to reach Ada Coldharbour before they did.
