## 7. A Trade in Impossible Things

The memory-broker kept her office on the guild's ninth floor, where the stairs stopped pretending to be honest and began to spiral in ways that made Ada's teeth ache. Nine floors up in the Gilded Oath was where the guild traded in the things that could not be weighed, and the architecture knew it. The walls here were paneled in pale wood that seemed to drink lamplight rather than reflect it, and the air had a taste to it—faintly sweet, faintly wrong, like fruit that had ripened somewhere other than this world.

Ada had climbed these stairs perhaps a dozen times in ten years, always for other people's trades. He had watched fishermen sell their luck, widows buy back an hour of a dead husband's voice, a swordsman purchase the memory of his own worst day so he could sleep again. He had recorded every transaction in a fair hand and never once imagined he would climb these stairs for himself.

The door at the top bore no nameplate. It didn't need one. Everyone in the free cities who dealt in impossible things knew where Maren Quill kept her wares.

He knocked twice.

"Enter, keeper." Her voice came through the wood as if the wood were only a courtesy. "You've been standing out there for four minutes deciding whether to knock. That's longer than most people take to decide whether to sell me their childhood."

Ada pushed the door open.

Maren Quill sat behind a desk that was less furniture than landscape—a sprawl of dark timber stacked with brass instruments Ada had no names for, glass vials of smoke that moved against the draft, and ledgers. Of course ledgers. Even here, everything came back to ledgers. Hers were bound in colors that hurt to look at directly.

She herself was small and gray-haired and utterly unremarkable, which Ada had long ago understood was the point. A broker who looked like a grandmother put clients at ease right up until she took a year off their lives with the gentleness of someone trimming a wick.

"Master Coldharbour." She folded her hands. "Ten years you've audited my trades and never once asked for one. I'd begun to think you were immune to wanting."

"I want plenty," Ada said. "I've just never been able to afford it."

"And now?"

"Now I need something more than I need the years."

Something flickered behind her eyes—not surprise exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman who had seen this exact expression on a thousand faces and knew precisely what it cost.

"Sit," she said. "Tell me what truth you're buying."

* * *

He told her as much as he dared. He left out Veyl's word—*ledgers*—spoken into a dying courier's mouth like a coin pressed into a beggar's palm. He left out the decrypted master ledger and the crown's annual tithes. But he told her the shape of it: that his god had been silent for ten years, and that silence, he now believed, was not absence but captivity, and that he needed to know where the cage stood.

Maren listened without interrupting, which was itself unnerving. Brokers interrupted. It was how they found the seams in a client's resolve, the places to widen until the whole bargain split open in their favor.

When he finished, she was quiet for a long moment. Then she rose, crossed to a cabinet that hummed faintly when she touched it, and drew out a single vial of gray smoke.

"You understand what I'm about to say is not a sales pitch," she said, setting the vial on the desk between them. "It's disclosure. Guild law requires it, and frankly, I require it, because I have sold truths before that destroyed the buyers, and I would prefer not to watch it happen to the man who audits my accounts."

"Understood."

"The knowledge you're asking for exists in exactly three heads in this world. One belongs to a corpse. One belongs to the thing that did the imprisoning. And one—" she tapped her own temple, "—belongs to me, because eight years ago a dying priest sold me his final confession rather than let it die with him. He thought the truth should survive him even if no one ever bought it. Sentimental fool. It has been sitting in my inventory ever since, gathering interest."

"What kind of interest?"

"The kind memories accrue when they're true and terrible." She turned the vial slowly. Inside, the smoke coiled, and for half a heartbeat Ada thought he saw a face in it—young, tonsured, mouth open mid-scream or mid-prayer, possibly both. "I can't tell you what he saw. I can't summarize it, soften it, or sell you the short version. Memory doesn't work that way. You buy the whole year of his knowing or none of it. And the price—"

"A year of mine," Ada said. "I know the rate."

"Do you?" She leaned forward. "Everyone knows the rate. Nobody understands it until it's paid. Let me explain what you're actually trading, keeper, because unlike some brokers in this building, I don't profit from clients who don't know what they're signing."

She held up one finger. "A year of your life means a year off the end. Not a year of suffering added—no. A year of *everything* subtracted. Your last year. Whatever it would have been. A granddaughter's wedding. A last summer. The morning you finally forgive yourself for something. Gone. You will die twelve months earlier than the ledger of your body intended, and you will never know what you missed, which is either mercy or theft depending on the day."

A second finger. "And there is the extraction itself. To move the priest's year of knowing into you, I must first make room. Memory is not stored in the head, whatever the surgeons say. It's stored in the soul, and the soul keeps its books the way you keep yours—every entry balanced. So I will take from you a year of your own past. Random. Unchosen. You will not know which year until it's gone, because if you chose, you'd choose wrong, and everyone chooses wrong."

Ada's mouth had gone dry. "Gone how?"

"Gone as in gone. There will be a hole shaped like a year, and around the hole, the people who lived it with you will remember things you cannot. Sera Vane—" Ada flinched, and Maren's eyes sharpened like a hawk's. "Ah. Yes. I thought so. If any part of the year I take involves her, you will lose it while she keeps it. Imagine that. Imagine her remembering something tender that you no longer remember happened. Imagine her realizing you've forgotten."

"That's monstrous."

"That's *inventory management*," Maren said flatly. "Monstrous is what the crown does with gods. This is commerce. Now." A third finger. "Last item. The priest's knowledge comes with the priest's terror attached. Knowing is not a clean commodity. He died knowing where your god sleeps, and the last thing he felt was the thing that caged it turning its attention toward him. When the memory settles into you, you will feel that too. Briefly. Vividly. Some buyers have wept for a week. One tried to claw his own eyes out, though in fairness he was already unstable."

Ada stared at the vial. The smoke inside had stilled, as if listening.

"And if I walk away?"

"Then you walk away, and I sell nothing, and you spend the rest of your shortened-by-nothing life wondering." She smiled, and it was almost kind. "That's the trap of my trade, keeper. Refusal costs nothing except forever."

* * *

He asked for an hour. She gave him a chair by the window instead, and a cup of tea that tasted of nothing at all, which he suspected was deliberate—nothing, so that the taste of the trade wouldn't be contaminated.

From the ninth-floor window, Ashfall spread out below him in the gray light of late afternoon. The counting house district, the fish-market, the shrine-row where the old altars still stood cold and unlit after a decade of silence. Somewhere beyond the eastern hills, the usurper's armies were burning their way toward the free cities, feeding on something vast and divine, and every prayer screamed once and went quiet.

*Ledgers,* Veyl had said. One word, after ten years. His god had spent its last free breath pointing him at a book.

Ada turned his cup in his hands. He thought about the routine he had built—the audits, the fair entries, the small debts balanced nightly, the decade of keeping his head down while a god he resented said nothing. He had told himself the silence was abandonment. Abandonment was bearable. Abandonment meant he owed nothing.

Captivity changed the arithmetic entirely.

If Veyl had been caged—if the crown had been drinking divinity through a straw stuck in his god's throat—then every silent night of Ada's resentment had been a night he'd spent cursing a prisoner for not writing letters. And worse: the ledgers in his own keeping, the ones he'd balanced so carefully, so *neutrally*, were the locks. Or the map to them. He didn't yet know which, and that was precisely why he was sitting in a broker's office drinking flavorless tea.

There was also, he admitted to himself, in the hour's honesty the tea demanded, a smaller and uglier reason. Sera. Sera, who knelt in the crown's church and prayed to gods the crown was eating. If the cage stood where he feared it stood, then her faith was built on a foundation of stolen stone, and every day he delayed was another day she prayed into a lie.

He set down the cup.

"Maren," he said. "Draw up the contract."

* * *

Guild contracts for impossible trades ran to eleven pages. Ada read all of them, because he was who he was, and Maren waited with what appeared to be genuine approval.

*Item the First:* the Buyer, Ada Coldharbour, keeper of ledgers, doth purchase one (1) contiguous span of knowledge, being the final confession of Anselm Dory, priest of the drowned chapel, deceased.

*Item the Second:* the Price shall be one (1) year of the Buyer's natural span, taken from the end, and one (1) year of the Buyer's remembered past, taken at random, irrecoverable by any art known above ground or below.

*Item the Third:* the Seller guarantees the knowledge to be true as held, not necessarily true as the world turns.

He signed. His hand did not shake until the third signature, and then only a little.

"Which year will you take?" he asked.

"If I told you, it wouldn't be random." Maren rose and lifted the vial. Up close, the smoke inside was moving again, restless, like something pacing a cell. "Though I'll grant you this much, since you balance my books and have never once padded an expense account: I don't choose the year. The extraction finds its own weight. It takes whichever year your soul can least afford to lose. The priests used to call that the tithe-year. They believed the soul surrenders what it hoards most tightly, because that's the only payment it respects."

"That's a horrifying theology."

"It's an accurate one." She uncorked the vial. "Breathe normally. It goes easier if you don't fight it, and harder if you do, and either way it goes."

The smoke poured out of the vial and did not disperse. It hung in the air between them, gathering itself, thickening, taking on weight and texture and—Ada realized with a lurch—the smell of a chapel he had never entered. Salt water and candle tallow and something older underneath, mineral and vast, like the air in a cave that had never seen sun.

Then it struck him in the chest, and the world inverted.

* * *

He was kneeling on wet stone. Candlelight. His hands—no, *another man's* hands, scarred, trembling—pressed flat against a floor that hummed. Above him, the vaulted ceiling of a crypt so deep beneath the capital that the bones of the city itself grew around it like tree roots around a coffin.

And below him—

Below him was a presence. Not a voice. Not a light. A *weight*, the way the sea is a weight when you stand at the bottom of it, and threaded through that weight, chains. Chains of paper. He could see them now, could not stop seeing them: thousands upon thousands of pages, each one inscribed in a fine clerk's hand, each page a record, each record a lock, wrapped around something luminous and drowning.

*Veyl.*

The god did not speak. Gods of silence don't. But Ada felt it feel him—or felt the priest feel it, the dead man's year pouring its knowing into him like cold water into a cracked vessel—and what he felt was not despair. It was *attention*. The god, chained in the dark beneath the holiest floor of the crown's cathedral, had noticed one small mortal kneeling above it, and had done the only thing left to it.

It had begun to count.

Every tithe the Crown-Thing took. Every year, every prayer, every stolen scrap of divinity, flowing down through the paper chains and *recorded on them*, because the chains were made of records, and records could be read, and read backward, and unmade—

The god could not break its chains. But it could teach someone else to read them.

And then the other knowing arrived, the terror Maren had promised, because the Crown-Thing had felt the god noticing, had turned its attention downward through the stone like a searchlight through deep water, and the priest had run, had run for days through tunnels and smuggler roads, carrying the location in his head like a coal, and at the end, dying in a ditch outside Ashfall, he had torn the knowing out of himself and sold it to a gray-haired woman on the ninth floor rather than let the thing that hunted him find it in a corpse—

The scream. Ada heard the priest's scream, felt it, wore it. And beneath the scream, patient and vast and *hungry*, something far below the cathedral turned over in its sleep and began, very slowly, to wonder whether anyone else had read the chains.

* * *

Ada came back to himself on the floor of Maren Quill's office, cheek against cold boards, gasping like a landed fish.

"There it is," Maren said quietly. She was crouched beside him, and her face, for the first time since he'd known her, looked genuinely sorry. "Breathe. It settles in an hour. The screaming lasts about three days. I'm told."

"How long—"

"Four minutes. You paid four minutes ago." She helped him upright, and he swayed, and the room tilted and steadied. Something was missing. He could feel the absence the way you feel a missing tooth—not the pain, just the strange smooth gap where something used to be. A year of his past, scooped out and gone. He reached for it and found nothing, and the nothing had edges.

"Which year?" he whispered.

"I told you. I don't—" Maren stopped. She was looking at him strangely. "Keeper. What did you see? In the trade. The specifics."

"The cathedral," he said. His voice came out scraped raw. "Chains of ledger-paper under the cathedral. Every tithe written on them. The god taught itself to be read."

Maren sat back on her heels. For a long moment she said nothing at all, and the sweet-wrong air of the ninth floor seemed to go very still.

"Eight years," she said finally, "that confession has sat in my inventory, and I never once opened it. Broker's discipline—we don't sample the stock." She laughed, short and humorless. "Do you know what I sold the crown last spring, Coldharbour? A routine appraisal. A courier's route-memories, worthless, three months of mud and tolls. And they paid triple for it and asked me one question: had anything unusual passed through my inventory in recent years. Anything *old*. Anything *priestly*."

Ice slid down Ada's spine. "What did you tell them?"

"The truth. That I appraise, I don't gossip." She stood, and began, briskly, closing the shutters of her window one by one. "But they asked, keeper. The crown's inquisitors asked a memory-broker whether she held a priest's dying confession, and I told myself it was coincidence, because the alternative was inconvenient. And now you climb my stairs asking to buy exactly that, and the smoke tells you a name I've never spoken aloud, and I find myself recalculating."

She turned to face him, and in the shuttered light her gray hair looked like frost.

"They know something survived that priest. They may not know what. They may not know where. But they are looking, and I am now the second person alive who knows what you carry in your head, and I am telling you—as your broker, as your auditor's client, and as the closest thing to a friend this floor allows—get out of Ashfall. Tonight. Because within the week, someone is going to come to this office asking questions, and I am a merchant, Ada Coldharbour, not a martyr. I will sell them whatever keeps my charter intact."

Ada got to his feet. The missing year ached in him like weather. Below the window, through the slats of the shutters, the lamps of Ashfall were coming alight one by one, ordinary and golden, and somewhere among them Sera was saying evening prayers to a god lying in chains beneath the enemy's cathedral floor.

"Then sell me one more thing," he said. "Not from inventory. From you. The fastest road south out of this city that the crown doesn't watch."

Maren Quill studied him for a long moment. Then she smiled, thin and sharp as a ledger-line.

"For that one," she said, "the price is a favor, to be named later. And keeper—I collect."
