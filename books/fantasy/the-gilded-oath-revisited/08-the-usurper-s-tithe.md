## 8. The Usurper's Tithe

The king dreamed of drowning.

He had dreamed of it every night for eleven years, and every night the Crown-Thing let him, because the dream was useful. A mind that believed itself drowning did not struggle. It clutched at whatever was offered—a rope, a hand, a voice—and held on with the desperate strength of the dying. Aldric had been holding on for a decade now, and his grip was very nearly gone.

The thing that wore him sat in the dark of the royal bedchamber and listened to the man beneath it whimper in his sleep.

*Soon*, it thought, in the way glaciers think of valleys.

It did not sleep. Sleep was a tax paid by living things, and it had stopped being a living thing before this kingdom's founders had learned to write. What it did instead was *digest*. It sat in Aldric's body—Aldric's hands folded on Aldric's chest, Aldric's lungs drawing slow, ceremonial breaths—and it turned over the day's intake the way a miser turns coins.

There had been so little today. That was the problem. There was always so little now.

A prayer from a border village, answered: a fever broken, a child spared. The prayer had cost the villagers nothing but hope, and hope was cheap, but the *answering*—the answering required divinity, and divinity had to come from somewhere. The thing had reached down through the church's consecrated channels and drawn up what remained in the local vein. The god of that river country had not screamed. It had barely stirred. There was almost nothing left to take. Eleven years of tithes, and the old gods were husks rattling in their shrines.

The thing catalogued the harvest anyway. Waste offended it more than scarcity did.

*Three drops,* it recorded, in the ledger of itself. *Muddy. Barely worth the channel.*

It had been magnificent once. It remembered magnificence the way a beggar remembers a palace—not as possession but as proof that possession was possible. Before the crown, before the cage it had built around the silent one, it had fed freely. Gods were not meant to hoard their power; that was its first principle, its founding heresy, the truth it had discovered in an age when the world was younger and the divine walked unhidden. Power pooled in gods like water in stone basins, and water, left standing, went stale. It had only ever wanted to *move* things along.

That it also drank what it moved was, it felt, a matter of perspective.

*

In the morning it was King Aldric again, and the morning was audiences.

The throne room of the capital smelled of incense and floor wax and the particular fear-sweat of petitioners who had rehearsed their speeches for weeks. The thing found the smell nourishing in a secondary way. Fear was not divinity, but it was related to divinity—the way fat is related to muscle, the way a candle is related to the sun. It could live off fear if it had to. It had lived off fear for years now, because the tithes were thinning, because the gods were running dry, because—

Because one basin still held water.

The thought arrived, as it always did, with a taste like copper at the back of Aldric's stolen throat. The silent god. Veyl. The one it had caged rather than drained, because some vessels were too deep to empty quickly and too strong to shatter. You did not drink from a well like that. You capped it. You built a cathedral on top of it and you waited, patient as rot, while the world above forgot the well was there.

Eleven years of patience. And now the wells elsewhere were dry, and the patience was beginning to feel less like strategy and more like hunger with nowhere to put itself.

"Your Majesty." The Lord Chamberlain bowed, a gray man with a gray voice. "The delegation from the free cities requests audience regarding the grain tariffs."

"Deny them," the thing said through Aldric's mouth. It had learned the king's voice perfectly, which was easy, because the king no longer used it. "Tell them the tariffs reflect the costs of protecting trade routes they are too weak to protect themselves."

"As Your Majesty wishes."

It watched the chamberlain go and noted, distantly, that the man's bow had grown shallower over the years. Small rebellions of posture. The court sensed something wrong with its king the way livestock sense weather. They could not have named it. But they stood a half-step farther away than they used to, and their laughter in the king's presence came a half-beat too fast, and the thing collected all of it—the distance, the flinches, the too-quick smiles—as a farmer collects leavings after harvest. Thin fare. Fare nonetheless.

*Aldric,* it said inwardly, addressing the drowned man. *Wake. Your kingdom is asking after you.*

Nothing. There was almost never anything anymore. In the early years the king had surfaced daily, raging, bargaining, weeping; the thing had enjoyed those years in a connoisseur's fashion, the way a diner enjoys resistance in the meat. But consciousness erodes when everything you experience arrives secondhand, when your hands move without you, when your wife's face—dead these nine years, mercifully, or perhaps the thing had simply arranged it—smiles at you from someone else's mouth. Aldric had gone under the way men drown in cold water: quietly, at first believing he was swimming.

Sometimes, in the deepest hour of night, the thing felt the last flicker of him and kept it alive deliberately, the way one keeps a coal alive under ash. Not mercy. Utility. A dead host decayed. A living one, even a hollowed one, drew breath, signed decrees, sat a horse at reviews. The body needed a soul the way a lamp needed oil—not much, just enough to keep the wick wet.

*Hold on,* it told the flicker, with something that in a kinder creature might have been tenderness. *I am nearly finished.*

*

The interruption came at dusk, and it came from the archives.

The thing felt it before it understood it—a wrongness in the tithe-channels, the way a spider feels a torn strand not by seeing it but by the sudden asymmetry of its web. It set down the state papers it had been pretending to read (it read them all; governance was camouflage, and camouflage required diligence) and turned its attention inward, downward, into the lattice of consecrated conduits it had spent a decade threading through the church.

One thread had gone quiet.

Not severed—severance would have announced itself, would have rung like a snapped harp string through every ward in the palace. This was quieter than severance. This was a thread that had been *unwritten*. Records of the annual tithe-draws flowed to the royal archive by law and by necessity; the thing required documentation the way a parasite requires a circulatory system, its feeding mapped in ink so the feeding could continue without collapse. Eleven years of entries. Ledgers of divinity, bound in calfskin, shelved in the restricted vaults beneath the Hall of Records.

Three volumes were missing. And the absence of them had been *smoothed over*—catalogue pages rewritten, indices adjusted, the memory of the missing books sanded out of the clerks who handled them. Someone had paid for this. Someone had traded for it. And there was only one institution in the world that dealt in trades of that texture.

The thing sat very still in the king's body, and around it the candles guttered, though there was no draft.

*The Gilded Oath.*

It knew the name. Of course it knew the name. A brokerage of impossible things, licensed in the free cities, tolerated at the margins of the crown's law because tolerance was cheaper than war. It had considered them, years ago, in the long planning of the cage: a guild that trafficked in memories and years and luck was either a threat or a tool, and it had judged them tools-in-waiting, corruptible, purchasable. It had even bought from them once, early on—a brokered forgetting, a witness in the western provinces made to misremember a shrine's destruction. The transaction had gone smoothly. The guild asked no questions about purpose. The guild, it had concluded, was a machine for asking no questions.

But machines had operators, and operators had eyes, and somewhere in the counting house of the Gilded Oath, someone had looked at eleven years of the crown's purchases and *kept records of their own*.

The thing rose from the desk. In the mirror above the hearth, King Aldric rose too—a broad man going gray, a face built for hearty laughter that had not laughed honestly in a decade. The eyes were the problem. The eyes had always been the problem. Courtiers spoke of the king's piercing gaze as a compliment. The thing let them. Piercing was accurate.

It reached out along the tithe-lattice again, carefully, feeling for the shape of the theft. Three volumes, spanning the middle years. And threaded through the archive's silence, faint as a scent trail, something that made the hunger rear up in it like a struck animal:

*Divinity. Residue. On paper.*

The ledgers themselves had soaked in what they recorded. Eleven years of written tithes, and the ink had become a vessel—you could no more burn those pages without releasing what they held than you could burn a god's name without the god hearing it. Which meant the missing volumes were not merely evidence. They were *keys*. Every entry described a lock it had built; every description, read rightly, was a blueprint for picking it.

And there was exactly one lock it could not afford to have picked.

*Veyl.*

The name moved through the thing like a swallowed coal. Beneath the cathedral, behind seven veils of consecrated stone, the silent god hung in chains of its own recorded debts, and the thing had told itself for eleven years that the cage was permanent, that permanence was the entire point of cages. But it had built those chains out of ledger-paper and spoken oaths, and here, now, three volumes of the master record walking out of its own archive on human legs—

Cages were only as permanent as the secrecy of their design.

*

It summoned High Inquisitor Maren Corvane at the ninth bell, in the small chapel adjoining the royal apartments, because the thing liked conducting certain business under consecrated roof—it enjoyed the irony, and besides, the chapel's wards kept conversations private in ways that mattered.

Corvane arrived as she always arrived: punctual, plain-robed, her face arranged into the expression of a woman who had burned away everything soft in herself and called the scar tissue faith. She knelt. The thing regarded her with something adjacent to approval. It had made her, in a sense—had found her thirty years ago, a novitiate with a talent for certainty, and had fed her doubts to her until nothing remained but appetite wearing vestments. She believed she served the church. She served the mouth.

"Your Majesty."

"Rise, Inquisitor. There is a theft in the Hall of Records. Three volumes from the restricted vaults, tithe-ledgers, years nine through eleven of the reign." It gave her the shelf-marks, the catalogue falsifications, the names of the clerks whose memories had been smoothed. He watched her absorb each detail without blinking. Good. "The thief is professional. The thief was paid. Find the payer."

"And if the trail leads outside the crown's jurisdiction?" she asked. Meaning: the free cities. Meaning: the guild.

"Then the trail has crossed into territory where the crown's writ runs thinner than the crown's patience." The thing let Aldric's voice drop into the register it saved for moments like this, the register that made hardened officers look at their boots. "You will go to the Gilded Oath. You will present yourself as an auditor of cross-border trades—that fiction exists for your use; the guild respects commerce and fears scrutiny, and you will be both. You will find their keeper of accounts. Every brokerage keeps one. The man or woman who touches every contract, who knows where the bodies of transactions are buried."

"You believe the ledgers were taken by the guild itself?"

"I believe," the thing said, "that the guild has spent ten years keeping accounts for everyone in the world, and that somewhere in their vaults is a page with my purchases written on it. I want the page. I want the keeper who can read it. And I want—" it paused, tasting the next words, savoring them the way it savored nothing else anymore, "—whoever the page was taken *for*."

Corvane's eyes lifted. Even she, hollowed as she was, felt the weight in that pause. "Taken for, Majesty?"

"Theft is commerce, Inquisitor. No one steals three volumes of divine accounting to admire the calligraphy. Someone commissioned the theft, or someone intends to sell what was stolen, and either way there is a buyer at the end of the chain—a buyer who already suspects what the crown's coin has been buying." It crossed to the chapel window. Below, the city kindled its evening lamps, thousands of small fires, thousands of households that prayed to gods the thing had already drunk to the dregs. "When I find that buyer, I will know how much the world knows. And then I will decide how much of the world needs to remain in it."

"You suspect the free cities' council?"

"I suspect everyone. It is my only reliable virtue." It turned back. "One instruction above all others, Corvane, and you will carry it in your bones: the ledgers are not to be burned. Whatever you find, whatever resistance you meet—if the choice comes to losing the volumes or losing your life, you will lose your life. Do you understand why?"

"No, Majesty."

"No," it agreed softly. "You wouldn't. But you will bring them to me intact, because those pages are not records of what I have done. They are the *recipe* of what I have done. And a recipe, in the right hands, can be undone."

It dismissed her, and she went, robes whispering, and the thing stood alone in the chapel under the painted gaze of saints it had personally starved.

*

Night again. The king's body lay in the great bed, and the thing sat up inside it, awake, doing what it did instead of sleeping.

It took inventory.

The wells: dry, or near enough. The river-country god, empty. The storm-god of the eastern isles, a skin of moisture over cracked clay. The mother-of-the-fields, whom three million farmers still prayed to at planting—there was a horror it permitted itself to enjoy: the prayers rose season after season toward a god that had nothing left to give, and the thing skimmed the *prayers themselves* now, the raw hoping of the faithful, thin gruel but constant. The old religions were becoming, in effect, farms it no longer needed to tend.

Except one.

Beneath the cathedral, the deep basin. Capped. Chained. Full.

It extended its awareness down through the palace foundations, through bedrock and consecrated strata, to the place where Veyl hung in the dark, and it listened—for the thousandth time, for the ten-thousandth—to the silence it had purchased at such extravagant cost.

Silence. As always. The god did not rage, did not plead, did not so much ripple. It had not made a sound in eleven years, and the thing had told itself this was defeat, this was the sound of a prisoner who had accepted the sentence.

But tonight, with three missing volumes sitting in its mind like stones, the silence sounded different.

Tonight it sounded like a held breath.

*Someone knows,* the thing thought. *Someone has been reading the recipe.*

And far below, in the dark under the cathedral, something that had been motionless for eleven years shifted its weight—one degree, no more, the smallest movement any captive had ever made—and the thing felt it through eleven fathoms of stone, and understood, with the first genuine emotion it had experienced in a decade, that the ledger it was hunting and the god it had caged were already in conversation.

It rang for the guard.

"Wake High Inquisitor Corvane," it said, in the king's voice, calm as still water. "She sails tonight."
