## 10. The Road to Veyl's Grave

The caravan moved by night and slept by day, which suited Ada fine. He had spent ten years learning that the safest place for a dangerous man was inside the dark, among people who asked no questions because they could not afford the answers.

Maren Dusk ran her wagons the way he kept his ledgers: every route memorized twice, every toll paid in coin or silence, nothing written down that could be read. She was sixty if she was a day, with a face like weathered oak and hands that had strangled at least one tax collector, according to guild rumor, though the rumor changed the reason each time it was told. She had taken his silver at the border post without blinking, looked once at the satchel chained to his wrist, and said only: "Whatever's in there, it stays under your coat until we're past the Salt Gate. I don't sell what I don't see."

"You don't ask what you carry?"

"I carry flour sometimes," she said. "I've never once needed to know what grew it."

So Ada rode in the third wagon, between bolts of salted linen and crates marked as dyestuffs that smelled of nothing at all, and watched the free cities fall away behind him like a shore receding from a ship he hadn't wanted to board.

He slept badly. That was the trouble with roads. In the counting house he had a routine so worn into him it functioned even when his mind refused: wake, wash, ink, audit, eat, sleep, in an order so fixed that Veyl's decade of silence had simply become another line item—a debit carried forward, year over year, never collected, never written off. On the road there was no routine. There was only the sway of the wagon, the creak of the axles, and sleep that came down on him like a dropped door.

And in that sleep, for the first time in ten years, Veyl spoke.

* * *

The first dream came on the third night out from Corvess, in the hour before dawn when the caravan made camp in a hollow of gorse and the sentries' pipes glowed like patient eyes.

Ada stood in the counting house. Not the real one—he knew that immediately, because the real one had never had windows, and these windows showed a sky the color of deep water, moving slowly, as if the whole room lay at the bottom of a sea. The ledgers were all in their places on their shelves, but they were wrong: the spines breathed. In and out. Slowly. Like sleeping animals.

At his own desk sat a figure with its back to him, writing. It wore his master's coat—the grey one, frayed at the cuffs where Aldous Wren used to rub his thumb when he was thinking—and when it turned, it had no face. Where a face should have been there was a page, blank, faintly ruled, waiting.

*You kept the accounts,* it said. The voice was not Aldous's. It was not anyone's. It was the sound a quill makes, stretched long enough to become speech. *Ten years. You did not miss a single entry.*

"I kept my oath," Ada said. His voice sounded small in the drowned room. "You never gave me anything back. Ten years of prayers into a well with no bottom. I audited you, old man. You know what I found? Nothing. A dead account."

The figure set down its quill. The blank page of its face tilted, and Ada had the sudden vertiginous sense of being *read*—every line of him scanned, weighed, entered in some column he would never see.

*You think silence is absence.* The shelves exhaled. *A ledger with no entries is not an empty ledger, Ada Coldharbour. Sometimes it is a hidden one.*

"Meaning what?"

But the water-sky brightened toward false morning, and the room dissolved around him like ink in a rinse-jar, and he woke with his hand already inside his coat, gripping the satchel strap, heart going like a courier's heels.

Maren was sitting on the wheel hub nearby, sharpening a knife against a stone, watching him with the flat patience of a woman who had seen men wake from bad dreams on three continents.

"Loud sleeper?" she asked.

"No," Ada said.

"Mm." She tested the edge on her thumb, nodded. "We make the Salt Gate tomorrow night. After that, king's road all the way to the capital. Whatever god you're arguing with—" she didn't look up, didn't soften, just said it like reporting the weather— "settle it before we get there. Roads like that one, arguments travel badly."

* * *

The second dream came the following night, and this time Veyl did not bother with rooms.

Ada stood on a road he recognized—the king's road itself, white stone running straight as a rule toward the distant smudge of the capital—but the land on either side was wrong. It was covered in paper. Fields of it, acres of it, pages fluttering in a wind that never touched him, each sheet dense with columns of figures. And walking the rows between them, bent like harvesters, were figures in crown livery, gathering the pages into carts, cart after cart after cart, rolling them all toward the city on the horizon.

*Do you see,* said the voice, everywhere now, in the wind and the wheels and the whisper of paper. *Every tithe. Every prayer answered and then taken back. Every year of a god's life weighed out in coin and carried to the cathedral below.*

"The tithes," Ada said. "The entries in the old ledger. Payments from the crown."

*Payments.* Something moved through the dream that might have been laughter, if laughter could be cold enough to frost glass. *Your guild calls them payments because your guild was built by practical men. They are not payments. They are chains. Every entry in that ledger is a link, and every link is locked, and the lock—*

The harvester-figures stopped. All of them, at once, in perfect unison, and turned their faces up toward him. They had no faces either. They had seals. Wax stamps pressed where features should be, the royal sigil sunk deep in red.

One of them pointed at him.

Ada woke with Maren's knife an inch from his throat and her other hand clamped over his mouth, and above them both the night sky was full of torchlight on the ridge—a patrol, six riders, the king's colors snapping in the wind.

Nobody breathed. The riders passed. After a long while Maren took her hand off his mouth and sheathed the knife.

"Dreaming loud again," she said.

* * *

He tried not to sleep after that, which lasted two nights and then betrayed him on the third, on the long climb through the hills above the Salt Valley, when exhaustion pulled him under despite the daylight and the noise of the caravan.

This time there was no road, no counting house, no fields of paper. There was only a cell.

It was small and round and utterly dark except for the walls themselves, which were made of ledger-paper laid leaf upon leaf upon leaf, thousands of pages thick, glowing faintly with the ghost-light of ink. And bound within those walls—pressed into them the way a flower is pressed into a book—was something vast.

He could not look at it directly. When his eyes went to the center of the cell they slid off, the way a thought slides off a word forgotten on the tip of the tongue. But he felt it. A pressure like deep water. An attention like winter sunlight. And threaded through everything, so quiet he almost missed it, a rhythm: slow, vast, patient. Breathing.

"You're really there," Ada said aloud, and heard his own voice crack on it. "All this time. Under the cathedral. They told us you'd gone. Abandoned us. Left the world to run on its own gears."

*Would a god who abandoned you keep count of every prayer?*

The voice was closer now. Not outside him. Under his skin, in the marrow, in the old scar along his ribs where a blade had found him in his twenty-third year and should have killed him and hadn't.

*You prayed to me that night. Do you remember what you asked?*

He remembered. He had been bleeding out in an alley behind the counting house, and he had prayed—not for life, he'd been past bargaining—but *let me finish the quarter's books. Let me balance the column first.* And something had held the wound closed until morning.

"I asked for time to finish an audit," Ada said bitterly. "That's what I prayed to a god for."

*Yes.* The vast attention settled on him, gentle and terrible. *And I gave it. Because you were mine, keeper. Bound by oath and ink. And because—*

The pressure in the cell shifted. The paper walls rustled, and Ada understood suddenly, with a clarity that arrived like a blade between the ribs, that the walls were not decoration. They were not a cage built around a god.

They were a cage built *of* the god's own account. Every page a record of every tithe. The ledger was the lock, and the lock was the ledger, and—

"That's why you went silent." His voice came out strange, flat, the voice he used for reading bad news aloud. "It wasn't abandonment. It wasn't punishment. If you spoke—if you answered a single prayer—they could trace it. Follow the sound back to where you were buried. The Crown-Thing was listening for exactly that."

*For ten years I let my faithful pray into darkness,* Veyl said, and for the first time the voice carried weight that bent him, grief with a god's full weight behind it, *because one answered prayer would have led them to the ledger, and the ledger is the key to my chains. Your silence-keeping, keeper. Your ten years of uncollected debts. You thought I had forsaken the account.*

"No," Ada said. "I thought you'd forsaken *me*."

*I hid myself inside your oath.* The breathing rhythm slowed, deepened. *Where better to bury a secret than in a man who keeps secrets for a living? Where better to hide a god than in a clerk nobody looks at twice? You were not my prisoner, Ada Coldharbour. You were my hiding place.*

The cell began to dissolve, the paper walls lifting away like leaves in wind, and in the last moment before waking Ada felt—not forgiveness, nothing so clean—but something older and more uncomfortable. Recognition. Two creatures who had both spent a decade pretending to be empty.

* * *

He woke at dusk with tears frozen on his face—he hadn't noticed the cold coming on—and found Maren sitting across the campfire from him, mending a harness strap, watching him with those oak-bark eyes.

"There's a shrine two miles back down the valley," she said. "Old one. Pre-crown. We pass shrines like it every hundred miles and nobody so much as spits at them anymore, because praying's been like shouting down a well since before you were born." She bit the thread, tied it off. "Last night, one of my boys swears he saw light in it. Saw the votive lamps lit that nobody lit. So." She looked at him across the fire. "Either the world's changing, clerk, or you're carrying something that changes it. Which is it?"

Ada put his hand inside his coat, on the satchel, on the master ledger with its dead master's hand and its ten years of stolen divinity recorded in neat columns.

"It's a debt," he said. "Owed to a lot of people. I'm taking it to be repaid."

Maren considered that for a long moment. Then she spat into the fire, which he had gathered was her version of a blessing.

"Salt Gate tonight," she said. "After that, no more hiding in caravans. King's road runs open to the capital, and the capital knows your face by now—I'd bet my wagons on it. Whatever your god wants from you, clerk, you'd best decide how much you're willing to pay. I've hauled cargo my whole life, and I'll tell you the one law of the trade: nothing gets delivered free."

That night, riding down through the Salt Valley under a sky needling with stars, Ada dreamed one last time, and this dream was short.

He stood again in the drowned counting house. The faceless figure in his master's coat was gone. In its place, on his desk, lay a single sheet of fresh paper, and on it, in ink still wet, a single line in a hand he knew better than his own:

*The account comes due beneath the cathedral. Bring the ledger. Bring yourself. Come alone — S.V.*

Sera's initials. Sera's handwriting—or close enough to break something loose in his chest that he'd spent a decade keeping balanced.

Veyl's voice, one final time, quiet as a closing book:

*She is already on the road, keeper. And she is not alone.*
