## 13. A Year Spent, a Truth Bought

The debt came due on a night of hard frost, in the back room of a caravan that smelled of tallow and old paper.

Ada had known it was coming the way a man knows a storm is coming—not from any sign, but from the shape of his own silence. For three days he had not spoken of the memory-broker's price. He had ridden at the head of the smuggler caravan through the pass towns, haggling tolls and forging manifests, doing all the small competent work that had kept him alive for a decade, and all the while the missing year sat inside him like a coin swallowed sideways.

Sera noticed first. She always did.

"You keep touching your temple," she said, across the fire, her church-trained hands steady around a tin cup even now that she'd burned her credentials and everything they stood for. "Like there's something behind your eye trying to get out."

"There is." He fed a stick to the flames. "A year I sold. It's coming home tonight."

"Can I—"

"No." Too fast, too sharp. He softened it with what passed, these days, for an apology. "You don't want to be in the room when it happens. Trust me."

She didn't argue. That was one of the thousand things he loved about her: she knew which doors to leave closed until he opened them himself.

* * *

The memory-broker traveled under the name Mistress Quill, though Ada suspected she'd worn a dozen names before that one and would wear a dozen after. She kept her wagon curtained in indigo silk, and inside it the air was thick as held breath. Shelves lined every wall, and on the shelves stood jars—not the grisly pickled organs of a hedge-surgeon, but glass vessels of colored smoke, each one stoppered and labeled in a hand so fine it looked embroidered. Memories. A decade of them, bought and banked, waiting for owners who might never come back to reclaim themselves.

Quill herself was small and gray and precise, with the eyes of someone who had watched ten thousand people remember who they really were.

"You're early," she said. "Debts are never early. That's how you know this one matters."

"The year," Ada said. "I want it back."

Something flickered across her face—pity, maybe, or its professional cousin. She turned to her shelves and ran one finger along the labels until she found the jar she wanted: a smoke the color of wet slate, shot through with a single thread of gold.

"I'll tell you what I told you when you sold it," she said, unstoppering nothing yet. "The price of buying back is steeper than the price of selling. You paid me a year; to take it back you pay the remembering. All at once. No anesthesia of time. Do you understand what that means?"

"It means I live it again. In one breath."

"In one breath," she agreed. "And here is the part sellers forget, keeper: memories don't come back clean. They come back *whole*. Whatever you cut out when you sold it—the thing you couldn't bear to carry—it's still in there. It's been waiting in my jar for ten years, growing fat in the dark." She set the vessel on the table between them. The slate-colored smoke stirred, like something turning over in sleep. "Are you certain?"

He thought about lying to her. He thought about the oath he'd sworn to Veyl, silent and resentful, and the newer, stranger purpose that had been growing in him since the road began—since the dreams, since Sera read the tithe entries aloud with her voice cracking, since the free cities put their walls and their lives behind a man who had spent his whole adult life being no one.

Veyl had chosen silence to hide the ledgers. Ada had chosen silence too. And he was beginning to suspect the two silences were the same silence, and that he could not free one without freeing the other.

"I'm certain," he said.

"Then breathe out," said Mistress Quill, "all the way, and don't take another breath until I tell you."

* * *

The year came back the way a flood comes back to a dry riverbed—all at once, and finding every channel already carved for it.

He was twenty-six again, and the counting house smelled of ink and beeswax, and Master Corvane Duskwater was alive.

Not dead. Not cold in the guild crypt with the ceremonial ledger laid on his chest, the lie Ada had helped write into the guild's own records. Alive, and standing in the archive's deepest vault with a candle guttering in the draft, holding the master ledger open to the tithe entries, saying in a voice gone strange and quiet:

*"Ada. Look at what the crown has been paying. Look at what we've been keeping safe all these years—and look at what it's been feeding."*

And then the door of the vault opened without anyone opening it, and the cold came in.

It wore no face yet, in those days. It wore King Aldric's body the way a thief wears a stolen coat—badly, at the seams—but it had already learned to wear the king's voice, and it used that voice to say, gently, reasonably, the way a creditor speaks to a debtor: *"You have been keeping accounts that belong to the crown."*

Corvane stepped in front of Ada. His old master, seventy years of ledgers in his shoulders, put his body between his apprentice and the thing that ate gods, and said the last words Ada ever heard him speak:

*"Run, boy. The ledger stays hidden only if the keeper is forgotten. Run, and be no one, and let no one find you—"*

And Ada ran.

That was the year. That was the whole of it, compressed into a single breath: the vault door, the cold, Corvane's hand shoving him toward the smuggler tunnel, the sound behind him—not a scream, because screams end, but a long diminishing note like a bell being swallowed—and then ten years of corridors and caravans and careful, invisible, cowardly competence, all of it built on the foundation stone of one simple fact:

His master hadn't died protecting the ledger.

His master had died because Ada ran.

* * *

He came back to himself on the floor of Quill's wagon, on his hands and knees, and the first breath he took tasted of blood where he'd bitten his cheek.

Quill crouched beside him, stoppering the empty jar with great care, as if closing a grave. "There it is," she said softly. "Welcome home."

"He didn't die," Ada heard himself say. His voice sounded borrowed. "The records say he died. I wrote—I helped write—they gave him a funeral—"

"The guild buried a ledger box," Quill said. "Grief makes excellent cover. Your master arranged it before he sent you running—he knew what was coming for him, keeper. He'd known for years. Why do you think he trained you to be invisible?" She rose, joints cracking, and looked down at him with those terrible patient eyes. "You didn't abandon him. You were the hiding place. Same as the god."

"Same as the god," Ada repeated, and laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle.

Because that was the poison in it, wasn't it. He had spent ten years telling himself a story: *the guild raised me, the work suited me, I chose the shadows.* A comfortable story. An orderly story, with every entry balanced. And underneath it, in the suppressed year, the truth had waited in its jar: he hadn't chosen anything. He had fled. He had taken the finest gift his master could give him—a decade of being no one—and he had called it a life.

The Crown-Thing had consumed Corvane Duskwater while his apprentice's footsteps faded down the tunnel, and Ada had built a career on top of the sound.

* * *

Sera was waiting outside the wagon when he finally emerged, hours later, into a night so cold the stars seemed nailed down. She took one look at him and didn't ask. She just walked beside him to the edge of the camp, where the caravan's lanterns gave out and the dark of the king's road began, and stood with him in the silence until he broke it.

"My master didn't die," Ada said. "Not the way the records say. The Crown-Thing took him. Ten years ago. While I ran."

Sera said nothing. She had been archivist to the crown's church; she knew better than anyone how records lie by omission, how the truest entries are the ones torn out.

"I thought I was careful," he went on. The words came out of him like splinters, one at a time. "I thought I was *disciplined*. Keeping my head down, paying my oaths, balancing other people's sins in neat columns. Ten years of it. And the whole time I was just—" His jaw worked. "Hiding. A child's trick. Don't move and the monster can't see you. Except the monster saw everything. It saw the ledgers. It's been hunting them for a decade and it never needed to find *me*, because I made sure I was nowhere worth looking."

"And now?"

"Now it knows exactly where I am." He turned to her. In the lantern-light her face was half gold, half shadow, and he loved her with a ferocity that frightened him more than the Crown-Thing ever had. "Now I'm the most visible man in the world. Envoy of the free cities. Keeper of the master ledger. The key to Veyl's chains." He shook his head slowly. "Do you know what the worst part is? Now that I can't hide anymore—now that hiding is dead and burned and buried with Corvane—I feel *lighter*. Ten years of weight, gone in one breath. Shame makes a poor foundation, Sera. But purpose—"

"Purpose makes a worse one," she said quietly. "If it's all you build on. Purpose burns hot and leaves nothing. I know. I had duty instead of purpose, and look what it cost me." She touched his arm, light as a ledger entry. "So don't build on shame either. Build on the debt."

He stared at her.

"You're a keeper," she said. "Think in entries. Corvane died covering your retreat—that's not a sin, Ada, that's a *loan*. The largest anyone ever extended you. And loans exist to be repaid." Her voice didn't waver. "You don't atone by drowning in what you owe. You repay it. In full. With interest."

For a long moment he couldn't speak. Then he laughed—really laughed, rough and startled, the first true laugh since the courier had died whispering to a silent god.

"With interest," he said. "Spoken like a woman of the Gilded Oath."

"Spoken like a woman who burned her credentials and has to believe the burning bought something." She squeezed his arm once and let go. "So. What does repayment look like, keeper? We're four days from the capital. The cities are holding their walls on your word. The god is dreaming at you every night. What's the entry?"

Ada looked east, down the frozen road, toward the distant smudge of the capital's lights—toward the cathedral whose spires he could just make out against the stars, pale as bone. Somewhere beneath it, in the dark, Veyl hung in chains of ledger-paper, every page a lock, every lock written in his own dead master's hand. And somewhere above it, wearing a king like a stolen coat, the thing that had eaten Corvane Duskwater sat on a throne, growing fat on prayer.

He thought of the smuggler tunnels. Kell had mentioned them once, drunk, contemptuous of the guild's founders—*old routes under the capital, from when the Oath was young and the crown was hunting us. Sealed now. Probably collapsed.* Probably. But the guild had been built to hide things, and Ada had spent ten years learning how the guild hid them, and if there was a way beneath the cathedral that the Crown-Thing had forgotten—

Then that was the road. That was the entry.

"Repayment looks like this," he said. "We go in under them. Through the old tunnels. We find the cage, we find the king, and we open the books." He flexed his fingers, feeling the ghost of the master ledger's weight in the satchel at his hip. "Every tithe returned. Every chain broken. Every debt collected—including mine."

Sera nodded once, slow, the way she did when an account finally balanced.

"And if the tunnels are collapsed?" she asked.

"Then we dig," Ada said. "I'm done letting the crown decide what's buried."

Far off to the east, above the cathedral spires, a light flared—brief, wrong, the color of the cold in the vault—and went out. Neither of them spoke. But Ada felt Veyl stir in the back of his skull like a sleeper turning over, and for the first time in ten years, the god's silence felt less like absence than like a held breath.

They broke camp before dawn.
