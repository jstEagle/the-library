## 14. The Cathedral Below

The tunnel mouth opened in the side of a drainage culvert three miles east of the capital's walls, behind a curtain of dead ivy that Ada had to cut away with his belt knife. The stone beneath the ivy was older than the walls themselves—older, he suspected, than anything the crown had built—and it bore a mark he knew from a hundred guild documents: a circle bisected by a vertical line. The Gilded Oath's oldest sigil, from before it had a name.

"Smuggler tunnels," Sera said, peering into the dark. Her voice was flat with exhaustion; they had walked two days since leaving the caravan, sleeping in ditches, speaking little. "I thought that was a story keepers told apprentices."

"It was a story we told ourselves." Ada struck a taper and held it to the dark. The flame leaned inward, drawn by air moving through the passage—air that smelled of stone and old water and something else, something faintly sweet, like incense burned down to nothing. "Every brokerage needs a way to move goods no one can see. We just forgot what our largest shipment ever was."

"And what was that?"

He didn't answer. He stepped through the ivy and let the dark take him.

* * *

The tunnels were not what he expected. He had imagined crawlspaces, low and wet, the kind of places smugglers actually worked. Instead the passage widened as they descended, its walls dressed in fitted stone, its floor worn smooth by feet long dead. Every hundred paces a niche held the stub of an ancient lamp bracket, and every bracket bore the same carved mark: circle, line.

Sera ran her fingers along the wall as they walked. "These aren't smuggler marks," she said quietly. She had spent fifteen years in archives; her hands read stone the way Ada's read ledgers. "This is masonry work. Royal quality. This tunnel was built *for* someone."

"For something," Ada said. "Look at the grade. We've been descending for an hour. Straight toward the cathedral hill."

She stopped walking. In the taper-light her face went pale and set at once—the look she wore when an archive yielded something terrible and true. "The cathedral sits on the oldest foundations in the capital. Everyone knows that. The church says the first temple was raised on holy ground."

"The church says a lot of things." Ada lifted the taper higher. Ahead, the tunnel forked, and both branches bore the same inscription, cut deep and filled with lead so it would still be legible after centuries: **HERE ENDS TRADE. HERE BEGINS TITHE.**

His skin crawled. He had written those words' inverse a thousand times in his own ledgers—*here begins trade, here ends debt*—without ever knowing he was quoting something older and darker.

"This isn't a smuggling route," Sera said. "It's a processional way."

"Yes." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "And I think my guild wasn't built to hide ledgers *from* the crown. Not originally. I think it was built to hide them *with* the crown. To keep the accounts of—" He stopped. Said it plainly, because there was no gentler version. "Of whatever the kings were taking. And then, somewhere in the last century, the guild forgot which side of the ledger it stood on."

They took the left branch, because Veyl had shown him the left branch—in the dream, three nights ago, the god had not spoken but simply *turned*, the way a reader turns a page, and Ada had woken with the certainty of a left-hand descent burned into him like a brand.

* * *

An hour deeper, the air changed.

Ada felt it before he understood it: a pressure against the ears, a heaviness in the chest, the sensation of walking into water without getting wet. The sweetness in the air thickened until it was almost a taste—frankincense and myrrh and underneath them something raw, animal, alive. Sera stumbled and caught his arm.

"Do you feel that?"

"Yes."

"It feels like—" She searched for the word, and he watched her find it and flinch from it. "Like standing too close to an altar during consecration. Like something is *paying attention*."

Veyl. Ten years of silence, ten years of unanswered prayers, and now, this close, the god's presence pressed against him like a hand against a door from the other side. Ada had resented that silence for a decade. He had told himself it was abandonment, indifference, the contempt of a power that had used him up. Standing here, feeling the pressure of that attention, he understood for the first time that silence could be a scream held in the throat.

The tunnel ended at a wall.

Not stone—paper. That was his first mad impression, and his second confirmed it: the wall blocking the passage was made of layered sheets, thousands upon thousands of them, compressed and fused into something harder than oak, pale as bone. And across every sheet, in hands he recognized—his master's hand, his own hand, the hands of keepers dead two centuries—ran columns of figures.

Entries. The wall was made of entries.

"Ada." Sera's voice was very small. "What am I looking at?"

He touched the paper-wall. It was warm. Under his palm he felt—not vibration exactly, but pulse. Slow. Patient. The rhythm of something breathing in a sleep that was not rest.

"Copies," he said. "Every tithe the crown ever paid, copied and bound here. The master ledger records the debts. This—" he pressed harder, and the paper gave slightly, like flesh, "—this is where the payments are *kept*. You can't steal a god's divinity and leave a receipt lying around. The receipts have to go somewhere. They have to be *held*."

"Held how?"

He looked at her, and saw she already knew, because she was an archivist and archivists understand that a record is never just a record. A record is a chain. A record is a lock.

"They're being used as chains," he said.

* * *

Beyond the paper-wall, a door. Iron, green with age, unbarred, unlocked—as if nothing in ten years had needed to keep people out, because nothing living came this way. Ada pushed it open and the light that spilled out was wrong: not torchlight, not daylight, but a gray luminescence like moonlight strained through cloth.

They stood at the top of a stair that spiraled down into the earth beneath the cathedral hill, and the stair was lined with niches, and every niche held a god.

That was the only word for them. Small figures, knee-high, carved of wood and bone and river-clay, some gilded, some plain, some so ancient their features had worn to suggestion. Gods of hearths and harbors, of childbirth and crossroads, of the small prayers ordinary people had whispered for a thousand years before the crown's church taught them to kneel to one throne instead of many. Each figure sat in its niche with its hands open, palms up, in the posture of receiving.

None of them moved. But all of them *watched*. Ada felt their attention the way you feel a room full of portraits following you—except portraits don't make your teeth ache.

"They're empty," Sera whispered. "Or—they're not empty. They're *hollowed*. These are vessels. When a god answers a prayer, part of it pours into the world, and when the prayer ends, the rest pours back. Someone caught what poured back. Someone has been bottling them."

"The tithes," Ada said. "The annual payments. It wasn't one theft. It was harvest. Year after year, king after king, siphoning the return-flow of every answered prayer in the kingdom." He started down the stairs because if he stopped moving he would be sick. "And the thing wearing Aldric's crown has been drinking the vintage."

At the bottom of the stair, the passage opened into a vaulted chamber vast enough to hold the cathedral above it—a buried twin, a negative of the holy place overhead, its pillars rising into darkness, its floor a single unbroken sweep of polished black stone. And in the center of that floor, lit by the gray light that fell from nowhere, hung the cage.

Ada had imagined chains of iron, of magic, of names spoken backward. He should have known better. He was a keeper. Of course it was paper.

Chains of ledger-paper, each link a folded page dense with entries, ran from four anchor-stones at the chamber's corners to a central mass—and the central mass was Veyl. He had expected the god to have a shape, and the god did, but the shape hurt to parse: a tallness, a stillness, a suggestion of wings folded wrong, of a face that was all patient eyes. Light moved under its surface like ink under ice, dimming and brightening with that slow pulse he had felt through the wall. Bound, starved, and yet—present. Ten years of silence, and the presence of it here was like standing inside a struck bell.

Every link of every chain bore writing. Ada walked to the nearest anchor-stone and read, and his blood went cold in a way the chamber's chill couldn't explain.

*Paid to the Crown, in the year of Aldric's coronation: one thousand answered prayers, rendered in full.*

His own guild's format. His own guild's hand—or his master's. Each entry was a link. Each recorded tithe was a lock. The accounting wasn't a record of the crime. The accounting *was* the crime, sustained, renewed, made permanent by the oldest law the guild knew: a debt honestly entered cannot be denied. As long as the ledger stood, the payment stood. As long as the payment stood, the chains held.

"That's why the ledgers matter," Sera said. She had gone white, and her voice shook, but her mind was working the way it always worked, fast and clear and merciless. "That's why the Crown-Thing wants them destroyed or controlled. If the entries are voided—if the debts are proven fraudulent, coerced, unpaid—"

"The locks fail," Ada finished. "Burn the ledger, break the chain."

"Then burn it now." She turned to him, eyes fierce. "You have it. You've carried it since the counting house burned. Burn it *now*."

He reached into his coat and felt the master ledger's weight against his ribs—heavy as a heart, heavier—and did not draw it out.

"Because it's not that simple," he said. "Look again. Read the last entry."

She read. He watched her lips move through the lines, watched her reach the final clause, watched her face change as she understood.

*Final payment pending: the Keeper's oath, rendered whole. Upon default, all prior entries void.*

"He wrote it," Ada said. "My master. Before the end. He hid a flaw in the binding—one entry left open, one debt unfinished. Mine. My oath to Veyl, sworn when I was nineteen and too angry to read the fine print. As long as I keep the ledger, my oath stands, and the chains stand with it. The moment the ledger burns—" his throat tightened, "—the keeper goes with it. Everything Veyl lent me to survive the years, everything that's kept these wounds closed and this body walking—it gets called due. All at once."

The gray light pulsed. Beneath the ice-skin of the god, something shifted—slow, immense, attentive—and though Veyl made no sound, Ada heard it anyway, the way he had heard one word from a dying courier's mouth ten years ago:

*Ledgers.*

Now the word was different. Now it was his own name.

Sera's hand found his. Her fingers were cold. "There will be another way," she said, and it was not a question, because she refused to let it be one. "There is always another way. We'll find it. We'll—"

Above them, faint through fathoms of stone, a bell began to ring.

Not the cathedral's bells. A harsher sound, metallic and urgent, repeated from tower to tower across the capital—the alarm of a city whose masters had finally learned where their missing keeper was hiding.

Ada looked up at the god in its cage of honest entries, and the god looked back with all its patient eyes, and between them passed the oldest transaction in the world: a debt acknowledged, a price named, a choice deferred no longer.

"They know we're here," he said.
