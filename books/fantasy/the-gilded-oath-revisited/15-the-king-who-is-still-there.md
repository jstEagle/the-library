## 15. The King Who Is Still There

The tunnels beneath the capital smelled of old stone and older secrets, and Sera Vane had been counting her heartbeats to keep from screaming for the better part of an hour.

Not that anyone would hear her. The smuggler passages ran beneath the cathedral district like veins beneath skin, and above them the Crown-Thing's inquisitors walked their rounds with lanterns and iron hooks, hunting two fugitives who had already burned everything they owned. Her church credentials were ash in a brazier three cities back. Her name was on a writ of execution she'd read by candlelight while Ada slept. *Sera Vane, archivist of the Crown's Church, traitor to the throne.* The ink had still been wet.

Traitor. She turned the word over as she followed Ada's lantern through the dark, and found it no longer cut. That was the strangest part—not the fear, not the cold, but how completely the word had stopped meaning anything. She had spent eleven years in the Church's archives, cataloguing prayers the way other women catalogued jewels: by weight, by luster, by what they could be traded for. She had believed, right up until the shrine at Marrow's Ford burned and the god inside it screamed, that the crown served something. That the tithes flowed upward to heaven like water finding its level.

She knew now that they flowed into a mouth.

"Stop." Ada's hand came back against her shoulder, gentle and absolute. His lantern guttered. Ahead, the passage ended at a wall of dressed granite—cathedral foundation stone, mortared with a precision no smuggler could match—and set into it, half-hidden behind a curtain of root and rubble, was a door.

Not a grand door. A servant's door, oak gone silver with age, its iron hinges rusted to lace. Someone had once painted a sigil on it, but plaster had been slapped over the paint, and under the plaster Sera could just make out the shape of what had been covered: a pair of open hands.

The old sigil of Veyl. The god who listened.

"They sealed it when the usurper took the throne," Ada murmured. He was thinner than he'd been on the road, hollowed out by the year he'd sold and the truths he'd bought back, but his hands were steady as he worked a pry-bar into the seam. "My master kept maps of every entrance to the undercroft. He said a keeper should always know where his ledger sleeps."

"And you trust a dead man's maps?"

Ada didn't answer at once. The bar groaned; plaster cracked and sifted down like dirty snow. "I trust that he died keeping them," he said finally. "It's more than I trusted him with, for a long time."

She understood. She was beginning to understand a great many things about Ada Coldharbour—the cowardice he'd confessed on the road, the decade of silence he'd mistaken for abandonment, the shame he carried like a second ledger. Love, she was learning, was mostly this: watching someone carry their worst knowledge and choosing to walk beside them anyway.

The door gave with a sound like a held breath released.

* * *

The undercroft of the Cathedral of the Ascension was supposed to be a crypt. Every archivist learned its layout in their first year: royal tombs, saintly reliquaries, the ossuary corridors where the bones of favored priests were stacked in decorative patterns. What Sera saw instead made her archivist's mind recoil and then, horribly, begin to *catalogue*.

The corridor stretched farther than the cathedral had any right to be deep. Its walls were papered—actually papered—with pages. Thousands of them, tens of thousands, pinned and pasted and layered until the stone disappeared entirely, each page dense with columns of figures in inks of varying age. Some were brown with decades. Some were fresh enough to glisten.

Ledger pages. Torn from bindings, she realized, her professional eye cataloguing even as her stomach turned. Torn from bindings and nailed here like trophies—or like scales.

"Veyl said chains of ledger-paper," Ada whispered. "I thought it was a figure of speech."

"It's never a figure of speech with gods." Sera touched one page and pulled her fingers back as if scalded. Not because it was hot. Because it *hummed*, faintly, the way a struck bell hums after the sound should have ended. She could feel the prayer in it—a farmer's prayer, a widow's prayer, someone somewhere begging a god for rain or mercy or one more season, and the answering power siphoned off mid-stream and recorded here, entered, *balanced*. "These are tithe-records. Live ones. The entries are still—" She searched for the word and hated it. "Still accruing."

Ada had gone very pale. In the lantern light his scars stood out like seams in old leather. "Then we're close."

They were. The corridor bent twice, past alcoves where the paper walls thickened into actual constructed barriers—woven sheets of vellum and record and prayer compressed into something like chain-mail—and then opened without warning into a space so vast the lantern couldn't find its ceiling.

Sera had seen the great vaults of the Church. She had seen the memory-brokers' halls beneath the Gilded Oath, where a year of a man's life hung in glass like a preserved fruit. Nothing had prepared her for this.

In the center of the chamber, suspended from darkness by links of compressed paper thicker than her waist, hung a shape.

It was difficult to look at directly—not because it blazed, but because it was *quiet* in a way that made the eye slide off, the way one can stand in a room and fail to see a silence. It had the suggestion of a figure, tall and gaunt, arms outstretched as if in the last moment of an embrace it had never been permitted to finish. Where a face might have been there was only depth, a patient dark, and around its wrists and throat and heart the paper-chains wound tight, each link bristling with entries, each entry a stolen prayer.

Veyl. The silent god. The god who had said one word in ten years—*ledgers*—and sent a humble keeper of accounts stumbling toward this room.

Sera had never been devout in the way the Church taught devotion. But she felt her knees soften anyway. Beside her, Ada had dropped to one stone floor, head bowed, and she heard him say, very quietly, "I'm here. I'm sorry it took me ten years."

The god did not answer. But the humming in the walls changed pitch—deepened, steadied, became almost a chord—and Sera felt tears start down her face before she understood why. It wasn't grief. It was recognition. Ten years of silence, and underneath it, all along, this: a god listening so hard to stay hidden that listening had become its whole existence.

"There's a door," she managed, when she could speak. "On the far side. Do you see it?"

He did. Of course he did. Beyond the caged god, where the paper walls gave way to living rock, a stair descended—no, ascended, she corrected herself, toward the cathedral proper, toward the throne room above where the thing wearing King Aldric's body received its tithes. And beside that stair, half-sunk into the stone like a tooth in a jaw, was a smaller passage. A cell, by the look of it. A walled-off room with a single barred vent, high and narrow.

From the vent came light. Faint, gold, flickering.

And from the vent came breathing.

* * *

"I don't like it," Ada said. They had retreated to the corridor's bend to argue in whispers, which was, Sera reflected, the most married argument they'd ever had, despite everything. "The god-cage is the mission. We burn the ledger here, the chains break, we're done. Walking into a sealed cell in the usurper's own undercroft is how people die in stories."

"People are already dying in stories, Ada. Whole cities." She kept her voice low but couldn't keep the edge from it. "That light isn't torchlight. I've archived enough relic-glow to know divine residue when I see it. Something in that cell is *burning* with it, and nothing else down here is alive except the god and us."

"The Crown-Thing—"

"Is upstairs, feeding. Inquisitors patrol the upper levels; Kell's maps show nothing between here and the sacristy but solid rock. Whatever's in that cell has been sealed away from its master." She met his eyes. "You told me once that your guild trades in impossible things. Let me make a trade: five minutes. If it's a trap, we run, and I'll never say I told you so."

"You'll say it constantly."

"I'll say it constantly," she agreed, and something in his face loosened—the ghost of the man who had once made her laugh across a archive table, both of them pretending they weren't falling in love among the tithe-records.

They went together. She would not have had it otherwise.

The cell door was iron, unbarred from outside, locked from within. Ada knocked—knocked!—and the breathing on the other side stopped.

For a long moment, nothing. Then a voice, rusty as a gate unused for years, thin as smoke:

"Is it the hour already? It's hard to keep hours, down here. The light doesn't change." A pause. "You're not the steward. The steward doesn't knock."

"Who are you?" Sera called through the iron.

A dry, terrible laugh. "That is the question, isn't it. Eleven years, and I'm still deciding." Then, sharper, suddenly urgent: "Wait. Wait—I know that voice. Archivist. You're the archivist, Vane, third chair, west scriptorium, you reorganized the prayer-index in my second year and fought the whole consistory about the cross-referencing—" The voice cracked. "You used to bring the wrong tea on purpose so I'd correct you. Nobody brings the wrong tea unless they want to talk."

Sera's hand was flat against the iron door before she knew she'd moved. Her heart was slamming. Because she *had* done that—had brought the deliberately mislabeled tea to King Aldric's private archivist sessions, had argued pagination with the young king while the court slept, had—

No. No, that wasn't possible. Aldric had been crowned eleven years ago. The man she'd argued with had been a junior clerk, a colleague, a friend whose face she suddenly could not remember because it had been replaced, overwritten in her memory by the hollow majesty on the coinage, the distant figure on the balcony, the Crown-Thing.

"Aldric?" she whispered.

The lock turned from within. The door swung.

* * *

He was sitting on the floor of a cell barely larger than a tomb, in a king's body gone gray and starved, wearing a priest's rough robe belted with rope. His hair had been allowed to grow; his beard was unkempt; his hands, folded in his lap like a penitent's, trembled with a palsy that had nothing to do with age. He looked, Sera thought, like a man who had been buried alive and had decided, somewhere in the dark years, to make peace with the grave—and then, impossibly, had heard digging.

But his eyes. His eyes were alight. Not with the Crown-Thing's hungry gold but with something banked and stubborn, a coal refusing the ash, and when they fixed on her face she saw the flicker she'd heard about in rumors dismissed as sedition: the king surfacing, briefly, in his own drowned body.

"Sera Vane," he said, wonderingly. "They let you live?"

"They don't know I'm here." She knelt across from him, heedless of the filth, and Ada lowered the lantern between them. Up close the divine residue was stronger—it clung to the king like frost, like the glow of a banked fire—and she understood at last what she was seeing. "Your Majesty. You're... anchored. Here. How?"

"He threw me away and forgot the door." Aldric's laugh was awful and human. "Eleven years ago it came to me in the coronation hour. Wore my face before the oil was dry. It took the body first—the strength, the hunger, the sleep—and then it started taking the rest. Memories go first, did you know? It ate my mother's face in the fourth year. My brother's voice in the sixth. By the ninth I couldn't remember why I'd wanted to be king at all." He looked at his trembling hands. "But I was an archivist's king before I was anything else. I kept records. Private ones. And I hid myself the way you hide anything worth stealing—from yourself. Sealed the true Aldric behind a wall in my own mind, in a room with no windows, and left only enough of me on the surface to keep the body walking where it walked."

"The flickering," Ada said quietly. "The moments the court whispers about. When the king pauses too long, or weeps at nothing, or asks a servant's name."

"The leaks." Aldric smiled, and for a moment he was young. "Every leak costs me a day of the coal burning lower. But I needed to know if anyone was still looking. If anyone would ever come." His gaze moved to Ada, and sharpened. "And here you are. The keeper. I've watched it hunt you through my own eyes, Master Coldharbour. It dreams about your ledgers. Did you know a thing like that can dream?"

"It knows the ledger survives," Ada said. "It sent inquisitors."

"It will send worse." Aldric leaned forward, and the coal in his eyes flared. "Listen to me. Both of you. I have had eleven years in the dark to think, and thinking is the only muscle I have left, so believe me when I tell you I have thought this through." He raised one shaking finger. "First: do not rescue me. I know what you're considering—the god freed, the king restored, the storybook ending. But a restoration means putting me back on that throne, and I have seen what sits on thrones in this country. I was eaten by one."

"Then what do you want?" Sera asked, though her heart already knew, because she had wanted it too, since Marrow's Ford, since the screaming god.

"Justice." The word came out of him like an arrow leaving a bow. "Not vengeance—justice, witnessed, lawful, *public*. I want that thing dragged before the free cities and the survivors of every burned shrine and made to account. I want every tithe it took read aloud into the record—all of them, every prayer it stole, every god it devoured, entered into the ledger of the realm where no crown can ever bury it again. I want the people who prayed those prayers to get back what was taken. Rain for the farmers. Years for the dying. Luck for the fisherman who sold his and drowned anyway." His voice broke and he mastered it, the way kings master things, the way archivists master things. "I was a bad king. I was vain, and I loved the ceremony more than the work, and I let the Church flatter me while it built this place under my feet. But I never consented to *this*. And the difference between a bad king and a monster is whether the realm gets to say so out loud."

The silence after his voice stopped was total. Even the humming walls seemed to lean in.

Sera found she was crying again, silently, and this time she knew exactly why. For eleven years she had served a church that taught her justice was a hierarchy—that some voices counted more than others, that the crown's accounting was the realm's accounting. And here in a cell beneath everything, the crown itself, the true crown, the starved and flickering remnant of a king, was telling her the opposite. That the ledger belonged to the people who appeared in it. That justice was a reading-aloud. That the lowest entry mattered most.

Pity had brought her to her knees. Something harder lifted her up.

"We can give you that," she said. "If the god is freed—if the tithes return to their owners—then the evidence returns with them. Every stolen prayer, remembered by the one it was stolen from. The whole realm becomes a witness."

"Not the whole realm." Aldric's eyes went past her shoulder, to the dark of the corridor, and the coal in them guttered with something like fear. "It will feel the moment the chains break. It will come down here, and it will be strong—eleven years of eaten gods strong. You cannot fight it. You cannot outrun it. So you must do the one thing it has never prepared for." He gripped her wrist, his starving king's fingers surprisingly fierce. "When it comes—don't hide. Don't flee. *Convene.* Bring the army to the doors. Bring the record. Make it stand trial in front of ten thousand witnesses while the god it chained stands over it, and let the sentence be spoken by the realm itself. A thing that eats in the dark cannot survive being *seen* entire. I am the proof. It left me alive because a dead king is a martyr, but a mad king is a cautionary tale. It has always needed the dark. Take the dark away."

Above them, muffled through fathoms of stone, a bell began to ring. Not the cathedral's measured hours—something faster, harsher, an alarm rolling from tower to tower.

Ada was on his feet, lantern raised, face bloodless. "They know we're here."

"No." Aldric shook his head slowly, and terrible understanding dawned across his ruined face. "That's not for you. That cadence—that's the *tithe-bell*. It rings when my—when *it* begins the great feeding." He looked up toward the ceiling, toward the throne room, toward the thing wearing his body, and Sera watched the last warmth drain out of him. "Something has happened upstairs. Something it wants strength for. It's calling in every debt at once."

The bell rang on, and on, and beneath it, from the direction of the god-cage, the humming of the paper walls began—very softly, very terribly—to rise in pitch. Like a scream being drawn breath by breath out of a long silence.

Ada turned to Sera, and in the lantern light she saw the question he couldn't ask, and answered it.

"Now," she said. "We burn it now."
