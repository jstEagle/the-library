## 16. The Price of Unbinding

The god-cage hummed.

That was the first thing Ada had learned in the hours since they'd found it—that a prison built from paper had a sound. Not the rustle of pages, nothing so gentle. It was a vibration in the teeth, in the breastbone, a note too low to hear and too present to ignore, like standing inside a struck bell that never finished ringing. Veyl hung at the center of it, and Ada could not stop looking.

The god was not what he had imagined. Ten years of silence had let him imagine everything: a vast blind thing, a coiled serpent of dark, an absence shaped like a presence. What he saw instead was almost worse in its intimacy. Veyl looked like a man who had been sitting still for a very long time—gaunt, gray-skinned, wrapped in chains that were not iron but vellum, thousands upon thousands of ledger-pages wound tight around limbs and throat and chest, each page dense with columns of figures Ada's eyes refused to focus on. The god's head was bowed. Its eyes were closed. It might have been sleeping, or praying, or simply enduring, and Ada understood with a lurch that he could not tell the difference because he had never once, in ten years of service, tried.

*You are staring,* Veyl said. The voice arrived the way it always had—not in the ears but behind them, in the marrow, patient as frost.

"You're smaller than I thought."

*All gods are.* A pause, long enough that Ada wondered if the connection had frayed. *We are only as large as what is owed to us.*

Sera stood at the edge of the cage's light, her burned church credentials still a smell of char in the air between them, her hands clasped so tightly her knuckles had gone white. She had not spoken since they'd descended past the last of the smuggler tunnels, past the ossuary shelves where the cathedral's founders slept above their god's grave. Now she said, quietly, "Ask it. Ask what we came here to ask."

Ada already knew. He had known since the memory-broker's price came due, since the year he'd paid away returned to him carrying its cargo of shame—his master's face, the Crown-Thing's hunger, his own feet running. He had spent three days on the road telling himself there would be another way, some clause in the old contracts, some loophole in the guild's founding law. Keepers were trained to find loopholes. It was the whole of his trade.

But loopholes were for trades between equals, and this was not a trade. This was arithmetic.

"Veyl." His voice came out steadier than he felt. "What does unbinding cost?"

The god opened its eyes.

They were ledger-colored. That was the only way Ada could describe it—the pale cream of old paper, ruled through with faint lines like columns waiting to be filled. They fixed on him, and Ada felt himself being read, entry by entry, every debt and credit of his life tallied in a glance.

*You know,* Veyl said. Not cruelly. Simply, the way a scale tips.

"Say it anyway. I want it witnessed." He glanced back at Sera. "I want *her* to hear it from you, not from me guessing."

The chains of paper stirred, though there was no wind in the deep place beneath the cathedral. Pages whispered against pages, a sound like a counting house at closing time, like ten thousand quills finishing ten thousand lines.

*The binding was made of tithes,* Veyl said. *Divinity, paid out year by year into the crown's coffers, recorded in your master ledger. Every entry is a link. Every figure is a knot. The ledger is not a record of my imprisonment, keeper. It is the imprisonment. Burn it, and the knots have nothing left to hold.*

"And the cost," Sera said. Her voice cracked on the second word. "You're avoiding the cost."

*There is no cost to burning a book,* Veyl said. *Paper burns cheaply. But the ledger must be burned by its keeper, and the keeper is bound to it as I am bound by it. You have carried it for ten years, Ada Coldharbour. You have slept with it under your head. You have signed your name into its margins more times than you know. When it burns, everything it holds of you burns with it.*

"What does it hold?" Ada asked, though his mouth had gone dry, though some cold animal part of him was already backing toward the tunnel entrance, wanting to run before the answer landed.

*Your protection. My hand has been over you since the day you swore. Every blade that should have found you turned aside. Every fever broke early. Every wound closed clean. You thought yourself lucky, or skilled, or both. You were neither. You were insured.* The god's gaze did not waver. *And you were kept. The borrowed years—I lent them to you when your master died, when you fled, when the wounds you took running should have ended you. There are nine of them folded into your blood. Nine years that were never yours. When the ledger burns, they return to me.*

The silence afterward was enormous. Somewhere far above, muffled through stone, the cathedral bells were ringing the hour, and Ada counted them without meaning to—seven—and thought absurdly that he had seven hours left of whatever this body was, if the Crown-Thing didn't kill him first, which it probably would, which meant the arithmetic was even simpler than he'd told himself.

"Nine years," Sera repeated. Her voice had gone flat, the archivist's voice, the voice she used when a number was too large to feel. "He's walking around with nine borrowed years and he never—" She stopped. Started again. "You never told me. In all the years, you never told me what you were."

"I didn't know." And that was true, and it wasn't, and he watched her see both truths at once. "I knew I healed fast. I knew I'd survived things that should have killed me. I told myself it was Veyl's favor. I never asked what favors cost, because—" He laughed, and it came out ugly. "Because asking questions about accounts is how keepers die. Because I liked not knowing. Gods, Sera, I *liked* it. Ten years of comfortable ignorance, and I called it discipline."

"Don't do that." She crossed the space between them in four strides and took his face in her hands, and her palms were shaking. "Don't you dare turn this into a confession of greed. You want to know what I see? I see a man who is about to be told he's going to die, and he's already doing the sums, and he hasn't once asked if there's another way, because he doesn't believe he deserves one."

"There isn't another way."

"You haven't asked!"

*Sera Vane speaks truly,* Veyl said, and Ada flinched at the intrusion, at the god's attention settling over the two of them like weather. *There may be no other way. But you did not ask. That is a different failure, and a familiar one.*

"What other way could there be?" Ada pulled free of her hands gently, because if he didn't do it gently he wouldn't do it at all. "The ledger is the cage. The cage opens when the ledger burns. The ledger burns by its keeper's hand, and the keeper—" He spread his own hands, scarred, ink-stained, ordinary. "Keeper. That's the whole word, isn't it? I'm part of the binding. I've been part of it since I signed my oath. Burning it isn't sacrifice, it's just... completing the transaction. Closing the account."

"It's dying," Sera said.

"It's *mortality*. There's a difference, and I'm a professional, so trust me on the terminology."

"Don't you joke at me. Don't you *dare*—" Her composure went all at once, the way a dam goes, not leaking but breaking. "Ada. Listen to me. Listen to what you're actually saying. You lose the protection, you lose nine years, and then you walk into a fight against a thing that eats *gods*, wearing a body held together by decade-old wounds and stubbornness, and you're calling that a transaction? I have read enough ledgers to know when someone is cooking the books. You're pricing your life at zero because it makes the column balance."

The words hit somewhere he hadn't armored. He stood very still among the whispering chains and let them land, because she was right, and they both knew she was right, and the worst part was that she'd found the flaw in his accounting faster than any guild auditor ever had.

"The alternative," he said slowly, "is we walk away. We take the ledger north to the free cities, we publish it, we let the council try to negotiate with a thing that devours prayers. And maybe that works. Maybe the Crown-Thing looks at a wall of angry mortals and decides it's full." He shook his head. "It won't. You saw the shrine burn. You heard the god scream. It doesn't negotiate. It *tithes*. It takes its percentage and moves on to the next account. If we leave, it finds us, it takes the ledger, and it locks Veyl's chains tighter with our names added to the entries. There's no version of walking away where anyone survives. There's only versions where I survive slightly longer and everyone else doesn't."

"Then find a version where someone else lights the fire."

"She can't." He said it before Veyl could, and hated how quickly it came. "The keeper's hand or nothing. That's why the guild hid me for ten years without knowing why. That's why Kell wanted the knowledge buried. I'm not a man holding a key, Sera. I *am* the key. Keys don't get to stay in the lock after the door opens."

She stared at him. In the paper-light of the cage her eyes were wet and furious and more alive than anything else in this buried place, and Ada thought: *this is what I'm buying.* Not the free cities, not justice for Aldric, not even Veyl's freedom, though all of those were real. He was buying the right to stand here one more time and be looked at like this. Everything after that was interest he'd never collect.

"How long," she said finally. Her voice had gone quiet again, quieter than the flat archivist tone, down into something raw. "If you burn it tonight. How long do you have?"

Veyl answered, because gods answer questions put to them; it was the oldest term of their existence. *Without my hand over him? The wounds will reopen within days. The oldest ones first. He will have perhaps a week of strength, less if he spends it fighting. Less still if he bleeds.*

"A week," Sera whispered.

*A week,* the god agreed. *Perhaps eight days. Mortality is generous in small increments.*

"That's not a price. That's not a *trade*. Trades go both ways. What does the world give him for it?"

*The world gives him what it gives everyone,* Veyl said. *An ending. Which is more than the Crown-Thing offers, and more than I can offer, and more than he believes he deserves. Do not mistake me, archivist. I have watched this man for ten years through the narrow window of his own soul. I know precisely how little he thinks he is worth. It is the reason I chose him, and it is the reason I am ashamed.*

Ada's breath caught. "You *chose* me?"

*I choose keepers who will not spend themselves cheaply,* Veyl said. *It is a poor criterion. It builds men who cannot recognize their own value on a ledger, because they have never once entered it. Your master had the same flaw. It killed him differently.* The paper-chains tightened, relaxed, tightened—a gesture, Ada realized, that might have been a shrug, or grief, or both. *I owe you a truth, keeper, since you are about to pay me everything. I did not bind myself into these chains to hide. I bound myself because the alternative was letting the Crown-Thing consume me whole and use my power openly. A caged god can be freed. A digested one cannot. Your master understood this. He bought me time with his life, and he bought you time with his death, and he prayed, with his final breath, that you would never learn the price of what he'd done—because he knew you would pay it gladly, and he wanted better for you than that.*

"So this is his fault," Sera said fiercely. "This whole arrangement, this—this *inheritance* of dying, it's a dead man's guilt, and you're both honoring it like scripture—"

"It's mine now," Ada said.

The words came out level, and he was surprised by them, and then he wasn't, because he'd been walking toward them since the courier died in the snow saying *ledgers*, since the first prayer answered in ten years, since every step of a road that had only ever gone one direction no matter how many times he'd checked the map.

"Sera. Look at me." He waited until she did. "I resented Veyl for a decade. Silence, I called it. Abandonment. I did my bookkeeping and I kept my oaths paid and I told myself I served a dead god out of habit. And the whole time, the god was in a cage made of its own worship, and the man who fed it scraps of freedom was me, signing entries, keeping the lock oiled. I'm not paying a debt to Veyl. I'm not honoring my master's guilt. I'm correcting an error in the books. Ten years of tithes stolen from every soul who ever knelt and prayed—they're sitting in that thing's coffers upstairs, wearing a crown. Somebody has to close that account. I'm the only hand that fits the pen."

"And I'm supposed to what?" Her voice broke open. "Stand here? Watch? Help you strike the match and then spend the rest of my life—which, unlike yours, will actually *have* a rest of it—carrying the fact that I stood here and let you?"

"Yes," Ada said softly. "That's exactly what I'm asking. And it's a harder ask than the burning. I know that."

"It's an *awful* ask."

"It's the only honest one. If you asked me to walk away, I'd do it—I'd hate myself for it, but I'd do it, because I have never once in my life won an argument with you. So don't ask. Please. Ask me anything except that."

For a long moment she didn't speak. The cage hummed. Far above, the bells rang the half-hour, and Ada counted again without meaning to, and caught himself doing the arithmetic he'd sworn off—hours remaining, wounds pending, odds of reaching the crown chamber before the Crown-Thing's patience ran out—and made himself stop, made himself stand in the moment instead of the sum of it.

When Sera finally moved, she moved like an archivist. She went to the satchel where the master ledger rode wrapped in oilcloth, and she lifted it out, and she held it against her chest the way you'd hold something wounded, and she said:

"Then we do this properly. If you're going to burn ten years of records and nine years of your own life, you're going to do it with clean books. Every entry read aloud. Every tithe named—for the fisherman who sold his luck, for the mother who borrowed a year for her dying son, for every soul whose prayer got diverted into that thing's treasury. We witness them. All of them. Before one page catches." Her jaw set. "You taught me that debts deserve witnesses, Ada Coldharbour. You don't get to die anonymously in a hole under a cathedral. Not while I'm standing here."

Above them, muffled by fathoms of stone, the cathedral bells began to ring again—but wrong, arrhythmic, a peal gone jagged, and beneath it came a sound like the building itself groaning in its sleep. Dust sifted from the vaulted ceiling. The paper-chains shivered.

*It knows,* Veyl said, and for the first time in ten years of silence, the god sounded afraid. *It has felt the ledger come home. Keeper—we are out of time. The reading will have to be short.*

Ada took the ledger from Sera's arms. Their fingers overlapped on the cover, hers refusing to let go until the last possible moment.

"No," he said. "It'll be long enough. Every name gets its line."

The ceiling cracked.
