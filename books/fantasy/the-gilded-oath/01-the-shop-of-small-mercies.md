## 1. The Shop of Small Mercies

The last customer left at sundown, and Sable Pemberton counted himself lucky she'd only wanted a luck-knot and not a conversation.

He turned the sign on the door — CLOSED, in letters he'd repainted so many times the C had gone soft as a thumbprint — and began the ritual. Shutters first, then the lamp behind the counter dimmed to its legal ember, then the ledger squared, the coin drawer counted twice because inspectors loved nothing more than a miscounted drawer to justify a search. The Shop of Small Mercies was eleven feet wide and smelled of dried lavender and candle smoke, and it held exactly nothing worth arresting anyone for.

That was the point.

In the window he arranged tomorrow's wares with a shopkeeper's care: knotted red thread against headaches, iron nails wrapped in wool for bad dreams, little glass beads he swore — honestly, mostly honestly — had been kissed by river-light. Harmless charms. Legal charms. The kind of magic that wasn't magic at all anymore, just superstition with good packaging, which was the only kind Ashfall permitted. A generation ago they'd passed the Edict of Ash, and the free city that had once blazed with wonders decided, all together, like a man blowing out candles before bed, that it would rather be safe than bright.

Sable had been nineteen when the Edict came down. He was thirty-six now, and he could not remember the last time he'd done anything that would have gotten him arrested.

He could remember, though. That was the cruelty of it. His hands remembered.

* * *

The knock came at half past nine, when the streetlamps were already lit and no honest shop should have been open.

Sable went still behind the counter, one hand flat on the wood, listening. Boots on cobbles outside — two pairs, unhurried, the particular rhythm of men who were paid by the hour and intended to work every minute of it. Magic inspectors. They'd been walking the Lantern District since dusk, their brass badges catching lamplight, checking ward-seals on doorframes the way tax men checked seals on crates.

The knock came again. Not on his door. On the door of the chandler next door, and then muffled voices, and Sable let out a breath he'd been holding for what felt like a year.

But his heart kept its quick rhythm, because there was one thing in this room worth finding, and it was not in the window or the drawer.

He knelt. The floorboard under the counter came up with a practiced twist — he'd oiled the joint until it whispered — and from the dark beneath he lifted the book.

It was small, smaller than a hymnal, bound in leather gone the color of old tea, and it weighed far more than paper had any right to weigh. No title. No clasp. Just a sigil pressed into the cover: a lantern with a hollow center, the mark of an order that had once been ten thousand strong and was now three old fools and a charm-seller who'd sworn off everything real.

The Hollow Lantern's Book of Hours. Prophecies, if you believed the old members, written across centuries by hands that had never met but shared the same ink. The Guild had surrendered its grimoires, its staffs, its seals, everything with power in it, when the Edict came. Everything except this. Grandmother Wren — ninety years old and built out of spite and prayer — had pressed it into Sable's hands seven years ago and said, *You're the only one of us nobody suspects. Isn't that a fine thing to be?*

He hadn't decided yet whether it was a compliment.

Boots stopped outside his own door now. Sable lowered the book into its nest beneath the floorboard, laid the board back, and set a potted fern over the seam with the ease of long practice. Then he stood, smoothed his apron, and opened the door before they could knock, because a man who opens his own door has nothing to hide.

"Evening," said the taller inspector. He had a face like a filed-down nail. "Late hours, merchant."

"Counting stock." Sable leaned on the doorframe, letting tiredness sit on him where they could see it. Tiredness was armor. Nobody searched a tired man. "You're welcome to look, if you've a warrant with my name spelled right. It's Pemberton. Two B's. Inspectors always get it wrong."

The shorter one almost smiled. Almost. They glanced past him into the little shop, at the knots and nails and beads, at the fern, at nothing. Filed-Down-Nail sniffed once — some inspectors carried scent-charms, illegal ones, irony being dead even in Ashfall — and found only lavender.

"Lantern District's quiet tonight," the tall one said.

"It usually is."

"That'll change." He said it the way men say weather predictions, without opinion, and the two of them moved on down the street, boots marking time.

Sable closed the door and stood with his forehead against the wood for a moment. *That'll change.* Inspectors didn't say things like that for no reason. Somewhere above his head, in the offices of the Wardens' Hall, someone had decided the Lantern District needed watching, and Sable Pemberton, harmless seller of headache-knots, felt the cold finger of that attention slide along his spine and keep going.

He waited until their footsteps faded. Then he knelt, lifted the board, and took the book out again, because he was apparently a fool, and carried it to the counter, and opened it by feel to the pages near the end, where the ink grew fresher, the handwriting younger, the prophecies closer.

He'd read them a hundred times. Wars, mostly. A crown worn by something that wasn't a king. Cities burning like tallow. And always, threaded through the fire, two names repeated like a refrain:

*the keeper of small mercies,* and *the man out of his century.*

He'd assumed, for seven years, that the keeper was metaphorical. Books liked metaphors. Metaphors couldn't be hanged.

* * *

The light started bleeding through his floor at a quarter to midnight.

Not lamplight. Lamplight sits politely in pools. This rose up between the boards like water through a cracked dam, gold-white and moving, and it made no sound at all, which was somehow worse than thunder. Sable stood in the middle of his shop with the prophecy book open in his hands and watched impossible light pour upward through eleven feet of legally boring floorboards, and his first coherent thought was: *the fern.*

His second thought was that he should run. His third was that his legs had resigned without consulting him.

The boards didn't break. They simply stopped being an obstacle, the way rules stop mattering in dreams, and a hand came through first — a long-fingered, ink-stained hand, gripping empty air — and then a shoulder, and then a man, folding out of the floor of the Shop of Small Mercies like a letter unfolding, and landing among Sable's innocent wares in a sprawl of limbs and cloth.

The light went with him. It poured off him, off his coat, off the blood at his temple, actual blood, except where it touched the air it glowed instead of dripping. He was bleeding light. There was no other way to say it. Every wound on him wept a slow golden radiance, and the shadows in the shop leaned away from him as if he were too loud.

Sable looked at the stranger. At the clothes — a longcoat cut in a style Sable had seen only in engravings, high-collared, buttoned to the throat, dusted with plaster dust that smelled of another century. At the satchel strapped across his chest, stamped with a cartographer's compass rose. At the young-old face, gray with pain, eyes the color of wet slate finding Sable's and fixing there with desperate, drowning focus.

And Sable looked at the book in his hands, open to the page he'd read a hundred times, to the verse he'd never quite believed:

*On the night the lamps burn low,*
*he falls through the floor of the world*
*into the shop of small mercies,*
*bleeding light, wearing yesterday's coat,*
*and the keeper will know him*
*by the weight of the hour.*

The stranger tried to speak. What came out was a language Sable didn't know, old vowels, courtly shapes, and then he stopped, closed his eyes, and tried again, and this time the words came out rough and modern and human:

"Please. Is this — what year is this?"

Sable's mouth was dry. Outside, distantly, the inspectors' boots had stopped. Someone, somewhere, had felt whatever had just torn through the world and landed on his sales floor.

"Ashfall counts differently now," he managed. "But if you're asking what I think you're asking — you're a hundred years late."

The stranger laughed, a broken sound, and light spilled from the corner of his mouth when he did. "Then I'm right on time," he said. "I was told — I was *told* there'd be someone. A keeper." His slate-colored eyes dropped to the book in Sable's hands, and something in his face went white beyond the pain. "That's mine. I mean — I drew that map. On the endpaper. In 1823."

Sable looked down. The endpaper, which he had studied for seven years and never understood, showed a coastline and a compass rose and a signature he'd always taken for decoration.

*Corin Vale, Royal Cartographer, by appointment to the King.*

The floor seemed very far away and very close at once. Seven years of careful, lawful, forgettable life — the knots, the nails, the fern, the two-B spelling of his name — all of it tilted, groaned, and slid toward the stranger bleeding gold on his floor.

"You'd better tell me everything," Sable heard himself say, "before whoever felt that arrives."

And from somewhere down the Lantern District, faint but closing, came the sound of running boots.
