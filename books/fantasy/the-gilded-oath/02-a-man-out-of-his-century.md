## 2. A Man Out of His Century

The stranger bled light onto Sable's floorboards, and the first thing he said was an apology.

"Forgive me," the man managed, one hand pressed to the doorframe as if the room might be moving. "I was — there was a corridor. The palace has a corridor that shouldn't exist, and I —" He looked up, and his eyes were the exact gray of the ink Sable had been mixing an hour ago. "Where is this?"

"Ashfall," Sable said. "Free city. You're in my shop."

The man's gaze traveled the shelves: luck-knots, salt-charms, little glass lanterns filled with nothing at all. Harmless things. Legal things. His expression curdled with something like grief.

"These are toys," he said.

"They're inventory," Sable said. "And you're bleeding on it."

The blood wasn't red. It caught the lamplight and threw it back gold, thin threads of it running down the man's wrist and pooling at his boot heel like spilled honey. Sable had seen a wound glow once, seven years and one lifetime ago, when magic went wrong in a way that ended careers and lives. He knew what glowing blood meant. He knew what it meant for him specifically, standing in a shop full of inspectors' footprints on the street outside.

"You need to leave," Sable said.

"I don't think I can." The man laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "I don't think this century can either. My name is Corin Vale. I am the royal cartographer to King Aldous Ren, and as of — " he checked a pocket watch that was still ticking, stubbornly, faithfully — "as of four minutes ago, I have no idea what year it is."

Sable's mouth went dry. "Say that again."

"Four minutes ago—"

"Not that part."

Corin Vale looked at him. Outside, boots rang on cobbles; the night patrol, making its rounds past the shuttered shops of Lantern Row. Sable watched the stranger do the arithmetic every child in Ashfall learned before they learned letters: glowing blood plus a king's name plus a man who didn't know the year equaled gallows, gallows, gallows.

"The year," Corin said carefully, "is 1823. Where I was standing when the corridor took me."

"It's not," Sable said. "It's 1923."

* * *

Corin sat down on the floor. Not a chair — the floor, back against the counter, knees up, watch cradled in both hands like something that could anchor him. Sable should have thrown him out. Every survival instinct he'd sharpened over seven years of selling nothing told him to throw him out, douse the lamp, and never think about tonight again.

Instead he locked the front door and drew the shade.

"One hundred years," Corin said. He said it twice more, quieter each time, testing whether the words would hold weight. "One hundred years. Is the king — is Aldous —"

"I don't know anything about kings. Ashfall governs itself. Magic's been outlawed since before I was born." Sable crouched, keeping his distance from the golden blood. "Who cast the spell on you?"

"No one. That's what I keep returning to." Corin's hands were steady now, which impressed Sable more than the time travel did. A cartographer's hands, ink-stained at the cuticles even after a century. "I was mapping the under-corridors of the palace. Old work, older than the palace itself, walls that predate the foundation stones. There was a chamber the surveys always refused to record — ink wouldn't take, graphite smeared, my assistants dreamed about it for weeks afterward. Last night I went in alone. There were words carved into the floor. Old script. And a circle, and —" He flexed his fingers. "Someone lit it from the other end. Someone *pulled*. I felt it like a hook behind my ribs. Whoever cast that spell knew exactly who they were catching."

Sable stood very still.

He knew where he kept the book. He'd known for eleven years, since Master Ellery pressed it into his hands the night before she died and said, *You'll know when.* He'd kept it beneath the loose floorboard under the counter through three inspections, two raids, and one very bad winter, and he had never once opened it, because opening it felt like answering a question he wasn't ready to hear.

"Stay here," he said. "Touch nothing. If anyone knocks, you're a drunk and I'm your cousin."

"Sable Pemberton," Corin said.

Sable froze with his hand on the floorboard. "What did you say?"

"That's your name. On the ledger in the window. Sable Pemberton, Charms, Licensed." Corin almost smiled. "Did you think I'd traveled a hundred years without learning to read?"

* * *

The book came up out of the dark smelling of cold earth and candle smoke, exactly as it always did, as if it had spent its century underground rather than eleven years. Sable set it on the counter between them and did not touch it further.

"What is it?" Corin asked.

"The Guild's last relic. The Hollow Lantern kept it when everything else burned. Prophecies." Sable heard his own voice go flat, professional, the voice he used with customers. "Mostly nonsense. Mostly very old nonsense."

"And you've never read it."

"I've never needed to."

Corin reached out, then stopped, his hand hovering over the cover. "May I?"

Everything in Sable wanted to say no. Instead he nodded once, and Corin opened the book with the reverence of a man handling a surveyor's original plates.

The pages were dense, handwritten in an iron-brown ink, margins crowded with annotations in at least four different hands spanning what looked like centuries. Corin turned pages slowly. Sable watched him move from curiosity to focus to stillness, and understood, with a lurch, that they had arrived somewhere together.

"Here," Corin whispered.

Sable leaned over his shoulder. The heading was plain, unadorned, in the oldest hand:

*Of the Man Drawn Through the Dark, and the Keeper of Small Mercies.*

Below it, the verse:

*When the lantern burns hollow and the law sleeps sound,*
*a man out of his century shall bleed gold on common ground.*
*The keeper of small mercies, who buried both his name and art,*
*shall open door and floorboard both, and open, too, his heart.*

"There's more," Corin said. His voice had gone strange. He turned the page.

*A mapmaker's eye shall see the crown for what it wears,*
*a gilded face above a throat of smoke.*
*The keeper knows the cost of oaths; the keeper pays it twice.*
*What the prophecy binds, let no man break —*
*and what it binds shall burn.*

Sable read it three times. Then a fourth, because the third time his eyes had slid off the last line like water off glass.

"Keeper of small mercies," he said. "That's — the shop. The Shop of Small Mercies. I named it that because —" Because it was a joke, a private joke, the kind of joke a man makes when he's selling trinkets to people who deserve real miracles. "Nobody knows that. I painted the sign myself. Two years ago."

Corin was looking at him with an expression Sable couldn't parse — wonder, yes, but underneath it something frightened.

"Read the date," Corin said.

There was a date. In the margin beside the verse, in a smaller, newer hand, someone had written it out plainly, the way a clerk records a delivery: *the ninth of Frostmonth, 1923.*

Tonight.

And beneath the date, underlined once, in ink that had dried a hundred years ago:

*S.P.*

Sable stepped back from the counter. His hip hit a shelf; a luck-knot fell and rolled across the floorboards, coming to rest against Corin's golden-smeared boot.

"No," Sable said. "No. This is a trick. This is someone's idea of a joke, or a test, or — the inspectors plant things, they do that, they —"

"Do they plant books?" Corin asked gently. "A century old? With your name in it?"

"Somebody wrote this!" Sable's voice came out louder than he meant, and he strangled it down to a whisper. "Somebody sat down with ink and decided how this goes. Decided that I — that we —" He stopped. The verse sat there, patient as a trap. *Shall open, too, his heart.* "I don't answer to a book."

"No," Corin agreed. He closed it, softly, and laid his palm flat on the cover. "But I think it answers to us. Or to something using us." He looked up, and in the lamplight his gray eyes were steady in a way that made Sable's chest ache with a feeling he refused to name. "I was pulled through a hundred years by a hook behind my ribs, Mr. Pemberton. Somebody spent enormous power to place me precisely here, precisely now, bleeding on your floor. Whatever wrote that page knew it would happen. Which means whoever cast the spell read this book. Which means —"

"Which means the spell-caster believes it," Sable finished. "And belief is fuel."

They looked at each other across the counter, across the book, across a hundred years and one impossible night. Outside, the patrol passed again, boots fading toward the harbor. Somewhere in the dark of the city, Sable thought, someone had lit a circle a century ago and was perhaps still holding the other end of the line, waiting to learn if their hook had caught.

"If I help you," Sable said slowly, "it's not because of the verses. Understand that. I'm not walking into some script because a dead man underlined my initials."

"Then why?"

Sable looked at the golden blood drying on his floor, at the watch still ticking faithfully in Corin's pocket, at the only living proof that the world was larger and crueler and stranger than the small legal life he'd built inside its smallest room.

"Because somebody yanked you a hundred years to be a weapon," he said, "and I've been a weapon before. Nobody should be one twice."

Corin held out his hand across the counter. Ink-stained, gold-flecked, a cartographer's hand that had drawn coastlines that no longer existed.

"Then show me the city," Corin said. "My century's gone. Yours might be next."

Sable took the hand — and the moment their skin touched, the prophecy book, closed and silent on the counter between them, began to glow through its covers like a lantern relighting.

And beneath the floorboards where the book had lain, faint but unmistakable, came the sound of knocking.

Three slow raps. From underneath.

From the Hollow Lantern's old signal — the one that meant: *they know. Run.*
