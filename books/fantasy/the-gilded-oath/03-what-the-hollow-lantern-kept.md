## 3. What the Hollow Lantern Kept

The Guild hall stood at the end of Candlemaker's Row, behind a door that hadn't opened for anyone in seven years.

Sable had walked past it every day since his exile, and every day he'd told himself the same lie: *I don't miss it.* The building had once been the pride of Ashfall — a lantern of white stone at the heart of the city, its spire lit with a flame that never went out, its halls full of mages who could read a man's grief in the air and mend it. Now it was just another ruin the inspectors didn't bother with, because the Hollow Lantern Guild was officially extinct, and extinct things made poor arrests.

He stopped in front of the door with Corin Vale hovering at his shoulder, still wearing that impossible coat, still leaking faint gold light from the wound in his side like a cracked lantern.

"This is a bad idea," Sable said. "I want you to know I'm aware of that."

"You've said so twice."

"Because it keeps being true." He pressed his palm flat against the wood. "The people inside are going to have questions. About you. About me. Mostly about me, honestly. Try not to bleed on anything old."

"I'll do my best." Corin's voice had steadied since the shop, but there was a rawness under it, the sound of a man holding himself together by manners alone. "Who are they? Your family?"

Sable laughed, and it came out wrong. "No. Worse. Colleagues."

He knocked — three slow, two fast, one slow, the rhythm drilled into him before he could write his own name — and waited. Somewhere beyond the door, something heavy scraped across stone. A bolt turned. The door swung inward on darkness and the smell of dust and tallow, and a woman's voice said:

"If this is about the rent again, Master Quill can take it up with the ghost of the landlord—"

Maren Thistle stopped mid-sentence. She was sixty now, gray-streaked hair tied back with what looked suspiciously like a curtain cord, and she was holding a candlestick like a weapon. Her eyes went from Sable to Corin to the light seeping through Corin's coat, and her face did something complicated.

"Well," she said. "Seven years, and he brings us a bleeding relic."

"Maren—"

"Don't 'Maren' me. Get him inside before someone sees. Do you have any idea what the patrols would pay for whatever *that* is?" She stepped back, jerking her head. "In. Both of you. And Pemberton — you're explaining everything, and I mean *everything*, before that wound gets tended. I want the whole ridiculous story."

* * *

The great hall had been the most beautiful room in Ashfall once. Sable remembered it from childhood visits: vaulted ceiling painted with constellations, a hundred lanterns burning in niches along the walls, each one dedicated to a mage the Guild had lost. His mother had brought him here when he was six, and he'd cried because the lanterns were so bright he thought the stars had come indoors.

Now there were three lanterns lit. Three. The rest were cold glass and dead wicks, and the constellation ceiling was water-stained into shapes no astronomer would recognize. The long table where forty mages had once argued philosophy over supper held a teapot, a stack of unpaid bills, and a cat who regarded their entrance with the deep offense of the elderly.

"Careful of the floorboards," said a voice from the shadows near the hearth. Old Quill emerged, ancient and bent, wrapped in three sweaters despite the season. He squinted at Corin for a long moment. Then he smiled, and it was the saddest smile Sable had ever seen. "Ah. So it's tonight after all."

Sable's stomach dropped. "You *knew*?"

"We knew the book said tonight," Maren said, setting down her candlestick. "We didn't know if we believed it anymore. Sit down, boy. You look terrible."

"He looks terrible," Sable said, pointing at Corin. "*I* look tired."

"You look like a man who hasn't slept in seven years," said the third voice, dry as old paper, and Vesper Lune unfolded herself from the chair by the cold hearth. She was the youngest of them — fifty, maybe, though magic had always been unkind to her face — and she was already crossing the room toward Corin with her hands raised, reading the light around his wound the way other people read weather. "Century-jump. Crude spellwork. Whoever cast this wasn't trying to be gentle; they were trying to be *fast*. Sit. Before you fall down."

Corin sat. The cat relocated to his lap with the entitlement of royalty, and Corin — cartographer of a dead century, man out of time — accepted this with the bewildered grace of someone who had decided the world had gone mad and resistance was pointless.

Vesper's hands hovered over the wound. "This will sting."

"It has stung," Corin said politely, "since 1823."

She laughed — one short, surprised note — and began to work. Gold light bled upward through her fingers instead of out of him, drawn back into her palms like thread onto a spool. Sable watched, and something in his chest twisted. Seven years since he'd seen real healing done. Seven years since anyone in this room had dared.

"The book," Maren said, arms crossed. "Show him, Quill."

Old Quill shuffled to the far wall, where a portrait of the Guild's founder hung crooked above a panel that looked like ordinary wood. He pressed his knuckles against it — three times, twice, once — and the panel swung open on a cavity lined with velvet gone gray with age.

Inside lay the book.

Sable had seen it exactly once before, the night of his exile, when the Guild had shown him what he'd done and what it cost. It was small — smaller than any holy text had a right to be — bound in leather the color of dried blood, with a lantern stamped in gold on the cover. The Gilded Oath. The oldest relic the Hollow Lantern possessed. Older than Ashfall itself, older than the free cities, older than the law that had outlawed everything the Guild stood for.

Quill carried it to the table like a sleeping child and set it down between them all.

"Open it," Maren said quietly. "To tonight."

Sable's hands weren't steady. He noticed this distantly, the way you notice rain starting. He opened the book.

The pages were vellum, soft as cloth, covered in an ink that seemed to move if you didn't look directly at it. The script was old High Lantern, the formal hand used for oaths and bindings, and Sable had learned enough of it during his training to translate slowly, painfully, word by word.

But he didn't need to be slow tonight. Because the page fell open on its own, the way a book falls open to the passage you've read a hundred times, and the heading was written in letters that seemed freshly inked:

*The Ninth of Emberwane.*

Tonight's date.

And beneath it, in that same living ink:

*A man crosses the centuries, torn from his own time by a dying king's desperate craft. He arrives bleeding light in a shop of small mercies. The keeper of small mercies receives him.*

Sable read his own name next. Not *the keeper*. His name. *Sable Pemberton*, written in a book sealed before his grandmother's grandmother was born.

"There's more," Vesper said softly, not looking up from her work. "Keep reading."

He kept reading. The words came slower now, because each one weighed more than the last.

*The man out of his century carries the key in his blood: he mapped the true king's roads, he drew the king's own hand, and the usurper who wears the king's face will know him on sight. Unless the true king is unbound from the thing that stole his crown, the usurper will march on the free cities within the year, and the free cities will burn as Ashfall burned before.*

*The keeper of small mercies must relearn what he burned away. The keeper must unbind the king. And the gilded oath demands its price: the man out of his century does not leave this page alive.*

The hall went very quiet. Even the cat seemed to hold its breath.

"No," Sable said. It came out flat, automatic, the way you say *no* to a bill you can't pay. "No, that's — prophecies are interpretations. That's the first thing they teach you. The words are true but the meaning shifts, that's the whole *point* of—"

"Sable." Maren's voice was gentle, which was worse than sharp. "Sit down."

He sat. Or rather, his legs stopped pretending they wouldn't.

"How much of it is accurate?" Corin asked. He sounded calm. Sable hated how calm he sounded. "The date. My arrival. The shop."

"All of it," Quill said. "It has never been wrong. In four hundred years, the Oath has never been wrong. That is why we hid it, and why we stopped reading it, and why half the Guild wanted to burn it and the other half couldn't lift the flame to do it." He lowered himself into a chair with a creak of old bones. "A prophecy that never errs is not a blessing, boy. It is a cage with very beautiful bars."

"Then tell me about the king," Corin said. "My king. Aldric. I drew his coronation map. I dined at his right hand twice. He was — he was *good*. Dull at cards, generous with the kitchen staff, terrified of his own father's shadow. What happened to him?"

Nobody answered immediately. Vesper finished her work and stepped back, and the gold light around Corin's wound dimmed to nothing, leaving only an ordinary bruise. She looked at Sable, then at Maren, then at Quill, some silent negotiation passing between the three of them.

Finally Maren spoke.

"Eight years ago, King Aldric fell ill. Fever, they said. The court physicians despaired. And then, overnight — recovery. Miraculous. The king rose from his bed hale and whole, and everyone rejoiced." She pulled out a chair and sat across from Corin, folding her hands. "Except the king who rose wasn't quite the king who lay down. He was *gilded*, Corin. Polished. Perfect. Too perfect. He ended a war in a fortnight that had dragged for a decade. He balanced budgets that had defied three treasurers. He charmed ambassadors who'd spent careers hating the crown." Her mouth twisted. "And he outlawed magic in every territory he touched. Every charter, every guild, every hedge-witch and charm-seller and lantern-keeper. Snuffed. All of it."

"That was the king's edict?" Corin asked. "Aldric banned magic?"

"Aldric loved magic," Quill said simply. "His mother was one of ours. Why do you think your Guild survived at all, boy? Why do you think we're sitting in this hall instead of a cell? The old king protected us. The new king—" he tapped the prophecy book with one gnarled finger, "—hunts us. Something wore his body home from that sickbed, and it has been wearing him ever since. We call it the Wearing Crown. And it is patient, and it is brilliant, and it is *hungry*."

"And the spell that pulled me here—"

"A dying king's desperate craft," Sable quoted, before he could stop himself. His voice sounded strange in his own ears. "That's what the book says. Corin — I think part of Aldric is still in there. Trapped. And I think he's the one who reached across a hundred years to grab the one man who knew his face well enough to know it had been stolen."

Corin stared at the table. When he looked up, his eyes were wet, and he didn't bother hiding it. "So he's alive. In there. Behind that thing's smile."

"Maybe," Vesper said. "A splinter. A memory. Enough to scream, perhaps. Not enough to act."

"Then we get him out." Corin said it the way he might have announced the weather, and all three elders stared at him. "You have a book that says it must be done. You have the man it says must do it." He nodded at Sable. "And you have me. So we do it."

"You don't understand what you're volunteering for," Maren said sharply. "The unbinding rite hasn't been performed in two hundred years. It requires power that doesn't exist anymore. It requires—"

"It requires me," Sable said.

The room turned to him.

"The keeper must relearn what he burned away," he recited, and laughed, and it was an awful sound. "You know what that means. You've all known since I walked in. It means I have to go back to the night I killed Master Ellery. It means I have to open up everything I cauterized shut and pour power through the scar tissue. That's what the book is asking. That's the price on *my* page, before we even get to his." He nodded at Corin, at the last line none of them had said aloud yet. *Does not leave this page alive.*

Silence. The cat purred obliviously in Corin's lap.

"Seven years ago," Sable said, quieter now, "this hall held thirty-one mages. I know. I counted them at my last supper here, before the trial. Thirty-one lanterns lit, and every one of them sworn to the same oath: *we keep the light, whatever it costs.* And then the inspections came, and the arrests, and the trials, and one by one the lanterns went dark — not because the mages died, but because they *chose* to put themselves out. Renounced their gifts. Signed the abjuration. Went home to be farmers and clerks and nobody, because a live coward survives longer than a dead hero." He looked around the hall, at the three lanterns burning in their niches, at the ninety-seven empty ones. "Three of you kept the flame. Three. Out of thirty-one. And I wasn't even brave enough to do that — I broke my oath so badly they exiled me from the cowards."

"Sable—" Vesper started.

"No, let me finish, because I've had seven years to rehearse this and I'm only saying it once." His voice cracked and he let it. "I sold charms. Harmless ones. Love-luck and traveler's-blessing and nothing that would ever make an inspector blink, because I swore — I *swore* — never again. And now a book older than my country says the free cities burn unless I become the thing I promised I'd never be, and the man it dragged here to make me do it is sitting in front of me looking at me like I'm somebody worth following." He dragged a hand down his face. "And the worst part — the genuinely pathetic part — is that some ruined little corner of me is *glad*. Glad the book exists. Glad it needs me. Because for seven years I've been waiting for someone to tell me the exile wasn't the whole story."

The hall was silent. Outside, faintly, boots on cobblestone — a patrol passing, unhurried, certain of its city.

Maren reached across the table and closed the prophecy book with a soft, final sound.

"Here's what happens now," she said. "Quill starts teaching you the old forms tomorrow — the ones you learned before, and the ones they don't teach anymore. Vesper will ward this place until it screams. And I am going to write three letters to three people who signed the abjuration and hate us for it, asking them to remember what they were." She looked at Corin, then at Sable, and her expression was grim as a headstone. "Because if the book is right — and the book is always right — then the Wearing Crown felt your friend arrive the moment he crossed over. Which means its agents are already looking. And they won't stop at questioning."

As if the words had summoned it, a knock sounded at the outer door. Three slow, two fast, one slow.

The Guild knock.

Everyone froze. Maren's eyes met Sable's, and in them he saw the same thought arriving at the same moment:

Only four people alive knew that rhythm.

And all four of them were already in the room.
