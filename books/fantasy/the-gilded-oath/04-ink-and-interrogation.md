## 4. Ink and Interrogation

They came for me on the third night, and I will say this much for them: they were polite about it.

I had been sleeping in the back room of the charm shop, on a pallet that smelled of dried lavender and old smoke, in a century that was not mine. Sable had gone out to buy bread. I remember thinking, as I drifted off, how strange it was that a man could be kidnapped from one life into another and still be felled by something as ordinary as exhaustion — that my body did not care about prophecies or usurpers, only that it had walked too far and slept too little and eaten food fried in oil I didn't recognize.

The door didn't open so much as *unhappen*. One moment there was wood and lock; the next, three figures stood in the room as though the room had always contained them.

The one in front wore a gray coat cut in a style I almost recognized — thirty years ahead of mine, perhaps, tailored by someone with more money than mercy. His face was pleasant. That was the worst part. He looked like a man who might ask you for directions.

"Corin Vale," he said. "Royal cartographer to King Aldous the Second." He said it the way you'd read an epitaph. "You've been *missed*."

"I've been dead a hundred years," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt. "Whoever missed me has poor records."

"Everyone's records are poor," he said, "compared to ours." He glanced around the little room — the lavender, the shelves of harmless charms, the floorboard Sable had shown me where the book slept. His eyes lingered there a half-second too long, and I understood, with a cold drop through my stomach, that he knew exactly what was under this floor.

"You're going to come with us," he said. "You're going to tell us what the Hollow Lantern showed you. And then—" he smiled, and the smile was like a coin: bright, flat, stamped with someone else's face— "we're going to decide whether you're worth keeping."

I thought about running. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger they keep of me: I *thought* about it. But the two behind him had not blinked once since they arrived, and their stillness had a quality I recognized from maps — the quality of borders, of lines drawn by men with armies.

So I went.

* * *

They took me to a house that looked abandoned from the street and was anything but within. Inside, every surface gleamed. Gilding ran along the walls like veins — actual gold leaf, applied in patterns that hurt to look at directly, because the patterns *moved*, slow as honey, rearranging themselves when I wasn't watching.

"Ink," the pleasant man said, gesturing at the walls with something like affection. "Do you know what ink is, cartographer? It's memory made portable. It's a promise that outlives the mouth that made it." He sat across from me in a chair that hadn't been there a moment before. "Our master is very fond of promises."

They asked questions for hours. Where was the book. What did the prophecy say about the unbinding. Did the Guild know the words of the rite. Had Sable Pemberton touched real magic since the accident.

That last one they asked twice, and both times the gilded veins in the walls pulsed faintly, like a heartbeat quickening.

I told them nothing, which was easy, because I knew nothing — I had been in this century four days. But they didn't believe ignorance. Ignorance was, apparently, a currency they'd stopped accepting.

Somewhere past midnight — I judged by the ache in my shoulders, tied to a chair that held me more firmly than rope — the pleasant man leaned close and said, quietly, conversationally:

"Do you know what happens to a man pulled out of his own time? He becomes *unwritten*, Corin. No debts. No grave. No record anywhere that he ever lived." He tapped my knee, twice, gently. "A man like that can be rewritten. Do you understand? We could make you anyone. We could make you *ours*."

And here is the thing I have never told anyone, the thing I am setting down now because the ink of this page is the only witness I trust:

For one moment — one long, gold-lit moment — I wanted it.

Not to betray Sable. Not to serve the thing wearing the king's face. Just to *stop*. To stop being the man who fell asleep in 1823 over a map of a coastline that no longer existed, and woke up drowning in a century that hated everything he was. To stop being a ghost haunting his own future. The offer wasn't power. The offer was *rest*.

Then I thought of Sable's face when he'd opened the prophecy book and read his own name in a dead man's handwriting. The way his jaw had set — not fear, exactly. Something more stubborn than fear. A man deciding, right in front of me, that he would not be written by anyone.

I did not want rest badly enough to become a sentence in someone else's book.

"No," I said.

The pleasant man sighed, genuinely regretful, and nodded to the others, and the gilding on the walls began to move faster.

* * *

Here is what I know of what happened next, reconstructed later from what Sable told me, because from inside the chair I saw none of it — only heard, distantly, the front of the house come apart.

Sable would tell it plainly, the way he tells everything, hands jammed in his pockets, eyes anywhere but mine: that he'd come home to an empty room and a door that had been *unhappened*; that he'd known instantly, the way you know a theft in your own home; that he'd gone to the Guild hall and stood in front of Maerlin and Odessa and the third one whose name changes depending on who tells it, and said, "Give me the tracking rite."

And Maerlin had said what any of them would say: *No. It's forbidden. It's the kind of magic that gets guilds burned.*

And Sable — I love this part, I confess I love it, though I pretend I don't when he's in the room — Sable had said, "Then burn me after."

Seven years he'd kept his hands empty. Seven years since the accident, since the fire that took his mentor and took everything else, since he'd sworn an oath with ash still in his mouth that he would never again reach for the deep magic, the true magic, the kind that costs. And now he stood in a crumbling hall and reached for it anyway, because a stranger from a dead century was tied to a chair in a gilded house, and some equations are simple even when everything else is impossible.

The tracking rite requires blood and a name spoken backward. Sable had my name from the prophecy book — *Vale* — and he had my blood from the first night, when I stumbled into his shop bleeding light and he'd pressed a cloth to my palm and, being a careful man, had kept the cloth. Being a careful man. Being a hoarder of small mercies. He'd kept it without knowing why, and the prophecy, I suspect, had known why all along.

He drew the circle in chalk on the Guild hall floor with hands that shook, and spoke my name backward — *Elav* — and the magic rose in him like water finding an old channel, seven years dry and then not, and Maerlin said afterward that the whole hall lit blue-white and every relic on every shelf rang like struck glass, and Odessa said Sable screamed, and Sable says he doesn't remember screaming, only the direction. North-by-northeast. Three streets. A house that looked abandoned.

He broke his own law to find me. Write that down too.

* * *

What I saw, from inside: the wall exploded inward in a bloom of cold light, and standing in the wreckage was Sable Pemberton, charm-seller, exile, a thin man in a patched coat with chalk dust to his elbows and his eyes full of a blue fire that had no business being in anyone's eyes.

The pleasant man turned, unhurried, and said, "Ah. The other one."

"Let him go," Sable said, "or I do to your house what I did to my life."

I have thought about that sentence many times since. It was not a threat, exactly. It was a confession used as a weapon — the most dangerous kind. He meant it. Everyone in the room knew he meant it, including, I think, himself, and it frightened him more than it frightened them.

The gilded veins in the walls surged toward him like ivy in fast-forward, and he raised one chalk-white hand, and the light that came off him was not gold. It was the color of the sky before snow. The gilding touched it and *screamed* — a sound I felt in my teeth — and recoiled, and the pleasant man's pleasant face finally, briefly, showed something underneath.

"Prophecy-bound," he said, almost wonderingly. "No wonder the master wants you both."

Then the chair released me — whether because Sable's magic unmade its holding-spell or because the agents calculated that two prophecy-bound men were worth more alive than one dead captive, I never learned — and Sable grabbed my wrist, and we ran, out through the hole he'd made, into streets slick with rain, while behind us the gilded house sealed itself shut like a wound closing.

We ran six blocks before either of us could breathe. Then we stopped in the shadow of a bridge, both soaked, both shaking, and Sable looked at his own hands like they belonged to a stranger.

"I used it," he said. His voice was very quiet. "After seven years, I — the first thing I did with it was *this*. For *you*."

"Yes," I said.

"They'll have felt it. Every agent in Ashfall felt that. Maerlin was right, it's the kind of magic that gets guilds burned, and I've just — I've painted a sign on all of us—"

"Sable." I took his face in my hands, rain and all. "Look at me. You broke your oath tonight. Your oldest one. The one you made over ashes."

His throat worked. "Don't."

"And it was the easiest thing you've ever done," I said. "I watched it be easy. So here's what I know now, cartographer's certainty, measured and mapped: whatever the prophecy thinks it's written about us — whatever script it's got us walking — *that* wasn't in any book. You don't break a seven-year vow for a line of text. You do it for a person."

He stared at me. Rain ran off his chin. And slowly, terribly, wonderfully, he started to laugh — a cracked, exhausted, disbelieving sound — and pulled me against him, and I held on, and neither of us said anything else for a long time, because some things are truest in silence.

When we finally separated, he was serious again, and colder, and older.

"We can't stay in Ashfall," he said. "Not after tonight. There are roads under the mountains — old smuggler roads, pre-Interdiction. They lead to the capital." He met my eyes. "To him. To the thing wearing the king's face."

"The prophecy," I said carefully, "has verses about the road."

"I know," Sable said. "I've read them." He looked away, toward the mountains black against the cloudy dark. "I just haven't figured out yet how to tell you what they say."
