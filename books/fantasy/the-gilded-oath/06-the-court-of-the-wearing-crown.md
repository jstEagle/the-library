## 6. The Court of the Wearing Crown

The capital had a smell Sable hadn't forgotten: coal smoke and cut stone and, underneath it, the faint sweet rot of gilding — real gold leaf applied over things that were not gold. He'd been nine years old the last time he stood in Vharen's streets, holding his mother's hand at a coronation parade. He remembered thinking the king looked like a coin someone had polished too hard.

He'd been right.

Now he walked those same streets with a peddler's pack on his back and a forged guild license in his pocket, and Corin walked three paces behind him the way servants did here, head bowed, hands empty. It had taken Sable two days to teach him the posture. Corin kept wanting to look up. In 1823, apparently, cartographers looked up.

"Charm merchant," Corin murmured as they passed the first checkpoint, where a guard in the king's black-and-gold was waving through anyone with the right papers and the right dullness in his eyes.

"Guild-licensed charm merchant," Sable corrected under his breath. "The license is what gets us into the palace quarter. Everyone wants luck on coronation week. Even tyrants."

*Especially* tyrants, he thought. The usurper had announced a public court for the eve of its own crowning — an old rite, the heralds said, where the sovereign hears petitions from the common folk before taking the oath. A gesture of mercy. A performance of it, anyway. The whole city was being funneled toward the palace like water toward a drain.

Sable felt the pull of it too, though his came from somewhere else: the prophecy book, wrapped in oilcloth against his spine, warm as a sleeping animal. Since they'd left Ashfall it had been getting warmer by the day. Tonight it was nearly hot. Whatever waited in that throne room, the book wanted him to see it.

That was the part he didn't say out loud. Books didn't want things. He'd told himself that every night for a week, and every night the oilcloth burned a little hotter against his skin.

* * *

They got through the gates because Sable sold a guard a fertility charm and lied beautifully about his grandmother.

Inside the palace quarter, the city's noise fell away and something else replaced it — a hum, low and constant, like a struck bell that never finished ringing. Sable stopped on the flagstones and put a hand flat against his own sternum.

"You feel that?"

Corin nodded slowly. His face had gone the color of old paper. "The spell that brought me here felt like this. Like standing inside a note." He swallowed. "It's stronger here than anywhere we've been."

"It's coming from the throne room."

"It's coming from *him*," Corin said, and there was something in his voice Sable hadn't heard before — not fear exactly. Recognition. Grief with a map attached.

They joined the petitioners' line in the great antechamber, a river of farmers and clerks and widows shuffling between pillars of veined marble. Every pillar was wrapped in gold leaf from floor to capital. Not painted — *wrapped*, the way you'd bandage a wound. Sable counted four pillars before he understood what he was looking at, and then he couldn't stop counting.

Gold over everything. Gold over stone, gold over iron, gold over the faces carved into the friezes — kings of the old dynasty rendered featureless beneath the leaf. And threaded through all of it, so fine you'd miss it if you weren't Guild-trained, ran lines of script. Tiny, precise, repeating. Binding verses.

"He's gilded the building," Sable breathed. "The whole palace is one continuous ward."

"A cage?" Corin asked.

"No." Sable's mouth had gone dry. "A *seal*. You seal something in. This isn't decoration. This is the thing keeping itself together."

The line moved forward. The hum grew louder. And Sable realized, with the particular horror of a man who has read too many forbidden books, that the binding script on the pillars was the same hand, the same age, the same *ink* as the verses in the prophecy book beneath his coat. The usurper and the book were written by the same author. Or were the same author. He genuinely could not decide which possibility frightened him more.

* * *

The throne room doors opened, and the sound that came out was applause.

Not wild applause. Measured. Rhythmic. The applause of people who have been told when to clap. Petitioners went in groups of twenty and came out minutes later, pale and silent, escorted past the line by guards who watched their hands. One woman came out weeping without any expression on her face at all, tears sliding down cheeks that had forgotten how to move. Sable watched her go and made himself breathe.

Then their group's turn came, and the doors swung wide, and Sable saw the king.

Or the shape wearing him.

The body was right — Corin had described King Aldric often enough on the road, late at night, in the careful voice of a man describing a friend: tall, broad through the shoulder, gray coming in early at the temples because he laughed at his own worries. This body matched. But the light was wrong. Courtiers' chandeliers blazed overhead, and none of it seemed to touch the figure on the throne; he sat in his own illumination, a soft honeyed glow that came from *inside*, from under the skin, as if the man had been dipped in molten gold and it had cooled there, brightening when he smiled.

And he was smiling. At everyone. Continuously. The smile never reached anything. It sat on his face like the gold leaf sat on the pillars — applied, maintained, sealing.

"That's not him," Corin whispered. His hand found Sable's sleeve and gripped it hard enough to hurt. "Sable. That's not—"

"I know."

"*I know him.* That's his face but it's—" Corin's breath went ragged. "There's something else moving behind his eyes. Something looking *out* through them. Like watching a lit window and knowing someone else is standing in the room."

Ahead of them, a farmer knelt and stammered a petition about grain tithes. The king leaned forward, gracious, radiant, and spoke — and his voice was the worst thing yet. It was a beautiful voice, warm and resonant, and every word landed a half-beat after his lips moved, like a translation arriving late.

"The crown hears you," it said. "The crown provides. Rise, and be gilded."

Two attendants stepped forward and pressed a small gold medallion into the farmer's shaking hands. He clutched it like a pardon and was ushered away, and Sable saw, as the man turned, the faint shine already spreading across his knuckles where he'd touched the thing.

It marked them. Every petitioner left carrying a piece of it.

Their turn. Twenty of them shuffled down the marble aisle, and Sable kept his eyes lowered the way he'd coached Corin, and it worked for exactly eleven steps — until the presence on the throne *noticed* them.

Sable felt it happen. Felt attention swing toward him like a searchlight, heavy and hot, and the prophecy book against his spine flared so hot through the oilcloth that he gasped. The king's glowing face turned. The smile widened by precisely the amount a smile can widen without becoming something else.

"Come closer, merchant," the golden voice said, landing its half-beat late. "You carry something old."

The guards didn't move. They didn't need to. Sable walked forward because the alternative was being carried, and beside him he heard Corin's breathing go thin and fast.

He stopped at the foot of the dais. Close enough now to see what the glow hid: hairline cracks in the king's skin, fine as the script on the pillars, and through the cracks — nothing. Smoke. Slow-turning, patient smoke, gilded at the edges where it caught its own light.

And underneath the smoke, deep down, in the place where a man keeps his own face when everything else has been taken — a pair of eyes. Tired. Human. *Aware.* Looking straight at Corin.

Corin made a sound Sable would remember for the rest of his life. Small. Broken. The sound of a man seeing a drowned friend wave from under the ice.

"Your Majesty keeps a fine court," Sable said, and his voice only shook a little, which he would count later as the bravest thing he'd ever done.

"The crown keeps many things," the usurper said. Its gaze slid from Sable to Corin and fixed there, and the temperature of the room seemed to drop even as the glow brightened. "Some things travel very far to be kept. Don't you, *cartographer?*"

The word dropped into silence like a stone into a well. Around them, twenty petitioners held their breath. Somewhere high above, the false king's smile gleamed, and behind it, far behind it, the true king's trapped eyes begged.

Sable's hand drifted, casually, to the oilcloth bundle at his spine — and felt the prophecy book open itself, all on its own, to a page it had never shown him before.

The verse was short. It was addressed to him by name.

*On the eve of the gilded crown, the merchant stands before the throne, and the price of the unbinding is written here: the king must die by the hand that loves him most.*

Sable read it twice in the space of a heartbeat, and understood, finally, why the book had been getting warmer.

It wasn't warning him.

It was counting down.
