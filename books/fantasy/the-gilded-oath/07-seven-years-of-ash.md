## 7. Seven Years of Ash

The fire in the safehouse hearth had burned down to a red eye, and Corin couldn't sleep.

He'd tried. He'd lain on the narrow cot with his boots off and his coat folded under his head, listening to the strange night sounds of a city a century past everything he knew — the clatter of a late tram, someone laughing too loudly in the street below, the hum of electric light through the window like a trapped insect. Sleep wouldn't come. His body still remembered the interrogator's table, the questions asked in a voice like wet gravel, the moment the door had burst open and Sable had come through it with light pouring off his hands like water off a ledge.

That was the thing that kept him awake. Not the fear. The light.

He'd seen Sable work before — small things, harmless things, the little charms he sold in his shop. A candle that burned without heat. A key that remembered its lock. Pretty tricks, parlor work, the kind of magic a man might learn from a pamphlet. But what Corin had seen in that cellar was something else entirely. Sable had put his hand flat against the wall and *asked* the city where they were keeping him, and the city had answered, and the answering had cost Sable something. Corin had watched him stagger afterward, watched him press his palm over his heart as though holding a door shut against a wind.

And now Sable sat across the room in a battered armchair, pretending to read a book by lamplight, and Corin decided he was done pretending not to notice.

"You're not reading," Corin said.

Sable turned a page without looking at it. "I'm resting my eyes."

"Your eyes are moving."

"They do that when I rest them."

Corin sat up. "You pulled me out of that room with magic I've only read about in the Guild's oldest texts. Magic the inspectors would hang you for. And you're sitting there acting like it cost you nothing."

"It didn't cost me nothing." Sable closed the book. His voice was flat, careful, the voice of a man setting down something fragile. "It cost me plenty. You just weren't meant to see the bill."

"Then show me the bill."

For a long moment Sable said nothing. The lamp hissed. Outside, the tram sang its last run down the avenue and went quiet, and the silence that followed felt deliberate, like the city itself was waiting to hear what he'd say.

Then Sable laughed — one short, ugly note with no humor in it at all.

"The bill," he said. "All right. Here's the bill, cartographer. Seven years ago I was the strongest mage the Hollow Lantern had produced in two generations. Not charm-seller strong. *Real* strong. The kind of strong that makes old men nervous and young men hungry. Master Ellery Voss took me as his apprentice when I was sixteen because he said I had 'an unreasonable relationship with power.'" He turned the word over like a coin he suspected was counterfeit. "He wasn't wrong."

Corin swung his legs off the cot. "What happened?"

"What happened is that I was twenty-three and certain and fast, which is the most dangerous combination a mage can be. There was a binding gone wrong — a spirit caught in a dying woman's house, angry, half-mad from decades of being ignored. Standard work. Ellery had done a hundred of them. He told me to hold the outer circle and *not touch the working*. Those were his words. Not touch the working." Sable's hands had started moving as he talked, sketching shapes in the air that Corin realized were the geometry of a spell circle, drawn from memory, drawn from nightmare. "But I could feel it slipping. I could feel the spirit tearing at the edges of his circle, and I thought — I *knew* — I could reinforce it faster than calling for help. So I reached in."

"And it went wrong."

"It went wrong the way a dam goes wrong. All at once, everywhere, and everyone downstream drowns." Sable's voice didn't rise. That was the worst part, Corin thought. It stayed level, worn smooth like river stone, like he'd told this story to himself so many times the telling couldn't cut anymore. Except his hands had stopped moving, and they were shaking. "My power came through the circle sideways. It didn't reinforce the binding. It fed the spirit. For about four seconds I was the strongest thing in that room, and then I was nothing at all, and Ellery was between me and it, because that's what mentors do, they stand where the damage lands—"

He stopped.

The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, a dog barked twice and gave up.

"He died?" Corin asked quietly, though he already knew.

"Burned out from the inside. What the spirit didn't take, the backlash did. They buried what was left of him in the Guild crypt with full honors, and then the Council held a hearing, and I stood in front of the same men who'd called me a prodigy and told them the truth: that I touched the working. That I killed my teacher with my own power." Sable looked at the fire, not at Corin. "They didn't exile me formally. Exile requires a vote, and a vote requires admitting we still had enough power left to lose. Instead they gave me the shop. Small mercies, harmless charms, keep your head down, don't touch anything real. Seven years of ash, cartographer. That's what you've been traveling with. A man who already failed his oath once."

* * *

Corin stood. He hadn't planned to. His legs simply decided.

"So that's it," he said. "That's why you flinch every time the prophecy mentions power. That's why you look at the unbinding rite like it's a cliff edge."

"That's why I *am* a cliff edge," Sable said. "Yes."

"Then tell me why I should trust you with mine."

The words came out harder than Corin intended, and he saw them land — saw Sable's shoulders come up like a man bracing for a blow he'd been expecting all evening.

"There it is," Sable said softly. "There's the question you've been carrying since the cellar."

"Can you blame me? The prophecy names *you*, Sable. Not me. You're the one who performs the unbinding. You're the one who has to channel enough power to peel a usurper off a living king, and I'm supposed to stand next to you and believe that the last time you channeled real power, you killed the person standing closest to you." Corin heard his own voice crack and hated it. "I'm the person standing closest to you."

"I know." Sable rose out of the chair slowly, like an old man, like a man whose body remembered damage his mind kept trying to file away. "You think I don't lie awake doing that arithmetic? Every mile of those smuggler roads, every verse of that cursed book that describes your hands, your voice, the way you say my name — you think I haven't wondered if the prophecy made a mistake? If it reached back a century and grabbed the wrong man to bind to the wrong mage?"

"Has it?"

"I don't know!" It finally cracked through, the level voice splitting down the middle, and Corin flinched at the sound. "I don't *know*, Corin. That's the truth you actually want, isn't it? Not the story about Ellery. You want to know if I'm going to get you killed, and the honest answer is that I got the last person who trusted me killed, and the prophecy says I'm going to need more power than I've ever held, and I have spent seven years being *grateful* that I can't—" He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes. "I was relieved. Do you understand that? When they took it from me, I was relieved. And now this book wants it back, and some traitor part of me wants it back too, and I don't trust a single thing in my own chest."

The room was very quiet. Corin crossed to him — three steps, no more — and stopped close enough to see the lamplight trembling in Sable's eyes.

"Say the rest," Corin said. "Whatever it is. Say it."

"The rest is that I would rather tear the page out than perform it." Sable's hand dropped. He looked at Corin straight on, and there was nothing performed in his face at all, nothing charming, nothing shopkeeper-smooth. Just exhaustion and terror and something underneath both that Corin had been watching grow since the first night, since the bleeding light and the impossible doorway. "If the prophecy ends with you dead and me triumphant, then I'll burn the whole book before I let it happen. I failed one oath, Corin. I'm not going to fail the second one. Even if failing it costs everything the Guild has left."

"That's not an answer to my question."

"No," Sable agreed. "It's a better one."

Corin wanted to argue. He wanted to lay out the logic, the risk, the arithmetic of trust — he was a cartographer, mapping danger was his whole trade. But standing there in the red light of the dying fire, looking at a man who had just handed him the worst truth about himself unprompted, unwrapped, with no excuse attached, Corin found that the map had changed. Trust, he was beginning to understand, wasn't a certainty you charted in advance. It was a country you walked into blind, with someone, and named together.

"Ellery stood where the damage landed," Corin said at last. "That's what you said. He stood between you and it."

"Yes."

"Then here's my answer." Corin took Sable's hand — the right one, the one that had asked the city where they were keeping him, the one still faintly warm with spent power. "Next time you reach into a working, I'm not standing behind you. I'm standing beside you. Where the damage lands, remember? We split it."

Sable stared at their joined hands like a man reading a document in a language he'd forgotten he knew. "That's the stupidest tactical doctrine I've ever heard."

"I'm a century out of date. My tactics are all wrong."

And Sable laughed — really laughed this time, wrecked and startled and alive — and pulled Corin's hand up against his chest, over the heart, over the door he'd been holding shut for seven years.

"Stay awake with me," Sable said. "Until morning. I don't want to do the arithmetic alone tonight."

So they sat in the armchair together, too narrow for two, neither willing to fix that, and the fire burned down to embers, and outside Ashfall slept on, unaware.

It was nearly dawn when Corin noticed it — the prophecy book, still packed in Sable's satchel by the door, glowing faintly through the leather. A soft gold light, pulsing slow, like a heartbeat.

Like something turning a page.

"Sable," Corin whispered. "The book's awake."
