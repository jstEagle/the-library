## 8. The Usurper's Bargain

They came for me at dusk, and they didn't bother with a knock.

The door of my rented room above the dyer's shop simply opened, the way a mouth opens, and the light in the stairwell bent toward it instead of through it. I knew before I saw him that it was the king. Not the true king — the *wearing* one. You can tell by the way candles behave around him. Real kings make shadows stand at attention. This thing made them lean.

"Master Pemberton." The voice was warm as fresh bread, which was the worst part. It had stolen the voice of a man who had probably been kind once. "You've been difficult to find. Your guild taught you well — or badly, depending on your view."

I stood up from the chair by the window, where I'd been sitting with Corin's compass in my hands for the better part of an hour, not really looking at it. "I'm just a charm merchant."

"You're a Pemberton." He stepped inside without seeming to cross the room. One moment he was in the doorway; the next he was close enough that I could smell him — gilding and smoke, like a burned church. "Your grandmother unmade a plague with a word. Your mother held a bridge against a flood for six hours with nothing but her will and a bad temper. And you sell love charms to teenagers for three coppers apiece."

"That's honest work," I said.

"It's *buried* work." He smiled, and the smile fit his face the way a glove fits a hand that isn't yours. "Sit down, Sable. Please. I've come a long way to offer you something, and I'd rather you hear it standing than running."

I didn't sit. He seemed to find that charming.

"I know what you're planning," he said. "The unbinding. The little procession tomorrow, the rite, the page torn from your precious book. Did you think the Hollow Lantern kept secrets? Three old people in a rotting hall, whispering over a relic? I have been wearing this crown for seven years. I know every thread in the tapestry, because half of it was woven from my own loom."

"Then why come yourself?" I asked. "Send soldiers. Send the inspectors who patrol my street every night."

"Because soldiers kill men," he said, "and killing you would be a waste. Killing Corin Vale would be a tragedy — and worse, a *fulfillment*. No. What I want is simpler, and crueler, and far more interesting." He gestured, and something appeared between us in the air: a small flame, gold-white, burning without heat. I knew what it was before he said it. Every member of the Hollow Lantern knew what it was, the way an orphan knows a photograph of a parent's face.

"The Guild's fire," I said. My voice came out strange. Flat. Far away.

"The Guild's fire," he agreed. "Taken the night your order knelt and signed the Accord. All of it — every working, every rite, every secret your founders bled for. Sealed in my keeping these twenty-three years. I am prepared to return it. All of it. To *you*."

The flame hung there, patient as a debt.

"You could be what your grandmother was," he said softly. "What your mother was. What you were, before—" He stopped, delicately, the way a surgeon stops before naming the tumor. "Before the accident. Before Ashfall took your power and your mentor and gave you a shop of small mercies instead. You could have it back. Everything. Tonight."

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about losing magic: it doesn't feel like loss. It feels like a room in your house that you've bricked over, and after enough years you stop hearing the sounds behind the wall. You stop dreaming about the door. You tell yourself the room was never real.

But it was real. And when the usurper held that flame in the air between us, every brick in that wall lit up from behind, and I heard everything.

Seven years ago I had been the strongest novice the Guild had produced in a generation. Seven years ago I had tried a working too big for me, alone, proud, certain — and Vessa Thorne had thrown herself into the circle to pull me out of it, and the fire had gone through her like water through a net, and what was left of her wouldn't fit in a coffin. They gave me her ashes and a choice: exile to the charm shop, or binding. I chose the shop. I told myself it was penance. Mostly it was fear.

And now here was a monster made of smoke and gilding, holding out the only thing in the world I had ever truly wanted.

"All of it," I said. My mouth had gone dry. "Every working."

"Every working." He let the flame drift closer. It didn't warm the air. It warmed something behind the air. "And more. Consider the second gift, Master Pemberton, which I suspect will interest you even more." He produced, from nowhere, a folded paper — travel writs, sealed with the crown, valid across every border in the nine cities. "Corin Vale goes home. Unharmed. Unhunted. A ship, an escort, safe passage to his own century's door — or as close as my art can bring him. He wakes in 1823 with a headache and a story no one believes, and he lives out his life mapping coastlines. He never dies in a war. He never burns on a pyre because your prophecy demands it."

The room tilted. I gripped the back of the chair.

That was the knife of it. Not the power — I could have refused the power, maybe, on a good day, with Vessa's ashes in my memory. But Corin. Corin, who had been dead a century before I was born, who had walked into my shop bleeding light because a dying king's desperation had grabbed him out of his own life like a hand through water. Corin, whom the prophecy had sentenced to die so neatly, so *poetically*, on the steps of a coronation. Corin, who two nights ago had fallen asleep against my shoulder on the smuggler's road and murmured, half-dreaming, about the smell of ink in his father's shop.

He could go home. Alive. Whole. Unwritten.

"No one would ever know," the usurper said, reading me the way I read customers — watching the eyes, waiting for the waver. "Not the Guild. Not him. You keep your fire, he keeps his life, the free cities keep their usurper, and everyone is exactly where history put them. Prophecy is a machine, Sable. I am merely offering you the chance to switch it off."

"And the cities?" I managed. "The war. The marching armies. The people who burn when your reign continues."

"People burn under every reign." He said it gently, almost kindly, and that gentleness was the most obscene thing I had ever heard. "Kings come and go. Crowns are worn. You cannot save everyone, Master Pemberton. But you can save *him*. Isn't that worth more than an abstraction? Isn't one true thing worth more than a hundred written ones?"

The flame drifted another inch toward me.

And I want to be honest, because honesty is the only oath I have left: I nearly took it.

My hand rose. I felt it rise, felt the fingers open, felt seven years of ash and grief and three-copper charms all leaning toward that little golden light like flowers toward a sun. I thought: *Vessa, I could finish what I started. I could be whole.* I thought: *Corin, you could go home.* I thought: *the prophecy says he dies, the prophecy says he dies, the prophecy says—*

The prophecy.

Written a century ago. By hands I'd never seen. Naming tonight, naming him, naming me — scripting our every step, our every stumble, our every kiss on the cold road beneath the mountains. Scripting his death with the same calm ink.

And the usurper offering to preserve that script forever. To keep the book closed. To keep the machine running, with him wearing the crown it built for him.

"You said no one would ever know," I said quietly.

"No one."

"But *he'd* know." I looked up. "Corin. He'd wake up in 1823 and live sixty years knowing he left someone behind. Knowing there was a version of this where he stayed and fought and *chose* — and that I sold that version for a box of spells and a boat ticket. That's not saving him. That's just a kinder-shaped cage."

The usurper's smile thinned by a degree. "Sentiment."

"Choice," I said. "It's the only thing he hasn't had since the day a dying king's spell yanked him out of his century. Everyone else has decided his life for him — your prophecy, your predecessor's spell, your bargain. I won't be one more author." I closed my hand, and the flame — still hanging there, still patient — reflected in my knuckles. "Keep your fire. Keep your writs. Tomorrow we do what we came to do."

For a long moment the room was silent except for the dyer's vats turning over below us, slow as breathing.

Then the warmth went out of the usurper's face all at once, like a lamp snuffed, and what looked out through the king's borrowed eyes had nothing human in it at all.

"A pity," it said. "I did so want to avoid the mess." It turned toward the door, then paused, courteous as a headsman. "The procession begins at dawn, Master Pemberton. Wear something gilded. Everyone does." 

The door closed. The light unbent. The candle shadows stood back up.

I stood there a long time, shaking, my hand still half-open around the shape of a flame I hadn't taken. Then I picked up Corin's compass from the chair, and its needle swung — not north, but toward the capital, toward the coronation square, steady as a heartbeat.

And underneath my window, soft as snowfall, the first of the usurper's soldiers began sealing the streets.
