## 9. Unbinding

The bells began at dawn.

Not the usual bells of Ashfall — the cracked ones, the ones the inspectors had left hanging out of spite — but the capital's great bronze throats, ringing from the Citadel hill all the way down to the river. A coronation peal. The usurper was being crowned at noon, and every street between here and there would be packed with people ordered to cheer.

Corin stood at the mouth of the smuggler tunnel with his back against cold stone and tried to breathe.

*This is where it happens,* he thought. *This is where I die.*

He'd known since the mountains. The verses hadn't hidden it. They'd dressed it up in beautiful language — *the cartographer's blood upon the gilded stone, the map completed, the journey ended* — but Sable had read it aloud once by firelight and then gone very quiet, and quiet was worse than weeping. Quiet meant he'd understood it too.

"You don't have to be here," Corin said.

Sable didn't look up from the chalk lines he was drawing on the tunnel floor. His hands were steady. That frightened Corin more than shaking would have. "Yes, I do."

"Sable—"

"The rite needs two anchors." He drew the last line and sat back on his heels. "One bound to the crown. One bound to me. There's no version of this where you wait safely at home, because there is no home anymore, and there's no version where I do it alone, because I'd burn out before the second verse. So." He stood, brushed chalk dust off his knees, and finally met Corin's eyes. "We go together. Like everything else."

Behind them, the tunnel filled with the rest of it: Maren Holloway with her white braid and her lantern-staff, old Tam Voss carrying a crate of relics like a man hauling his own coffin, and behind them — impossibly, wonderfully — forty strangers in shopkeepers' coats and dockworkers' boots, the hidden mages of the free cities, come up out of seven years of silence because a charm-seller's letter had reached them one by one, hand to hand, like contraband.

Maren looked at the crowd and then at Sable, and something in her face softened. "You always were better at gathering people than the rest of us."

"I sell things," Sable said. "It's the same skill."

Corin laughed despite himself. It came out cracked.

Sable crossed to him, took his face in both hands, thumbs at his jaw, and for a moment the tunnel, the bells, the war — all of it receded. "Listen to me," Sable said. "The rite has three movements. We hold the first two together. The third—" His voice caught, just barely, just enough. "The third is mine. Whatever happens, whatever the book says, you stay with me. Do you understand? You *stay with me.*"

Corin wanted to say yes. He wanted it so badly his teeth ached. But he was a cartographer; he knew what maps did. They told you the truth about the ground whether you liked the shape of it or not.

"I'll stay as long as the world lets me," he said.

Sable closed his eyes. "That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one."

* * *

The procession route was a river of gold.

They came up out of the tunnel beneath a tanner's yard and merged into the crowd two streets from the Citadel, and Corin felt the rite wake around them like a second heartbeat. The mages had spread through the press of bodies in pairs and threes, ordinary as market-day, each carrying a piece of the working: a coin, a nail, a scrap of the Guild's oldest banner, a child's charm shaped like a lantern. Harmless things. Things no inspector would look twice at. Each one a stitch in a net they were throwing over six blocks of the capital.

Sable walked beside him wearing a merchant's gray coat, hood back, visible, unremarkable. His right hand was wrapped in linen to hide the sigils he'd inked onto his palm that morning — the first true marks he'd drawn on his own skin in seven years. Corin had watched him do it. Watched him hesitate over the needle and ink for a full minute, jaw tight, before pressing the first line home.

"What does it feel like?" Corin had asked.

"Like opening a door I bricked shut," Sable said. "With someone still standing behind it."

Now, in the crowd, under the bells, Sable's wrapped hand found Corin's and held it. Around them the net tightened. Corin could feel it — a low hum in his bones, forty small magics agreeing to be one thing for one hour.

Then the trumpets, and the procession turned the corner, and Corin saw the king.

Or the thing wearing him.

King Aldric had been sixty when he cast the spell that pulled Corin across a century, dying, desperate, magnificent. The body on the gilded open carriage was still sixty, still broad-shouldered, still crowned — but it moved wrong. Too smooth. The smile was painted on from the inside. And when the sunlight hit the crown, Corin saw what he'd seen once before in a scrying glass and never forgotten: smoke, coiling beneath the skin of the man's throat, threaded through with gold like veins in marble.

The usurper's gaze swept the crowd.

And stopped.

For half a heartbeat the painted smile didn't matter, because the eyes found Corin across four hundred screaming loyalists, and the eyes were ancient, and hungry, and *amused*.

*There you are,* the look said. *Right on schedule.*

The carriage rolled past. The bells crashed into their final peal. And Corin felt the rite seize him like a hand closing around his spine.

"Now," Sable breathed.

* * *

The first movement was the circle.

Forty voices spoke as one, not loudly — a murmur under the crowd noise, a tide coming in — and the streets changed. Corin felt it happen: the gold leaf on the lampposts dulling, the banners' gilt thread going gray, the very sunlight losing its shine. The usurper's power was gilding, Maren had explained. It needed the world to believe in its brightness. The circle was unbelief, made structural.

On the Citadel steps, the procession faltered. The painted smile flickered.

The second movement was the naming.

Sable unwrapped his hand. The sigils caught the light — black ink, silver-edged, alive — and he spoke the true name of the thing on the throne, the name Tam Voss had recovered from a burned archive at the cost of his own library, syllables that tasted like rust and river water. Corin felt the words leave Sable and strike the air like a hammer on glass. A crack ran across the sky. Not literally — or maybe literally; in Ashfall's capital it was hard to say — a seam of darkness splitting the noon blue above the Citadel, thin as a hairline fracture in porcelain.

The crowd screamed. Some of them, Corin realized, were screaming in joy.

The usurper rose from its throne. The king's body came apart at the edges, unraveling into smoke and gold thread, and the thing beneath leaned out over the steps and *looked* at them — at Sable, at Corin, at the little knot of traitors standing in its beautiful city — and spoke with a voice like coins poured on stone:

"You would unmake me with borrowed courage and a dead guild's arithmetic?"

"We'd unmake you with a book," Sable said, "and a promise."

He opened the satchel. The prophecy book came out into daylight for the first time in a century, and it *blazed*, every page lit from within, and the third movement began.

* * *

The third movement was the price.

Corin felt it arrive the way you feel weather change — pressure dropping, birds going silent. The rite reached for its final anchor. The verses flared white on the open page, and Corin read his own ending in letters of light:

*The cartographer's blood upon the gilded stone.*
*The map completed. The journey ended.*

"No," Sable said.

The rite didn't care what he said. Power moved through the circle like floodwater finding its channel, and the channel ran through Sable — through the door he'd bricked shut, now torn open, seven years of dammed magic roaring back into him — and the flood wanted its toll. The verses pulsed. The crack in the sky widened. The usurper shrieked, smoke streaming from the king's dissolving mouth, held at bay by forty murmuring voices and one man's terrible reopened well of power.

But the rite was built a century ago by people who believed prophecy was architecture. It had one exit, and the exit was written.

Corin stepped forward.

"Don't." Sable's voice broke apart on the word. He was on his knees now, light pouring off his inked hand, tears cutting clean lines through the ash on his face, holding the whole collapsing sky up with his bare will — and looking at Corin like a drowning man looks at shore. "Don't you dare. I didn't reopen *this* to close it on you."

"The rite doesn't finish without it." Corin kept walking. The gilded stones of the plaza were warm under his feet, warm as the page that had named him. "You taught me that. Everything costs. You've paid seven years. Maren paid her guild. Tam paid his life's work. This is my line item, love. Let me pay mine."

"I *forbid*—"

"You can't." Corin smiled, and it hurt, and it was real, which was more than anything the usurper had ever managed. "That's the whole point of you. You don't forbid. You *choose*. So choose something else."

He knelt on the gilded stone. The light gathered above him, patient, scriptural, certain.

And Sable Pemberton — charm-seller, exile, oath-breaker, the man who had failed one promise and spent seven years learning how to keep another — looked at the prophecy book in his own blazing hand.

Looked at the page.

Looked at Corin.

And reached for the binding.
