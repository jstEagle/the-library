## 10. Unwritten

The page was warm under my fingers.

That was the thing I would remember afterward — not the smoke, not the screaming gilding, not the way the procession had frozen mid-step like a painting interrupted. The warmth. As if the book had been alive all along, and only now, with my thumb hooked beneath the corner of the binding page, did it notice what I intended to do.

*And the cartographer shall give his breath to the unbinding,* the verse read, in ink that had dried a century before Corin Vale drew his first breath. *And the charmwright shall live to carry the grief, for a prophecy requires a witness more than it requires a king.*

A witness. That was my role. Not lover, not savior. Grief-carrier.

Corin stood at the center of the unbinding circle we'd cut into the coronation square's marble, light pouring off him in threads as the rite pulled the true king loose from the thing wearing his face. He was looking at me. Even now, even with the spell chewing through him, he was looking at me like I was the one being unraveled.

"Sable." His voice came out frayed. "It's all right. It says—"

"I know what it says." I had spent seven years obeying what things said. Guild law. Inspector edicts. The small print of survival. And every time, the price had been paid by someone else — by my mentor, burning; by the Hollow Lantern, dimming; by me, selling lucky pennies to people who deserved real hope.

The usurper turned its head.

It still wore King Aldric's body, but the face had gone wrong in the way gilt goes wrong — cracking, showing the dull metal beneath the shine. Smoke poured from the seams of its ceremonial coat. When it spoke, the whole square heard it, the way you hear thunder through floors.

"Charmwright." Almost gentle. "Tear that page and everything ends. The guilds. The wards. The old compacts your order died protecting. You will be nothing."

"We're already nothing," I said. "We've been nothing for a generation. You just kept the receipts."

Behind it, the true king — Aldric, whole and hollow-eyed, kneeling in the wreckage of his own stolen shape — lifted his head. A hundred years trapped inside a parasite's shine, and the first thing he did with his freed voice was whisper: *"Let go."*

Not to me. To himself. Permission.

I tore the page.

---

Sound went out of the world first. Then color. For one long heartbeat the square existed only as pressure — the sensation of a held breath the size of a city — and then the book screamed.

I mean that literally. The prophecy book, the oldest relic of the Hollow Lantern, made a sound like a struck bell dying, and every word on every remaining page bled upward off the vellum at once, a hundred years of certainty lifting into the air as black mist. The verses that had named this night. The verses that had named Corin. The ones that had described, in language too precise to be comfort, the exact way his hand fit against mine on the road beneath the mountains.

All of it unwriting itself. All of it becoming weather.

The usurper came apart the way a firework comes apart — from the center outward, gilding flaking into ash, smoke losing its memory of shapes. It reached for the torn page in my hand. Of course it did. It had been anchored to that page since before I was born; some desperate court mage a century ago had bound a hungry thing into a story because a story is the hardest prison to escape. You can break a chain. Who thinks to break a sentence?

Its fingers closed on air. Its mouth shaped the king's voice one last time — *but I was promised* — and then there was no usurper. There was a drift of gold dust settling over the coronation square, glittering in the lantern-light like cheap confetti, and the crowd of free cities' mages and hidden guildsmen and ordinary terrified citizens stood in the silence after, waiting for history to tell them what happened next.

History had nothing to say. I'd seen to that.

---

Corin collapsed.

I got to him before anyone else — I don't remember crossing the circle, only that my knees hit marble and his head was in my lap and the light was draining out of him like tide going out, slow and silver and, God, *stopping*. Stopping. Because the rite demanded a death only when the prophecy supplied the ending, and I had fed the ending to the wind.

"You tore it out." He was laughing, or crying, or both; with Corin the border was always negotiable. "You absolute — you tore the page out of a book older than my *grandmother*—"

"It seemed rude to let you die on schedule."

His hand found my collar and pulled me down until our foreheads touched. Up close he smelled of ozone and road dust and the particular exhaustion of a man who had been yanked across a hundred years and had decided, somewhere on the smuggler roads, to stop apologizing for it.

"The book," he said. "Sable. What happens to the Guild without it?"

I looked over his shoulder. Master Ivenne stood at the edge of the square with the other two survivors of the Hollow Lantern, and she was watching the last of the black mist dissolve, and her face — I had prepared myself for fury, for grief, for the particular disappointment of a woman who had spent her life guarding a cage — her face was *relieved*.

She caught me looking. She shrugged, an old woman's shrug, carrying seventy years of vigil-keeping in it.

"Someone had to," she called across the square. "We certainly weren't going to."

---

King Aldric abdicated three days later, in a hall that still smelled faintly of gold dust.

He did it standing, refusing the throne behind him, and his speech was short enough that every newspaper could print it whole: "I was absent from my own life for a century. I will not begin my return by ruling it. The free cities have governed themselves longer than I governed them — they've simply never been permitted to admit it. Consider yourselves admitted."

Then he walked out of the Coronation Hall past rows of stunned ministers, paused beside where Corin and I stood among the guild delegations, and gripped Corin's forearm like a soldier.

"You carried me," the king said. "A hundred years, you were the map that remembered where I was buried. Whatever you ask of this country, it owes you."

Corin glanced sideways at me — that quick, crooked glance I already knew better than my own handwriting — and said, "Actually, what we'd like is passage home. Both of us. Ashfall, if the trains are running."

"Ashfall," the king repeated, tasting it. "The city that outlawed magic and produced the man who broke a prophecy." Something moved through his tired face that might have been the ghost of humor. "Mind how you go. They'll write songs about you whether you permit it or not."

"They'll get it wrong," I said.

"They always do," said Aldric, and left.

---

The Hollow Lantern Guild hall smelled the same as it always had: candlewax, cold stone, the sweet rot of old paper. I stood in the archive room while Master Ivenne set the prophecy book on the table between us — lighter now, visibly lighter, its spine slack, its pages blank as fresh snow.

"The charter says the Guild exists to guard the book," she said. "The book is empty. By our own founding law, we are finished."

"That seems dramatic."

"It's *accurate*, which is worse." She traced the cover with one finger, almost fondly. "Do you know why I never stopped you? In the square. I could have. Three of us, one exhausted charmwright. I could have taken the page back and let the boy die and the prophecy complete, and the Guild would have its power restored by morning."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because I read the whole book, Sable. Every night, for forty years. And do you know what I found in the margins, in the oldest hand, the founder's own?" Her eyes were dry and very bright. "*If ever the cost outweighs the keeping, burn the cage and call it mercy.* We were always meant to end it. We just lacked the nerve. Or the reason." She looked at me steadily. "You found the reason."

Outside the archive window, Ashfall went about its evening — lamplighters working the streets, inspectors' whistles sounding almost apologetic now that the emergency ordinances were lapsing, someone in the lane below laughing at something small and stupid and human. A city that had spent a generation holding its breath.

"What happens to us?" I asked. "The Guild."

Ivenne smiled, and for the first time since I'd known her it reached all the way up. "We become what we should have been all along. Teachers. Healers. People who sell charms that actually work, and teach children the difference between a blessing and a cage." She pushed the empty book across the table toward me. "Take it. First blank page of the rest of it. Write something worth guarding."

---

Corin was waiting outside the hall, leaning against the doorpost in the blue hour, wearing clothes I'd bought him that finally fit the century. He'd been quiet all day — the good quiet, the kind we'd learned on the road, two people who no longer needed to fill silence with proof.

"Well?" he said.

"She gave me the book."

"And?"

"And I'm going to start a new one." I weighed it in my hands. Blank pages. No dates, no names, no deaths scheduled in advance. "First entry might be boring. Weather. Train schedules. What you look like when you sleep."

"Scandalous," Corin said. "Utterly unprophetic."

"There's one problem." I turned the book over. "No title. The old one was *The Gilded Oath*. I'm not carrying that forward."

He considered this with the seriousness he brought to maps and pastry selection. Then he took the book from my hands, opened it to the first page, and wrote — in that careful cartographer's script, letters placed like landmarks — a single line. He turned it back so I could read it.

*Unwritten. Two travelers, no destination yet. Conditions favorable.*

"Cartographically speaking," he said, "that's not a title. It's a starting point. Everything else, we survey as we go."

I kissed him there on the Guild steps, in front of the empty hall and the waking lamps and whatever history was bothering to watch, and history — for once — kept its opinions to itself.

"Come on, then," I said, taking his hand. "Let's go get lost somewhere with no verses about it."

And we did.
