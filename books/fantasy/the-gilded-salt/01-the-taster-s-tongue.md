## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The bell above the kitchen door rang at half past five, as it had rung every morning for ten years, and Sebastian Kavanagh was already awake.

He was always already awake. Sleep came to him the way it came to soldiers and to hares — thin, shallow, one ear cocked toward the world — because a man whose work began in his mouth could not afford to let his mouth grow careless overnight. He lay a moment in the narrow dark of his room above the scullery, listening to the palace breathe: the groan of pipes warming themselves, the first clatter of the bakehouse below, the distant cough of the night porter making his last round along the eastern wall. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a kingdom that did not know his name and never would.

He dressed in the dark without needing light. Grey coat, grey waistcoat, grey stockings — grey not by decree but by instinct, the color of things that are meant to be looked through. He tied back his hair with a plain cord, checked that his ledger was in its inner pocket where it always rode against his ribs like a second heart, and went down into the warm yeast-and-iron smell of the palace kitchens.

"Morning, Master Kavanagh." The under-cook, a boy called Pell who could not have been more than sixteen, bobbed his head as he passed with a tray of rolls. "Porridge is on. Cook says the honey's from the new hives at Amberly."

"The new hives," Sebastian repeated, and made a note of it before he had even reached the tasting table. *Amberly honey, first pressing.* It mattered. Everything mattered. That was the whole of his art, if you could call it an art: the patient, unglamorous accumulation of small facts, each one a stitch in a garment of proof that would hold up under any inquiry. If the king sickened after breakfast, Sebastian would be able to say, precisely and under oath, what the king had eaten, where it had come from, who had touched it, and how long it had stood. He kept his ledgers the way other men kept their souls.

The tasting table stood apart from the cooks' benches, behind a low rail of carved oak, as though he were something exhibited. In a sense he was. He sat alone at it every morning while the kitchen roared around him, and every morning the dishes came to him first — not the king's dishes exactly, but their twins, prepared from the same pots by the same hands, set before him on plain pewter while the gilded versions waited under cloths for his verdict.

He tasted the way a jeweler examines stones. First the porridge: barley, cream, a thread of salt, the honey dark and faintly smoky. He held it on his tongue and counted. Ten. Twenty. Thirty. His tongue was scarred in fine white traceries, like cracked porcelain glaze — the record of ten years of small deliberate poisons, each dose a little larger than the last, each one survived and catalogued until his blood had become a museum of everything the kingdom's enemies might try. Aconite, in his third year. Arsenic, slowly, over a winter. The bitter alchemy of yew and the sweeter treachery of oleander. He had been sick more times than he could count, though he had never once been seen to be sick; he had learned to leave the table with a bow and finish his suffering privately, in the dark, ledger open on his knees so he could write down the symptoms even as they wrote themselves across his body.

Nothing in the porridge. He swallowed, rinsed, noted: *Barley porridge, cream, Amberly honey. Clean. Slight bitterness at finish consistent with hive variety, not toxin.*

Then the breads. Then the preserved plums, which he suspected of nothing but tasted anyway, because suspicion was not a mood for him but a discipline. Then the king's morning posset, warmed wine curdled with egg, and here he paused.

There was salt in it. There was always salt in it — a pinch, no more, the cook's quiet signature, drawn from the great blue crock by the hearth. But this morning the posset tasted wrong to him, and it took him three spoonfuls to understand that the wrongness was not in the cup. It was in him. The salt tasted *loud*. It rang on his tongue like a struck bell, harsh and glittering, and he found himself wanting to spit it out, which had never happened before. Salt was the one flavor a taster never tired of; it was the honest taste, the one that could not lie or hide anything beneath itself.

He finished the posset. He declared it clean, because it was clean. But he sat a moment longer than usual with his pen hovering over the page, and then, in the margin beside the entry, in his small precise hand, he wrote:

*Salt tastes louder today. Note for future reference.*

It was such a small thing. He told himself it was such a small thing.

* * *

The king took his breakfast in the morning room, a long chamber facing east over the formal gardens, where the light came in gold and lay across the table like spilled butter. Sebastian stood at his appointed place — three paces behind the king's right shoulder, close enough to step forward, far enough to be furniture. This was the geometry of his life. Three paces. He could have walked those three paces blindfolded, drunk, asleep. He had, in fact, done all three during various crises, though no one had noticed, which was rather the point.

King Aldric was fifty-one years old and had ruled for twenty-six of them, and for the last ten of those years he had been, by every public account, a marvel. The Great Sickness had nearly taken him a decade ago — the whole court remembered the season of black banners and whispered prayers, the physicians coming and going with faces like closed doors. And then, impossibly, the king had recovered. Not merely recovered but *bloomed*. The man who rose from that sickbed was vigorous where he had been frail, decisive where he had once agonized, cheerful in a way that some found inspiring and others, in unguarded moments, found slightly strange — the way a portrait can be strange when the painter has gotten every feature right and still missed the person entirely.

Sebastian had been appointed six months after the recovery. He had never known the earlier king except through the portraits in the long gallery and the anecdotes of old servants, and he thought of this sometimes: that he served a king he had no way to compare against anyone. A man is only strange next to what he used to be.

This morning the king ate heartily. He had the venison pie — Sebastian had tasted it twice, once hot and once cold, because game birds and game meats got the double tasting — and the plums, and the posset, and he talked between bites in his easy rolling voice about the harvest festival, about the new bridge at Marlow Ford, about whether the ambassador from Vessany was a fool or merely played one at court.

"And what do you say, Master Kavanagh?" the king asked suddenly, turning. "You've tasted everything on this table and lived. Surely that qualifies you to have opinions."

It was a joke the king made perhaps once a month, always with the same warmth, always as though for the first time. Sebastian bowed. "I only have opinions about what might kill Your Majesty, sire. Everything else on this table is safe."

The king laughed, delighted, as he always did. "A philosopher! Cook, we must poison him something interesting sometime, just to hear what he says." He raised his posset cup — and stopped.

Sebastian saw it happen. Saw the cup reach the king's lips and halt there, trembling almost imperceptibly, the way a horse halts at a snake it has not yet consciously seen. The king lowered the cup. He looked into it. His face, for the space of two heartbeats, was not the king's face at all — it was blank, and young, and lost, like a man waking in an unfamiliar room.

"Sire?" Sebastian said quietly.

"Nothing." The king smiled, and the smile settled back onto his features like a mask being tied on. "The posset's gone cold, that's all. Take it away."

It was not cold. Sebastian had tasted it four minutes earlier and it had been the exact temperature it always was. He stepped forward anyway, removed the cup with both hands and a bow, and retreated to his three paces, and inside his chest something small and cold turned over, the way a key turns in a lock you didn't know your house had.

Because he had seen the king do this before. Not often — five times in ten years, and he had the dates in his ledger, because he had the *everything* in his ledger. Always at the moment the food touched the king's mouth. Always that same flicker of blankness, that stranger's pause, gone before it could be named. He had recorded it each time under a heading he had invented himself and shown to no one: *Episode. Momentary aversion at tasting. No pattern established.*

Five entries in ten years. And now, this morning, the sixth — and for the first time, Sebastian noticed what connected them, because this morning his own tongue had flinched from the same thing the king's mouth refused.

Salt. Every episode, there was salt somewhere on the table. The posset. Once, the salted plums. Once, merely the salt cellar sitting near the king's hand, untouched, and the king had moved it away with two fingers, delicately, the way one moves a dead moth from a book.

He wrote it down that evening, in the little room above the scullery, by the light of a tallow candle. He wrote it the way he wrote everything: plainly, without adjectives, as though fear were a spice that spoiled the record. *Sixth episode. King declined posset, citing temperature (inaccurate). Common element across all six episodes: presence of salt. Hypothesis: none.*

He sat looking at the word *none* for a long time. Then, because the discipline of ten years was stronger than the unease of one evening, he blew out the candle and went to bed, and slept the thin hare's sleep, one ear cocked toward the ordinary sounds of the palace breathing.

* * *

Three days later, the market day, the thing happened that ended the comfortable arithmetic of his life — though of course he did not know it yet. On that morning he knew only that the king had refused salt twice more, and that Sebastian's own tongue had begun to find the taste unbearable, ringing and glittering and *wrong*, as though some vast quantity of it were dissolving somewhere out beyond the walls, beyond the city, seeping up through the earth into every well and loaf and cup in the kingdom.

But that is getting ahead. Let the record show, as his ledgers would demand, how the last ordinary morning actually went.

He rose at half past five. He dressed in grey. He tasted the porridge, the breads, the plums, the posset — and paused over the posset, because the salt in it had begun to taste not loud now but *hungry*, which made no sense and which he therefore did not write down, a failure of discipline he would regret for the rest of his life. He declared the meal clean. He stood at his three paces while the king ate and joked and laughed, and the king drank the posset this time, all of it, grimacing only slightly, like a man taking medicine.

"You're quiet today, Master Kavanagh," the king observed, wiping his beard. "Quieter than usual, I mean. Which for you is saying something."

"I'm always quiet, sire. It's the job."

"It is the job," the king agreed, and there was something in the way he said it — a small, odd emphasis, as though the words meant more than they said — that Sebastian would turn over in his mind many times afterward, in darker rooms than this one. "Well. Keep eating, keep living, keep writing your little books. The kingdom runs on men like you and never thinks to thank them."

"That's rather the point of men like me, sire."

The king laughed, and the laugh was warm and easy and perfect, and Sebastian bowed, and the morning went on.

Afterward he made his rounds of the pantries as he did every week — checking seals, checking dates, checking the salt crock by the hearth, which he now looked at longer than he needed to. The cook caught him staring.

"Something wrong with my salt, Master Kavanagh?"

"No," Sebastian said. "No, it's fine salt. Very fine."

And it was. It was the finest salt in the kingdom, white as frost, and it sat in its blue crock doing nothing at all, being only what it was, and yet he felt — standing there in the warm kitchen with the bread rising and the boys shouting and the whole enormous machine of the palace grinding through another blameless morning — he felt the way a man feels when the floor of a familiar room gives the smallest possible creak beneath his feet. Not a collapse. Not even a crack. Just a sound where there had never been a sound before, in a place he had trusted absolutely.

That evening he entered the final line of the week in his ledger, in his small precise hand, and read back over the seven days, and found that his neat columns had begun to describe a shape he did not like. Six episodes of aversion in ten years; three in one week. His own tongue, changing. The king's laughter, perfect and frequent and somehow, lately, always pitched at exactly the same warmth, like a recording, like a bell that could only ever ring one note.

He closed the ledger. He told himself, as he had told himself for ten years, that he was a man who noticed things so that other people would not have to, and that noticing was not the same as fearing.

Outside his window, far off across the rooftops, the market district was lighting its lamps for the evening, and somewhere down in those bright crowded streets a courier was saddling a horse with a wax-sealed tube strapped to his chest, riding through the dark toward a taster who did not yet know he was waiting — and Sebastian blew out his candle, and slept, and dreamed, for the first time in years, of the sea.
