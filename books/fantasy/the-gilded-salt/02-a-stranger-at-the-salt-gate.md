## 2. A Stranger at the Salt Gate

The market district smelled of wet wool and frying onions, and Sebastian Kavanagh moved through it the way he moved through everything: without leaving a mark.

He had learned early that invisibility was not a gift but a discipline. It required maintenance, like a garden. One did not simply *be* forgettable; one practiced it. He wore gray because gray gave the eye nothing to argue with. He walked at the pace of a man going nowhere in particular and everywhere unremarkable. When vendors called out to him, he bought something small—a twist of dried plums, a stub of tallow candle—and paid exactly what was asked, no haggling, because haggling made a man memorable. He tipped his head to the fishwife who remembered everyone's name and never offered his own, and she filled the silence with her own voice, as people always did, and by the time she'd finished telling him about her daughter's betrothal he had become, in her mind, some vague pleasant shape that had once stood near her stall.

Ten years of this. Ten years of being the man nobody looked at twice, and he had come to love it the way other men loved their wives or their wine—deeply, specifically, with an appreciation refined by long familiarity.

It was Thursday. Thursdays were market days, which meant the palace kitchens received their week's provisions, which meant Sebastian spent the morning walking the stalls with Master Corvel's list, tasting samples before they were carted up to the citadel. It was tedious work and he was excellent at it. The grain merchant's rye had a faint bitterness at the back of the throat—not poison, just mold near the sack seams; rejected. The honey from the southern apiaries was clean but thin; accepted with a note. A wheel of white cheese had been rubbed with something resinous to disguise its age; Sebastian caught it in half a breath, spat neatly into the gutter, and wrote *deceitful* in his ledger beside the cheesemonger's name, which would cost the man a fine and earn Sebastian nothing but the quiet satisfaction of precision.

His ledger went everywhere with him. Leather-bound, brass-cornered, its pages ruled in his own careful hand. *Thursday, 14th of the Reaper's Month.* Below it, columns: item, vendor, taste, verdict. Some men kept diaries of their feelings. Sebastian kept a diary of everything that had ever entered his mouth, and it had saved his life four hundred times.

Because here was the truth of Sebastian Kavanagh, known to perhaps three living people: things that killed other men did very little to him. Not nothing—he felt them, catalogued them, suffered their smaller cousins—but the killing edge was blunted in him, worn smooth by some accident of blood. Arsenic gave him a headache and a strange metallic singing on his tongue. Nightshade made the world go soft and blue for an hour. Wolfsbane, once, in his apprenticeship, had stopped his heart for the length of three prayers before it thought better of the matter and let him live. He had built his immunity dish by dish, year by year, a mason builds a wall—one stone at a time, each one laid in fear, each one holding.

So when the stranger fell against him at the mouth of Tanner's Row, Sebastian's first thought—shameful, immediate, honest—was: *I have not tasted anything today that could cause this.*

The man came out of the crowd sideways, like a ship listing. He was young, twenty perhaps, dressed in the dust-colored traveling clothes of a courier, and the front of his shirt was soaked so thoroughly dark that Sebastian's second thought arrived cold and professional: *that is too much blood to be alive, and yet.*

"Sir—" Sebastian began, in the tone he used for beggars and drunks, the tone that ended conversations.

The man's hand closed around Sebastian's wrist with shocking strength. His fingers were cold. His palm was slick. And pressed between their two bodies, wedged into Sebastian's grip before he could decide whether to accept it, was a tube of dark wood capped at both ends with wax—the deep oxblood red of sealing wax, stamped with a sigil Sebastian did not recognize: a circle broken by a vertical line, like a door seen edge-on.

"The Hollow Court remembers," the courier said.

His voice was barely a voice. It was the sound breath makes when it has to climb over blood to get out. His eyes were fixed on Sebastian's face with an intensity that made the skin crawl, because no one looked at Sebastian like that. No one looked at Sebastian at all.

"You're—" The courier coughed, and the cough was wet and wrong. "You're the one. I was told—gray coat, ledger, walks like a ghost. You're the one."

"I think you're mistaken." Sebastian heard his own voice go flat and pleasant, the voice he used on inquisitors' clerks and suspicious stewards. "I'm a provisioner. Let me find a physician—"

"There's no time." The man's knees buckled. Sebastian caught him—had no choice; the grip on his wrist dragged them both down together—and lowered him against the shuttered front of a chandler's shop as the market flowed around them, parting without interest, the way crowds do around dying things. Someone would remember later, maybe. Someone would say *a man collapsed near the chandler's*, and no one would recall who had held him.

That was how it worked. That was how Sebastian had arranged his whole life to work.

But the courier was looking at him as though Sebastian were the only solid thing in a dissolving world, and he said, with terrible clarity: "They'll ask if you saw me. Say no. Say you never saw me. You're good at that—I can see you're good at that." A ghost of a smile. "It's why they chose you."

"Chose me? Who chose—"

"Wax," the man said. "Don't break the wax. It knows whose hands are meant for it. Press it to your tongue if—if you need to see. Only your tongue." His hand slid down Sebastian's arm, leaving a dark smear. "The Hollow Court remembers. Tell them the salt remembers too. They'll understand. The ones left will understand."

And then, because the world is efficient about such things, the man died.

It was not dramatic. There was no last shudder, no loosening of the grip. His eyes simply lost their focus the way a lamp loses its flame, and his weight settled more fully against the shutters, and the market noise rushed back in around the little silence of him like water around a dropped stone.

Sebastian knelt there for the length of four heartbeats, doing arithmetic. Blood on his sleeve, his cuff, the heel of his right hand. A dead courier against his shoulder. A sealed tube hidden—when had he done it? The man had pressed it there, in those first seconds, tucked beneath Sebastian's coat against his ribs, and Sebastian had allowed it, had *assisted* it, with the smooth unconscious competence of a man who had spent ten years making things disappear into his person unnoticed. Old habit. Taster's habit. You learn to palm what must not be seen.

*I am now a man with evidence on his body,* he thought, with the strange calm that had carried him through every poisoning. *Panic later. Inventory now.*

He stood. He arranged the courier's limbs with a propriety that would look like decency to any observer. He raised his voice to the proper pitch of alarmed civility—"A man! Here! Someone fetch the watch!"—and became instantly, perfectly, the Good Citizen Who Found Him, a role he slipped into as easily as his gray coat. Three shopkeepers converged. A woman began to weep preemptively, in the way of women who enjoy weeping. And through it all Sebastian kept his hands visible, his face appropriately troubled, and his mind running cold and fast along a single track:

*They arrive too quickly. Watch when they arrive. Too quickly means they were already close. Already close means they knew where he would fall. Knew where he would fall means they were following him. Following him means they know what he carried. And what he carried is now against my ribs, warm from my own body heat, and every second I stand here performing grief is a second it spends getting closer to being mine.*

They came in less than six minutes.

Sebastian counted. He couldn't help counting; counting was his spine. Six minutes from his shout to the arrival of four men in the livery of the Crown Inquisition—charcoal-gray cloaks, silver salt-cellars embroidered at the collar, the new fashion since the king's recovery. Six minutes across half the market district, on a crowded Thursday. Either the watch had grown wings, or these men had been within two streets of a dying courier whose death they were now, presumably, investigating.

Their leader was tall and dry-voiced, with a face like a folded map and eyes the color of dishwater, and he did not look at the corpse first. He looked at Sebastian. He looked at Sebastian's sleeves.

"You found him?"

"I did, sir. He came out of the crowd and fell. I called for help." Sebastian gestured with his stained right hand, openly, honestly, letting the man see the blood and draw whatever conclusions came naturally. Never hide what can be explained. Explain what cannot be hidden. "I'm afraid I've bled on myself catching him. I hope that's not improper."

"It's not improper." The inquisitor crouched by the body with the unhurried economy of a man who had knelt beside many corpses. He turned one cold hand over. Ran a thumb along the inside of the wrist. Whatever he found there made his mouth tighten by a degree too small for most witnesses to register. Sebastian registered it. "Did he say anything? Before the end. Dying men often talk."

"He said—" Sebastian let his brow crease, let confusion assemble itself honestly on his features, because the best lies are mostly true. "He said 'the hollow.' Or 'hollowed.' I couldn't tell. He was trying to say something to me and it wouldn't come. Then he said—" Sebastian paused, and the pause was real, because this next part was true and true things are dangerous. "He said I was mistaken for someone else. That he'd grabbed the wrong man."

The inquisitor's dishwater eyes came up slowly.

"Did he."

"That's what I understood, sir. I told him I was a provisioner for the palace kitchens. He seemed disappointed." Sebastian shrugged, a small apologetic movement. "Dying men reach for whoever's nearest, I suppose. I'm sorry I couldn't make more sense of it."

For a long moment the market noise seemed to arrange itself around the silence between them. The inquisitor studied Sebastian's face the way Sebastian himself studied a suspect dish: patiently, professionally, waiting for the flavor to declare itself.

Then, mercifully, another inquisitor called from the body—"Nothing on him, sir. Purse cut away. Boots gone. Street thieves got here first"—and the moment broke like a thread under tension.

"Thieves," the leader repeated, without inflection. He rose. He produced a small notebook of his own, and Sebastian felt an unexpected kinship of craft even as dread pooled cold in his stomach. "Your name, provisioner."

"Kavanagh. Sebastian Kavanagh."

"House?"

"Palace kitchens. Under Master Corvel. I taste the provisions." He said it the way he always said it, flatly, boringly, the sentence that had ended a thousand conversations. *Food-taster.* Nothing emptied a room of interest faster.

But the inquisitor didn't glaze over. Something flickered instead—recognition, or its cousin—and he wrote the name down with care, letter by letter, as though spelling mattered.

"A taster," he said. "How interesting. How long?"

"Ten years, sir."

"And in ten years, I imagine you've learned every way a man can be poisoned." He closed the notebook. "You'll be available should we have further questions. The palace keeps its servants findable, I assume."

"The palace keeps everything findable, sir."

"Mm." The inquisitor looked at him one last time—that long, patient, flavor-waiting look—and then, unexpectedly, extended his hand. Sebastian took it. The man's grip was dry and cool and entirely ordinary, and yet Sebastian's skin prickled where they touched, some animal instinct older than his training whispering *this hand has sorted many men into piles.*

"Master Kavanagh. A pleasure. Brann. Inquisitor Brann." He smiled, and it was a courteous, reasonable smile, and it frightened Sebastian more than anything the dead courier had said. "Do take care on your walk home. The market's been full of strangers lately. One can never be sure what they might be carrying."

* * *

Sebastian did not run. Running was memorable. He walked home at his usual pace, bought his usual twist of dried plums from a different stall than usual—small deviations, invisible deviations—and let himself into his rooms above the cooper's yard with hands that only began to shake once the bolt was shot.

He sat on his bed. He took out the tube.

Up close, in the gray light through his one window, it was beautiful in a severe way: dark wood polished by handling, the wax seals unbroken, that strange sigil—a door seen edge-on—stamped deep in oxblood. It weighed almost nothing. It hummed almost nothing. But when he held it, the hair on his forearms lifted, and somewhere behind his sternum an old instinct stirred, the same instinct that had made him spit out the deceitful cheese, the instinct that said: *this thing is not inert. This thing is waiting.*

*Press it to your tongue if you need to see. Only your tongue.*

"Not tonight," he said aloud, and his voice sounded strange in the small room, rougher than he'd meant it. He wrapped the tube in oilcloth and hid it inside the false bottom of his ledger case, beneath ten years of columns, beneath the most boring object in the kingdom—which was, he reflected, exactly where he would have hidden it, had he been the dying sort.

Then he cleaned the blood from his sleeve, boiled his cuff, ate a supper of bread and hard cheese that he tasted automatically, methodically, noting the cheese was fine and slightly over-salted—

—and stopped, fork halfway to his mouth.

Over-salted. Everything lately was over-salted. The kitchen brines had doubled since midwinter. The king had developed his aversion—Sebastian had noted it in the ledger weeks ago, *H.M. refuses the salt cellar; orders all dishes unsalted; cooks bewildered*—and the kitchens, chasing the royal palate, had swung the whole palace the other way, and now everything Sebastian ate tasted of the sea.

*Aversion to salt,* he had written, in his neat hand, because it was his job to write things down. He had thought nothing of it. Kings were eccentric. Recovery changed appetites.

But a dead courier had died saying *the Hollow Court remembers*, and had died saying *tell them the salt remembers too*, and Sebastian sat in his narrow room above the cooper's yard with salt on his tongue and the beginning of a terrible understanding assembling itself in the dark behind his eyes, the way a shape assembles in fog: not seen, only *implied*.

He opened the ledger instead. He turned to the fresh page. He wrote, in his steadiest hand:

*Thursday, 14th. Market day. Rye rejected (mold). Honey accepted (thin). Cheese rejected (resin, deceitful).*

Then, after a moment, below the columns, in the margin where no official eye would ever look:

*A man died holding my wrist today. He said I was chosen. I said nothing worth recording.*

He looked at that sentence for a long time. It was the first dishonest entry in ten years of ledgers, because it was not true: he had plenty worth recording, and he had recorded none of it, and they both knew it, he and the book.

Outside, the evening bell rang from the citadel, and beneath it—faint, rhythmic, unmistakable to a man who had spent a decade listening to the city the way he listened to sauces—came the sound of boots. Many boots. Moving not past the cooper's yard but toward it, unhurried, in step, the way men move when they are not searching but *collecting*.

Sebastian blew out his candle, put his back to the wall, and held the sealed tube against his chest in the dark, feeling it wait.

Six minutes, he thought. They had come in six minutes. He wondered how long they would take to climb one staircase.

He did not have to wonder long. There came a knock at his door—measured, courteous, patient as a flavor about to declare itself—and Inquisitor Brann's dry voice said pleasantly through the wood:

"Master Kavanagh. Forgive the hour. Might we continue our conversation?"
