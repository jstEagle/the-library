## 4. The Alchemist Who Owes Everyone

The map's first coordinate was a street name that did not exist.

I checked three times. Once by candlelight in my rooms above the taster's quarters, once at dawn against the city's official registry—a ledger I had bribed a clerk to let me copy years ago, because a man who survives on precision keeps copies of everything—and once from the roof of the chandlery where I sometimes ate breakfast, looking down at the crooked seam of buildings where Candlewick Lane should have run. The registry said Candlewick Lane had been renamed after the fire of Aldric's fourth year. The map disagreed. The map, written in ink that moved like oil on water when I wasn't looking directly at it, insisted that Candlewick Lane was still there, tucked behind the tanneries like a secret kept so long even the city had forgotten it was keeping one.

I went anyway. I told myself it was diligence. A taster who notices an anomaly in the king's relationship with salt does not simply set down his pen; he investigates, quietly, the way he does everything. That was the story I fed myself as I dressed in my dullest coat and took the long way through the fish market, past stalls where brine hung in the air thick enough to chew, and told no one where I was going.

That was the last morning for a long time when I was only myself.

* * *

Candlewick Lane existed the way a held breath exists. You found it by walking between two tannery walls until the sound of the city thinned, and then there it was: a slot of shadow barely wide enough for a handcart, cobbled with stones worn to the smoothness of old teeth. No signs. No windows at street level. But smoke rose from somewhere—sweet smoke, not woodsmoke, with an undertone like burnt sugar and something mineral beneath it, something that made my back teeth ache in a way I recognized from my work.

Salt being heated. Or salt being *unmade*. My tongue knew the difference before my mind caught up, which is the trouble and the gift of a tongue trained ten years to know things before it should.

The door at the end of the lane was black oak, unmarked except for a small brass plate polished blank. I knocked the way couriers knock—three quick, one slow—the pattern still living in my hands from the dead man's grip on my wrist. Nothing. I tried again. From inside came a voice like dry paper tearing:

"If you're selling, the answer is no. If you're collecting debts, the answer is also no, and also you're too late, there's nothing left to take. If you're either of those things and you knock again, I have a kettle of vitriol with your name on it."

"Neither," I said. "I'm following a map."

Silence. Long enough that I counted my own heartbeats—eleven of them—before the voice returned, closer now, pressed against the door itself.

"What kind of map?"

"The kind that tastes me back."

Another silence. Then bolts—I counted four—drawn one after another, each with the reluctant scrape of a mechanism that hadn't been asked to work in years. The door opened six inches. One eye appeared in the gap, brown-ringed, bloodshot, magnified slightly behind a cracked lens strapped to her head like a second face.

"You're not guild," she said.

"No."

"You're not inquisitor. Inquisitors don't say 'tastes me back.' They don't think in tongues." The eye narrowed. "What are you?"

"A food-taster," I said. "Royal. Ten years."

The door opened another inch. "Show me your hands."

I showed her. She looked at them the way a horse-trader looks at teeth—turning them over with fingers that smelled of copper and camphor, reading the calluses, the faint chemical stains around the nails that no amount of scrubbing ever fully lifted. Then, without warning, she gripped my jaw and tilted my face toward the light.

"Tongue."

I showed her that too. The scars. The fine white lattice across its surface where a decade of small poisons had been survived, dish by dish, dose by dose, each one leaving its signature the way frost leaves ferns on glass.

She released me and stepped back, and the door swung wide, and I saw the cellar behind her—or rather, saw that the shop above had been swallowed by the cellar below, stairs descending into a room lit by dozens of glass vessels glowing with their own soft light. Alembics. Retorts. Racks of stoppered vials labeled in a cramped, furious hand. And everywhere, everywhere, salt: dishes of it, blocks of it carved into shapes, jars of it suspended in oils, a whole wall of it ground fine and sorted by color into rows of little drawers like a cabinet of impossible spices.

"Odile Vane," she said, not offering her hand. "Formerly of the Gilded Salt. Currently of nowhere, which is a promotion, if you're wondering." She pulled the lens up off her eyes and rubbed the red grooves they'd left. "You'd better come down before someone sees you standing in my doorway like an apology."

* * *

Her laboratory notes, which I would read much later—when she was asleep, or pretending to be, in the weeks after everything burned—begin, in that period, like this:

*Day 411 since the last honest customer. The boy from the tanner's bought his usual—three drops of borrowed courage, enough to ask the miller's daughter to dance without shaking. He'll pay me in bread crusts and gossip. Fine. Gossip is currency too.*

*A man came today claiming to follow a map. Tongue like lacework. Ten years of poisons layered in him like sedimentary rock, and he walks around upright as if that were normal. He says he's nobody. Nobody doesn't survive what's written on that tongue. Nobody isn't standing in my cellar asking questions about the Hollow Court with a dead courier's seal wax still under his fingernails.*

*I lied to him twice. I'm writing this down so I remember which lies.*

*First lie: that I don't recognize the seal. Second lie: that I don't owe the guild anything. Both are load-bearing. Pray neither collapses while he's standing under them.*

* * *

In the cellar, over a kettle of something that smelled like rain on hot stone, I told her as much of the truth as I could bear to tell. The courier dying against me in the market district. The tube. The words—"the Hollow Court remembers"—and how they had sat in my ears for three days like a stone swallowed wrong. I did not show her the map itself. Some instinct, some taster's caution, kept my hand flat over my breast pocket.

She listened while stirring, never stirring, stirring again—a woman whose hands could not be idle any more than her mouth could be kind.

"Hollow Court," she repeated at last, and laughed, and the laugh had rust in it. "You know what people pay me for? Down here, in this lovely hole?" She gestured at her vials. "Memories. Counterfeit ones. A widow wants one more evening with her husband—I brew her an hour of him, warm and imperfect, better than the real thing because I can leave out the coughing. A man wants to forget a debt, or a daughter, or a war. I sell forgetting by the dram. It's honest work, comparatively speaking, because at least I tell them it's false." She looked at me over the steam. "The guild didn't tell anyone anything. The guild traded in the real thing. Years. Names. Faces. Grief. And every trade went into a ledger, and every ledger went—" She stopped herself. Her jaw worked. "Why are you here?"

"The map has coordinates. This is the first one."

"I'm not a coordinate. I'm a person. There's a difference the guild never learned." She set down the spoon with exaggerated care, the way angry people do when they're trying not to throw things. "Whatever you think I can do for you, I can't. I owed the guild everything and paid it everything and what it left me was this cellar and these hands and a name that gets people hanged. Find yourself another alchemist."

"There are no other alchemists. The map says so. It says—" I hesitated, then decided precision was worth more than prudence now. "It lists the surviving members. There are four names. Yours is first. Two are crossed out. The third is a cartographer." Her face did something complicated and terrible at that word. "And the fourth entry just says *the key*, with no name at all."

"And you think that's you."

"I think the map tasted me back. I think it won't open for anyone else. I think a man died holding it, and his last act was to give it to a stranger, which means he had no one left to give it to, which means everyone else is dead." I heard my own voice go quiet and level, the voice I used reporting to the king's chamberlain: *nothing amiss today, my lord.* "I don't want any of this. I want my ledgers and my routine and my invisibility. But the inquisitors came to the market within minutes of that man dying. Minutes. Someone knew exactly where he'd fall. And if they can find him, they can find the map, and if they find the map, they find the names, and yours is first."

"So you're here to warn me. How noble." She snorted. "Or you're here because you need me and you've dressed the need up as charity. Also fine. I respect a well-made lie more than a badly-made truth." She turned away, busying herself with a rack of vials, sliding each one a half-inch along its shelf as though the world could be ordered by friction. "Go home, taster. Drink your king's soup. Forget my face—it shouldn't be hard, I hear forgettability runs in certain families."

That last sentence landed strangely, like a key turning in a lock I hadn't known was there. But before I could ask what she meant, we both heard it.

Boots. Above us. Not one pair—many, moving with the coordinated weight of men who had done this before. Then the particular sound I will hear for the rest of my life, whatever length that turns out to be: the dry *whump* of pitch catching flame.

"They torched the shop," Odile said, in a voice of pure, astonished offense, as though the inquisitors had broken an etiquette rather than committed arson. "They torched my *shop*."

Smoke began threading down the stairwell almost immediately, sweet and then acrid. Through the floorboards came shouting—the formal, liturgical shouting of inquisitors at work: *"By order of the Crown, in the matter of forbidden trades—"*

"They're not here for you specifically," I said, understanding arriving cold. "They're burning the addresses. All of them. The list on the map—they have their own copy, and they're working through it."

"Then they'll be at the cartographer's next, and the crossing-out will be theirs instead of mine." She was already moving, snatching vials off shelves with a speed that contradicted every other thing about her, stuffing them into a leather satchel with the tenderness of a woman packing children. "Well. Congratulations, taster. You wanted an ally. Ruin makes excellent glue."

"I didn't—"

"You did. Everyone does. It's how the guild recruited too, incidentally—find people at the moment the world burns their address and offer them a new one." She slung the satchel across her back, grabbed a second bag, threw it at my chest. "Carry that. Drop it and I'll feed you something you'll regret for a week; I know exactly what your immunity covers and it isn't my specialty."

There was a back way, of course. There is always a back way in a city built by smugglers and improved upon by liars. It ran through her coal cellar and out through a hatch into the tannery yard, and as we climbed through it, the heat of her burning shop pressed against our backs like a hand hurrying us along. At the top of the ladder she paused, one leg in the daylight, and looked back down at her cellar—at the wall of sorted salts, the racks of counterfeit evenings, eleven years of exile arranged just so—and I watched her decide, in real time, not to grieve yet. Grief was a luxury for later. Later was a country she clearly intended to reach.

"Well?" she said, seeing me watch her. "Say it. Everyone says it eventually."

"Say what?"

"*Thank you.* For coming to warn me. For dragging me out." She pulled herself up into the light and stood silhouetted against the smoke, a thin woman with a cracked lens pushed up on her forehead and a satchel full of other people's memories riding her spine. "Don't thank me yet. I know things about that map, taster. Things that will make you wish the inquisitors had found you first. When you hear them—and you will hear them tonight, because I refuse to carry them alone—you'll understand why the guild crossed out two names before yours ever touched that tube."

We went up into the tannery yard, into the reek and the ordinary noise of the city, two fugitives smelling of smoke among a hundred people smelling of smoke, invisible for the moment even to ourselves.

Behind us, Candlewick Lane burned. Ahead of us lay the flooded quarter, and a cartographer's daughter, and the third coordinate on a map that tasted of salt and memory.

And in my pocket, against my heart, the parchment shifted its ink, very slightly, like something settling in its sleep.

* * *

From Odile Vane's laboratory notes, undated, written that night in whatever room we found:

*He didn't ask what I meant. Not once, all day, though I watched the question eat at him like acid through a stoppered flask. Either he has discipline or he has fear, and in my experience the two look identical from the outside.*

*Tomorrow I tell him about the night the king recovered. About what the guild took, and what filled the space, and why the ledger entry for that date is signed in salt instead of ink.*

*Let him sleep first. It's the last good night he gets for a while.*

*God forgive me. I'm starting to like him.*
