## 6. The Cartographer's Daughter

The flooded quarter had been a neighborhood once. Now it was a memory of one—rooftops rising from green water like the backs of drowned cattle, bell towers leaning at angles that defied prayer, and bridges that connected windows to nothing at all. The city had stopped maintaining the levees the year King Aldric "recovered," and the river had taken the low streets as its due, patient and total, until three thousand souls moved uphill or under.

Odile said the guild had once petitioned the crown for the coin to save it. She said this in the flat voice she used for things that hurt her, rowing us through a street that had become a canal, past a bakery whose ovens still stood black above the waterline like altars to some forgotten domestic god.

"They were told the treasury was strained," she said. "By a king who could not stop weeping long enough to sign anything."

I dipped my oar and said nothing. Ten years of tasting had taught me when a silence was more useful than a sentence.

Sefa lived—or so the map insisted—in the quarter itself. That detail had unsettled Odile when we'd decoded it on the second night out of her burning cellar: no one sane lived in the flood. But the ink had been unambiguous, curling through the parchment in that slow, deliberate way it had, spelling out an address in a district where addresses no longer existed. *Where the baker's oven stands black, follow the rope-walk east. The daughter keeps the high room. Bring flour.*

*Bring flour.* I had turned that instruction over more times than any other on our journey. It read like a recipe. Perhaps, I thought now, watching Odile measure out our last sack into the boat's bow, it was one.

The rope-walk was exactly what its name promised: a long covered gallery strung between two towers where ropes had once been twisted from hemp. Its walkway stood above the waterline still, sagging but sound, and someone had hung lanterns along it at intervals—not lit, just hung, like a promise kept against hope. At its far end rose the high room: a garret built onto the roof of a granary, with a chimney breathing a thin gray thread of smoke into the evening air.

Someone was home. Someone was alive. In a drowned district, that alone felt like a miracle performed quietly, without witnesses.

Odile shipped her oars. "You knock," she said. "She won't know my face. And if her father told her about me—" She didn't finish. Her hands, wrapped around the gunwale, had gone white-knuckled. I understood then that there were entries in the guild ledgers even she hadn't wanted to read twice.

I climbed the ladder alone.

* * *

The door opened before I knocked.

She was small—smaller than I expected, though I couldn't have said what I expected. Seventeen perhaps, or twenty; hunger and work blur ages. She wore a man's coat cut down badly at the shoulders and boots two sizes too large, stuffed with straw at the toes. Her hair was bound back with twine. And her eyes—

Her eyes went straight past my face to the tube slung across my back, and widened, and filled, and did not spill over. I watched her decide, all in the space of a breath, not to cry. It was such a practiced decision, so worn smooth with use, that I knew she made it often.

"You're Sefa," I said. Stupidly. As if names mattered here.

She shook her head—no—and stepped aside to let me in.

Inside, the high room was warm in a way rooms only get when someone tends them fiercely against great indifference. A iron stove. A bed with a quilt stitched from sailcloth scraps. Shelves of jars: rainwater, dried fish, pickled reed-shoots. And dominating everything, a table.

It was a beautiful table, absurdly beautiful for a garret in a drowned city—driftwood legs, a top of planed planks rubbed pale with use, scrubbed to a shine that caught the stove-light. On the wall above it hung tools: dividers, calipers, a magnifying lens in a brass ring, brushes of every size, and a framed certificate bearing a wax seal I recognized now, having seen its twin pressed into the courier's tube. *Master Cartographer Elyan Sef. By right of craft and chart, keeper of the guild's true ways.*

Below the frame, drawn directly onto the wall in charcoal, was a map of the flooded quarter so exact I caught my breath. Every rooftop. Every submerged lane rendered as a dotted line beneath a wash of blue-gray. Little annotations in a fine hand: *depth at spring tide. safe crossing, dry summer. family of four, third house, gone south.* It wasn't a map. It was a ledger—the same instinct that lived in me, the need to write the world down precisely enough that it couldn't lie—but drawn instead of written, and kept by someone who had lost almost everyone it recorded.

Sefa followed my gaze to the wall, then touched her own throat briefly—a gesture I would learn meant *my father taught me this*—and held up one finger. *Wait.*

She went to the shelf, took down a crock, and poured white flour across the pale wood of the table in a thin, controlled stream, tilting the crock with the ease of long practice. Then she looked at me expectantly.

"I don't—" I began, and stopped. *Bring flour.* I fumbled the sack from my shoulder and set it beside her crock.

She nodded once, briskly, businesslike. Then she reached out, took my hand—her fingers were flour-soft and startlingly strong—and drew me to stand at the table's edge, facing the spread powder the way a priest faces an altar.

And she began to draw.

I have seen cartographers at work since. I have seen the king's own surveyors with their instruments of brass and glass. None of them worked like this. Sefa's finger moved through the flour the way a swimmer moves through still water—without hesitation, without correction, without a single wasted stroke. Lines appeared. Rivers. A coastline. Mountains rendered in quick hatch-strokes. A city grew under her fingertip in the space of a minute, complete with walls and gates and the little starred mark surveyors use for a palace.

It was the capital. Drawn from memory, in flour, by a girl who had never, as far as I knew, left this garret in years.

Then she swept half of it away with her palm—gently, the way you'd close a dead man's eyes—and drew again.

This time the lines came slower. Deeper. She pressed harder, and the flour parted to reveal dark wood beneath, so the drawing seemed to rise out of the table rather than sit upon it. A mountain range I recognized from our flight north. The tollbridge. The village with the widow's house. And threading through all of it, connecting them in a route neither Odile nor I had ever suspected, a line that curved south, and south again, and ended—

At the palace. At the starred mark. Beneath it, she tapped twice, and drew a small descending spiral, and a chamber shaped like a heart.

I stared at it. The shifting ink on the courier's map had never shown us a destination—only coordinates, waypoints, a trail of trades. But here, drawn in flour by a mute girl in a drowned city, was the shape of the whole journey, completed. The Hollow Court. Under the palace. We had been fleeing away from the thing we were walking toward.

"How," I said, and my voice cracked on it. "How do you know this?"

Sefa looked at me with something old and tired moving behind her young face. Then she pointed at the flour-map, at the spiral beneath the palace, and drew in the air with one finger the shape of a key turning.

Her father had been the guild's master cartographer. He had mapped the Hollow Court itself—had walked its salt corridors and charted them, because someone in the guild always had to know where the traded things were kept. And before the inquisitors came for him, he had sat his daughter at this table and taught her every line he'd ever drawn. Not on paper. Paper burned; paper could be seized. In her. In the one archive the impostor couldn't raid, because no one thought to look inside a silent girl's head.

I understood then why she couldn't speak. Not the mechanism—Odile would later explain that her voice had been part of her father's payment, a cruelty I still can't write about without my jaw tightening—but the sense of it. What use is speech, when your whole inheritance is a language of lines? She had been made into a map herself. A living chart of everything the crown wanted erased.

I sat down slowly at her table, careful of the flour, and did the only thing I could think of to do.

I took her crock and, clumsily, in my taster's precise hand, drew beside her capital a small figure standing at a palace gate. Then I drew a second figure beside it. Then a third, smaller, with wild hair. I pointed at each in turn: myself. Odile. Her.

We are coming back for what's below. Will you show us the way?

Sefa studied my terrible drawing for a long moment. Then, without warning, she laughed.

It was a strange sound—rusty, half-formed, more breath than voice, the laugh of someone who hadn't used the machinery of laughter in years. But it was unmistakably a laugh, and it transformed her face entirely, and something in my chest that had been clenched since the courier died against me in the market square loosened by a single degree.

She wiped the figures away with her sleeve and drew in answer: three small figures, then a fourth beside them, and connected all four with a loop of flour-line like a thread through beads. A circle. A household. She pointed at it, then pressed her floured hand flat against my chest, leaving a perfect white print over my heart.

I am the fourth. You will be my circle.

I have been offered many things in my life. A salary. An immunity. A pardon I haven't yet refused. Nothing has undone me like a flour handprint on a borrowed coat.

* * *

We stayed the night, because the water was high and the lanterns on the rope-walk needed lighting anyway, and because Sefa fed us reed-shoot soup and smoked fish with the fierce generosity of people who have learned that food is how you say the words you cannot. Odile arrived after dark, hauled up the ladder with our supplies, and froze in the doorway when she saw the certificate on the wall.

"Elyan," she said. Just the name. It fell out of her like a stone dropped down a well, and we waited a long time for it to land.

Sefa crossed the room, took Odile's flour-dusted hands, and turned them palm-up. Then she drew on each palm, with one careful fingertip, a single line—the same line, on both hands—so that when Odile closed her fingers the lines joined into one continuous mark across her clasped fists.

I didn't understand it. Odile clearly did. She stood very still in the stove-light with her fists closed around her dead colleague's last message, and when she finally spoke her voice was rough as wet rope.

"He forgave me. For the grief-trade. He—" She stopped. Started again. "I signed the requisition. The night they took the king's sorrow. My name is in the ledger, girl. Your father read it. He must have read it."

Sefa tilted her head, considering. Then she took the flour-crock and drew, quickly, a scale—the kind from a justice's office, two pans on a beam. Into one pan she poured a small mound of flour. Into the other, a much larger one. She pointed at the small pile, then at Odile. The large pile she pointed at herself, at the wall-map with its annotations of the missing and the fled, at the drowned city outside the window.

Then she swept both piles together with her forearm and mixed them into one.

That was all. Odile sat down on the bed and put her face in her hands, and Sefa put an arm around her shoulders, small and fierce, and I stood at the window and watched the black water lap at the drowned rooftops and understood that I was witnessing something rarer than alchemy.

Later, while Odile slept, Sefa showed me how she preserved the day's maps: a damp cloth passed lightly over the flour to fix it, then the whole design transferred onto treated paper with a roller of her father's devising. We worked side by side in silence, fixing the route south, and I found the silence easy in a way silence almost never is for me. With most people I was invisible; with her I was simply present. Two keepers of records, working by lamplight while the river breathed beneath us.

Near dawn, she woke me by touching my shoulder and pointing urgently at the wall.

She had added something new to the charcoal map of the quarter overnight—a detail I was certain hadn't been there before. At the eastern edge, where the rope-walk met the old customs road, she had drawn a small cluster of marks: riders. Six of them. And a banner, rendered in a few economical strokes, that I recognized from the courier's dying description and my own nightmares since.

The inquisitors' sigil. Brann's men. Already in the quarter—or coming, soon, along the customs road, toward the one high room where the guild's last map still lived inside a living girl.

Sefa looked at me, calm and grave, and pointed at the riders, then at the flour-map of the route south, already fixed and rolled into a tube on her table. Then she pointed at the packed bags by the door—which, I realized, had been packed for days.

She had known we would come. She had known they would come after.

She had been waiting for whichever of us arrived first, keeping the lanterns lit either way.

I dressed quickly in the cold. Below, Odile was already waking, sensing the shift in the house's weather the way she sensed every change of pressure in her cellar-still. Outside, far off across the water, a lantern flickered on the customs road—then another, and another, a string of small fires moving toward the rope-walk through the ruins of a city that had once belonged to a grieving king.

Sefa took down her father's certificate from the wall, wrapped it in oilcloth, and tucked it against her heart. Then she picked up the flour-crock, considered it, and—after a moment's deliberation—handed it to me to carry.

A mapmaker travels with her medium. Wherever we were going, whatever was waiting beneath the palace, she intended to go on drawing the truth of it, one line at a time, for as long as they let her hold the flour.

We went down the ladder into the gray morning, three fugitives and a rolled map, while the riders' fires crept closer along the drowned streets behind us—and ahead, faint on the northern wind, I heard for the first time the bells of the capital, ringing the hour as if nothing underneath them had ever been stolen at all.
