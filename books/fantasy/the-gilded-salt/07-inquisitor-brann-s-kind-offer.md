## 7. Inquisitor Brann's Kind Offer

The cells beneath the Salt Gate had no windows, which Brann considered a mercy. Men who could see the sky remembered it, and men who remembered the sky grew restless in ways that made his work slower and less kind than he preferred it to be.

He stood outside cell nine with his hands folded behind his back, listening to the sound of the prisoner breathing. Fast. Shallow. The breathing of a man who had been awake for two days because sleep meant dreams, and dreams, lately, meant questions that Inquisitor Brann would rather not answer at three in the morning with nothing but a candle and his own conscience for company.

"Master Corvel," Brann said through the grate. "You're wanted."

A scrape of chains. "I've told you everything I know."

"You've told me everything you think I want to hear," Brann corrected gently. "It's not the same thing, and we both know it. You were guild-trained. You know how interrogation works from the other side of the door. So let's not insult one another."

Silence. Then, sullen: "The courier was dead when I found him. Whatever he carried, he never gave it to me."

"No," Brann agreed. "He gave it to someone else. A food-taster. Sebastian Kavanagh." He let the name sit in the damp air like a coin dropped down a well. He heard Corvel's breath catch, and smiled without warmth. "There it is. You do know him."

"I know *of* him. Everyone in the trade knows of him. Ten years tasting for the king and never once sick. That's not a man, that's a legend."

"He's not a legend," Brann said. "He's a ledger with legs. And you're going to help me understand why a courier of the Gilded Salt spent his last breath running toward him."

* * *

Brann had been guild once. That was the thing none of his inquisitors knew, the thing he kept folded away like a letter he could neither burn nor reread. Thirty years ago—no, twenty-eight, he was precise about numbers, precision was all he had left of that life—he had apprenticed under Master Ellery Voss, learning the weights and measures of human fortune. Years. Grief. The memory of a mother's voice. The color blue, traded by a painter who needed rent money more than he needed sunsets. All of it entered in ledgers, sealed in wax, weighed against grain and gold according to the old formulas.

He had believed in it then. He believed in it now, if he was honest, though belief had changed its shape over the years, the way a river changes shape around stones.

What broke him was a trade he'd witnessed at nineteen: a woman selling her daughter's laughter to pay a debt, and the daughter, afterward, sitting in the corner of the guildhouse smiling at nothing, her mouth making the motions, her eyes as empty as unsalted bread. Brann had asked Voss how the guild could permit it. Voss had looked at him with genuine puzzlement and said, *Permit? Child, we facilitated it. She came to us freely. Free will is the whole foundation. Would you rather she'd sold the girl herself?*

Brann had left within the year. He had told himself he left because the guild was monstrous. It took him another decade to admit the truth: he left because Voss was right, and Brann could not live inside an institution that was right about something so wrong.

And then King Aldric had fallen ill—the real illness, the long gray decade of it—and the crown had come looking for men who understood the arithmetic of the soul, and Brann had answered, because the alternative was watching a kingdom grieve itself to death.

He did not regret it. That was the terrible thing. Ten years on, standing in the cold below the Salt Gate, he still did not regret it.

* * *

He went up to see the king at midday.

Audiences with His Majesty were held in the Amber Room, where the light came through honey-colored glass and made everyone look healthier than they were. The salt-king—for Brann thought of him that way in the privacy of his own skull, though never, ever aloud—sat by the window with a cup of barley tea, watching the market crowds below.

"You look tired, Brann," the king said.

"The city keeps me honest hours, Majesty."

"Mm." The king turned the cup in his hands. He had a habit of doing that—handling things carefully, as if their weight surprised him. Ten years ago, Aldric had been a big man, loud, given to laughing with his whole chest. The thing wearing him now was quieter. Thinner. It had learned the body well enough, learned the voice, learned the signatures, but there were moments—like this one—when it seemed to be listening to its own hands.

"The taster," the king said. "Kavanagh. Tell me again what you know of him."

Brann recited it. He had prepared the recitation the way he prepared everything: born in the Salt Quarter, orphaned at eleven, taken into palace service at nineteen, promoted to first taster after demonstrating immunity before witnesses. No wife. No children. No debts. No close friends. A man who ate alone every night in a small room and slept eight hours and woke at the same bell each dawn.

"And the courier died reaching for him specifically?"

"Specifically, Majesty. Pressed something into his hands. We haven't recovered it."

The king was quiet for a long moment. Outside, a fishmonger's cry drifted up through the glass, thin and bright.

"When I was ill," the king said at last, and there was something strange in its voice, something almost wistful, "in the bad years—I remember fragments. Not memories exactly. More like... descriptions of memories. Someone read my grief back to me once, in a list. *Item: sorrow for the queen. Item: fear of the dark. Item: love of the sea.*" It looked at its own reflection in the tea. "Do you know what it's like, Brann, to remember your own life the way a scholar remembers a battle he never fought?"

Brann did not answer. He had learned that the king's confidences were traps of a peculiar kind—not set deliberately, but dangerous all the same, because pity was the one poison Brann had no immunity to.

"They say the map has awakened," the king said. "The old guild wards are singing. Somewhere in my city, a piece of paper remembers what it is." It smiled, and the smile fit the face perfectly, which was somehow worse than if it hadn't. "Bring me the taster, Brann. Alive. Unhurt. I want to meet the man who cannot be poisoned. I want to know what that's like, too."

* * *

Brann found Sebastian Kavanagh three days later, or rather, arranged to find him, because a man like Kavanagh could not be *found*, only intercepted at the points where habit made him predictable. The evening market. The cheese stall on Tanner's Row, third stall from the fountain, where the taster bought aged goat's cheese every fifth day and always counted his change twice.

Brann watched him from across the square. Ordinary. That was the word, and it was not an insult—it was a description so complete it approached artistry. Brown hair going gray at the temples. A face built for crowd scenes, the kind of face a witness describes as *medium*. Clothes mended neatly but without vanity. He moved through the market the way water moves through sand: present everywhere, gripping nothing.

And yet a dying courier had chosen him. Out of an entire city, out of ten thousand strangers, the Gilded Salt had pressed its last secret into those unremarkable hands.

Brann crossed the square alone. No guards. He had dressed plainly—a wool coat, no insignia—and he stopped beside the taster at the cheese stall as if examining the same wheel of goat's cheese.

"Master Kavanagh."

Kavanagh didn't startle. That interested Brann. Most men startled when a stranger knew their name.

"You have the advantage of me," Kavanagh said mildly, counting coins into the cheesemonger's palm.

"Corvus Brann. Chief inquisitor to His Majesty." He watched the taster's hands—watched them go very still, then resume counting, half a heartbeat late. Trained. Interesting. "I'd like a word. There's a bench by the fountain that doesn't smell of fish. Rare thing, in this quarter."

"I'm expected home."

"You are. That's the trouble with you, Master Kavanagh—you're always exactly where your habits say you'll be. It's a wonder no one's robbed you." Brann gestured toward the bench. "Five minutes. After that, you're free to go, and I'll forget your face—which I suspect is easier for you than for most."

Something flickered behind the taster's eyes at that. Something that wasn't quite amusement.

They sat. The fountain murmured. Somewhere a child was shrieking with delight over a paper windmill, and the sound cut through the conversation like a bright thread.

"A courier died in the market district nine days ago," Brann said. "Stabbed twice, professional work, and before he died he gave you a sealed tube. I'm not going to ask if it's true. I watched you buy cheese just now instead of fleeing the city, which tells me either you don't have it, or you've decided running would draw more attention than staying. Both answers interest me."

"If I had such a thing," Kavanagh said carefully, "why would I give it to the man hunting it?"

"Because I'm not going to hunt it. I'm going to make you an offer." Brann turned to face him fully. "Full pardon. Sealed by the crown. New name, new papers, a stipend for life, a house wherever you like—coastal, I'd guess, you strike me as a man who'd like the sound of water at night. In exchange: the tube, and everything you've learned from it, and the names of anyone helping you."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I arrest you tonight, and you spend the rest of your life in a room without windows, and eventually I get the tube anyway. But I'd rather not." Brann held up a hand before the taster could speak. "Before you decide I'm simply a threat with better manners—come with me. One hour. See what I show you. Then choose."

* * *

The cells beneath the Salt Gate again. Brann led him past rows of iron doors, past the smell of wet stone and tallow, to a corridor Kavanagh's escort clearly had not been meant to see. At the end of it, Brann unlocked a door and stood aside.

"Count them if you like," he said quietly. "I have."

The cell beyond was large—a converted cistern, ringed with iron-barred alcoves. In each alcove, a man. Some sat. Two lay down. One stood at his bars with the perfect stillness of a man who had stopped expecting anything, including visitors.

"Couriers," Brann said. "Eleven of them. Guild runners who carried pieces of the old network's correspondence over the last ten years. When the king began recalling the guild's records, these men ran. Not because they were guilty—they mostly weren't, they were hired feet—but because they'd seen enough to be afraid." He walked slowly along the row, and the prisoners watched him with the flat patience of the long-confined. "This one—" he touched the bars of the standing man "—carried messages between Odile Vane and the cartographer Hallow, Sefa's father, the year before the executions. This one ferried the original map to a safehouse in the flooded quarter. None of them know where the Hollow Court is. None of them ever did. They were legs, not heads. And yet here they are, because the order came down that everyone who *might* remember must be contained until the matter resolves."

Kavanagh stood in the doorway. His face did what faces do—tightened, paled, composed itself—but his eyes stayed on the men, moving from alcove to alcove, cataloguing. Brann recognized the look. It was the look of a man reading a ledger and finding columns that shouldn't exist.

"Why show me this?" Kavanagh asked.

"Because you deserve to know what refusal costs, and I'd rather you know it accurately than imagine it." Brann's voice stayed level, almost gentle. "But also because I want you to understand something. I keep them alive. Fed, warm, unhurt. Do you know why? Because I believe the king's purge is temporary, and when it ends, these men walk free. I have written that into my reports. I have spent political capital on it. Eleven lives, Master Kavanagh, bought with an inquisitor's reputation, and I would spend it again."

"You call this mercy. A cage."

"I call it the difference between a cage and a grave." Brann turned to face him fully. "Now ask me the question you actually came here wanting to ask. Go on. You've been polite for an hour; you're permitted one rude question."

Kavanagh's jaw worked. "Why does the king want me alive? Why does any of this—if the map is so dangerous, burn it, kill me, done. Why the house by the sea? Why the *pardon*?"

"Because the king isn't afraid of the map," Brann said. "The king is lonely."

The words came out before he could dress them properly, and he saw immediately that they were true, and that Kavanagh saw they were true, and that neither of them knew what to do with that.

"Ten years ago," Brann continued, quieter, "this kingdom was drowning. You were too young in service to see it closely, but you tasted for the court—you must have read it in the kitchens, in the servants' faces. The king had lost his queen, then his son, then his health, in three years. He wept openly at council meetings. He signed pardons for men he'd sworn to hang, then hanged men he'd pardoned, because grief made him two different kings on alternating days. Grain rotted in the granaries because he couldn't bear to sign requisitions that mentioned his wife's home province. The border lords armed themselves. The priests preached that God had withdrawn His favor. I stood in those council rooms, Master Kavanagh. I watched a kingdom die of a broken heart, slowly, publicly, while its king apologized to everyone for being unable to stop."

He let the silence stretch. The standing prisoner shifted in his alcove.

"Then the recovery. Overnight, the histories call it—*the Miraculous Convalescence.* The grief lifted. The king rose from his bed clear-eyed, decisive, kind. He settled the border dispute in a fortnight. He opened the granaries. He forgave the priests their sermons and funded their charities. Crime fell. Harvests came in. In ten years, there has not been one famine, one purge of the smallfolk, one war." Brann spread his hands. "You taste his food every morning. Tell me the kingdom is worse off. Look me in the eye and tell me the grieving man was better for this country than the one who rules it now."

Kavanagh was silent a long time. When he spoke, his voice was careful, a taster testing an unknown dish. "At what cost?"

"There's always a cost. The guild taught me that before the crown did. The question is only *who pays*, and whether the payment buys anything worth having." Brann stepped closer. "One man's grief, weighed against a decade of peace. I know which side of that scale I stand on. I've stood there for ten years, and I sleep at night, Master Kavanagh. That's more than most idealists can say."

"And the couriers?" Kavanagh nodded at the cells. "Where do they sit on your scale?"

"On the cost side," Brann said simply. "Which is why they're alive, and fed, and why I will spend whatever remains of my career getting them out when this ends. I am not a good man, Kavanagh. I'm a man who does arithmetic and hates himself for how often the sums come out right."

For a moment—one long, suspended moment—he thought it had worked. He saw the doubt move through the taster like weather, saw him glance at the prisoners, at the door, at the middle distance where a house by the sea might stand. Brann had made this offer before, in other forms, to other people, and he knew the exact texture of the moment when a man began negotiating with himself.

Then Kavanagh looked at the standing prisoner—the one who had carried messages between Vane and Hallow—and asked, softly, "Does he have family?"

"A sister. In the river towns."

"Does she know he's alive?"

Brann hesitated. "No."

"Then here's my rude question," Kavanagh said, and his mildness had gone hard at the center, like a pit inside fruit. "If your king is so kind, so *better*—why is his kindness measured out in windowless rooms? Why does mercy need to be smuggled past his own orders? You talk about scales and arithmetic, Inquisitor, but I've kept ledgers my whole life, and I know a falsified column when I see one. You're not defending the king's reign. You're apologizing for it. Every hour. With your own reputation as the currency." He stepped back toward the door. "Keep your pardon. I've seen what your kingdom pays its couriers."

He walked out. Brann let him. There were guards enough in the corridors above, and orders enough, and Brann could have whistled the whole apparatus down on the taster's head before he reached the street.

He stood in the cistern instead, among the breathing men, and listened to the door boom shut, and felt the old familiar vertigo—the sense that somewhere in his fine balanced ledger, a page had turned and shown him a column he'd been avoiding for ten years.

The standing prisoner spoke for the first time in weeks. "That was the taster, wasn't it. The one they're hunting."

"Yes."

"What'll happen to him?"

Brann looked at the man a long moment. "I genuinely don't know," he said, and was disturbed to find it was the truest sentence he'd spoken all day.

* * *

That night he wrote his report. *Subject contacted. Offer extended. Subject declined. Subject remains at large, likely aided.* He signed it, sealed it, and set it aside for the morning rider.

Then he sat by his candle a while longer, and drew out a second sheet of paper, and did not write on it. On it, in his head, he drafted the letter he would never send: to the sister in the river towns, telling her that her brother was alive, that he was fed, that an inquisitor with a bad conscience counted him among the living the way a miser counts coins he means, someday, to spend.

In the morning, the report would go out, and the hunt would tighten, and Brann would ride at the front of it because that was where he could do the least damage and the occasional concealed good.

But as he snuffed the candle, one line from the taster would not leave him. *You're apologizing for it. Every hour.*

Below the Salt Gate, eleven men breathed in the dark. Above, the city slept peacefully under a king who dreamed, they said, of brine.

And somewhere east, Brann was certain of it now—east, toward the tollbridge, toward the old guild roads—a food-taster with a forged column of a life was choosing his companions, and the map was writing itself onward, and the kingdom's longest lie was beginning, at last, to come due.

Brann blew out the candle and began, for the first time in ten years, to be afraid.
