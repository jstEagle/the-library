## 8. Payment in Memory

The bridge appeared out of the fog the way a debt appears at the end of a ledger—suddenly, and all at once, though it had been accruing for miles.

We had walked since dawn through marsh country where the reeds grew white as bone and the water lay so still it seemed to be holding its breath. Odile led us now, following the map's shifting ink, which had begun to glow faintly beneath Sefa's careful hands whenever we strayed from the true path. The girl carried the parchment against her chest like something alive, which, I was learning, it was. It pulsed when danger neared. It warmed when lies were told in its presence. And twice on that gray morning it had gone cold as riverbed stone, and both times Odile had stopped us, sniffed the air, and said nothing—which frightened me more than any words could have.

Then the fog thinned, and there it was.

The tollbridge spanned a gorge I could not see the bottom of. Its stones were black and slick with salt-bloom, crusted white along every seam as though the structure itself wept brine. No river ran below—I listened, and heard nothing but wind moving through the gorge like breath through a flute. At the near end stood an archway of fused salt-crystal, and within the archway sat a figure.

I say *sat*, though it did not sit the way people sit. It was robed in gray, its face hidden behind a mask of polished salt that caught our reflections and held them, warped. In its lap it held a set of scales—not brass, not iron, but two shallow dishes of crystal suspended from a beam of black wood. The scales were empty. They waited.

"The Warden of Weighing," Odile murmured. She had gone very pale, which for Odile Vane was saying something; her usual complexion suggested a woman who slept in her own chemical fumes. "I'd hoped the tollhouses had fallen out of use."

"You knew about this," I said. Not a question. My voice came out flat, the voice I used in the king's dining hall when a dish smelled faintly of bitter almond and I needed to keep my hands steady.

"I knew the guild kept bridges." She would not look at me. "I didn't know they still collected."

"Collected what?" Halvard asked. The knight had been quiet all morning, his hand never far from his sword hilt, his eyes doing that soldier's arithmetic of ours: four bodies, one map, no banner. "Coin?"

Odile laughed then—a short, ugly sound with no humor in it whatsoever.

"No, Sir Halvard. The guild stopped dealing in coin three hundred years ago. Coin is only memory made metal, anyway. Everyone knows that." She looked at me then, and her eyes were wet and furious and afraid. "It deals in the thing itself."

* * *

The Warden did not rise as we approached. Perhaps it could not. As we drew closer I saw that the robe did not quite contain a body—the gray fabric moved in ways cloth should not, folding around absences, settling over shapes that shifted like sand under water. The salt mask turned toward us with a grinding whisper, and when it spoke, the voice came from everywhere and nowhere, the way a bell's note lives in the air after the bell has stopped.

**FOUR TRAVELERS. ONE PASSAGE.**

"We wish to cross," Odile said. Her chin was up. Her hands, at her sides, were shaking. I had spent ten years reading people's hands across a dinner table—it was how I survived—and I read hers now the way I read a poisoned cup. "Four fares. We'll pay."

**THE BRIDGE REMEMBERS WHAT IT CARRIES,** the Warden said. **IT WILL NOT CARRY DEBTORS. STATE YOUR PAYMENT.**

"And if we can't pay?" Halvard's hand was on his sword now, openly. "If we refuse?"

**THEN YOU WILL STAND HERE,** the Warden said, with terrible gentleness, **UNTIL YOU FORGET WHY YOU WANTED TO CROSS. THE GORGE IS PATIENT. IT HAS ROOM FOR EVERYONE WHO EVER CHOSE PRIDE OVER PASSAGE.**

Somewhere below us, in that soundless dark, something sighed.

Sefa tugged my sleeve. When I looked down she was already drawing—she always carried chalk stubs and charcoal nubs in every pocket, and she'd taken to sketching on scraps of the flour-sack cloth she kept folded in her pack. Her fingers flew. She held the cloth up to me.

*A drawing of a door. Behind the door, a shape like smoke wearing a crown. Below it, in her precise, uncanny hand: an arrow pointing at the bridge, and a second drawing—a heart, split open, with coins spilling out.*

She met my eyes. *This is a trap,* the drawing said. *And we pay anyway.*

Because behind us, far back down the white reed-road, there was dust. Riders. Brann's riders, or worse. The map had gone cold twice that morning, and now I understood why. The impostor's hounds had our scent, and the gorge offered exactly two choices: pay, or be found standing here with a treasonous parchment pressed to a mute girl's chest.

"Right," I said, and my voice surprised me by being steady. Ten years of tasting poison will teach you how to swallow. "How does it work? What do we do?"

**YOU PLACE YOUR HAND UPON THE SCALE,** said the Warden. **AND YOU THINK OF WHAT YOU OFFER. THE BRIDGE TAKES ONLY WHAT IS GIVEN FREELY. A MEMORY CLUTCHED IS A MEMORY KEPT; A MEMORY RELEASED IS A MEMORY SPENT.** The mask tilted, and I felt its attention settle on me like a physical weight, cold and granular. **CHOOSE WELL, TASTER. THE BRIDGE KNOWS THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN A MEMORY YOU CAN SPARE AND A MEMORY YOU CANNOT. IT PRICES ACCORDINGLY.**

"What does that mean?" I asked. "Priced accordingly?"

**A SMALL MEMORY BUYS A FEW STEPS.** The scales swayed, though nothing touched them. **THE CROSSING IS FOUR HUNDRED PACES. AND THE BRIDGE SETS ITS OWN EXCHANGE.**

* * *

Halvard went first.

He said he wanted to spare the rest of us the sight of him hesitating, which was a lie, because knights are the worst liars I have ever served alongside—he wanted to go first the way he'd once wanted to stand first in a shield wall, out of some battered code of honor he carried like a wound. He put his broad hand on the left-hand scale and closed his eyes.

For a long moment, nothing. Then his face changed.

I have watched men die. I have watched men drink what I drank and live, and watched them drink what I drank and die, and I thought I had seen every expression the human face could make. I was wrong. What crossed Halvard's features was grief and relief braided into one another so tightly they became indistinguishable—the look of a man setting down a burden he has carried so long he cannot remember who he was without it.

When he opened his eyes, they were wet, and he was smiling, and it was the worst thing I have ever seen.

"It's done," he said hoarsely. He flexed his hand as if it ached. "I don't—" He stopped. Started again. "There was a dog. When I was a boy. I know there was a dog. I remember that there was a dog." His voice cracked down the middle. "I can't remember its name. I can't remember its color. I know I loved it. That's all that's left. Knowing."

**ONE HUNDRED AND TWELVE PACES,** said the Warden. **THE BRIDGE FOUND THE DOG SMALL BUT BELOVED. IT PAID FAIRLY.**

Halvard walked onto the bridge. Each stone he stepped upon lit faintly white beneath his boot, and each light dimmed and died behind him, so that he crossed a corridor of fading glow, leaving darkness in his wake like a wake behind a boat. At one hundred and twelve paces he stopped, and stood, and waited for us in the fog.

* * *

Odile went next, and she bargained.

Of course she bargained. She was Odile Vane, disgraced alchemist, woman of cellar and crucible, and she approached the Warden's scales the way she approached everything—as a problem to be solved rather than a sentence to be endured.

"Define your terms," she said crisply. "If I give you a memory, do I lose the skills attached to it? If I surrender a day of my life, do I lose what I learned that day? There are distinctions. Precision matters."

**PRECISION MATTERS,** the Warden agreed, and something in the salt mask's tone suggested it found her amusing, the way a cat finds a mouse amusing. **THE BRIDGE TAKES THE LIVING OF IT, NOT THE LEARNING. IF YOU GIVE A DAY, YOU KEEP ITS LESSONS AND LOSE ITS LIGHT.**

"Then I'll give a day I've already mined," Odile said. "Take my wedding day."

The word dropped between us like a stone into a well.

I had known Odile for—what, six days now? Seven? Long enough to learn she took her tea bitter enough to strip varnish, that she talked to her equipment, that she slept four hours a night and lied about it. I had not known she had ever been married. She had never mentioned it, and I am a man who notices omissions the way other men notice knives.

"You were married," I said stupidly.

"Widowed," she corrected, and did not look at me. "Ten years ago. Same season the king recovered, as it happens. Bad year all around." She rolled up her sleeve and placed her forearm across the crystal scale, jaw tight. "His name was Tomas. Take the day, bridge. The whole of it. The bells and the vows and the terrible dancing. But you'll leave me the fact of him. You said—you said I keep the lessons and lose the light. So I'll keep knowing I loved him. I'll keep knowing I was loved. I simply won't be able to *see* it anymore."

**THAT IS THE BARGAIN,** said the Warden.

"Then take it before I lose my nerve, because I assure you, my nerve is a finite resource."

The scale sank. Light rose up through the crystal like dawn seen from underwater, and Odile made a sound I will hear until I die—a small, bitten-off sound, half gasp and half word, the beginning of a name cut short. Her knees buckled. I caught her elbow. For one moment she leaned into me, shaking, and then she straightened, wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, and said, in a perfectly level voice:

"Well. That's that. I appear to have been married. Isn't that strange? I feel like a house with one room bricked over. I know the room is there. I can't get into it."

**TWO HUNDRED AND NINETY PACES,** said the Warden. **THE BRIDGE FOUND THE DAY HEAVY WITH JOY. IT PAYS BEST FOR JOY.**

"That," Odile said unsteadily, "is the most obscene thing I have ever heard, and I once distilled memories for a living."

She walked onto the bridge, and the stones lit beneath her, and where she passed the salt-bloom on the balustrades curled away from her hand as if recognizing her.

* * *

Which left Sefa.

She stood before the scales with her chalk-stained fingers laced together, small and straight-backed, and the Warden regarded her for a long, silent moment. The salt mask ground softly, thinking.

**THE CHILD CANNOT SPEAK HER PRICE.**

"She can't," I said, stepping forward before I had decided to. "So she won't. I'll pay double. Whatever remains of my fare, she rides on mine. That's allowed, isn't it? Payment given freely for another?"

**IT IS ALLOWED,** said the Warden. **BUT IT IS NOT WISE. AND IT IS NOT WHAT SHE WISHES.**

I looked at Sefa. She was watching me with those dark, patient eyes, and slowly, deliberately, she shook her head. Then she knelt, right there on the salt-white stone, and drew.

She drew herself. A small figure, chalk-simple. And above the figure's head, where a name would go, she drew a circle, and struck a line through it.

Then she looked up at me, and pointed at the drawing, and pointed at herself, and her eyes were dry and fierce and absolutely certain.

"No," I said aloud. "No. Absolutely not. Your father gave his life. Your mother gave—" I stopped, because I didn't actually know what her mother had given, and the look on Sefa's face told me I had stumbled near the answer. "You've already paid enough for one lifetime. There has to be another way."

She shook her head again. And then, gently, with the terrible patience of someone who has lived her whole life being spoken for, she took my hand and pressed it flat against the drawing of herself.

*I choose this,* her gesture said. *Me. Myself.*

**THE GIRL'S NAME,** the Warden said softly. **NOT THE SOUND OF IT—THE HAVING OF IT. SHE WILL KNOW SHE WAS CALLED SOMETHING. SHE WILL SEE OTHERS CALLED, AND ANSWER TO NOTHING. SHE WILL WRITE HERSELF INTO LEDGERS AS A BLANK.** The mask turned toward me. **DO YOU UNDERSTAND WHAT SHE OFFERS, TASTER?**

"Yes," I said, and my throat hurt. "I understand exactly. Because it's the same coin I've been paying in for ten years. Being no one. Being a blank in the ledger." I crouched down to her level, and I took both her hands in mine, chalk and callus. "Listen to me. Names come back. I don't know how, I don't know when, but I have watched this kingdom trade in human pieces for a decade and I have learned one thing: nothing the guild takes is ever truly destroyed. It's only *held*. Somewhere, there is a ledger with your name in it, and I swear to you—I will find that page. I will find it if I have to read every ledger in this rotten kingdom cover to cover. And until then—" my voice cracked, and I let it, because she deserved honesty more than comfort— "until then, you're not a blank. You're Sefa. I'll say it. Every day. I'll keep it for you. That's what family does. It holds things."

Something moved behind her eyes—something bright and drowning—and then she nodded, once, and turned to the scale, and laid her hand upon it as calmly as a merchant closing a sale.

The light that rose from the crystal was different this time. Not gold, not white. Silver, cold and clean as moonlight on snow, and it poured upward through her small body and out through the crown of her head, and her mouth opened in a soundless cry, and for one horrible instant I thought—

But no. The light faded. She swayed. I caught her. She was lighter somehow, or seemed so, the way a book feels lighter after pages have been torn out.

She looked up at me. Her lips moved. Nothing came. And then, slowly, she raised one chalk-white finger and pointed at herself, and tilted her head, and her expression was not grief.

It was a question. *Who was I?*

"Sefa," I said firmly. "Your name is Sefa. Say it back."

Her mouth shaped the word silently. Then she smiled—a real smile, wobbly at the edges—and tapped her chest twice, and pointed at me, and I understood: *you hold it now. Keep it safe.*

**THREE HUNDRED AND FORTY PACES,** said the Warden. **THE BRIDGE FOUND THE NAME SMALL BUT ENTIRELY HERS. IT PAID GENEROUSLY. NAMES ARE RARE COIN THESE DAYS.**

* * *

Which left me.

I stood before the scales alone, the others already out on the bridge—Halvard a distant silhouette, Odile a smudge of motion, Sefa waiting with her hand outstretched toward me, trusting me to follow. Behind us, down the reed-road, the dust of riders had resolved into distinct shapes. Half an hour, perhaps less. Maybe much less.

**AND YOU, TASTER,** the Warden said. **WHAT WILL YOU GIVE?**

Here is what no one tells you about being forgettable: you spend your whole life accumulating almost nothing. No close friends. No lovers. No debts, no grudges, no history. I had built my anonymity brick by brick, dish by dish, and I had called it safety, and I had been proud of it. And now the bill had come due, and I stood before the scales of the guild my father had served, and I searched myself for coin, and found how little I owned.

Except.

There was one thing. One room in the house of me that I had kept locked even from myself, because opening it hurt, and I was a man whose entire art was the avoidance of hurt.

My mother.

I remembered her hands—flour-dusted, quick, pressing dough into pans in a kitchen that smelled of yeast and woodsmoke. I remembered her humming. I remembered that she sang while she worked, songs in the old dialect, low, half under her breath. I remembered she had died in the fever winter, when I was nine, and that my father had not let me see her body, and that I had hated him a little for that, for years, before I understood it had been mercy.

But her face.

I reached for it and felt the edges go soft, the way ink goes soft in rain. I could see the hands. The humming. The kitchen. But the face above those hands was a smear, a watercolor left out in weather, and I realized with a lurch that I had been losing her for years, grain by grain, the way salt dissolves, and I had never once chosen to stop it.

**YOU TREMBLE,** observed the Warden.

"Everyone trembles," I said. "That's not the same as refusing."

I put my hand on the scale.

"I give her face," I said. "Not the hands—they stay. Not the humming—that stays. Only the face. The part I look at when I try to picture her. Take that, and only that, and leave me the rest, because the rest is what she *was*, and the face is only what she *looked like*, and I am choosing to believe that's a distinction that matters."

**THE BRIDGE WILL WEIGH IT.**

The scale sank. And the light came for me.

It did not feel like loss, at first. It felt like warmth—like standing in a doorway with sun on your face, like the first sip of broth after fasting, like every good thing I had ever tasted gathered into one rising tide. And in the middle of that warmth was a face. Younger than I remembered, older than I remembered, laughing, tired, alive, alive, *alive*—and I held onto it with everything I had, and then, deliberately, finger by finger, I let go.

The tide went out.

I was on my knees. I didn't remember falling. The stone was cold and salt-white beneath my palms, and somewhere far away the Warden was speaking, its voice coming to me through water:

**NINETY-ONE PACES REMAIN. THE BRIDGE FOUND THE FACE MUCH LOVED AND POORLY KEPT. IT PAYS FOR LOVE, TASTER. IT ALWAYS PAYS FOR LOVE.**

I got up. My legs held. Barely.

And here is the thing I will carry longer than the ache: I could still remember that she had a face. I could remember that I had loved it, that I had known it better than my own, that I had once fallen asleep counting the details of it the way children count stars. I knew the love remained. Only the sight of her was gone—bricked over, like Odile's wedding bells, like Halvard's dog, like Sefa's name. A house full of sealed rooms, all four of us, walking across a bridge built of everyone's sealed rooms.

I stepped onto the first stone. It lit beneath my boot, warm as a hearthstone, and I understood suddenly why the bridge paid best for joy, for love, for names—for all the things the guild had spent three centuries teaching this kingdom to sell. The bridge wasn't a tollhouse.

It was a lesson. The last one the old guild ever built. *Look what you're trading. Look what it costs. Look what it buys.*

I walked. Ninety-one paces, each one lit and left dark behind me, and halfway across I passed Odile, who squeezed my arm without a word, and further on Sefa, who fell into step beside me and slipped her small hand into mine and did not let go.

Behind us, the Warden's voice followed us out over the gorge, soft as falling salt:

**THE BRIDGE THANKS YOU FOR YOUR CUSTOM. IT NOTES, FOR THE LEDGER, THAT ALL FOUR PAID MORE THAN REQUIRED. IT NOTES THAT THIS IS USUALLY TRUE.**

* * *

We came off the far end into thin mountain air, and the fog swallowed the bridge behind us, and I let myself believe, for one whole minute, that we had bought our passage cleanly.

Then Sefa stopped dead in the road.

She was staring back the way we'd come—at the fog, at the invisible gorge, at nothing I could see. Her free hand went to her chalk. She dropped to her knees on the frozen mud and drew fast, faster than I'd ever seen her draw, and thrust the cloth up at me with a hand that was not steady.

A rider. Drawn in three swift strokes. And on the rider's shoulder, unmistakable even in chalk: the sigil of the crown.

And beneath the rider, her most precise, most terrible detail yet—the horse's hoofprints, pressed into the white reed-mud of the road we had just left.

They were already there. They had been there for hours.

Brann's men hadn't been following us at all.

They'd been *waiting*.
