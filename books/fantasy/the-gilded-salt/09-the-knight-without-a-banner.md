## 9. The Knight Without a Banner

The road to Vell's Crossing smelled of rain that had not yet fallen, and Halvard Dane had been waiting on it for three days.

He had chosen the spot the way he once chose ground for a defensive line: a bend where the old guild road narrowed between two walls of gorse and grey stone, close enough to the ford that travelers would be tired of mud but not yet desperate for shelter. A man who has spent twenty years guarding kings does not lose the habit of reading terrain. He only loses the king.

His horse, a bay gelding named Patience because naming him anything hopeful had felt like blasphemy, cropped the wet grass at the roadside. Halvard stood beneath the gorse with his sword slung low and his exile's cloak—undyed wool, no crest, no color at all—beaded with mist. He had been a knight once. The word still fit his body like armor made for a younger man: everything in the right place, nothing comfortable.

Three days of waiting gave a man time to think, which was the one thing Halvard had spent ten years learning not to do.

He thought about the night it happened. Everyone in the capital remembered the night King Aldric recovered—the bells, the bonfires, the proclamation read from the balcony while snow came down like ash from a gentler fire. Halvard remembered it differently. He remembered standing post outside the royal bedchamber, third door from the left in the long gallery, and hearing through the oak a sound he had never heard a king make. Not pain. Kings were paid to endure pain; it was practically in the coronation oath. This was something smaller and worse. It was the sound of a man weeping without the strength to be ashamed of it, hour after hour, while physicians came and went with their cases of instruments and their faces of professional blankness.

And then, near dawn, the sound stopped. Not faded. Stopped, the way a clock stops—all its small machinery suddenly absent—and what remained behind the door was silence with a shape to it, a silence like a room after the furniture has been carried out.

At morning court the king had appeared, hale and laughing, and embraced his chancellor, and thanked the gods in a voice pitched perfectly for the gallery. Halvard had knelt with the rest of the guard and felt the wrongness go through him like cold water through cloth. The king's face was the king's face. But grief had lived in that face for a decade—grief for the queen, dead in childbed; grief for the son who followed her into the dark—and grief does not leave a face cleanly. It leaves silt. And there was no silt. The face was scoured, new-minted, a coin too bright for its purse.

Halvard had said nothing. That was the whole of his treason, and it was everything. He had stood his post, drawn his pay, watched the bright king year by year grow no older in the eyes, and told himself that a guard's duty was to the crown and not the man wearing it, and that perhaps the physicians' salts and tinctures had simply worked a miracle, and that a soldier who saw ghosts in daylight should keep his ghost-seeing to himself.

Then came the night he did not stand his post. The one night in twenty years. His daughter had been feverish—no, that was the excuse he had polished smooth as riverstone over ten years of handling; the truth was uglier and simpler. The truth was that a man in the guild's livery had found him in a tavern off the soldiers' row and slid a purse across the table and said, *the king will not need guarding tonight, ser, or any night, if you take my meaning,* and Halvard—who had already seen the wrongness, who had already begun drinking to keep the wrongness at arm's length—took the purse. Took it and drank half of it in one sitting and told himself the money was for his daughter's fever, and by the time he climbed back up the palace stair the doors below were sealed and men in grey were carrying out a covered litter, and whatever had been done had been done, and his absence was part of the architecture of it.

They never even exiled him properly. That was the refinement of cruelty the new reign specialized in: no trial, no accusation, just a quiet reassignment, then a quieter dismissal, then a letter thanking him for his service and informing him that his pension would continue provided he resided beyond fifty miles of the capital and held his tongue regarding matters of state. The kingdom forgot him with the same efficiency the king forgot everything. Halvard Dane, knight-banneret of the realm, became a large weathered man who hired out as a caravan guard and drank alone and kept, sewn inside his saddle-roll, the white cloak of his office folded so many times it had gone soft as linen.

So when Inquisitor Brann's riders had come to him a month ago—Brann himself, grey-eyed and reasonable, with that voice like a well-oiled hinge—Halvard had listened. Brann remembered him. Brann knew what he'd taken that night, and knew also what Halvard had seen through the bedchamber door, and offered him the thing he wanted most and least: a way back in. Find the courier's map before it reached the Hollow Court, deliver it to the inquisition, and the exile ended. Pardon, reinstatement, a banner again. *You were a good soldier once,* Brann had said, and the terrible thing was that he said it kindly, without irony, as though the years between had been an administrative error rather than a wound.

Halvard had agreed. Of course he had. A man does not refuse the shape of his own life being handed back to him.

And so here he was, three days in wet gorse, waiting to rob four strangers on behalf of the thing that wore his king's face.

* * *

They came up the road in the last gold light of afternoon, and Halvard almost laughed at the sight of them.

He had expected the courier's confederates to be dangerous. What picked its way along the muddy wheel-ruts instead was a procession of ruin: a thin, forgettable man in a food-taster's drab greys, walking like someone perpetually apologizing for taking up space; a woman in alchemist's leathers with singed eyebrows and the swagger of someone whose laboratory had recently been on fire; and a girl of perhaps fourteen leading a mule, silent as a held breath, her eyes doing the work of a whole company of scouts.

No escort. No weapons worth the name except the woman's belt-knife and a short sword the taster wore like an heirloom he didn't believe in. Halvard stepped out of the gorse with his blade drawn—not leveled, merely present, the way you show a farmer a wolf is possible—and said the words he had rehearsed:

"Stand where you are. I'm not here for your purses."

The effect was instructive. The alchemist went for her knife with the speed of long practice and poor odds. The taster went pale and very still, the stillness of prey that has survived by freezing. But the girl—the girl looked at him, really looked, the way no one had looked at him in ten years, and tilted her head as if he were a problem she intended to solve.

"The map," Halvard said. "The tube. Give it to me and ride on, and none of you see another drawn blade. I have no quarrel with—" he gestured vaguely at all of them, "—whatever this is."

"You're the one with the quarrel," the alchemist said. Her voice was smoke-cured, unimpressed. "Odile Vane. You're Halvard Dane. I know the face from the old proclamations—you were taller in the engravings." She smiled without warmth. "Come to sell us to Brann? He pays in pardons, I hear. Counterfeit coin. I could tell you what they're minted from."

"I've come for the map," Halvard repeated, and hated how little conviction the sentence carried out loud. Rehearsed words are like rehearsed meals; they cool fast.

"Then take it," said the taster, unexpectedly, and Halvard blinked. The man had gone from frozen to oddly calm, the way some small animals do when they decide the predator is confused. "Take it to Brann. Tell him where we're going, how many we are, what we look like. He'll pay you, and then in a year or two, when we're dead and the map is ash, he'll pay someone else to find out what you know about what you delivered. Men like Brann don't close accounts, ser knight. They just move them to a different ledger."

It was a good speech for a man built entirely of apology, and it bought silence long enough for the girl to act.

She had crouched at the roadside with a stick of charcoal from her pocket and a board of pale wood, and she drew. Fast, sure strokes, no hesitation—she drew the way other people breathed—and then she turned the board around and held it up to Halvard.

Halvard looked.

It was a face. A man's face, forty-ish, worn, with the heavy jaw and deep-set eyes of the Aldric line—but drawn as Halvard had not seen it in ten years. Drawn grieving. The mouth pulled down not in weakness but in the particular dignity of sustained sorrow; the eyes red-rimmed, exhausted, *human*, with silt in them, with ten years of silt, and above the brow a faint suggestion of the iron circlet, and beneath the collar of the drawn figure the white mantle of a king in mourning, which Aldric had worn every day of his widowhood until the miraculous recovery, after which no one had ever seen it again.

The girl had never met the king. Halvard understood that in one cold drop: this was memory, someone's memory, passed hand to hand like contraband, rendered in charcoal by a mute child on a roadside.

His knees informed him, quietly, that they would prefer to buckle. He did not permit it. Twenty years of drill held him upright while something behind his sternum tore along a seam that had been waiting ten years to tear.

"That's not possible," he heard himself say. His voice came out wrong, gravel over hollow. "That's not—I saw him. After. He was well. He was *happy*."

The girl turned the board over, wiped it with her sleeve, and drew again. Quicker now. She drew the bedchamber door—third from the left, he knew that grain of oak, those iron hinges—and behind it, in the negative space, she drew a shape: a man-shaped absence, an outline of a king with nothing inside it, and around the outline she drew fine radiating lines, like salt crystals growing across a windowpane.

Then she drew a knight standing outside the door. She drew him carefully, lovingly almost, with his sword and his white mantle and his face turned away down the gallery. And beside the knight she wrote, in a child's careful letters, the only words she would ever give him:

*WHERE WERE YOU*

The rain arrived then, finally, as if summoned by the drawing—a soft grey curtain hissing on the gorse, beading on his blade, running down the board and beginning to blur the charcoal. Halvard watched the grieving face dissolve and found he could not breathe properly.

"I was bribed," he said. The confession came out of him like a splinter coming out of flesh, tearing on the way. "The night they took him. I took a purse to walk my post drunk, and I told myself it was for my daughter, and it wasn't, it was because I had already seen the wrongness and I wanted to stop seeing it, and they *knew*, they knew exactly what I was, and they bought me for less than a warhorse costs." He was shaking. Rain, he told himself. Only rain. "I have spent ten years telling myself the kingdom runs better under the bright king. Grain prices are up. There's no war. My daughter thinks her father died a coward's death in a border skirmish, which is more than I deserve, because at least that lie has a body in it."

The alchemist had lowered her knife somewhere in the middle of this. The taster watched him with an expression Halvard couldn't parse—pity, maybe, or recognition, the look of a man who understood purchased qualities better than anyone alive.

The girl wiped the board dry with her sleeve and drew one more thing. Small, quick. She held it up: a knight, kneeling this time, sword laid flat on the ground before him, offering it hilt-first to a small group of ragged figures. Above the figures she drew a rough circle—a ring, a family, a table—and placed the kneeling knight just outside its edge. Not in it. Beside it.

Not forgiven. *Present.*

"I can't ask for that," Halvard said hoarsely. "I know what I am."

The taster—Kavanagh, the alchemist had called him—stepped forward through the rain. Up close he was even less remarkable than at distance: brown hair, brown eyes, a face you would lose in a crowd of three. But his voice, when it came, was steady as a scale settling.

"We need someone who knows the palace guard rotations," he said. "We need someone Brann believes is still his. And we need," and here he paused, choosing the word with a taster's precision, "someone who carries what you carry. Guilt is load-bearing, ser. We're building something heavy."

Halvard looked at the girl. She was watching him with those scout's eyes, and slowly—deliberately, the way she did everything—she nodded once. Then she pointed at the board, at the kneeling knight outside the circle, and tapped the space between the circle and the figure. The gap. The distance yet to cross.

"Not earned," Halvard translated, his throat tight. "Just... walked."

She nodded again, and something in his chest that had been clenched since the night of the bells let go by a single degree. Not enough to breathe freely. Enough to breathe.

* * *

They made camp that night in a shepherd's bothy above the ford, and Halvard sat apart, as was proper, sharpening a sword that did not need it.

The rain had moved off eastward, and through the bothy's broken roof the early stars showed hard and cold. Odile Vane brewed something in a dented pot that smelled of pine resin and burnt sugar, and the taster—Sebastian, he insisted, just Sebastian—divided travel bread with the exact fairness of a man who had weighed portions professionally for a decade. The girl, Sefa, sat cross-legged by the fire drawing: the road behind them, the road ahead, and once—when she thought no one watched—a small picture of four figures and a mule inside a circle, with the fifth figure, the knight, drawn slightly larger than scale, as if she were testing whether he fit.

Halvard cleaned his gear and did not sleep. Around midnight, Sebastian crossed the bothy and sat against the wall beside him, uninvited, unhurried, and said nothing for a long time. It was, Halvard would reflect later, the first conversation he'd ever had that consisted entirely of silence, and one of the most honest.

Finally the taster spoke, quietly, so as not to wake the others.

"When I was eight, my father sold something of mine to the guild. I learned that recently. I don't know what it was yet—some quality, some piece. Whatever it was, I've been living in the space it left my whole life and calling the space myself." He turned the bread in his hands. "You sold yourself too. The difference is you remember the price."

"Aye," Halvard said. "And I'd give the remembering back if I could."

"No, you wouldn't." Sebastian's voice was mild and absolute. "Men who'd give it back stop carrying it. They set it down somewhere and call it someone else's fault. You've carried yours ten years without setting it down once. That's why Sefa drew you outside the circle instead of nowhere at all."

From across the dying fire, the girl's eyes opened—Halvard would have sworn she'd been asleep—and regarded them both, and closed again.

Near dawn, Halvard finally slept, and dreamed of the gallery, and of the bedchamber door, and in the dream he did not walk away down the corridor. In the dream he put his shoulder to the oak and broke it open, and what waited inside was not a king but a tide, patient and glittering and vast, and it looked at him with his king's borrowed eyes and said, in a voice like sand shifting underwater:

*Too late, ser knight. Too late by ten years.*

He woke with his hand on his sword hilt and the taste of brine in his mouth, and found Sefa awake, watching him, charcoal already moving. She turned the board toward him.

On it, rendered in swift grey strokes, was a column of riders on the southern road—lances, grey cloaks, the inquisition's salt-white pennons—and beneath them, a number, written in her precise child's hand:

*SIX DAYS.*

And below that, one more drawing: the bothy, burning.
