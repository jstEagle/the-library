## 10. Salt on the Windowsill

The mountain took them the way a miser takes coins—slowly, grudgingly, and with every intention of keeping them.

For three days they had climbed through pine and scree, following Sefa's flour-drawn lines north while the lowlands behind them filled, rumor said, with Brann's riders. Odile's knees had begun to complain in a language of mutters and grimaces. Halvard carried her pack without being asked and pretended he hadn't. Sefa walked with her sketchbook clutched to her chest like a psalter, stopping every few hours to crouch over some stone or streambed and add a line of certainty to their uncertain route.

Sebastian counted things. It was what he did when fear came knocking: he counted. Forty-one switchbacks since the river. Sixteen hours since the last sign of pursuit. Three loaves, half a wheel of cheese, a jar of Odile's bitter tonic that no one volunteered to finish. Nine blisters between four travelers, if you counted Halvard's soldier's calluses as blisters, which Halvard insisted you should not.

It was on the ninth day of the month of Emberfall, at dusk, with snow beginning to sift down like ash from some distant burning, that they saw the village.

It sat in a bowl of the mountainside the way an egg sits in a spoon—a cluster of slate roofs and smoking chimneys around a crooked bell tower, terraced fields below it gone gold and stubbled with the last of the barley, and above it all, cut into the living rock, a white seam of salt glittering in the dying light like a vein of frozen lightning.

"Salt-miners," Odile said, shading her eyes. "Guild territory, once. Before my time." Her mouth twisted. "Before everything."

"They'll have food," Halvard said. "And beds. And—" he glanced back down the trail, where the dark was pooling like spilled ink, "—walls."

"And questions," Sebastian said quietly. "Travelers in Emberfall are either pilgrims or fugitives, and we look like neither. We look like exactly what we are."

Sefa tugged his sleeve. She had already opened her sketchbook. On the page, in quick sure strokes: a woman at a doorway, a lamp, a loaf. Then she looked up at him, eyebrows raised, and the question was so plain in her face that words would have been redundant.

"You think someone will simply *feed* us," Sebastian said.

She nodded once, firmly, the way she did everything.

He almost laughed. Ten years in the palace had taught him that nothing was given freely—that every kindness was a transaction with the receipt hidden somewhere, that the safest meal was one you had tasted first and paid for after. But Sefa was already walking down toward the lights, and Odile was groaning to her feet, and there was nothing for it but to follow the girl into the falling snow.

* * *

The widow's name was Marta Bren, though she told them this only after she had fed them, as if names were a course that came after bread.

She was small and brown and quick, with hands like tree roots and eyes that missed nothing, and she had opened her door before they finished knocking—as if she'd been watching the road all evening, as perhaps she had. She took one look at four mud-caked strangers and said, "Kitchen's warm. Sit. You can tell me your lies after you've eaten; they always taste better on a full stomach."

The kitchen was low-beamed and amber-lit and smelled of onions and rosemary and woodsmoke, and the stew she set before them was barley and mutton and root vegetables, thick enough to stand a spoon in, and Sebastian—who had tasted the finest cuisine in the kingdom for ten years, who had eaten off gold plate while kings watched him chew—found his eyes stinging over the bowl.

It wasn't the food. It was the freedom of it. No ledger. No second spoon. No physician waiting with his little case of antidotes. Just a woman who had cooked too much, as country women do, and four strangers who were hungry, as travelers are.

Sefa ate with her whole body, leaning into the bowl, and when Marta saw the girl's silence—not the silence of deafness but the deliberate quiet of the voiceless—the old woman simply reached over and turned her own cup upside down on the table. Then she drew, with one wet fingertip on the wood, a little bird.

Sefa went very still.

Then she took up the salt cellar, poured a small mound of salt onto the scrubbed table, and drew in it—not a bird, but the whole valley: the village, the terraces, the salt-vein in the rock above, and a road winding away south, and on the road, small and precise, four figures and behind them many more.

Marta looked at the drawing for a long moment. The fire popped. Outside, the snow hissed against the shutters.

"Hunters," she said at last, not a question.

Odile set down her spoon. "Worse. Inquisitors."

Something moved behind the widow's eyes—something old and tired and not entirely surprised. "Brann's men came through in spring," she said. "Took the miller's boy for asking why the tithe-granaries were locked. Gave him back in autumn." She didn't say what condition. She didn't need to. "But they left the harvest alone. Left the salt-tax alone, too—first year in twenty the crown hasn't doubled it come winter." She shrugged, a small hard motion. "A king who leaves the small folk full-bellied can burn whom he likes, far as most here care. That's not treason, alchemist. That's arithmetic."

Sebastian felt the words go into him like a splinter.

Because it was true. He had seen it from the palace side for ten years—the granaries open, the roads safe, the guild-halls rebuilt, the plague-years gentle. He had tasted the impostor's meals and read its ledgers and filed its strange appetites under *eccentricity*, and all the while the kingdom underneath had been quietly, genuinely, fed.

"The true king grieved," Odile said softly. "Grief makes poor harvests. Poor harvests make dead children. I know which trade I'd have made, if it were mine to make." But her hands, wrapped around her cup, were trembling.

"That's the filth of it," Marta said, gathering bowls with those root-hard hands. "They didn't even give us a *tyrant*. They gave us a good king and called it mercy." She paused at the hearth. "You'll sleep in the loft. And tomorrow you'll be gone before light, because whatever you're carrying—and don't trouble denying it, I've buried a husband and raised three sons, I know cargo when I see it—that cargo is worth more than my village, and I won't have it priced in my neighbors' blood."

That night, lying in the hayloft with the others breathing slow around him, Sebastian looked out the small window and saw, on the sill below, a little dish of salt set out against the glass.

Gilded at the rim. Old work. Guild-work.

He stared at it a long time before sleep took him, and dreamed of ledgers whose columns ran red.

* * *

He woke to the smell of smoke that was not the hearth's.

For one heartbeat he lay in the hay-dark and catalogued it, the way he catalogued everything—woodsmoke, yes, but under it something sharper, resinous, *wrong*, the smell of pitch and oil, of fire that had been *fed*. Below him, Marta's kitchen was silent. Too silent. No clatter of pans. No early bank-and-rekindle of the fire.

Then the church bell began to ring, ragged and frantic, and the screaming started.

"Sefa—" He was moving before the word finished, shaking the girl awake, then Odile, then Halvard, who came up out of sleep like a blade coming out of a sheath, already asking "how many" in the flat voice of a man who had asked it before.

Through the loft window, the village was burning.

Not all of it—not yet. Flames stood up from the granary first, black and orange against the snow, and then the mill, and riders moved through the streets with torches trailing long tails of fire, unhurried, methodical, *practiced*. There were maybe thirty of them. More than enough. Villagers stumbled out into the snow in their nightclothes, and the riders didn't cut them down—they didn't need to. They just burned, house by house, in a pattern Sebastian's mind refused at first to read and then read all too clearly: they were leaving the church. Leaving the well. Leaving enough for survivors.

An example. Not a massacre. A *message*.

"For us," Odile whispered beside him. Her face in the firelight was grey. "They tracked us here. God help me, they tracked us and they waited until we were inside it, so the cost would land on our account."

"No." Halvard's voice was iron. "It lands on theirs. Always theirs. That's how Brann works—he taught me that much before I ever wore his colors. Make the fugitives carry the weight. Make them *choose*." His jaw worked. "We go down there. Now."

"And do what?" Sebastian heard his own voice, thin and strange. "Four against thirty? We die, and the village burns anyway, and the message gets sent twice."

"Then we don't fight," Halvard said. "We *carry*."

What followed, Sebastian would later be unable to count—which was itself a kind of wound, for a man who counted everything. He remembered fragments: the kitchen door bursting open and Marta standing in it with a meat-knife and a face like flint, already herding grandchildren toward the back pasture. The snow going orange. A rider wheeling toward them out of the smoke and Halvard stepping into his path bare-handed, taking the man's torch, breaking his wrist, putting him in the snow—all of it in the time it takes to draw breath. Sefa pulling a dazed child by both arms through a lane of falling embers, her mouth open in a scream that made no sound at all, and Sebastian thinking, with a clarity that felt like madness: *she has been paying for her voice since before she met us, and still she spends what she has.*

And he remembered the windowsill.

Because when they finally cleared the last terrace, driving the widow's surviving family and a handful of neighbors up the sheep-tracks into the rocks, Sebastian looked back—everyone looks back, that is the oldest law of flight—and saw the widow's house still standing, untouched, flames bending around it as if around a stone in a river.

Left standing. Of course. Brann was precise. The house that sheltered them would be spared, so that the village would know exactly whom to hate.

Except that as he watched, a figure detached itself from the smoke at the edge of the yard—small, brown, quick, root-handed—and went *back inside*. Toward the sound of crying that Sebastian hadn't let himself hear until that moment: someone left behind, someone trapped, someone the fire had gotten to first.

"Marta—" He was turning. Halvard caught his arm, grip like a shackle.

"She made her choice," the knight said, and his voice cracked on it, and Sebastian understood that Halvard had made the same kind of choice once, ten years ago, and had been paying for it every day since.

"I'm done," Sebastian said quietly, "with other people making choices about what I get to keep."

He broke Halvard's grip—it surprised them both—and ran back down into the burning.

He found her in the smoke of her own kitchen, dragging the miller's grandson—the boy returned in autumn, the boy with the ruined hands—toward the door, and together, the forgettable man and the stubborn widow, they got him out into the snow. Behind them the roof beam went, and the little dish of gilded salt on the windowsill went with it, into the heart of the fire, and Sebastian thought, absurdly, of guild-law, of payment, of the price of things.

They knelt in the snow with the boy coughing between them, and up the slope the villagers watched their houses burn, and no one spoke, because there was nothing yet that could be said.

* * *

Dawn found them in a shepherd's cave high above the valley, the survivors huddled under wool, the village below them smoking like a snuffed candle.

Sebastian sat apart, at the cave mouth, doing the only thing he knew how to do. He had taken Odile's spare journal and he was writing. Not poisons. Not routes. He wrote the miller's name. The baker's. The names of the two who hadn't come out. He wrote *Marta Bren, widow, saved the boy, lost her house, lost her gilded salt.* He wrote each entry in his taster's hand, precise and unadorned, because precision was the only prayer he knew.

Odile settled beside him, stiff and smelling of smoke. For a while she said nothing. Then:

"You understand now why I ran from the guild for six years."

He didn't answer.

"We told ourselves it was mercy, what we traded. Grief for plenty. Pain for peace. And the small folk *did* eat, Sebastian. You saw it. You'll see it again in every village between here and the capital, and part of you will want to stop, will want to say *look what the salt-king built, maybe the cage was kind.*" She pulled her knees up. "And then they burn a village to send a message about four people, and you understand. Kindness that costs the powerless nothing is real. Kindness that can be revoked with a torch was never kindness. It was *credit*. And credit is called in."

Below them, the smoke thinned. Somewhere down there, Marta Bren was standing in the ashes of her kitchen, and Sebastian had put her there.

No. Brann had put her there. Brann and the thing that wore Aldric's crown and dreamed, apparently, of being loved.

But knowing where the guilt properly belonged did not lift it. Guilt never reads its ledgers correctly. That was the first lesson of the trade.

Sefa came and sat on his other side. She didn't draw anything at first. Then she opened her book to a fresh page and drew, slowly, carefully: a dish of salt on a windowsill. Underneath it, she drew a flame. And underneath *that*, holding the page so he couldn't look away, she drew a single figure walking toward a great gate in a mountain of pale stone—and she pointed, with one smoke-stained finger, down the valley, south, toward the capital.

Toward the Hollow Court.

"Yes," Sebastian said. His voice came out rough. "I know."

Halvard appeared at the cave mouth with the widow's eldest son, who had agreed, for reasons of his own, to guide them two days along the high passes before turning back. The knight looked at Sebastian, and there was something new in his face—not trust, not yet, but the beginning of the shape trust leaves behind.

"Brann will expect us to scatter now," Halvard said. "Or to hide. He burned that village to teach us that our road is paved in other people's blood—that every mile costs innocents, so that decent men turn back." He spat into the snow. "So we don't turn back. We walk faster."

Sebastian closed the journal. In the last line of the page, beneath the names, he wrote one more entry, small and final, in the hand he used for verdicts:

*Paid in full. The rest is owed.*

Then he stood, and shouldered his pack, and turned south with the others, down out of the mountains, toward the palace where a salt-born king sat dreaming of love, and a chief inquisitor sat sharpening his next example.

Behind them, in the ruined kitchen of a burned house, on a windowsill that no longer existed, a little dish of gilded salt had melted into the fire—

—and somewhere far below, in the deep places under the capital, something ancient stirred in its brine, and dreamed, for the first time in ten years, of being *thirsty*.
