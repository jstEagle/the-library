## 13. Betrayal in the Salt Marshes

The marshes began three days before I betrayed them, and I have wondered since whether the betrayal did not begin earlier still—whether it began the night I stood in the palace corridor and heard a king weep behind a door that had never, in eleven years of service, been locked against me.

I tell myself it began with the signal. That is the kinder accounting. A man must keep his ledgers somewhere, even a man who has burned his own.

* * *

We came into the salt marshes on a morning of low grey light, the kind that makes no shadows and therefore makes no liars. The ground had gone soft and brackish beneath us for two days, stitched with channels of standing water the color of pewter. Reeds grew taller than Sefa's head. The air tasted of iron and old tide, and every footfall made a sound like a mouth opening.

Odile hated it immediately.

"This place is a soup," she announced, picking her way along a causeway of packed shell. "A cold, sentient soup that wants my boots. Nothing good has ever happened in a marsh. Name one thing."

"The Battle of Redford Crossing," Halvard said.

"We lost Redford Crossing."

"I was there," I said. "It was very memorable."

"You would remember," Odile muttered. "That's your whole tragedy, taster. You remember everything and no one remembers you."

I did not answer, because she was wrong in a way that mattered, and because ahead of us Sefa had stopped walking.

She stood at the edge of a channel where the water lay perfectly flat, mirror-still despite the wind that moved the reeds around her. She crouched, dipped two fingers, and drew on the wet mud at her feet: quick strokes, sure ones—a bird, then the bird crossed out, then a fish, then an arrow pointing down into the water.

"Fish," Odile translated. "There are fish. Wonderful. We'll dine like kings."

Sefa shook her head, impatient, and drew again: the fish, and beside it a small figure kneeling at the water's edge, and beside the figure a cupped hand.

"She's saying there's food here," I said slowly, "if you know how to take it. Her father's maps covered the coast. She grew up in places like this."

Sefa nodded once, briskly, the way a schoolmistress nods at a slow pupil, and I felt something in my chest turn over—the warm, foolish thing I had been trying not to feel since the flooded quarter. Family, my mind supplied, unhelpfully. You are collecting one.

Halvard crouched beside her and studied the drawing without touching it. He had taken to doing that—studying her drawings as though they were dispatches from a front he'd once held. He asked her questions with gestures now, clumsy but sincere: *how deep, how far, which direction*. She answered in lines. Between them they had built a language that needed neither voice nor literacy, and watching it had become, for me, a private and painful pleasure.

"Channels run east," Halvard said, straightening. "Deep enough to hide our wake if we use the reed boats. Two days through the marsh and we're past Brann's riders entirely. They'll expect us to swing north toward the high road."

"They'll expect it because it's sensible," Odile said. "Which is why I distrust it."

"It's why I chose it," Halvard said.

He said it easily. He said everything easily; that was the armor of him, the polished surface of a man who had spent ten years being sorry in public. I believed him. That is the fact I cannot forgive myself for: not the signal, not the letter, but the believing. I had built a small faith out of his shame, out of the careful way he looked at Sefa's drawings, out of the night he'd stood watch without being asked and let me sleep four hours whole. I had decided a man who carried that much regret could not carry treachery too.

Regret, it turns out, weighs nothing. It takes up no room at all. A man can pack it alongside anything.

* * *

We camped that first night on a shell island barely larger than a kitchen, a midden of some forgotten fisher-folk, heaped with broken nets and the white vertebrae of things long eaten. Odile brewed something bitter over a smokeless fire and pronounced it medicinal, which meant undrinkable. Sefa sat close to me, shoulder almost touching mine, sketching by the light of the coals—not the map tonight, but faces. She drew Odile mid-scold, all elbows and fury. She drew Halvard with his chin up, looking at something far away. Then she drew me, and paused, and frowned at her own work, and drew it again.

When she turned it toward me I understood why she'd frowned. She had drawn me smiling. She tried again. The second attempt was the same. She stared at the page a long moment, then at me, then deliberately, defiantly, kept the smile.

I did not know what to do with that. I put another log on the fire and told myself the smoke was why my eyes stung.

Halvard took first watch. He always volunteered. I thought then: *a man making up debts*. I think now: *a man keeping appointments*.

* * *

On the second day the marsh changed character. The channels widened, the water darkened, and the reeds gave way in places to stands of dead trees, black and salt-bleached, rising from the shallows like the masts of a drowned fleet. Birds stopped singing. Not gradually—all at once, between one channel and the next, as though the marsh itself had closed its mouth.

Sefa noticed first. She caught my sleeve and pointed: a heron, standing in the shallows, motionless. Dead. Standing. Preserved upright in the salt water like a thing in a glass case.

"Brine-pool," Odile said quietly. All her flippancy had gone. "Super-saturated. Anything that dies in it doesn't rot. It just... stays." She looked at the heron a long time. "The guild used to call these places honest. Everything they touch keeps its shape forever. No decay, no change, no mercy of forgetting." Her jaw tightened. "The impostor's country is getting bigger."

We went around the brine-pools in a wide silence, and I caught Halvard looking back at them twice, three times, with an expression I catalogued—I, the great cataloguer—as unease. It was not unease. It was recognition. He knew what was coming, and he was measuring it.

That evening, while Odile argued with her own alchemical notes and Sefa slept curled against my side like a comma, Halvard sat across the fire from me and asked, casually, terribly casually:

"When we reach the capital. What happens to the rest of us? After the king is restored?"

"I hadn't got that far," I admitted.

"Pardons," he said. "There'd have to be pardons. For everyone who helped." His eyes were on the fire. "Even those who—" He stopped. Started again. "Some men did worse than help. Some men opened doors."

"Halvard—"

"A knight who abandons his post," he said, "doesn't get to be forgiven by winning. That's not how the stories go. The stories are written by people who weren't there." He looked up, and his face in the firelight was open, wrecked, pleading in a way he probably didn't know. "What if a man wanted to come in from the cold, Sebastian? What if he'd do anything—"

"Then he'd walk in with us," I said. "And stand with us. That's the toll."

Something moved behind his eyes—relief or grief or arithmetic—and he nodded and said, "Yes. Of course. That's the toll." And I went to sleep believing I had settled the matter.

I had given him the answer, you understand. I had handed him the choice plainly, and he had weighed it, and I mistook the weight of his silence for the weight of shame. It was the weight of a price being compared against a price.

* * *

It rained in the night, a thin salt-hazed drizzle, and in the grey before dawn I woke to find Halvard gone from the watch position and Sefa standing rigid at the edge of the island, staring south, her whole body a drawn bow.

I followed her gaze.

Out across the flats, where the dawn should have been empty water and reed, there were lights. Lanterns. Many lanterns, strung out in a line that could only be a search formation, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who knew exactly where they were going.

"No," Odile said behind me. She had seen. "No, no—they can't know the channels, the channels aren't on any map since her father—" and then she stopped, because we all stopped, because the same thought arrived in all three heads at the same instant, wearing different words but the same face.

The channels weren't on any map. Except the one in a cartographer's memory. And the one in the head of a royal guard who had spent ten years studying routes of retreat.

Sefa turned and looked at Halvard's bedroll. Empty. His sword gone with him.

"He went out before midnight," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "He said the reed beds needed checking."

Odile laughed—one short, awful bark. "And we let him. We *let* him." She pressed both hands over her face. "Gods. Gods, I argued for him. In the flooded quarter I said trust the big sad fool, grief makes men clumsy but honest—"

"Sefa." I touched her shoulder. She was shaking. Not crying—she did not cry, I had learned that; her tears had been part of her father's payment, or so Odile guessed—but shaking like a struck bell. She looked at me and her eyes said it more clearly than any drawing ever had: *I drew him smiling at something far away. I drew him on the first night. I saw it and I kept it anyway.*

The lanterns were closer. There was no longer any question of reaching the reed boats. Behind us, the only other direction, rose the black stands of the dead trees and, beyond them, the vast white gleam of the brine-pools.

"Options," Odile said, and her voice had gone hard and quick, the laboratory voice, the voice that solved problems with whatever was on the bench. "We run for the boats anyway and die wet. We hide in the dead trees and they burn us out by noon. Or—"

She didn't finish, because Halvard walked out of the reeds.

He came alone, unarmed, hands open at his sides, walking the slow deliberate walk of a man approaching a horse he might spook. Behind him, at the edge of visibility, the lantern-line had halted. Waiting.

"Don't," he said, before anyone could speak. "Whatever you're about to say—don't. Let me say it and then hate me properly, because you'll do it better with the facts."

Nobody spoke. The marsh dripped. Somewhere a dead heron stood in the water, keeping its shape forever.

"Three nights ago I sent a signal from the ridge above the mill village. A rider's lantern, shuttered—three flashes, pause, three. Brann's code; I helped write it, years ago, when I was still a man the crown trusted." His voice did not waver. He had decided to spend his shame like coin, all at once, and be done. "I told them the route. I told them the channels. In exchange I asked for one thing. Not pardon. Passage. My exile lifted, my name entered back in the rolls, and safe conduct for—" his eyes went to Sefa and could not stay there— "for the girl. Only the girl. I told them she was coerced, a child of a traitor, blameless. I tried to buy her innocence with my guilt. It was the only currency I had left."

"You could have had ours," I said quietly. "You could have had it for free. I told you. Walk in with us. Stand with us."

"And if we lost?" Halvard's composure cracked at last, just at the seam. "If the antidote fails, if the impostor tastes your blood and laughs—if we all hang, Sebastian, what does *standing with you* buy her then? A grave with company?" He dragged breath through his teeth. "I've watched brave men die for ten years in my dreams. I wanted—gods help me, I wanted one life saved that I could *point to*. One. Even bought dirty."

"That's not how it works," Odile said. Her voice was strange—furious and sorrowful at once. "You don't get to commit us to save her. Our lives weren't yours to stake."

"I know."

"You signaled from the *ridge*, Halvard. The ridge above the widow's village. Did they need the route, or did they need to know where we'd slept?" She stepped toward him. "Did they already know about the village when they burned it?"

The silence went on too long. That was the answer.

Sefa made a sound.

It was not a word. It was smaller than a word—a single note, low and cracked, the sound a door makes when it hasn't been opened in years. Then she turned her back on him. Deliberately, fully, the way you turn your back on a stranger in the street, and walked to the far edge of the island, and stood facing the water with her arms wrapped around herself, and did not look at him again.

Of everything that morning, that was the thing that broke him. I watched it happen. I watched ten years of exile and one night of treachery meet in his face, and I watched him understand that the child he had tried to purchase innocence for had just priced him beyond redemption.

"Sebastian." Odile touched my arm. "Say something. Whatever you decide—we follow it. But say it."

They were all looking at me. The lanterns waited in the grey. And I found that I had nothing—no verdict, no sentence, no ledger entry clean enough to write. I had spent ten years tasting for poison and I knew every bitterness there was, and none of them was this.

So I gave him the only judgment I had.

I said nothing. I turned away, the way Sefa had turned, and began gathering the packs, and behind me I heard Halvard breathe in as though the silence had struck him harder than any blade, and I heard Odile mutter, "Oh, that's cruel, taster. That's cruelest thing you could have done," and I heard Halvard say, to no one, in a voice scraped hollow:

"Yes. Yes, it's what I deserve. Silence and the road."

But the marsh was not finished with judgments. Because at that moment the lantern-line moved—not toward us, but apart, splitting, and from the dead trees to the north came a sound I knew from ten years of palace service and had hoped never to hear in open country: the high, carrying note of an inquisitor's whistle, three tones, ascending.

*Come out. Come out. The king is generous.*

And out of the reeds between us and the boats rose riders—six of them, eight, ten, closing the water in a slow crescent, and at their center, dismounting with the unhurried care of a man arriving at his own dinner table, Inquisitor Brann.

He looked older than his portrait in my memory of the palace halls. The marsh light was unkind to him. He smiled at Halvard first, warmly, horribly.

"Ser Halvard. Punctual as ever." Then his eyes found me, and the warmth dropped away like a mask on a string. "Master Kavanagh. His Majesty asks after your health daily. He wonders—" the smile returned, thinner— "what immunity *tastes* like, from the inside."

Behind me, Sefa's hand found mine in the dark. Her grip said: *whatever comes, we are still a family, minus one.*

Ahead, Brann spread his hands, magnanimous as a host.

"Well," he said. "Shall we discuss terms?"
