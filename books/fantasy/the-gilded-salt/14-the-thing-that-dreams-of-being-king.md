## 14. The Thing That Dreams of Being King

The king dreamed in brine.

That was the first thing it had ever known—before the crown, before the name, before the long corridors and the bowed heads and the taste of wine at evening—it had dreamed in brine, warm and close and without edges, the way a thing dreams before it has decided to be anything at all.

It did not remember being born. It remembered *arriving*, which was different. It had come up through the salt like water coming up through stone, patient, inevitable, drawn by an emptiness so vast it had seemed less like a wound than like an invitation. Ten years ago there had been a man above the ground who grieved, and his grief had been traded away piece by piece by men with careful hands and careful ledgers, and where the grief had been there was a hollow shaped exactly like a king.

The hollow had called. The salt had answered.

Now it sat upon the throne in the body of King Aldric the Third, wearing his face the way one wears a coat cut for another man—correct at every seam, and yet never quite warm enough at the shoulders. It wore his memories too. Those were harder. The memories came to it secondhand, pressed into its substance during the long years of the trade, and they fit as poorly as the coat. It knew the smell of the queen's hair from a memory that was not its own, and when it reached for the feeling beneath the smell—the love, the particular gravity of her—it found only the shape where the feeling used to be, like a hand closing around a space in the air.

They had sold the grief away, the guild-men. But grief, it had learned, is not a single thing. Grief is load-bearing. Take it out of a man and everything built on top of it sags: the joy sags, the patience sags, the love itself sags into something the shape of love but lighter, hollower, ringing faintly when struck.

*Your Majesty.*

The voice came through the salt. All voices came through the salt; it was how the palace spoke to him, or he to himself, or both—he had never fully untangled which. The walls of Vellmarin Keep were mortared with it, grain by grain, laid down over ten years by masons who believed they were following the royal architect's new specifications. Every corridor hummed faintly. Every footstep reported itself. He was, in a sense, never alone in the palace, and yet—

And yet.

He rose from the dreaming chair—a chair of black oak beside the eastern window, where the true king had once liked to watch the harbor fog burn off—and went to the window himself. Below, the city of Carreth spread out in the morning light like a spilled purse of coins. Roofs. Markets. Smoke rising straight up in the windless cold. Children, small as beetles from this height, running along the canal paths.

He watched them the way he watched everything: hungrily, and without comprehension.

*Why do they suffer?*

He asked the question aloud some mornings, to the empty room, because the empty room was the only thing that never flinched. The reports came to him daily—Brann's reports, neat and terrible—and he read them all, every line, because a king reads everything. Grain riots in the western quarter. A weaver hanged for sedition whose sedition, when Brann read the man's letters aloud in the council chamber, amounted to saying the king had changed. A mother in the flooded quarter who had drowned herself rather than be separated from her children during the purges.

He did not understand any of it. That was the truth no one dared tell him, and the truth he could not stop circling. He had made the bread cheaper. He had ended the border war in its second year—not out of mercy, but because war required a kind of passionate arithmetic he could not perform, and it had seemed simpler to simply *stop*. He had opened the granaries. He had pardoned debtors. By every measure the ledgers could hold, his reign was kinder than the reign of the man whose face he wore, that gray, grieving, half-present king who had ruled for six years with his dead wife's name always halfway to his lips.

And still they whispered. Still they burned effigies in the night. Still they looked at him across a throne room with eyes full of something he could see but not read, the way a man born blind can feel heat but cannot say what fire looks like.

*I am better than he was,* he told the window glass, and his breath fogged it, and the fog was the closest thing to an answer he ever received. *I am better, and they hate me for it, and I do not know why.*

There was one memory he returned to more than the others, though it was among the poorest fits. It belonged to Aldric, and it was very old—the king had been perhaps seven—and in it he stood in his mother's kitchen while she salted a winter ham, and she let him press one finger into the coarse crystals and touch them to his tongue, and she laughed and said, *Careful, little lord. Salt remembers everyone who takes it freely.*

Salt remembers. He had built an entire architecture on that promise. Salt remembered footsteps and voices and the weight of bodies in beds. Salt remembered the true king, sleeping far below, wrapped in threads of it finer than spider silk, dreaming the slow dream that kept him from waking into ten lost years all at once. Salt remembered everything except, apparently, how to make the people love what wore their king's face.

* * *

Inquisitor Brann arrived at the tenth bell, as he always did, with his boots polished and his reports bound in gray leather and his face arranged into the expression of a man who feels nothing—which was, the salt-king had long suspected, its own kind of costume, though he could not have said what it concealed.

"You have news," the king said. Not a question. The body he wore had a voice like gravel rolled in honey, and he had learned to use it, but he noticed that he still phrased things as statements. Questions required genuine uncertainty, and genuine uncertainty required wanting, and wanting was the one garment Aldric had left behind that had never fit him at all.

"Three of them crossed the tollbridge at Marrow Ford four nights ago." Brann set the report on the table between them and did not open it; he had learned that the king preferred to be told. "The taster, Sebastian Kavanagh. The alchemist Odile Vane. And a girl—Sefa, daughter of the cartographer Maren, executed two years past. They paid passage."

"Paid with what? The bridge takes coin of a particular mint."

"Memories, Majesty. As it always has." Brann's jaw tightened fractionally. "The girl paid her name. The alchemist paid her wedding day. The taster—" a pause, delicate as a surgeon's probe, "—paid his mother's face."

The salt-king turned this over. Somewhere below his feet, deep in the foundations, the sleeping man shifted in his dream, and the whole palace felt it the way a ship feels a whale passing beneath the keel—a great slow displacement, a pressure against the hull of the world.

"And the fourth?" he said. "Your report says three crossed. Reports also say a knight rides with them. Sir Halvard. My own guard, once."

Brann's face did something complicated and brief, there and gone like a fish under ice. "Halvard is mine to manage, Majesty."

"He was mine to manage once too. He stood outside my door the night I 'recovered' from my illness, and then he stood outside no doors at all for ten years." The king let the silence stretch. He had learned silences from Aldric's memories—from watching, secondhand, the living king deploy them in council—and they were the one borrowed tool that worked perfectly, because silence requires nothing of the wearer. "You were guild, before you were mine. You know what the map is. You know what it opens. So you will understand why I am about to say what I say next, Inquisitor, and you will not mistake it for sentiment."

"Majesty."

"Bring me the taster. Alive. Unmarked. Whatever else burns, whatever else drowns, whatever else your riders must do in the marsh country—Kavanagh comes to me breathing, and Kavanagh comes to me *whole*."

Something moved behind Brann's eyes—not fear, the king thought, but the older cousin of fear, the thing fear grows from when it is left long enough in the dark. Recognition, perhaps. Or grief. "May I ask why?"

"No," said the king. Then, because the body he wore had been a just man once, and justice leaked through the seams sometimes like light under a door: "Yes. Sit down, Brann. I will tell you a thing I have never told anyone, which is easy, since there is no one I could tell it to."

Brann sat.

"I want to taste what immunity feels like."

The words came out of him strangely, and he heard the strangeness himself. He had rehearsed this sentence for eleven days, alone in the dreaming chair, testing it against the dark the way a tongue tests a cracked tooth.

"Ten years ago," he said, "your guild fed this body poison. Small doses, measured doses, doses fine as flour sifted through cloth—enough to keep the true king weak enough to trade with, strong enough to survive the trading. Every dose passed through a taster's mouth before it passed through mine. Do you understand what that means, Inquisitor? For ten years, every poison meant for me touched another man's blood first. His body learned each of them. His blood carries the ledger of everything I am." He rose, and walked to the window again, because looking at Brann while saying these things made the words snag. "I have tasted everything, and I am immune to nothing. Do you know what I mean by that? Wine tastes of wet stone. Bread tastes of nothing at all—I eat it because the body requires it, and the body is a furnace that does not care what it burns. When the queen died—when *his* queen died—I felt the court's grief move through me like weather through an empty house. Doors banging. Nothing staying."

Behind him, Brann said nothing. The silence had a texture now. The king pressed on, because he had started, and starting was the hard part, and if he stopped he would never begin again.

"The taster's blood holds ten years of every toxin this court ever brewed, survived, mastered, *answered*. When I drink from him—or when his blood enters mine by whatever means your alchemists devise—I will finally taste what it is to be unkillable. To take the world into your mouth and have it fail to hurt you." He turned. "Do you understand? I have worn this man's crown, his face, his bed, his memories, his kingdom. I have worn everything he owned. I want the one thing he *was*. I want to know what it feels like to swallow the world and live."

For a long moment the only sound was the faint crystalline hum of the walls, the salt remembering them both.

"That is not why you want him," Brann said quietly.

The king regarded him. Of all his servants, only Brann ever spoke back, and the king had never decided whether this was treason or the last courtesy left in the kingdom.

"It is reason enough for a report," the king said. "You may write it down exactly so, and your riders may believe it, and it may even be true. But yes. There is another reason, and it is this: he is the only man alive who has eaten from my table every day for ten years and lived. He knows my food. He knows my household. He has stood nearer to me, meal after meal after meal, than any priest, any wife, any friend the true king ever had—and he did it invisibly, which means he *chose* to be near me, or near the shape of me, ten thousand times, and never once asked for anything." The king's borrowed hands curled slowly at his sides. "Everyone in this kingdom wants something from the throne. Everyone who approaches wants. This man approached for a decade and wanted *nothing*, and that, Inquisitor—that is the rarest flavor there is. I have never tasted it. I do not believe anyone has ever offered it to me."

"You want him to be your friend," Brann said, and there was something awful in how gently he said it.

"I want him brought to me alive," the king said, "and I want the rest of it left unwritten."

* * *

That night he went down.

He went down the way he always went, alone, dismissing the guard with a flick of the hand that Aldric's muscles performed without instruction. The stair spiraled into the rock beneath the keep, older than the keep, older than the dynasty, and the salt thickened around him as he descended until the air itself tasted faintly of the sea that had never been within a hundred leagues of this place.

At the bottom was the chamber the maps did not show. At the center of the chamber was the bed of white crystal, grown rather than carved, and in the bed lay King Aldric the Third, the true one, translucent as sea-glass, threaded through with filaments of salt so fine they shone like frost on a web.

The impostor knelt beside him. It always knelt. It did not know why kneeling felt correct here; none of the borrowed memories accounted for it. Perhaps the body remembered reverence even when the mind wearing it could not produce any.

"Hello," it said. It had tried other words over the years. *Majesty. Brother. Myself.* Hello was best. Hello was what you said to someone whose room you entered hoping to be welcome.

The sleeping king did not answer. He never answered. But his face moved sometimes, in the dreaming, and the impostor had spent years learning to read those movements the way sailors read water.

Tonight the sleeping brow furrowed. Tonight the translucent lips parted slightly, and a sound came out—not a word, but the ghost of one, a syllable dissolving even as it formed.

The impostor leaned close. Salt remembered, and salt carried, and through the lattice of threads it caught the fragment as it passed: a name. Not the queen's name. Not the impostor's borrowed one.

*Kavanagh.* Or something very like it. Spoken the way one speaks a name one has trusted at ten thousand breakfasts without ever once looking at the man who bore it.

The impostor stayed kneeling a long time after the sound faded. Above them, the palace hummed. Beyond the walls, riders were already moving into the marsh country with torches and orders, and somewhere out in that darkness a thin, forgettable man was walking toward the capital carrying a map written in shifting ink, and the impostor found—to its considerable surprise—that what it felt was not triumph, or hunger, or even the cold strategic satisfaction it had rehearsed.

It felt hope. Thin and briny and strange, like the first breath of a thing surfacing after a decade underwater.

"Hurry," it whispered to the sleeping king, to the dark, to the distant taster it had never met and already missed. "Hurry, and come to dinner. I have kept your seat."

Far above, in the dreaming chair by the eastern window, the fog on the glass had not cleared. And in the fog, though no finger had touched it, a shape was forming—small, deliberate, unmistakable: the outline of a hand, pressed flat against the pane from the outside, five fingers and a palm, waiting to be let in.
