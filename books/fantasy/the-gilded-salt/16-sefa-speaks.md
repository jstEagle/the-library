## 16. Sefa Speaks

The capital announced itself before we saw it.

We had walked three days through the salt marshes with the taste of betrayal still in our mouths—Halvard's confession hanging between us like a blade no one would pick up—and on the morning of the fourth day the wind changed and brought us the smell of the city: woodsmoke and tallow and bread baking in a thousand ovens, and beneath it all, faint as a watermark, the mineral breath of brine that I had known all my life without ever once questioning it.

I stopped on the ridge road and looked down at Vellamere, and my chest did something strange.

Ten years I had lived inside those walls. Ten years of dawn corridors and tasting spoons, of ledgers written in my small precise hand, of being a shadow so complete that even the shadows forgot me. I had thought of the palace as a cage only in retrospect; at the time it had simply been the shape of my life, comfortable as an old boot. Now, seeing it from above—the white walls, the salt-glazed roofs throwing back the early light like scales, the great banner of the crowned stag drooping windless from every tower—I felt what Odile must have felt standing before her ruined shop. The particular grief of recognizing a thing you loved as the instrument of your ruin.

"It's beautiful," Sefa said.

I turned. She hadn't spoken—of course she hadn't—but her hands said it, quick and certain, and then she pointed at the city and made the sign she used for *home*, which was two fingers pressed to the breastbone and drawn outward, like pulling something precious out of your own chest and offering it.

Odile snorted from where she crouched over her pack, rewrapping the vials she had carried across half a kingdom. "It's a trap with good architecture," she said. "Look at the gates. Look at the walls. Brann knows we're coming. He's known since the marshes."

"He's known longer than that," I said quietly.

All three of us did not look at Halvard, who stood apart at the edge of the ridge with his back to us, watching the city he had sworn to guard and then abandoned. He had not asked for forgiveness. He had not explained himself beyond the confession he'd already given—that he had signaled our position to Brann's riders in the desperate arithmetic of exile, trading four lives for the chance to come home. He simply stood there, gray in the gray light, wearing his shame the way other men wore cloaks, and waited to see if we would cut him loose or carry him.

Sefa crossed the grass to him. We all watched. She took his gauntleted hand—the hand that had held the gate at Thornmere, the hand that had killed six riders to buy us the marsh crossing after his signal had brought them—and she turned it palm-up, and into it she placed a smooth white stone she had been carrying since the flooded quarter.

Then she signed something long and fluid that I could not follow, though I caught the shapes for *debt* and *water* and *again*.

Halvard closed his fist around the stone. His shoulders shook once, the way a wall shakes when something enormous strikes it on the far side, and then he was still again.

"She says the debt runs both ways now," Odile translated, not looking up from her vials. "She says a man who saves you after betraying you has paid more honestly than a man who never erred. Her father wrote that. In the margins of a map." She tied off the last knot. "Come on. If we're going to die at those gates, I'd rather do it before noon, while the light's still honest."

* * *

We came down to the city with the market crowds, because a wolf is safest among sheep and we were four tired travelers among ten thousand morning bodies. Odile had dyed her hair with walnut ink and bound her breasts flat under a carter's smock; Halvard had surrendered his sword to the bottom of a flour barrel and gone armless, shoulderless, a big soft-bodied drover leading a mule we didn't own. Sefa wore a hood and kept her eyes down, drawing nothing, which for her was its own kind of agony—I could see her fingers twitching against her thigh, sketching the world in invisible lines because she could not bear to see it unrecorded.

And I wore my old self. That was the strangest part. Somewhere between the marshes and the ridge road I had realized that the best disguise in Vellamere was the one I had worn for a decade: the forgettable man, the taster, the ledger-keeper whose face slid off memory like grease off slate. I put on my servant's gray, my ink-stained cuffs, my expression of pleasant nothing, and I felt the crowd's eyes pass over me like water over oiled cloth, and I hated how much it comforted me.

My father sold this, I thought, walking through the gate square where I had passed a thousand times unremarked. My father stood in some guild office and traded away my very presence in the world, so that I would be safe, so that no one would ever look twice—

So that I would be alone.

"Left," Odile murmured. "The chandlers' row. There's a cellar door behind the tallow-renderer's that connects to the old guild tunnels. Sefa's father drew them."

Sefa confirmed it with a rapid sequence of signs: the tunnel ran beneath the merchant quarter and surfaced inside the palace undercrofts, within forty paces of the sealed stair that led down to the Hollow Court. Forty paces. After everything—the tollbridge and the memories bleeding out of us, the burning village, the marshes, the betrayal—we were going to walk into the heart of the lie through a door behind a candle shop.

That was when the bells began.

Not the hour bells. Not the alarm bells either, at first—something worse, something I recognized from my ten years in the palace and had hoped never to hear from outside it: the *processional*. The salt-king was coming out. The impostor was leaving the palace, in daylight, in state, and every bell in Vellamere was announcing it.

The crowd around us changed texture instantly. People pressed toward the processional route, faces lifting with genuine gladness—faces like the widow Marla's in the mountain village, faces that had known real kindness from the thing on the throne—and the streets emptied toward the grand avenue like water finding a drain.

"The tunnels," Odile breathed. "Now. While every eye is on the avenue—"

"No," Halvard said.

We all stopped. He was staring up the processional route, at the far end where the palace gates stood open, and his face had gone the color of old tallow.

"What?" I said.

"The gates." His voice was very quiet. "They're not open for a procession. Look at the guard disposition. Pikes facing *outward*. Chain rigged across the archway. That's not ceremony. That's a trap with the mouth propped open." He turned to me, and there was something terrible in his eyes—not fear for himself but the specific horror of a soldier who has finally learned to read a battlefield correctly, one lesson too late. "He doesn't want to catch us sneaking in. He wants us to *try*. The whole procession—it's theater. It's bait. Every guard in the city is positioned to funnel anyone moving against the flow straight into the open gate, and whatever's waiting inside the archway—"

He didn't finish, because the ground finished for him.

A sound rolled up from beneath the city, deep and grinding, like a millstone the size of a cathedral turning over once. The cobbles shivered. Somewhere ahead of us, along the processional route, a chimney collapsed in a bloom of dust, and the crowd's cheer faltered, wavered, became something confused and frightened.

And Sefa screamed.

There is no preparing for that sound. I have heard men scream in poison-sweats, heard women scream at burnings, heard Halvard scream in the marshes with a rider's lance through his shoulder. None of it was this. This was a voice that had been locked away for years—a voice paid away, traded by her father along with whatever else the guild took from him—coming out of her all at once, raw and rusted and enormous, cracking on its first word and refusing to stop:

"*THE BRIDGE!*"

Every head within thirty feet snapped toward her. I grabbed her arm—her voice, gods, her actual voice, hoarse and young and shaking—and she was pointing past me, past the crowd, past the processional route, to the old canal bridge that spanned the sunken quarter between the chandlers' row and the merchant district. The bridge we would have crossed to reach the cellar door. The bridge that stood directly on the guild tunnel line.

"The bridge," she said again, and her voice broke apart on the second word into something between speech and sobbing, and she was signing with her free hand even as she spoke, doubling herself, saying it twice in two languages because one apparently wasn't enough anymore: "*It's going to fall. They've cut it. They'll drop it when we're on it. Papa's map—the supports—they're salt-cut, they're hollowed—*"

"How does she know—" Odile started.

"I drew them," Sefa said, and now the tears were coming, cutting tracks through the road dust on her face, and she laughed, horribly, wetly, in the middle of it. "I drew them when I was nine. I drew everything Papa drew. I memorized the whole city. I know where every support stands and I can *feel* it, I can feel the map in my head and the bridge is wrong, it's been wrong since yesterday, they've eaten it out from underneath—"

The second grinding sound came, closer, and this time everyone heard it. The crowd began to move—not toward the processional now but away from everything, a slow animal panic rippling outward—and through the thinning bodies I saw them: four figures in gray-and-white livery pushing against the current toward us, and at their head, tall and patient as a headsman, Inquisitor Brann.

"There," Halvard said. "Bridge or no bridge. Move."

* * *

What follows, I will set down as precisely as I can, because precision is the only prayer I have ever known how to say.

We ran for the bridge because there was nowhere else. Behind us, Brann's inquisitors; ahead and below, the sunken quarter with its single span across the canal; and the whole time Sefa running beside me with her ruined voice calling out the bridge's anatomy like a midwife calling a birth—*"third pier is false, stay to the left rail, don't touch the fourth post, it's salt all the way through"—*and Odile shouting back questions about load and span and Sefa answering in words, actual words, spending her voice like a woman spending her last coins, and I understood suddenly and completely what it was costing her. Every sentence was a wound reopened. She spoke anyway.

We reached the span. The old canal lay forty feet below, green-black and stinking, its banks lined with the drowned foundations of the flooded quarter. The bridge was timber on stone piers, sagging, ancient, exactly as unremarkable as I had always found it—and under Sefa's direction we went along the left rail in single file, fast, Halvard last with his empty scabbard swinging, and the boards groaned and the third pier, when I glanced down through a gap, showed me a glimpse of what she meant: stone that wasn't stone, a pale crystalline core where granite should be, riddled through with galleries of emptiness like a honeycomb, like a tooth.

Halfway across, the bells stopped.

In the sudden silence I heard, very clearly, a man's voice from the near end of the bridge, carrying effortlessly the way Brann's voice always carried, reasonable and warm and utterly without mercy:

"Master Kavanagh. You're making this difficult for everyone."

Halvard turned. "Go," he said.

"No," I said.

"Sebastian." He caught my shoulder, and his grip was gentle, which somehow made it worse. "Listen to me. That bridge comes down the moment we're all on it or the moment we're all off it—he wants us in the middle, he wants us *committed*. Someone has to make him spring it early, on his terms instead of ours, and someone has to hold the far side open long enough for you three to get through the cellar door." He smiled, and it was the worst smile I have ever seen, because it was happy. "You need me for exactly one more thing, and it's the thing I'm best at. I held a gate at Carrow Ford for six hours against twice this number. Let me hold this one for five minutes."

"You betrayed us," Odile said. Her voice was shaking. "You signaled Brann in the marshes. Why would he believe—"

"He won't believe anything," Halvard said. "That's rather the point. He'll just kill me, and while he's killing me, you'll be gone." He looked at Sefa. "The stone. Keep it. When this is done—if any of this is ever done—put it on my grave, or don't. Either way the debt's paid."

Sefa made a sound that was not a word in any language, and then it was a word, one word, torn out of her: "*No.*"

"Yes," he said gently. "This is what I'm for. I spent ten years forgetting it."

Behind us, boots on the planks. Brann, unhurried, flanked by his four, and beyond them at the bridgehead a dozen more emerging from the chandlers' row, and beyond *them*, high on the palace walls, the open gate gleaming like a held-open jaw.

"Sir Halvard," Brann said, and there was something almost tender in it. "The exile who keeps coming home. Step aside. Your pardon stands—I keep my offers open longer than most men keep their promises. All of them stand. Even yours, Kavanagh. Even now."

"The king under the palace," I said. "Does your offer cover him too?"

Something moved behind Brann's eyes—grief, maybe, or its fossil. "There is no king under the palace," he said. "There's a dream. There's a poor mad ghost in the dark, and the man on the throne feeds the city better than the ghost ever did. I've made my peace with the arithmetic. Make yours."

"We have," I said. "Ours includes him."

Brann sighed, and raised two fingers, and gave the order that ended the argument: "Drop it."

The grinding came up through the piers, and the bridge *moved*—a lurch, a settling, the left rail dipping—and Sefa shrieked "*NOW,*" and shoved us forward, and Halvard stepped backward into the space we'd vacated, planting himself in the center of the span like a doorpost, arms wide, weaponless, magnificent.

"Five minutes," he called after us. "I've held longer for worse kings."

Then the first inquisitor reached him, and I stopped watching, because Odile had my sleeve and Sefa had my hand and the far end of the bridge was coming apart under our feet in exactly the way Sefa had promised—the false third pier shedding its crystalline heart, the whole span groaning into its death-arc—and we ran the last twenty feet on a bridge that was becoming a ramp, becoming a slide, becoming a memory, and came off the far abutment onto solid cobbles as the entire structure folded behind us into the canal with a sound like the world clearing its throat.

The spray reached us where we stood. Green water, old rot, and salt—salt in the mist, glittering, gilded for one instant in the morning light before it fell.

At the center of the collapsing span, still upright on a tilting island of planks with inquisitors falling away from him into the water on both sides, Halvard looked back at us. He was smiling. He touched two fingers to his breastbone and drew them outward—the sign for *home*, Sefa's sign, the one she'd taught him without either of them noticing—and then the pier went, and the canal took him, and the bridge was gone.

Sefa was screaming his name in her broken, borrowed, beautiful voice, and Odile was holding her up, and I was dragging them both backward into the shadow of the chandlers' row because the fight wasn't over, because Brann was alive—I could see him on the far bank, soaked, furious, alive—and because somewhere behind the tallow-renderer's shop a cellar door was waiting, and forty paces beyond that, a stair, and beneath the stair, a dream.

But Sefa pulled against my grip, once, hard, and pointed back at the water, and signed with her free hand, fierce and final: *he goes in the ledger.*

"Yes," I said. My voice cracked on it. "First page."

* * *

The cellar door opened to Odile's knife-work and Sefa's remembered hinges, and the dark swallowed us, and the last thing I saw of the morning was the palace gate far above, still propped open, still waiting, its trap sprung on nothing.

Forty paces, I told myself, as we went down into the guild's old arteries with the sound of the fallen bridge still ringing in the dark behind us. Forty paces, and then the Hollow Court, and then the truth.

Behind us, faint through the earth, I heard the bells begin again—and this time it was not the processional.

This time, the whole city rang with alarm.
