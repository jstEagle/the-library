## 17. The Hollow Court

The way down was salt.

Not the honest kind—not the coarse grey crystals Odile carried in her satchel, not the flakes Sefa drew with on her flour-dusted table—but something older and stranger, a mineral light that seeped from the walls themselves and turned our shadows into pale ghosts walking ahead of us. The passage beneath the palace had no torches because it needed none. Every surface glowed faintly, like a thing remembered rather than seen.

I counted steps. It was what I did when fear came; I had done it for ten years at the king's table, one dish after another, ledger lines in my head where other men kept prayers. Forty-one steps down the spiral stair. Sixty-three along a corridor that narrowed until Halvard had to turn his shoulders sideways. Then the corridor opened, all at once, like a held breath released, and we stood in the Hollow Court.

It was a throne room.

That was my first thought, absurdly domestic: *it is a throne room, only wrong.* The proportions were almost right—the dais, the pillars, the long sweep of floor where petitioners would kneel—but everything had been built by someone working from memory, and memory rounds off corners. The pillars leaned a degree too far inward. The dais rose in steps that were each a finger's width different in height. And the throne itself—

The throne was made of salt. White, translucent, veined with threads of gold that caught the wall-light and scattered it, so that the whole seat seemed to burn coldly from within. It was beautiful. It was the most beautiful thing I had ever seen, and I wanted to be sick looking at it.

Because it was empty, and it was not meant to be empty, and yet the man we had come for sat upon its steps like a beggar waiting out the rain.

"Sebastian." Odile's voice came hushed behind me. "Don't—"

But I was already moving. Ten years of tasting had taught me one law above all others: when something is wrong with the food, you go toward it. You lean in. You put your tongue to the poison or you die of ignorance. I crossed that impossible throne room with my heart hammering against my ribs like a courier demanding entry, and I knelt before the man who had been King Aldric of Vessany for eleven years before he stopped being anything at all.

He was translucent.

There was no gentler word. His skin had the look of sea-glass, of candle-wax held up to flame—you could see the faint architecture beneath, the shadow of bone, the ghost of blood moving slow as sap through channels grown narrow. His hair was still dark, still threaded with the silver I remembered from state dinners, but it moved slightly, always slightly, as though stirred by water we could not feel. Salt threads ran from his wrists and ankles and throat into the floor, into the walls, into the throne above him—a hundred fine white filaments, glistening, pulsing with a rhythm I recognized.

A heartbeat's rhythm. But not his.

"He dreams," Sefa signed beside me, her hands quick and precise even now. She had come down the last stretch of stairs pressed close to Halvard's back, and now she stood staring at the king with an expression I had never seen her wear: recognition. As if she had drawn this room a hundred times in flour and ash and never believed she'd stand inside it.

"He dreams," the king said.

His voice was the worst part. It was Aldric's voice—I had heard it across the tasting table a thousand mornings, dry and wry, asking me whether the chef had finally learned restraint—and hearing it now, worn thin as paper, was like hearing a song through a wall.

"You're awake," I said. Stupidly. Obvious things are the first to leave me when I'm frightened.

"I am awake." He smiled, and the smile cost him something; I watched it drag across his face like a cart over stones. "That is the cruelty of it, taster. They tell me the sleeping ones are lucky. The dreaming ones drift. But I—" He lifted one glass-pale hand, and the salt threads at his wrist tightened, gleaming. "I have been awake for ten years. Awake, and held under, and made to watch."

"Watch what?"

"Everything." The word broke in half. "Every feast. Every judgment. Every morning he sits in my chair and wears my face and laughs with my laugh, and I am here, underneath, feeling all of it pass through me like water through a net. Do you know what it is to attend your own life as a guest? To taste every meal you are never given?" His eyes found mine then, and they were Aldric's eyes, sharp and brown and terribly tired. "You would know. You tasted for me. I remember your face. It was the only face that never changed."

Something in my chest turned over. I had spent ten years being forgettable, invisible, unremarkable—and here was a man who had been imprisoned beneath a copy of himself, and the one thread of continuity he had clung to was *me*. The man whose job was to be nothing.

"The impostor," Odile said, coming forward, her voice taking on the clipped cadence of her laboratory notes. "The salt-born. It draws from you. The threads—it feeds on your memories, your grief, the shape of your kingship, and it wears them. That's why it can't be unmade by force. Compelled blood only feeds it. We know. We've—" She stopped. Swallowed. "We helped build this. The guild. Ten years ago. We traded away his grief, and something came to fill the hollow."

"I know what the guild did." The king said it without heat, which was somehow worse than fury. "I have had ten years to understand it. My grief was killing me, they told me later. My queen dead, my son dead, and I sat in this court unable to rule, unable to eat, unable to sleep. So the guild came with their ledgers and their scales, and they offered to take the weight away. And I said yes." He laughed, a small terrible sound. "I signed for it. Remember that, alchemist, when you hate yourself. I signed."

Odile's face went white. Sefa reached out and touched her sleeve.

"But the trade left a hollow," the king went on, "and hollows want filling. And something answered. Something that had been waiting in the salt for a very long time, wearing patience the way I once wore a crown. It took everything they traded—my grief, my rage, my terrible love—and it grew itself a body from the pattern. And the guild realized too late what they had midwifed, and they ran, and they hid, and they were hunted one by one." His gaze moved over us: Odile, Sefa, Halvard standing grim at the stair. "And now they've brought me a taster."

"They brought you a key," I said.

"A key." He looked at his own translucent hands. "Do you know what waking means? Have any of them told you?"

* * *

They had not. Odile had explained the antidote theory in her rapid-fire way—every toxin, willingly given, the accumulated poisons in my blood as solvent—and I had understood the mechanics of it the way I understood any recipe: precisely, and without imagining the taste.

"Waking means ten years arriving at once," the king said. "Every hour I have been held under will come due. All of it. The feasts I watched. The executions. The day he—" his voice caught, steadied— "the day he gave my son's name to a new heir, some child of distant blood, and the court wept with joy, and I felt the joy because he feels it and I feel everything he feels, and I felt my son erased and called it happiness." The salt threads pulsed. Somewhere far above us, faint as a bell heard through stone, the palace was holding a morning audience. "Waking means all of that, uncompressed, unsoftened, in a single breath. Men have died of less. I may die of it. I have had ten years to consider dying of it, and I have made my peace, taster. Leave me dreaming. Let the threads keep me. It is kinder."

"No," I said.

His eyebrows rose. For a moment, just a moment, I saw the king he had been—the man who had once, at a state dinner, sent back a soup in front of a foreign ambassador because it was mediocre, consequences be damned.

"You would refuse rescue," I said. "You sit in a cage made of your own suffering and call the cage mercy."

"I call it survivable."

"So is poison," I said, "in small doses."

The words came out of me before I knew I had them, and once they were out, they arranged themselves into sense, the way ingredients arrange themselves into a dish. I knelt straighter. I heard my own voice go quiet and level—the voice I used at the table, reporting, precise.

"I have eaten poison every day for ten years. Not to survive it—to survive *past* it. That was the whole art. A taster who merely endures the dose dies eventually; the accumulation takes him. A taster who learns each poison, who lets his body study it, who builds the antidote into his own flesh dish by dish—that taster becomes the one thing poison cannot kill. I am full of death, Your Majesty. Decades of it, layered, catalogued, mastered. And it has never once been wasted on me." I looked at the threads running into his wrists. "Those are feeding on you because you have nothing in you to fight them. You are clean. You are empty. You were emptied on purpose, and the emptiness is what they drink. But I am not empty. I am the opposite of empty. I am a decade of every venom this kingdom could brew, and none of it owns me, because I took it freely—one dish at a time, eyes open, choosing."

The king stared at me.

"You're saying you can wake me."

"I'm saying Odile thinks so, and Odile is usually right, which she'll tell you herself at length if you let her." Behind me, Odile made a sound that was either a sob or a scoff. Probably both. "But there's a condition, and it isn't mine, and I need you to hear it. The antidote works because it is given. Willingly. Freely. Blood taken by force only feeds the thing; blood offered breaks it. That's the oldest law of the guild, the one they wrote first and betrayed last: willing payment returns. Which means I cannot do this to you, Your Majesty. No one can drag you out of the dream. You have to choose the pain."

"And if I don't." His voice was very soft. "If I choose the dream. If I say to you, taster, that ten years of feeling nothing slowly is better than one breath of feeling everything—what then? Will you cut the threads anyway? Will you decide for me, as the guild decided for me? As everyone decides for kings?"

The question hung in the salt-lit air. Halvard shifted behind me; I heard the creak of his sword-belt, the sound of a man who had abandoned this king once and was measuring the distance to his own redemption. Sefa's hands were still. Even Odile said nothing.

And I thought about my mother's face.

Three nights ago I had paid it at the tollbridge—laid it on the warding-scales like a coin, and felt it lift out of me, warm and specific and gone. I could no longer see her. I could describe her to you in facts: brown hair, flour on her wrists, a scar on her thumb from a bread knife. But the face itself, the living warmth of it, had been the price of crossing a bridge, and I had paid it because the people behind me needed crossing more than I needed remembering. And I had learned something in the losing that no ledger had ever taught me: that a thing given away is not the same as a thing taken. The guild took from the king. I gave from myself. And the difference between those two acts was the difference between the cage and the cure.

"No," I said. "I won't decide for you. I'm a taster, not a tyrant. I'll tell you what's in the dish and then the choice is yours. That's the whole of my office, and I have kept it faithfully for ten years." I reached out—slowly, so he could see me coming—and laid my hand over the salt threads at his wrist. They were cold and slick and they pulsed with that borrowed heartbeat, and beneath them I could feel, faint as a pulse under linen, his own. Slow. Stubborn. Still there. "Here is what's in the dish, Your Majesty. Pain beyond anything I can describe, because I haven't survived it and you haven't felt it yet. Grief with its full weight restored—your wife, your son, ten lost years, all of it arriving home at once. Possibly death; Odile puts the odds at better than even, and she's accounting for my blood in the mixture, which changes the arithmetic. And on the other side of it—" I made myself hold his eyes. "On the other side is your kingdom. Being ruled by a thing that dreams of being you. Being freed by four people who owe you nothing and came anyway. Being *real*, Your Majesty. Translucent, scarred, grieving, real. The dream is pain stretched thin forever. The waking is pain concentrated once. I have taken both kinds of dose in my life. The concentrated one is worse. The concentrated one ends."

For a long time the king said nothing. The threads pulsed. Far above, the false morning continued, and somewhere in it a man with a stolen face was smiling at petitioners.

"My son," Aldric said at last, and his voice cracked like river ice. "When I wake—if I wake—I will feel him die again. Properly feel it. The guild took the grief and I have missed it, do you understand? Ten years without mourning him has been its own torture. I would rather—"

"I know," I said. "I know exactly. I gave away my mother's face three nights ago, and I would crawl back over that bridge on my knees to pay double to have it returned. Grief is the receipt of love, Your Majesty. They stole yours because it was proof you had loved. Take it back. Take all of it. It's *yours*."

He looked at my hand on his wrist. Then, slowly, with the deliberation of a man signing a ledger he knows will ruin him, he turned his own hand over and gripped mine. His grip was cool and faintly granular, like holding a hand of sea-spray, and it was the strongest thing in the room.

"Taster," he said. "What is your name? Your true one. Not the forgettable one."

"Sebastian Kavanagh."

"Sebastian Kavanagh." He said it as though entering it into a record. "When I am myself again—if I am myself—I will remember that you asked. That you told me the truth about the dish and let me choose. No one has asked me anything in ten years." His glass-pale face set into something that was almost the old dry smile. "Wake me, then. Give me the whole poisoned cup. And Sebastian—"

"Your Majesty?"

"When I scream—and I will scream—do not stop. Whatever happens, whatever I beg for in the middle of it, do not stop pouring."

Behind me, Odile was already unpacking her satchel, vials chiming softly, her hands steady in the way they only ever were when the world was ending properly. Sefa knelt and began, with one fingertip, drawing lines in the salt dust around the king's knees—wards, old ones, her father's hand evident in every curve. Halvard drew his sword and turned to face the stair we had come down, because a man who guards a door once badly spends the rest of his life guarding doors well.

And I bared my forearm, where three days of agony had brewed Odile's impossible recipe into my veins, and pressed a knife to the skin above a decade of swallowed deaths.

Far above us, faint through the stone, a bell rang for the changing of the guard.

The salt threads began, very softly, to sing.
