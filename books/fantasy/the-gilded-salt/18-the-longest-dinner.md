## 18. The Longest Dinner

They dressed him for it.

That was the detail Sebastian would remember afterward, if he survived to remember anything at all—the absurd tenderness of it. Two servants in the king's livery had bathed him in water scented with juniper, combed his hair, and dressed him in a doublet of deep blue velvet that fit him as though it had been cut for his shoulders specifically. Perhaps it had. The impostor had ten years of Aldric's memories and apparently some of Aldric's habits, and one of those habits, according to everything Odile had told him in the sunken archive by candlelight, was that the royal taster be presented cleanly at table. *A man who stands between me and death should look like he is worth the standing,* the true king used to say—or so the guild's records claimed.

Sebastian studied himself in the polished silver mirror and did not recognize the man there. Not because of the clothes. Because of what was underneath them.

Three days. Three days of Odile's antidote, brewed into his bloodstream drop by drop through a needle-fine glass cannery she'd made from a broken alembic and sheer spite. Three days of every poison he had ever swallowed in ten years of service rising up out of the marrow where his body had banked them, called home by her tincture like iron filings dragged across a table. He had vomited black. He had wept without knowing why. He had lain on the cold floor of their hiding place beneath the granary and felt his own blood turn against him, each toxin waking in sequence—nightshade first, with its sweet creeping numbness; then salts of lead, heavy as regret; then the pale arsenic they'd once dusted over a traitor's pheasant; then things with no common names, things the guild had traded away from dying men and sold to kings.

*It will feel like dying,* Odile had warned him, her hands steady even when her voice wasn't. *That's how you'll know it's working.*

He had not died. He had become something else instead: a vessel, a walking antidote, every toxin the salt-king had ever fed its stolen body now sleeping in his veins, waiting to be asked—no, *given*. That distinction mattered more than anything else in the world. Odile had said it a hundred times until Sefa drew it for him in flour on the granary floor: a hand taking, crossed out; a hand opening, circled twice.

*Compelled blood feeds it. Given blood unmakes it.*

So here he stood, in borrowed velvet, being led down corridors he knew better than his own heartbeat, toward a dinner he was expected to taste.

The palace had changed in ten years, or perhaps he had simply never been permitted to see it clearly before. The walls were paler than he remembered—or no, that wasn't right either. They were *whiter*, bleached, as though something in the air leached color slowly out of stone the way it leached it out of memory. The tapestries hung faded at the edges, their hunting scenes dissolving into fog. Servants moved through the halls with a strange smooth quiet, faces pleasant and slightly unfocused, like people walking in their sleep. When one of them smiled at Sebastian, the smile arrived a half-second after the eyes had already looked away.

*Salt-born,* Odile had said of the court. *Not all of them. But enough.*

The great doors of the feast hall opened onto warmth and light and music, and for one treacherous moment Sebastian almost forgot what he was. Candles by the hundred. A table long enough to be a road. Roast meats and glazed birds and towers of sugared fruit, and above it all, at the far end, the King of Cerenholt raising a goblet in welcome.

King Aldric. Or the thing wearing him.

Up close—and Sebastian had tasted for this king every morning for ten years, had stood three feet from him daily—he could never quite find the seam. The face was right. The grey threading the beard was right. The laugh lines were right, though the king had laughed rarely enough in the years before the illness. It was only in motion that something went wrong, some fractional delay between intention and expression, as if the face were being remembered rather than inhabited. Tonight the impostor wore joy the way a man wears a coat cut for a taller brother: correctly, completely, and yet somehow wrong at the shoulders.

"Taster." The voice was warm. It had always been warm. "Come. Stand where you have always stood."

Sebastian walked the length of the table. His boots sounded very loud. At intervals along the board sat courtiers—perhaps thirty of them, perhaps fewer—and as he passed, conversation thinned and thinned until the only sounds were the fire and the fiddles and the small wet sounds of eating. None of the courtiers looked at him. That was usual. That had been the gift his father had bought him, if the ledger was true: forgettability, purchased at whatever terrible price, woven into him so thoroughly that even now, walking visibly through a room full of eyes, he left no impression behind.

Except on the king. The impostor's gaze followed him the whole way, and there was nothing forgettable in it at all. It was the look of a starving man watching bread carried past on a tray.

"You look well," the salt-king said when Sebastian reached his place. "Better than you have in some time. There's color in you." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "You've been ill, I think. I dreamed it."

"I recovered, Majesty."

"Recovery." The king turned the word over like a coin of doubtful minting. "Yes. I know something of recovery."

The meal began. And Sebastian did the thing he had done four thousand mornings and evenings of his life: he tasted.

Quail in a glaze of honey and bitter orange—he held the meat on his tongue for the count of sixty, watching his own body for the flicker of response, feeling nothing but the ordinary sweetness. A soup of white beans and smoked ham, thick enough to hold a spoon upright; he took three swallows, waited, breathed. Wine from the southern slopes, decanted an hour early. Bread. A custard flavored with rosewater that sat in his mouth like a lie, because rosewater had been Queen Maren's favorite and the true Aldric had banned it from the royal table after her death, and only a thing that had eaten the king's memories would serve it, thinking it a kindness.

Each dish he pronounced clean. Each pronouncement was true, in the narrow sense. There was no poison in the food.

There didn't need to be. The poison was already in the king, had been poured into the hollow of him grain by grain over ten years, and tonight Sebastian carried its entire history in his blood like a ledger brought to balance.

Between courses, the impostor talked to him. That was new. In ten years, the king had never once spoken to him beyond the ritual words—*well?* and *clean?* and *you may go.* Now it leaned across the salt cellar—it never touched the salt cellar; Sebastian had noted that aversion in his ledgers a decade ago, the first crack in the world, the king who flinched from salt—and studied him with Aldric's hazel eyes.

"Do you know," it said, "what I taste when I taste you?"

"Majesty?"

"Nothing. Nothing at all. Every soul in this palace has a flavor. Fear tastes of copper. Grief of brine. My dear Brann tastes of old smoke and older guilt. But you—" the king smiled, and the smile lagged behind the eyes by that same terrible half-second, "—you are a blank page, taster. A door painted on a wall. Do you know how maddening that is? To sit at the head of a kingdom and find one man in it who is *nothing*?"

"I'm told it's a virtue in my trade, Majesty."

"It is a virtue in no trade." The impostor lifted its goblet, turned it, watched the wine climb the glass. "I have wanted to open you for years. Like a letter addressed to someone else. Tonight, perhaps, I will."

Sebastian's hands were steady. He noticed this with a kind of distant pride, the last professional satisfaction of his career: ten years of training, and his hands would not shake even now. Under the velvet, under the skin, his blood hummed like a struck bell that hadn't yet decided to ring.

* * *

The fish course came and went. The venison. Somewhere past the middle of the table a courtier laughed too loudly at nothing, and the sound dropped dead in the air like a bird hitting a window. Sebastian tasted each dish and named each dish clean, and between dishes he let his mind move quietly along the plan the way a tongue moves along a cracked tooth.

Odile and Sefa were beneath the city, in the old cisterns below the granary quarter, holding the wards open. Halvard was dead—that was the price of the gate, of Sefa's voice breaking ten years of silence to scream them clear of the falling bridge, of the knight turning to hold the passage alone with a sword and ten years of shame and nothing else. Sebastian had not seen him fall. He had heard it, which was worse, and he carried the sound folded up inside him next to his mother's absent face and all the other payments.

*Given, not taken,* he reminded himself. Even grief. Especially grief. He opened his hand around it rather than closing his fist, and it hurt less, which was the cruelest lesson of this whole terrible year.

The impostor clapped its hands. The tables were cleared. And then the servants brought in the final course, and Sebastian's heart, which had been steady as a metronome all evening, finally stumbled.

It was a plain dish. That was what made it terrible. A bowl of broth, golden and clear, floating shreds of chicken and a single herb leaf, the kind of food a mother makes for a sick child, the kind of food a convalescent king might crave. And beside it, on a small silver salver, a knife, a heel of bread, and—

A cup. Unfilled. Waiting.

"The last course," the king said softly, "is you."

Guards moved in—not the palace guard, but Brann's inquisitors, grey-cloaked, faces shuttered. Brann himself stood at the hall's far door, arms folded, and would not meet Sebastian's eyes. Good, Sebastian thought. Let him look at the floor. Let him remember the cell full of failed couriers, and the widow's village still smoking in the mountains, and every other kindness this reign had ever performed and then burned.

A lancet was placed in Sebastian's hand. His own hand. They had not bound him; they had not needed to. Ten years of obedience was chain enough.

"You will open a vein," the impostor said, and its voice had gone strange, layered, like two speakers an instant apart, "and fill the cup. You will do it because I ask. Because I am your king. Because you are *nothing*, taster, and nothing exists to be poured."

And here was the moment Odile had feared, the flaw in the whole design, the reason she had wept over her alembic while telling him: compelled blood feeds it. If Sebastian cut his wrist because the salt-king commanded it—if fear or duty or simple habit of obedience tipped his hand—then his blood would arrive as tribute, and the thing would drink ten years of accumulated poison and grow stronger, and the kingdom would be lost inside an hour.

It had to be given. Freely. For his own reasons. With his whole heart.

Sebastian looked at the lancet. Then he looked up at the king, at the stolen face, and did the bravest thing he had ever done, which was to speak honestly in a room full of powerful people.

"No," he said.

The hall went silent. Even the fire seemed to lean back.

"Not because you ask. Not because you're my king." He set the lancet down on the table, deliberately, the small click of it enormous in the silence. "Because I choose to. There's a difference. You wouldn't understand it—you've spent ten years wearing a man's life and you still don't understand the difference between having a thing and being given it."

The impostor stared at him. And for the first time, the mask slipped—not the face, the face held perfectly—but something behind the face, something vast and cold and terribly young looking out through Aldric's eyes with an expression that was almost, horribly, loneliness.

"What are you?" it whispered.

"A taster," Sebastian said. "It's my job to swallow what's meant for you."

He picked up the lancet again—his choice, his hand, his reasons, grief and love and fury and the memory of a widow's free bread and a mute girl's flour drawings and a knight's last stand all gathered into the gesture like a fist closing around a coal—and he opened his own forearm along the old scar-line where a thousand tasting-spoons had nicked him, and he filled the cup.

The blood came dark and slow. And as it filled the silver cup, it changed. Odile had promised this and he had not believed her: the red brightened, then paled, then shimmered, and a smell rose off it like rain on hot stone, like the sea at low tide, like every medicine and every murder he had ever swallowed coming home. The nearest inquisitors recoiled. One of them made a small sound, high and involuntary, and Brann at the door finally raised his head.

The salt-king reached for the cup.

"Don't," Sebastian said—not commanding, offering. "Or do. It doesn't matter anymore. This is yours. All of it. Every drop you ever fed him, I kept safe for you. I kept *everything* safe for you. That was the whole of my life." His voice cracked, and he let it. "Consider it delivered."

The impostor drank.

For a moment, nothing. The hall held its breath. The king stood with the empty cup in its hand and Aldric's face composed in mild pleasure, and Sebastian thought, with a strange cool clarity: *it hasn't worked, we were wrong, Odile was wrong, I am going to die in this velvet for nothing—*

Then the king's hand began to snow.

It started at the fingertips. The flesh of the index finger whitened, crystallized, and sloughed away in fine grains that caught the candlelight as they fell—a soft hiss, like sand through an hourglass, like the sea withdrawing. The cup dropped and burst. The impostor looked down at its own unraveling hand with an expression of pure, childlike bewilderment, and when it raised its face to Sebastian, the face was running. Features sliding, blurring, salt streams cutting channels down through the beard, through the eyes, and the eyes—Aldric's hazel eyes—dissolved last of all, and in the instant before they went, Sebastian saw what was inside them.

Memories. Spilling out. The impostor screamed, and the scream was not one voice but dozens—Aldric's voice crying his wife's name, a courier's voice, voices of everyone whose memories the guild had ever traded into that hollow, all of them pouring out of the breaking shape at once, grief upon grief upon grief, ten years of a stolen life demanding to be felt in a single unbearable moment.

The candles guttered. The tapestries rippled in a wind that came from nowhere. Along the banquet table, courtiers were freezing mid-motion, frosting over, collapsing into drifts of white that steamed faintly and smelled of the sea. The walls themselves had begun to weep, long saline tears tracking down the bleached stone, and somewhere far below—beneath the hall, beneath the floors, beneath ten years of lies—Sebastian felt, rather than heard, a deep groan of ancient doors being forced open.

*The crypt,* he thought. *Odile. Sefa. The wards. Hold.*

His legs gave. He went down among the falling salt, velvet and blood and dissolution, and the last thing he saw before the grey took him was the impostor's shape kneeling where its king had stood, growing smaller and smaller and smaller, a mound, a drift, a scatter—still screaming in borrowed voices, still dreaming, even now, of being a king—

—and the doors at the end of the hall, the ones leading down, swinging wide into darkness that smelled of deep earth and, impossibly, of morning.
