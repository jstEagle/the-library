## 20. The Price Already Paid

The floor of the Hollow Court was made of salt, and it was dissolving beneath him, and so was he.

Sebastian knew this the way he had once known the difference between hemlock and fool's parsley: not with his mind but with his tongue, with the whole geography of his body. The air tasted of brine and unraveling. Somewhere above him—far above, past the flooded stairwells and the weeping walls—the impostor was coming apart, screaming in a voice stitched together from a dead man's grief, and every thread that snapped was a thread snapping also in Sebastian's own blood.

He crawled.

It was not dignified. He had spent ten years being dignified in small ways—standing straight at the king's left hand, bowing precisely, writing his ledgers in a hand so neat it might have been printed—and now dignity was a country he could no longer afford passage to. His arms shook. The stone—or the salt pretending to be stone—was slick under his palms, and where he touched it, it did not resist him. It welcomed him. That was the horror of it. Everything salt-born wanted to be given things, and here he was, leaking himself into the white grain of the floor like wine into bread.

*Given,* he thought, and almost laughed, and the laugh came out as blood on his lips instead.

*Not taken. Given.*

He had understood it three days ago, lying in Odile's cellar while she brewed the antidote into him drop by drop, each vial a fresh agony, his veins learning to carry every poison the guild had ever fed a king. She had explained the theory then, in her quick, guilty way, her hands never still among her glassware. *Compelled blood merely feeds it,* she'd said. *The salt-thing eats what is forced from people. That's how the guild bound it in the first place—they gave it grief that wasn't offered freely, they stuffed the hollow with stolen goods. So the unbinding has to be the opposite. It has to be a gift.*

He had understood it then as theory. Now, dying on the floor of the court the guild had built, he understood it as law.

The guild's oldest law. Odile had recited it once, half-mocking, over a cup of bad wine in the flooded quarter: *What is taken is debt; what is given is payment; what is paid returns.* He had thought it merchant's superstition, the kind of rhyme old couriers muttered before sealing their tubes. But the map had answered his tongue. Sefa's flour-drawings had answered his silence. And now, as his heart stuttered through its final measures, he could feel the law moving in the world around him like a tide answering the moon—patient, enormous, indifferent to whether he believed in it or not.

*What is paid returns.*

But only what is paid. Not what is spent. Not what is lost along the way.

He thought about that distinction with the last clean corner of his mind, because it mattered, because he had always been a man for ledgers, and even dying he wanted the accounts correct.

His blood was payment. His blood would return—he could feel it already, faintly, the way you can feel dawn behind a mountain before any light arrives. But the antidote had burned through him like fire through a granary, and fire does not distinguish between the crop and the chaff. Some of what it consumed was simply gone. He could feel the gaps already, soft-edged absences scattered through his memory like moth-holes in a winter coat. He reached for his mother's face and found the shape of reaching without the face itself—a gesture toward a window, a smell of rosemary, and then nothing, nothing, nothing. He had paid that away at the tollbridge, weeks ago, and had told himself it was bearable. It was less bearable now, when everything else was leaving too, when the losses were stacking like coins and he could no longer tell which ones he had chosen.

*Odile,* he thought. *Sefa. Halvard. The widow's village burning. Brann's cell full of failed couriers.* He held those names the way a drowning man holds driftwood, testing each one, terrified of finding another gap. They held. They all held. Whatever else the fire took, it seemed to spare the family he had never meant to make.

* * *

The crypt doors were open.

That was the first thing he saw when he dragged himself around the last fallen pillar: two doors of black iron standing wide, and beyond them a chamber lit by no lamp he could name, and in the chamber a bed, and in the bed a man who was more light than flesh.

King Aldric. The true one. Translucent as sea-glass, threaded through with white filaments that pulsed slow as a sleeping pulse—salt threads, dream-threads, ten years of enforced sleep woven into a cage shaped like rest. His eyes were closed. His chest barely moved. Around the bed, the dissolving palace rained fine white dust, and the dust did not touch him; it curved away, respectful, the way water curves around a standing stone.

Sebastian crawled across the threshold. The iron doors groaned behind him—not closing, settling, like an old dog lying down at last.

"Your Majesty," he said. His voice was a ruin. He tried again. "Your Majesty. I've brought—" What? What had he brought? A taster brings nothing. A taster stands between a king and death and is forgotten by both. "I've brought the bill," he finished, absurdly, and coughed blood onto the salt-white floor, and the floor drank it gratefully.

The king's eyes opened.

They were grey, Aldric's eyes, the grey of the northern sea in winter, and they were awake—had been awake, Odile had warned them, for some time now, aware inside the dreaming the way a man is aware inside a fever. They fixed on Sebastian with terrible clarity.

"You're dying," the king said. His voice was thin, but it carried the habit of command the way an empty glove carries the shape of a hand. "Young man. You are dying on my floor."

"Yes," Sebastian agreed. It seemed important to be accurate. He had built a life on accuracy. "Mostly."

"Mostly." Something moved at the corner of the king's mouth—not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the way everything about him was ghosted now. "My taster used to say that. When I asked after his health. *Mostly well, Majesty.* I never learned his name."

Something in Sebastian's chest cracked, quietly, like ice on a spring river.

"I know," he said. "I know you didn't. That was rather the point of me."

*Forgettable.* His father had sold it for him—sold his son's forgettability to the guild, signed the ledger in a hand Sebastian had seen only once, in the sunken archive, in ink that shifted like water. *To protect him,* the entry had said, or Odile had said it said; parts of that night were already moth-eaten too. He could remember the ledger. He could remember Odile's face when she found it. He could not remember his father's voice anymore, and he suspected—knew, with ledger-precision—that he never would again. The fire had taken it. Payment honored, loss permanent.

And yet. And yet the forgettability itself was gone too, burned out of him somewhere between the tollbridge and this floor, because you cannot give yourself away anonymously. The moment he had pressed his scarred tongue to the map, the moment he had stood in front of Brann and lied badly, the moment he had decided to die for a king who didn't know his name—every one of those acts had been a signature. You cannot sign yourself invisible.

"Listen to me," Sebastian said, and dragged himself the last arm's length, until his bleeding hand rested against the edge of the salt-threaded bed. The threads recoiled from his touch. Of course they did. He was poison now, all of him, a walking pharmacopoeia, a decade of small deaths distilled into one final dose. "The thing wearing your crown upstairs—it's unbinding. My blood is doing it. But it isn't finished, and I'm not either, and there's one last piece."

The king watched him. Waiting. Even caged, even translucent, he had the patience of a man who had endured ten years of dreaming.

"The threads," Sebastian said. "They're anchored in you. In your grief. That's what the guild traded away, all those years ago—your mourning, your rage, your ten years of sorrow, and something hollow walked in and filled the space. The antidote dissolves the thing. But the hollow stays hollow unless—" His breath caught. He rode through it. Tasters learn to ride through things. "Unless you take yourself back. All of it. The grief too. Waking means feeling ten lost years at once. I know. I know what I'm asking."

"Do you," the king said softly.

No. No, he didn't. No one could. But he thought of Sefa, who had paid her own name and spoken anyway, at the gates, with a voice like a bell cracking open. He thought of Halvard holding the gate alone, buying seconds with his shame. He thought of Odile, who had brewed counterfeit memories for desperate strangers because she couldn't bear the world's real ones, and who had brewed this last true thing anyway.

"No," Sebastian admitted. "But I know what refusing costs. I've watched it cost a kingdom."

The salt threads pulsed. Above them, muffled by stone and brine, the impostor's screaming changed pitch—lower now, slower, the scream of something running out of borrowed time. The dust fell thicker. Sebastian's vision was going grey at the edges, narrowing to a tunnel at the end of which was a bed and a ghost and a choice.

"There is a law," Sebastian said. "The guild's oldest. *What is given is payment; what is paid returns.* I don't fully understand it. I think it's older than the guild—I think the guild just found it, the way you find a well. But I've been giving all day, Your Majesty. Blood, memory, everything. And the law answers. I can feel it answering. So if you wake—if you take back your grief, freely, knowing its full weight—" He swallowed blood. "Then the law owes *you*. Ten years of stolen life. It has to pay it back. That's how it works. That's how it's always worked. The guild just never told anyone, because a guild that trades in human pieces can't afford for the merchandise to know it's owed."

The king was silent for a long moment. The dust fell. The screaming faded. Somewhere far above, Sebastian imagined he could hear Odile's wards singing, and Sefa's hands flying through flour and chalk, holding the lines that held the world.

"And if you're wrong," the king said at last.

"Then we both die in a room under a palace, and honestly, Majesty, I've had worse Tuesdays."

This time the smile came all the way. Ghosted, translucent, but whole. "You're certain you weren't a courtier?"

"Taster," Sebastian said. "We're trained never to be interesting."

"Well." The king drew a breath that moved through him like light through water. "You are the most interesting thing to happen to me in ten years. Give me your hand, taster. If I must feel a decade of grief in a single heartbeat, I find I would prefer not to do it alone."

Sebastian gave him his hand.

*Gave*, he noted distantly, ledger-precise to the end. Not lent. Not lost. Gave.

* * *

The waking was not gentle, and Sebastian felt all of it.

He felt the king's grief come home the way a flood comes home to a dry riverbed—filling every channel at once, ten years of mourning for a wife dead before the illness, ten years of terror during it, ten years of theft, of dreaming, of watching his own hands wave from balconies worn by someone else. It came into the room like weather. The salt threads shrieked and dissolved. The light in the king's body flared white, then gold, then settled into something warmer and more human than it had been, and King Aldric wept—great, silent, kingly weeping, tears that fell on Sebastian's wrist and steamed faintly, as if the grief itself were hot from ten years' storage.

And the law answered.

Sebastian felt it happen. There was no other word. It came up through the floor, through the salt, through the opened doors, through the whole patient architecture of the world: a tide of return, vast and unhurried, pouring back into him everything he had paid. Not the gaps—the gaps stayed gaps, moth-holes permanent, his mother's face gone forever, his father's voice gone forever, whole seasons of his childhood burned to warm the antidote's fire. But the blood came back. The warmth came back. His heart, which had been stumbling through its final bars, found its rhythm again the way a found drum finds the procession, and breath flooded his lungs, and the grey tunnel of his vision widened, widened, widened—

He woke on the crypt floor with a king's hand gripping his and a king's tears drying on his skin, alive, diminished, and—this was the strange part, the part he would turn over for months afterward—*lighter*.

"Mostly well?" the king asked hoarsely. He remembered. Of course he remembered; he had been awake for it.

"Mostly well, Majesty," Sebastian said, and discovered he was smiling, and discovered further that it did not hurt.

* * *

They climbed out of the Hollow Court together, slowly, the king steadying Sebastian on the stairs, Sebastian steadying the king on the landings, two ruined men leaning on each other like the last two posts of a fence. The palace above was quiet in a way palaces are never quiet. The flood of dissolving salt had passed through and gone, leaving everything rinsed, everything pale, everything smelling of rain.

In the great hall they found the others.

Odile sat on the steps of the dais, soot-faced, singed, surrounded by shattered glassware, holding her empty hands palm-up as if she still couldn't believe they'd held. Sefa was beside her, chalk in one hand, and when she saw Sebastian she dropped the chalk and ran—not to him, past him, to the king, stopping short at some ancient instinct of distance, staring up at the true face of the true king with her father's precision in her eyes. Then she turned and looked at Sebastian, and her mouth worked, and though no sound came—her voice was spent, spent at the gates, spent saving them—she mouthed it clearly enough:

*You lived.*

"Barely," Sebastian said. "Mostly."

Halvard was not there. Sebastian's stomach dropped before Odile saw his face and shook her head quickly, quickly. "The gate. He held it. He was alive when we came through—we heard him shouting orders at Brann's men like they were recruits, it was magnificent—but the fighting—" She stopped. Started again, alchemist-precise. "We don't know. We went back. The gatehouse is rubble. We haven't found him."

Sebastian looked at the king. The king looked at Sebastian. And the true King Aldric of Caelmere, newly returned from ten years of salt and sleep, said in a voice still raw with grief and steadier than it had any right to be:

"Then we dig. Tonight, if need be. I have spent a decade owing debts I never incurred. I should like to begin my reign by paying one I did."

* * *

Later—much later, near dawn, when the digging parties had torches and the rubble of the gatehouse had yielded, impossibly, a battered knight with a broken collarbone and a grin full of blood—Sebastian sat alone in a corridor with a scrap of parchment and did what he had always done.

He took inventory.

*Alive:* himself, Odile, Sefa, Halvard, the king. *Lost:* his mother's face, his father's voice, the summer he was nine (he had checked; it was simply not there, a year with a hole in it), and smaller things he would discover missing for years afterward, like stones surfacing in a plowed field. *Changed:* everything. His forgettability, gone. His anonymity, gone. His careful, ordered, invisible life—gone, and good riddance, though he suspected he would mourn it in odd moments, the way one mourns a coat outgrown.

At the bottom of the scrap, after the inventory, he wrote the guild's oldest law in his precise, printed hand. *What is taken is debt; what is given is payment; what is paid returns.* Below it, after a moment, he added a line of his own, a taster's amendment, earned on the floor of the Hollow Court in his own blood:

*Some prices are not refunded. Pay them anyway.*

He folded the scrap into his pocket, next to the stub of the map, which had gone blank at last—its work done, its shifting ink settled into plain white paper, the most honest document in the kingdom.

From the direction of the gatehouse came voices: Odile arguing with a physician about leeches, Sefa laughing—laughing, soundlessly, her whole body bent with it—at whatever Halvard had just said, and the king's low voice asking questions, listening, learning his own kingdom back one name at a time.

Sebastian pocketed the inventory, stood, and went to join his family.

Behind him, unnoticed, on the windowsill of the ruined corridor, a fine white dust had settled overnight—salt, ordinary salt, the last trace of the impostor, catching the first grey light of morning. And where the dawn touched it, it glittered. Faintly. Gildedly. Like something waiting to be remembered.
