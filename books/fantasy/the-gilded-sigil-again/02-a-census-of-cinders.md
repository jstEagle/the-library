## 2. A Census of Cinders

The hunters came at dawn, and the Sigil's doors opened for them the way doors open for weather—because there was no sense refusing.

Ada heard them before she saw them: boots on marble, six sets, moving with the unhurried rhythm of men who had never once been told to hurry. Then Master Veyne's voice, pitched higher than she'd ever heard it, performing a warmth he did not feel. *The Gilded Sigil welcomes the crown's servants. Always.*

She was at her desk in the transcription hall when they entered, quill moving, eyes down—the posture of invisibility she'd spent seven years perfecting. The ink on her page was a contract for a widow's memory of her husband's face, sold back to her by the quarter-hour. Ordinary work. Safe work.

The hunters spread through the hall like spilled water, finding every crack. They carried brass-bound ledgers of their own, and long silver styluses that hummed faintly when they passed near anything written in gifted hand. Ada felt one pass behind her chair and kept her breathing even, kept her quill moving, and thought: *I am paper. I am nothing but paper.*

"Name."

She looked up. The man standing over her was younger than the others, unarmored where they wore the crown's grey, and he held his census ledger the way she held hers—with his thumb resting in the spine, as if it were something alive. His eyes were brown and patient and entirely wrong for a witch-hunter.

"Winterbourne. Ada Winterbourne."

"Your gift?"

"I have none." The lie came out smooth as ruled lines. "I'm debt-scribed. I transcribe what others dictate."

He wrote it down. She watched his stylus move and felt the strange vertigo of watching someone record you into existence. *Winterbourne, A. No gift. Debt-bound.* Two words that would follow her now, filed in some archive beneath the palace, waiting to be contradicted.

"And your master holds your indenture?"

"Master Veyne does."

Something flickered across his face—not suspicion exactly. Recognition, maybe. He closed his ledger gently, the way you'd close a door on a sleeping child.

"Thank you, Mistress Winterbourne."

* * *

Corin Vale had interviewed forty-one people in three days, and forty-one had lied to him.

That wasn't cynicism; it was arithmetic. The census edict said the crown required a count of the gifted for their protection. Everyone Corin interviewed understood *protection* to mean *the pyres at Thornmark*, because everyone had heard about Thornmark, whatever the official dispatches claimed. So they lied. They lied badly, mostly—the cook whose hands trembled when the stylus hummed near her, the apprentice who'd memorized his confession of innocence so thoroughly he recited it word for word.

Corin wrote down their lies. It was easier for everyone.

His orders came from Magister Halloway, sealed in grey wax: *A full accounting. No exceptions. The realm remembers its debts.* Corin had been a hunter for six years and had learned to read between the lines of such language. *No exceptions* meant no mercy. *The realm remembers* meant the realm intended to use what it remembered.

On the fourth morning he returned to the counting-house to reconcile the guild's own rolls against his interviews, and that was where he found the discrepancy.

It was small. A child—Pell Ashgrave, age nine, listed on the guild rolls as a foundling taken in for kitchen labor. But the guild's intake ledger, the older one, the one nobody thought a scribe would bother falsifying because who falsifies history?—that ledger said *Ashgrave, P., gift of warming, minor, bound at intake per standard terms.*

Someone had burned the original entry out with acid and rewritten it. Recently. The ink sat slightly proud of the page, and under lamplight it caught the light differently than the surrounding script.

Corin sat with the ledger for a long time.

Nine years old. Gift of warming. In Thornmark, a child like that would have been taken for assessment, which meant the tower, which meant—

He took out his own census ledger and wrote: *Ashgrave, P. No gift. Kitchen staff.*

Then he sat there, hating how easy it had been, and wondering why he'd done it.

* * *

Ada knew the moment Corin Vale found the falsified entry, because she'd written it, and writing left fingerprints only she could feel.

She'd done it two nights before the hunters arrived—acid-pen and steady hand, scrubbing Pell Ashgrave from the rolls while the boy slept in the kitchen loft above the coals he couldn't help but warm. Veyne knew about Pell. Veyne didn't care; the child's debt was small and his gift smaller. But Veyne didn't know about the census, not truly—he thought it theater, another royal performance. Ada had seen the hunters' styluses humming near the archives and understood otherwise.

So she'd falsified the oldest ledger, the one place a careful reader might look, and prayed.

Now she watched, from across the reconciliation room, as the young hunter sat with that very ledger. Watched him turn pages. Watched him stop.

Her heart performed a slow, terrible arithmetic of its own.

He looked up once—found her already looking, which was a mistake, which she corrected too late—and held her gaze for a breath, two. His face gave away nothing. Then he wrote something in his own book, and turned the page, and moved on.

That evening, leaving the hall, he paused beside her desk. The other hunters had gone. Lamplight lay between them like a drawn line.

"The Ashgrave entry," he said quietly. "The ink is newer than the page."

Ada's mouth went dry. "Ledgers get repaired. Water damage. Mice."

"Mice," he repeated, without inflection. He set something on her desk: a slip of paper, folded once. "The crown's styluses will be calibrated next week. Sharper. Whatever's written in this building should be true by then."

He left before she could answer.

She unfolded the paper. On it, in his precise hand: *Ashgrave, P. — recorded clean. Keep it that way. Burn your drafts.*

Not an accusation. An instruction.

Ada sat alone in the counting-house until the lamps burned low, holding a piece of advice from an enemy that read, unmistakably, like a warning from a friend—and wondering which was more dangerous.

* * *

Corin reported to Magister Halloway by courier that night, as required. He summarized forty-one interviews, noted the guild's cooperation, and appended the standard line: *No significant gifts detected among staff.*

He did not mention the falsified entry. He did not mention the scribe with ink-stained fingers who had watched him find it and hadn't run.

But in his private notebook—the one no magister ever saw—he wrote a different kind of entry, the kind he'd started keeping after Thornmark, the kind that was slowly becoming a list of everything his orders asked him to forget:

*The Sigil's ledgers are heavier than they should be. Something in this building is being counted twice—once for the crown, and once for whoever pays better.*

He underlined it. Then, because the building was quiet and no one would ever know, he added a second line:

*Ask the Winterbourne woman what the guild actually sells.*

* * *

Ada didn't sleep. Instead she went down to the deep archive, past the contract vaults, to the shelf where Veyne kept the ledgers no auditor was permitted to touch—and pulled the oldest one, the one bound in leather gone dark as dried blood.

She told herself it was about Pell, about leverage, about protecting herself if the census turned ugly. That was mostly true.

But when she opened it and her gift rose up to meet the ink—that old, forbidden hunger, the words blooming into memory against her will—what she found was not contracts at all.

It was fire.
