## 3. What the Ledgers Remember

The counting-house kept its secrets the way a miser kept coins: close, counted, and never spent. I had learned its rhythms over six years of borrowed ink—when Veyne walked the floor, when the clerks took their supper, when the lamplighters came and the ledgers slept. Tonight I waited for all three.

Veyne left at the ninth bell, as he always did on audit nights, his cane tapping a rhythm down the marble stair that I could have set a clock by. The clerks followed in ones and twos. By half past nine the great room was mine alone, lit by the guttering lamps and the green glow of the sigil-glass windows, where the guild's mark burned like a cat's eye over the sleeping city.

I went to the deep archive.

Not the public vaults—those held mortgages and marriage settlements, debts of the ordinary miserable kind. The deep archive was behind the iron door at the back of the record-room, the one that required two keys and a word spoken aloud. I did not have either. But I had something better: six years of transcribing the guild's correspondence, and a memory for paper that no one suspected a debt-scribe could possess.

The night-warden's key hung on a hook by his desk, because wardens are paid little and trust their rooms more than their pockets. The second key lived in Veyne's private drawer, and Veyne's lock was a Corvane pattern, third generation, and I had copied it from wax impressions taken over four months of cleaning his study as part of my indenture. The word was harder. Words spoken to locks are usually names, and men like Veyne name locks after the things they love.

*Gilded Sigil*. It opened on the first try.

Inside, the deep archive was cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature. The air tasted of old smoke, of something burned long ago and never fully extinguished. Shelf after shelf of black-lacquered boxes, each stamped with the guild's mark, each heavier than its size should allow. And on a lectern in the center, under glass, a single contract laid open—not written in ink at all, but in characters that seemed to smolder when I looked away and cool when I looked directly at them.

Dragon-fire. I knew it before I knew how I knew it. Some knowledge lives in the blood older than memory.

Every gift has a cost. Mine was this: ink remembers what hands felt when they wrote it, and I feel what ink remembers. Fear, greed, love, the small cruelties of commerce—all of it soaked into paper like rain into cloth. Six years I had waded through other people's feelings, learning to keep my face still while contracts screamed under my fingers.

I lifted the glass.

The moment my skin touched the page, the world ended.

* * *

Fire first. Fire everywhere, fire as weather, fire as god. A sky the color of an open furnace, and beneath it a city—I knew its bones, though its towers were taller then, its river wider—a city burning not in destruction but in *glory*, dragons wheeling overhead like storm-fronts given wings.

And then the war. Not the sanitized version in the schoolbooks, where brave kings drove the dragons east and the age of man began. This was the truth the ink remembered, because the contract had been written by a hand that was there:

I saw the last dragon, old beyond reckoning, her scales gone grey as ash, standing in the ruins of her eyrie while men with the Sigil's mark on their banners offered terms. Not victory. *Negotiation.* The guild had not conquered the dragons. The guild had *bought* them—or bought the ending of them. The last flame, the fire that could unmake or remake anything, surrendered into human keeping in exchange for a promise: that it would be kept safe until the world was ready to remember.

And the guild had kept it. Kept it locked in black lacquered boxes, hoarded like everything else they touched, sold in whispers to kings who wanted plagues ended and rivals erased—and the world forgot dragons entirely, forgot the war, forgot the bargain, because forgetting was cheaper than honoring.

The contract released me and I staggered against the lectern, gasping, my palms scorched with phantom heat. Grief hit me sideways—not my grief, the dragon's, the long patient sorrow of a creature who had traded her fire for a promise and watched the promisers grow rich on her silence.

Complicity settled over me like ash. Every ledger I had transcribed, every debt I had recorded with my careful invisible hand—the money flowing through those pages had been dragon-fire, sold and resold, the last wonder of the world ground into interest payments.

I had helped them forget. We all had. That was the true business of the Gilded Sigil: not lending, not scribing. *Forgetting*, at compound interest.

"That," said a voice behind me, "was a mistake you cannot unmake."

* * *

Veyne stood in the doorway, two keys in his fist, his cane abandoned somewhere. He looked older without the cane, smaller, and for one absurd moment I pitied him—before I remembered what pity costs, and who had taught me the exchange rate.

"Master Veyne." I kept my voice level. My palms were still burning. "You're back early."

"The warden sent a boy." His eyes went to the open contract, then to my hands, then—slowly, terribly—to my face, and I watched him do the arithmetic he'd been doing for six years without ever quite finishing it. "You read it. Not the words. The *memory*."

I said nothing. Denial was a coin I no longer carried.

"You're a reader." He breathed the word like a curse and a prayer in one. "All this time. In my own counting-house." He came closer, and I made myself stand still, though every instinct screamed to run. "Do you know what the witch-hunters would pay for you? What they'd *do*?"

"I imagine you're about to tell me."

He almost smiled. Almost. "Here is what happens now, Ada. Tomorrow, Corin Vale interviews the staff again. He'll bring the census rolls. There is a child on those rolls—Mira Sennet, the binder's daughter, eight years old, marked gifted in our records because her mother was a fool and wrote it in a letter I hold." He let that sit. "You will confirm the entry. You will hand him the letter yourself. And in exchange, your gift stays between us. Your falsified entries stay between us. Your debt—" he tapped the ledger chain at my wrist, the thin iron band every debtor wore, "—stays exactly where it is, invisible, unremarkable, safe."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then tomorrow I mention that my senior scribe reads memory from paper, and the hunters take her, and I collect whatever bounty the crown offers for unregistered gifts—which, incidentally, would clear your debt twice over." He straightened his cuffs. "You see the elegance of it. Either way, I profit. I only ask you choose which way."

The child's face rose in my mind—Mira, who sat in the bindery folding quires, who hummed when she thought no one listened, whose gift I had seen flicker once, pages stirring without wind. Eight years old. The same age the world had been, once, when it chose forgetting over honor.

"You're asking me to burn her so you can keep banking ashes," I said.

"I'm asking you to be sensible." Veyne gathered the contract, closed the glass, and paused at the door. "Sensible people survive, Ada. It's the honest ones who burn."

The lock clicked shut behind him.

I stood alone in the dark among the black boxes, my palms still warm, the ghost of dragon-fire still singing in my skin—and understood, with terrible clarity, that sensible had never once saved anyone.

Tomorrow Corin would come with his census rolls and his unsettling gentleness, and I would have to decide which lie to tell him—and whether telling the truth, for once in my hidden life, might be the only way to stop burning other people to keep myself warm.

One way or another, I thought, laying my hand flat against the nearest black box, feeling the ember inside it pulse like a heartbeat—

the forgetting ends tomorrow.
