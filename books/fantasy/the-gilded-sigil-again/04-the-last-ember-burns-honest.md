## 4. The Last Ember Burns Honest

**Ada**

The vault door drank light.

I had imagined it a hundred ways—iron, wardglass, some sorcerous alloy forged in the guild's better centuries—but standing before it in the dead hour past midnight, I saw it was only wood. Old oak, black with age, banded in brass gone green. Ordinary. That was the trick of the Sigil: everything monstrous wore the face of bookkeeping.

The key was warm against my sternum, where it hung on its cord beneath my shirt. Veyne's key. He had pressed it into my hand three days ago with his bargain—*betray the child, keep your secret*—and I had smiled and said yes and hated myself for how easily the lie came. Scribes are trained to lie well. We are taught that ink is honest but hands are not.

Tonight the ledger under my arm was the only thing in this building telling the truth. The contract written in dragon-fire, the one I'd read until my eyes bled, the one that showed me a sky full of wings and a guild of clerks deciding, in tidy columns, to end the war by *hoarding* it—not ending anything, just banking the last flame like interest, letting the world forget so no one would come asking for withdrawals.

I set my palm against the oak.

My gift stirred, the way it always did near old paper. The vault remembered being a tree. It remembered rain. It did not remember what was behind it, because whatever slept back there was older than memory's manners.

"Ada."

Corin's voice, low, from the shadow of the stairwell. My heart did something complicated—relief and terror braided together, which is apparently what love looks like when you're committing arson.

"You came," I whispered.

"I told you I would." He stepped into the thin moonlight from the high window, hunter's grey, sword at his hip, his face doing the thing it did when he was holding two truths at once. "And I told you what happens if you do this. They'll burn you for it. Not the debt—the *you*. Witch-fire doesn't care about ledgers."

"So stay outside."

"No." He crossed to me, took my hand, the one not holding Veyne's key, and laced his fingers through mine like it was the simplest sentence he'd ever spoken. "I've spent six years carrying out orders I didn't read closely enough. Tonight I'm reading them again. And they're wrong."

The lock turned like a sigh.

* * *

**Corin**

The vault was cold in the way deep water is cold, and it was not empty.

Shelves rose into darkness, and on every shelf, caged in lead-glass reliquaries, burned small stubborn lights. Embers. Dozens of them. Some guttered like dying candles; one or two still pulsed with a rhythm too slow for fire and too alive for anything else. Dragon-fire. The last of it, catalogued, accessioned, *depreciated*—I had seen the depreciation schedules, had signed off on them without understanding what the numbers meant was a species going out.

Ada walked down the center aisle like someone entering a temple she'd been excommunicated from.

"There," she said.

The last ember sat in a reliquary at the aisle's end, larger than the others, burning a color I had no name for—gold shot through with red, red threaded with gold, the way dusk refuses to choose. Ada lifted it with both hands. The glass was warm. Through it, faintly, I heard something that might have been breathing.

"Ada." My voice came out wrong. "Once it's loose, we can't put it back. It'll show everyone. Every soul in this city will remember the war, the dragons, what the Sigil did—and they'll remember *her*. The girl on your falsified census line. They'll remember you."

"I know."

"They'll come for you with torches and warrants and very polite letters."

"I know, Corin." She looked up at me, and there was ash in her eyes and dawn behind it. "But look around you. This is what forgetting costs. Someone has to pay it."

Boots on the stairs.

Many boots.

* * *

**Ada**

Veyne came first, as I knew he would, flanked by hunters with drawn steel. His coat was buttoned wrong—he had dressed in haste, and some petty human corner of me found comfort in that. Even monsters fumble their buttons when their gold catches fire.

"Put it down," he said. Quiet. Reasonable. The voice he used on debtors. "Winterbourne. Put it down and we'll discuss terms. You know I always offer terms."

"That's the problem, Master Veyne." My hands were steady on the reliquary. Six years of transcription had given me steadier hands than any surgeon. "Everything with you is terms. The war ended in terms. The dragons died in installments. I'm done with terms."

"The city can't hold what's in that flame." For the first time, his voice cracked along a seam. "Ten thousand people remembering all at once—they'll tear each other apart. Order is mercy, girl. Forgetting is mercy."

"It's also profit."

Steel rang behind him. Corin had drawn—not on me. On the hunters moving to flank. He stood between them and me with his sword low and his voice carrying the authority of his rank like a shield: "By order of the Crown census, this vault is sealed until audit. Nobody advances."

"You'd take her side," Veyne said, staring at him.

Corin didn't hesitate, and I will love him for the lack of hesitation until the day I die. "I'm taking the ledger's side."

So Veyne did the only thing left to a man whose ledgers have failed him. He lunged for the reliquary himself.

I let go of the glass.

* * *

**Corin**

I have been asked since—by magistrates, by historians, by my own sleepless conscience—what it felt like when the last dragon-fire broke open.

It felt like being *read*.

Light went through us, not around us. Through the vault, up the stairs, through floors and walls and streets, out into Ashfall City like a bell struck once and never allowed to stop ringing. And inside the light was memory—not images shown to us but memories *become*, lived, worn from the inside:

A sky blackened by wings. The heat of a dragon's grief as her clutch burned. The long terrible council where men in Veyne's coat decided that wonder was a liability and wonder could be warehoused. A hatchling's last small fire cupped in clerk's hands and entered into inventory. Centuries of dark shelves. Centuries of a city sleeping soundly because it had paid someone else to hold its nightmares and lose the receipt.

Around me in the street above—when we finally staggered up into it—people stood in doorways and windows, lit gold-red, weeping. An old woman knelt in the road saying a dragon's name over and over. Hunters dropped their swords. Somewhere a guild seal cracked like ice.

And on Ada's wrist, where the debt-brand sat, the ink was lifting. Curling off her skin in threads of smoke, letter by letter, the contract unwriting itself—as though the flame had audited her account and found the balance obscene.

She watched it go with her hand pressed to her mouth.

"It's gone," she said. "Corin. It's actually—"

Behind us, the Sigil's doors swung wide and Master Aldous Veyne walked out into the light of everything he'd stolen, and the crowd turned toward him with ten thousand years of remembered grief in their faces, and I moved to stand beside Ada, shoulder to shoulder, my sword still drawn but pointed at nothing, at everything, at history.

Whatever came next—the trials, the reckoning, the child on the falsified line whose gift was now known to every soul in the square—we would meet it standing in the same direction.

The ember's light settled over the city like snow that refused to melt.

And far off, beyond the rooftops, something vast answered it—a sound none of us had ever heard and all of us now remembered.

A wingbeat.
