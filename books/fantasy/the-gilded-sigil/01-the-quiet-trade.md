## 1. The Quiet Trade

The bone told Beatrix Zhou everything before she ever touched the knife to it.

It was a shinbone, human, bought honestly from the charnel house on Salt Lane for three coppers—the price of a bad loaf—and it had been whispering to her since she'd carried it home wrapped in oilcloth like a fish. Most bones murmured. They held echoes the way river stones held cold: faint, diffuse, a pressure of *someone* without any particular someone attached. But this one had a voice in it, whole and impatient, and it had been talking at her for two days while she waited for the widow to come.

*Tell her I didn't suffer,* the voice said, as it said every morning when Beatrix sat down at her workbench and unwrapped it. *That's what she needs to hear. Tell her the fever took me fast, that I went in my sleep. She keeps saying she should have been there. Tell her there was nothing to be there for.*

Beatrix turned the bone over in her hands. In the grey light of her back room—she kept the shutters half-closed even in daylight, a habit ten years deep—it looked like any other piece of carved stock. Pale, dense, faintly yellowed at the knuckle end. To anyone else it would be exactly that. To her it was a man named Corvin Hale, dockhand, forty-one years dead by drowning, who had opinions about his wife's spending habits and an abiding tenderness he'd never once said aloud while alive.

*And tell her about the blue shawl,* the voice added, because it always added. *The one she wanted from Merren's shop, the year before I died. I put the coin aside. It's in the flour tin behind the loose brick. Tell her.*

"I'll tell her," Beatrix said quietly, to the empty room, and picked up her knife.

She worked small. That was the first rule of staying alive in Vellamare, and she had learned it so thoroughly it no longer felt like a rule—it felt like the shape of her hands. Small carvings. Small charms. Mourning pieces mostly, because grief made people discreet; nobody asked too many questions of a woman who sold them something to hold at a funeral. A charm the size of a thumbnail, worn at the throat or tucked into a coffin lining, and if it seemed to hum with comfort, well, grieving folk imagined all manner of things. Grief gave her cover the way fog gave cover to smugglers. She had built a quiet little trade on it, and she intended to die old and unnoticed inside it.

The knife moved. Bone carving was slow work, patient work—you couldn't rush it or the grain split and took the voice with it, fraying the echo into static. She cut a spiral first, always the spiral, the oldest shape, the shape of listening. Then the interlocking bands around it that would hold the intention steady. Her blade was a repurposed boning knife with a walnut handle worn smooth as sea glass, and it made a sound against the bone like a fingernail drawn slowly down silk.

*You're good at that,* Corvin's voice observed. He liked to watch, insofar as the dead watched anything. *Better than the last one who tried. There was a woman, oh, sixty years back, who used my femur for a button. A button!*

"You've mentioned."

*I have?*

"Twice." She blew away a curling thread of bone dust. "You're repeating yourself more lately. That happens. The older the death, the fewer words you have left. You should save them."

*Save them for what?*

"For her. She's coming today."

The voice went still in the way only the dead could go still—not silent, but gathered, concentrated, like a held breath. Beatrix felt it press against the inside of the bone, against the spiral she was cutting, and she had learned long ago not to flinch from that pressure. It wasn't malice. It was want, pure and enormous, the want of someone who had been waiting forty-one years to say four sentences.

She understood that want better than she let herself admit. Ten years since the Ban, since the Proclamation read from every temple step in Vellamare declaring the practice of magic a crime against the realm, punishable by the pyre or the Quiet Room depending on the magistrate's mood. Ten years since the last sanctioned god-talker stopped talking and the temples went hollow and the whole city agreed, loudly and in public, that there had never been anything to hear anyway. Ten years of Beatrix keeping her head down and her gift smaller than a coin, hearing the dead in the bones she touched and telling absolutely no one.

Her mother had taught her. That was the part that still caught in her chest when she thought of it—that there had been a time, before, when this was just a thing women in her family did, unremarkable as bread-baking. Her mother had pressed a chicken bone into her palm at seven years old and said, *Listen. What do you hear?* And Beatrix had heard, and that had been that. Then came the Ban, and her mother's arrest, and the pyre smoke visible from the harbor, and Beatrix had learned the second rule of staying alive: whatever you are, be less of it.

*She's here,* Corvin said suddenly. *I can tell. The floorboards do that thing.*

They did. Widow Hale had a walk like weather coming in—heavy, deliberate, apologetic to the stairs themselves—and Beatrix heard her on the third step, set down her knife, and arranged her face into the mild professional blankness that customers expected. Not warm. Warm invited conversation. Not cold. Cold invited suspicion. Mildly sympathetic, faintly tired, the face of a craftswoman thinking about grain angles and nothing else.

"Signora Zhou?" The door opened onto Maren Hale, sixty-odd, dressed in the brown of formal mourning gone soft with wear. Her eyes were red-rimmed in the way of eyes that had cried themselves into a kind of dry exhaustion. "The chandler's wife said you did... remembrance pieces."

"Remembrance pieces," Beatrix agreed. It was a good phrase. It meant nothing and sounded like something. "Come in, signora. Sit, please. May I offer you tea?"

"No tea." Maren sat anyway, perching on the edge of the stool like a bird ready to fly off. Her hands twisted in her lap. "I don't—I don't know how to ask for what I want. The temple says he's with the Silent God now, past all speaking, and that's supposed to comfort me, but it doesn't. It sounds like being alone forever."

Beatrix kept her gaze on the workbench. This was the delicate part, the part where she walked the line between service and crime, and she had walked it perhaps two hundred times in ten years without once falling off. She could not promise anything. She could not say the word *voice*. She could only make the object and let the customer's own heart do the confessing.

"Some people find," she said carefully, "that holding a piece of carved bone helps. Something worked with care. Something that seems to..." She searched for the safe word. "...settle. When they hold it. Some say it settles their thoughts. Some say it feels like the person is near. I can't speak to what it truly is. But I carve well, and I take my time, and I use good bone."

"And it costs?"

"Two silvers for a mourning spiral. One silver if times are hard, and we don't discuss which."

Maren Hale almost smiled—a cracked, exhausted thing, but real. "Times are hard," she said, "but he'd be ashamed of me haggling over his own—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. "Two silvers. Please."

Beatrix took the coins, counted out nothing in return because none was needed, and reached beneath the workbench for the finished charm. She'd completed it the night before, after midnight, working by candle stub with Corvin's voice coaching her on the details—the width of his shoulders in the spiral's turn, a joke only he and Maren would understand encoded in the number of bands. Three bands. *Because I fell off the pier three times courting her,* he'd insisted, delighted with himself. *She'll know. She laughed until she cried the third time.*

She placed it in Maren's open palms.

The widow went very still.

Beatrix watched, as she always watched, with her breath held and her heart doing that painful double-beat it did at this moment, every time, the moment where everything hung on whether a stranger's grief would recognize what it was holding.

"Oh," Maren said softly. "Oh, it's *warm.*"

"They often seem warm," Beatrix said. "Bone holds heat."

But Maren wasn't listening anymore. She had closed her hands around the little spiral and bowed her head over it, and tears were sliding down her face in silence, and her lips were moving without sound, and Beatrix knew—she knew with absolute certainty—what was happening on the other side of that silence. Corvin was talking. Pouring out his saved-up words. *I didn't suffer. The flour tin. The blue shawl. I fell off the pier three times and I'd have fallen a fourth.*

*Tell her the shawl money's there,* Corvin's voice said through the bone on the bench, fainter now, spending itself. *Tell her I loved her more than the boat. She always thought it was the boat.*

"The shawl money is in the flour tin," Beatrix said quietly. "Behind the loose brick in your kitchen. He put it aside for you. He wanted you to have the blue one."

Maren's head snapped up. For one terrible instant her wet eyes were wide and sharp and full of a question that could bring soldiers to this door, and Beatrix met the look steadily and did not blink and thought, with strange calm, *Well. This is how it ends, then. Over a shawl.*

But Maren Hale was a woman who had lived forty years beside a dockhand and knew which questions got people burned. She closed her mouth. She clutched the charm to her breastbone. And she said, in a voice scraped raw:

"He always said it was the boat."

"It was never the boat," Beatrix said.

Maren stood, and pressed both of Beatrix's hands between her own—warm, trembling, human hands—and said nothing else at all, because nothing else could safely be said, and left. Beatrix listened to her footsteps descend the stairs, slower than they'd come up, heavier in a different way, and then fade into the street noise of Vellamare: cart wheels, gull cries, a fishmonger's bell, the ordinary machinery of a city pretending very hard that its dead were silent.

*Thank you,* Corvin whispered. The voice was thin now, unraveling, a coat giving up its threads. Forty-one years of waiting spent in ten minutes. *Thank you. That's... that's all of it. That's everything I had.*

"I know," Beatrix said. "Rest well, Corvin."

*Will she wear it?*

"Every day. Until she joins you."

*Good,* he said. *Good.* And then, fainter, almost amused, almost fond: *You should charge more.*

And he was gone—not dead, he had been dead for decades, but dispersed, the pattern finally scattered, the way a wave gives itself back to the sea. Beatrix sat alone in her half-dark room with the empty bone in her hands and felt the familiar ache settle into her chest, the one that never quite healed, the tax her gift collected on every transaction. She heard what no one else heard. She carried messages across the oldest border in the world. And every single time, she stood on the shore afterward, waving at someone departing, alone on her side of the water.

Ten years of this. Ten years of small charms and careful lies and mild professional blankness, of watching widows weep over warmth and saying *bone holds heat*, of knowing the names of the dead better than the living, of going home to an empty room above an empty shop where the only voices were the ones in the stock. She had friends of a sort—the chandler's wife, the charnel keeper who didn't ask why she bought human bone in such quantity. None of them knew her. You couldn't know someone who was mostly hidden. Being known was a debt, and debts got called in, and called-in debts in Vellamare got paid in ash.

She cleaned her knife. She swept the bone dust into the jar she kept for it—waste nothing, waste was suspicious—and she wrapped Corvin's spent shinbone in fresh oilcloth and set it aside for grinding into powder, which made excellent polish and carried no voice at all anymore. Ordinary routines. Safe routines. The architecture of a small life, load-bearing walls of habit, and she inspected each one the way she inspected her shutters each night: closed, latched, invisible.

Outside, the afternoon was turning gold over the rooftops. Somewhere a temple bell rang the hour, flat and toneless—the temples had melted down their singing bells after the Ban, another thing the city pretended not to miss. Beatrix stood at her window with her arms crossed, looking out at the free city of Vellamare, beautiful and frightened, and told herself what she told herself every evening: *Stay small. Stay quiet. Tomorrow, another charm, another whisper, another goodbye. It's enough. It has to be enough.*

It was almost true. It had been almost true for ten years.

She was reaching to close the shutters when she saw it—on the shelf above her bench, among the finished stock, among the dozen quiet spirals waiting for customers who might never come.

One of them was glowing.

A soft, impossible, blue-white light, like moonlight trapped under skin, pulsing gently in the dim room. A charm she had carved three years ago from a nameless scrap of rib, sold to no one, forgotten. It had never done that. Nothing had ever done that.

And as she stared, frozen, her hand still on the shutter, the little bone began—to sing.

* * *

It had no words at first. Just a sound, high and clear and ancient, threading through the back room like light through water, and every other bone in Beatrix's shop answered it. The drawer of raw stock rattled softly against its stops. The spent pieces in their oilcloth stirred. The whole quiet inventory of her careful, hidden, ten-years-small life woke up at once and began to murmur, dozens of voices overlapping, the dead of Vellamare rising in her shelves like a tide.

Beatrix backed against the wall. Her heart slammed. Every instinct screamed at her to smother it, bury it, throw the thing in the harbor—but her hands wouldn't move, because underneath the terror was something she hadn't felt in ten years and had given up expecting to feel again.

Wonder.

The glow brightened. The song found words—or found the shape of words, vast and slow, like something enormous turning over in its sleep after a very long time. It was not Corvin's voice. It was not any dead man's voice. It was older than dead, older than the city, and it said only one thing, in a language she somehow understood without knowing how:

*We hear you now, little listener. We are answering.*

Then the light went out, and the shop was dark, and every bone in Vellamare went silent at once—

—and somewhere across the city, a bell that had not sung in ten years began, very faintly, to ring.
