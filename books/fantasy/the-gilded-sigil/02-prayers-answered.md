## 2. Prayers Answered

The charm had been dead for three years, and then it wasn't.

Beatrix Zhou noticed it the way one notices a change in weather—not all at once, but as a pressure behind the ears, a wrongness in the air of the shop that made her set down her carving knife and stand very still among her shelves of bone and silence.

It was the widow's charm. No—the fisherman's. The one she'd carved for old Tomas Ferren back when the Ban was still new and she had been foolish enough to believe that small kindnesses could stay hidden forever. A knucklebone of river cod, polished smooth, carved with the sigil of the Drowned Mother so that Tomas's daughter could hold it to her ear on stormy nights and hear her father's voice telling her, over and over, *the nets are mended, little fish, the nets are mended.* She remembered making it because it had been one of the last things she'd made before she learned fear properly—before the inquisitors burned the charm-seller in Saltmarket Row and Beatrix had understood exactly what her hands could cost her.

Tomas's daughter had moved away years ago. The charm should have gone with her, or gone silent, or gone to dust in some drawer in some house across the harbor.

Instead it sat on her workbench where she had no memory of putting it, and it glowed.

Not brightly. That was the terrible part. It glowed the way a coal glows under ash—a deep, patient blue-white light seeping up through the grain of the bone, following the channels of her carving like water finding its way down a dry streambed. And from it came a sound.

Beatrix had spent ten years listening to the dead. She knew every texture their voices could take: the papery whisper of the long-buried, the wet urgency of the newly drowned, the looping, broken repetitions of those who died confused. This was none of these. This voice came *through* the bone the way sunlight comes through a curtain—filtered, transformed, and unmistakably not of this world.

*"—heard, heard, heard at last—"*

She snatched her hand back from the bench as if the charm had bitten her.

The shop around her was dim, evening coming on, the shutters half-drawn against the salt wind off the Vellamare harbor. Outside, cart wheels rattled over cobbles; somewhere a fishwife argued about the price of eels; the ordinary sounds of an ordinary city going about its ordinary, magicless evening. Ten years ago, the Ban had fallen over the free cities like a lid over a pot, and everything beneath it had learned to simmer quietly. The gods had stopped answering. The shrines had gone dark. The dead had grown faint, fainter, until only someone like Beatrix—someone born with the listening in her bones, the way other people were born with perfect pitch—could catch them at all, and even then only through the medium she knew best: carved bone, old death made into a vessel for older voices.

She had built her whole life inside that quiet. Small shop. Small trade. Mourning charms sold discreetly to grieving families who told themselves the comfort they bought was only superstition, only psychology, only the shape of a bone warm from a living hand. She kept her prices low and her door plain and her name off no register. She had made herself a woman the world could look straight through, and for ten years that invisibility had been the closest thing she had to a friend.

And now a dead charm on her workbench was glowing like a piece of the sky and saying words no dead man knew.

*"Heard,"* said the voice again, and there was such relief in it, such vast and ancient astonishment, that Beatrix felt tears startle hot in her eyes before she could stop them. *"After so long. After so long below. Who prayed? Who called? We heard."*

"Who are you?" she whispered. Then, absurdly, because ten years of caution die hard: "Quietly. Please. Quietly."

The glow pulsed once, almost like laughter.

The Drowned Mother. It had to be. Beatrix had carved Her sigil into the bone a hundred times without ever once believing the goddess might actually be on the other end of it—sigils were keys, her mentor used to say, but a key is nothing without a door, and the Ban had locked every door in the world. Sefa had taught her that. Sefa had taught her everything, back when teaching it hadn't been a hanging offense, and Sefa had died believing the doors would open again someday, though whether that was faith or stubbornness Beatrix had never determined.

The doors were opening. The thought arrived whole and cold in her chest, and with it came its twin, equally cold: *and when doors open, people notice.*

* * *

She did not sleep that night.

She tried. She banked her stove, barred her door, tucked the fisherman's charm into an iron box lined with wool and buried the box under the floorboards beneath her bed, and lay in the dark listening to the harbor bells count the hours. But the dead would not let her alone.

They had always been there, faintly, the way stars are there at noon—invisible but present, if you knew how to look. For ten years their voices had come to her thinned and distant, needing bone as a lens, needing her stillness and patience, each one a small labor of attention like threading a needle by candlelight. Tonight they were everywhere. Lying in her bed she could hear them through the floor, through the walls, through the very ground: the drowned murmuring in the harbor silt, the plague-dead sighing under the cemetery hill, an old woman two streets over who had died in her sleep last winter repeating her daughter's name with the patience of tide against stone. Not screaming. Not risen. Just—*louder*. As if a hand had turned up the wick of a lamp that had been burning low for a decade.

And threaded through all of it, underneath it, that same enormous voice, or voices—it was hard to tell if it was one god speaking or many, the way it is hard to tell if the sea is one thing or countless drops—murmuring out of the dark:

*We heard. We heard. Who called us?*

Because that was the other thing. That afternoon, after she'd recovered enough to close the shop early, she had walked the long way home along the waterfront, and she had passed the shrine niche in the seawall where fishermen used to leave oil and coins for the Drowned Mother before the Ban. The niche had been empty and dark for ten years, like all of them. Tonight it was not empty. Someone had left a stub of tallow candle, freshly burnt, and a handful of copper coins green with age, and scratched into the stone beside it, crude and desperate: *please bring him back safe.*

A prayer. An actual prayer, offered to a goddess who had not answered one in ten years, by someone who had no reason to hope except hope itself.

And the gods had heard it. Beatrix carried no doubt of that; she had heard the hearing itself, vast and startled, echoing up through the bones of the world. Somewhere out on the black water tonight, a boat was coming home through weather it should not have survived, and its crew would never know why.

She lay in the dark and pressed her palms against her eyes and felt the fragile architecture of her life groan around her like a ship in ice.

Ten years. Ten years of staying small. Of selling comfort disguised as craft, of keeping her gift folded up small as a moth's wing, of watching the inquisitors' gray cloaks pass her window and feeling not guilt exactly—she had told herself it was not guilt—but something quieter and more corrosive. Relief. *Not me. Not today.* Every bonfire she had not been thrown onto had been purchased with her silence, and she had paid it gladly, and she had hated herself for paying it gladly, and she had done it anyway, day after day, year after year, because staying alive is a habit that roots deeper than shame.

But the gods were awake. And the dead were loud. And charms that had been silent for years were glowing on workbenches, and prayers scratched into seawalls were being answered by something immense and hungry-for-contact, and none of that could stay secret. Magic was a tide, whatever the Ban's authors had believed; you could dam it, but it did not stop existing, it only waited, and grew, and found the cracks.

When the tide comes in, Beatrix thought, staring at the ceiling, it does not ask permission of the things standing on the sand.

* * *

Near dawn, she gave up on sleep and went down to the shop, and that was when she discovered how bad it truly was.

Every charm was awake.

Not glowing—all of them, thank all the quiet powers, not glowing—but *speaking*, each one murmuring at the edge of her hearing like a room full of sleepers talking in their dreams. The mourning charms waiting on her shelf for customers who had not yet come. The rejects in the drawer, the mis-carvings, the failures. Even the raw materials: the box of unworked bone scraps in the corner had begun to whisper, because bone remembers whose it was, and for ten years that remembering had been too faint to matter, and now it mattered, now it filled the shop like fog filling a valley.

Beatrix stood in the middle of her own workspace and put her hands over her ears, which was ridiculous, which had never once worked, and listened anyway, because she could not not listen; the listening was not in her ears, it was in her marrow.

An old man's charm reciting the names of his grandchildren, over and over, tender as a rosary. A soldier's finger-bone humming a marching song with the wrong words, the words he'd really been thinking. A child's charm—gods, a child's—saying *it doesn't hurt anymore, tell Mama it doesn't hurt anymore,* and Beatrix had to sit down on her stool and breathe through the wave of grief that broke over her, because she had carved that one six years ago for a mother in the Tanner's Quarter and had never let herself wonder, until this moment, whether the mother still held it, whether she fell asleep some nights with her dead son's voice against her ear, and whether that was mercy or a wound that never closed.

The dead were louder. That was the truth of it, the simple and catastrophic truth. Whatever had sealed the world for ten years was cracking, and everything that had been pressed down was rising, and Beatrix Zhou, who had survived the Ban by being no one, was suddenly the most dangerous kind of person in Vellamare: a listener, in a city full of voices.

She was still sitting there, dawn graying the shutters, when the knock came.

Three knocks. Measured. Unhurried. The knock of someone who expected the door to open and was prepared to wait for it.

Beatrix went cold from the scalp down. Inquisitors did not knock like that—they knocked like battering rams, or worse, they didn't knock at all. Customers did not come at dawn. Nobody came at dawn to a bone-carver's shop except—

Except whoever had sent the dream.

Because that was the last thing, the thing she had not yet let herself think about directly, the thing that had been circling her all night like a gull over a drowning man. In the thin hour before morning, she had dreamed—not slept, she had not slept, but she had dreamed nonetheless—of a woman's voice, young and ragged with desperation, praying. Praying to anyone. To the old gods by their forgotten names, to the saints the priesthood had struck from the calendars, to the dark itself. *Please. Please, someone. He's dying and I don't know what he needs and the priests won't come, they say it's against the law to pray for healing, please, please, someone hear me—*

And the prayer had not dissipated into the indifferent air. It had *traveled*. Beatrix had felt it arrive, felt it settle against her like a hand on her shoulder, felt it carry with it a direction, a street, a name half-formed: Oduya. The woman was praying for someone named Oduya, or the woman was named Oduya herself, and she was praying in a room that smelled of beeswax and blood, and the prayer had crossed the sleeping city and chosen Beatrix—chosen her, out of everyone, because she was the one who could hear.

Prayers were supposed to go up. This one had come *sideways*, looking for a person, and found her, and now here was a knock at her door at dawn, and Beatrix stood in her shop full of whispering bones and understood that the quiet life was over. It had ended sometime between yesterday's dusk and this dawn, cracked open like an eggshell, and everything she had hoarded her whole adult life—her safety, her smallness, her careful gray invisibility—was spilling out into a world that was waking up whether she liked it or not.

The knock came again. Three measured strikes.

She looked down at her hands. Carver's hands, scarred and steady. Hands that had stayed folded for ten years.

Then she crossed the floor, lifted the bar, and opened the door.
