## 3. The Priest Who Fell

Anselm Oduya had not prayed in nine years, and the prayer that finally broke his silence was not his own.

It came to him in the hospice ward of Saint Verity's, where he had gone to wash linens and change bedpans and otherwise make himself small among the dying—a penance he had assigned himself after the Ban, though no confessor had asked it of him. The woman in the corner cot was past speaking. Her lungs rattled like dice in a cup, and her eyes had gone the color of watered milk, fixed on the ceiling as if she could see through it to something better or worse. He had knelt beside her to sponge her wrists, a task beneath notice, beneath suspicion.

And then she had prayed. Not aloud—her throat was long past that—but *outward*, somehow, with a force that made the air above her cot shimmer like heat off summer stone. Anselm had spent eleven years of his life learning to detect prayer, to weigh its cadence and catch its scent on the wind of a room. He knew what a prayer felt like when it landed. This one did not land. It rose.

*Let someone find her,* the dying woman's soul said, in words that were not words. *The carver on Lantern Row. Let someone warn her before they come.*

Anselm's hands went still on her wrists. The sponge dripped. In the old days—the days before the Ban, when he had been a young acolyte of the Vigilant Order with a brand-new censer and a heart full of certainty—he would have known exactly what to do with a prayer like this. He would have written it down. He would have carried it up the chain of command, and somewhere above him a man in grey vestments would have marked the woman's name in a ledger, and within the week men with iron staves would have come for whoever it was she was praying for.

He had done it forty-one times. He kept count. That was part of his penance too—not the washing, which was easy, but the counting, which was not. Forty-one names in a ledger he could still recite in order, awake at three in the morning, staring at the ceiling of his rented room. Forty-one people whose doors he had knocked on, whose shops he had searched, whose children he had watched led away to the re-education houses while their parents wept into their sleeves so as not to give the inquisitors the satisfaction.

*The carver on Lantern Row.*

He did not know who she meant. But he knew what it meant that a prayer could rise at all. For ten years, since the Ban, since the Silence fell over Vellamare like a lid on a pot, prayers had gone nowhere. Everyone knew it. The priests still performed the offices because the law required offices, but everyone understood they were letters posted to an address that had burned down. The gods were gone—or bound, or fled, or dead; the theology of it remained fiercely disputed by people who had never once heard a god—and prayer had become a habit without a destination.

This prayer had a destination. It had risen like smoke finding a chimney draft, and Anselm had felt it pass through him on its way, cold as well-water, purposeful as an arrow.

The old gods were answering again. And whatever else that meant, it meant the world was about to break open, and the woman in the corner cot—with her last breath, with the last coin of her failing strength—had spent it not on herself, not on her own salvation, but on a warning for a stranger.

Anselm sat back on his heels. His knees cracked. Somewhere across the ward, another patient moaned in fevered sleep, and the tallow lamps guttered in their brackets, and outside the rain came down on Saint Verity's slate roof with the patience of something that intended to go on forever.

"All right," he said quietly, to the woman, to himself, to whatever had just listened to her. "All right."

---

Lantern Row was easy enough to find. What took him two days was deciding to go.

He told himself he was being careful. A man of his former profession did not walk into a bone-carver's shop on rumor and a deathbed whisper; he verified; he observed; he built a case. And it was true that he was careful—he took the long way through the fish market, doubled back along the canal, watched the shop from the doorway of a chandler's across the street while pretending to examine wicks. But underneath the tradecraft, older and more honest, was simple cowardice wearing carefulness like a borrowed coat. Because he knew what he would find there. He had always known, in the way that a man knows the shape of his own sin. Ten years ago, when the Vigilant Order had swept Vellamare district by district, there had been a list of suspected practitioners, and on that list, in his own handwriting, copied faithfully from his superior's notes: *Zhou, Beatrix — carver, Lantern Row — bone charms, possible resonance — insufficient evidence, deferred.*

Deferred. Not cleared. Deferred, because the evidence had been thin and the quotas for that quarter already filled, and because Anselm Oduya, twenty-six years old and hungry for advancement, had not argued the point. He had moved on to the next name. Forty-one names. And one name he hadn't moved on, and had told himself for a decade that the omission was mercy.

Now a dying woman's prayer had named her, and the gods themselves—if the gods themselves—had seen fit to carry it, and Anselm understood with terrible clarity that his decade of small kindnesses and counted sins had been building toward this: he was going to have to walk across that street, open that door, and confess to a stranger that he was the reason her life had been a held breath for ten years.

The shop was narrow and dark, wedged between a dyer's and a shuttered tea house, its window displaying rows of carved bone pieces on black velvet: charms shaped like sleeping birds, like interlaced knots, like tiny doors. Mourning work, mostly. The kind of carving that was legal precisely because it was believed to be empty—pretty shapes in pretty materials, sentiment made solid, nothing more. The Ban had been thorough about living magic and oddly tender about grief. People needed somewhere to put their dead.

A hand-painted sign above the door read *B. Zhou, Carver*, in neat, unassuming letters. No sigils. No wards that he could see, though he had learned never to trust his own sight where craft was concerned.

He stood before the door for a long moment, rain dripping off the brim of his hat, and rehearsed nothing, because there was nothing to rehearse. There was no version of this speech that ended well. Then he went in.

---

The bell over the door gave a single flat note, and the woman behind the counter looked up from her work, and Anselm saw her understand him instantly—saw it happen, the whole recognition, in the space of half a breath.

She was perhaps thirty-five, sharp-boned, with ink-stained fingers and the wary stillness of a creature that has survived by not moving. Her carving knife rested against the edge of a half-finished charm, and her eyes—dark, level, unhurried—went from his face to his hands to the way he stood, weight even, shoulders squared out of old training, and he watched her arrive at the conclusion. He had worn the Order's grey for eleven years. Some things stayed in the body.

"You're not here for mourning work," she said. Not a question.

"No." His voice came out lower than he intended. He cleared his throat. "My name is Anselm Oduya. I was—I am—" He stopped. Started again. "I was a Vigilant. Third Circle, Office of Resonance. I helped enforce the Ban. I wrote reports that sent people to the re-education houses. Forty-one of them. I can name every one."

The silence that followed had texture. Rain against the window. The tick of the shop's single lamp. Somewhere below the floor, water moving in a pipe.

Beatrix Zhou set down her knife with great care, the way one sets down a thing that might otherwise be thrown. "That," she said, "is a remarkable way to introduce yourself to a stranger."

"It seemed dishonest to do it any other way."

"Did it." She studied him the way she might study a piece of bone before committing the knife to it—looking for the flaw, the hollow place, the lie inside the material. "Most men who come to confess bring a priest. You're telling me you *are* the priest."

"I'm telling you I'm the thing priests confess to now. Or was." He pulled the wet hat from his head. Water ran off it onto his boots. "I've spent nine years trying to decide if that makes me qualified to ask forgiveness or uniquely disqualified from ever receiving it. I haven't decided. I may never decide. But that isn't why I'm here."

"No?"

"A woman is dying at Saint Verity's. Name of Odile Ferran. She hasn't the strength left to speak, but last night she prayed—truly prayed, the kind that goes somewhere—for the first time in ten years, and her prayer went somewhere. I felt it pass through me on its way up." He watched the woman's face carefully as he said it, and he was rewarded—or punished—with the smallest flicker, quickly mastered, around her eyes. She knew. Whatever he was about to tell her, some part of her already knew. "She prayed for someone to warn the carver on Lantern Row. Before they come."

The flicker became stillness. Deeper stillness. The stillness of a pond freezing from the edges inward.

"They," Beatrix Zhou repeated.

"The inquisitors are coming back to Vellamare. All of them. From the capital." He said it plainly, because plainness was all he had left to offer. "I still have friends in the Order. Former friends. One of them drinks too much and talks too much, and three nights ago he told me the Crown has issued new writs. Full authority. House-to-house resonance sweeps, starting with the artisan quarters. They're calling it the Purification of the Coast Cities." He paused. "They're calling it that because the last time they used that phrase, in Harrowgate, they burned eleven shops and took nineteen people. Two of them were children."

The lamp flame leaned in a draft and straightened itself. Beatrix Zhou had not moved, but Anselm saw her hand had drifted, without her seeming to notice, to rest on the counter beside the half-finished charm—as if it were a railing and she a passenger on a pitching deck.

"Why?" she asked. It was barely more than breath. "Ten years. Why now? The Ban worked. Everything's quiet. Everyone's *quiet*."

"Because it isn't working anymore." He stepped closer to the counter, slowly, telegraphing every inch, and lowered his voice though there was no one to hear. "That's what my friend didn't understand, what none of them understand yet. The new writs aren't precautionary. Prayers are landing again, all over the realm. Little ones, mostly—cures, blessings, a fisherman's wife in Saltmarsh whose drowned son sat up in his shroud, if the story's true. The Crown knows. The Crown has known for weeks. And the Crown is afraid." He let that sit. "You don't send inquisitors with full writs against a fear unless the fear is real. Something has changed, mistress Zhou. The gods are answering. And the thing that sits the throne does not want to be answered to."

Something moved behind her eyes then—something quick and bright and frightened, and under the fright, unbelievably, a kind of fierce gladness, like a woman hearing that a prisoner she'd mourned had been seen alive. She looked down at her hands, at the fine white dust of bone in the creases of her fingers, and when she spoke again her voice had changed, gone quieter, almost to herself.

"I carve charms," she said. "Mourning work. Legal work. Pretty shapes in dead material." Her thumb moved over the half-finished piece on the counter, a small bird with folded wings. "That's what I've told myself for ten years. That's what I told the Vigilants, the one time they came. And every night I take the day's shavings and I burn them, and every night I listen to what the bones say, and every night I tell myself it's nothing. It's echo. It's grief talking to itself." She looked up at him. "Last week one of my own charms started glowing. One I carved four years ago. It spoke. Not the dead man in it. Something *else*. Something old." Her jaw tightened. "I told myself that was echo too."

Anselm felt something loosen in his chest that had been clenched for nine years. Not forgiveness—nothing so clean—but recognition, which might be its poor cousin. Here it was, then. The two halves of the same broken law, standing on opposite sides of a counter: the man who had enforced the silence, and the woman who had broken it nightly and called it nothing. Both of them small. Both of them careful. Both of them complicit in their different ways—she in the complicity of hiding, he in the complicity of hunting—and both of them, apparently, done.

"They'll sweep Lantern Row first," he said. "Artisan quarters, then the temples, then outward. You have days. Perhaps less. My friend expects the writs to be executed by week's end."

"And you came to tell me this. Why? Out of guilt?" There was no cruelty in it. It was an honest question, honestly asked, and she deserved an honest answer, and he found that after nine years he finally had one.

"At first. Guilt is a good engine; it runs whether you feed it or not." He turned his hat in his hands. Water dripped. "But no. I came because a dying woman spent her last prayer on you, and the gods carried it, and I don't think the gods carry messages for nothing. I think—" and here his voice did fail him briefly, and he steadied it, "—I think I've been waiting nine years for someone to give me a job worth doing. And this seems to be it."

Beatrix Zhou looked at him for a long time. Outside, the rain thickened, drumming on the awning, and the lamplight caught the bone dust in the air between them, turning it briefly to a faint gold haze, like something consecrated.

"You understand," she said at last, "that I can't trust you. That everything you just said might be the oldest trick in the Order's book—walk in, confess beautifully, see what the witch says when she thinks she's safe."

"Yes."

"And you understand that if you're lying, I will know. Eventually. The bones remember everyone who touches them, priest. Even you. Especially you."

"Yes," he said again. "I'm counting on it."

Something that was almost, almost a smile touched the corner of her mouth and withdrew. She reached beneath the counter and came up with a second stool, old and three-legged, and set it across from hers with a small definitive knock.

"Sit down, Anselm Oduya," she said. "If we have days, we'll spend them properly. Tell me everything your drunk friend told you. Every name, every date, every route the sweeps will take." She picked her knife back up, and the little bone bird caught the light, wings folded, waiting. "And then you're going to help me decide what a woman does with a lifetime of listening when the listening is about to become a death sentence."

He sat. The stool held. Outside, far off across the rooftops of Vellamare, a church bell began to ring the hour—and beneath its bronze voice, so faint that only two people in the whole city might have heard it, something answered. A low tone, vast and patient, rising out of the ground itself, out of the walls, out of the bones of the city.

Prayers were landing. And something below the world had begun, at last, to reply.

* * *

Two streets away, in the doorway of the shuttered tea house, a man in travelling clothes finished writing in a small grey notebook, closed it, and slipped away into the rain without hurrying.
