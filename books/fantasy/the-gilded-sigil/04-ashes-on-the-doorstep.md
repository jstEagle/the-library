## 4. Ashes on the Doorstep

The smoke woke Beatrix before the bells did.

It came through the gap beneath her shutters smelling wrong—not the honest stink of a chimney fire or a tallow-maker's accident, but the sweet, chemical reek of consecrated oil, the kind the Inquisition burned in silver censers when they wanted to purify a room of what they called *residue*. She had smelled it once before, ten years ago, from the far side of a market square, and she had done what everyone did: she had pulled her hood up and walked the other way.

She was out of bed and at the window before her mind caught up with her feet.

Across the lane, Old Marta's shop was on fire.

Not all of it—not yet. The flames had taken the front room where Marta sold her dried herbs and her luck-knots and her little clay figures of the river mothers, figures that were technically just folk art, technically harmless, technically nothing the Wardens of the Ban needed to trouble themselves over. Firelight poured through the shattered display window and lay across the cobbles like spilled honey. Above it, three figures in grey-and-silver moved with the unhurried economy of men doing a job they had done many times. Inquisitors. Two held the long brass pokers they used to stir the burning; the third stood at the door with a ledger open against his forearm, checking names off as though he were taking inventory of a shipment rather than a life.

And at the edge of the light, bound to the hitching post with wire that bit into her wrists, stood Marta herself, watching her shop burn.

Beatrix's hand went to her mouth. Below her own window, in the shadow of her own doorway, she could see the small pile of her own sign—*B. ZHOU, BONE & IVORY WORKS, EST. LICENSED*—the word *licensed* painted in careful gold letters she had paid extra for, because licensed meant invisible, invisible meant safe. Ten years she had kept that sign polished. Ten years she had carved only what the permits allowed: buttons, dice, knife-handles, mourning charms filed under *commemorative craftwork, non-liturgical*. Ten years of hearing the dead whisper through everything she touched and saying nothing, carving nothing true, being no one.

*Marta is going to die,* she thought, with terrible clarity. *They are going to burn her shop and then they are going to burn her, and I am going to stand at this window until it is finished, and then I will go downstairs and sweep ash off my doorstep and open for business as usual.*

She knew this because she had seen it before. Because this was how the Ban worked—not with armies but with windows, with every gifted person in Vellamare watching from behind glass and choosing the glass.

The fire crackled. Somewhere in the burning front room something made of clay burst with a small domestic pop, like a cork.

Then Beatrix heard singing.

It came from inside the house—from the back rooms, above the fire—and it was a child's voice, thin and clear and utterly unafraid, threading up through the smoke:

*"River Mother, wide and deep,*
*hold the fire, hold it sleeping—"*

The flames along the front wall stuttered.

Beatrix felt it happen. There was no other way to describe it—a pressure against her ears, the same sensation she got when a charm spoke, but vast, a tide instead of a trickle. The old gods. Ten years of silence since the Ban, ten years of prayers falling into a well with no bottom, and now—since three nights ago, since her own old charms had begun to glow on their shelves like embers—the gods were answering again. Answering *her*, answering everyone, answering, apparently, a child singing in a burning building.

The fire bent away from the back wall. It was subtle; an inquisitor glancing up would see only smoke behaving oddly, wind through broken glass. But Beatrix saw it the way she heard the dead—clearly, undeniably, with the whole of whatever she was.

The singing stopped. A cough. Small. Then silence.

*There's a child in there.*

She was moving before she decided to move. That was the thing about defiance, she would think later—it didn't arrive as a decision. It arrived as a body already in motion, a door already opening, feet already on cold stairs while the sensible part of her screamed about the license, the sign, the ten years, the glass. She grabbed the heavy wool cloak off its peg and the bucket by the hearth and let herself out the back, into the alley that ran between her shop and Marta's, the alley where Marta had hung her washing and once given Beatrix a heel of honey-bread over the fence and said, *You've got dead men's hands, girl, you know that? You touch things and they remember.*

The heat hit her at the connecting door. The back of Marta's shop was not yet burning—the inquisitors had set the fire at the front and were letting it eat its way inward, patient as accounting. Beatrix put her shoulder to the door and it gave, and she was inside, in Marta's kitchen, where a kettle still sat on a cold stove and a cat shot past her ankles and out into the night, wiser than any of them.

"Hello?" Her voice cracked. "Marta?"

"Up here." Not Marta. A child's voice, hoarse with smoke. "I'm up here. The stairs are hot."

The back stairs. Beatrix took them two at a time with her cloak over her head, and the wood scorched through her boots, and on the landing above, curled into the corner of a narrow bedroom like a bird sheltering from rain, sat a girl of maybe seven or eight, soot-striped, enormous-eyed, holding a wooden doll with no face.

"The window," Beatrix gasped. "Can you—the window—"

"I asked the River Mother to hold the fire," the girl said, with the grave precision of someone reporting on a chore. "She's holding it. But she's tired." The child looked at Beatrix then, and Beatrix felt the look like a hand on her sternum. "You're the bone lady. Marta said if anything ever happened, run to the bone lady. But the bone lady came here instead."

"I—" Beatrix's throat closed. *Run to the bone lady.* Marta had known. Of course Marta had known; hedge-witches recognized each other the way dogs recognize dogs. And Beatrix had spent ten years pretending otherwise, nodding politely over the fence, keeping her gift folded up small as a coin purse, and Marta had never once said a word, had just quietly, in the way of neighbors, made herself a shield around Beatrix's secret without ever asking for one in return.

And now Marta was wired to a post in the street.

"Come here," Beatrix said. "Arms around my neck. Don't let go."

The girl weighed almost nothing. Beatrix got them both to the window, kicked the shutter wide, and looked down at the drop into the alley—twelve feet, maybe fifteen, onto packed earth and Marta's wash-line post. Behind her the floorboards were beginning to tick and groan. Below, faintly, she could hear the inquisitor with the ledger speaking in his flat administrative voice: *"...structure condemned, residue confirmed, subject detained for trial..."*

Trial. They both knew what trials meant now. Trials meant ash.

"Hold tight," Beatrix said, and dropped them both.

She landed badly, rolled, felt something tear in her shoulder and did not care. The girl was still clamped to her, doll and all, breathing hard against her neck. Beatrix ran—down the alley, between the drying sheds, out past the cooper's yard—with the firelight sliding across the walls beside her and the tide-pressure in her ears swelling, swelling, and she understood distantly that the child was praying again, silently now, lips moving against Beatrix's collarbone, and that somewhere a god who had been silent for ten years was clearing a path through the dark for them, moving obstacles she never quite saw move.

* * *

Anselm Oduya found them at the shrine.

It was a nothing-shrine—one of the hundreds that had dotted Vellamare before the Ban, a niche in the harbor wall where fishermen used to leave copper coins for the Tide-Father, bricked over a decade ago and forgotten. Beatrix had gone there because it was the only place she could think of where no one would look, and because the child had been shivering and praying under her breath the whole way, and Beatrix had begun to understand that the prayers weren't comfort. They were weather. Things happened near this child. Doors unlocked. Dogs lost their aggression mid-snarl. Once, a Warden patrol had turned down a different street for no reason any of them could see, and Beatrix had felt the air go soft and strange around them like the hush before snow.

She had the girl wedged in the niche behind the loose brick, wrapped in the wool cloak, feeding her the last of the honey-bread Beatrix had bought from a night-stall with shaking hands, when footsteps came down the harbor stairs—quick, uneven, the steps of a man who walked fast to outrun his own thoughts.

"Don't," Beatrix said, rising, putting herself between the niche and the stairs. "Whatever you are—Warden, informer—I'll scream the whole quay awake."

"It's Anselm." He stopped, hands raised, and the moon caught his face—that long, exhausted, honest face she'd seen three nights ago in her shop, when he'd come in off a dying woman's prayer and told her the inquisitors were coming, told her *they have a list, Zhou, and your name has been on it longer than mine.* He was carrying a satchel and wearing a priest's coat with the collar torn off, which was how all disgraced clergy dressed now: stripped, but not yet disguised. "I went to your shop. The door was open. There was fire across the lane and a crowd watching a woman burn at a post, and I thought—" His jaw tightened. "Is it Marta? The herb-wife?"

"They're waiting for dawn." Beatrix heard her own voice come out flat and strange. "Trials start at dawn. That's the custom. They want an audience."

Anselm closed his eyes. When he opened them, he looked older. "Then we have until dawn."

"We?" Beatrix laughed, and it came out ugly. "No. There is no we. There is me, and there is a child hiding behind a brick, and there is you, who spent nine years as the Inquisition's own confessor before they threw you out for—what was it, mercy? And I am going to take that child east on the morning barge and disappear, because disappearing is the one skill I have actually practiced, Father, for ten years, every single day—"

"Mama?"

The small voice came from the niche. Beatrix froze. Anselm went very still.

The girl climbed out from behind the brick, dragging the cloak, and looked up at Anselm with that grave, unblinking stare, and said, "Are you a god? You walk like the pictures of the Tide-Father. All sorry and tall."

"No, little one." Anselm crouched. Up close, Beatrix could see how much the question cost him—how the word *god* landed on him like a stone on wet cloth. "I'm a priest. Or I was. Which means I'm a man who used to carry messages upward, and lately..." He exhaled. "Lately the messages have started coming back."

"That's what Marta said." The girl nodded seriously. "That the gods woke up. That's why they came for her. She prayed too loud." The child's chin trembled, just once, and then steadied in a way that frightened Beatrix more than tears would have. "My name's Wren. Marta wasn't my mama really. But she said she was."

"Wren." Beatrix crouched too, so they were eye to eye, three of them folded together in the harbor dark like a small congregation. "Listen to me. What happened tonight—did you pray? Did you ask the River Mother to help Marta?"

Wren's face did something complicated. "I asked her to hold the fire. I didn't ask her to stop the grey men. I should have asked for that first." She said it like a lesson learned, like a child reciting arithmetic, and Beatrix felt something break open in her chest—not grief exactly, though grief lived there, but the thing underneath grief, the thing that had been sitting in her for ten years wearing grief's clothes.

Guilt. Complicity. The specific weight of every window she had ever watched from.

Because here was the truth she had built her whole small careful life on top of, like a shop built over a sinkhole: she had *heard the dead*. For ten years. While the Inquisition burned hedge-witches and unlicensed charm-cutters and one entire family of tide-speakers down in the Saltrow, Beatrix Zhou had sat in her licensed shop with her golden-painted sign and her permitted buttons and her permitted dice, hearing the murdered whisper through her own two hands, and she had said *nothing*. Had carved nothing true. Had stayed small. Had called it survival, had called it wisdom, had called it staying alive for some future that never required anything of her except continued smallness.

And Marta had covered for her anyway. Marta, who prayed too loud, who left offerings on the old feast-days even after the feast-days were crimes, who had hung washing over the fence and slipped honey-bread through and said *run to the bone lady* —Marta had spent her courage protecting a woman who was hoarding hers.

Dawn was in four hours. Marta was wired to a post. And Beatrix could take the morning barge, and disappear, and be small forever, and know—to the exact grain—what her smallness had purchased.

Or.

"Wren," Beatrix said. "When you pray, and the gods listen—can you feel where they listen *from*? Can you tell?"

The girl frowned, concentrating. "It's like... warm spots. Like standing near lamps. There's lots of warm spots in the city tonight. More than yesterday. They're waking up all over." She looked at the bricked-over niche beside her, and reached out, and laid her small palm flat against the old stone. "This one's barely warm. Poor thing. Nobody's fed it in years."

Anselm made a sound low in his throat. "Ten years of the Ban," he said softly, "and the shrines starved. And now—" He looked at Beatrix, and there was something in his face she hadn't seen there before: not pity, not the condescension of the formerly righteous, but recognition. Two people who had broken the same law in opposite directions—he from above, enforcing; she from below, enduring—and met, finally, in the wreckage between. "Zhou. Tell me what you're thinking. All of it."

"All of it is a bad idea."

"Those are usually the right ones."

Beatrix looked down at Wren, at the soot on her cheeks and the faceless doll clutched against her chest, and then out at the black water of the harbor, where the Tide-Father's bricked-over niche sat cold and hungry in the wall, and she thought about warm spots waking all over the city like lamps being lit one by one by unseen hands.

"Marta taught me one true thing over that fence, years ago," Beatrix said slowly. "She said the old ways don't die in a city. They just go underground and wait. Like seeds." Her heart was hammering. Her shoulder throbbed where she'd landed. Somewhere across the city, a woman was tied to a post with wire, waiting for dawn, and Beatrix found that her mouth was already forming words she could not call back. "The Inquisition thinks fire makes people small. It doesn't. It makes smoke, and smoke goes everywhere, and every gifted soul in Vellamare is awake tonight, smelling it."

"You want to make noise," Anselm said.

"I want to make *witness*." The word came out of her like something carved, like it had been waiting in the bone of her for ten years. "At dawn, when they bring her to the trial-ground, I'm going to stand in the crowd, and I'm going to do the one thing they can't forbid, because they don't believe it exists anymore. I'm going to listen. To her. To whoever they've burned before her. And whatever the dead say, I'm going to say it louder."

Anselm stared at her. And then—slowly, terribly, wonderfully—he began to smile, the smile of a man setting down a weight he had carried so long he'd mistaken it for his spine.

"The trial-ground," he said. "Where the whole city can hear."

"Father," said Wren gravely, patting his knee, "the warm spot just got warmer."

Above the harbor, far off across the rooftops, the glow of Marta's burning shop still stained the sky—and Beatrix understood, watching it, that the fire had crossed the lane already, in a way the inquisitors with their ledgers would never measure. It had crossed into her. Whatever small careful creature had lived behind that golden-painted sign was ash now too, and what remained was something larger and far more dangerous:

A witness, with four hours until dawn, and nothing left to lose but the silence itself.
