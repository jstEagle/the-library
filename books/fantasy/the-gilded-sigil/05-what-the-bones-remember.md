## 5. What the Bones Remember

They walked out of Vellamare with the smoke still rising behind them.

Beatrix kept Wren's hand in hers and did not let herself look back at the burning quarter, because looking back was a luxury and luxuries got people killed. Anselm walked ahead with his priest's collar torn off and stuffed into his pack, dressed now in a dyer's smock he'd stolen from a line behind the tanneries, and he moved like a man who had spent years memorizing which shadows were safe. The three of them looked like nothing. A widow, an orphan, a laborer. That was the trick of survival she had learned over ten quiet years: be nothing, want nothing, carve your charms in the dark and burn the shavings.

Except the shavings didn't burn anymore. Except her whole satchel of bone charms hummed against her hip like a hive that had woken angry, and every mile they put between themselves and the city made it louder, not quieter.

"Your bag is singing," Wren said for the fourth time since dawn.

"Bones creak," Beatrix said. "It's nothing."

"It's not nothing." Wren said it without argument, the flat way children state facts, and Beatrix's chest tightened. The girl was seven years old and had watched a hedge-witch burn on her own doorstep two nights ago, and she did not cry about it. She just stated things. *The fire was green at the edges. The witch prayed and something answered. Your bag is singing.*

Anselm glanced back at her. "There's a place," he said. "Old road shrine, maybe. Or—"

"I know where we're going," Beatrix said.

She hadn't known until she said it. But her feet knew, and so did the bones in her satchel, pulling at her hip like a compass needle finding north. East along the cliff road, past the salt farms, to where the land folded down into a hollow full of wind-scoured grass. There had been a village there once. She'd passed through it as an apprentice, twenty years ago, following Sefa from town to town while Sefa taught her which bones held voices and which held only silence—

The thought of Sefa caught in her throat as it always did, a fishhook of grief, and she swallowed past it and kept walking.

* * *

The charnel house stood in the hollow like a tooth left standing in a rotted jaw.

It was older than the Ban, older than the kingdom, maybe. A low round building of black stone, its roof long collapsed into a crown of ferns, its doorway a mouth without a door. The village around it had died centuries ago; only foundation-stones remained, soft green mounds in the grass, and sheep grazed among them with the placid indifference of animals who had never been told what the building was for.

"They used to bring their dead here," Beatrix said. Her voice came out hushed, though there was no one to disturb. "Before the cities grew. Before there were priests to do the grieving for you. You carried your mother's bones here yourself and laid them on the shelves, and you came back every year to talk to them."

"You've been here before?" Anselm asked.

"Once. With my teacher." She stopped at the threshold. The humming in her satchel had become a chord, low and insistent, and beneath it—if she tilted her head, if she listened the way she'd been taught to listen—there was something else. Something vast and patient underneath the sound, the way the sea is underneath the sound of one wave.

Wren peered past her into the dark. "It's full of dead people."

"Yes."

"Good," said Wren. "Maybe they know what's happening."

Children, Beatrix thought, should not find comfort in charnel houses. But then, children should not have prayers answered literally by gods who had been silent for a decade either. She took Wren's hand again and stepped inside.

The interior was cooler than the day deserved. The shelves still stood, most of them, cut into the walls in tiers, and on them lay the bones of the old dead—not skeletons, exactly, but arrangements: femurs crossed over ribs, skulls set facing outward, small bones laid in patterns Beatrix half-remembered meaning *beloved* or *forgiven* or *come back soon*. Centuries of dust lay over everything like grey snow. In places the dust had been disturbed—badgers, probably, or rain through the broken roof—but the bones themselves endured, patient as geology.

Anselm stood in the doorway and did not come in. "I should keep watch," he said.

"You're afraid of it," Beatrix said. Not cruelly. She understood. He had spent his career enforcing a faith that taught the dead belonged to the Silent Order's keeping, that bones outside consecrated ground were contamination. Old doctrine dies hard, even after the doctrine has been burned out of you.

"I'm afraid of what I've done," Anselm said quietly. "Places like this make it loud." And he turned away and climbed to the top of the hollow to watch the road, and Beatrix let him go, because some penances could only be paid alone.

She set Wren in the middle of the floor with bread and dried apples and told her to eat slowly and touch nothing, and then she unslung her satchel and knelt, and began to listen.

* * *

Her charms were screaming. There was no other word for it. The little carved vertebrae and finger-bones, each one holding a whisper of some dead client's beloved, had all begun to speak at once—not the usual murmurs, the fond repetitions of the dead (*tell her the blue coat, tell her I said the blue coat*), but urgent, overlapping, terrified speech. She pulled them out one at a time and held them to her ear like a child with seashells.

"—the towers first, they take the towers—"

"—she wore my face, I watched her wear my face in the mirror—"

"—no one remembers my daughter's name but me and I'm going and I'm going—"

The third charm was an old woman's hip-bone she'd carved eight years ago, holding the voice of the woman's drowned son. The son had never spoken of anything but fishing boats. Now his voice came thin and frantic: *the king is dead, the king has been dead for years, we all saw it happen and none of us remember seeing it—*

Beatrix sat back on her heels. Her hands were shaking.

Because that was the thing she'd been carrying for three days now, since the night the gods woke and the sky over Vellamare had flickered like a guttering lamp: fragments. Scraps. Every charm she owned, every dead voice, circling the same absence. The king. The old king, Aldric the Fourth, whose face was on the coins and whose edicts had outlawed magic ten years ago—the Ban itself had come from him, signed in his own hand. Except the dead remembered things the living did not, and the dead remembered that the edict-signing hand had already gone wrong. The dead remembered a hunting trip. The dead remembered a fever, or something shaped like a fever. And then nothing, a hole, a smooth place in the record where seven years of reign should have been.

And today, here, surrounded by bones older than the kingdom, she felt the hole itself. Felt it the way you feel a missing tooth with your tongue.

"Wren," she said. "Don't wander off."

But Wren had already wandered. The girl stood at the far shelf tier, on her toes, reaching toward a skull set high up where the wall met what remained of the ceiling—a skull larger than the others, yellowed to the color of old ivory, resting on a folded arrangement of bones that Beatrix's trained eye read as honors. A king's burial? Out here, in a village charnel house?

"Don't—" Beatrix started.

Wren touched it.

The world went white and then very, very clear.

* * *

Later, Beatrix would try to describe what happened next and fail, because hearing the dead through bone was like listening through a door, and this was the door being taken off its hinges.

She heard a man's voice. Not a whisper, not the faded echo she knew—a voice with weight and warmth and tiredness in it, a living-sounding voice coming out of a skull that had been dry dust for six hundred years.

*Ah,* the voice said. *At last. At last, someone comes in through the front instead of digging down.*

Beatrix's knees hit the dusty floor. She hadn't meant to kneel. "Who are you?"

*A king. Once. They brought me here secretly, after—well. After.* A pause, and in the pause something ancient shifted, like a mountain settling. *You hear me. Ten years the gods have been strangled and the dead have been shouting into wool, and now the strangling is loosening, and the first one who hears me is a bone-carver in a stolen smock. The gods have a sense of humor. I never appreciated that when I was alive.*

"What do you want?" Her mouth was dry. Around her, every bone on every shelf had begun to glow faintly, a phosphorescence like moonlight underwater, and Wren was watching with wide, unafraid eyes, and somewhere above Anselm was calling down, "Beatrix? Beatrix, what's happening?"

*Want?* The laugh had rust in it. *I want to tell you how I died. No one else can tell you. Everyone who saw is dead, and everyone who remembers has been made to forget, and that is the trick of it, girl, that is the whole filthy trick.*

"The king," she whispered. "Aldric—"

*I am not Aldric.* Impatience now, sharp as a blade drawn fast. *Aldric is the puppet on the throne now, or rather the shape wearing Aldric. I am the one before him. The one whose murder they built the Ban on top of, like paving stones over a grave. Listen. I will only say this cleanly once, and then I will say it however many times it takes, because the dead have nothing but time and you people have almost none left.*

She pressed both hands flat against the cold floor. The glowing bones pulsed in rhythm, like a heartbeat.

*I ruled well. Remember that—I insist on it, vanity is the last thing they let you keep. And in my twentieth year a gift came to court. A crown. Beautiful beyond reason, worked gold and something darker under the gold, sent by no nation anyone could name, given by an ambassador who smiled too much. My wise men warned me. My wife begged me to melt it down. I wore it, because kings wear crowns, it is the entire occupation.* The voice dropped low. *It does not devour you at once. That is the mercy and the horror of it. It eats a memory a day. Small ones first. The name of a servant. The taste of a fruit you loved. You wake each morning slightly less yourself and slightly more obedient, and the thing wearing your face grows more convincing as you grow less real. By the end I watched it sit at my own table in my own body and sign decrees with my own hand, and I screamed, and no one heard, because everyone who might have heard had already forgotten I existed.*

The floor seemed to tilt. Beatrix gripped the stone.

*It wears them one after another. King to king, crown to crown. It ate me, and when I was hollow it passed to my heir, and then to his heir, and each time it grew stronger and each transition was arranged to look like fever, like accident, like grief-madness. Ten years ago it finished eating Aldric's soul, and then it did the cleverest thing it ever did. It outlawed magic. Do you understand why?*

Beatrix did understand, suddenly, horribly. "Magic is memory. The old rites. The gods—they hold what happened. They witness."

*The gods are witnesses,* the king's voice said, and every bone in the room blazed. *Prayer is testimony. It could not eat what the gods remembered, so it starved the gods. Cut the prayers, silence the shrines, kill the listeners—and the world forgot the theft even as it was committed. Ten years of forgetting. And now the gods are waking, the strangling is failing, and the thing on the throne is frightened for the first time in six hundred years. That is why the inquisitors burn hedge-witches, girl. Not because a hedge-witch matters. Because every answered prayer is a loose thread, and it is afraid of unraveling.*

Wren spoke up, small and clear in the blazing dark: "How do we kill it?"

*Kill it?* The voice softened. *You don't kill it, child. You unmake it. The true king's soul—it keeps the souls it eats, chained inside the gold, fuel for the wearing. Free the souls, and the crown is only metal. But the unbinding must be done by a living hand that can hear the bound dead, in the place where the anchor lies, and it will cost—* The voice faltered. For the first time it sounded afraid. *It will cost more than I can see from here. I am sorry. The dead always know less than we pretend.*

Silence, except for the wind moving through the broken roof and the distant bleat of sheep. Beatrix realized her face was wet. She didn't remember starting to cry.

Above them, boots on stone—Anselm sliding down into the hollow, breathless. "Riders on the coast road. Four of them. Grey cloaks." His eyes went from the glowing bones to Beatrix's face and widened. "Gods' blood. What did it tell you?"

Beatrix rose. Her legs felt like someone else's, but her hands had gone steady—the way they did at the carving bench, the way they did whenever everything else fell apart and only the work remained. She looked down at the ancient skull, at the king who had waited six hundred years in the dark for someone to listen.

"It told me why the dead have been waiting," she said. "All of them. Every charm, every whisper, every voice I've ever heard through bone." She picked up her satchel and slung it across her shoulder, and the humming inside it had changed—no longer fear now, but something with direction, something like an army turning to face a battle. "They haven't been haunting us, Anselm. They've been recruiting. And the riders are four hours behind us, so we'd better give them something worth chasing."

She took Wren's hand, and the three of them climbed out of the hollow together, eastward, toward the capital and the crown that ate.

Behind them, in the dark of the charnel house, every bone on every shelf glowed a moment longer—like watch-fires answering, one by one, down the length of the world.
