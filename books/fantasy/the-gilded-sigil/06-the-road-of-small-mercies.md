## 6. The Road of Small Mercies

Anselm Oduya had spent eleven years learning how to be small, and the road east was teaching him the opposite lesson: that smallness, done properly, required more courage than any grandeur he had ever attempted in his vestments.

They traveled by night and slept by day, tucked into hedgerows and abandoned shepherd's huts, moving through a countryside that seemed to hold its breath. The Ban had not lifted so much as thinned, like ice on a spring pond—solid enough to walk on, thin enough to hear the water underneath. And under it, everywhere, the water moved. Anselm felt it now in ways he never had before. Prayers rose from farmhouses they passed like woodsmoke, and sometimes—more often each day—something answered.

He watched Wren feel it too.

The girl walked with her chin tipped up as though listening to weather only she could see. She was seven years old, maybe eight; she didn't know herself, and Beatrix had stopped asking. What Wren knew was that when she wished for things, the world sometimes obliged, and that this was not always kind. In Vellamare she had prayed for rain and drowned a neighbor's garden. She had prayed for her mother to stop crying, and her mother had stopped—everything—stopped entirely, and Wren had run before anyone could ask what happened.

So Anselm taught her what he had once taught novices, back when teaching prayer was legal and he still believed the Church's reasons for outlawing everything else.

"Prayer is a door," he said, crouched beside a cold fire on their third morning out, drawing shapes in ash with a stick. "You don't fling it open. You knock first. You say who you are."

"What if I don't know who I am?"

"Then you say that." He smiled. "'It's me. I don't know my name yet.' The gods have heard worse openings. I've given some of them worse ones myself."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "And then I ask for something *small*?"

"Small," Anselm confirmed. "Never 'save me.' Never 'fix everything.' You ask for a dry sock. A safe mile. A bird to look at. Small mercies, little doors. Big prayers are for people who know what they're asking for, and no one knows that, ever."

Beatrix sat sharpening her carving knives against a whetstone, pretending not to listen, which was how she participated in most conversations. But when Wren bowed her head over the ash-drawn door and whispered—*it's me, I don't know my name yet, please let today be a boring day*—Anselm saw the carver's hands go still.

That afternoon it rained, gently, for exactly long enough to fill their water skins, and then stopped. A pheasant wandered out of the bracken and stood regarding them with idiotic calm until Wren had laughed at it twice.

"Boring," Beatrix said, and there was something in her voice Anselm couldn't name. Something almost like grief, or its opposite.

* * *

The patrols found the roads before the roads found the capital.

They were inquisitors' men mostly—black-liveried, riding in pairs, checking papers at crossroads with a thoroughness that spoke of new orders from somewhere far away. Anselm learned to read them at distance: the way they rode too close together, nervous; the way they'd begun carrying iron censers that smoked without fire. Memory-burners. He had helped design those, in another life, in a white office above a white courtyard where men had wept and begged and been made smooth and empty anyway.

He did not tell Beatrix that part yet. He told himself he would, eventually, when the road was kinder. He suspected the road would never be kinder, and that he was a coward wearing a penitent's gray like a costume that had grown into his skin.

Instead he taught Wren the practical liturgies of hiding.

"When they stop us," he murmured, walking her through it in an orchard gone wild and unpruned since the Ban, "you are my daughter. Your mother died of fever. We travel east because your uncle has an orchard."

"I don't want a fake dead mother," Wren said, scandalized.

"It's not fake. It's a story, and stories are true in the way that matters—they keep people alive. Besides," he added, and here he knelt so their eyes were level, "your real mother isn't gone. Not really. Nothing that loved you is ever entirely gone. That's rather the whole point of everything we're doing."

Wren studied him with those eyes that sometimes caught light wrong, that sometimes held a depth no child's eyes should hold. Then she reached up and patted his cheek twice, the way one pats a large, slow animal.

"You're sad," she announced. "All the time. Underneath."

"Yes."

"Is it because of the white office?"

Anselm went very still. He had never spoken of the white office aloud. He had never described it to anyone, had barely allowed himself to remember it—

"You think about it loud," Wren explained, apologetic. "Like shouting. Even when your face is quiet."

He should have been afraid. A year ago he would have been—a god-touched child reading his mind was exactly the sort of thing he'd once burned people for. Instead he found that some knot in his chest loosened, just slightly, at being known.

"Yes," he said. "It's because of the white office. One day I'll tell you about it. Both of you."

"Okay." She patted his cheek again. "But not during dinner. Dinner should be nice."

* * *

Dinner, that night, was eels.

Beatrix had rigged lines in a stream while they waited out a patrol, with the patience of someone who had survived ten illegal years by never wasting waiting. She cooked them over green sticks with wild garlic and a handful of grain meal pressed into flatcakes, and it was, objectively, the finest meal Anselm had eaten since his fall—or perhaps hunger had simply become his best chef.

Wren ate three eels and a flatcake and then, mid-chew, began to laugh.

It started as a hiccup. Then her whole body caught fire with it—she doubled over, wheezing, pointing at Anselm, who had garlic leaf stuck to his chin and did not know it. She laughed until she cried, until she fell sideways against Beatrix, until the sound rang off the orchard trees and up into the darkening sky where, Anselm would have sworn on any relic you cared to name, something listened and was glad.

He realized he was laughing too. He could not remember the last time. It hurt his face, like using a muscle long atrophied.

Beatrix wasn't laughing. She was watching Wren with an expression Anselm had seen once before, years ago, on the face of a woman holding a candle in a windstorm—fierce, terrified tenderness, the look of someone guarding a flame with her whole body.

"She hasn't laughed before," Beatrix said quietly, when Wren finally collapsed into sleep between them, boneless as a kitten. "Not once, all the time I've had her. I thought—" She turned the whetstone over in her fingers. "I thought maybe the gods had taken it out of her. Taken things out of her, when they answered. Paid themselves."

"And now?"

"Now I think she was just waiting to feel safe." Beatrix looked at him across the embers. "Which means we did that. Which means—" She stopped, jaw working, unaccustomed to the shape of the sentence. "Thank you. For coming to my shop. For being useless in useful ways."

Anselm bowed his head. "I've had practice at uselessness. Eleven years of it."

"That's not what I meant."

"No," he agreed softly. "I know."

* * *

It was Beatrix who taught him to hear the dead.

She began without announcing it, the way she did everything. They were sheltering in the ruins of a tollhouse, and she pressed a small carved thing into his hand—a charm of sheep bone, worn smooth, shaped like nothing in particular and everything at once.

"Hold it. Don't pray. Just listen."

"I don't—"

"You've spent your whole life talking at the divine. Talking is easy. Listening is the work." Her eyes, in the low light, were the color of river stones. "Close your mouth. Open whatever's behind your sternum. The dead don't speak in words, not usually. They speak in *want*. In unfinished things. Listen for the unfinished."

He held the bone. He closed his mouth. He opened whatever was behind his sternum, feeling foolish, feeling like a novice again, feeling—for the first time in eleven years—not ashamed of it.

At first: nothing. His own heartbeat. Wren's breathing. Wind in the broken roof.

Then, faint as a finger drawn through dust: *cold. So cold. Where did everyone go? Why did everyone go?*

Anselm's eyes flew open. "There's someone—there was a toll-keeper, wasn't there? Someone died here."

"Eleven years ago. When the Ban came down and the soldiers cleared the roads." Beatrix watched him with an expression he was beginning to recognize as approval, though she wore it like an ill-fitting coat. "What do they want?"

"To know why everyone left." His voice cracked on it. "They're cold, and they don't understand why everyone left."

"They never do." She took the charm back gently, cradling it. "That's most of what the dead say, if you listen long enough. *Why did everyone leave.* Ten years I've heard it, and ten years I could do nothing but carve it into bone so it had somewhere to live besides me." She looked up. "That changes now. If the gods are answering, then the dead can be answered too. That's the work, priest. Not the king, not the crown, not the war. This. Making sure no one else dies wondering why everyone left."

Anselm found he could not speak. He nodded instead, and in the silence that followed, the toll-keeper's cold faded from the charm like breath from glass, and Wren slept on, and somewhere far to the east a hollow thing wearing a crown fed patiently on the memory of the world.

* * *

They made a ritual of the small hours, the three of them.

Wren would knock her little ash-door and ask for small things, and the world would oblige in its strange literal way—a hedgehog to watch, a mushroom ring to dance around (which Beatrix forbade, firmly), one cloud shaped like a horse. Anselm taught her the old responses, the call-and-answer of a faith that had driven him out and that he loved anyway, and discovered that in her mouth the ancient words sounded less like doctrine and more like skipping rope.

And Beatrix—Beatrix began, slowly, grudgingly, to teach him the names of bones.

"Femur carries the longest memories. Ribs hold breath—last words, mostly. Skulls are greedy; they keep everything and give up nothing. Finger bones gossip." She handed him a hawk's wing joint one evening, delicate as porcelain. "Birds remember flying. Always. Even caged ones. Especially caged ones."

He held it and listened, and heard—yes—wind. Enormous, unhurried wind, and beneath it a longing so clean it made his throat ache. *Up. Up. Up.*

"The Ban took flight from that bird twice," Beatrix said. "Once when it was caged, once when it died. But the memory stayed. That's what I'm telling you, Anselm. They can burn every book and empty every shrine, but the remembering itself—the dead keep it. All of it. We're carrying a library inside a graveyard, and nobody thought to burn *that*."

He looked at her—at this woman who had stayed small for ten years, carving whispers into bone in a hidden shop, keeping faith alone in the dark—and understood suddenly that she was the bravest person he had ever met. Braver than martyrs. Martyrs got to stop. She had kept going.

"Wren asked me about the white office," he said. It came out before he could stop it. "She reads me. Says I'm loud even when I'm quiet."

Beatrix said nothing, but she turned the whetstone over, and waited, the way you wait for someone crossing ice.

"In the early days of the Ban, there was a facility. White rooms. People who'd been reported for magic were brought there, and we—" his hands had begun to shake; he watched them as though they belonged to a stranger, "—we used memory-burners. Censers. The theory was mercy. Better to forget the magic than be executed for it. We told ourselves that. I told myself that, for six years, and then I saw a man after his fourth burning, and he looked at his wife, and he didn't know her, and he *smiled*, because there was nothing left in him that knew what he'd lost, and I understood that we hadn't been merciful. We'd been hungry. We'd been doing, in small white rooms, exactly what that thing in the crown does to the whole world."

The fire cracked. Wren slept. Somewhere an owl called and was not answered.

"Why are you telling me this now?" Beatrix asked. Not cruelly. Genuinely asking.

"Because you deserve to know what's walking beside you. Because Wren deserves to hear it from me and not from a nightmare. And because—" he laughed, without much humor, "—because I've been practicing saying it. Out loud. To someone. The road seemed as good a place as any. The road is where I've been forgiven everything already, by two people who had every right not to."

Beatrix was quiet a long moment. Then she reached over and took the hawk bone from his hand, and set it down, and put her rough carver's hand briefly, heavily, on top of his shaking ones.

"The dead forgive faster than the living," she said. "I've asked enough of them to know. But the living—" she squeezed, once, "—the living get to try. That's the deal. You try. Every day. That's the whole rite."

"That's not in any scripture I studied."

"No," she said, and the ghost of a smile crossed her face. "Scripture was written by people who talked. This is listening-work. Different curriculum."

* * *

On the ninth night, the road gave them a gift and a warning in the same breath.

They had taken shelter in a hay barn, and Wren had knocked her ash-door and asked—as instructed—for something small: *please let tomorrow be a boring day.* And the night had settled around them, warm and safe, and Anselm had drifted toward sleep believing, for one whole hour, that the world might permit them gentleness.

Then Wren sat bolt upright in the dark.

"There's a man," she whispered. "In the road. He's cold and he doesn't know why everyone left, and he says—" her voice wavered, "—he says the black riders took his family east. To the capital. He says they're collecting people who *remember things*."

Beatrix was already awake, already reaching for her knives. "Collecting."

"He says the crown is hungry," Wren whispered, "and the crown has learned to like the taste of people who remember magic best."

Anselm lay in the hay and felt the words settle over him like snowfall—quiet, and cold, and burying. Eastward, then. Toward the capital, toward the hollow court, toward the thing that ate. And behind them, somewhere on the dark road, the inquisitors were not merely hunting runaways anymore.

They were harvesting.

Beatrix struck a light and cupped it over a fresh-cut piece of bone, her face half gold, half shadow, already carving.

"Then we walk faster," she said, "and we listen harder. Because if the crown wants the rememberers—" the knife whispered through the bone, "—then every soul it's collected is a witness we haven't heard yet. And I intend to hear all of them."

Far to the east, past sleeping farms and silent shrines, the towers of the capital stood against the stars—and Anselm, watching Beatrix carve by firelight, understood that they were no longer fleeing anything at all.

They were being summoned.
