## 7. The Mentor's Ghost

The dead had always come to Beatrix in fragments—a word caught in the grain of a knucklebone, a sigh pressed into the hollow of a skull, a name repeated over and over like water dripping into a stone cup until the stone gave way. Ten years of listening had taught her that death was not silence but erosion: the dead spoke less and less as they faded, their voices wearing thin against the walls of whatever held them.

So when the voice came to her whole—unbroken, impatient, *loud*—she dropped the bowl of washwater she'd been carrying and stood in the middle of the charnel house road with water soaking her boots and her heart hammering like a fist on a door.

*"You're standing in a puddle, girl."*

Beatrix did not turn around. She couldn't. Turning around would make it real, and if it was real, then it could end again, and she had already survived one ending and did not think she had it in her to survive a second.

"Say it again," she whispered.

*"You're standing in a puddle. You've been standing in puddles since you were six years old and I told you a hundred times to lift your hem. Some things never change, and apparently one of them is your talent for ignoring good advice."*

The voice was dry as old paper and warm as a hearthstone both at once, and Beatrix knew it the way she knew her own hands—the way she knew the particular rasp of it when Sefa Illanev was tired, the way it sharpened on the second syllable of *girl* when she was fond and trying not to show it.

She turned.

The ghost of Sefa Illanev stood in the middle of the road in the grey hour before dawn, and she was more solid than any shade Beatrix had ever heard through bone. Not flesh—not quite—but present, definite, outlined against the paling sky like ink on wet paper. She wore the same patched traveling coat she'd died in, seven years ago, in a room that smelled of tallow and blood, with Beatrix's hand clenched in hers. Her silver hair was braided back the way she'd always worn it for work. Her arms were crossed, which is what they did when she was about to say something Beatrix wouldn't want to hear.

"You're dead," Beatrix said. It came out stupidly, and she hated herself for it.

*"Astute as ever. Ten years carving bones and you still lead with the obvious."* But Sefa's eyes—grey, sharp, alive in a way that made Beatrix's chest ache—softened as they moved over her face. *"Look at you. Thinner. Tired. Hiding in a charnel house with a defrocked priest and a child who smells like lightning. I leave you alone for seven years and this is what you do with yourself."*

"I didn't—" Beatrix's voice cracked. She pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "I didn't do anything. That was the point. I stayed small. I stayed quiet. I did everything you told me."

*"Yes,"* Sefa said, and there was something underneath the dryness now, something that sounded almost like sorrow. *"You did. And look how well that kept you safe."*

* * *

They sat inside the charnel house because Wren was asleep on a pallet of old vestments and Anselm was keeping watch by the door, though Beatrix noticed he'd positioned himself where he could see them both, and that his hand rested near his iron pendant the way a man rests his hand near a weapon he no longer believes in.

Sefa could not sit—or rather, she could, but she chose to stand, drifting a half-inch above the floor, pacing the length of the room in slow passes while Beatrix told her everything. The glowing charm. The gods answering. The king's voice in the ancient bone, describing his own murder. The thing wearing his crown, feeding on memory. The road east. Harrowgate. The council that would meet within the week to decide whether the free cities would fight or kneel.

When she finished, the charnel house was very quiet. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the bone-niches and made a sound like a thousand small mouths breathing.

*"No,"* Sefa said.

Beatrix blinked. "No?"

*"No. You are not taking a child to the capital to unbind a soul from a devouring god-thing while armies march. No. Absolutely not. Have you lost what little sense you were born with?"*

"Sefa—"

*"Don't 'Sefa' me. I taught you better than this. I taught you to survive. I spent twenty years teaching you how to be invisible, how to be nothing, how to be so small the world forgets you're there—and the first time the sky cracks open and the gods start shouting, you decide to march on the palace?"* The ghost's form flickered, edges blurring with something that might have been anger or might have been fear; with Sefa, they had always been the same thing. *"You were supposed to live, Beatrix. That was the whole lesson. Everything else was decoration."*

"I know what you taught me." Beatrix rose too, and found she was shaking. "I lived it. Ten years, Sefa. Ten years of being small and quiet and invisible, and do you know what it bought me? A neighbor burned out of her home. A child hunted through the streets. And every charm I ever carved whispering in my ears while I pretended I couldn't hear, because hearing was illegal and helping was worse." Her voice broke and she let it. "You taught me to survive. Fine. I survived. And everyone around me drowned."

Anselm shifted by the door but said nothing. He was learning, Beatrix thought, when to be absent even while present.

Sefa drifted closer. Up close, the ghost's edges shimmered like heat off summer stone, and Beatrix could see the wall behind her through the fall of her hair—and yet her eyes were as solid as anything Beatrix had ever known.

*"And now?"* Sefa asked quietly. *"Now you mean to drown with them?"*

"Now I mean to teach them to swim."

Something passed across the ghost's face—pride, maybe, or its grief-stricken cousin—and then it was gone behind the familiar dry mask. *"Sentimental. You get that from me, unfortunately. There's no cure."*

* * *

Wren woke an hour after sunrise, and Beatrix watched the girl take in the ghost with the untroubled acceptance of a child who had recently prayed a hedge-witch's cottage back from embers and seen a minor god answer.

"You're dead," Wren said, matter-of-fact, chewing on the heel of yesterday's bread.

*"Very,"* Sefa agreed. *"And you're the one who prays like she's throwing stones through windows. I've been listening to you for three days. You woke up two entire river spirits outside Vellamare and didn't even apologize."*

"They didn't seem to mind."

*"They never do. That's the problem with gods—they have terrible boundaries."* But Sefa crouched down to the girl's level, and her voice gentled in a way Beatrix remembered from a lifetime ago, from fevered nights and skinned knees in another city, another life. *"What's your name, little storm?"*

"Wren."

*"Wren. Do you know what you're carrying? What's riding along behind your eyes, waiting for you to call it forward?"*

Wren considered this with grave seriousness. "It's warm," she said finally. "Like sitting near a fire. Sometimes it talks in my sleep and I don't remember what it says."

Sefa looked up at Beatrix over the girl's head, and her expression was complicated—fear and wonder fighting for the same ground. *"A vessel,"* she murmured. *"Or a friend. We'll find out which, won't we, and pray it's the second."*

"She's not a tool," Beatrix said sharply.

*"No,"* Sefa said. *"She's a reason. There's a difference, and I suspect you already know it, or you wouldn't have pulled her out of that fire."*

* * *

They stayed a second night in the charnel house because Sefa insisted on it—insisted with the particular stubbornness of someone who had spent decades winning arguments before her opponent knew they'd started. The roads east were crawling with inquisitors, she said. The gods' return had not gone unnoticed; the Ban's enforcers were frightened, and frightened men with authority burn things. They would travel at night. They would take the pilgrim paths, the ones worn smooth by feet carrying offerings to shrines that hadn't answered in a decade.

"And who made you an expert on roads?" Beatrix asked, only partly to needle her.

*"I've been dead seven years, girl. Where exactly do you think I've been?"* Sefa settled onto the edge of the worktable, translucent hands folded, and for a moment her bravado thinned. *"Everywhere and nowhere. Drifting. Listening to the world get quieter. When the Ban came, the dead went first—it wasn't just magic they outlawed, it was memory. Every charm smashed, every shrine walled up, every voice like mine pushed further out. I used to be able to reach you. Did you know that? In the early years. Little pushes. A dream here, a feeling there. You never heard me."*

"I heard *something*," Beatrix said. "Some nights. I thought it was longing. I thought I was making you up because I couldn't stop grieving."

*"And now?"*

"Now I don't know what's real, and I'm tired of pretending that doesn't terrify me."

Sefa was quiet for a long moment. Outside, dusk gathered in the charnel house doorway like a patient animal. Then she said, softly, without any of her usual armor: *"I'm sorry. For leaving you the way I did. For making you promise to stay small—I thought I was protecting you, and instead I taught you to disappear. That's not a gift, Beatrix. That's just a slower kind of dying."*

"Don't." Beatrix's throat closed. "Don't apologize. If you apologize, it means it's really happening—you're really here—and then you'll go again, and I'll have to lose you twice, and I don't—" She stopped. Breathed. "I can't do it twice."

The ghost reached out, and though her hand passed through Beatrix's like cold smoke, Beatrix felt it anyway—a pressure, a presence, the unmistakable weight of attention. Of being *seen*.

*"Then we won't waste the time we have on goodbyes,"* Sefa said. *"We'll spend it on arguments. I'm very good at those."*

Despite everything, Beatrix laughed—a cracked, wet sound, but a laugh.

* * *

They left before midnight, four figures on the pilgrim road: a woman with a satchel of whispering bones, a priest with his iron pendant turned backward against his chest, a child walking between them holding a hand of each, and a ghost ranging ahead like a lantern only three of them could see.

It was Wren, naturally, who asked the question Beatrix had been circling for two days.

"If the gods are awake again," the girl said, skipping over a rut in the road, "does that mean ghosts can stay? Forever? Now that it's legal?"

Sefa's stride hitched—could a ghost hitch?—and Beatrix saw her look away, toward the dark horizon, before answering.

*"Nothing is forever, little storm. Not crowns, not kingdoms, not ghosts."* She glanced back at Beatrix, and her smile was the old one, the barbed-and-tender one, the one Beatrix had carved into her memory so deeply she sometimes thought she'd never see anything else again. *"But some things last longer than others. And some things come back when they're called properly."*

"The right prayer," Wren said wisely, "said by the right person."

*"Exactly. Which is why we're going to Harrowgate. Because there's going to be a great deal of praying soon, and most of it will be done wrong."*

They walked on through the sleeping countryside, and Beatrix listened to the dead mentor and the living child trade observations about the stars, and she thought: *this is what I was afraid of. Not losing her again. Loving her again.* Grief had been a wound, but hope was an open door, and doors could be walked through by anything—including the thing that waited in the capital, wearing a dead man's face, hungry for exactly this: love, memory, the bright specific weight of a person held close.

Two days later, from a ridge above the river valley, they saw Harrowgate for the first time—its white walls catching the morning sun, its harbor crowded with masts, and beyond it, far to the east, a smudge on the horizon that Anselm studied through a stolen spyglass and did not want to name.

"That's not cloud," he said at last. "That's dust. Marching dust."

Sefa materialized beside him, squinting eastward with the expression of a woman doing arithmetic she didn't like.

*"Well,"* she said. *"I suppose we'd best go convince a room full of cowards to be brave. How hard can that be?"*
