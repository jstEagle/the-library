## 8. The Free Cities Convene

The Council Hall of Harrowgate had been built to make men feel small, and Anselm Oduya remembered, from his years of enforcing the Ban, exactly how it worked. The ceiling arched up into gloom where the banners of nine cities hung like the wings of sleeping birds. The floor was a mosaic of the Concord—the old sigil of the free cities, a circle of joined hands—worn smooth by two centuries of boots. Every echo arrived late and softened, as though the room itself were reluctant to repeat what was said in it.

He had stood in this hall once before, ten years ago, on the other side of the argument. He had been younger then, and certain, and he had helped draft the language of the Ban that now made everything he loved a crime. He had watched from these same benches while priests and magistrates voted to silence the gods, and he had told himself it was mercy—that a world without miracles would be a world without burnings.

He knew better now. He had seen the hedge-witch's shop still smoking in Vellamare. He had carried Wren out of that smoke with her prayers still glowing on her lips like embers she couldn't spit out.

Now he stood before the council again, and the room was full, and no one wanted to hear him.

* * *

They had given him an hour past dawn, wedged between a dispute over harbor tariffs and a petition from the dyers' guild. Nine delegations sat in a horseshoe of worn oak: Vellamare's merchants with their ink-stained fingers; Harrowgate's own magistrates, fat and pale as river fish; the delegates from Saltmarsh who hadn't looked at one another once since arriving; a woman from the hill towns who kept touching a bone charm at her throat and then hiding her hand when she noticed anyone watching. Anselm saw that last gesture and felt something twist in his chest. They all wore their dead close, now. They just didn't know why the dead had started whispering back.

"Master Oduya." The Speaker of the council was a narrow man named Corvane, with a voice like dry paper and eyes that had long ago stopped seeing anything they didn't already believe. "You have asked the floor. You are aware your standing is... complicated."

"I am aware," Anselm said, "that I was defrocked. I am aware I broke the law I once swore to uphold. I stand here anyway, because there is no one else the council will listen to, and because what I have to say will not wait for a cleaner messenger."

Murmurs. Somewhere to his left, a delegate laughed softly, the laugh of a man who has already decided.

Anselm let the murmur die. He had learned, in his years of enforcement, how institutions killed inconvenient truths—not by refuting them but by waiting them out, by letting procedure be a slow soft pillow pressed over a mouth. He would not give them the pillow.

"Three weeks ago," he said, "the inquisitors burned a shop in the Tanners' Row district of Vellamare. Not for magic. For suspicion of association. The woman who kept the shop—a hedge-healer named Maren Ostreva, sixty-one years old, who pulled teeth and brewed fever-tea and had never so much as lit a candle to a forgotten god—was given no trial. Her neighbors were made to watch. One of them was a child."

He did not say Wren's name. He had promised Beatrix he wouldn't. But he let the word *child* sit in the air, and he watched it land on faces around the horseshoe like a stone dropped into still water.

"The crown's armies," he continued, "crossed the Ashen Ford eight days ago. I have spoken with grain merchants who saw the columns. I have spoken with a deserter who walked away from the camp at Sallow Bridge. By every account, the host numbers forty thousand, and it marches east along the King's Road toward the free cities. At its pace, it reaches the borderlands within the month."

"That is a rumor," said Delegate Hesse of Saltmarsh, a round man with rings on every finger. "The crown has not moved against us in ten years. Why would it start now?"

"Because it can finally finish," Anselm said. "You have all heard the stories coming out of the capital. The king who no longer ages. The king who has not been seen to eat or sleep in six years. The king whose edicts arrive written in a hand that changes month to month, as though different fingers hold the pen. You comfort yourselves by calling these rumors. I am telling you they are symptoms. Something wears the crown, gentlemen, and it is hungry, and the Ban you built has spent ten years clearing the table for it."

Silence, of the heavy kind. In the gallery above, ordinary citizens of Harrowgate leaned over the rail—fishwives and clerks and a cluster of students—and Anselm could feel them listening harder than the council was.

"You speak of hunger," Corvane said carefully. "In metaphor."

"No," Anselm said. "I do not."

And here was the precipice. Beatrix had argued, on the road, that he should keep the worst of it back—that councils heard horror and reached for reasons not to believe it. But he had watched hesitation kill people before. He had been the instrument of that hesitation once, carrying writs signed by men who needed another week to deliberate.

"There is a girl traveling with me," he said. "She is nine years old. She is god-touched—born after the Ban, marked by powers she never chose. When she prays, things answer. A well filled for her when she was thirsty. A locked door opened. And three nights ago, frightened by soldiers on the road, she prayed for safety, and the river beside our camp rose four feet in the space of a breath and turned a patrol aside without drowning a single man." He paused. "The gods are answering prayers again. All across the realm. Your own citizens are hearing voices in their charms and finding their candles relighting themselves. You may outlaw it again if you wish. You may vote, today, to double the inquisitors' stipend. But I promise you this: you cannot outlaw what is already happening, and the thing in the capital knows it. That is why it marches. It is coming to stamp out the fire while it is still small enough to stamp."

He let it stand. Let the hall hold it.

It was the hill-town woman who spoke first, quietly, not rising. "My grandmother's charm spoke to me last month. My grandmother has been dead eleven years." She looked around the horseshoe, defiant. "I did not report it. I want that minuted."

"Minuted," Corvane said faintly, and someone wrote it down, and Anselm thought: *there it is. The whole disease of you, in one word.*

* * *

They broke at midday, which meant they accomplished nothing, which was the purpose of breaking at midday. Anselm found the delegation benches emptying and the corridors filling with the low urgent music of committees forming to study the question of whether questions could be studied.

He found Beatrix in the courtyard of the pilgrims' hospice where they'd taken rooms, sitting on the lip of a dry fountain with her knife and a length of sheep bone, carving with the small fierce economy he'd come to think of as her native language. Wren sat beside her, braiding grass and unbraiding it, watching the pigeons with the flat suspicious stare of a child who had learned that cities were places where doors got kicked in.

"Well?" Beatrix asked, without looking up.

"They'll convene formally tomorrow. Today was theater." He sat heavily on the fountain's edge. "Hesse wants to send envoys to the crown. Corvane wants a fact-finding mission to the borderlands, which will take a month to organize and a month to travel and a month to write its report. The Vellamare merchants are split—their eastern trade routes run through crown territory, and half of them are doing arithmetic about their warehouses instead of their children."

"And the other half?"

"The other half are frightened enough to listen. It isn't nothing." He rubbed his eyes. "I stood in that hall ten years ago and helped build the thing that's killing us, and today I stood in it again and begged, and I could see in their faces that most of them were thinking about the cost of grain."

Beatrix set down the knife. The half-carved charm lay in her palm—a mourning piece, he realized, the kind she used to make in the old days, before the Ban, before everything. "You know what I heard last night?" she said. "Through the wall. The innkeeper's daughter praying. Just—praying, quietly, the way kids do. And something answered her. Something small and warm, some household god that's been asleep in the chimney for ten years. She laughed, Anselm. She laughed like the world had just gotten bigger." She closed her fist around the charm. "That's what's happening everywhere, right now, in every kitchen and stable and gutter in the free cities. And tomorrow a room full of men in good coats is going to vote on whether it's convenient."

Wren looked up from her grass. "If they say no," she said, in her careful, too-old voice, "do we still go to the capital?"

"Yes," Anselm and Beatrix said together, and then Beatrix added, "especially then."

Wren nodded, satisfied, and went back to her pigeons, and Anselm felt the familiar ache in his chest that Wren produced in him lately—the ache of watching a child carry weight that should have crushed her, and watching her refuse to put it down.

* * *

The formal session convened at the ninth bell the next morning, and it went worse than theater. It went worse than arithmetic.

Corvane read a prepared statement about the necessity of stability. Hesse moved that envoys be dispatched to the crown to "clarify intentions"—a motion seconded by three cities whose delegates could not meet each other's eyes. The hill-town woman, whose name was Aldith Renn, rose to speak of the burned shop in Vellamare and was ruled out of order on a technicality of delegation standing. A student in the gallery shouted something about Maren Ostreva and was dragged out by guards while the council pretended, with magnificent collective discipline, not to hear.

Anselm stood at the floor's center and made the argument again, better this time, sharper—he laid out the timeline, the troop counts, the deserter's testimony, the pattern of memory-loss reports drifting west from villages near the capital, whole hamlets where no one could recall the names of their own dead. He watched the words land and slide off. He watched men look at their hands. He understood, with a clarity that felt like a wound, that he was not speaking to cowards. Cowards could be shamed. He was speaking to men whose entire moral architecture had been built on the premise that the world was governable, and who would rather lose the world than admit it wasn't.

"The motion to send envoys," Corvane intoned, "carries. Seven in favor, one opposed, one abstaining. This session is concluded until—"

The doors of the Council Hall slammed open.

Not pushed. *Slammed*, as though a hand the size of the doorway had struck them, and the sound went through the mosaic floor and up through the soles of Anselm's feet like a bell being rung underground. Every head turned. Cold harbor air rolled down the central aisle, smelling of salt and storm, though the morning outside had been mild and windless.

Wren stood in the doorway.

She was supposed to be at the hospice. Beatrix had left her there with strict instructions and a protective charm, and Anselm's stomach dropped through the floor at the sight of her—small, breathing hard, hair loose, her eyes wide and shining with a light that was not entirely her own. Behind her, visible through the open doors, the sky over Harrowgate's harbor had gone the color of a bruise, though the sun had been high and yellow an hour ago.

"Wren," Beatrix breathed, from the gallery, already moving.

"I'm sorry," Wren said, and her voice came out doubled, layered, a child's voice with something vast underneath it speaking in unison. "I tried to wait. I tried to be patient like you said. But they're going to send *letters*." Her chin trembled. "There's a fleet. I heard the water talking. There's a fleet in the deep channel and it's flying the crown's colors and it's going to be here in six days, and you're all in here talking about *envoys*—"

"Child," Corvane began, with the weary condescension of a man addressing an interruption, "guards will escort you—"

The harbor answered him.

Every person in the hall heard it—a long, groaning sigh, like the sea drawing breath—and then the light through the high windows changed, greenish and strange, and somewhere beyond the walls came a sound none of them had ever heard before and all of them understood at once: the sound of the entire harbor of Harrowgate rising.

Anselm reached the doors first. Below the council hill, the great basin of the harbor was lifting—not gently, not kindly, but like a beast rolling over in its sleep. Water stood up in a gray-green wall taller than the lighthouse, taller than the temple spire, hanging there over the anchored fishing boats and the merchantmen and the sleek dark shapes beyond the breakwater that had not been there yesterday and that even at this distance wore the crowned sigil of the usurper on their sails.

The wave held. Suspended. Waiting.

And Wren, standing in the doorway with her fists clenched and tears cutting clean lines down her face, said in her small doubled voice:

"*Tell me again how you'd like to proceed.*"

* * *

No one moved. No one spoke. Down in the harbor, forty thousand tons of ocean hung in the air like a held note, and the crown's fleet rocked at anchor beneath it, and gulls wheeled screaming through the green light.

Anselm looked at the council—at Corvane's face gone the color of tallow, at Hesse gripping the bench, at Aldith Renn of the hill towns standing very still with her hand wrapped white-knuckled around her grandmother's charm—and he understood that the debate was over. Not won. Never won, in any way that would satisfy him. Simply ended, the way storms end arguments.

Beatrix came down the aisle and took Wren's hand, and the child sagged against her, suddenly only nine years old and terrified, and the wave trembled above the fleet like a question awaiting permission.

"Careful, little one," Beatrix murmured, too quiet for anyone but Anselm to hear, and her eyes, when they lifted to the suspended sea, held no awe at all—only the grim focus of a woman doing arithmetic of her own. "Whatever you've called up—we need to know how to put it down. Or how to point it."
