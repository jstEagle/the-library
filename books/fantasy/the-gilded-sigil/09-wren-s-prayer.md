## 9. Wren's Prayer

Wren had learned to count the councilmen the way she counted crows on a rooftop: by their hunches, by which ones looked at her and which ones looked through her. There were eleven of them in the great round chamber of Harrowgate, beneath a ceiling painted with ships that had probably never sailed, and nine of them would not meet her eyes.

She sat very still on a bench between Beatrix and Anselm, her hands folded in her lap like Anselm had taught her, like a good penitent, except Wren was not sorry for anything and did not know how to pretend it. Her feet did not quite touch the floor. She had worn her best dress—the blue one Beatrix had bought off a rag cart and scrubbed twice—and she had braided her own hair that morning, badly, one braid thicker than the other, because nobody here was going to do it for her.

The councilmen were talking about tides.

That was the funny thing. That was the thing Wren kept wanting to laugh about, right up in her throat where laughing went to die. They stood around a table with a map of the harbor carved into its wood—little wooden ships, little wooden walls, little wooden soldiers no bigger than her thumb—and they argued about whether the usurper's fleet could cross the shallows at Greywater, and whether the sea forts would hold, and whether surrendering now might be counted as wisdom later, when the histories were written by whoever was left alive to write them.

And all the while, under the floor, under the harbor, under everything, something old was listening.

Wren could feel it the way you feel a dog leaning against your leg under a table. Not looking at you. Just *there*, warm and heavy and patient.

*They are afraid,* said the thing under the harbor, in a voice like water moving through a cave. It didn't use words exactly. It used the feeling of words, the way dreams do. *The small ones in fine clothes. They are afraid of the big hunger, so they will feed it the small cities first.*

Wren pressed her lips together. She was not supposed to answer out loud. Beatrix had been very clear. *In a room full of strangers, you keep your mouth shut and your prayers quieter than your heartbeat.* But her heartbeat was already loud. It had been loud since the councilman with the grey beard—Lord Casserin, someone called him—had said the word *surrender* for the fourth time, gently, like he was setting down something fragile.

"Vellamare has already fallen," Casserin was saying. "Or will fall within the season. The question before this council is not whether we can stop the crown—it is whether we can survive it. Terms might be reached. Tribute. The free cities have bought peace before."

"With coin," Anselm said. He stood at the map table, and his voice had that edge it got when he talked about the Ban, the edge of a man sharpening himself against his own past. "You bought peace from pirates with coin. This is not a pirate. This is a thing that eats what people are. You cannot pay it tribute in gold, Lord Casserin. It does not want your gold. It wants your grandmothers' names. It wants the song your mother sang you. It wants every prayer anyone ever whispered over a sick child—and then it wants the child's memory of being sick, and the mother's memory of whispering, until there is nothing left in Vellamare but bodies that obey."

Murmurs. Someone said *madness*. Someone else said *priest*, the way you'd say *leech*.

Wren watched Beatrix. Beatrix sat with her scarred hands flat on her knees, watching the councilmen the way she watched bone before carving—not with hope, but with attention. Waiting to see what was true underneath.

"The girl," said a woman councilman near the far side—a hard-faced woman with a chain of office and ink stains on her fingers. "They say the child's prayers get answered. Is that true?"

Every face turned toward Wren. Nine of them looked through her anyway, even while looking at her.

"She is a child," Beatrix said quietly, "not a weapon."

"I asked if it was true."

"It is true," Wren said.

Her voice came out smaller than she wanted. The chamber was so big. The ceiling with its painted ships seemed very far away, and the wooden fleet on the table seemed very small, and somewhere below all of it the old thing under the harbor shifted its attention toward her like a whale turning in deep water.

"Then let us test it," the woman said. "One prayer. Here, now. If your gods are back, as these two claim—if they answer—then show us. Show me, and I will vote for war tonight."

"Wren." Beatrix's hand found hers under the bench. Her grip was careful, the way she held bone. "You don't owe them anything."

But Wren was already shaking her head. Because that wasn't the problem. The problem was the thing she had not told them, the thing that had been sitting in her chest since the charnel house, since the road, since the night the hedge-witch's shop burned and Wren had prayed *please don't let them find us* and the smoke itself had bent away from their hiding place like a servant bowing.

The problem was that her prayers were never small. She didn't know how to make them small. When Wren wanted, things happened *all the way*.

"I'm scared," she said honestly, because Anselm said honesty came first even when it cost. "Last time I prayed hard, the well in that village got angry. And the time before that—" She stopped. The time before that was the fire, and the men who had come out of the fire were not something the councilmen needed to hear about from a seven-year-old girl in an uneven braid.

"Child," said Lord Casserin, kind and terrible, "no one blames you. But you must see that stories of answered prayers are worth less than paper. Harrowgate cannot march its sons east on the word of a girl who talks to wells."

Under the floor, the old thing laughed. It was not a mean laugh. It was the laugh of something enormous that had watched a thousand councils just like this one, in cities that no longer existed.

*Ask me,* it said. *Ask me anything, small one. I have been waiting under this water for three hundred years for someone to ask.*

* * *

They left the council chamber without a vote. Of course they did. The woman with the ink-stained fingers abstained rather than stake her reputation on a child's god, and Lord Casserin called for a recess, and recesses in Harrowgate lasted days, and days were the one thing the map on the table said they did not have. Anselm walked out with his jaw tight enough to crack walnuts. Beatrix walked out holding Wren's hand too tightly and pretending she wasn't.

The council hall stood on a bluff above the harbor, and the evening came down over the water all gold and bruise-colored, fishing boats coming home in strings like beads, gulls screaming their stupid greedy joy. Wren loved Harrowgate's harbor. It was the biggest thing she had ever seen that was also gentle.

She stopped at the balustrade and looked down at it and felt the old thing looking up.

"You're angry," she said.

Beatrix and Anselm stopped arguing mid-sentence.

"Not angry," said Wren, because that wasn't the right word either, and words mattered, Anselm always said so. "Lonely. It's been lonely a long time. Since before the Ban. Nobody prayed to it right. People pray to the sea for safe crossing and good catches, but they never pray to the *harbor*. The harbor keeps everybody safe and nobody thanks it. It says it's the door, not the water. Doors get forgotten."

Anselm crouched down to her level, robes pooling on the stone. His eyes were tired and kind and frightened, all three at once, which Wren thought was probably what grown-ups felt like most of the time. "Wren. What did it say to you in there?"

"That I should ask it something." She twisted her braid around her finger. The thin one. "It said it's been waiting three hundred years."

"And what are you going to ask?"

Wren looked at the harbor. The last light lay on the water like spilled honey. Out past the sea-forts, past the shallows at Greywater, the east was getting dark in a way that had nothing to do with sunset. Somewhere out there was the fleet. Wooden ships full of hollow men, sailing under a king who wasn't a king, carrying the hunger that ate memories to the door of the free cities.

She thought about the hedge-witch's shop burning. She thought about the smell of it, and about how Beatrix had gone *into* the smoke, and how Beatrix pretended to be hard but cried at night sometimes, quiet, the way you cry when you think children are asleep. She thought about the councilmen with their little wooden ships, counting tides while the world ended.

"They're not going to fight," Wren said. "Are they. Even if I showed them. They'll have another recess, and another one, and then the ships will be here and they'll say there was nothing anyone could have done."

"No," Anselm said simply. "I don't think they'll fight. Not in time."

"So somebody has to."

"Wren." Beatrix knelt too now, and took Wren's face in her two scarred hands, and made her look at her. Beatrix's eyes were brown and steady and scared. "Listen to me. Whatever is under that water, whatever has been talking to you—it is not your friend yet. Gods are not friends. Gods are weather. You can love the rain and still drown in it. Do you understand me? If you pray, it will answer, and it will answer *big*, because that is what gods do, and it will not ask you afterward whether you meant all of it."

"But if I don't," Wren said, "who will?"

Beatrix didn't have an answer for that. Wren saw her not have it—saw the answer die behind her eyes, saw her decide, finally, to let go. Her thumbs brushed Wren's cheekbones once, like sealing something.

"Pray small," Beatrix whispered. "Whatever you do. Pray as small as you can."

* * *

Wren went down alone to the water.

Not all the way alone—she could feel them on the bluff above her, two small worried shapes against the darkening sky—but the stairs to the quay were hers to walk, and the last hundred feet of stone were hers, and the moment when she stood at the harbor's edge with the tide lapping the steps below her was hers, and nobody could make it otherwise. She was glad of that. Grown-ups forgot you needed some things to be yours.

The fishing boats were all moored for the night. Lamps burned along the quay in a crooked line. Somewhere a woman was singing to a baby, a song with no words Wren knew, just sounds, the oldest kind of song.

Wren knelt at the edge of the lowest step, where the black water breathed against the stone, and folded her hands, and found that she was crying a little without having decided to.

"I don't know your name," she said out loud. "Nobody knows your name anymore. Is that okay? Can I pray to you anyway?"

The whole harbor went still.

Not calm—still. The difference was enormous. Waves stopped mid-lap. The boats quit their gentle knocking. Even the gulls shut up. The world held its breath, and Wren understood suddenly, completely, the way you understand things at seven that you will spend the rest of your life re-understanding: she was not talking *at* the water. She was talking *to* it, and it was listening, and it had been waiting so long.

*I am the Door,* the harbor said, and now the voice was everywhere, in the stone under her knees and the salt on her lips and the lamp flames bending seaward. *I am the Keeping-Place. I am the wall of water that stands between the open sea and the sleeping city. Three hundred years, little one. Three hundred years of holding the line while they forgot there was a line.*

"There are ships coming," Wren said. Her voice wobbled. She made it straight again. "Bad ships. With bad men. And the people up there"—she pointed at the council hall, a lit crown on the dark bluff—"won't stand in front of them. They'll open the doors themselves and call it wisdom. And everyone in this city will forget their mothers' names, and then they'll forget they ever had mothers, and then—" Her voice broke after all. She let it. "Please. Please help them. Help *us*."

*Ask,* said the Door.

And here was the moment. Wren felt it coming like a wave you can see gathering a mile out, the one that will either lift you or break you, and Beatrix's voice was in her ears—*pray small, pray small*—and Anselm's voice too, all those lessons on the road, *a prayer is a door you open, child, and you cannot fully know what walks through*—

And Wren thought: I am seven years old. I am not wise. I don't know how to pray small. I only know how to mean it.

So she meant it.

"Keep them out," she said. "Keep the bad ships out of the harbor. Keep this city safe. As long as you can. As hard as you can. *Please.*"

The sea stood up.

* * *

Later—in the stories, in the songs, in the testimony Wren would give a hundred times to a hundred disbelieving faces—they would call it the Miracle of Harrowgate, and they would describe it in orderly terms: a surge tide, unprecedented, driven by some confluence of moon and wind; the usurper's vanguard fleet of nineteen ships caught in the harbor mouth at slack turn; the waters rising twelve feet in a quarter hour, not in waves but in a single smooth wall, like a hand closing; and the fleet lifted, turned, and dashed against the Teeth, the black rocks at the harbor's northern jaw, with a sound like the sky cracking.

All of that was true. None of it was the truth.

The truth was that Wren knelt on the step and the water rose around her like a cathedral building itself, and she was inside it and not wet, held in a column of standing sea, and the Door spoke through the whole of it, and the Door was not gentle.

*THEY COME WITH A HUNGER IN THEIR HOLLOWS,* it said, and the voice was thunder heard underwater, vast and grieving and furious. *THEY CAME BEFORE, IN THE OLD YEARS. THEY TOOK THE SHRINES FROM MY SHORES AND THE NAMES FROM MY PRIESTS. I HAVE HELD THIS DOOR THREE HUNDRED YEARS AND NO ONE HAS ASKED ME TO HOLD IT ONCE MORE—*

Ships screamed. Wren couldn't see them from inside the water-wall, but she could hear them, wood groaning and splitting, men crying out, and her stomach clenched, because some of those men weren't hollow, were they, some of them were probably pressed sailors, boys maybe, boys like her almost, and—

*Don't kill them,* she gasped. *Please—you don't have to kill them—just—*

*NO ONE DROWNS TONIGHT,* the Door said, and it sounded almost offended. *I AM NOT THE OPEN SEA. I AM THE DOOR. I KEEP. I DO NOT DEVOUR. THAT IS THE OTHER ONE'S TRADE.*

And the water did something Wren had no word for. It didn't smash the fleet. It *refused* it. Nineteen ships were picked up like toys a child declines to play with and set down outside the harbor mouth—set down hard, masts snapping, hulls grinding on the Teeth, sailors tumbling into churning foam—but the foam itself pushed them, every one of them, coughed them up onto the rocks and the shingle beyond, gasping and alive and stranded, while their ships went to splinters. The sea stood between the wrecked men and the city like a wall with a will, and the wall said, in a voice only Wren could hear:

*LET THEM LOOK AT WHAT ANSWERED. LET THEM RUN HOME AND TELL THE HOLLOW KING THAT THE WATER ITSELF HAS CHOSEN A SIDE.*

Then it began to lower, slowly, the great column of sea settling back into its bed, and Wren felt the Door's attention begin to fold away like a giant lying down to sleep, and panic went through her, sharp and sudden.

*Wait!*

*...LITTLE ONE.*

"What's your name?" she called up into the settling spray. "Your real name! I want to know it. I want to thank you *right*."

A long pause. The lamps along the quay relit, one by one, though no one had touched them.

*I HAD A NAME,* the Door said. *IT WAS EATEN WITH EVERYTHING ELSE. BUT YOU MAY CALL ME WHAT THE OLD SAILORS CALLED ME, BEFORE THE BAN. THEY CALLED ME THE THRESHOLD. AND THEY LEFT MILK AND HONEY ON THE QUAY-STONES AT EACH TURNING OF THE TIDE.*

"I'll bring milk and honey," Wren promised. "Every tide-turning. I swear it."

*THEN WE ARE BOUND, SMALL ONE.* And the voice, going down into the deep, going away, grew almost—tender. *YOU PRAYED LIKE THE OLD ONES PRAYED. WITHOUT COUNTING THE COST. BE CAREFUL OF THAT. THE WORLD WILL TAKE ADVANTAGE OF A HEART LIKE YOURS. SO WILL THE GODS. FORGIVE US. WE ARE HUNGRY TOO.*

Then it was gone, and the harbor was just a harbor, breathing normally, and Wren was kneeling on wet stone in the dark, soaked to the skin though the water had never touched her, and she was shaking so hard her teeth clicked.

* * *

They found her there, of course. Beatrix came down the quay-stairs at a dead run with Anselm behind her, both of them shouting her name, and Wren tried to stand and her legs wouldn't hold, so Beatrix just dropped and gathered her up, and Wren buried her face in Beatrix's shoulder and shook and shook.

"You're soaked," Beatrix kept saying, into her hair, half laughing, half sobbing. "You're soaked through, you foolish, brave, impossible child—"

"The water didn't touch me," Wren said. "It held me. It held me like—" Like you do, she almost said. Like you held me out of the fire. "Beatrix, it *listened*. It listened and it was so big and so sad and it didn't hurt anybody, it *kept* them out, that's what it does, it keeps—"

"I know." Beatrix rocked her. "I know, little bird. I heard. Half of Harrowgate heard. Look."

Wren looked. The bluff above the harbor was crowded with people—hundreds of them, pouring out of houses and taverns, silent, staring at the wreckage strewn along the Teeth, at the surviving sailors huddled on the far shingle, at the sea lying calm and ordinary as a sleeping dog. And on the council-hall steps, tiny with distance, stood the eleven councilmen. Even from here Wren could see that they were no longer hunched. Something had gone out of their spines—or into them.

And above the great doors of the council hall, in the stone lintel, in a carving so old no one had known it was there—Wren saw it glow. Faintly. A threshold-mark. A door-sign. Waking.

"They'll vote tomorrow," Anselm said quietly. He had taken off his outer robe and put it around Wren's shoulders without asking, and his hand rested on her head, light as a blessing. "There will be war. There will be an army marching east within the month. One child's prayer did what a week of speeches could not." He exhaled, long and unsteady. "Gods forgive me, I taught seminarians for twenty years and I never once saw faith move anything but paperwork."

Wren should have felt proud. Some part of her did—a hot bright coal of it, *I did that, I asked and it came*—but underneath the coal was cold water, and in the cold water the Door's last words kept circling like a fish.

*We are hungry too.*

"Beatrix," she said, very small, into her shoulder. "When a god answers a prayer... does the god choose? Or does the prayer choose? Am I..." She hunted for the word she'd been avoiding for weeks. "Am I its vessel? Or its friend?"

Beatrix pulled back and looked at her, and Wren saw that Beatrix didn't know either—that the question scared her worse than any answer would have. Behind her, out on the black water, far past the Teeth, a single light appeared where no ship should be: small, green, patient, watching the harbor that had broken nineteen ships.

Watching, Wren was almost sure, *her*.

"We'll find out together," Beatrix said at last. "Come on. You need dry clothes and honey, and I need to sit down before my legs learn what yours just did."

But as they climbed the quay-stairs, Wren looked back once at the green light on the horizon, and she made herself a promise, quiet and fierce, the kind of promise that doesn't need a god to witness it: *I'll be your friend. Whoever you were before they ate your name—I'll find that out too. And I'll bring the milk and honey myself.*

Behind them, unnoticed, the harbor's water lapped the stones in a rhythm almost like a heartbeat. Almost like a knock.

Almost like a door, opening.
