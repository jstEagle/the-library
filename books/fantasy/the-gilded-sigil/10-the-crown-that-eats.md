## 10. The Crown That Eats

The bone had been waiting for her.

Beatrix felt it the moment Anselm lifted it from its wrappings of oilcloth and ash—a pull behind her sternum, like a hook set gently in flesh. Not a scream, not the whispering chorus she had lived with for ten years. Something older. Something patient.

They had made camp in the ruins of a shepherd's cot two days east of the charnel house, high enough on the moor that the road below was only a brown thread stitched through gorse. Wren slept by the fire with her thumb in her mouth, curled against Sefa's ghost as though a dead woman were the warmest thing in the world—and perhaps she was; Beatrix had seen the girl lean into that cold outline and sigh like a cat. Anselm sat across the flames from her now, the bone cradled in both hands as if it might bite him.

It was a femur. Human, heavy, yellowed to the color of old tallow. The charnel keeper had sworn it came from a barrow no one had opened in three hundred years, and had charged them an outrageous sum for the privilege of carrying it away.

"You don't have to do this tonight," Anselm said.

"Yes, I do." She took off her coat and spread it over her knees, a habit of her mother's she'd never questioned—work done on cloth, so the cloth could take what spilled. "Every mile we walk east, I've been telling myself I'll understand it better when we get there. But I'm tired of walking toward something I can't name."

"And if it's worse than you imagine?"

"Then I'll have imagined it honestly at least once."

Anselm almost smiled. He had a priest's face even disgraced—long, grave, built for blessing or mourning with equal ease—but the smile reached his eyes more often now than it had in Vellamare. He passed her the bone.

It was warm. That was the first wrongness. Bone should be cool, always, even in summer, even by firelight; bone held the temperature of the earth it came from. This was warm as a sleeping animal, warm as a hand, and beneath the warmth she felt the pulse of something vast and slow, like a whale passing under a rowboat.

She settled it across her knees. Closed her eyes. Did what she had done ten thousand times in a shop that smelled of tallow and juniper: she listened.

* * *

The world went out like a pinched candle.

Not darkness—darkness has edges, has texture. This was absence, total and seamless, and then, slowly, light bled back in the way dawn bleeds through fog, gray and sourceless, and Beatrix understood she was standing somewhere that did not exist except in the memory of a dead man three centuries gone.

A hall. High rafters black with smoke, banners hanging stiff as shed skins. Torches burned without flickering, which told her this memory had been revisited so many times it had worn smooth, polished by repetition into something almost solid. And at the far end of the hall, on a throne of pale wood that might once have been alive, sat a king.

He was not what she expected. She had imagined—she didn't know what. Gold. Grandeur. A figure out of chapel frescoes, bearded and remote. Instead he was thin, gray at the temples, with ink stains on two fingers and the harried look of a man who would rather be reading. His crown was a simple circlet of iron, unadorned, and he wore it the way a man wears a debt.

"Hello," Beatrix said, and her voice sounded strange here, too loud and too present, a living thing in a room of preserved moments.

The king looked up. His eyes found hers with terrible precision, and she realized with a lurch that this was not a memory playing out. He could see her. Whatever was left of him had been waiting in this worn-smooth moment like a man waiting at a window.

"You're listening," he said. His voice was hoarse, rusted with disuse. "After all this time. Someone is actually listening."

"I hear the dead," Beatrix said. "It's—it's all I can do. I carve their voices into bone."

"The dead." He laughed, and it was an awful sound, dry as pages turning. "Do you know how long I have been neither? Come closer. Please. I have so little left, and most of what remains is this room."

She crossed the hall. Up close she could see the wrongness in him—the way his edges blurred when he moved too quickly, the way the torchlight passed through his shoulders. He was a memory wearing himself, and he was fraying.

"My name was Aldric," he said. "I was crowned in spring, in the rain, because the priests said rain at a coronation meant the gods approved. I believed things like that, then. I believed the world was legible." He flexed his stained fingers. "I want to show you what happened. It will hurt you. I am sorry for that. But you cannot fight what you have not seen, and everyone who comes to me wanting to fight—I show them, and they run, and they forget, because forgetting is easier, and then there is no one left who knows."

"Show me," Beatrix said.

He closed his eyes. "Hold on to yourself. However you understand that instruction—hold on."

* * *

The hall dissolved.

She stood in a bedchamber hung with green silk, decades earlier by the king's unlined face, and she watched Aldric—young Aldric, crown still new enough to feel like a gift rather than a sentence—wake in the night because the room was cold.

Cold. That was the first thing. Not dread, not omen. Just cold, the specific cold of a window left open in autumn, and a young king pulling a robe over his nightshirt and padding to the casement to close it, annoyed, thinking of the council meeting at dawn.

The thing was already in the room.

It stood where the shadows pooled deepest, and Beatrix's mind slid off it the way a boot slides on ice. She caught impressions: tall. Wrong-jointed. A face like a face seen through running water, features assembling and disassembling, and above it all—

The crown.

It floated a finger's width above the thing's head, iron-black, and it was the only part of it that stayed fixed, the only part her eyes could hold. It was beautiful the way a drain is beautiful to water. Looking at it, she felt a tug behind her eyes, a gentle suggestion that she had something it wanted, something small and shining she wouldn't miss.

"No," she said aloud, in the vision, in the bedchamber, and the thing turned toward her voice with the whole of its attention, and she understood that attention from it was like being looked at by winter.

But it wasn't looking at her. It was looking past her—at young Aldric, frozen by the open window, and it spoke, and its voice was the kindest voice Beatrix had ever heard. That was the horror of it. It sounded like a grandmother, like a lover, like the voice inside your own head on your best day.

*You look cold,* it said. *Let me help.*

And it touched his brow, just below the hairline, and Beatrix watched it drink.

There was no other word. The thing fed, and what it fed on poured out of the young king in threads of light so fine they were nearly invisible: the smell of his mother's kitchen; the name of the dog he'd had at seven; the particular blue of the sea off the coast where he'd been born; the feeling of his wife's hand in his on their wedding day, which hadn't happened yet but which he dreamed about, and the dream went too, sucked away like broth through a straw.

Aldric swayed. His face went smooth—not peaceful, empty, the smoothness of a stone worn by water. Then the thing withdrew, and the king blinked, and frowned faintly at the open window, and closed it, and went back to bed, and did not remember any of it.

*That was the first night,* Aldric's voice said, everywhere, the narrator of his own violation. *It took a little every night after. Never much. Never enough to notice. That is how it works. You wake each morning slightly less than you were, and the person you were yesterday cannot testify, because you cannot remember being him.*

* * *

The visions came faster after that, years collapsing into one another like cards.

She saw the middle years: Aldric at thirty-five, signing decrees whose reasons he could no longer recall, trusting ministers whose names felt unfamiliar in his mouth. She saw him pause in the middle of a sentence, hunting for a word, and the word was gone, and the courtiers exchanged glances. She saw his wife study him across a breakfast table with growing fear, saying *you used to laugh at my jokes before I finished them,* and watch him answer, politely, *did I?*

She saw the nights. Always the nights. The thing returning, patient as tide, and the threads of light growing thinner each year, sparser, the harvest of a field grazed down to stubble. And she saw the crown change hands—or rather, she saw the truth beneath the story the histories told.

*They say it killed me,* Aldric said. *Even the ones who know anything at all, they say the usurper murdered the king and took his shape. Murder implies an ending. There was no ending. There was a diminishing.*

She saw the last night. Old Aldric—though he was not old, only emptied, sixty years of age with nothing left inside but duty and the memory of having once been someone—sitting on his throne at midnight because he could no longer remember why he shouldn't. The thing knelt before him, and the crown rose from its head, and drifted, and settled onto his.

And Beatrix felt what Aldric felt in that moment, and it was the worst thing in all the visions, worse than the feeding: it felt like coming home. The crown fit. Of course it fit. It had been shaped by decades of taking him, carved to his contours the way water carves a bed. When it settled on his brow, the last bright scraps of him—the memory of his own name, the knowledge that he had ever been anything other than obedient—drained up into the iron, and what remained behind the king's eyes was a quiet, vacant readiness, a vessel awaiting instruction.

*It doesn't wear my face,* Aldric said, and his voice was very small now, a man speaking from the bottom of a well. *Understand this. It never needed to. It ate my face out from the inside and kept the shape. What sits on the throne now is my obedience given legs. Everything else—the cruelty, the armies, the inquisitors burning hedge-witches—that is appetite. The crown is hungry, and a hungry thing with a kingdom's worth of hands will feed it however it can.*

*Memory,* Beatrix whispered. *It eats memory.*

*It eats everything memory holds.* The ruined hall reassembled around them, the worn-smooth throne room of his prison. *Magic first, because magic is memory—the oldest memories, the land remembering itself, the gods remembered into being by ten thousand years of prayer. Ban the practice, burn the books, and you slow the meal. But prayers are still said in secret, aren't they? Charms are still carved. People still remember. So long as anyone remembers, it feeds, and it grows, and it wants more.*

He turned to her fully, and up close she could see that his eyes were the exact color of the iron crown, and she wondered if that had always been true or if the crown had colored what it drank.

*You are going east,* he said. *To the capital. To me.*

"How do you know that?"

*Because the bones remember, and the bones are mine, and I sent them ahead like messages in bottles.* He smiled, and it cracked his fading face. *Three hundred years, listener. Do you know what I have had time to do? I have memorized myself. Every night it takes what it can reach, and every night I rebuild, from the copies I hid in places it does not look—in this room, in the marrow of bones scattered across the realm. It thinks it emptied me. But I have been keeping a ledger. I remember being a king. I remember my name.*

His voice gathered strength, and the gray hall brightened around him, torches flaring.

*I can be put back. Do you understand? The rite exists—I have carried its shape in my mouth for three centuries, waiting for a throat that could speak it. But the rite requires a carver who can hear the dead, and a soul-steady hand, and—* he faltered, and the brightness guttered, *—and a price. There is always a price. The crown consumed a king's worth of love to make me hollow. To fill me again, love must be paid back into the rite. Equal weight. Equal warmth.*

"What kind of love?" Beatrix asked, though some cold, prescient part of her already knew, already felt the question settle into her chest like a splinter working toward the heart.

*A memory of it,* Aldric said gently. *Given wholly. Not borrowed, not shared. Given. The rite will ask you for the dearest thing you carry, listener, and it will not tell you which thing that is until your knife is already moving.*

The hall began to dissolve, torches dying one by one, and Aldric with them, his edges unraveling into the gray.

*Come quickly,* he said. *It grows stronger every season. And listener—one more thing, while you can still choose to hear it—*

The last torch went out.

* * *

Beatrix came back to herself with a gasp, the femur rolling from her lap into the ashes at the fire's edge. Her whole body was shaking. Her cheeks were wet. She had no memory of crying.

Wren was awake, sitting bolt upright, staring at her with those god-touched eyes gone wide and silver in the firelight. "You were gone," the girl said. "You were looking right at me and you weren't anywhere. Your face was—" She stopped, searching. "Your face was like someone reading a letter that hurts them."

Sefa's ghost stood behind the child, translucent and grave, and Beatrix looked at her mentor—her sharp-tongued, stubborn, beloved dead teacher—and felt the splinter in her chest turn, though she didn't yet know why.

"Tell me," Anselm said quietly. He had a hand on her shoulder, warm and steady. "All of it."

So she told them. The cold room. The kind voice. The threads of light. The crown settling home like a key into a lock it had spent decades cutting. She told them about the ledger, the hidden copies, the king who had memorized himself against oblivion for three hundred years. And when she reached the price—the equal weight of love, the dearest thing given wholly—her voice did not shake, because she was watching Sefa's face as she said it, and Sefa had gone very still, and ghosts, Beatrix thought, should not be able to go pale, and yet.

"It eats memory," Anselm repeated at last. He was staring into the fire. "Ten years ago they banned magic, and I helped them do it. I dragged practitioners to the pyres and called it mercy." His jaw worked. "We weren't enforcing a law. We were rationing its food."

Outside, far to the west, thunder rolled across the moor—except there were no clouds, and the sound came again, rhythmic, deliberate, and Beatrix realized with a chill spreading through her ribs that it was not thunder at all.

It was drums. Marching. Many miles off, but marching east, toward the free cities, toward the capital, toward the hollow king and the crown that ate.

Wren stood up, hugging her elbows, and pointed west with a trembling finger. "They're coming," she said. "I can hear them praying. All those soldiers. They're praying to win."
