## 11. Betrayal at the Ford

The river had a name once, Beatrix was sure of it, but the maps had burned with everything else and now it was only water—wide, brown, patient, moving east toward the sea they were trying to reach. They came down to the ford in the grey hour before dawn, when the mist lay on the current like a shroud laid over a body, and Beatrix felt, for the first time since Vellamare, something close to hope.

Three days out from Harrowgate. Three days of Anselm's quiet prayers and Wren's small hand tucked into hers and Sefa's voice drifting beside her like woodsmoke. Three days without a single charm screaming.

*Too quiet,* Sefa had said last night, watching the fire. *You've been hunted before, girl. You know how this feels. This is the feeling right before.*

Beatrix had told her she was being morbid. She was already composing an apology.

The ford itself was shallow here, gravel-bright, edged with reeds that hissed in the wind off the water. On the far bank the road climbed away toward the hills, and beyond the hills, days of travel, and beyond those, the capital, and beneath it, if the dead king's bone spoke true, a soul still faintly alive and waiting for someone to listen.

Wren splashed ahead into the shallows, laughing when the cold bit her ankles, and for a moment Beatrix let herself watch the child instead of the road behind them. Wren's prayers had grown quieter lately—not smaller, but more careful, as though she'd learned that divinity answered like a loaded trap answers. The god-touched girl cupped her hands in the river and whispered something to it, and the water, absurdly, seemed to whisper back.

"Come on," Anselm said, shouldering their pack. His collar was long gone, replaced by a plain wool coat, but he still carried himself like a man perpetually about to apologize to a congregation. "Before the light comes up all the way."

They were halfway across when Beatrix heard it.

Not with her ears. With the bones in her pack—the king's femur wrapped in oilcloth, which had been silent as sleep for three days, and which now woke with a low, grinding hum, like a millstone turning somewhere far underground.

She stopped in the middle of the ford. Water pushed at her knees.

"Sefa," she said. "Sefa, listen—"

"I hear it." Sefa's ghost stood on the water beside her—or rather, Beatrix realized with a lurch, Sefa had been standing between them and the road behind them for some minutes now, turned around, staring back the way they'd come. Her mentor's form flickered like a candle in a draft. "Girl. Get out of the water. *Get out of the water.*"

Then the mist tore open and the riders came down the near slope.

There were more than Beatrix could count in the first terrible heartbeat—a dozen, two dozen, black coats and pale tabards bearing the crowned sigil of the usurper, horses steaming, lances couched. They did not charge blindly. They fanned out along both banks with the practiced ease of men who had done this many times to many fleeing people, cutting off the far road first, then the near one, closing the circle the way a hand closes around a moth.

"They knew," Anselm breathed. He had pulled Wren against his chest, one arm locked around her, and his face had gone the color of old ash. "Beatrix. They knew exactly where we'd cross. Nobody knew where we'd cross except—"

"Harrowgate," Beatrix said. The word tasted like copper. "The council. Someone on the council."

"There was a man," Anselm said quietly. "Third night. Asking about our route. I thought he was just curious. Gods forgive me, I *told* him."

There was no time for recrimination. The first rider reached the water's edge and leveled his lance, and Beatrix saw his eyes through the slit of his helm—flat, dutiful, untroubled eyes, the eyes of a man doing a job—and something in her chest went hard and cold as river stone.

She had spent ten years staying small. Ten years of carved bone and lowered eyes and pretending she heard nothing. And these men would cut down a child in the shallows because a paper in the capital said she mattered.

"Anselm." Her voice didn't shake, and she was proud of that later, in the way you are proud of things done while drowning. "Take Wren. Cross. Don't stop for anything. Don't stop for me."

"Beatrix—"

"*Cross.*"

He crossed. She saw him lift the girl bodily out of the water and run, stumbling up the gravel, and she saw Wren twist in his arms, screaming something Beatrix couldn't hear over the river, and then Beatrix turned back to the riders and did the thing she had never dared do in ten years of hiding.

She unwrapped the king's bone, held it high above the water, and called every dead thing within earshot to rise.

What answered was not subtle.

The river gave up its dead the way a wound gives up pus. They came up out of the mud and the reeds and the drowned places downstream—the old bones of old battles, forgotten skirmishes from before the Ban, soldiers whose names had worn off their own grave-markers. They were not solid. They were not even much of anything. But they were *loud*, and Beatrix poured the king's grief through them like a bellringer pouring strength into a bell, and the riders' horses went mad.

Animals feel ghosts the way children feel weather. The line dissolved into screaming horseflesh, rearing, plunging, throwing men into the brown water, and Beatrix stood in the ford with her arms raised and her teeth bared and thought: *this is what I am, this is what I've been all along, and you come to my river.*

But there were so many of them. More spilling down the slopes now, dismounted, wading in with drawn steel, and the risen dead were thin things, half-heard things, and Beatrix felt herself thinning too, stretched across dozens of voices, holding each one against the current of silence that had ruled her whole adult life.

A blade found her shoulder. Not deep—the man wielding it seemed more startled than she was—but hot, sudden, real, and she staggered and nearly went under, and the chorus faltered—

—and then the world went white.

Later, Beatrix would try to describe what Sefa did, and fail, and try again, and fail better.

Sefa had been a teacher in life. She had taught Beatrix the grain of bone, the patience of the knife, the discipline of listening when listening hurt. And now, standing on the water in the full morning light with the usurper's soldiers wading toward her student, Sefa Thackeray—dead eleven years, faded to a whisper, sustained these past weeks by nothing but the returning gods and her own stubborn refusal to leave—did what teachers do.

She gave her student everything she had left.

The light came off her like heat off a forge. Beatrix felt it pass *through* her, felt it take stock of her with something almost like tenderness, and then Sefa stepped forward onto the water and became, for one impossible moment, entirely present—more present than the living, more present than the sun. Every soldier in the ford froze. Every horse dropped to its knees. Even the river seemed to pause between one ripple and the next.

"You will not touch her," Sefa said, and her voice was the voice of every lecture Beatrix had ever slept through, every correction carved into her work, every "again, girl, again" that had made her the carver she was—and it was also, unmistakably, underneath everything, afraid.

Ghosts burn. Beatrix hadn't known that. No one had ever stayed long enough to burn.

Sefa held the ford alone for what might have been ninety seconds and might have been nine years. She drove the soldiers back with hands made of remembered light. She cracked the ice of their courage and sent them scrambling up both banks. And Beatrix, bleeding into the river, watched her mentor grow thinner with every passing moment—watched the edges of her go soft, then translucent, then threadbare, like cloth worn through by too many years of honest use—and understood, with a clarity that felt like a knife going in, exactly what was happening.

"No," Beatrix said. "No, no, *no*, don't you dare—"

"Girl." Sefa turned. She was smiling. It was the same smile she'd worn over Beatrix's first finished charm, the one that meant *you've surpassed me, and I'm glad, and it hurts*. "You always carved too fast. Slow down. Listen first."

"Sefa—please—I'll find another way—we'll get you more time, the gods are back, there must be—"

"There is no other way. There never was. That's the lesson, love." Her voice was fraying now, letters dropping out of words. "I chose. Do you understand? Ghosts choose too. We're not... we're not echoes. We never were. I'm choosing *her*"—and she gestured, with the last of her substance, toward the far bank, toward Wren—"and *him*, and mostly, mostly I'm choosing you. Now *run*, Beatrix Zhou. Run, and carve, and listen. Promise me you'll keep listening."

"I promise," Beatrix sobbed, "I promise, I promise—"

"That's my girl."

And Sefa Thackeray, who had died poor and unforgiven by the city that outlawed her craft, who had spent eleven years waiting in the dark for the gods to wake, spread her arms across the width of the ford like a mother over a cradle, and burned out.

The light did not fade. It simply ended, the way a held note ends, and the silence afterward was the worst sound Beatrix had ever heard.

* * *

They ran.

Anselm carried her part of the way; she never learned which part. The king's bone was clutched against her chest, still humming, and her shoulder bled through Anselm's coat, and somewhere behind them the inquisitors were regrouping on a riverbank littered with men too frightened to pursue. Wren ran beside them, white-faced, silent in a way no child should be silent.

They didn't stop until noon, until a fold of hills swallowed the river from view, until Anselm finally set Beatrix down against a stone wall older than any kingdom and pressed water to her lips and stitched her shoulder with hands that shook only after the work was done.

Wren sat apart, knees drawn up, staring at nothing.

Beatrix looked at her—at the child she had pulled from ashes, whom Sefa had scolded and spoiled and secretly adored—and felt the loss arrive properly, the way floodwater arrives, all at once and everywhere.

"She's gone." Her voice sounded foreign to her. "Anselm. She's gone. I can't even—I can't hear her. There's nothing. There's just *nothing* where she was."

Anselm sat down heavily beside her. For a long time he said nothing, because Anselm had learned, over the weeks of the road, that Beatrix did not need words the way most people needed words. She needed witness.

Finally he said, "I have spent my whole life believing that sacrifice was a doctrine. Something written down. To be explained." He turned the stitching needle over in his fingers. "Today a dead woman showed me it's a choice. Made fresh, every time, by anyone willing." He looked at Beatrix. "That doesn't make it hurt less. But it means it wasn't waste. Nothing given freely is ever wasted."

"She told me to keep listening."

"Then that's the eulogy," Anselm said gently. "Live it."

Near dusk, Wren crept over and climbed into Beatrix's lap without asking, the way she did when the world had grown too large. Beatrix held her and stared out at the darkening hills and the capital somewhere beyond them, wearing its crown, eating its memories, and felt something settle in her chest—not peace, nothing so kind as peace, but purpose, honed now to an edge that would not blunt.

"They took her," Beatrix murmured into Wren's hair. "The thing in the crown took the king, and the Ban took our gods, and the fear in people took everything else. And today it took her." She closed her eyes. "So we're going to take it back. All of it. Everything they stole."

Wren stirred against her. When she spoke, her small voice was very quiet, and very certain, and it frightened Beatrix more than the riders had.

"The river told me something. Before the soldiers came." Wren lifted her head, and her eyes in the dusk were wide and old and lit from within by something that was not quite her own. "It said the crown knows we're coming now. It said..." She hesitated, and Beatrix felt the child's heart hammering against her own ribs. "It said the crown has heard of me. And it's *hungry*, Mama-Bea. It's coming to meet us."

Far to the east, past the dark hills, the sky above the capital glowed faintly—not with fire, but with a pale, hungry light, like a lamp lit in a window for someone expected home.

Or like an eye, opening.
