## 12. The Hollow Court

The road to the capital was paved with penance, and Anselm Oduya had learned to walk it well.

He had traded his traveling cloak for a penitent's gray—coarse wool, undyed, smelling of woodsmoke and humility—and had let three days of road dust settle into its weave until he looked like what he claimed to be: a priest of the old rite, walking barefoot to the capital to beg forgiveness of the Crown for his faithlessness. It was not even entirely a lie. That was the trick of it, he had found. The best disguises were confessions. He *was* faithless. He *had* broken things that could not be mended. All he had done was add one small falsehood—that he sought absolution from the throne instead of its destruction—and lies nested inside truths like worms in fruit, invisible until you bit down.

The pilgrim roads filled as he traveled east. Others walked beside him in ones and twos, then dozens, then a river of gray wool flowing toward the capital's gates. Word had spread through the free cities like fire through dry grass: the king had seen visions. The king had spoken of renewal. The king called the faithful home.

Anselm walked among them with his hood up and his heart hammering against his ribs like a prisoner against a cell door.

*They're coming because they felt it,* Beatrix had said, the night before they parted at the crossroads outside Harrowgate. She had been carving something small and fierce from a knucklebone, her knife moving in the lamplight. *Something in that palace is calling them. Like a bell. And bells don't ring for good news.*

*Then we should have talked them out of coming,* he'd said.

*You can't talk moths out of a flame, Anselm. You can only get there first and smother it.*

So here he was, first, or nearly. A moth dressed as a priest, walking into the flame with his eyes open.

* * *

The capital announced itself long before he saw it—not by its walls, which were famous, nor its spires, which were taller, but by its silence.

Anselm noticed it first as an absence in his ears, a pressure like descending into deep water. Then he realized what was missing: birdsong. For ten miles around the city, the hedgerows held no larks, the ditches no wrens. Crows circled the distant towers but did not land, wheeling in slow, uneasy orbits like flies around a wound. Even the wind seemed muted, as though the air itself had been taught manners.

And the people—the closer he came, the quieter they grew. Villages along the road spoke in murmurs. Children were hushed before they could finish laughing. In the last town before the gates, Anselm watched an innkeeper serve ale with hands that never quite stopped trembling, and when Anselm asked, gently, how the king fared, the man's eyes flicked to the corners of the room as if the walls might be listening.

*He is well,* the innkeeper said. *The king is always well.*

*Always?*

*The king does not age,* the man whispered, and then flinched as though the words had been torn out of him rather than given. *Forgive me. I—I've said nothing. You heard nothing.*

That night, Anselm wrote nothing in the journal hidden in the false bottom of his prayer book. He simply lay awake in the stable loft and listened to the silence pressing against the roof, and understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water poured down his spine, that fear here was not an emotion. It was an architecture. Ten years of it, laid stone by stone into every voice, every glance, every unfinished sentence. The usurper had not needed to garrison every town. He had built fear into the people themselves, and now they policed their own tongues more efficiently than any inquisitor ever could.

*This is what it feeds on,* he thought. *Not just memory. The silence where memory used to be.*

* * *

The gates of the capital took him without ceremony.

Penitent priests were waved through with barely a glance—the Ban had emptied the temples, but the Crown still found uses for holy men, if only as ornaments. Anselm kept his eyes lowered, his hands folded, his steps measured in the shuffling rhythm of a man worn down by long roads and longer guilt. Inside the walls, the silence deepened until it felt physical, a weight on the chest. The streets were clean. Too clean. No beggars—beggars remembered too much hunger, asked too many questions. No dogs. No music from the taverns, though they stood open and half-full, patrons drinking in the muted way of men at a funeral.

And everywhere, everywhere, the shrines.

They lined the avenues like teeth. Marble alcoves bearing the crowned profile of the king, each one fresh-cut, each one identical, each one flanked by a brazier in which something burned that gave off no smoke but a sweetish smell that clung to the back of the throat. Anselm passed a woman kneeling before one, lips moving in prayer, tears running silently down a face utterly devoid of feeling, as though the tears had been ordered and the grief forgotten on the way.

He stopped at one shrine under pretense of devotion and studied the face carved there. It was a good likeness—he had seen portraits of King Aldric in the old temple archives, back when such things were permitted. The strong jaw, the heavy brow, the scar through the left eyebrow earned at the Battle of the Fords. But the sculptors had gotten something subtly wrong, some essential proportion, and it took Anselm a long moment to identify it.

The eyes. They had carved the eyes too open. Not the eyes of a living man, who squints and narrows and softens. The eyes of a thing that has forgotten how to blink.

His skin crawled. He bowed to the shrine, murmured a prayer to gods he was no longer sure were listening, and moved on.

* * *

He had expected the palace audience to take weeks to arrange. It took four days.

That should have frightened him more than it did. In the old days, petitioning the Crown meant months of queues and bribes and intercessions. Now, it seemed, anyone the palace wished to see was seen quickly—and Anselm could not shake the certainty that he had not fought his way into the audience chamber so much as been *allowed*, drawn in like a fish on a line. Perhaps the crown-thing wanted priests. Perhaps it wanted to be worshipped by one. Perhaps it could smell what he was and found him amusing.

On the fourth morning, a chamberlain with wax-pale skin and eyes like unlit lamps led him up through the palace's heart, past guards in black lacquered armor whose faces never moved behind their visors, past windows through which the capital spread below in its terrible quiet, up stairs worn hollow by centuries of royal feet.

The doors of the great hall opened onto a vastness of marble and echo, and Anselm Oduya, who had faced inquisitors and buried friends and once held a dying girl while her prayers went unanswered, felt his courage gutter like a candle in wind.

The throne sat atop nine steps of white stone. Upon the throne sat the king.

Or the shape of him. From this distance, across the empty hall, the figure wore Aldric's face and Aldric's regalia, the iron-and-gold crown gleaming beneath the high windows' pale light. Robes of state draped a body that held itself with perfect, ceremonial stillness. Everything about it was composed, regal, correct.

Everything except that it did not move.

Anselm had seen statues breathe more. The chest did not rise. The fingers did not shift upon the armrests. As the chamberlain led him forward across the echoing floor, footfalls slapping loud as gunshots in the silence, Anselm searched desperately for some sign of life—a swallow, a twitch, the unconscious micro-motions that every living body makes—and found none. None at all. The thing on the throne was as still as the marble beneath it, and only the faintest shimmer of heat-haze around its outline suggested it was anything other than sculpture.

They stopped twenty feet away. Protocol, apparently, demanded even the illusion of distance.

"Your Majesty," the chamberlain intoned, "a penitent of the old rite, come to swear himself to the Crown."

Silence.

Then the king turned his head.

It was the single most horrible motion Anselm had ever witnessed. Not because it was fast—it was slow, glacially slow, the head rotating degree by degree with the smooth mechanical patience of a millstone—but because everything else remained motionless. The shoulders did not follow. The hands did not adjust. Only the head turned, on a neck that did not bend, until those carved-open eyes fixed upon Anselm with an attention that felt less like being looked at than being *weighed*.

"You are Oduya."

The voice was Aldric's voice—or had been, once. Now it sounded like a bell cracked down the middle: recognizable, resonant, wrong. Each word came out perfectly formed and perfectly dead, a recitation rather than speech.

"I am, Majesty." Anselm sank to his knees, bowing his head, buying time, steadying his breath. "I have come to confess my faithlessness and offer my service."

"Faithlessness." The head tilted, a fraction, studying him. "You helped write the Ban. We remember. We remember everyone who helped." A pause, long enough to drown in. "Why do you come back?"

Because I helped build the cage, Anselm thought, and now I mean to break it. Because I have watched children burn for praying. Because somewhere on a road west, a carver of bones and a god-touched child are betting their lives that I can do this.

"Because the gods are waking, Majesty," he said aloud, and let the words fall into the silence like stones into a well. "I have felt it. The people feel it. I thought... perhaps Your Majesty feels it too."

The silence that followed stretched so long that Anselm dared, finally, to raise his eyes.

And the thing on the throne blinked.

Once. Slowly. Both lids descending and rising with deliberate, effortful precision, like a man remembering an action he had read about in a book. And in that instant of closure and opening, Anselm saw—beneath the king's face, beneath the flesh, in the brief dark between blinks—something else looking out. Something vast and patient and terribly hungry, regarding him the way a man regards a meal that has walked willingly to the table.

Then the king's face returned, and the mouth smiled. It was a perfect smile. Every tooth accounted for. Nothing behind it.

"The gods are stories," the king said. "We have made certain of it. There are no gods. There is only the Crown, and the Crown provides." The head rotated back toward the hall's far end, degree by patient degree. "You will serve at the evening rites, priest. You will kneel where the others kneel. You will learn how the realm is kept."

"Yes, Majesty."

"You may rise."

Anselm rose. His knees ached as though he had knelt for hours rather than minutes. As he was escorted backward from the presence, head bowed, he counted his heartbeats and found them steady, and marveled at that, and feared it too—for a man who could stand before that thing and keep a calm pulse was either very brave or already partly devoured, and he genuinely did not know which he was.

* * *

The evening rites were held in the Hall of Remembrance, a vaulted chamber behind the throne room where the ceiling was painted with ten thousand faces—every soul the Crown had supposedly saved during the wars, rendered in fresco, gazing down in eternal gratitude.

Two hundred courtiers knelt in rows upon the cold marble. Anselm knelt among them, gray-robed, hood shadowing his face, and watched the ritual unfold with the eye of a man who had spent thirty years studying liturgy and knew, in his marrow, when worship had curdled into something else.

There was no hymn. There was no sermon. There was only the king, seated on a lesser throne at the chamber's heart, and the courtiers reciting in unison, in flat, toneless voices, the Litany of Provision:

*The Crown remembers for us. The Crown provides for us. What we forget, the Crown keeps safe. What we lose, the Crown restores. Blessed is the stillness. Blessed is the provision. Blessed is the king who does not age, does not hunger, does not sleep.*

Over and over, the litany cycled, and with each repetition Anselm felt something brush against his mind—faint as a moth's wing, delicate as a thief's fingers at a purse-string. Testing. Tasting. He clenched his thoughts around his oldest memories, the ones he had sworn to protect: Sefa's laugh—no, not Sefa, that was Beatrix's ghost, but he had met her, he had heard her voice; Wren's first laughter on the road, bright and startled, like water over stones; the taste of the bread he had shared with Beatrix at dawn outside Harrowgate, warm and salted and human.

The brushing stopped. Whatever moved through that chamber found nothing loose to take—or perhaps found his memories too freshly loved, too firmly gripped. But he saw the cost paid elsewhere. Look at the courtiers. Really look.

A woman two rows ahead had the fine bones of someone who had once been beautiful, but her face was smooth in the wrong way, unlined as a mask, and when she rose at the litany's end she paused, frowning faintly, as though trying to recall why she had come. A young man nearby mouthed the words of the litany without sound, his lips moving in perfect time, his eyes vacant as glass. And throughout the chamber, Anselm noticed the small telltale signs he would otherwise have missed: courtiers who could not name their own children without pausing; a lord who wore a wedding band but stared at it, periodically, with the baffled expression of a man who has found an artifact of an unknown civilization; servants who moved through the rituals with the automatic grace of sleepwalkers.

They were being grazed. Slowly, gently, so gradually none of them could mark the loss—they were being eaten alive, memory by memory, and every evening they came back for more, kneeling in rows, offering themselves up with thanks.

And the king—the thing wearing the king—sat among them, and did not eat, and did not drink, and did not blink, and Anselm understood.

*Dread sharpened into clarity*, Beatrix had written to him once, in a letter about her craft: *the moment the horror stops being shapeless and becomes a mechanism.* Here it was. The mechanism entire.

The crown-thing was weak.

Oh, it was terrible—vast, patient, hungry beyond reckoning. But it was also, in the way of all parasites, dependent. It could not hunt. It could only receive. It needed the rites, needed the litany, needed two hundred willing throats opening themselves night after night. It needed the silence, the fear-architecture, the shrines on every corner feeding it the small daily surrenders of a cowed populace. Strip those away—one city that refused to kneel, one litany left unsaid, one generation raised to remember loudly—and the thing would starve. It was not a god. It was a tapeworm in the gut of a nation, grown fat on ten years of forced fasting, and it lived or died by the consent—manufactured, terrified, but consent nonetheless—of its hosts.

Anselm knelt in the flickering light of the Hall of Remembrance, surrounded by the slowly devoured, and felt something kindle in his chest that he had not felt since before the Ban: not hope exactly, but its rough draft. Purpose, maybe. The old fire, banked low these ten years, catching at last at dry tinder.

*We don't need to kill it,* he thought, watching the unblinking king receive the litany like a man drinking from a bottomless cup. *We need to make the realm stop feeding it. One unsaid prayer at a time. One remembered name at a time. And underneath the palace—*

Underneath the palace. Where Beatrix and Wren would come, following the river-god's passage, following the bone-voice that had waited ten years for someone to listen. Where the true king lay, whatever remained of him, anchored in bone and stolen years.

Anselm closed his eyes and prayed—not to the Crown, not to the litany, but to the old gods, quietly, in the manner Wren had taught him on the road: *safely, small, like setting a candle in a window.* *Let me find the way down. Let me be worthy of the company I keep. Let me do one thing right, at the end, after all the wrong.*

When he opened his eyes, the king was looking at him.

Across the chamber, above the bowed heads of two hundred kneeling courtiers, the crowned thing had turned its head again—that slow, millstone rotation—and its carved-open eyes were fixed on Anselm with an expression that was almost, almost curiosity. Its lips parted. The cracked-bell voice carried impossibly across the silent hall, quiet and clear as a thought inserted directly into the skull:

*"Priest. You pray wrong."*

The litany continued around him, toneless, obedient. The braziers burned their smokeless sweetness. And Anselm Oduya, kneeling in the house of the enemy with his heart going like a war drum, understood that the game had changed—that the thing knew, or suspected, or was playing with him as a cat plays, and that somewhere beneath the marble, a dead king was waiting to learn whether the priest who broke the world could help unbreak it.

He bowed his head lower, and began, softly, to pray *right*.
