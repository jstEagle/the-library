## 13. Gods in the Gutter

The capital had a smell Beatrix hadn't expected: old smoke and older salt, tallow and wet stone, the particular sourness of ten thousand people pretending not to be afraid. Vellamare's fear had been a quiet thing, folded into daily life like a knife wrapped in cloth. Here the fear was loud. It hung from the eaves of the tenement rows in the form of new proclamations pasted over old ones, each layer praising the Crown's eternal vigilance, each one curling at the edges where the rain got at them.

She kept her hood up and her hands in her pockets, where her fingers found the bone charms strung along her wrist without her asking them to. They were quiet today. That was its own kind of warning. In the charnel house they had sung; on the road east they had murmured like a congregation settling into pews. Now they were silent as stones, and Beatrix understood why before she could name it: the dead did not like this city either.

"Keep close," Anselm murmured at her shoulder. He had traded his priest's collar for a laborer's coat two towns back, and he wore the disguise the way he seemed to wear everything now—with a kind of deliberate humility that was either genuine penance or very good acting. She still hadn't decided. "The Wardens patrol these streets in pairs after dusk. And the slums have their own eyes."

"The slums' eyes are for sale," Beatrix said. "That's what makes them honest."

Wren laughed—a small, startled sound, quickly smothered—and Beatrix felt it land somewhere beneath her ribs. Eleven days since Sefa burned out at the ford. Nine days since Beatrix had stopped crying about it in front of the others. The grief sat in her chest like a stone she'd swallowed whole, and every time she thought she'd grown around it, she shifted wrong and felt its edges again.

They moved down through the city the way water moves down through sand—finding the cracks, following them. The palace district gleamed above on its hill, white walls and gilded spires, and below it the city fell away in terraces of gray slate roofs and leaning timber, until at the bottom there was only the Gutter, which was what everyone called it, because no one who named things here had ever been interested in kindness.

It was in the Gutter that Beatrix began to feel it.

At first she took it for exhaustion—the low hum behind her eyes that came from too many nights sleeping in doorways. But the hum had a direction. It pulled. Not toward the palace, where their purpose lay coiled like a snake under the throne. Sideways. Down. Toward the river wall, where the poorest of the poor stacked their lives against the flood line.

"There," Wren said suddenly, and stopped so hard that a fishwife swore at them and shuffled past with her basket held high.

Beatrix followed the girl's gaze to a stretch of wall between a dyer's shop and a shuttered alehouse. It was nothing. Cracked plaster, old brick, a stain of damp shaped like nothing at all. Except—

Except the stain was moving. Slowly. The way breath moves a curtain.

"Do you see it?" Wren whispered. Her hand had found Beatrix's sleeve and gripped it. "In the wall. There's a face."

Anselm looked too, and Beatrix watched his expression do something complicated—watched the priest and the penitent fight for control of his mouth. "I see brick," he said carefully. "And I see that we should not be standing here staring at brick."

But Beatrix saw it. Of course she saw it. A face pressed against the inside of the wall the way a child presses against a window, watching the street it is not allowed to join. The features were soft, unfinished, more suggestion than certainty—but the longing in it was as clear as a shout.

"It's a shrine," she said. "Or it was." She stepped closer despite Anselm's hissed protest, laid her palm flat against the plaster. It was warm. Warm as skin. Beneath her hand, faint as a heartbeat heard through a floor, something pulsed.

*Oh,* said a voice—not aloud, not in her ears, but in the place behind her sternum where the dead spoke to her. *Oh, someone feels me.*

Beatrix snatched her hand back as if scalded.

Not a ghost. She knew ghosts the way she knew her own hands—she had spent ten years among them, carving their last words into bone, carrying their small griefs and smaller jokes. Ghosts were echoes. This was not an echo. This was something *present*, something aware and awake and starving, and it had been waiting inside a wall in the worst neighborhood of the capital for longer than the Ban had lasted.

"What is it?" Anselm had moved to her side, one hand hovering near her elbow, ready to catch or steer. "What did you touch?"

"A god," Beatrix said, and heard how absurd it sounded, and saw that neither of them laughed. "A small one. A forgotten one. It's—" She searched for the word and found it with a chill. "*Awake.*"

* * *

They spent the rest of the day finding more.

Once Beatrix knew what to feel for, the Gutter lit up around her like a drowned city seen through clear water. A well in a courtyard off Rope Lane, its bucket rope worn to fraying silk, held a presence that tasted of cold iron and patience. A niche above a baker's door, empty for decades except for pigeon droppings, had begun—very faintly—to glow at dawn, a warmth like the first minute of sunrise trapped in stone. Under a bridge where the tide pooled and stank, three children were playing a game that involved pressing their foreheads to a mossy pillar and giggling, and when Beatrix passed close she heard the pillar humming a lullaby in a language dead five hundred years.

"They're coming back everywhere," she told Anselm and Wren in the rented room they could barely afford—a closet above a chandler's, smelling of tallow and mouse. She sat on the floor with her back to the wall because the chair had three legs and opinions. "Not just the great gods. Not the ones the Ban was written for. The little ones. Household gods. Well-gods. Gods of thresholds and drains and the hour before dawn. Things too small to bother outlawing."

"And no one noticed," Anselm said slowly, "because no one prays to them anymore. No one remembers their names."

"But someone must have started," Wren said. She was sitting cross-legged on the bed, knees drawn up, watching Beatrix with those eyes that were too old for her face. "Gods don't just wake up. Someone has to call them. That's what you taught me. Prayer is a door, and doors need both sides."

Beatrix looked at her. "You think someone in the Gutter has been praying."

"I think people pray all the time even when it's forbidden." Wren shrugged, a small, bitter motion she had learned somewhere before Beatrix found her. "People in places like this always pray. They just do it quietly, where the priests can't hear. Maybe the gods couldn't answer for ten years. But they could *listen*. And listening piles up. Like snow." She spread her small hands. "Enough snow, and something breaks through."

Silence settled over the room. Outside, a Warden's whistle shrilled twice and faded, and all three of them flinched in the same instant—an ugly little choreography they had perfected on the road.

"The Ban didn't kill the gods," Anselm said at last, and his voice had the raw edge it got when he talked about his old work. "I helped enforce it. I know what it killed. It killed the *speaking*. The temples, the rites, the trained voices. But prayer—" He shook his head. "Prayer is older than priesthood. You can't burn it out of people. We tried. I tried." His hands were knotted together in his lap. "I told myself we were cutting away rot. All this time, I think we were just teaching people to whisper."

* * *

It was Wren who found the shrine that mattered, two days later, and she found it by falling down.

They had gone out at gray dawn to buy bread with coins counted three times, and Wren, darting ahead as she did when the city frightened her less than standing still did, caught her heel on a broken step and went sprawling into an alley mouth. By the time Beatrix reached her the girl was sitting up, bread miraculously saved, but she wasn't looking at her scraped palms. She was looking at the wall she'd fallen against.

"Beatrix," she said. "Come look. Come *listen*."

The alley was narrow enough that Beatrix's shoulders brushed both walls. At its end, where the buildings leaned together like conspirators, there was a hollow in the masonry—a shallow alcove, maybe the height of a hand span, half-filled with ash and rainwater and the curled husks of old offerings. Someone had tended this once. Someone had left flowers here, years ago, and wax, and a child's clay figure with its arms broken off.

And it was burning.

Not with fire. With attention. The little alcove glowed the way a coal glows when breathed upon, and the air above it shimmered, and out of the shimmer came a voice like wind through winter reeds.

*Who walks? Who falls? Who bleeds on my doorstep?*

Beatrix went to her knees beside Wren without deciding to. This close, the presence filled the alley like water fills a cup. It was old and small and patient and *hungry*—hungry the way a dog is hungry after years of thin meals, hopeful and ashamed of hoping.

"We're travelers," Beatrix said quietly. "We didn't mean to disturb you."

*Disturb.* The voice turned the word over like a coin of doubtful metal. *No one has disturbed me in thirty years. Before that, forty. I am the god of this alley. I was the god of safe passage through it, of the cutpurse turning aside, of the drunk finding his way home. My name—* a pause, a ripple of something like embarrassment *—my name I have almost lost. Names are the first thing to go.*

"You're awake," Beatrix said. "After the Ban. After ten years of silence. How?"

*Little prayers,* the god said. *Whispered ones. A mother here, a thief there. I fed on crumbs for a decade. And then—* the glow brightened, and Beatrix felt the whole Gutter shift around her, dozens of small attentions stirring in walls and wells and bridge-stones, like embers all fanned by the same wind *—and then the doors opened. Something tore loose in the world, and everything we had swallowed came back up. Now we are awake. Now we are *starving.*

"What do you want?" Anselm asked from the alley mouth, where he stood watch, though his eyes kept returning to the glow.

*What any beggar wants. To be useful again. To be paid.* The god's voice gentled, and that gentleness was the most frightening thing about it. *I can help you, bone-carver. I know why you've come to this city—I taste your purpose on you like smoke in wool. You walk toward the palace and the crowned hunger, and you walk blind. I can show you the ways beneath the streets. I can tell you which of the Wardens' eyes are bought and by whom. I can keep your sleep dreamless and your footsteps silent.*

Beatrix's heart was beating hard. "And the price?"

*Ah.* A long pause. The glow dimmed, brightened, dimmed, like breathing. *Memory. Small ones, for small services. A face you barely knew. The taste of a meal. The color of a room you slept in once. We are made of remembering, little carver—it is the only coin we can eat. The great gods take blood and harvest. We take only what you will not miss.*

*Only what you will not miss.*

Beatrix heard the words and felt them slide into her like a key into a lock she hadn't known was there.

Because she had a stock of memories she would give anything not to carry. The ford. The light going out of Sefa. The sound the ghost had made—not a scream, worse, a *willingness*—as she poured herself out against the soldiers' shields. Eleven days of walking with that playing behind her eyes every time she closed them. If some gutter-god would reach into her chest and lift it out—

"How much," she heard herself say, "for the largest thing you can do?"

"*Beatrix.*" Anselm's voice, sharp.

But the god had heard. The glow swelled, eager, and she felt its attention wrap around her like warm water, and oh, it would be easy, it would be so *easy*—

*I could take the burning day from you,* the god murmured. *The ford, the loss, the ghost who chose. I could make it a smooth blank place where a wound used to be. You would walk lighter. You would sleep. And you would not remember what you paid, so the price would never hurt you at all.*

"That's not a price," Wren said suddenly. Her voice cracked on it. "That's—that's just losing more. That's letting it win twice."

"Wren—" Beatrix began.

"No!" The girl grabbed her wrist, hard, her small fingers digging in. "Listen to me. When my village burned, I prayed to forget. I prayed and prayed and a *thing* answered, and it took pieces of me I never agreed to, and I have holes in me, Beatrix, holes where I can feel something *should* be, and they ache worse than the memories ever did." Her eyes were blazing, wet. "You taught me that. You said the dead deserve to be carried, not dropped. You don't get to drop *her*."

The alley was very quiet. Somewhere above, a shutter opened and banged closed. The god waited, glowing, patient as stone, and Beatrix knelt between them with her heart tearing neatly down the middle.

Because Wren was right, and Beatrix hated her for it, and loved her for it, and knew—if she was honest, if she was truly honest in the dark of herself—that she had known it before she asked. The temptation had never really been relief. It had been permission. Permission to set the weight down and let someone else carry the story. Permission to walk into the palace unburdened and come out the other side having lost nothing, having risked nothing that mattered, having *kept walking away from all this* while calling it strategy.

Sefa had died holding a ford so that Beatrix could reach this city. Forgetting her wouldn't honor that. Forgetting her would just be the second death, the one the crown-thing wanted, the slow devouring that started with grief and ended with obedience.

"No," Beatrix said. Her voice came out rough. She cleared her throat and said it again, steadier, to the glowing alcove. "No. Thank you. Keep your price."

The glow flickered—disappointment, or perhaps respect; with gods it was hard to tell. *Few refuse,* it said. *Fewer refuse twice.*

"Then remember that I did." Beatrix rose, knees aching, and looked down at the little god of the little alley. "But I'll make you another offer. When this is over—when the crown-thing is dust and the Ban is broken—someone is going to come back to this city and speak your name back to you. Someone is going to leave flowers in your alcove again. Help us now, freely, and I swear it on my craft and my bones."

Silence. Then the reed-voice laughed, a dry rustling sound, astonished.

*A bargain with no coin,* it said. *How very like the old days. Very well, bone-carver. Listen, then, and listen closely—for the ways beneath the palace are older than the palace, and there is a door under the river that the kings sealed and forgot. And you should hurry. Because you are not the only ones the small gods are feeding.*

Beatrix went cold. "What do you mean?"

*Others have paid,* the god said, and its glow guttered low, like a candle in a draft. *This very night. A child came to the well-shrine on Rope Lane with tears on her face and offered the sweetest memory she had—one of love, bone-carver, one of *family*—and the well-god took it, because a price offered in love cannot be refused. She walks toward the same door you seek. And she no longer remembers why she loves the people she is walking with.*

Behind Beatrix, Wren made a small sound.

Beatrix turned, and the girl's face had gone white as carved bone.

"That was hours ago," Wren whispered. "Beatrix—the well on Rope Lane—I passed it on my way back from the bakery. I stopped. I *talked* to it." Her hand rose slowly to her own chest, fingers splayed, as if checking that everything was still there. "I don't remember what I said."
