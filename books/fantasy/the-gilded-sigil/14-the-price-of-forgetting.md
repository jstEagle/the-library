## 14. The Price of Forgetting

The river beneath the capital had no name on any map. It ran through the roots of the palace like a vein through a wrist, black and slow and older than the stones that had been built over it, and the small god who lived in it was old enough to remember when the first foundation blocks were laid—old enough, Beatrix suspected, to remember being worshipped properly.

They found its shrine in a maintenance tunnel two levels below the aqueducts, a niche in the wall where someone centuries dead had set a basin of river stone and carved a face into the rock: not a human face, but something like one, all smooth planes and no mouth, with eyes that were only deep grooves where water gathered and dripped. Someone had left offerings once—dried flowers gone to dust, a child's clay boat, coins fused green with age. The drips from the ceiling fell into the basin at the rhythm of a heartbeat.

Wren stood before it with her hands clasped the way Anselm had taught her, fingers laced, thumbs crossed, the old posture of petition. She was eleven years old and up to her ankles in cold water, and she was talking to it the way she talked to everything now—earnestly, fearlessly, as if divinity were simply another neighbor you could knock on the door of.

"I need us to go under the palace," she said. "There's a door in the river wall. Sefa—" She stopped. Beatrix saw her stop, saw the small flinch, because Sefa was gone now, burned out at the ford, and Wren still reached for her every day the way a tongue reaches for a missing tooth. "There's a door. The king is under there. We have to get to him before the armies break the gates, because if the crown-thing feels him waking, it'll move him, or kill him, or worse."

The water in the basin shivered.

Beatrix felt the god attend. There was no other word for it. The dripping changed tempo, quickened, and the air in the tunnel went cool and mineral, tasting of deep places, and along the walls the damp began to glow faintly, a phosphorescent shimmer like fish-scale light. She had been finding these shrines for days now—waking in wells, in wall-cavities, in the mouths of storm drains—and each time the same vertigo: the city was honeycombed with gods, sleeping for ten years under the Ban, and they were all awake now, and they were all *hungry*.

"What do you want for it?" Wren asked. She'd learned that much already. Gods didn't give; they traded. "I don't have any money. I have—" she patted her pockets, produced a bent iron nail, half a heel of bread wrapped in cloth, and the bone charm Beatrix had carved for her, the little sparrow shape that hung at her throat. "Not this. Not the sparrow. But I've got other things."

The light in the grooves of the god's eyes brightened.

"No," Beatrix said.

It came out flat and hard, harder than she'd meant, and Wren turned, startled, and Anselm looked up sharply from where he'd been keeping watch at the tunnel mouth, his hand going to the knife he still carried out of habit though he'd sworn off violence years ago.

"Beatrix—"

"It wants memories." She stepped forward, boots splashing, and took Wren by the shoulders. Up close, in the fish-scale light, the girl's face was thin and smudged and absolutely certain of herself, and that certainty frightened Beatrix more than anything the inquisitors could do. "Look at its eyes. Look how they lit up. It wasn't listening to your prayer, love. It was listening to what you're carrying."

"But I *want* to give it something," Wren said, and her voice had that stubborn edge Beatrix knew too well, the edge that had flooded a harbor against an enemy fleet. "It's just one memory. I've got loads. I'd give it the one about the orphanage kitchen—the porridge, the smell of it, I hate that one anyway, it makes my stomach hurt. Then we get under the palace and save the king and nobody has to know."

"You'd know," Beatrix said. "That's the whole horror of it. You wouldn't know."

She heard her own voice crack on the last word and hated it, hated the way both of them were looking at her now—Anselm coming slowly down the tunnel, water sounds swallowing his footsteps, his face doing the careful thing he did when he was trying not to intrude.

"Beatrix," he said gently. "Perhaps let her—"

"No." She hadn't let go of Wren's shoulders. Her hands were shaking. She could feel the god watching them, patient as water, patient as stone, and she understood suddenly that it would wait forever, that patience was the oldest thing about it. "You don't trade pieces of yourself to get into buildings. You don't—you don't pay for doors with *yourself*, Wren. You hear me? Whatever's behind a door isn't worth—"

"Then why do you know?" Wren asked.

The question landed like a dropped stone.

Wren's eyes were wide and unflinching, the terrible honesty of children, of god-touched children most of all. "You said *you'd know*. Like you know. Like it happened to you."

The dripping filled the silence. Drip. Drip. Drip. Somewhere far above, muffled by feet of stone, the capital went on with its occupied evening—boots on cobbles, a curfew bell, the distant hymn-singing the inquisitors made the districts perform at dusk, ten thousand voices praising a thing that never blinked.

"There's a year I can't account for," Beatrix said finally.

Anselm went very still.

"When I was nineteen. Maybe twenty. I know where I was living—I had a room above a tannery on Salt Lane, I remember the smell, I remember the landlady. And then I know where I was at twenty-one, different room, different street, and between those two points there's a gap like a page torn out of a book. A year. Maybe more." She made herself keep looking at Wren while she said it. "I don't know what I gave away. I don't know who I gave it to. I woke up one morning with coin I couldn't remember earning and a scar on my palm I couldn't remember getting, and a feeling—" Her voice caught. She pushed through it. "A feeling like homesickness, but for a place I couldn't name. Like grief with no corpse attached. It never went away. Ten years, and it never went away. I just got better at building my life around the hole."

The glow in the god's eyes had dimmed slightly, the way a fire dims when it's fed. Beatrix wondered if it recognized her. Wondered if it remembered her better than she remembered herself. The thought made her skin crawl.

"That's why your charms scream sometimes," Anselm said quietly. It wasn't a question. He'd told her once, back in Vellamare, that her gift had a wound in it, a place where the hearing went strange. "The hole. The dead pass near it and it pulls at them."

"Maybe. I don't know. That's the point, Anselm. I don't *know*. I've spent ten years not knowing, and I will spend the rest of my life not knowing, and every so often I find something—a song I somehow know all the words to, a street I flinch at without knowing why—and I understand that whoever I was before, whatever I lost, she's still in me somewhere, screaming, and I can't even hear her properly." She laughed, and it came out wrong, wet and short. "And you want to volunteer. At eleven. Because the porridge memory is inconvenient."

Wren's chin came up. "I'm not a baby."

"No," Beatrix agreed. "You're not. That's what scares me. A baby would cry and run. You'd stand here and make the trade and thank the god afterward, and you'd walk into the palace missing a piece, and you'd never miss it, and that's the cruelest trick of all. It doesn't hurt, giving it away. That's what nobody tells you. The god takes the memory and the *missing* goes with it. All that's left is the hole, and holes don't hurt. They just leak."

She let go of Wren's shoulders. Stepped back. Made herself look at the shrine—at the smooth faceless thing in the rock, the patient gathering water, the ancient hunger wearing the shape of helpfulness.

"And I'm asking you," she said, and her voice steadied, found ground, "as the person who leads this ridiculous expedition, and as the person who loves you: don't. Not for a door. Not for anything less than everything. Promise me."

Wren looked at her for a long moment. Then at Anselm, who said nothing, only nodded once, gravely, the way he did when he was letting someone arrive at their own conclusion—which was, Beatrix had learned, both his greatest virtue and his most infuriating habit.

"Okay," Wren said. "Okay. I promise." And then, because she was Wren: "But then how do we pay?"

Beatrix turned back to the shrine. The basin brimmed now, trembling, overflowing in a thin silver sheet that ran down the wall and joined the black river without a ripple. The god waited. They always waited. Hunger was patient; that was the whole architecture of it.

"We negotiate," she said. "That's what it means to deal with gods instead of feeding them. You offer what you can spare, not what you can't. You set terms. You make them take less than they ask for." She rolled up her sleeves, waded closer, and held her palms over the water the way she held them over fresh-carved bone. "My mother taught me bargaining in the fish market before she taught me letters. Let's see if it works on something older than the palace."

The light in the god's eye-grooves flickered—amusement, or offense, or simple surprise. Perhaps nothing had haggled with it in a thousand years.

"I'll give it names," Beatrix said to the water, to the dark, to the listening deep. "Dead names. Names of the forgotten dead of this city, the ones the Ban erased, the ones whose graves were paved over. I'll carve them into the riverbank where your water touches, so they're spoken every time you rise. Memory—but not mine. Not hers. Memory that belongs to everyone, given freely, kept forever."

The dripping stopped.

For three heartbeats the whole tunnel held its breath—the black river, the glowing walls, the child with her sparrow charm, the priest with his careful face. Then the water in the basin sank, slowly, drawing down like a lung emptying, and in the stone of the wall beside the niche, a seam appeared where no seam had been: a hairline crack in the shape of a doorway, breathing cold air that smelled of the river's deepest channel and, faintly, unmistakably, of bone.

"The way is open," Anselm murmured. "Gods preserve us."

"It will," Beatrix said grimly, shouldering her pack. "That's rather the problem."

Wren slipped her hand into Beatrix's as they stepped toward the seam, and Beatrix squeezed it and did not let go, and neither of them mentioned that below them, past the water and the dark, something crowned and hollow sat upon a throne, and something else—something murdered and patient—lay waiting in bone for the sound of carving knives.

The crack in the wall widened as they approached, groaning like a ship's hull, and beyond it the passage sloped down into a darkness that the god's light did not follow.

"Stay close," Beatrix whispered. "And whatever you hear down there—whatever voices—it hears promises better than prayers. So make none you don't mean."

The dark swallowed them one by one.
