## 15. The King Beneath

The stair went down farther than any stair had a right to go.

Anselm Oduya counted the steps because counting was a prayer, and prayer was the only thing he had left that still worked. Four hundred and eleven steps, spiraling into the dark beneath the palace of Vellamare's conquerors, each one worn smooth at its center by feet that had climbed and descended for centuries before the Ban. His candle guttered in air that tasted of stone and old salt and something else—something sweetish and wrong, like fruit rotting behind a locked door.

Behind him, Beatrix Zhou moved with the silence of a woman who had spent ten years teaching herself not to exist. Wren came after her, small hand hooked in the hem of Beatrix's coat, and the girl was humming. Not a tune Anselm recognized—a thread of sound without melody, the kind of hum a child made when something vast was listening through her and she was trying to pretend it wasn't.

"Stop that," Beatrix said softly.

"I'm not doing it." Wren's voice was thin. "It's doing me."

Anselm turned his head. "Let her hum," he said. "If the gods are speaking through her down here, I'd rather know."

Beatrix shot him a look he had learned to read over these weeks on the road: *you are a fool, priest, but you are my fool.* She said nothing. The hum continued, low and wavering, and the walls seemed to drink it in.

At the bottom of the stair, the passage opened into a vaulted chamber so large that his candlelight could not find its far wall. Pillars rose around them like the legs of drowned giants, and between them lay rows upon rows of sarcophagi—kings, queens, the old dead of the realm sleeping in their marble beds. Dust lay thick as snowfall. No one had mourned here in a decade.

But at the chamber's heart stood something newer. A cage of black iron, tall as three men, and within it—

"Oh," whispered Wren, and stopped humming.

Within it lay a king.

He was preserved in bone—not bone as Beatrix carved it, not the small white tokens she strung on leather cords, but bone grown over him like coral, like frost, like a second skeleton built outside his flesh. It sheathed him from crown to heel, translucent in places, and through those translucent places Anselm could see skin, withered but unbroken. Chest rising. Falling. Rising again, so slowly that he might have imagined it.

"He's alive," Beatrix breathed. She was already moving forward, fingers hovering over the iron. "Gods below. He's been alive this whole time."

"Anchor," Wren said. The word came out of her flat and strange, the way words came out of her when they weren't entirely hers. "The crown needs an anchor or it drifts. You can't wear a dead man forever. It has to keep the man barely alive, just enough, just enough—" She shuddered and clapped both hands over her mouth.

Anselm felt the old shame rise in him, familiar now as his own heartbeat. Ten years ago he had stood in halls of marble very much like these and read the decrees of the Ban aloud in his clear, carrying voice. *Magic is a wound in the world. Magic is theft from the living.* He had believed it. He had helped make a country where a woman like Beatrix had to carve her gift into hiding places no bigger than a knucklebone, where a child like Wren could be burned out of her home for praying too honestly.

And all the while, beneath everything, this. A murdered king, kept breathing the way a farmer keeps a hen laying—just enough life, never enough freedom.

"We have to get him out of the cage," Beatrix said. Her hands were shaking. "Can we—Wren, can you ask the lock anything? Anselm, there has to be a rite—"

"There is." His mouth was dry. "There are several. And every one of them is forbidden."

She looked at him. In the candlelight her face was all sharp planes and tired eyes, and he saw that she already understood what he was about to say, and was waiting for him to say it anyway. That was its own kind of mercy. Beatrix never let anyone lie their way past a hard thing.

"The Rite of the Stilled Tongue," he said. "It doesn't free a soul. It holds it steady—pins it in place against whatever is pulling at it. The old faith used it for the dying whose souls were being contested. Demons, mostly. Hungry things." He swallowed. "I performed it twice, before the Ban. Both times under supervision. Never alone. Never on a soul this old, this damaged, this—anchored to something that will fight back."

"Then we do it together," Beatrix said simply. "You say the words. We hold the line."

*We.* The word landed in his chest like a coal. For ten years he had done penance alone—fasting, flagellation, the long gray disciplines of a man trying to scrub himself clean by himself, in himself, for himself. It had never once occurred to him that absolution might be something other people could help carry.

"Yes," he said. "All right. Yes."

* * *

Breaking the cage took less time than he feared. Wren laid both palms against the iron and spoke a word in no language Anselm knew, and the lock simply forgot how to be a lock—it hung open, bewildered, its purpose dissolved. Beatrix pried the bars apart with a crowbar and a strength that seemed borrowed from somewhere, and then the king lay before them on his bed of grown bone, breathing his slow, terrible breath.

Up close, Anselm could see his face through the thinner plates of the growth. He had seen the king once, years ago, at a distance—from a reviewing stand, a golden figure, a wave of a gloved hand. This face was the same face starved down to its essentials. Cheeks hollowed. Lips cracked. Eyelids sunken but sealed, as if even sleep had been rationed to him.

"How long?" Beatrix asked quietly. "How long has he been like this?"

"Since the beginning," Anselm said. "Whatever wears his face upstairs has been feeding on him ever since. Slowly. The way you'd feed on a well, not a meal." He set down his satchel and began unpacking with hands that, to his surprise, were steady. Chalk. Oil. The little brass censer he had carried through disgrace and exile and burning cities, though he'd told himself for years it was only habit. "Help me turn him gently. We need his sternum bare."

They worked in silence, the three of them, and it struck Anselm that this was the first honest work he had done in a decade. Everything else—the sermons, the inquisitions, the gray penitent's robe he wore now—had been performance, even the penance. Especially the penance. But kneeling in the dust of dead kings, baring the chest of a murdered man so his soul could be steadied—this was real. This would cost something. He did not yet know what, and he found, examining himself, that he no longer cared.

He drew the circle in chalk around the king's body, wide enough for all four of them. He poured oil along its rim and lit it, and blue flame ran the ring's circumference, low and steady, and the darkness of the crypt seemed to lean back from it.

"Wren. Inside the circle, at his head. Whatever you hear, don't answer it unless it's him. Beatrix—at his side. Your charms will scream. Let them. They're telling the truth; the truth is supposed to hurt." He took a breath. "When I begin, I cannot stop. Whatever happens, whatever comes out of the dark to argue with me—I finish the rite or the king's soul tears loose, and then there is nothing left to save. Do you understand?"

"Yes," said Beatrix.

"No," said Wren, honestly, and then: "But I'll do it anyway."

Anselm almost smiled. He laid his palms flat on the bone-grown chest, feeling the faint, appalling flutter of a heartbeat underneath—slow as a funeral drum—and began.

* * *

The words of the Rite of the Stilled Tongue were older than the Ban, older than the kingdom, older than the language he preached in. They came up out of him in the old tongue, syllable by syllable, and with each one he felt the crypt respond—the dust stirring, the pillars listening, the dark beyond the candlelight gathering itself like a held breath.

The king's soul was easy to find. It was everywhere and nowhere—threaded through the bone-growth, pooled in the hollow of the throat, flickering in the ruined chambers of the heart like a lamp about to go out. And it was frayed. Anselm had steadied dying souls before, and they had been whole things, merely slipping their moorings. This soul had been *eaten*. Great tracts of it were simply gone, chewed away across years of slow feeding, and what remained clung to the body the way ivy clung to a ruin.

"Hold," he murmured, in the old tongue. "Hold. You are found. You are named. Hold."

The soul stirred toward his voice like a drowning man toward light, and that was when the crown-thing noticed them.

It came not as a presence but as an absence—a cold that moved through the crypt, snuffing nothing, changing nothing, only making everything mean less. Anselm felt it press against the circle of blue flame and heard Wren gasp. The chalk lines held. But a voice came with the cold, and it used the king's own voice, warm and reasonable and terribly tired.

*Priest. You are wasting your time. There is hardly anything left of him. Look at it—half-eaten, half-mad. What you're pinning down isn't a king. It's leftovers.*

Anselm kept his palms on the bone and kept speaking. *Hold. You are found.*

*You were one of ours once,* the voice said, gentler now, insinuating itself under his words like water under a door. *You read the decrees so beautifully. Such a clear voice. You understood order. You understood that some things must be fed so that others may be safe. Come back upstairs. Kneel. It doesn't hurt, after the first century.*

"You mistake me," Anselm said aloud, in his clear carrying voice, the voice that had once filled cathedrals, "for someone who negotiates."

He pressed harder into the rite. The words came faster now, and the soul of the king gathered toward them, threading itself along the sound of his voice like beads onto a string. He could feel the crown-thing pulling from above—patient, immense, a hunger the size of a dynasty—and he planted himself against it the way he had once planted himself against reformers and heretics and every other thing he had been certain was wrong.

That certainty had been poison. This one was bread. He knew the difference now. Certainty in service of a hungry thing versus certainty in service of a starving man. He had spent ten years confusing the two, and he would spend the rest of his life making up the difference, one rite, one kindness, one stilled tongue at a time.

*Hold,* he said. *You are found. You are named. You are Aldric, son of Maren, crowned in the year of the twin comets, and you are not leftovers. You are a man. Hold.*

The soul flared.

For one blinding moment the whole crypt lit with it—every sarcophagus glowing faintly, every pillar throwing shadows upward instead of down—and Anselm heard, distantly, from far above, a sound like a scream made of static and fury. The crown-thing recoiled. The cold withdrew up the stair, dragging its absence with it, and the blue ring of flame settled, and the king's heartbeat beneath Anselm's palms steadied into something almost human.

Not healed. Not freed. But *held*. Pinned against the pull, anchored to his own name, stable enough to survive what came next.

Anselm sat back on his heels. His arms trembled. Sweat had cut channels through the dust on his face, and when he tried to speak, his voice came out in ruins.

"It's done. He'll hold. Until—" He looked up at Beatrix. "Until you do your part. The unbinding. He's steady enough now for you to work on him. But Beatrix—" He caught her wrist. "The rite bought time, not victory. When you cut him loose from the crown, that thing will feel it. All of it. Every moment you spend carving, it will be fighting you. And the rite demands—"

"I know what it demands," Beatrix said quietly.

She did not look at Wren. Wren was asleep, curled against the king's bone-grown shoulder like a kitten against a warm wall, worn out from holding the circle's far edge with her small fierce mind. In the blue light she looked impossibly young.

Anselm rose, knees cracking, and gathered the exhausted girl into his arms. Above them, miles up, the usurper's court sang its endless hymns to a thing that did not blink, and the armies of the hollow king were even now closing on the free cities like a fist.

"Rest," he said to Beatrix. "Tonight, rest. Tomorrow we begin."

But as they made camp among the dead kings, Anselm stayed awake a while longer, watching the slow rise and fall of a murdered man's chest, and he understood at last what redemption actually was. Not the scrubbing away of sin. Not the gray robe, not the fast, not the decade of solitary grief. It was this: dirty, kneeling, useful. Hands on the wound. Words in the dark. Work that cost, done beside people who knew what it cost.

He had spent ten years asking to be forgiven.

Tonight, for the first time, he had simply gotten up and done the thing forgiveness was for.

Above them, faint through fathoms of stone, the palace bells began to toll—and did not stop.
