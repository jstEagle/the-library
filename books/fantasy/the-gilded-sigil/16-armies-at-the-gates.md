## 16. Armies at the Gates

Wren had never seen so many people in one place, and she wished with her whole aching heart that they were anywhere else.

They came over the eastern hills at dawn, the usurper's host, and for a long moment it did not look like an army at all. It looked like weather. A gray front rolling toward Harrowgate, swallowing the fields, the hedgerows, the little farmsteads that had emptied days ago. Banners hung limp and colorless above it—no sigils Wren recognized, no gods, just the crowned circle of the Hollow Court stamped ten thousand times on cloth that seemed to drink the light.

"They're not men," someone said near her. "Look at them march. No one marches like that."

But they were men. That was the worst part. Wren could hear them—not their voices, not yet, but the low murmur beneath, the way she heard everything now since the harbor, since the river god had poured through her like cold silver and left her changed. She heard the prayers rising off the army like steam off wet stone. Most of them were small and terrible. *Let me live till evening. Don't let me be first over the wall. I want to go home.* Ten thousand soldiers praying to gods who had been outlawed before most of them were born, because every man prays when he is about to die, even if he has forgotten who to pray to.

The prayers of the enemy sounded almost exactly like the prayers of everyone else. Wren held on to that thought like a rail in a storm.

"Wren." Anselm crouched beside her behind the barricade of overturned carts and sharpened stakes that was all the free cities had managed to build in nine days. His priest's robes were gone; he wore a soldier's padded coat two sizes too big, and his hands were steady only because he made them be. "You remember what we agreed."

"Stay behind the line. Pray small. Don't let anything big come through me unless—" She recited it dutifully, then stopped. "Unless there's no other way."

"There's always another way," Anselm said, which was a lie, and both of them knew it was a lie, and both of them were grateful for it anyway.

He squeezed her shoulder and moved down the line, going from knot to knot of defenders—fishermen with boar spears, city watchmen in mismatched armor, a company of Harrowgate dockhands who had painted a minor god of tides on their shields because it seemed better than nothing. He could not give them absolution anymore; his order had stripped him of that years ago. So he gave them what he had instead: words, plain ones, and the terrible comfort of a man who had once enforced the Ban and now stood openly against it.

Beatrix was somewhere up on the ridge with the signal fires, doing whatever bone-carvers did before battles. Wren hadn't seen her since before dawn. The found family, scattered across a burning field before the field had even caught fire. She kept reaching for them in her mind the way you reach for a stair that isn't there—Anselm's steadiness, Beatrix's dry voice saying something sharp and kind—and found only the roar of ten thousand approaching prayers.

* * *

The horns began at midmorning.

Not trumpets—the usurper's army didn't use trumpets, because music was too much like memory, and memory fed the thing on the throne, so its generals starved their own soldiers of everything beautiful. What Wren heard instead was a sound like a bell struck underwater, one long toneless note that rolled across the fields and made her teeth ache and made three men near her drop their spears and start weeping without knowing why.

Then the arrows came, and after the arrows came everything else.

The first hour was noise. Pure, total noise—wood splintering, men screaming orders and prayers in the same breath, the crack of the ballistae the council had finally, grudgingly mounted on the walls. Wren pressed herself against the barricade and did what she had promised: she prayed small. *Keep that boy alive. Keep that boy alive. Keep that boy alive.* She didn't even know the boy's name—a dockhand, seventeen maybe, bleeding from the scalp—but she could hear his prayer under the battle-roar, thin as a candle flame: *Mama, I'm sorry, Mama*—and so she took it and carried it upward, or outward, or wherever prayers went now that the old gods were listening again.

And something answered.

It was never what she expected. It was never lightning, never glory. This time it was simply that the arrow meant for the boy's throat went wide by a finger's width, and the boy lived another minute, and then another, long enough to drag a wounded comrade behind the carts. Small mercies. The road of small mercies, Anselm called it, and Wren understood suddenly that this was what divinity actually was—not the harbor-flood, not the great cold hand of the river god, but this: a thousand tiny bendings of the world, each one costing something, each one paid for by someone willing to listen.

She listened harder. She opened herself wider than she should have.

The dying came to her like water finding a drain. All across the field, men were falling, and in their last moments they prayed—to mothers, to forgotten saints, to the tide-god on the dockhands' shields, to nothing, to anyone—and the prayers had nowhere to go, because the Ban had cut the lines between the world and the divine for ten years and the lines were still frayed, still broken, still full of static. Except through her. Through Wren, god-touched, god-wounded, standing behind a barricade with blood drying on her cheek that wasn't hers.

So she became the line. She took the prayers in one side and passed the answers out the other, and it felt like being a door in a hurricane.

A spearman prayed *don't let me die face-down in the mud*, and he died sitting up, looking at the sky, and his last breath came out as thanks. A woman of the watch prayed *let my daughter remember me laughing*, and Wren caught that prayer and held it, actually held it, cupped it like a coal, because she didn't know how to answer that one yet but she refused to drop it. An old soldier prayed for forgiveness from a god whose name he'd been arrested for speaking twenty years ago, and Wren felt the name arrive in her mouth like a swallowed ember, and she spoke it aloud into the smoke, and somewhere in the burning field the old man's face went peaceful before the end.

That was the morning. That was her whole world: the door, the hurricane, the coals she caught and carried.

* * *

By noon the field was on fire in six places, and the shield-wall of answered hopes was holding, barely, the way a dam holds—visibly, horribly, with groans and leaks.

Wren knelt in the trampled grass behind the third barricade, shaking, her nose bleeding freely, her hands stained with ash. She had stopped counting the prayers. There were too many. There would always be too many. Every time she thought she had reached the bottom of her strength, another dying man's hope landed in her chest like a thrown stone, and she discovered she had room for exactly one more.

"You're killing yourself, little one." The voice came from beside her—an old woman of the watch, helmet gone, gray braid burned at the ends, pressing a waterskin into Wren's hands. "I've seen channelers work the old rites. You have to stop sometime."

"I can't." Wren's voice came out cracked. "If I stop listening, they die alone."

The old woman looked at her for a long moment. Then she did something Wren did not expect: she bowed her head, briefly, the way you bow to a shrine. "Then listen to mine too," she said. "I'm going back out there, and I'm old, and I'm slow, and I know how this ends. Let my last thought be useful."

And Wren took that prayer too, and folded it in with all the others, and sent back the only answer she had left: *You will not be forgotten. None of you will be forgotten.* She didn't know if it was true. She didn't know if she had the power to make it true. But the old woman smiled like it was gospel, and walked back into the smoke with her sword up, and Wren learned that afternoon that courage is not certainty. Courage is answering prayers you are not sure you can answer, and doing it anyway, because the alternative is silence, and silence is what the crown-thing wants. Silence is what it eats.

Across the field, the enemy's gray banners advanced another hundred yards. Behind them, far off at the edge of sight, something vast and pale moved among the command tents—something that did not walk quite right, something the soldiers around it flinched away from without seeming to know why. Wren felt it before she saw it clearly: a hunger, enormous and patient, tasting the air the way she tasted prayers.

It had noticed her. She was sure of it. Somewhere in that gray mass, something had turned its attention toward the small girl behind the barricade who was answering ten thousand prayers at once, and it had begun, very slowly, to be interested.

* * *

The wave broke at midafternoon.

The usurper's general—Wren never learned his name—threw his reserves at the center in one massed push, and the barricade of carts went over like a fence in a flood, and then it was not a battle anymore, it was a crush, a screaming press of bodies in the smoke. Wren lost her footing, lost the ground, lost everything except the prayers, which were coming so fast now they blurred together into one continuous scream of hope and terror, and she screamed with them, and answered what she could—

—and then arms closed around her, and she was lifted clean off the ground, and Beatrix's voice was in her ear, ragged and furious and more beloved than anything in the world: "I told you to stay *small*."

"You were supposed to be on the ridge!"

"The ridge fell. Move." Beatrix hauled her backward through the crush, carving knife still in her free hand, bone charms strung across her chest like armor, each one whispering, some of them screaming. Around them the line bent, buckled, did not break—dockhands locking shields, watchmen giving ground yard by yard, Anselm somewhere ahead of them shouting words of the old rites in a voice cracked raw, buying seconds with syllables.

And then the pale thing at the edge of the field stopped being distant.

It came through its own army like a ship through water, and where it passed, men forgot. Wren felt it happen—whole clusters of prayers simply *went out*, like candles pinched between fingers, soldiers dropping their weapons and standing blank-faced amid the slaughter because the thing wearing the pale cloak had drunk whatever made them them. Not killing them. Emptying them. Leaving them upright and hollow as the king it served.

Beatrix shoved Wren behind an overturned wagon and turned to face it, and Wren saw her mentor—her fierce, frightened, stubborn Beatrix—do the bravest thing Wren had ever seen: she raised a screaming bone charm above her head and shouted a dead king's name into the smoke.

The pale thing stopped.

Every prayer on the field stuttered. Ten thousand hearts missed a beat, and in that gap of silence, Wren heard what Beatrix heard, felt the charm in the carver's fist speak with a voice like dust and iron:

*"I know what you serve. And I know what it fears."*

The pale thing tilted its head. And then, unhurried, terribly, it began to move toward them.

Beatrix grabbed Wren's hand. "Run," she said, "and don't stop praying."
