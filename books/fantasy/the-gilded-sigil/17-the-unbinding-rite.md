## 17. The Unbinding Rite

The king's body breathed.

Not the way living men breathed—there was no rise and fall of the chest, no flutter at the throat—but something deeper, slower, like a tide that had forgotten which shore it belonged to. Beatrix knelt beside him in the bone-vault beneath the palace, her palms flat against the cold stone, and felt that breath move through the floor, through her knees, through the marrow of her own arms. Once a minute. Perhaps once in two minutes. A breath held for eleven years, waiting to be given back.

He had been beautiful once. She could see it even now, under the waxy pallor and the strange preservation of whatever rite had kept him from rotting. High cheekbones. A jaw that had been carved by laughter as much as by lineage. His hands were folded on his breast like a man sleeping in a field, and someone—long ago, before the thing above them had finished its slow meal—had tucked a sprig of dried rowan between his fingers. Rowan for warding. Someone had loved him enough to try.

It hadn't been enough. Nothing had been enough, or he would not be here, and the crown would not be up there wearing his face and eating the world's remembering one prayer at a time.

"Beatrix." Anselm's voice came soft across the vault. He had spent the night preparing—chalking the old circles of his forbidden faith onto stone that had never known them, arranging candles whose flames burned without heat, a pale gold color she had only ever seen in charms made from the bones of saints. He looked older than he had on the road. The penitent's disguise was gone; this was just Anselm Oduya, gray at the temples, hands steady in the way of men who had decided exactly how much they were afraid and had chosen to proceed anyway. "The circle is ready when you are."

Wren sat cross-legged near the entrance, keeping watch upward through the hidden stair, but her eyes kept coming back to the king. Children always looked at death too long, Beatrix thought. Not because they didn't understand it, but because they understood it more honestly than adults did—as a door, not a wall.

"Is he dreaming?" Wren asked.

"I don't know," Beatrix said.

"If I were asleep for eleven years, I'd dream about breakfast."

Anselm huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Pray it's a good one, then. What we do next may shake it loose."

Beatrix rose. Her kit lay open on the altar-stone Anselm had cleared: her knives, her rasps, the little awl she'd used since she was an apprentice with ink-stained fingers and no idea what the voices cost. And at the center, wrapped in cloth she'd woven herself, the king's bone.

She had taken it from his hand three nights ago—a single finger-bone, surrendered without resistance, as though the body had been waiting for someone to ask. It was the densest carrier she had ever worked. Even wrapped, even silenced with salt and prayer, she could feel it humming against her sternum like a second heart. And inside the hum, faint but constant, the voice she had first heard in the charnel house a lifetime ago:

*They put my face on it. They put my face on it and now it smiles at my children.*

She unwrapped the bone.

The vault went quiet—not silent, but *attending*. Even the candle-flames leaned toward her, all of them at once, like flowers finding a sun. Wren stopped fidgeting. Anselm closed his eyes and began the low drone of the stabilizing rite, the words of a faith that had been outlawed before either of them could walk, and the sound filled the chamber the way water fills a cup: completely, without effort.

Beatrix set the bone on the altar and picked up her finest knife.

*Here is what you must understand,* Sefa had told her once, in the shop, over a mourning charm gone wrong—the dead man's voice tangled in the carving because Beatrix had rushed the binding grooves. *Bone doesn't hold magic. Bone holds *memory*. Magic is just what memory does when you carve it somewhere to live.*

Sefa. The name came with its whole weight attached—the smell of cedar shavings, the rasp of her voice, the way she'd tap Beatrix's knuckles with a ruler when her grooves wandered. Eleven years of evenings. Every technique. Every story. The way she'd said *girl* when she meant *my whole heart*.

Beatrix's knife did not wander now. She began the unbinding grooves along the finger-bone's length, and the king's voice rose out of it like smoke out of a doused fire.

*Oh,* he said. *Oh, you're doing it. You're actually—* A sound that might have been a laugh, if laughter could rust. *I had begun to think the gods had forgotten me entirely.*

"The gods forgot nothing," Beatrix murmured, not lifting her eyes from her work. "They were muzzled. There's a difference."

*You sound like someone I knew.* The voice went distant, tender. *My wife. She argued with priests the way other women argue about weather.*

"Tell me about the crown."

Silence, then. The kind of silence that has mass. In the circle, Anselm's chant faltered for half a beat and steadied.

*I don't remember all of it,* the king admitted. *That's rather the point, isn't it? It ate me slowly. First my childhood—it took my mother's face first, I woke one morning and couldn't recall whether her eyes were blue or brown, and then I couldn't recall her at all, only that there had been a her. Then my wedding day. Then the war. Then my daughters' names.* A pause. *It left me my duty. It knew I'd need something to walk with. So it fed me duty while it drank everything else, and I smiled, and I signed, and I opened the gates, and every year there was less of me behind the smile until the night it finally set down my body and walked upstairs wearing my face, and I—*

The voice broke. Beatrix's knife paused. She forced it onward.

*And I stayed,* the king whispered. *In the bone. Because the body has to stay alive, do you see? It anchored itself to me. As long as my soul is bound to the crown, the body cannot die, and the thing cannot be killed. It made itself immortal out of my murder.*

"That's why the rite needs two carvers," Anselm said quietly, opening his eyes. "I hold the soul steady. You cut the binding. But Beatrix—" He hesitated, and she hated him a little for hesitating, because she already knew. She had known since Harrowgate, since the river god's price, since the moment she'd read the oldest version of the unbinding rite in a book that should have been burned a decade ago and found the line she'd hoped she'd misread.

"But the binding isn't thread," she said. "It's not something you can simply sever. It grew. Eleven years of it, rooted through his soul like ivy through a wall. Cut it and the soul comes away in pieces."

"So the rite asks for a replacement," Anselm said. "Something of equal weight. Something loved." He met her eyes. "A memory. Given freely, wholly, permanently. The crown consumed his memories of love; the rite requires love's memory to fill the wound, or the soul tears free and scatters, and everything we've done ends in the dark."

*There is another way,* the king said quickly, urgently. *Girl. Carver. Listen to me. There is always another way—you can find some other memory, some small thing, a taste, a—*

"You don't have any small things left," Beatrix said gently. "That's the whole horror of you."

The silence that followed had Wren on her feet, crossing the vault to stand at Beatrix's shoulder. "What does it want?" Wren asked. "How much?"

"It wants a memory of equal love," Beatrix said. "Equal to what it took from him. It took everything. It took eleven years of being loved and loving." She turned the bone in her fingers, feeling the grooves catch the candlelight. "But it doesn't measure quantity. It measures *weight*. One memory, heavy enough. Loved enough."

"And yours?" Wren asked, and her voice was very young suddenly, and very sharp. "Which one of yours weighs that much?"

Beatrix didn't answer. She didn't have to. Wren's face went white, and behind her, Anselm's chant stumbled and died altogether.

"No," Wren said. "No. Not her. Not Sefa."

"You don't—"

"I *do* know. I know everything about her. You talk about her when you think I'm asleep. Cedar shavings. The ruler on your knuckles. How she called you *girl* like it was a title." Wren grabbed Beatrix's wrist, hard, a child's grip with a child's desperate strength. "You give that away and you won't know who she was. You'll be standing right here and she'll be *gone*, really gone, twice-dead, and you won't even grieve her because you won't know there's anything to grieve!"

"Wren." Beatrix set down the knife. She crouched so they were eye to eye, the way she had on the road, the way she had the night Wren's prayers flooded a harbor and a fleet. "Look at me. Nothing is decided yet. I'm going to finish the grooves, and then we're going to sit down, all three of us, and think. That's a promise. I don't break promises to family."

Wren's grip loosened. It didn't let go. "You promise."

"On my mother's bones."

Above them, muffled by eleven feet of palace stone, the usurper's court was singing vespers. Beatrix could hear it faintly, a thousand voices raised in praise of the hollow thing on the throne, and underneath the singing, if she listened the way only she could listen, the sound of memories being eaten. A city forgetting, prayer by prayer, what magic had sounded like. What freedom had felt like. What their own grandmother's faces had looked like.

She returned to the bone.

*Don't,* the king whispered as her knife touched the final groove. *Please. I have watched this thing eat a kingdom. I will not watch it eat you too. Leave me bound. Leave me in the dark. I have practice at it.*

"Your wife argued with priests," Beatrix said. "I argue with kings. It's nearly the same work."

She carved.

The last groove closed, and the vault changed. The candle-flames stood straight up, rigid as spears. Anselm began the rite's true words—the ones from the deepest page, the ones that required the price—and the air itself seemed to lean in, the way the dead leaned in when Beatrix listened well. The bone grew warm in her hands. Warmer. Hot enough that she should have dropped it, and she did not drop it, because she was a carver and carvers hold on.

And into the silence between one word of the rite and the next, a voice spoke from everywhere and nowhere, from the bone and the walls and the space behind her own eyes. It was not the king's voice. It was older than the king, older than the Ban, older than the stones of the palace. It was patient the way winter is patient.

***THE RITE ASKS ITS PRICE.***

"I know," Beatrix said.

***IT ASKS A MEMORY OF EQUAL LOVE. WHAT WILL YOU GIVE?***

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Eleven years of hiding, of staying small, of carving whispers into bone while the world forgot—and now, at the end, her hands were as steady as Sefa's had ever been over a workbench.

She thought of cedar shavings. Of a ruler tapping her knuckles. Of *girl*, said like a benediction. Of the whole warm architecture of a life built between two people in a shop that smelled of bone dust and tea, and how it was the only home she had ever trusted, and how giving it up would not hurt—

—that was the trap, she realized. It wouldn't hurt. That was what surrendering it meant. She would stand in the ashes of her own history and feel nothing, and some stranger wearing her face would pick up a charm she'd carved for a woman she couldn't name and wonder, idly, why her eyes were wet.

*Real love includes grief,* Sefa had told her, once, in a memory she was about to lose. *Anyone can love the sweet part. Love is what stays when the sweet part is gone.*

"Yes," Beatrix said aloud, to the old voice, to the king, to Wren, whose sob cut through the vault like a knife through cloth. "Yes. I know exactly what it will ask. And I'm going to give it anyway."

The voice gathered itself, vast and gentle and terrible, and spoke the name of the price.

***

The name of the price was not a word. It was a *showing*.

The vault fell away, and Beatrix stood in the shop on Tallow Lane, ten years younger, sawdust in the light, and Sefa was alive at the workbench with her sleeves rolled up, arguing with a piece of elk antler that refused to take a groove cleanly.

"You're rushing it," Sefa said, without looking up. "You always rush it when you're excited. Excitement is fine for cooking, girl. For carving, you want the other thing. The slow burn."

"What's the other thing called?"

"Patience, if you're polite. Devotion, if you're honest." Now she looked up, and her eyes crinkled, and Beatrix's chest cracked open along a seam she hadn't known was there. "Same thing, really. Both mean: staying when staying costs."

"Sefa—"

"Mm?"

"I have to tell you something, and you're going to hate it."

Sefa set down the antler. Wiped her hands on her apron, the green one, the one with the singed hem from the fire that had taken her first shop. Gave Beatrix her whole attention, the way she gave it to nothing else in the world, the way she had never given it to any customer or priest or magistrate, and said: "Then say it, girl. I've got nowhere better to be. I've got exactly where I want to be."

And Beatrix, standing in a memory that was already becoming an offering, said: "Thank you. For everything. For all of it. I never said it properly while you were—"

"Girl." Sefa's voice went soft and rough at once. "You say it every time you carve true. You say it every time you listen to the dead instead of plugging your ears. You've been saying thank you for eleven years in the only language I ever cared about." She smiled, and it was the smile from the last morning, the ordinary one, the one Beatrix had carried through every dark road since. "Now. Are you going to help me with this antler, or are you going to stand there crying on my clean floor?"

And Beatrix understood, finally, fully, what the rite had shown her and why: not to torture her. To *weigh* her. This was the memory, whole and entire, the slow burn, the devotion, the staying. This was what equal love looked like when it was weighed against a king's stolen life.

It was enough. God help her, it was more than enough.

The shop began to fade at the edges, cedar and sawdust dissolving into gold, and Sefa—fading too, though she could not have known it, though she was only memory and memory does not know its own ending—reached out and tapped Beatrix's knuckles, one last time, gentle as snowfall.

"Atta girl," she said.

***

Beatrix came back to herself kneeling on the vault floor, the bone blazing in her hands like a coal, tears running freely down her face and—this was the terrible part, the part she would remember later with a stranger's confusion—she was already beginning to forget why.

The rite waited. The old voice waited. Somewhere above, the crowned thing had felt the first tremor of its unmaking, and the whole palace had begun to shudder, dust sifting down through the dark, and far off there was shouting, and the tolling of alarm bells, and Wren screaming her name.

One breath. She had one breath left in which she was still entirely herself.

She used it to look at Wren—at the child she'd pulled from the ashes, the daughter she'd never dared call that—and to press the bone charm, the small spare one she always carried, into the girl's palm.

"Hold onto this," Beatrix said. "Whatever happens. Whatever I forget. Hold onto it for me."

And then, knowing exactly what it would ask, and what it would take, and what she would lose—

Beatrix Zhou opened her hands, and said yes.
