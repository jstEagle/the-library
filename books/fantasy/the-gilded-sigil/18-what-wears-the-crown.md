## 18. What Wears the Crown

The knife went in, and the world went out.

Beatrix had carved ten thousand charms in her life—mourning pieces and luck-bones, whisper-sticks for the grieving, little slivers of femur and rib that held a dead voice the way a cup holds water. She knew every resistance bone could offer, every grain and flaw, every place where the marrow's ghost still clung. But she had never cut into a king.

The true king's body lay beneath the palace in a crypt that smelled of cold stone and older cold, something beneath cold, something like patience. Anselm knelt at the king's head, hands moving through the forbidden rites of a faith he'd helped burn, his voice a low thread holding the soul in place. Wren stood at the door of the crypt with her palms pressed flat to the stone, listening to whatever children listen to when gods lean close. And Beatrix knelt over the king's breastbone with her carving knife sunk half an inch into ancient ivory-white bone, and the rite opened around her like a door.

She heard him first. Of course she did. She always heard them first.

*Who is there?* The voice was thin as winter light, worn down to almost nothing, a man-shaped absence wearing the last rags of itself. *I dreamed someone was carving my name.*

"I am," Beatrix said aloud, though her lips barely moved. Her hands kept working, following lines that appeared under her blade only when her eyes were open and vanished when she blinked—the unbinding sigils, Sefa's sigils, the ones from the charnel house, the ones from the dream she'd stopped admitting was a dream. "My name is Beatrix Zhou. I'm going to take you out of the crown."

*Out.* The word came slow, like a coin dropped down a very deep well, waiting for its splash. *There is no out. There is only what it leaves.*

"There's an out." She cut the third line, and the fourth. The bone parted for her like flesh remembering it had once been flesh. "I've come a long way to prove it."

And then the crown woke up.

It happened all at once, the way a trap happens: no warning, no creak of mechanism, just the world snapping shut. The crypt's torchlight didn't gutter—it *inverted*, flame burning black for one impossible heartbeat, and in that heartbeat Beatrix felt something enormous turn its attention toward her. Not eyes. Not a gaze. A *hunger*, vast and old and patient as debt, and it poured itself into her through the open channel she herself had cut.

The crypt dissolved.

She was standing in her shop in Vellamare. Not the shop as it was—ash and ruin now, she'd seen it burn from three streets away—but the shop as it had been at its best, on a morning in autumn, dust motes turning gold above the workbench, rain-washed light coming through the window in bars, the smell of beeswax and bone shavings and the cheap tea she brewed too strong because strong tea was one of the few luxuries she allowed herself. Every detail perfect. Every detail *lovingly* perfect, rendered by something that wanted her to look closely, wanted her to step inside, wanted her to believe.

"Hello, little carver," said a voice behind her.

Beatrix did not turn around. Her heart was hammering so hard she could feel it in her teeth, and her hands—her traitor, trembling, hopeful hands—wanted so badly to turn around that she had to grip the edge of the workbench just to stay still.

"You're not real," she said.

"Isn't this real enough?" Soft footsteps crossed the floorboards. She knew those footsteps. She had listened to them for eleven years, had known Sefa Ilyich was approaching not by sight but by the particular drag of her left heel, the old injury she'd never explained and Beatrix had never asked about. The footsteps stopped beside her. The smell came next: pipe smoke and rosemary oil and underneath it something faintly mineral, like river stones in sun. "The tea's gone cold, dear one. You know how you get when you carve too long."

"It knows how I get," Beatrix whispered. Her throat was closing. "That's the whole horror of it. It's been reading me since I touched the bone."

She forced herself to turn.

Sefa Ilyich stood in the gold light of an autumn morning that had ended years ago, in fire and screaming and the specific silence afterward where a person used to be. She looked exactly as Beatrix remembered her: broad-shouldered, silver-streaked hair escaping its braid, deep laugh-lines around dark eyes, the faded blue scarf she wore even in summer because her neck got cold, she claimed, though Beatrix suspected sentiment, which Sefa would have denied with great offense. She was smiling. It was a good smile. It was the smile from the last evening they'd spent together, the night before the Ban's enforcers came to the shop district, the night before everything.

"Come here," the thing wearing Sefa said, opening its arms. "You've carried me long enough, Bea. Let me carry you."

Every part of her ached to move. That was the terrible genius of it—this wasn't a crude temptation, not a devil offering wealth or power. This was built from the inside of her own chest. Whoever—or whatever—had made this illusion had reached into her and found the exact shape of her longing, and poured itself into the mold, and the fit was seamless because the mold was hers.

"That's not her laugh," Beatrix said, mostly to herself. Anchoring. Anselm had told her once, back on the road, in one of his rare moments of priestly usefulness: *Illusions are perfect. That's how you know them. Nothing living survives memory intact—memory wears things smooth and gets them wrong, and love is mostly the getting wrong. If you see perfection, run.*

"What?" The false Sefa tilted her head, and it was such a Sefa gesture, so precisely her, that Beatrix's resolve cracked down the middle and she hated herself for how good it felt.

"The way you remember me," Beatrix said louder, steadier, finding the words as she spoke them the way her knife found the hidden lines in bone. "You don't have any flaws. Real Sefa hummed off-key. She burned the rice every single time, without exception, and then blamed the pot. She called me 'little carver' but only when she was annoyed—when she was glad to see me she said 'there's my girl,' and you haven't said that, have you? You've been saying the wrong thing this entire time."

Something flickered across the false face. For an instant, beneath the skin, Beatrix saw the other thing—the vast pale hunger, the shape of a crown made of teeth—and the shop flickered like a candle in wind.

Then it recovered, and it adapted.

"There's my girl," it said, and the words landed in her chest like arrows, because they were right, they were exactly right, and that was worse.

"No." Beatrix backed away, and the shop stretched to keep her inside it, walls receding, doorway retreating ahead of her like a horizon. "No, see, that's the trick, isn't it. You can learn. You're drinking me dry while I stand here—you're reading everything I remember about her and rebuilding yourself to match. But there's one thing you can't match, because I won't give it to you."

"And what is that?" The false Sefa followed, unhurried, kind, patient as hunger.

"The grief." Beatrix's voice broke, and she let it break. She was done being small; she'd spent ten years being small, and smallness had saved nothing and no one. "You want me to be happy. Because happiness would make me stop. Happy people don't finish rites. Happy people put down their knives and stay in the warm room. But I don't get to be happy. Do you understand? She died and I couldn't save her and I didn't even get to bury her properly—I got a scrap of cloth and a rumor and ten years of not-knowing whether the ashes were really hers. That grief is mine. It's the truest thing I own. And if you were really her—if some piece of her actually survived in that crown and was reaching for me—she would know that too. She'd know I couldn't take your hand without betraying everything we were."

The false Sefa stopped walking.

For a moment the shop held perfectly still, dust motes frozen mid-gold, and Beatrix stood in the middle of her own best memory with her fists clenched and her heart in ruins and waited.

"You would choose pain," the thing said, and the Sefa-shape thinned as it spoke, the voice layering over itself, a hundred voices, a thousand, the voices of everyone it had ever eaten, kings and priests and mothers and children, all of them flattened to the same hungry monotone. "You stand in a perfect reconstruction of your happiest day, offered the return of your dearest dead, and you would choose pain."

"Not pain," Beatrix said. "*Her*. All of her. The rice she burned. The songs she ruined. The way she argued with me about payment until I wanted to scream, and the way she secretly slipped coins into my apron pocket anyway, thinking I wouldn't find them, and I always found them, and I never said a word, because that was love too—letting each other keep our small stupid dignities. You can't copy that. Love isn't a set of details you can reconstruct. It's a wound shaped like a person, and you are not shaped like her. You're shaped like *nothing*. You're a hunger wearing the shapes of everything you've eaten, and I have listened to the dead my whole life, crown, and do you know what they all say, in the end?"

The thing said nothing. Around her, the golden shop trembled.

"They say *remember me as I was*. Not better. Not whole. As I *was*—stubborn and flawed and gone." Beatrix lifted her chin. "So no. I won't stay for tea. My tea was always too strong anyway, and you'd have gotten that wrong too."

The illusion shattered.

Not gently—it came apart like a window breaking inward, the beautiful shop exploding into shards of light that screamed as they dissolved, and Beatrix fell back into her body kneeling in the crypt with blood on her fingers and tears cutting tracks through the grime on her face. The black-flame moment passed. Torchlight returned, ordinary and orange. Anselm was shouting her name. Wren was screaming—not words, something older—and the air above the king's body was tearing open like wet paper.

Through the tear, the crown-thing looked at her.

It had no face. It was a suggestion of a face, the way a hole in a wall is a suggestion of a door: a tall figure robed in shadow that moved wrong, joints bending in directions grief has no use for, and where its head should be sat the crown—a spoked circlet of black iron and yellowed bone, and Beatrix understood with sick certainty whose bones those had been before. It did not speak. Speaking was for things with throats. Instead its attention simply *pressed* against her, immense and insulted, and the pressure said, in a language below language:

*You refuse comfort. Then take truth.*

The crypt changed again, but this time it took nothing from her. It gave.

Beatrix saw the king's last year. She saw him young-seeming and hollow-eyed signing decrees he didn't understand, his own hand moving while he watched it from somewhere far away. She saw the nights he sat alone in rooms stripped bare because the crown had eaten the memories attached to everything—his wife's portrait, his daughter's cradle, his mother's letters—all of it removed, carted away, burned, because objects were anchors and anchors were resistance. She saw him try, one final time, to write his own name, and she saw his hand produce instead a perfect inventory of the realm's granaries, and she saw him look at that page and feel *nothing*, because the crown had already taken the part of him that would have wept.

She saw what remained. A function wearing a man. Obedience with a pulse.

*This,* the crown pressed into her, triumphant. *This is all any of you are. Meat that remembers until remembering stops. I do not destroy you, little carver. I complete you. I take the ache out of the wound, the salt out of the grief, the weight out of the love—and what remains is peace. You cling to your imperfections, your flaws, your beautiful broken grief. I am offering you the version of your mentor who does not die. Who was never annoying, never gone, never lost. Perfection is only love with the suffering refined away. Why do you fight the refining?*

"Because the suffering is *hers*," Beatrix gasped. She was on her knees now, though she didn't remember falling, and the vision was crushing down on her like deep water. "Because when I loved her I agreed to lose her. That was the bargain. That's every bargain. You don't get the love without the losing—that's the whole contract, and you—you're a cheat, you're a thief promising people they can renege, and everything you touch turns to obedience!"

Anselm's voice rose behind her, cracking, desperate, the old rites pouring out of him faster now. Wren's screams had become something else—words, prayers, a child's voice calling names of gods Beatrix had never learned—and the stone beneath Wren's palms had begun to glow a faint defiant green.

The crown-thing's attention narrowed. Beatrix felt it change tactics one final time, felt it reach past her defenses for the deepest vault, the place where she kept—

Sefa's voice. The real recording of it, preserved in the oldest charm Beatrix owned, the first one she'd ever carved, a sliver of rib bone she still wore against her sternum. The charm that held the last thing Sefa ever said to her.

*I'm proud of you, little carver. Whatever comes, remember that. There's my girl.*

The crown-thing seized it. Beatrix felt the theft happen—felt the charm go silent against her chest, felt the voice ripped out of the bone and dragged up into that grinding dark attention, and the creature held it up before her like a jewel it meant to crush.

*Give me the rest,* it said. *Your memories of her. Every one. In exchange, I will let this voice live. I will let the king go. I will call back my armies. Everyone walks away, and you keep this—one true thing—forever. Refuse, and I will eat it slowly, while you watch, and then I will eat the child, and the priest, and then every memory of magic in your precious free cities, until no one remembers it existed, including you.*

The crypt was very quiet.

Anselm had stopped chanting. He was looking at her, and his face was gray, and he shook his head slowly—*don't, don't, don't*—but his hands kept moving through the rite anyway, because he was a man who had learned that belief was something you did with your body when your mind had failed.

Wren looked at her too, green-eyed and terrified and certain, and mouthed two words: *Finish it.*

Beatrix knelt in the dark beneath the palace, in the presence of a god-eating hunger wearing a murdered king's stolen voice, and she thought about her mentor's heel dragging on the floorboards. About burnt rice blamed on the pot. About coins in apron pockets and arguments about payment and a scarf in summer and a humming voice gone off-key, all of it already dissolving at the edges of her memory the way memories do, wearing smooth, getting wonderfully wrong.

She thought: *if I trade you away, I won't even know what I lost. And somehow that is the only thing left I cannot allow.*

"You want my memories of her," Beatrix said. She rose to her feet. Her legs shook. She rose anyway, the way the small rise, when they are finished being small. "Fine. Come and take them."

She raised the carving knife over the king's exposed breastbone, over the half-cut sigil that was the door out, and she brought it down with every year of silence behind it—

—and the crown-thing *screamed*, a sound with no sound in it, a shriek of pure appetite meeting resistance, and the whole crypt lurched like a ship struck by waves, and somewhere far above them the palace began to groan on its foundations, and Wren shouted words in a language that predated the city, and the last line of the sigil blazed white under Beatrix's bloody knife.

The rite demanded its price. She could feel it waiting, patient as the grave, asking the only question it had been built to ask.

*What do you love enough to lose?*

Beatrix closed her eyes, and answered.
