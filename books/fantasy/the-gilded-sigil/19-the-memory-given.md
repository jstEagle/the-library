## 19. The Memory Given

The rite had a shape, and the shape was a wound.

Beatrix knelt in the dark beneath the palace with the king's bones laid out before her on a slab of black stone, and she understood at last why the old carvers had called unbinding *opening*. Every charm she had ever made had been a small door—a way for a voice to pass through bone and be heard. This was not a door. This was tearing down a wall that had stood for nine years, a wall built of stolen memory and crowned hunger, and everything behind it was going to come through at once.

The knife in her hand was Sefa's. Of course it was. She had carried it from Vellamare without thinking, the way you carry a habit, and only now, with the blade's edge resting against the first groove of the rite, did she understand that the gods were not careless. They had let her bring exactly what the moment would require.

*Begin,* said the dead king's voice, thin as smoke in the charnel-dark. *Before it notices.*

Above them—three floors up, beyond the bone-dust and the buried foundations—the crown-thing sat its throne and did not blink. Beatrix could feel it the way you feel a storm building behind a mountain: pressure without sound, appetite without shape. Anselm's rites held the true king's soul steady, a lattice of whispered scripture glowing faint gold along the edges of the ancient bones. Wren stood at the chamber's mouth with her hands pressed flat against the stone, listening to whatever the small gods murmured up through the palace's roots, her face pale and set in a way no child's face should have to be.

"Beatrix." Anselm's voice came low across the dark. "Once this starts, there is no stopping it. The crown will fight. And the rite will ask—" He stopped. He could not say it. He had read the old texts; he knew what an equal exchange meant when the thing being freed had been emptied of *everything*.

"I know what it will ask," Beatrix said.

She did not look at Wren. If she looked at Wren, she would think about the river god, and the memory Wren had almost traded, and the fury that had risen in her like floodwater—*you don't get to give yourself away, not to save me, not for anything*—and the confession that had followed, wrung out of her like blood from a cloth: *I traded one once. I don't even know what it was. I've spent ten years feeling the shape of the hole and never once seeing what fit inside it.*

She would not make Wren live that. But the rite did not care what she would or wouldn't do.

She set the knife's tip to the bone.

* * *

The first cut sang.

Not screamed—sang, a single clear note that went up through the stone and out into the world, and everywhere above them, in walls and wells and forgotten shrines, the small gods turned their faces toward the sound. Beatrix felt them gathering the way birds gather before weather. She carved the first sigil into the king's femur and the bone *remembered*, and memory poured up through her fingers like heat.

She saw him young. She saw him crowned, and afraid, and then less afraid, because he had been a good man and good men learn to carry fear quietly. She saw the day the crown first touched his head—not the coronation, the *crown itself*, older than any coronation, older than the realm—and she saw the first thing it took: the name of his childhood dog. A small thing. A nothing. That was how it began. It always took small things first.

Then it took his mother's voice. Then the color of the sea at Harrowgate. Then the faces of his children, one by one, until he woke some gray morning knowing he had loved people whose names he could no longer find, and the grief of that loss was so vast that the crown drank the grief too, and left him smooth and obedient and hollow as a bell.

*This is what waits for all of you,* the crown-thing's voice said, from everywhere, patient as rot. *I do not hate you. I am only hungry. You will not even miss what I take.*

"You took a king," Beatrix said through her teeth, carving. "You didn't even notice he was gone."

The second sigil. The third. Each one cost her something—not memory yet, but strength, warmth, the feeling in her hands. Her fingers were going numb. The knife grew heavy as an anchor. Somewhere far away Anselm was chanting, his voice cracking and steadying and cracking again, holding the soul-lattice together by faith and stubbornness alone.

And then the crown-thing noticed.

* * *

It came down through the palace like cold coming through a window left open all winter.

The torches guttered blue. Wren cried out—a short, sharp sound—and then began to pray, fast and fierce, and the stones themselves seemed to lean toward her voice. The darkness at the top of the stair thickened, gathered, *descended*, and Beatrix felt it arrive the way you feel a hand close over your mouth.

*Carver,* it said. It knew her. Of course it knew her; she had been screaming into its meal for months. *Little thief of voices. Look what I have for you.*

The chamber changed.

She was standing in Sefa's workshop in Vellamare, dust motes turning in afternoon light, the smell of beeswax and bone shavings so real her eyes stung. And there—by the window, half-turned toward her, wearing that expression of fond exasperation she knew better than her own heartbeat—

"Sefa," Beatrix breathed.

*You're late,* Sefa said, and smiled, and it was perfect. It was *perfectly* her—the crooked tooth, the ink stain on her left thumb, the way she tucked her chin when she was about to say something cutting. *Ten years, girl, and you finally come back to the bench. Here.* She held out a half-carved charm. *Finish it with me. Like old times. Leave the king to his bones and the priest to his guilt and the child to her gods. Finish the charm.*

Beatrix's hands were already moving toward it. That was the horror of it—that some part of her leapt without her permission, hungry for exactly this, for the ordinary miracle of an afternoon with her mentor, the thing she had grieved for a decade and been given back in a single impossible season.

But.

But the light was wrong. Afternoon light in Sefa's shop came from the west and lay gold across the floorboards; this light lay flat and sourceless, light with no sun behind it. And Sefa's smile—Sefa never smiled like that. Sefa's smiles were earned, grudging, *specific*. This smile was general. This smile was a portrait of a smile painted by someone who had only had it described.

Real love includes grief. Sefa had told her that once—had she?—over a broken charm and a botched commission, wiping shavings off the bench: *If you can't bear to lose it, girl, you never really loved it. Grief isn't the price of love. Grief is the proof.* And this Sefa offered her no grief at all. No ache, no argument, no barbed disapproval of her risks, no sharp-edged tenderness. Just comfort, smooth and endless and hollow as a bell.

"No," Beatrix said.

*Girl—*

"You're not her. She'd be furious with me right now. She'd tell me I'm doing this all wrong and my cuts are sloppy and I'm going to ruin everything." Her voice broke, and she let it break, and kept talking through the breaking. "That's how I know. The false version doesn't grieve. The real one always did."

The workshop shattered like ice.

* * *

What came through the breaking was rage.

The crown-thing stopped pretending. The dark poured into the chamber in earnest now, and Beatrix felt it seize the rite itself, felt it reach down the thread connecting the king's soul to the crown and *pull*, hauling the soul back toward its cage. Anselm shouted scripture until his voice tore. Wren screamed prayers and the small gods answered in tremors underfoot—but the crown was old, older than the small gods, fed on nine years of a kingdom's remembering, and it was winning.

There was only one thing left. There had only ever been one thing left. The rite demanded a memory of equal love to fill the space the crown had eaten, a weight to balance the scale, and Beatrix had known since the charnel house, since the road, since the night Sefa's ghost first appeared to her in the firelight—she had known and had not let herself finish the thought.

The memories of Sefa. All of them. Ten years of grief and twenty years before that of learning: the first lesson, the last argument, the ghost at the fire, the knife in her hand. Everything. The whole architecture of the person who had made her who she was.

The scale did not lie. Nothing else she owned weighed that much.

"Oh," Beatrix said, very quietly, to no one. "Oh, it was always going to be you."

She raised the knife. She spoke the final syllable of the rite—an old word, a word for *giving back*—and she reached into herself the way she reached into bone, found the bright tangled thread of Sefa running through every year of her life, and *cut*.

* * *

It did not hurt. That was the cruelest part.

It felt like exhaling after holding your breath for years. The memories left her in a long golden rush—she felt them go, felt their *weight* go, felt the rite drink them like water into cracked earth—and she watched them pass, the way you watch a bird fly out an open window, knowing it will not return and unable to be sorry because the sky needed it more.

The thread of gold poured into the king's bones.

And the bones *woke*.

Light ran through the skeleton like dawn through frost, joint by joint, rib by rib, and the soul-lattice Anselm had built blazed white and then simply—opened, like a fist unclenching, like a door finally off its hinges. The king's soul came home to his body the way a river comes home to its bed, and the sound he made was not words. It was the sound of a man remembering his own name after nine years of forgetting, and it filled the chamber, and it filled the palace, and it went up through stone and slate and banner-pole into the burning air above the city where two armies waited.

Above them, three floors up, the crown-thing screamed.

It was not a kingly sound. It was the sound of a hunger finding its food withdrawn, of a parasite cut loose from its host, of a bell with no clapper struck too hard. Beatrix, sliding down into darkness, heard it thrash and diminish, heard ritual collapse into panic into silence—and then, distantly, wonderfully, heard it *crumble*: a long sighing avalanche of dust, nine years of stolen majesty falling apart into nothing, drifting down through the throne room like snow that had never been snow at all.

The last thing she saw was Wren's face above hers, tear-streaked and lit gold, saying her name.

The last thing she thought was: *I hope I remember this. Whatever this is. I hope I get to keep this one.*

Then the dark took even that.

* * *

She woke to birdsong, which was absurd, because they were underground.

No—they weren't. Someone had carried her up and out. She was lying in a courtyard garden gone wild with neglect, in a shaft of honest morning sun, and the birdsong was real, and so was the ache in her hands, and so was the child sitting cross-legged beside her watching her with enormous careful eyes.

"Wren," Beatrix said. The name came easily. Good. Some things had stayed.

Wren's face did something complicated—relief and heartbreak fighting for the same ground. "You're back," she said. "Do you—Beatrix, do you know me?"

"Of course I know you, you ridiculous girl. You flooded a harbor." She pushed herself up on her elbows. Her head felt strange. Not empty—worse than empty. *Tidy*. Like a room someone had cleaned while she slept, everything in its place, everything accounted for, and one patch of floor conspicuously, perfectly bare. "Where's Anselm? Where's the king?"

"Alive. Both of them. The armies are breaking up—the king walked out onto the wall this morning, Beatrix, *himself*, and every soldier who saw him just... put down their weapons." Wren hesitated. "Anselm's with him. Planning things. Trials, and rebuilding, and all the boring salvation."

"Good," Beatrix said. "Good." She flexed her fingers. They worked. The world worked. So why did she keep reaching for something that wasn't there—why did her hand keep drifting to her side, to the space where a person should be walking?

Her fingers closed on bone.

A charm hung at her belt. She lifted it into the light: a mourning piece, fine work, her finest—she recognized her own knife-cuts the way you recognize your own handwriting. Carved for someone specific. Mourning charms always were. Along its length, in the old script, she had written a name.

She couldn't read it.

Not that the script was worn—it was fresh, days old at most—but the name slid off her mind like water off wax. She looked at it and looked at it and felt the tidy room in her head, the clean bare patch of floor, and understood.

"There was someone," she said slowly. "Someone I loved. Someone I—" The sentence wouldn't finish. The grief was still there, she realized. That was the strange mercy and the strange cruelty of it: the *shape* of the loss remained, an ache with no object, a wound that had healed around its own absence. She could feel that she had lost something enormous. She simply could not remember what.

"Beatrix." Wren's voice was very small. "Her name was Sefa."

The name landed on her like snow landing on a roof: it touched her and slid away, leaving nothing behind. But her eyes were wet anyway. Her body remembered what her mind could not.

"She was your teacher," Wren said, and her own tears were falling freely now. "She was a ghost, and she was cross about everything, and she loved you so much she came back from the dead to argue with you about it. She burned herself out at the ford holding off the soldiers so we could run. She—" Wren's breath hitched. "She used to call you *girl*. Like it was an insult and a blessing at the same time."

*Girl.* Something stirred, deep under the tidy floor, a warmth with no shape attached, and faded before it could become anything.

Beatrix looked down at the charm in her palm—at the name she had carved for someone she could not name, the gift with no recipient, the love with no address. Around them the city was waking to its first free morning in nine years. Somewhere a king was learning to be a man again. Somewhere the small gods were singing in the wells.

And somewhere—she was certain of this, certain the way you are certain of gravity—there was a story she needed told back to her. A person she had been, who had loved someone she could no longer see.

"Wren," she said, and her voice was steady, and her hands were not. "Tell me about her. Start at the beginning. Don't leave anything out."

Wren wiped her face, nodded, and took a breath—and in the doorway behind them, unnoticed by either of them, a faint shape of dust and morning light lingered a moment longer than it should have, as if something were waiting to hear its own story told.
