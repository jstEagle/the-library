## 21. The Charm Without a Name

The charm sat on the workbench where it had lived for three days now, and Beatrix still could not say whose hand had carved it.

She knew it was hers. The knife-work was unmistakable—her own habit of leaving the grain unpolished at the base so the bone could breathe, her own way of cutting the binding sigil in a single unbroken stroke because a broken stroke let the voice leak out sideways and go strange. No one else in Vellamare, in Harrowgate, in all the free cities, cut a sigil that way. She had invented it herself, or someone had taught it to her, and either way it lived in her hands now like a scar.

But when she held it to her ear—and she had held it to her ear a hundred times, at dawn, at midnight, in the grey hours when Wren slept and Anselm kept his vigils—she heard nothing she recognized.

There was a voice in it. Of course there was a voice; there was always a voice. But this one was wrapped around her own name the way ivy wraps a wall, and every time she listened, the voice said *Beatrix* with such fierce, particular tenderness that her chest ached as if she'd been struck, and she could not remember ever being struck by anyone who loved her enough to hurt like that.

She set the charm down. Her hands were shaking again. They had been shaking for three days.

* * *

Wren found her there, as Wren always did, materializing in the doorway of the workshop with the particular gravity of a child who has learned that adults left alone too long do dangerous things. The girl was nine years old and carried the gods behind her eyes like coins behind a shutter, and lately she had taken to watching Beatrix the way one watches weather.

"You're doing it again," Wren said.

"Doing what?"

"Sitting there looking like somebody died."

Beatrix laughed despite herself—a short, cracked sound. "Somebody did die. Several somebodies. A whole war's worth." She turned the charm over on the bench. "I just can't remember which one is breaking my heart."

Wren came closer. She did not touch things without asking anymore; the gods had taught her that much, or the burning hedge-witch's shop had, or the shield-wall of answered hopes at the gates of the capital. She looked at the charm for a long moment, and then her face did something complicated—the look of a child deciding whether to tell a truth that will cost something.

"That's her," Wren said quietly. "Isn't it. The ghost."

"I don't know who 'her' is."

"Yes you do. You just don't know that you know." Wren pulled up the stool across from her, the one Anselm had mended twice, and folded her hands on her knees like an old woman settling in for gossip. "You hold that thing like it's going to bite you. And you talk to it when you think nobody's listening. Last night you said, *'I know, I know, stop nagging me.'*" Wren's imitation of Beatrix's exasperated tone was devastatingly accurate. "Ghosts nag. That's how Anselm explained it. So."

So. Beatrix looked down at the little curve of bone, sheep femur, sanded smooth, the sigil glowing faintly now that magic was legal and the dead no longer had to whisper through cracks in the world. Three days since the crown-thing had collapsed into dust beneath the palace. Three days since she had woken on cold stone with Anselm's face above her, wet-eyed, saying her name over and over, and she had looked past him at the empty air where—where something should have been—and felt the shape of an absence so precise it could only have been cut by love.

"The rite took my memories of her," Beatrix said. "A memory of equal love. That was the price. I knew it would be. I chose it anyway." She flexed her fingers. "But nobody told me the body remembers what the mind forgets. My hands keep reaching for things that aren't there. I keep turning to say something to someone who isn't standing beside me. It's like—" She searched for it. "It's like walking through a house where all the furniture's been moved two inches to the left. Nothing's wrong exactly. Everything's wrong."

Wren nodded solemnly, the way children nod when they understand more than they let on. Then she said: "Do you want me to tell you about her?"

Beatrix's breath caught. "You remember her?"

"I remember everything." Wren said it simply, without pride. "That's what I'm for, kind of. Remembering. Holding prayers. Being the cup things get poured into." She shrugged. "Anselm says I'll probably never be able to forget anything, even when I want to. He says that's a blessing and a burden and mostly a burden." A pause. "He cried about it once when he thought I was asleep. About you, actually. He thought he'd lost both of you—the ghost went wherever ghosts go when their work is done, and you woke up not knowing her name, and he was the only one left carrying the whole story. He said that to the ceiling. I wasn't asleep."

Beatrix closed her eyes. "Yes," she said. "Tell me. Please. Tell me everything."

* * *

Wren told it the way children tell stories, which is to say: truthfully, out of order, with digressions that turned out to be the heart of the matter.

"She came back when the gods started answering. You were in your shop and I wasn't born yet, but Anselm says—" Wren caught herself, grinned. "No. Wait. I should start where I saw her. At the charnel house? No, before that. On the road. She walked ahead of us the whole way east, just a shimmer, like heat off stones, and only you could see her properly. She complained constantly. She said your pack straps were wrong. She said you carved like your hands were cold when they weren't. She called you *girl* even though you were grown, and you hated it, and you loved it, I could tell you loved it because you always smiled when you were mad about it."

Beatrix's hands had gone still on the workbench. Listening. Listening the way she listened to bone—with her whole self, leaning into a voice from somewhere she couldn't see.

"She taught you the sigil," Wren said. "The unbroken one. Before the Ban, when teaching magic was still allowed. She was your teacher for six years, in a village called Lowmarsh, near the fens. She had white hair she kept short with a knife because scissors were for people with patience. She drank tea with salt in it on purpose. She had a laugh like—" Wren frowned, hunting for it. "Like a door opening. That's what Anselm said. Like a door opening onto somewhere better."

*A door opening.* Something moved in Beatrix—not a memory, nothing so kind, but the echo of a memory, the groove worn where the memory used to sit. Her eyes stung.

"When the inquisitors came to Lowmarsh," Wren went on, softer now, "she stayed behind so you could run. You didn't want to. You fought her about it. She told you—" and here Wren's voice changed, took on a cadence older than a child's, rougher, a woman's cadence, a teacher's: *'Girl, listen to me. Being alive is the rebellion. Stay small, stay breathing, stay stubborn. Grief me properly later, when it's safe. Promise me you'll grieve me properly.'*

"And you promised. And then you spent ten years grieving her in secret, carving charms to hear her voice, because the Ban made mourning itself illegal if the dead talked back." Wren looked up. "And then she came back. And she got to walk beside you for one whole season. And she held the ford for us. She burned herself out holding the soldiers off so we could cross, and the last thing she said—I heard it, I hear everything—she said, *'There. Properly grieved. Now live, girl. Live loudly.'*"

Silence filled the workshop like water filling a bowl.

Beatrix discovered she was crying—not elegantly, not the way one cries in front of strangers, but the ugly, whole-bodied weeping of a woman finally permitted to mourn in daylight. Ten years of hidden grief, and then a season of impossible joy, and then a sacrifice that had eaten even the grief along with the joy, and now here was a nine-year-old handing it all back to her piece by piece like someone returning stolen goods.

"I don't remember her," Beatrix managed. "I listened to everything you said and none of it came back. There's just—there's a hole shaped like her and everyone keeps describing the hole to me."

"I know." Wren reached across the workbench and put her small hand over Beatrix's trembling ones. "Anselm says forgetting isn't emptying. He says it's like a river carrying silt—the water moves on but the valley stays the shape the water made. You're the valley. She's the shape of you." The girl blushed suddenly, aware of having said something too big for her size. "Sorry. I've been spending too much time with priests."

Beatrix laughed through her tears. It hurt. It helped.

* * *

They talked until the light went amber and then grey. Wren emptied herself out in pieces: Sefa's opinions on Anselm (suspicious, gradually thawing, ultimately *"that man has a spine after all, buried under all that guilt"*); Sefa's delight at Wren's first answered prayer (*"the gods picked well"*); Sefa's habit of humming old fen-songs while Beatrix worked, songs Beatrix apparently hummed now without knowing their names. Every detail landed in Beatrix like rain on dry ground—absorbed, unfelt, changing the shape of things anyway.

At last Wren ran out, or ran down, and sat rubbing her eyes, and Beatrix looked at the charm on the bench with new understanding.

Because that was the thing. That was the whole terrible, beautiful thing of it. The charm still held Sefa's voice. Bone remembered what minds could not. Somewhere in that curve of femur, her mentor was still talking—had been talking for three days—to a student who couldn't hear her name in it and know her own heart.

"Wren," Beatrix said slowly, "when I asked you to tell me about her. Why did you hesitate? At the beginning. You almost didn't ask me."

Wren squirmed. "Because I was scared you'd get sad."

"I'm already sad."

"No, I mean—" The girl struggled. "Because maybe it's kinder not to know? Anselm said that too. He said, *let her rest from the loss.* But then he also said—" Wren straightened, imitating his pulpit voice, deep and deliberate: *'We do not heal by amputation. We heal by telling the truth until the truth fits inside us.'*"

"He would say that. He's a priest."

"Disgraced priest."

"Rebuilding priest," Beatrix corrected. "He decided that last week, remember? Trials are done. He's rebuilding."

Wren grinned, then sobered. "So. What are you going to do?"

Beatrix picked up the charm. Held it to her ear one more time. And this time, knowing what she knew—knowing the voice was Sefa's, knowing the tenderness in it was earned over six years and ten more of silence and one final season of walking together—it was almost enough. Almost. The voice said *Beatrix* and the valley of her shivered, and she understood that she would never remember, and that she would spend the rest of her life loving a woman she could only know secondhand, through a child's retelling and a bone's persistence.

And that was not nothing. That was, in fact, everything anyone gets. Nobody remembers anybody perfectly. Even Sefa, alive, had been known only in fragments—by her students, her neighbors, her gods. Memory was always someone else telling the story. Love was always inheritance.

"Well," Beatrix said, and her voice steadied as her hands took up the carving knife, an old reflex, a true one. "I'm going to carve."

"What?"

"A new charm. For her. Not a listening charm." She selected a fresh piece of bone from the drawer—ox femur, dense and pale, good stock. "Those hold voices for the living to hear. This one's going to hold the story. Everything you told me today. Her name—Sefa, Sefa of Lowmarsh, teacher, fen-witch, salt-in-her-tea, laugh-like-a-door-opening. The sigil she taught me. The promise I made her. All of it, cut into the bone so it outlasts all of us." She began to sand, smooth strokes, muscle memory guiding her. "Because here's what I figured out, Wren. The crown-thing ate memories because memories are how people stay real. It wanted a world of hollow obedient things. Well. I'm going to make the opposite of that. I'm going to make a thing that makes sure a dead woman stays real forever."

Wren watched, chin on her fists, as the knife began its work. "Who's it for?"

"For whoever needs her next." Beatrix blew bone-dust from the first groove. "For students I haven't met. For a city learning to listen again. For me, on the bad days, when I need someone to tell me who I'm grieving." She glanced up, and her eyes were wet and clear at once. "Grief doesn't have to be a hole, Wren. Anselm's right—we don't heal by amputation. We heal by making the loss into something you can hand to another person. That's all a charm ever was. That's all any story is."

She carved through the afternoon and into evening, and Wren stayed, feeding her details as the blade asked for them—*no, shorter hair than that, no, she'd hate that word, use this one instead*—until the sigil was complete, unbroken, one single stroke, and the bone began to glow with a warm gold light that had nothing of screaming in it, nothing of hunger, only the patient shine of a lamp left in a window for someone coming home.

When it was done, Beatrix held it up between them, and the light fell across Wren's face and her own.

"There," she whispered, and did not know why those words, of all words, felt like the ending of a promise kept. "Properly grieved."

The charm pulsed once, gently, like a heartbeat, like a door opening—and from somewhere inside the bone, faint and unmistakable, came the sound of a woman laughing.

* * *

Below, in the shop that was becoming a school, Anselm lit the lamps and called up the stairs that supper was ready, and that tomorrow the first students arrived—three of them, nervous, gifted, sent from Harrowgate with letters of recommendation—and that Wren had better not fill their heads with theology before breakfast.

Beatrix set the new charm carefully in the window box where the morning sun would find it, beside the older one, the nameless one, which she no longer needed to press to her ear to know what it said.

She knew what it said now. Wren had told her. The whole city would know, eventually, because she intended to teach them all to listen.

She went down to supper with her family, and behind her in the dark workshop, two charms glowed side by side in the window—one holding a voice, one holding a story—and between them, the night passed gently, the way it does when the dead are satisfied and the living have finally learned to carry them properly.
