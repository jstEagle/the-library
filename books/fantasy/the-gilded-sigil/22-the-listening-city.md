## 22. The Listening City

The sign above the door was new, and that was the whole miracle of it.

*Zhou & Daughters — Bonecarving, Listening, Instruction.* Beatrix had painted it herself, three coats, because the first two kept running when her hands shook. Not from fear. She hadn't shaken from fear in months. It was something else — the strange vertigo of writing your own name where anyone could read it, after ten years of carving it into nothing, leaving no mark, being no one. The paint had dried crooked at the corner of the *D*. Wren said it looked like a bird taking off. Beatrix decided to believe her.

She opened the shutters on a morning that smelled of salt and bread and the green, growing reek of the harbor — the harbor that a minor god had flooded for a frightened child, and which the city had spent the intervening months learning to love again, silt and all. Across the lane, the hedge-witch's shop stood rebuilt, twice its old size, its new owner a round-faced woman who grew light in jars and sold it by the pinch. She waved. Beatrix waved back. Neither of them pretended anymore that they didn't see each other.

That was the other miracle. Vellamare had become a city that saw itself.

Inside, the workroom smelled of tallow and bone-dust and the particular clean scent of consecrated ash that Anselm insisted on, though his faith had been dissolved and refounded twice since the unbinding and he no longer pretended to know which rites were forbidden. "None of them were," he'd concluded finally, with the relief of a man setting down a load he'd carried so long he'd forgotten his own shoulders. "We just stopped listening to what they were for."

He was at the long table now, teaching. That was the word they'd all agreed on, though what Anselm taught was prayer, and what Beatrix taught was hearing, and what Wren taught — Wren, who was eleven and had once flooded a harbor — was something none of them could name and all of them recognized. *How to be answered without being devoured*, Beatrix thought sometimes. *How to knock without breaking down the door.*

There were six students this morning. Six. A year ago the number would have meant arrest, trial, the pyre in Fishbone Square where they'd burned old Marta's charms while she wept. Now it meant chairs, and chalk, and Wren passing around honey-bread she'd absolutely stolen from the kitchen.

"Again," Beatrix said, and lifted the practice charm — a rib bone, plain-carved, holding nothing but silence. "What do you hear?"

"Nothing," said Tomas, the fisherman's son, frustrated. He was fourteen and all elbows and eagerness, the kind of student who broke things by wanting too hard.

"Good," Beatrix said, and watched six faces fall. "No — listen to me. *Nothing* is a sound. Silence has a grain to it, like wood. Learn the grain of empty and you'll know true quiet from a dead thing's rest. Hold it against your ear-bone—" she tapped the mastoid behind her ear, and six hands copied her, "—and wait. The dead are patient. They've had practice."

Tomas pressed the bone to his neck, scowling with concentration, and then his scowl changed. Softened. Went somewhere young and wondering.

"I hear," he whispered, "rain. Just — rain on a roof. Someone liked rain."

"Someone did," Beatrix said quietly. "That's how you'll know you're really hearing, and not hoping. Hope sounds like what you want. The dead sound like what they loved." She let that settle over the room. "Carve nothing today. Just listen. Tomorrow we begin the whorls."

Anselm caught her eye across the table, and there was a whole conversation in it, the kind they'd gotten fluent in over the months: *look at them, look what we built, look how small it started and how much it holds.*

* * *

At midday the students left and the real work began.

The mourning charms came first — they always did. Vellamare had lost people in the war, in the siege, in the terrible confused weeks when the crown-thing's armies had broken apart and some of their officers had burned what they couldn't keep. There were a great many grieving people in the free cities now, and Beatrix had learned that grief did not become lighter because magic was legal. It only became speakable.

The widow came at noon exactly, as she had every seventh day for two months. Mistress Corvane, gray as gull-feather, carrying her husband's finger-bone wrapped in linen like something newborn.

"You've heard nothing new," she said. It wasn't quite a question.

"He rests," Beatrix said, gently, the way she always did. "Rest isn't silence, mistress. It's a fullness. Like a jar of water set down carefully."

"But the others—" Corvane's hands twisted in her apron. "The Harrowgate listeners say their dead talk and talk. My sister swears she heard her boy laughing through a charm last feast-day. Why won't Aldric—"

"Because your Aldric finished," Beatrix said. "Some souls go on speaking because they have more to say. Some go quiet because they said everything, and it was heard, and they could let go." She took the widow's hands, both of them, cold and knotted. "You gave him forty years of being known. He doesn't need the charm anymore. He needs you to live loudly enough to hear about, later."

Corvane cried a little, the way she did every seventh day, and then laughed a little, the way she was beginning to, and left her coin in the dish — fair price, no haggling, another small revolution — and went out into the sun.

Wren appeared in the doorway of the back room the moment the bell over the door stilled. She did that now; she always knew when a hard conversation ended. God-touched children developed strange instincts.

"She's going to be all right," Wren said.

"Yes."

"The god of small doors told me." Wren shrugged, matter-of-fact. Gods spoke to her the way weather spoke to sailors — constantly, and not always conveniently. "Well. He *implied* it. He mostly wanted to complain about people locking their gardens."

"What did you tell him?"

"That I'd put in a good word if he stopped following me into the bath."

Beatrix laughed — fully, easily, the laugh of someone who had forgotten how to do this for a decade and had relearned it the way you relearn anything: badly, painfully, and then all at once. Wren grinned and stole a piece of honey-bread off the counter, and Beatrix let her, because some thefts were blessings.

Then Wren's grin faded into something older, and she reached into her pocket.

"I found one," she said. "Behind the loose stone in my room. I didn't put it there."

Beatrix's breath went still.

It was a charm. Small, whalebone, worn smooth at the edges the way only her own carvings wore — the whorl pattern she'd invented herself, the one Sefa had called *your signature in the world's skin*. She knew it before her fingers closed around it. She knew it the way you know a scar.

She carved these sometimes. She'd been doing it for months without deciding to — waking with knife and bone in hand, the design already half-finished, made for someone whose name sat in her chest like an amputation. She never remembered making them. She never knew who they were for. But her hands remembered, and her hands were faithful past all reason.

"This is yours," Wren said softly. "Isn't it. From before."

"There was someone," Beatrix said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "I don't know who. I paid a river god, or a rite, or — something. I paid, and the price was her, whoever she was. Everyone says her name to me now like it's a story. *Sefa. Your mentor. She held the ford. She chose to burn out so you could run.*" She turned the charm in the light. "And I believe them the way you believe a map of a place you've never been."

"And you still carve for her."

"My hands don't know she's gone." Beatrix smiled, and it hurt, and it was also — and this was the strangest discovery of her whole strange year — *true*. "Or maybe they know something I don't. Maybe love keeps working after the memory moves out. Like a house that stays warm because of the fires that used to be lit in it."

Wren leaned her head against Beatrix's shoulder, eleven years old and briefly ancient. "When I tell you about her," she said, "you cry at the right parts. Every time. Even though you don't remember. So maybe you're not missing her. Maybe you're just missing *knowing* her. Those aren't the same thing."

"No," Beatrix agreed. "They aren't."

She set the charm on the shelf above her bench — the shelf that held eleven of them now, a row of small smooth bones, each carved for a woman she could not name, each humming faintly with a warmth she could not explain. Anselm had offered, once, very carefully, to ask the gods to return the memory. The gods had declined. *Some prices are also gifts,* the god of small doors had said, or so Wren reported. *She will meet her again in the work, if not in the remembering.*

Beatrix hadn't understood that, then.

She was beginning to.

* * *

The afternoon brought the students back, and then the customers, and then the thing that Beatrix would later understand had been waiting for her all along, patient as the dead.

It was near dusk when the bell rang and a young man came in — dockworker's shoulders, ink-stained fingers, the nervous air of someone who had rehearsed a speech. Behind him hovered an older woman, his mother perhaps, wringing her hat.

"You're Zhou," he said. "The listener. They say — they say you teach people to hear. Really hear. Not just — not just the tricks the priests sell."

"I teach listening," Beatrix said. "The hearing comes from the dead. They decide who they talk to."

"It's my father." He unwrapped a bundle with shaking hands: a jawbone, yellowed, old. "He died when I was six. Before the Ban even. We never had a charm made — couldn't afford it, and then it was illegal, and Ma kept the bone hidden in a wall for twenty years." His voice cracked. "Now everyone says the dead are loud again, and I keep thinking — twenty years, he's been waiting twenty years in a wall, and I never — I don't even remember his voice. I was six. I don't remember his *voice*."

The mother was crying silently now, holding her hat like a shield.

Beatrix took the jawbone. Set it against her ear-bone, behind the mastoid, in the old way, the way Sefa must have taught her — must have, because her body knew the motion before her mind did, tilting her head at precisely the angle that opened the inner ear of the soul.

And the bone whispered.

Not words, at first. Words came second, the way meaning comes second to music. First there was a feeling — flour-dust and early mornings, a low hummed song in a language the city had half forgotten, big warm hands, the enormous tenderness of a man watching a child sleep and knowing he wouldn't see him grow. Then the words, thin and clear as light through water:

*—tall already. Look at him. He'll be taller than me by eight. Tell him the sea's honest if you're honest first. Tell him I wasn't afraid, I want him to know I wasn't—*

Beatrix lowered the bone. Her eyes were wet. She didn't hide it; hiding was over.

"He remembers you at six," she said. "He remembers your height against the doorframe. And he wants you to know he wasn't afraid." She paused, and gave the young man the truth entire, because these people deserved it. "He's quiet now. He said what he waited twenty years to say. But he said it *to you*, through me, and now it's yours. That's what the charms are. Not voices on demand. Letters delivered late."

The young man held the jawbone to his own neck, and his face did what Tomas's face had done that morning — went somewhere young and wondering — and his mother made a sound like a hinge opening after years rusted shut, and Beatrix stood behind her counter in her own shop, under her own crooked-painted sign, and watched two strangers receive a letter two decades late.

This. This was the whole of it. Not the unbinding, not the armies, not the king restored or the gods returned. This: a city learning to hear itself again, one whispering bone at a time. Ten years she had stayed small, stayed silent, carved in secret and burned the shavings, and she had thought invisibility was safety. It had only ever been starvation. You cannot feed a gift by hiding it. You cannot feed a life that way either.

Anselm appeared beside her, sleeves rolled, smelling of incense and honest labor, and said nothing, because he had learned that from her — when to let a moment stand unbuilt.

Wren climbed onto the stool behind the counter, chin in hands, watching the dockworker's family weep and hold each other, and said, with the terrifying simplicity of children:

"We should open a bigger school."

"A school," Beatrix said.

"For listeners. In every city. Harrowgate, and the Salt Quarter, and up north where they burned the shrines deepest. There are bones everywhere, Beatrix. There are *letters everywhere* nobody's delivered." She said it the way she said everything the gods touched — casually, enormously, as if suggesting lunch.

Beatrix looked at Anselm. Anselm looked at the ceiling, the way he did when he was trying not to smile and losing. "I know a few defrocked priests," he offered. "And a king who owes us rather a lot, and has recently discovered he enjoys founding things."

"We'll need a name," Beatrix said.

And her hands — her faithful, stubborn, remembering hands — were already moving before she thought, sketching on scrap paper the whorl pattern she carved without knowing why, the signature in the world's skin, the mark of a woman she had loved and lost and somehow, impossibly, still carried.

"*Sefa's House*," she said, and did not know why the name made her throat close, and did not need to know, and wrote it down anyway.

* * *

Evening came the way it did now, gently, with lamps and leftovers and Wren doing her lessons at the counter under Anselm's patient eye. The students gone home. The sign outside catching the last light. Below in the street, the lamp-lighter passed, and nodded up at the window — nodded, at *her*, knowingly, the way neighbors nod at neighbors — and Beatrix found that she still, after all these months, felt the small shock of it. Being seen. Being ordinary-seen, daylight-seen, the seen-ness of shopkeepers and aunties and people who belong to a street.

The bell rang once more, near closing. A girl of maybe sixteen, rain-wet, clutching a charm to her chest — one of Beatrix's own, months old, bought back when the shop was new.

"It started whispering again," the girl said, breathless. "Last night. I thought it was done — it went quiet after the funeral — but it woke up, and it won't stop, and I didn't know who else to—"

She held it out. Beatrix took it, set it behind her ear, in the old way, the way she had been taught by a woman whose name she carried like a coal in cupped hands.

And the charm whispered — a grandmother's voice, amused and fond and utterly unhurried, telling a story about a girl in a doorway, about a shop with a crooked sign, about patience, about how the dead are patient and so, at last, is the living —

—and Beatrix Zhou smiled, and settled onto her stool, and listened.
