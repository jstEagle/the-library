## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The trouble with selling charms for fifteen years is that you get very good at telling which customers are lying to themselves.

The girl at my stall was lying beautifully. She stood in the market crush with her hood up and her shoulders around her ears, the way people do when they're trying not to be recognized, and she asked me — in the careful, over-rehearsed voice of someone who'd practiced in a mirror — whether I had anything "for luck. Ordinary luck. Nothing strange."

"Madam," I said, spreading my hands over the tray of trinkets I'd spent the morning polishing, "everything here is ordinary. That's rather the point of it."

She wasn't buying it, which was fair, because neither was I. She had her father's jaw — Warden-Captain Hollis's jaw, unmistakable, the jaw of a man who'd spent fifteen years inspecting my gate license and finding it, grudgingly, in order. Which meant she knew exactly what the Gilded Spire was, and exactly what I was, and had come anyway, on the day before the autumn census, to buy a charm from the keeper of the only door between Vell and everything else.

I sold her the copper acorn. It did nothing, except perhaps remind her that small things could be held onto. That's most of what luck is, if you ask me. Nobody does.

"That'll be two coppers," I said, "and my professional advice, which is free: whatever you're afraid of, it's usually smaller at midnight than it looks at noon."

"It isn't," she said, and paid, and left, and I watched her go and thought: *well, that's the mood of the whole city in one transaction.*

Because it *was* the mood. Fifteen years I'd kept the Spire, and in all that time the wards had hummed along beneath the city like a second heartbeat — so constant that even the wardens had stopped pretending to check them. But lately the hum had gone thin. Not broken. Thin, the way bread goes thin when there are too many mouths. And everyone in Vell felt it without having a word for it, the way you feel weather coming in your teeth. The market was louder than usual. People were buying charms they didn't believe in. The baker had hung a horseshoe over an oven that hadn't cooled since the last reign.

I told myself it was nothing. I had gotten extremely good at telling myself things. It was, after all, my primary trade skill, more than the charms: I told myself the wards didn't need tending. I told myself my mother's work would hold forever, because it had to have been built to hold forever, because the alternative was unthinkable. I told myself I did not think about Auren. I told myself so thoroughly and so often that on most days I almost believed it, and on the other days I simply kept my hands busy polishing copper until the thinking stopped.

It was nearly dusk when the sky went out.

Not darkened. Not clouded. *Out* — like a lamp pinched between wet fingers. One moment the evening stars were pricking through above the rooftops, the first few, the early ones; the next, the whole bowl of heaven was a flat black nothing, as if someone had reached up and taken the lid off the world and found no light underneath.

The market did not scream, at first. It did something worse. It went quiet — four hundred people stopping mid-haggle at once, the silence arriving like a physical thing, a wave of held breath rolling down the street toward the harbor.

Then the wards shattered, and I learned what they had sounded like all along by hearing them stop.

It came up through the cobbles, through the soles of my boots, through the bones of my feet and legs and spine — a note breaking, a long sustained chord that had played under Vell for three centuries snapping all at once, every string. I felt it in my teeth. I felt it in the scar on my palm that I'd never been able to explain to any physician. I felt it in a place behind my ribs that I had spent fifteen years refusing to name, and that place *screamed*, silently, in a language I had sworn I'd forgotten.

The Gilded Spire was half a mile away. I felt it die like it was my own heart.

Around me the market came apart. Shutters banging, carts overturning, the baker's horseshoe clattering to the ground where nobody picked it up. Someone shouted that it was the plague come back. Someone else shouted that it was the wardens' doing. A woman grabbed my sleeve and demanded to know if it was the gate, if it was *me*, and I said — calmly, God knows how — "The gate holds," which was true, technically, in the way that a man with a knife in him is technically still standing.

The gate held. The covenant didn't. There's a difference, and I understood it instantly and completely, the way you understand a thing you've always known and merely pretended not to. The wards weren't walls. They were a promise. And somewhere on the other side of the world, the promise had just been called due.

I ran toward the Spire. Of course I did. Fifteen years of quiet duty doesn't evaporate; it just stops being quiet.

* * *

The crowd on Gate Street moved against me, a river of torches and panic flowing away from the Spire while I pushed upstream through it. The great doors of the gatehouse loomed ahead, gold leaf catching torchlight, and I remember thinking, absurdly, that I should have re-gilded the left panel this year, that Mother would have noticed the wear, that some part of my mind had chosen this exact moment to worry about *gilding* —

And then I saw Sable.

They were standing under the old customs arch, half in shadow, and the crowd broke around them the way water breaks around a stone — not because anyone saw them clearly, but because something in the animal brain said *not from here*. Fifteen years, and I knew the set of those shoulders before I knew anything else. I knew the way they stood slightly too still, holding themselves together out of habit. I knew the silver threading their black hair now, and the travel-worn coat, and the eyes that found mine across forty feet of screaming crowd with the terrible calm of someone who has walked a very long way to say a very short sentence.

They were supposed to be dead. Or exiled past death, which amounts to the same thing. Fifteen years ago they had walked out of my life through a door I helped close, and I had buried them so deep and so carefully that I'd genuinely believed the grave was empty — believed I'd never loved them at all, or only a little, or only in a way that didn't count.

The look on their face said otherwise. The look on their face said *you knew*.

"Dominic." Their voice shouldn't have carried over the panic. It did. It carried the way it always had, straight through noise and sense and fifteen years of careful forgetting. "The stars aren't gone. They're being *taken*. And your mother's the one who taught us how."

The crowd surged between us — a wall of torchlight and terror — and when it thinned, the arch was empty.

I stood in the middle of Gate Street with the dead chord still ringing in my bones and my mother's name in a stranger's mouth, and I understood, finally, with total clarity, that the past I had buried had not stayed buried. It had been digging its way out this whole time. It had simply waited for the sky to go dark enough to make its entrance properly.

Behind me, the Gilded Spire stood silent and gilded and hollow, a door with no promise left inside it.

Ahead of me, somewhere in the fleeing city, Sable was walking away, and every year of my life was walking with them.

I went after both.
