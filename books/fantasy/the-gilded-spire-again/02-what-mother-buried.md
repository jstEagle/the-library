## 2. What Mother Buried

The undercity of Vell smelled like wet stone and old lies, which felt appropriate, given the company I was keeping.

Sable moved through the drainage tunnels the way other people moved through their own kitchens—without looking, without hesitating, as if fifteen years hadn't passed since they'd last walked these passages. Fifteen years since the night I'd stood at the gate and watched them walk into Auren and told myself it was better this way. Told myself a lot of things, back then. I'd been very good at telling myself things.

"Keep up, keeper," Sable said over their shoulder. Their voice had changed. It used to be all warmth and mischief; now there was frost in it, a careful distance, like someone who'd learned that reaching for things got them taken away.

"I'm keeping up," I said. "I'm also keeping an eye on the fact that you're leading me deeper into a hole in the ground while wardens hunt us above."

"Would you prefer to go up?"

I thought about the market square—the screaming, the charm-sellers' stalls overturned, the warden's daughter clutching her luck charm like it might actually work now that the sky had gone black. I thought about the way my bones had sung when the Spire's wards shattered, every fracture in the covenant ringing through me like a struck bell.

"No," I admitted.

"Then keep walking."

* * *

The safehouse was a cistern once, dry for decades, its walls furred with pale moss that glowed faintly in the dark. Auren-moss. Someone had carried it across long ago, planted it here like a seed of homesickness. Sable lit no lamp; the moss was light enough, and lamplight drew eyes.

They sat on an old crate and looked at me for a long moment, and I realized with a lurch that they were deciding something. Deciding whether I could be trusted with it. Whatever *it* was.

"You knew my mother," I said. "Before. You were her—" I stopped, because I'd never actually known what Sable had been to Elara Whitlock. Emissary. Friend. The word *lover* sat behind my teeth, unspoken, along with everything else I'd never asked about those years before I was born.

"She was my friend," Sable said, which was an answer and not an answer, delivered in the tone of someone who had practiced the distinction. "And she was the finest gatekeeper Vell ever produced. And she broke the world."

The moss-light made their face strange, half-lit, half-shadowed. "You felt the wards die tonight. You've spent fifteen years telling yourself they didn't need tending. So tell me, Dominic—what did you think kept them alive?"

"Habit," I said. "Maintenance. The covenant—"

"The covenant is a promise written in blood and renewed by consent. Your mother wrote it. And fifteen years ago, she unmade it." Sable leaned forward. "Not carelessly. Not in anger. She did it deliberately, carefully, with the full weight of her office—and she did it to save this city."

I laughed. It came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Vell doesn't need saving from anything. Vell is the safest city on either side of the gate. That's the whole point of the Spire."

"Yes," Sable said softly. "It is. And do you know what it cost?"

* * *

Here is what I learned in a dry cistern beneath the city I loved, from the person I had betrayed, about the mother whose grave I visited every year with lilies:

Fifteen years ago, a plague came out of the deep roads—not from Auren, but wearing Auren's shape, riding starlight the way a thief rides a stolen horse. It crossed at the Spire during a festival, when the gate stood open and the wards sang high. Three hundred dead in the first month. The physicians called it the Grey Sleep. You may have heard your grandmother call it that too, if your grandmother was lucky enough to have survived it.

Elara Whitlock could have closed the gate entirely. Sealed it forever, cut Vell off from the other realm, let Auren fend for itself. But the covenant wasn't just a door—it was a circulation. Auren's warmth flowed into Vell's soil, Vell's rain fell into Auren's rivers, and if you cut the flow, both realms sickened. Slowly, but surely. Vell would have grayed and withered within a generation.

So instead, my mother did something worse. Something cleverer.

She rewrote the covenant. She tilted it. She built a valve where there had been a river, and she set the valve to take. Vell would receive Auren's warmth—enough to stay green, enough to stay safe, enough to keep the plague's kin from ever crossing again. And Auren would receive... less. Less each year. A slow starvation dressed up as bookkeeping.

"She told herself it was temporary," Sable said. Their voice had gone flat, the way voices go when feeling has been pressed so long it flattens into something else. "She told the council it was a tourniquet. She told herself she'd find another answer. And meanwhile, on our side of the gate, winter began."

"Our side," I repeated.

Something flickered across Sable's face—pain, or its fossil. "Did you really never wonder why I left? Why the emissaries stopped coming? Why the trade caravans thinned to nothing, year after year, until the Spire stood empty except for you and your harmless charms?" They stood, pacing the cistern's edge, and the moss-light followed them like something loyal. "Auren is dying, Dominic. Has been dying for fifteen years. The stars are going out one by one because the realm can't feed them anymore. Its people forget their own names because names are warm things, and warmth flows toward Vell. My exile wasn't punishment. It was evacuation. I was sent here to find the one person who could undo what your mother did."

"And that person is me."

"That person is you." They stopped pacing. Looked at me. "Your mother bound the new covenant in her own blood, which means only her blood can rewrite it. She died before she could. Which leaves you."

I sat with that. The moss glowed. Somewhere far above, boots rang on grates—wardens sweeping the undercity entrances, methodical as clockwork.

"My mother," I said finally, "visited the Spire every week until she died. She taught me the wards. She taught me the charms. She never once said—"

"Of course she didn't say. Guilt makes excellent masons, Dominic. It builds very quiet rooms." Sable's voice gentled, which was somehow worse than the frost. "She loved you. She loved this city. And she chose, and the choosing killed a world slowly, and she carried it alone because carrying it alone was part of the penance. You inherited her office. You inherited her silence. And now you inherit her debt."

"Inheritance is supposed to be a gift," I said.

"Inheritance is a bill," Sable said. "Someone always paid before you. Someone always pays after."

* * *

The wardens found us an hour before dawn.

I heard them before I saw them—boot-heels in the upper tunnels, the particular click of warden-irons being readied, the low voice of a sergeant giving orders. Sable heard them too; their head came up like a deer's, and for a moment the exiled emissary and the failed keeper looked at each other across a dry cistern and understood the same thing at the same time: there was no way out that didn't lead through Auren.

"They'll hang you," Sable said. "Gatekeeper who let the wards fail. They need someone to blame, and you're convenient. Me, they'll want alive—for questioning. For worse." A pause. "There's a service tunnel behind the east sluice. It runs under the wall. It ends at the Spire's root, at the threshold itself. On the other side—"

"Auren. Where the plague-shapes ride starlight and the people forget their own names."

"Auren," Sable agreed. "Where I'm exiled, and where you're a stranger, and where neither of us will be welcome." They held out their hand. It trembled slightly, and I pretended not to notice, because they had pretended not to notice mine trembling for fifteen years' worth of reasons. "The covenant-stone still stands, at the heart of the old grove. If we reach it—if we can renew what your mother broke—"

"If," I said. But I took their hand.

Their fingers closed around mine, warm despite everything, despite the dying realm they came from, and I thought: *fifteen years, and this is the first honest thing between us.*

Above us, the sergeant's voice rang off wet stone: "Seal the sluice gates. Nothing gets to the Spire."

Sable pulled me toward the dark.

"Run, keeper," they said. "Your mother's debt is due—and I know exactly where to pay it."
