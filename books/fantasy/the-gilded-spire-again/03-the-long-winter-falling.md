## 3. The Long Winter Falling

I had forgotten how Auren smelled. That was the first thing the cold took from me, apparently — not my hands, not my name, but the memory of rosemary and river-stone and the particular sweetness of air that has been loved for a thousand years. I stood on the far side of the gate with Dominic's hand still wrapped in mine and thought: *this is what dying smells like*, and then I thought: *no, this is what it smells like when no one tends the garden.*

The Spire's threshold had opened like a wound. One moment we were pressed into the undercity's dark while wardens hammered at the door above; the next, the wards had simply given up their argument with gravity, and the way through lay open, exhaling frost.

"Charming," Dominic said, because they had always coped with terror by being insufferable about architecture. "Fifteen years of pretending this place didn't exist, and it turns out to be a drafty hallway."

"Fifteen years," I said, "of pretending *I* didn't exist."

They had the grace to look stricken. Good. I wanted them stricken. Stricken was honest; everything else between us was varnish over rot.

We walked into Auren.

* * *

You should understand what Auren was, before I show you what it is. It was a country lit from within — lanterns that burned on gratitude, orchards that fruited out of season because the trees had never learned to sulk, a sky so crowded with stars that night was merely day wearing its evening clothes. The covenant kept Vell safe from our wildness and kept us warm with Vell's belief. Two realms, one heartbeat, passed back and forth across a gilded gate like a cup of tea neither side would finish.

Now the stars were going out one by one, and I do not mean dimming. I mean that you would look up and there would be a hole where a star had been, neat as a punched ticket, and the dark around it would be hungry.

We crossed the Amberfields by what light remained. The wheat had gone silver-grey, standing stiff as wire, and when Dominic brushed their hand along it, the stalks did not bend. They crumbled.

"It's beautiful," Dominic whispered, and I understood what they meant. Even dying, Auren was beautiful the way a fresco is beautiful — all the more heartbreaking for how clearly you could see what it used to be.

A village huddled at the edge of the fields, or what had been a village. The houses still stood. The people still moved among them, sweeping steps that needed no sweeping, setting tables for meals no one would eat. An old man sat on a bench outside his door, whittling a piece of wood that had long since frozen past carving.

"Grandfather," I called to him, using the word the way Auren folk use it, for any elder. "What is your name?"

He looked up. His eyes were the pale blue of thin ice, and for a moment something flickered behind them — recognition, maybe, or just the reflex of courtesy.

"I had one," he said pleasantly. "It was a good one. My wife chose it." He frowned, as if the sentence had cost him something. "She chose... she chose..."

He went back to his whittling. The knife made no sound against the wood.

Dominic's grip found my sleeve. "Sable. He doesn't—"

"Forgetting goes name-first," I said. "Names are anchors. When the warmth drains out of a place, the names are the first things to slip loose." I had seen it before, in the border villages, fifteen years ago, before I was exiled for saying too loudly what was happening. "By midwinter he won't remember he ever had a wife. By spring he'll forget to breathe, and he'll do it smiling, because even dread takes remembering."

"That's what Mother traded away." Dominic's voice came out flat and strange. "For Vell. For us."

"Yes."

"And you still—" They stopped. Restarted. "You still came back for me. After what my family did to yours."

I looked at the old man on his bench, whittling nothing into nothing, and I did not answer, because the true answer was too large for a field of dead wheat. The true answer was that I had spent fifteen years telling myself I hated the Whitlocks, and had gotten very good at lying to everyone except myself at three in the morning.

* * *

The covenant-stone stood in a hollow above the River Liss, which had stopped flowing sometime in the last decade and now lay beneath its ice like a held breath. Once, the stone had been the size of a chapel, veined with gold, singing — actually singing, a low chord you felt in your teeth. Pilgrims had come from both sides of the gate to press their palms to it and hear the two realms agree to go on existing together.

What remained was a stump. Knee-high, black-veined, silent. Around it, the ground was scoured bare in a perfect circle, as if even the grass had decided not to witness whatever happened here.

Dominic knelt and put both hands flat on the stone. I watched their face do several things quickly — hope, horror, and then the particular blankness of someone reading their own death warrant.

"It wants blood," they said. "No. More than blood. It wants a life. Freely given." They looked up at me, and their eyes were wet and furious. "That's why Mother couldn't fix it. She broke it to save lives. She couldn't pay with one without making the saving pointless."

"She could have paid with her own," I said quietly.

"She had children." Dominic said it like an accusation aimed at themselves. "So do I, apparently. A whole city of them."

And here is the thing I had planned for three hundred miles of frozen road, rehearsed in the undercity dark while wardens kicked doors above us, polished and turned over until it shone: I would reach the stone first. I would speak first. Because the ritual demands a life *freely given*, and there is no version of this where Dominic Whitlock dies on that stone — not after their mother already stole half a world to keep them breathing, not after I had waited fifteen years to see them again and intended to wait considerably longer than that.

I put my hand on the stone beside theirs. It was cold the way the space between stars is cold.

"Auren needs a keeper who belongs to it," I said. "Vell needs a keeper who belongs to it. You're the only bridge left, Dominic. If you give yourself, both worlds lose the gate forever. If I give mine—" I smiled, and I am told my smiles have always been slightly wicked, which is useful at funerals. "If I give mine, Auren gets its emissary home, Vell keeps its keeper, and everyone gets exactly what they deserve except me, which is how I prefer it."

"Sable—"

"I loved you," I said. "Past tense is a lie. Present tense costs me nothing now. I loved you when your mother closed the gate, and I loved you when you stood in Vell market square and pretended not to know me, and I love you now, kneeling in the ruin your family made, and I have never once stopped, and it has been the single most inconvenient fact of my entire exile." My voice cracked on the last word, which I resent deeply. "So let me spend it. Let it finally buy something."

I bared my wrist to the stone. The old gold veins stirred, faintly, like fish rising toward bread.

"Dominic," I said, "don't watch if you can't bear it. But let me—"

Their hand closed over my wrist.

And they spoke first.

* * *

Later — much later, though I will not learn how much later, nor remember whose voice taught me the word for starlight — I will wake in a city called Vell with snow on my shoulders and a stranger's kindness in my pocket. But that is another chapter, and it does not belong to me.

Here, at the stone, Dominic Whitlock looked at me with tears freezing on their cheeks and said, in the tone of someone laying down a winning hand they'd hidden for fifteen years:

"You always did insist on going first. It's the most annoying thing about you." And they pressed their own palm, bleeding, onto the covenant-stone, and began to speak the words of giving — and every word was my name.
