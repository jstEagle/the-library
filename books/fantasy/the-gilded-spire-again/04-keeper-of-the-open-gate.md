## 4. Keeper of the Open Gate

I have been told, since, that I screamed. That Sable screamed louder. That the sound of us together was what cracked the frost off the covenant-stone in the first place — two voices arguing over who got to die for love of a dying world, which is either the most romantic thing that ever happened on that frozen hillside or the most ridiculous, and knowing Sable, they'd insist it was both.

But memory is a strange country to narrate from, so let me tell it as I remember remembering it.

Sable had already laid their palm flat against the stone. The old Auren script along its face had begun to glow, hungry, patient, the way a lamp glows before you've decided to light it. *A life freely given,* the ritual wanted. Not taken. Not traded. Given — which is the cruelest fine print ever carved into rock, because it means the stone cannot be tricked, only persuaded.

"No," I said.

"Dominic—"

"No." I said it again because the first one came out too small, and some words need saying at full size. "Fifteen years ago I let you walk through that gate and I told myself it was duty. It wasn't. It was cowardice wearing duty's coat. I am not doing that twice."

"You don't understand what it's asking," Sable said, and their voice did something I'd never heard it do — it shook. "It doesn't just take your breath, Dom. It takes *you*. Everything you are goes into the seal. Every face. Every name."

"Good," I said. "Then everyone I've wronged gets to forget me too. Seems fair."

"It isn't—"

"Sable." I took their hand off the stone. Their fingers were freezing; everything about them was freezing; fifteen years of exile will do that. "You spent those years carrying my mother's sin across a border she sealed shut. You came back. You found me. You still—" I stopped, because finishing that sentence out loud felt like spending money I hadn't earned yet. But the stone was listening, and maybe honesty spends better in front of witnesses. "You still looked at me like I was worth crossing a dying world for. Let me be worth it once."

And then, because I have always been better with objects than with people, I put both hands on the covenant-stone and I started giving.

*

Here is what pouring yourself into an ancient magical artifact feels like: it feels like being a shopkeeper on the last day of business, selling everything off, watching each item go and feeling lighter and emptier with every transaction.

The market day went first. The nervous warden's daughter with her luck charm — gone, though I felt the shape of her leaving, a small warm absence like a coin-sized hole in a coat pocket. Then Vell itself: the crooked streets, the smell of frying onions near the Spire steps, the particular way rain sounded on the gate's copper roof. My mother's hands teaching me ward-script. My father's laugh, which I had not thought about in twenty years and which left so fast I didn't even get to say goodbye to it properly.

Sable fought me the whole way down. I could feel them clawing at the edges of the ritual, trying to pull memories back out of the stone like a child rescuing toys from a flood. They saved three. I know this because those three are all I woke up with, and I have spent every night since trying to imagine what the rest of me looked like.

What survived: the smell of cedar smoke. The color of a certain kind of dusk. And a single sentence, floating unattached in my head like a boat cut loose from its mooring:

*Some inheritances must be paid forward, not kept.*

I don't remember thinking that. I think someone loved me enough to leave it behind as instructions.

The last thing I gave the stone was the newest thing I owned, and the most expensive: the look on Sable's face when I said their name like a promise instead of an apology. I held onto that one until my fingers — metaphorical fingers, the ones you grip your own life with — went numb. Then the stone drank it, and the hillside lit up like dawn had gotten lost and arrived six hours early, and starlight came pouring back into Auren through a crack exactly the size of one stubborn former gatekeeper.

I would like to report that I experienced transcendence. What I actually experienced was the distinct sensation of being folded, neatly, like a letter someone intends to read later.

*

I woke in Vell.

This surprised me. I had assumed becoming a living seal meant staying a living seal — standing at the Spire forever, arms presumably getting tired. But it turns out a seal is not a wall. A seal is a hinge. And hinges are allowed to swing.

The stars were back. All of them, or nearly — a few constellations still missing, like guests stuck in traffic, but the sky over Vell had its jewelry on again and the whole city had turned out to stare at it. I was sitting on the Spire steps with a blanket around my shoulders that I did not put there, and there were people around me, and every single one of them knew my name.

"There they are!" cried a broad woman in a fishmonger's apron, gripping my hand like we were old friends. "Dominic! Stars above, Dominic, you're awake!"

"Welcome back," said a young man with a warden's badge, eyes red-rimmed. "We thought — after the night the sky went black, when you walked into the Spire and the light took you — Dom, we thought we'd lost you."

*Dom.* Everyone called me *Dom*. The familiarity should have been terrifying. Instead it was like being handed a gift box with no card: I knew it was mine, I just couldn't remember buying it.

"I'm sorry," I said carefully. "Have we met?"

The fishmonger laughed, wet-eyed. "Met? You sold my daughter her first charm when she was seven. Charged her a button for it. You've kept the gate for fifteen years, Dom. You're the reason any of us slept easy."

"The wards," the young warden said, and his voice dropped reverent. "They're different now. Softer. Like the city's breathing again. Whatever you did in there—"

Whatever I did in there. I nodded along, wearing gratitude like a borrowed coat, and searched the crowd over their shoulders for a face I couldn't name but would apparently know on sight.

I found it at the edge of the square, half in shadow, where the lamplight gave up.

They were tall and thin and wrapped in a traveler's cloak, and they were looking at me the way people look at a house they used to live in. When our eyes met, they flinched — braced, actually braced, for a blow or a recognition, I couldn't tell which. Cedar smoke reached me across the crowd. Dusk-colored eyes.

My chest did something complicated and unauthorized.

I stood. The blanket fell. The crowd murmured. And I crossed the square toward a stranger who was crying without making any sound about it, holding out the three things I had left — the smoke, the dusk, the sentence — like a beggar presenting coins he can't account for.

"I don't know you," I said. "But I think I paid a great deal for you. Would you — would you tell me what I bought?"

And Sable — exiled emissary, keeper of other people's sins, the person I had evidently loved hard enough to hollow myself out for — smiled through the tears and said:

"It'll take a while. Fifteen years, give or take. Do you have time?"

The first new star of the evening chose that exact moment to come home overhead, bright as a struck match, and I heard myself answer before I understood why the words felt like a key turning in a lock I didn't know I had:

"For you? I suspect I always did."
