## 3. Ash on the Wind

Three days past the last shepherd's hut, the mountains stopped pretending to be kind.

Coral had known the Thornfells all their life — had drawn them a hundred times from the safety of Lowmark's back rooms, from memory and rumor and that other sense, the one they never named aloud — but knowing a mountain and walking it were different countries entirely. The pass narrowed to a throat of grey stone. Wind came down off the icefields in long, keening gusts that smelled of nothing at all: no pine, no smoke, no sheep, no people. Just cold, and the mineral breath of rock that had never been touched.

Sable Quill walked ahead, as they always did now, boots finding purchase on scree with the easy economy of someone who had been lied to by mountains before and refused to be lied to twice. They hadn't spoken in an hour. The silence between them had changed texture since Lowmark — since the criers, since the burning map, since Coral had watched Sable watch them with an expression too complicated to be simple greed and too hungry to be anything else.

*You see places that don't exist,* Sable had said on the first night out, not accusing, just laying the words down like cards on a table. *That's worth more than gold up here. That's worth a way through.*

Coral had not answered. Some truths were doors you didn't open until you knew what was standing behind them.

The gift, though — the gift did not ask permission. It rose in Coral unbidden whenever the world thinned, and here, in this high and empty country, the world was thin as old paper. All morning it had been flickering at the edges of their vision: a bridge where there was no bridge, glimpsed for half a heartbeat over a gorge; a line of cairns that appeared and vanished like breath on glass; once, terribly, a road — a real road, paved, wide enough for carts — running straight through a cliff face, there and gone again before Coral could fix its shape in memory.

Each time, Coral's chest tightened with the same mixture of wonder and sickness. *This is why they hunt us.* Not because the gifted were dangerous. Because the gifted were proof that the world was larger than the crown's maps allowed, and crowns could not abide a larger world.

"Smoke," Sable said suddenly, without turning around.

Coral lifted their head. At first there was nothing — only wind and the pale glare of afternoon on snowfield — and then, threading through the wind's keening, a smell. Not woodsmoke. Woodsmoke had a homeliness to it, a hearth-smell. This was older. It smelled of struck flint and hot iron and something beneath both, something almost sweet, like resin burning in a distant temple. It pulled at something behind Coral's eyes, some door in them that had been rattling on its hinges for three days.

"That's not a campfire," Coral said.

"No." Sable had gone very still, hand drifting toward the knife at their belt in a way that was probably unconscious and probably also not. "That's not a campfire."

They followed it anyway. There was nowhere else to go; the pass ahead bent eastward, and the smell bent with it, and Coral's gift was pulling now in earnest, a hook set deep behind their sternum, reeling them forward through the thinning air.

* * *

They found the dragon at dusk, in a bowl of stone below a glacier's broken snout, and Coral understood immediately that everything they had ever drawn — every careful line, every loving contour, every map that had made their name in Lowmark's markets — had been the scribbling of a child who had never seen the sea.

Emberlast lay along the basin's floor like a fallen cathedral. Her scales were the color of cooling coals, black shot through with veins of dying orange, and where the light caught her flanks it moved — actually moved, a slow pulse traveling under the scale-plates as though her body were full of banked fires deciding whether to go out. She was vast beyond reason. A wing, folded against her side, was torn along its lower edge, the membrane ragged and crusted dark. Three arrows stood in the muscle of her shoulder, their shafts snapped short, fletched in crown blue.

She was breathing. That was the terrible part. Each breath drew in with a sound like a bellows in an empty forge and went out again as a thread of smoke so faint it was nearly invisible, rising from her nostrils into the freezing air and unraveling there, unwritten.

"She's hurt," Coral whispered, though whispering was absurd. You might as well whisper before a mountain range.

"She's bait," Sable said flatly. "Or a trap. Crown arrows, Coral. Someone put those in her, and whoever it was will come back to finish it." But Sable hadn't turned away either. Even the smuggler — pragmatic, mercenary, self-interested down to the marrow — stood at the basin's rim with their face lit orange by a fire that wasn't burning, and looked, for one unguarded moment, like a person witnessing scripture.

The dragon's eye opened.

It was the size of a cart wheel and the color of molten gold going grey at the rim, like a coin left too long in a dead man's pocket, and it fixed on them with a slowness that was worse than any quickness. Coral felt that gaze the way they felt the gift: as pressure, as attention, as being *seen*. For twenty-nine years Coral had hidden what they were, folding the gift small each morning like a letter no one must read. Now two ancient eyes read them anyway, top to bottom, and what they found there made the great head shift, grinding stone to gravel, until the nostrils — close enough now that their exhalation stirred Coral's hair, warm and smelling of iron and incense — hovered above them.

"You," the dragon said.

Her voice was not a voice. It was a landslide given grammar, a depth-sound from some ocean beneath the world, and it resonated in Coral's ribs and teeth and the backs of their knees. Sable swore and stumbled backward, knife out, and the dragon did not even glance at them. Her attention was a physical weight, and all of it rested on Coral.

"You are one who sees," she said. "I can taste it on you. Like salt on wind."

"I don't know what you mean." The lie came automatically, worn smooth from use, and the dragon's nostril flared — a laugh, or the ghost of one, if dragons laughed.

"Little cartographer. I am four hundred years old. I have eaten liars cleverer than you and slept through better ones." The golden eye narrowed, and something in it guttered — literally guttered, the light within it dipping like a candle in a draft — and when it steadied again, the arrogance in it had been replaced by something that made Coral's throat close. Weariness. Ancient, bottomless weariness. "But I have not the strength to waste on games. So I will say it plainly. My name is Emberlast. I am the last keeper of the fire, and I am dying, and before I die there is a door that must be found. And you, little seer, are the only creature left in this world who can find it."

"The fire," Sable said from several safe paces away, voice carefully level. "You mean dragon-fire. The crown's been hunting—"

"The crown has been hunting *me*," Emberlast said, and now she did turn her head, slowly, and Sable went white. "For my flame. Do you know what dragon-fire is, smuggler? It is not heat. Heat is common. Every peasant's hearth has heat. Dragon-fire is *memory made burnable*. It is the only substance in creation that can write upon things that have been unwritten." Her gaze slid back to Coral, and the weight of it doubled. "And your gift, little seer, is the only pair of eyes that can still find the things that were unwritten. Do you begin to understand what you are? What we are, together?"

Coral's mouth was dry. "No," they said honestly. "Show me."

And the gift chose that moment — or the dragon called it, or the dying fire in her blood reached out and struck Coral's like flint on steel — to flare.

* * *

Later, Coral would try to describe it and fail. It was not a vision, exactly. Visions happened *to* you. This was more like a page turning in a book Coral hadn't known they were holding.

One moment they stood in a stone basin beneath a glacier. The next, the stone basin was still there — but so was everything else, layered over it like ink showing through cheap paper.

A tower stood where the glacier's snout had been. It rose from the rock impossibly, slender and white-shot-with-gold, climbing higher than any structure Coral had ever seen or imagined, its spire catching a light that did not come from the sun — a warm, honeyed radiance, sourceless, the light of a country that remembered its own mornings. Gilt ran down its sides in rivers frozen mid-flow. Windows climbed its height in ranks, hundreds of them, thousands, and every window was lit, and behind every window Coral glimpsed movement: figures passing, hands raised in greeting or warning, laundry strung between balconies, a whole vertical city alive inside a single needle of stone.

At its base, half-buried in the living rock, was a door. Enormous, banded in gold, carved with script Coral's eyes could not parse but their gift drank in like water — and the door was *shut*. Sealed. Chains of light lay across it, and the chains were fraying, link by link, and with each link that parted Coral felt the tower flicker, felt the windows dim, felt the whole impossible structure lean a degree closer to vanishing.

And written across the door, in letters of fire that Coral somehow understood without reading:

*THORNFELL.*

The name hit them like a blow. Not because it was unfamiliar — because it was *familiar*, because it lived somewhere in them already, in the same place the gift lived, a word their grandmother had hummed once over the wash-tub and never explained, a word that appeared in no book, on no map, in no ledger of the realm, and yet here it was, enormous, gilded, *true*, and Coral's whole body shook with the recognition of something they had never been told and had always known.

"Coral." Sable's voice, far away, urgent. "Coral, your nose is bleeding. *Coral.*"

The vision held one heartbeat longer. Long enough for Coral to see, at the tower's summit, a figure standing at the highest window — small, distant, unmistakably looking back at them. Long enough to feel the door strain against its chains. Long enough to understand, with a certainty that settled into their bones like frost, that the tower was dying. That whatever it was — country, refuge, heart — it was going out, link by fraying link, and had been going out for a very long time.

Then the glacier returned. The wind returned. Coral was on their knees in the scree with blood dripping from their chin onto the stone, and the dragon's golden eye was inches from their face, and in it they saw reflected a small, bleeding figure kneeling in the ash-colored light.

"You saw it," Emberlast said softly. The softness was worse than the landslide-voice. It was the tenderness of the very tired. "Tell me what you saw."

"A tower," Coral managed. Their voice came out shredded. "Gold and white. Full of people. And a door, chained shut, and—" They stopped. Swallowed. "Thornfell. It said Thornfell. But there's no such place. There's never been—"

"There has," said the dragon. "There is. There simply is no longer room for it in the world the victors drew." She lowered her great head until her brow nearly touched the stone, and the gesture was so human in its exhaustion that Coral's fear cracked clean through. "Four hundred years ago, little cartographer, there was a country called Thornfell. It sat in these mountains the way a jewel sits in a setting. And it was erased. Not conquered — *erased*. Unwritten from every map, every memory, every record, by a treaty signed in blood and sealed in fire, until not even its own scattered children remembered its name. Only three things survived the unmaking. The door, which cannot be destroyed. The fire, which alone can rewrite what was unwritten." Her breath went out, a long thread of smoke. "And the seeing. Which passed down through bloodlines, thinning, thinning, generation by generation, until it pooled in you."

The wind keened through the pass. Somewhere above, ice calved from the glacier with a crack like a rifle-shot, and neither of them flinched.

"Why me?" Coral whispered. "Why show me?"

"Because I am dying, and the door dies with me, and I would rather spend my last strength opening it than gutter out guarding a tomb." The golden eye closed, then opened, and the light in it was dimmer than before. Coral watched it happen. Watched the fire in her recede like a tide going out. "The crown hunts me for my flame because flame is power, and crowns collect power the way magpies collect silver. They do not know what the flame is *for*. If they knew—" The dragon's lip curled back from teeth like plough-blades. "If they knew, they would not merely kill me. They would take the fire to the Spire and burn the door to ash, and end it forever, and call it peace."

Behind Coral, Sable had gone very quiet. Too quiet. Coral knew that quiet; they'd heard it in Lowmark's taverns, in the pauses of men counting money that wasn't theirs. But there was no time to turn, no time to wonder, because Emberlast was speaking again, and her voice had dropped to something barely louder than the wind.

"They are coming for me even now, little seer. Inquisitors. I can smell their cold iron on the wind from here. When they come, they will find me too weak to fight, and they will find *you*, and they will make you show them the door." The great head lifted, with effort, until the dying gold filled Coral's entire sky. "So I offer you the only choice there is. Walk away now, and live, and let the last door in the world close forever. Or stay. And learn what your gift is truly for. And help me spend what remains of the fire on something greater than survival."

Coral knelt in the ash-smelling wind, blood drying on their chin, awe still ringing through them like a struck bell — and beneath the awe, spreading slow and cold as meltwater through a crevasse, the dread.

Because they had seen the tower. They had seen the fraying chains. And they had understood, in that final flickering instant, exactly one thing more:

The figure in the highest window had not been greeting them.

It had been *warning* them away.

And far below, back down the pass they had climbed, so faint it might have been imagination — but Coral's gift never dealt in imagination — a second smell threaded the wind. Cold iron. Oiled steel. Inquisitors' wagons, rolling toward the mountains, carrying men who burned maps and the people who made them.

Emberlast's nostril flared. The golden eye fixed on Coral, and in it, plain as print, was the same knowledge.

"They're coming," Coral said.

"Yes," said the dragon, and began, painfully, to rise.
