## 4. What the Crown Wants

The cells beneath the customs house in Lowmark smelled of brine and old fear. Halvard Vane had learned to read that smell the way a sailor reads weather: the fresher the fear, the more useful the prisoner.

He descended the stairs with his ledger under one arm and his lantern held high enough to cast no shadows worth hiding in. The stone steps were slick with sea-damp; gulls cried somewhere above, muffled by three floors of granite and crown authority. Below, in the row of cells carved into the harbor's bones, seven people waited for him. Six of them were nothing. One of them, he suspected, was everything.

"Master Inquisitor." The gaoler, a soft-bodied man named Dreech whose hands trembled whenever he thought himself unobserved, fumbled with his ring of keys. "They've been fed. As ordered."

"I didn't order them fed out of kindness, Dreech."

"No, sir. Of course not, sir."

Vane did not explain himself. Explanation was a currency he spent only where it bought something. He had learned long ago — first as a tax clerk in the capital, later in the Inquisition's gray halls — that hunger made people honest, but starvation made them stupid. A mind kept at the edge of want stayed sharp enough to answer questions well. He wanted these prisoners sharp. He wanted their memories intact, their tongues quick, their terror precise rather than blunted into animal dullness.

Justice, properly administered, required precision.

He paused before the first cell. Inside, an old woman sat on the plank bed with her hands folded in her lap, as though she were waiting for a coach rather than an inquisitor. Her gift, according to the arrest report, was the finding of lost things — buttons, rings, children. Small magics. The kind the crown had tolerated for generations until it hadn't.

"Mistress Aldery," Vane said. "You slept?"

"A little."

"Good. Sleep keeps the mind clear, and I want your mind clear when you tell me again about the man from the eastern passes."

Her folded hands tightened, just slightly. He noted it the way he noted everything: without expression, without hurry, entering it into the ledger of small truths he carried behind his eyes.

* * *

Halvard Vane had been an inquisitor for eleven years, and in eleven years he had never once raised his voice in an interrogation. This was not mercy. It was method. Shouting told a prisoner where the edges of your knowledge lay; silence left the edges unmapped, and unmapped places frightened people far worse than any cruelty. People rushed to fill silence with confession. They invented crimes to please you. They offered up neighbors, lovers, children.

It was remarkable, Vane often thought, how much a person would surrender simply to make a quiet room less quiet.

He began, as always, with the smallest questions. What did she eat this morning. How did the porridge taste. Was the blanket sufficient. Each answer was a thread, and each thread taught him the weave of her lies. Mistress Aldery lied about small things — she claimed to have slept soundly when her eyes said otherwise, claimed the food was fine when she had left half of it — which meant she was capable of lying about large ones, and knew it, and was ashamed.

"The man from the eastern passes," Vane said again, settling onto the stool Dreech had placed for him outside the bars. He opened his ledger. "Tell me about his hands."

She blinked. "His... hands?"

"You mentioned them yesterday. Before you grew tired. You said he had ink on his hands that wouldn't wash off. I'd like you to tell me everything you remember about that. Take your time. We have all night, and I have nowhere else to be."

It took her an hour, coaxed along in Vane's unhurried murmur, to give up what she had. A traveler had come through Lowmark's eastern road two weeks past, a woman — Mistress Aldery corrected herself, a woman, not a man, memory settling like sediment once stirred — who had paid for lodging with coin and asked strange questions. Where did the Thornfell passes go. Had anyone ever mapped the high country beyond the last shepherd huts. Did the locals tell stories about roads that weren't there.

"And her hands?" Vane prompted.

"Ink," the old woman whispered. "Stained to the wrist. She drew while she talked — little maps on the table, on napkins, on the back of my son's letters. Places I'd never..." She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Places that aren't there, Master Inquisitor. She drew a valley I've dreamed about since I was a girl and never seen. My mother sang a lullaby about it. A valley with a white tower. And there it was, on her napkin, drawn plain as Lowmark harbor."

Vane wrote: *white tower.* His pen did not pause, did not press harder. A man's hand betrayed him when his face did not, and he had trained his hand into perfect obedience years ago.

"Did she give a name?"

"No. But the innkeeper's boy called after her when she left. Coral. He said her name was Coral Winterbourne. She'd bought charts off him the week before. Old ones. Charts of the Thornfells."

* * *

By the third hour, Vane had worked through four prisoners and assembled the shape of something extraordinary.

A cartographer named Coral Winterbourne, last seen in Lowmark, gifted — the gift itself still unclassified, though *perception of unmapped places* appeared in three separate testimonies now, each witness unaware the others had spoken. A woman who could see what did not exist on paper. Who drew valleys out of lullabies. Who had come asking after the high Thornfells and then vanished into them the same day the criers read the Edict of Gifts and the hunt began.

And threaded through the other testimonies, half-buried like gold in slag: a Spire.

The drunkard in cell five — a hedge-witch whose gift was the souring of milk, hardly worth the cell she occupied — had babbled about her grandmother's stories. *The Gilded Spire, the door at the end of the world, locked from the other side.* The smuggler in cell six, a hard-eyed man named Ferren whose gift let him hold his breath for a quarter hour underwater, had gone rigid when Vane mentioned the word *Spire* and denied knowing anything for forty minutes before admitting he'd heard sailors talk of it in the Anchor & Ash. Sailors' nonsense, he insisted. Drunk talk. A tower of gold at the heart of the mountains, older than the kingdom, older than the maps.

Older than the maps.

Vane closed his ledger and sat for a moment in the lamplight, listening to the sea breathe against the harbor walls above.

He was not a superstitious man. That was what he told the novices he trained, and it was true in the way all official truths are true. But he understood power, and power had a smell, and the air in this cellar had changed since sundown. Somewhere in those mountains was a thing the crown's oldest edicts danced around without naming. He had spent a week in the archives before coming to Lowmark, reading treaties sealed under triple wax, and he had noticed what a careful reader notices: gaps. Whole paragraphs redacted not by ink but by *absence*, as if the words had been removed from the page and from the memory of everyone who'd written them. A treaty about border settlements that never said which borders. A clause of perpetual obligation owed to a party whose name had been scraped away.

Thornfell. The word surfaced in one surviving marginal note in a clerk's cramped hand, and even that note had been struck through so thoroughly the parchment had thinned to translucence.

Vane did not know what Thornfell was. But he knew what it meant that no one else did either.

* * *

Cell seven held the one he'd saved for last.

The boy — no, young man, nineteen perhaps, with the rawboned look of someone who'd grown tall faster than his courage — sat pressed into the corner of his cell as far from the door as the walls allowed. His gift, per the report, was the mending of broken things. Kettles, wagon axles, a miller's arm once, badly. Useless magic. Harmless magic. The kind of magic that should never have put him in a cell, and everyone involved in his arrest had known it, which was precisely why Vane had chosen him.

"What's your name?" Vane asked, though he knew it. The question was a kindness, and kindness, deployed correctly, was the sharpest tool in the kit.

"T-Tobin. Tobin Marsh."

"Tobin Marsh. You mended a kettle for the harbormaster's wife. She reported you herself, did you know? Two days after you fixed it. She wept when the constables took you."

"She did?" The boy's voice cracked. "I only — it was cracked, and she was crying over it, her mother's kettle, and I just—"

"I know what you did. I have the full account." Vane let the silence do its work. The boy filled it, as they always did, spilling out the whole small history of his gift: the things he'd mended, the fear he'd carried, the way he'd hidden it for years until one act of foolish compassion undid him. Vane listened with the appearance of total attention and the reality of total patience, and when the boy finally ran dry, trembling, Vane spoke softly.

"Tobin. Do you know why you're here, and not the others?"

"N-no, sir."

"Because you're innocent." He let that land. Watched the boy's face perform the whole theater of hope — the widening eyes, the caught breath, the terrible fragile lifting of the shoulders. "Your crime is a mended kettle. The Edict is broad, but the crown is not blind, and neither am I. There are people in these cells who have done true harm. You are not one of them. And I believe in proportion, Tobin. Justice without proportion is merely violence wearing a uniform."

"Then — you'll release me?"

"I am going to do better than release you." Vane rose, and Dreech, hovering, unlocked the cell with hands that shook. "I am going to give you a task worthy of your innocence. There is a woman in these mountains. Coral Winterbourne. She may be dangerous; she may be deceived; she may be nothing at all. I need someone to find her and speak with her. Someone harmless. Someone she won't fear." He produced a folded paper — travel writ, provision chit, a purse heavy enough to be heard — and held it out. "Find her. Tell her the crown's ear is open. Tell her that if she comes to me willingly, she will be treated as a witness, not a traitor. Can you do that, Tobin Marsh?"

The boy took the purse like a man accepting absolution. "Yes. Yes, sir. Thank you, sir. I swear I'll—"

"I know you will." Vane smiled. It was, by all accounts, a reassuring smile; he had practiced it in a mirror until it passed for warm. "One more thing. If you think of fleeing instead — if the mountains tempt you, or she does — remember that I released you. Which means I can find you. I found six strangers in a city of thousands in a single week. Finding one grateful boy will take me an afternoon."

The smile remained. The warmth did not. Tobin Marsh nodded very fast and fled up the stairs toward the gray daylight, and Vane watched him go with the satisfaction of a man releasing a carrier dove.

* * *

He climbed to the customs house's upper floor, where the harbor wind came through ill-fitted windows and the gulls screamed their endless grievance, and stood at the window looking east. The Thornfells stood up beyond the low hills like the spine of some buried god, snow-streaked, blue-shadowed, indifferent. On every map Vane owned, the high country beyond the last passes was blank. Not empty — blank. The surveyors' guild marked unknown lands with hatching and a legend: *unexplored.* These mountains bore no such courtesy. They simply stopped, the way a sentence stops when the writer has forgotten what he meant to say.

*A valley with a white tower,* an old woman had said. *A door at the end of the world,* a drunkard had babbled. *Older than the maps.*

Vane opened his ledger to a fresh page and wrote in his small, exact hand:

*Coral Winterbourne. Cartographer. Gift: sight of unwritten places. Last seen entering the Thornfell high country, day of the Edict. Seeks: the passes, the old charts, the roads that are not there.*

Below it:

*The Gilded Spire. Confirm existence. Determine why the throne's own archives bleed around the word.*

He considered the second line for a long moment. Then, because a good inquisitor trusted paper least of all things, he committed it to memory and burned the page in the window's draft, watching the ash spiral out over the harbor like dark snow.

Ambition was a word his enemies would use, if he ever allowed himself enemies. Vane preferred *thoroughness*. The crown had declared the gifted traitors, and he served the crown, and somewhere in the blank heart of those mountains was a truth the crown had spent generations not-knowing. Men had been destroyed for learning such truths. Men had been made for it. Halvard Vane had spent eleven years climbing the gray ladder of the Inquisition one rung at a time, patient as erosion, and he knew — with the cold certainty he brought to everything — that ladders ended either at the top or at the neck.

He intended to reach the top. And if the way up ran through a hunted cartographer and a door at the end of the world, then so much the better. Truth, properly handled, was the most valuable cargo a man could carry.

Below him, small on the eastern road, a figure with a mended-kettle gift and a liar's errand walked toward the mountains.

Vane watched until the distance took him, then turned back to his ledgers, already composing the report that would make his fortune: *Progress in the Lowmark inquiries. A subject of exceptional interest has been identified. The crown's justice follows.*
