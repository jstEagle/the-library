## 5. The Road That Isn't There

The road began where the map ended.

That was not a figure of speech, though Coral had learned to speak in figures the way other people learned to breathe. It was a literal truth, and it unsettled them more than any of the strange things that had happened since they'd pulled Emberlast's arrow from her shoulder: the road simply *began*, a seam of pale stone threading up through the scree like a vein in flesh, and no one had ever drawn it because no one had ever seen it.

Coral saw it.

They stood at the edge of the pass with the dawn coming up gray behind them, Emberlast's breath steaming warm against their back, and watched the road unspool itself into the mountains ahead — narrow, switchbacked, climbing toward a col that appeared on no chart ever inked in Lowmark or the capital or anywhere else. It was *there*. Coral could have walked it blindfolded. They could have described every bend of it, every loose stone, the way water pooled in its ruts after rain. And yet when they looked away — truly looked away, letting their gift go quiet the way they'd learned to as a child, folding the sight down small and tight — there was nothing at all. Just cliff face and talus slope and the indifferent white noise of the Thornfells.

"It's not real," Sable said.

Sable stood a few paces off with their arms crossed against the cold, hood back, dark hair whipping in the wind that funneled through the pass. They had the look they always had at first light — like someone who'd been awake all night doing arithmetic they didn't like.

"It's real," Coral said. "It's just not *written*."

"That's a distinction without a difference."

"No." Coral crouched and pressed their palm flat against the pale stone. It was cold, of course; everything in these mountains was cold except the dragon. But beneath the cold was something else, faint as a heartbeat through a wall. "Paper is written on. This is written *in*. Feel."

Sable hesitated — Sable always hesitated, weighing everything, trusting nothing — then knelt and laid their hand beside Coral's. For a long moment nothing happened. Then Sable's breath caught, a small involuntary sound, and their fingers curled against the stone as if it might bolt.

"There's a—" They stopped. Started again. "There's a word under it. Under the rock. I can't read it but I can feel the shape of it."

"You can't read it because it isn't for you." Coral rose, brushing grit from their knees. "It's for me. Or it was, once, before whoever wrote this country out of the world made sure only the gifted could still see the handwriting."

Emberlast rumbled behind them, a sound like a millstone turning over in sleep. The dragon lay along the shelf of rock with her wounded wing folded close, scales the color of banked coals gone dull, and even now, three days after they'd found her bleeding in the high snowfields, she moved like something conserving herself for a purpose she hadn't yet named aloud. Her great head swung toward the invisible road, and her nostrils flared.

"*Old,*" she said. Her voice was a landslide given grammar. "*Older than the erasing. The treaty-mages burned what they could burn and buried what they couldn't, but roads remember feet. Enough feet, and a road will not be forgotten. Not entirely.*"

"Then who walked it?" Sable asked.

"*No one living.*"

Coral felt those words settle into their chest like swallowed river stones. But there was no time to sit with the feeling, because far below them — so far it might have been imagination, except Coral's gift had never once mistaken imagination for danger — a thread of smoke rose from the tree line in the valley behind. Woodsmoke. Cookfire smoke. And beneath it, thin and wrong, the particular bitter reek of a signal fire burning pitch.

"They've found the camp we left," Coral said quietly.

Sable turned, shading their eyes. "Too fast. We covered our trail."

"We covered it from men." Coral looked at the smoke a moment longer, then shouldered their pack. "Not from whatever they're using to hunt us. Vane has gifted prisoners, Sable. He doesn't need trackers when he can make a traitor feel for us the way I feel for a road."

Something flickered across Sable's face — quick, quickly buried, a shutter closing over a window. Coral had seen it twice before and told themselves both times it was only fear. Fear was reasonable. Fear was honest. They hoisted the pack higher and did not look at Sable's hands.

"Well then," Sable said lightly, too lightly. "Shall we take the road that isn't there?"

"We shall," said Coral, "and pray it stays that way."

* * *

Walking an unwritten road was like walking inside a held breath.

By midmorning they were deep into country that Coral knew intimately and had never seen — which was, they were beginning to understand, the whole condition of being what they were. Their gift didn't show them imaginary places. It showed them *unrecorded* ones, places that existed stubbornly outside the reach of ink and decree, and until three days ago Coral had assumed such places were merely gaps: smugglers' trails, hidden valleys, the small blank spaces every kingdom accumulates like lint.

Now they knew better. Now they knew that the largest unwritten place in the world was a whole country, erased by treaty magic generations ago, surviving only in the margins — and that Coral's gift had been quietly reading the margins their entire life without understanding the language.

The road climbed. It switchbacked up a shoulder of mountain that the crown's maps showed as sheer cliff, and the sheer cliff obligingly became a rampart of weathered stone on their left while the drop on their right fell away into cloud. Pine forest clothed the slopes in dark green, dense and old, the trees massive in a way trees weren't anymore in the mapped world. There were no stumps. No cut limbs. No blazes on trunks, no charcoal marks, none of the small signatures travelers leave. The forest had never been logged because, as far as the world's memory went, it had never been found.

And yet the road was worn. Hollowed, actually — centuries of passage had dished it into the mountainside like a spoon dragged through wax.

"Someone built this," Sable said. They'd been quiet for hours, which Coral had learned meant their mind was working hard. "Someone quarried this stone and graded this grade and cut back this forest, and then everyone forgot them. Not died out. *Forgot.* You don't forget a road like this by accident."

"The treaty-mages didn't leave accidents," Coral said. "Emberlast said they burned what they could burn and buried what they couldn't. A road is hard to burn. So they buried it — in forgetting. Everyone's forgetting. Except—"

"Except people like you." Sable glanced sideways. "How many are left? People like you?"

"I don't know. Fewer every year, since the hunt started." Coral kept their eyes on the road. "My mother could do what I do. She called it *seeing the seams*. She said the world was a garment and most people only ever saw the cloth. She died when I was nine. I used to think it was fever." They paused. "I think about it differently now."

Sable said nothing to that, but their silence had a different texture than before — heavier, more careful, the silence of someone handling something fragile. Coral almost asked. Almost said, *you know something, don't you, you've known since Lowmark, since the letter you hide in your coat and think I haven't noticed.* But the words snagged on the same hook they'd snagged on for days: if Sable was hiding something, forcing it into the open here, on this road, with hunters behind them and no way forward but trust, would break the thing that was keeping them both alive. Trust deferred was still trust. Barely.

Ahead, Emberlast lumbered along the road's outer edge, too large for it really, her tail sweeping scree into the void with each sway. She'd insisted on walking rather than flying. *A dragon in the sky is a shout,* she'd said. *A dragon on the ground is a rumor.* Even wounded, even dulled, she understood pursuit better than either of them.

Midday brought them to the col, and the col brought Coral's gift flaring wide open like a struck bell.

They stopped so suddenly that Sable nearly walked into them. The vision came the way it always did — not seen with the eyes but *remembered forward*, as if the place were introducing itself — and for three heartbeats Coral stood in two worlds at once.

In this world: a saddle of bare rock between two peaks, wind-scoured, empty.

In the other: a gatehouse. Twin towers of gilded stone, a road running through them wide enough for wagons, lanterns hanging from iron brackets, the air thick with voices speaking a language Coral didn't know and somehow understood anyway. A toll-keeper laughing. A child chasing a dog between cart wheels. All of it vivid, all of it *present*, all of it happening in a place that had been dead for a hundred years and was, in some sense that made Coral's teeth ache, still alive.

"Gilded," Coral whispered. "Everything was gilded. The Spire isn't just gold-colored, Sable. The whole *country* was—"

The vision snapped shut. The col was empty rock again. Coral swayed, and Sable's hand shot out and gripped their arm — steadying, quick, warmer than expected.

"Coral. Hey. Breathe."

"I'm fine." They weren't, quite. The visions were getting stronger, closer together, the nearer they drew to — to *it*, to the Spire, to the door Emberlast had spoken of in her low grinding voice on the second night, when she'd told them what she was the last keeper of. "It's just loud here. The past. Like standing next to a wall with shouting on the other side."

"Can you tell how far?"

Coral closed their eyes and let the gift reach, carefully, the way a hand reaches into dark water. "Two days. Maybe three, if the weather turns." They opened their eyes. "There's a problem."

"There's always a problem."

"The road forks ahead, down in the next valley. One branch goes on toward the Spire. The other—" Coral frowned, working at the shape of it. "The other goes down into a valley that isn't. On any map. It's not just unmapped, it's *unmapped*, deliberately, the way the whole country is. And there's a checkpoint on the fork. Crown soldiers. I can feel the iron of their pikes the way you'd feel a splinter."

Sable went very still. "How many?"

"Twelve. Maybe fifteen. And something else." Coral rubbed their arms against a chill that had nothing to do with altitude. "One of them feels like me. Gifted. Serving them willingly or unwillingly, I can't tell. But they're *listening*, Sable. Sweeping the passes for the seam-feeling. If we try to slip past on the open branch, they'll hear us coming like a bell in a cathedral."

"So we go around."

"There is no around. That's the point of a valley."

Sable looked at the sky, at the road, at the dragon. "Then what's your suggestion, cartographer? Because from where I'm standing our options are dying on a road that doesn't exist or dying on one that does, and I've got a preference for neither."

Coral looked down into the next valley, where the clouds parted long enough to show a fold of green shadow between two ridges — a fold that Coral's eyes alone insisted was a valley at all, and not empty hillside. And beneath the insistence, faint as the heartbeat in the road-stone, they felt it: the same buried writing. Old. Deliberate. *Erased.*

"Not around," Coral said slowly. "Through. We go through the valley that was taken off the map."

* * *

The descent took the rest of the day, and the last hour of it was the strangest walking Coral had ever done.

The erased valley announced itself gradually. First the birdsong changed — different species, songs Coral half-knew from their mother's humming, songs that belonged to no bird in any Thornfell field guide. Then the light changed, thickening, going golden at the edges as though the sun here remembered a different angle. Then the smells: cut hay, woodsmoke, apples rotting sweetly somewhere out of sight, the whole autumnal catalogue of a lived-in country — with no farmsteads, no orchards, no chimneys. Nothing but meadow grass waist-high and whispering, and the ghost-shapes of hedgerows that their eyes slid off of like water off oiled cloth.

"This is madness," Sable murmured. They'd drawn their knife without appearing to decide to, and kept it drawn. "This is beautiful and it's madness."

"It's a memory," Coral said. "The mages couldn't kill the valley, so they killed the *knowing* of it. Everything that lived here is still here. It's just that no one outside can perceive it, and no one inside can leave or be remembered." They walked on, grass hissing against their legs. "My mother told me a story once. About her grandmother, who went out one morning to check snares and came back saying she'd found a village in the hills where the people spoke with an accent nobody recognized, and by the time she led her neighbors back the village was gone — not abandoned, *gone*, houses and well and church bell, just hillside. Everyone laughed at her. She never spoke of it again." Coral's throat tightened. "She wasn't mad. She'd found a seam. And the people in that village probably watched her walk away and waited for her to come back, and she never did, because from their side the door had already shut."

Sable had stopped walking. When Coral turned, they found the smuggler standing rigid in the tall grass, face bloodless, staring at nothing — or at something.

"Sable?"

"There's a wall," Sable said. Their voice came out flattened, careful, like someone reading their own eulogy. "A drystone wall. Right there. With a gap in it and a stile, and sheep beyond it, and—" They blinked hard, twice. "No. No, it's grass. It's just grass." They looked at Coral with an expression Coral had never seen on them before, stripped of all the smuggler's easy armor: raw bewilderment, and underneath it, terror. "Why did I see that? I'm not gifted. I've never seen anything. Why *now*?"

"Because you're inside the seam," Coral said softly. "The erasure works from outside. In here, the rules run backward. The valley can't be perceived from the world — but the world can be perceived from the valley. You're seeing what's real, Sable. Both of them at once." They hesitated, then added, honestly, because honesty felt like the only currency worth anything in this place: "I don't know what that does to a person. My mother never said."

"Your mother," Sable repeated, and there was something odd in the way they said it, something that sounded almost like guilt, though that made no sense at all. Before Coral could press, Sable shook themselves like a wet dog and strode on. "Fine. Beautiful haunted valley. Let's cross it before it decides we're trespassers."

Emberlast brought up the rear, moving with unusual care, her head low and sweeping side to side. "*Do not touch the walls,*" she rumbled at one point, when Sable drifted near a hedgerow that was and wasn't there. "*What is remembered here has weight. The weight of a hundred years of waiting. It would rather hold you than let you go.*"

Dusk came early under the valley's golden light, and they made camp in the lee of a barn that existed only when Coral looked directly at it — solid enough to block the wind, insubstantial enough that Sable's bedroll half-sank through the wall until Coral rearranged it. They ate cold provisions by Emberlast's dimmed glow, and Coral sketched by firelight, because sketching was prayer and habit both: the fork, the checkpoint, the valley's shape, the road beyond. Their pencil moved fast, sure, recording a place that no record had touched in a century.

And that was when the feeling arrived.

It came up the spine first, a cold bead rolling vertebra by vertebra, and then spread outward — the unmistakable sensation of being *watched*, not by the ghosts of the valley, whose attention was diffuse and sorrowful and everywhere, like weather, but by something focused. Something patient. Something that had been following them since the col, or before it, keeping pace just past the edge of perception, and had chosen this moment — here, in the erased valley, where Coral's gift rang loudest — to let itself be felt.

Coral set down the pencil.

"Sable."

"I feel it." Sable was already on their feet, knife out, scanning the dark meadow. Emberlast's head had come up, coals brightening in her throat, a growl building like distant thunder.

"It's not soldiers," Coral whispered. "Soldiers feel like iron and boredom. This feels like—" They searched for it, reached for it, and the watching thing receded, smooth as oil, leaving only the meadow and the moths and the impossible barn. "Like someone reading over my shoulder."

"Vane's pet?"

"Maybe." But Coral didn't believe it, and heard the doubt in their own voice. Vane's hunters swept for seams; they didn't *hide* in them. Whatever this was, it knew the unwritten country from the inside. It had been waiting for them.

Emberlast spoke into the dark, very quietly, for a dragon.

"*We are not the only ones who remember the way,*" she said. "*Sleep in turns. Tomorrow we walk faster.*"

And far off across the whispering grass, at the exact edge of hearing, something answered — not in words, not in any language, but in the soft, unmistakable sound of a footstep on an old road, taken and then deliberately, courteously, withdrawn.
