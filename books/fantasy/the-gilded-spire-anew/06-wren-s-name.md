## 6. Wren's Name

The town of Hollowmere did not appear on any map Sable Quill had ever drawn, which was fitting, because Sable had never drawn a map in their life. They left that particular magic to the person currently asleep under an oilskin tarp in the back of the smuggler's wagon, curled around a satchel of sketches like a dragon around its hoard.

Sable reined the mule to a halt at the edge of the town and sat for a moment, letting the cold work its way through their coat, taking stock the way they always took stock: two exits north, one road east along the frozen river, chimneys smoking but few, and a gallows platform by the well that had not been there the last time they'd passed through. It was empty. For now.

"Up," Sable said over their shoulder. "We're here."

A rustle, then Coral's face appeared at the flap of the tarp, pale and sleep-creased, eyes going immediately — as they always did — to the horizon, as if reading it. Maybe they were. Sable had stopped doubting it somewhere around the third impossible pass.

"Hollowmere," Coral said. "It wasn't on my map."

"No one's map. That's the point." Sable climbed down and began unhitching the mule with hands gone stiff from three days of driving. "Smugglers' rule: the best places are the ones the crown can't find. We eat hot food, we buy salt and lamp oil, we sleep under a roof for one night, and we're gone before dawn."

Coral dropped from the wagon bed and stood beside them, breath fogging. They looked thinner than when Sable had found them in Lowmark, all those weeks ago — or lifetimes ago; it was hard to keep count out here where the days ran together like wet ink. The gift had been flaring more often lately. Sable had watched them stop mid-stride on the trail, staring at nothing, lips moving as though transcribing something only they could see. A tower. A spire. Sable had asked once what they saw, and Coral had said, *a door*, and then refused to say anything else about it, and Sable had let it go, because there were things Sable was also refusing to say, and fair was fair.

*Wren,* thought Sable, and the name landed in their chest like a stone dropped down a well, falling a long way before it hit anything.

They led the mule into Hollowmere.

* * *

The inn was called the Drowned Lantern, which Sable appreciated for its honesty. Its common room smelled of tallow smoke, wet wool, and onion stew, and it was half full of river men and half full of people trying very hard not to look like anything at all. Sable paid for a room, two bowls, and discretion with coin that had no crown stamp on it, and claimed a corner table with their back to the wall and a view of the door.

Coral ate like someone who had forgotten food existed and then remembered all at once. Sable ate slowly, watching the room.

It was the second bowl, or maybe the third cup of thin cider, that loosened the tongues at the next table. Two drovers, a woman with a ledger-keeper's squint, a young man in the gray coat of a capital courier who was trying to look like he wasn't. The courier was the one Sable watched.

"—marched them through Kettering two days past," the courier was saying, low, leaning in. "Chained in files of ten. My cousin saw them from the toll bridge. Said they looked half-starved already."

"How many?" the woman asked.

"Forty? Fifty? All of them flagged. Neighbors inform, you know how it goes. Someone hums the wrong tune, someone's garden grows wrong, someone *draws*—" He shrugged, and the shrug carried a whole winter of fear in it. "They're taking them to the capital. Inquisitor Vane's order. There's to be a public reckoning at midwinter. An example made."

Sable's spoon stopped halfway to their mouth.

*Vane.* The name had been following them down every road since Lowmark, whispered in wayhouses and behind shuttered windows: Halvard Vane, the crown's new inquisitor, a man who hunted the gifted with the patience of a man doing sums. And now the sums were coming due in files of ten, chained, marched to a reckoning.

*Fifty prisoners.* Sable made themselves take the spoonful of stew, made themselves chew and swallow, because Coral was sitting right there and Coral noticed everything except, thank all the quiet gods, the inside of Sable's skull.

"Wren," Sable said silently, shaping the name without breath. Wren Quill, who had been taken in the first sweep of Lowmark's docks, who had been nineteen years old and laughing at Sable's terrible singing the week before. Who had pressed her forehead to the bars of the holding cell and said, *Go. Don't be stupid. Go.* Who had been in Sable's dreams every night since, sometimes free, sometimes drowning, always looking at Sable with that expression that was not blame, which was so much worse than blame.

"You've gone somewhere," Coral said quietly.

Sable blinked. Coral was watching them across the table, stew forgotten, head tilted. Their eyes were strange in the lantern light — not quite the color of anything, the way water isn't quite the color of the sky it reflects.

"Tired," Sable said. "Roads do that."

"Mm." Coral didn't push. That was the trouble with Coral, Sable thought, and the gift of them too: they gave you rope and trusted you not to hang yourself with it.

The courier was still talking. "—they say the inquisitor pays for information. Pays *well*. Names, routes, hiding places. There's a man in Kettering who sold out his own brother for enough to buy a boat."

"And what happened to the brother?" the woman asked.

The courier looked into his cup. "What do you think happened?"

Under the table, Sable's hand had closed around the handle of their knife without their permission. They made themselves open it. Made themselves breathe.

*Pays well.*

The thought arrived the way rot arrives in timber — from the inside, invisible, already structural. Sable hated it instantly and completely, and it did not leave. It sat down at the table with them like an old friend back from the wars, and it laid out its wares: *You have something Vane wants. You have seen the sketches. You know the route through the erased valley, the passes that don't exist, the road Coral walks like it's stitched into their skin. You could trade it. Route for prisoner. Sketches for sister. One map against one life. It's arithmetic, Sable. You were always good at arithmetic.*

Across the table, Coral laughed at something — at nothing, at the stew, at some small joke they'd made to fill the silence — and the sound went through Sable like a fishhook.

*Don't,* Sable told the thought. *Don't you dare.*

But the thought had already seen the arithmetic, and arithmetic does not unsee itself.

* * *

They took the room upstairs. One bed, one cot, a window painted over with frost. Coral fell asleep almost immediately — the gift spent them in ways they never admitted, Sable had learned to read the signs, the gray lips, the trembling hands they tucked under their arms — and Sable lay on the cot listening to them breathe and staring at the ceiling beams and losing, hour by hour, the argument they had been having with themselves since the Drowned Lantern.

Because here was the shape of it, laid out plain:

Coral's sketchbook held the route. Not finished maps — Coral worked slow, careful, each drawing a small act of devotion — but sketches, working pages, the bones of the way through. The pass above the frozen river. The valley whose name had been scraped out of every memory that ever held it. The ridgeline that walked you toward the tower only Coral could see. If those pages reached Vane's hands, Vane would have the road to the Spire, whatever the Spire was. Vane would have *them*, eventually, because a man who buys a road buys the right to ride down it.

And if those pages reached Vane's hands, and Vane was a man who paid for information —

*One map against one life.*

Sable got up quietly, boots in hand, and crossed the cold floorboards to where Coral's pack leaned against the wall.

They told themselves they were only checking. Only looking. Only confirming what they already knew, which is what every thief says at the threshold of the house they've already decided to rob.

The sketchbook was bound in oiled leather, soft as skin from handling. Sable knelt with it in their lap and opened it, and this was the mistake, this was the wound of it: the drawings were beautiful. Of course they were. Coral drew the way other people prayed. Here was the frozen river rendered in strokes so fine they seemed less ink than breath on glass. Here was the erased valley — and even half-glimpsed, half-finished, it made the hair rise on Sable's arms, because the page seemed to hold a place that did not want to be held, lines that shimmered at the edge of sight like something remembered underwater. In the margin, in Coral's small precise hand: *the road remembers being walked. I only follow.*

Sable sat there on the freezing floor with their sister's name in their chest and another person's soul in their hands, and understood, finally and completely, what they were going to do.

They were going to copy it.

Not steal — Coral would notice a missing book the way they'd notice a missing limb. Copy. Charcoal rubbings of the key pages, quick notes on the margins, folded small and sewn into the lining of Sable's coat where the letters to Wren used to live, back when letters were allowed. Then, in the next town large enough to have a crown office, a discreet approach. A name offered sideways. A price named. *I can give you the cartographer's road. I want a girl called Wren Quill out of a prisoner file, alive, with papers.* And if Vane's people laughed, if the trade was refused, then the copies burned and no harm done and Sable would carry the rot of the attempt alone to their grave.

That was the story Sable told themselves, kneeling on the floor with charcoal dust on their fingers. It was a good story. It had a shape. Stories always did; that was how you knew they were lies.

The copying took less than an hour. Sable worked by feel more than sight, hunched over the pages in the dark, and every line they transferred felt like lifting a stone off a cairn that was holding something up. When it was done they folded the papers flat, flat, flat, and sewed them into the coat lining with numb, clumsy stitches, and put the sketchbook back exactly as it had been, spine aligned to the old wear-marks, and knelt there afterward unable to move.

Behind them, in the dark, Coral sighed in their sleep and turned over.

*Sable,* they murmured — or maybe just a sound like it, dream-syllables, nothing meant. But Sable froze anyway, heart slamming, a thief in every sense, waiting to be named.

Coral slept on.

Sable lay back down on the cot and did not sleep at all. They listened to the wind worry the shutters and the frost tick against the window glass, and they did the arithmetic again, because they couldn't stop: one sister, alive, against one cartographer, hunted. One betrayal, weighed against one rescue. And underneath the arithmetic, quieter, the thing that actually frightened them: that some part of them had felt *relief* when the decision was made. Relief, because a choice, even a rotten one, was lighter to carry than the endless waiting-for-the-world-to-end they had been doing since Lowmark.

*Guilt sharpening loyalty into betrayal* — that was how it felt, lying there. Like a blade being ground. Every pass of the whetstone was love: Wren's laugh, Wren's forehead against the bars, Wren's *go, don't be stupid, go*. And what came off the blade at the end was sharp enough to cut the one person who had kept Sable alive through mountains that ate travelers, who had shared their fire and their food and their impossible sight without ever asking what Sable was hiding, who trusted them — gods, who *trusted* them, slept six feet away with their back turned, defenseless as trust always is.

Toward morning, Sable heard Coral stir.

"Quill?" Sleep-rough, wary. Coral always woke wary now; the hunt had taught them that. "What time is it?"

"Early. Go back to sleep."

A pause. Through the frost-painted glass, the sky was turning the color of dishwater. "You didn't sleep."

"I slept."

"You're lying."

"I'm a smuggler," Sable said. "Lying's the trade."

Another pause, longer. Then Coral said, softly, in the voice they used for the visions, the voice that saw too much: "Whatever it is you're carrying, Quill — it sounds heavy. You shift your weight to the left side of the bed when you're carrying something. You've done it every night for a week."

Sable stared at the ceiling. The sewn lining of their coat hung on the hook by the door, six feet away, holding fifty lives' worth of road against one girl's name.

"It's nothing," Sable said. "Go back to sleep. We ride at dawn."

And Coral — trusting, terrible, generous Coral — believed them, or pretended to, and the silence that settled over the room after that was the heaviest thing Sable had ever carried, heavier than the papers, heavier than the name, and it rode out of Hollowmere with them at first light, westward, toward the tower only one of them could see.

Neither of them knew yet that the road ahead bent toward a snowbound cave, a confession, and a fountain full of names. Sable only knew that the papers against their ribs felt less like a plan now than a fuse — lit at both ends, burning inward, patient as winter.

Somewhere behind them, in a prison column marching on the capital road, a girl called Wren lifted her chained wrists against the cold and did not know she had already been traded for.

Or hadn't been.

Or was, by dawn, depending on which end of the fuse burned faster.
