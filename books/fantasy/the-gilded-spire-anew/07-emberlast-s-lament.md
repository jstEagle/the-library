## 7. Emberlast's Lament

I have counted the years the way mortals count coins — reluctantly, and always finding fewer than I hoped.

There was a time when I did not count at all. Time was a river then, and I was a stone in it, patient and unhurried, letting centuries wash over my scales like warm rain. Dragons are not made for counting. We are made for keeping. For holding things whole: oaths, fires, countries.

Now all I keep is a guttering flame in a cave above the snowline, and two small warm creatures who do not yet know what they have found.

The cartographer sleeps with her back against my foreleg, her maps spread beneath her hands even in dreams, as if she fears they will wander off without her. The smuggler sits by the fire she has built from wet pine, feeding it stick by stick, watching me with eyes that calculate everything. She thinks I do not notice. Dragons notice everything; we simply choose what to be troubled by.

But tonight the wind comes down off the high passes carrying a smell I had thought burned out of the world — juniper smoke, old stone, the particular sweetness of Thornfell's summer grass — and I find that I cannot keep silent any longer. A secret held too long becomes a stone in the chest. Even dragons drown on stones.

"Little mapmaker," I say, and my voice is thunder wrapped in wool, the way I learned to speak so as not to shatter the small bones of human ears. "Little thief of roads. Wake. It is time you knew what you are walking toward."

* * *

The cartographer opens her eyes the way her kind always does — all at once, startled by the world, as though existence were an ambush. The smuggler does not startle. She only goes still, the way prey goes still, which tells me more about her childhood than any confession could.

"You spoke," Coral says. Her voice is rough with sleep and wonder. "You've never — you haven't spoken to us before."

"I have spoken to your kind for longer than your kind has had a word for *speak*. You were sleeping, and I was grieving, and grief keeps its own counsel." I lower my head until one eye — gold shot through now with threads of grey, like tarnish creeping across a coin — fills their vision. "Tonight the wind brought me something I have not smelled in three hundred years. Tonight I will tell you why a dragon hides in a hole in the mountains, dying slowly, in a country that does not exist."

The smuggler's fire crackles. Somewhere below us, a night bird calls across the snow, and receives no answer.

"Thornfell," I say, and the name tastes of ash and honey. "Say it."

Coral says it. Sable does not.

"Do you feel that?" I ask. "When you speak it? That small wrongness, like a stair missing from a staircase?"

Coral's hand drifts to her chest. "Yes," she whispers. "Like hearing a chord with a note removed."

"That note is a country." I settle my bulk deeper into the cave floor, and the stone groans beneath me, grateful perhaps for the company of weight. "Listen, then. Both of you. And understand that what I tell you is not legend, because legends are what people remember, and this is the story of what no one was permitted to remember."

* * *

Before there were the Five Kingdoms whose names every child chants, there were six.

Thornfell lay east of the Saltmere, a country of black pines and silver rivers, of terraced orchards carved into mountainsides by farmers who sang to the stone until the stone grew sweet. Its capital — you have seen its ghost, little mapmaker, though you do not know it yet — was called Vael Amaranth, the City of Written Names, because in Thornfell a name was not merely given but *made*: inscribed in the Fountain of Names at birth, in water that glittered like hammered gold, so that every citizen carried a piece of the city inside them all their lives.

It was not a perfect country. Do not let me make it holy. Its kings were sometimes cruel, its merchants sometimes greedy, its winters long enough to breed despair in the strongest hearts. But it was *real*, root and branch, and its people loved it the way you love a body — not despite its flaws but including them, wholly.

And it had something the other kingdoms wanted. Not gold. Not grain. Thornfell sat upon the world's last living vein of wild magic — the raw stuff, uncut, singing under the mountains like a struck bell that never finished ringing. Every wonder in the Five Kingdoms, every ward and blessing and healing rite, drew from wells that had gone shallow centuries before. Only Thornfell's spring ran deep.

"So they came for it," Sable says. Not a question. Her voice is flat, and I hear in it the voice of someone who has watched strong things take from weak things her entire life.

"They came for it politely," I answer. "That is the horror of it, little thief. There was no war. There was a *treaty*. Sixty days of negotiation in a marble hall, with wine and honeyed figs and translators bowing over documents. The five crowns said: your magic is wasted on you, a nation of farmers and stonemasons. Give it to us, pooled and shared, and we will end drought and plague in a generation. Refuse, and we will take it anyway, and call the taking liberation."

"And Thornfell refused," Coral says softly.

"Thornfell refused. And so the five crowns did a thing that had never been done, because it required all five of them together, each contributing a piece of their hoarded magic to fuel it. They did not invade. They did not burn. They *unwrote*."

The fire pops. Neither of them moves.

"There is a magic older than borders," I continue, "the magic of erasure. It does not kill. Killing leaves graves, and graves leave stories, and stories leave vengeance. Erasure leaves nothing to avenge. In a single night — the Night of Smooth Paper, my mother called it, for she remembered it, and I remember her remembering — every road into Thornfell became a road to somewhere else. Every letter written to a Thornfell address arrived blank. Every portrait of a Thornfell face became a portrait of empty air. The treaties were amended; the histories were corrected; the maps—" I look at Coral, and her face in the firelight has gone the color of bone. "The maps were redrawn, and the ink used to redraw them was mixed with forgetting, so that even the cartographers who had drawn Thornfell's coasts wept without knowing why, and burned their life's work as if clearing rubbish."

"The people," Coral says. Her voice cracks down the middle. "What happened to the people?"

"Some died. When a country is unwritten, the land itself forgets how to hold its children. Rivers changed course through villages that were suddenly not there. But most — most were *scattered*. The treaty-magic could not destroy souls; even five crowns together cannot do that. So it scattered them, the way wind scatters seed, into the five kingdoms, into families and towns where they woke with names that felt borrowed and a homesickness they could not name. Their children inherited the ache without knowing its source. Your smuggler's grandmother sang lullabies in a language she called 'nonsense,' did she not, Sable Quill? Nonsense that was Old Thornic. I have heard it in your accent when you curse."

Sable's hand closes around the knife at her belt — not to draw it, I think, but because some part of her needs to hold something real. "That's not possible," she says, but her voice shakes, and we both know it is more than possible. It is *her*.

"It is not only possible. It is the design. Scattered people forget fastest. And the ones who could not forget — the gifted, those born with drops of the wild vein still singing in their blood, like you, little mapmaker, seeing places that were erased precisely because you carry Thornfell's stolen inheritance — they were hunted. Not for treason. For *memory*. A gifted person is a loose thread in the five crowns' great seam of lies, and threads get pulled."

* * *

"But the door," Coral says. She has drawn her knees up to her chest, and she stares into the fire as though the flames might resolve into answers. "You said the Spire holds a door shut. Held by whom? If everyone forgot—"

"Not everyone forgot." I close my eye, and behind it the past unfolds like a wing. "The dragons remembered. We are old, older than the five crowns, older than writing itself, and erasure-magic slides off a mind that has watched continents drift. My mother, Auriflamme, was Warden of the Vein. On the Night of Smooth Paper she flew into the heart of the working — into the light that was eating a country — and she did the only thing left to do. She could not stop the erasure. But she could *catch the edge of it*. She seized the last unmapped mile of Thornfell — the mountain fastness where the Gilded Spire stood, the tower the five crowns' cartographers had somehow never charted, the one place too deep in wild magic to be written down — and she folded it out of reach. Out of sight. Into the space between places, where unwritten things survive."

"She saved the Spire," Coral breathes.

"She saved the *door*. Understand the difference. The Spire was raised in the first age by Thornfell's founders as a failsafe — a keyhole cut into the country's own heart. While it stands, Thornfell is not dead, only dormant, folded like a letter waiting to be opened. Everything the country was — its streets, its fountains, its written names, its people's stolen inheritance — is held inside it, the way a flame is held inside an ember. Open the door, and the letter unfolds. The country returns, and takes back what was taken."

"Then open it!" Sable is on her feet, firelight leaping in her dark eyes. "If you've been sitting here for three hundred years guarding a *door*—"

"Open it yourself, then," I say gently, and watch the anger drain out of her as she understands. "The door does not open with hands. It opens with *sight*. It must be perceived to be reached, and perceived truly — not glimpsed, not dreamed, but known, mapped, *believed* in, stroke by stroke, by a mind that carries Thornfell's inheritance. The five crowns made sure such minds would be rare. Rarer every year. Hunted, imprisoned, extinguished." I let my gaze rest on Coral, heavy as a paw. "In three hundred years, little mapmaker, I have waited at this door for a cartographer who could see it. I have watched dozens of the gifted die in the crown's dungeons, each one a key melted down before it could turn. And then, three weeks ago, I smelled ink and mountain rain on the wind, and I saw a woman standing in a pass that does not exist, drawing a road that should not exist, with tears freezing on her cheeks — and I knew my vigil was ending."

Coral has gone very still. "Why didn't you tell us sooner?"

"Because telling changes nothing until you are ready. Because hope is a fragile thing and I have crushed it before by handling it too soon." I exhale, and the fire gutters and flares. "And because, little mapmaker, there is a second truth, and I did not want to see pity in your eyes while you still needed to trust me."

* * *

I rise, slowly, joints grinding like millstones, and step toward the cave mouth. Beyond it the night is vast and star-scoured, the peaks below us white as unwritten pages. I hold up one foreclaw and let them see.

The flame-light in my throat rises, and I breathe it out — a small tongue of fire, no larger than a candle's, trembling in the cold air. Once it would have been a river. Once I could have seared my name into a cliff-face a mile away. Once my mother's flame lit the sky of an entire country like a second dawn.

"Now," I say, "look."

Within the tiny flame, images move: streets of pale stone, fountains running with golden water, terraced orchards, a tower of impossible grace rising out of a fold between mountains, its gilding catching a sun that shines nowhere in the five kingdoms. The Gilded Spire. The heart of Thornfell, beating in exile.

"The door and my fire are one thing," I tell them. "My mother bound them with the last of her strength, so that no hand of the five crowns could ever force it — only a willing flame, freely spent, could hold the lock or open it. For three hundred years my fire has kept the Spire folded safe. And for three hundred years it has been going out." The candle-flame wavers, and in it the golden city flickers like a dying lamp. "Dragons do not die as mortals die. We gutter. Slowly, century by century, unless we spend ourselves — in hoarding, in warring, in *keeping*. I have spent mine on keeping. And now there is so little left that I can feel the Spire growing faint, the way you feel a held breath growing short. When my fire dies, the door dies with it. Thornfell will not be conquered. It will simply cease — the last ember of it scattering into the dark, and no one in all the world will ever again wake weeping for a home they cannot name, because even the ache will be gone."

Silence. The night bird calls again, and this time, far below, another bird answers, and I think: *even birds remember to answer.*

"So you see," I say, turning back to them, and my voice comes out smaller than I intend, worn smooth by three hundred years of talking to stone. "I am not your guide, little mapmaker. I am a fuse, burning down. What remains of a country hangs on what remains of me, and what remains of me is measured in weeks. Perhaps days, now that the wind has found me and carries my scent to whoever still remembers to hunt."

Coral stands. She crosses the cave — small, deliberate, her boots crunching on frost-rimed stone — and lays one human hand flat against my foreleg, over the place where my fire sleeps. Her palm is warm. Her gift hums against my scales like a struck tuning fork, and for one dizzy moment I feel what she feels: the shape of a hidden valley, the curve of an invisible road, the faint golden pulse of a door between worlds.

"I'll draw it," she says quietly. "Whatever it costs. I'll draw it."

Behind her, Sable Quill looks at the dying flame, and at the city inside it, and at the cartographer's hand on a dragon's scarred hide — and something moves behind her eyes that she does not say aloud, some ledger opening, some price being weighed. I am old enough to recognize a divided heart when I see one. But that is a storm for another night.

Because far to the south, down where the passes descend toward the world of written things, torches have begun to move. Many torches. Moving with purpose, along roads that lead, by paths only a gifted cartographer could perceive, toward us.

They have found the thread. Now they come to pull it.

"Rest quickly, little ones," I murmur, folding my wing around them both as the first grey light creeps over the peaks. "At dawn, we walk the road that isn't there — and I fear we will not walk it alone."
