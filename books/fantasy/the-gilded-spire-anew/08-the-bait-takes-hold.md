## 8. The Bait Takes Hold

The prisoner came out of the snow like something the mountain had coughed up.

Coral saw him first — or rather, Coral's gift did, a wrongness in the white, a shape that belonged to no map of this pass. They had stopped at midday in the lee of a granite shoulder, Emberlast curled against the cliff with her wings folded tight to hoard what warmth her guttering fire still made, and Coral had been sketching the road ahead on the back of an old supply list when the blank space at the edge of their vision resolved into a man.

He was limping. One boot was gone; his foot was wrapped in strips of blanket gone gray with blood and ice. His coat bore the torn remnant of a crown badge, ripped away but not cleanly — someone had tried to tear it off and only half succeeded, leaving a scrap of royal blue flapping at his chest like a wound.

"Please," he called, before he was even close enough for the words to carry well. His voice cracked on it. "Please — I saw your fire. I saw the dragon. Please."

Sable was on their feet with a knife out before Coral could speak. That was Sable's way: blade first, questions after, trust never. "Stay where you are," Sable said, low and flat.

"I'm not — I'm not one of them." The man dropped to his knees in the snow, hands raised, palms out, fingers blackened with frostbite at the tips. "My name is Aldous Vey. I was in Lowmark. In the cells under the customs house. He let me go. The inquisitor — Halvard Vane — he opened my cell and said *go*, and gave me bread, and said *go north, you'll find others like you.*"

Coral felt the words land somewhere beneath their ribs, cold as the snow they stood in.

*He let me go.*

No one let anyone go from Vane's cells. Coral had heard what happened in those cells — everyone in Lowmark had heard, in the way people hear things they cannot stop themselves from hearing, through walls and through the silences of neighbors who had stopped meeting each other's eyes. People went down into the customs house and did not come back up, or came back up changed, quieter, willing to say anything, sign anything, point at anyone.

"He said there were others," Aldous Vey babbled, closer now, Sable letting him come because he was clearly too frozen and too broken to be a threat, "gifted folk heading into the Thornfells, and if I found them — if I joined them — I'd be safe. He said the crown doesn't want to hurt us. He said it wants to *understand* us."

Emberlast lifted her head. Her eyes were the color of coals banked for the night, dull orange shot through with threads of gray ash, and when she looked at the kneeling man, Coral felt the dragon's attention like a change in pressure, a storm-front rolling through.

"He is not lying," she rumbled. Her voice made the snow shiver off the rocks above them. "That is what makes it cruel."

"I don't understand," Aldous whispered.

"No," Emberlast said. "You don't. That is also the design."

Coral crouched, keeping just outside the reach of the man's frostbitten hands. Up close he smelled of the cells — of damp stone and old smoke and something else, something chemical and faintly sweet that Coral couldn't name and didn't want to. "What exactly did he tell you?" Coral asked. "Word for word. It matters."

Aldous's eyes were wet and unfocused. "He said — go north through the Wethering Pass. He said gifted folk would be drawn to the high country because of — because of rumors. A spire. A door." He swallowed. "I didn't believe him. I swear I didn't. But I had nowhere else. My family's in Lowmark. He said if I came back having found nothing, nothing would happen to me. He *promised*—"

"He promised," Sable repeated softly. There was no kindness in it. Sable turned away, scanning the pass behind the man, the empty white distance, the wind-carved ridgelines. Coral watched them look and understood what they were looking for and felt their stomach drop through the ice of the world.

Because the pass behind Aldous Vey was empty.

It should not have been empty. If Vane had truly released this man three days ago — and the state of his feet said three days, maybe four, of walking — then either Vane had simply turned a broken man loose into the mountains to die, which was possible, or—

Or the riders were good enough to leave the road empty. To hold back beyond the sightline of the man they were using. To let him walk ahead of them like a lantern carried into a dark room.

"We have to move," Coral said quietly. "Now."

"He needs—" Sable began.

"He's the bait, Sable. Whatever he knows, whatever he meant to do, he's already done it. The moment he saw our fire, we were found." Coral was already kicking snow over the coals of their little cookfire, already rolling the maps — the precious, irreplaceable maps, the route sketches that lived inside their coat against their skin. "We move now, or we die here."

Aldous Vey looked up at them with an expression Coral would carry for a long time afterward: not guilt, not quite, because guilt required understanding, but the raw animal confusion of a man who had finally realized that the hand that fed him had been holding a leash the whole time. "I didn't mean—"

"I know," Coral said, and found, to their own surprise, that they meant it. "I know you didn't. That's what makes it work. Get up. Can you walk?"

"I can walk."

"Then walk behind the dragon and don't speak."

* * *

They went up.

It was the only direction left. Behind them lay the Wethering Pass and whatever moved along it, patient and silent; ahead the ridge climbed into country so high the maps Coral had ever seen simply gave up, trailing off into hatched lines and the cartographer's honest confession: *here be unprofitable heights.* Only Coral's gift showed what was actually there — a shelf of stone, a hidden couloir, a thread of a path that existed because Coral perceived it and would cease to exist, for everyone else, the moment Coral's attention slipped.

Emberlast led anyway. She knew these mountains in her bones, bones older than any treaty, older than the erasure itself, and she moved with a slow, terrible grace up slopes that should have been impossible for something her size, finding purchase where the rock remembered her kind and was glad of it.

The storm found them two hours later.

It came off the western peaks without warning, without the courtesy of a horizon darkening first — one moment the afternoon light lay pale and clean on the snow, and the next the world was a howling white wall, temperature falling so fast that Coral's breath crackled audibly in the air. Visibility collapsed to arm's length. Then to less.

"Sable!" Coral shouted into the white. No answer. "SABLE!"

The wind took the name and shredded it. Coral staggered on, one hand on Emberlast's flank — the scales were cold now, colder than stone, the dragon's fire burning so low she barely warmed the air around her — and the other hand pressed flat against their own chest, feeling the rolled maps through the wool of their coat. *Still there. Still there.*

Something loomed out of the storm. Coral's knife was out before thought caught up, and then thought caught up: it was Sable, hood thrown back, face scoured red by wind, shouting words that arrived whole only because they were close enough to touch.

"—can't stay together! We'll lose the dragon in this, or we'll all walk off a shelf! We need shelter!"

"Where?" Coral screamed back. "There's nothing—"

"There's a hollow. Half a mile, maybe less. I smelled woodsmoke — old smoke, old fire, someone wintered there once. Emberlast can find it. She has the nose for it."

"And us?"

Sable's jaw worked. Snow had gotten into the creases of their face and frozen there, tiny white seams. "We tie together. Rope. And we pray the knot's better than our luck."

They tied together — Coral to Sable, ten feet of smuggler's line between them, the kind of knot Sable tied without looking, without thinking, the kind of knot that said *I've done this before, I've done this in worse.* Aldous Vey stumbled between them, half-carried, his ruined foot leaving a dark smear on the snow that the storm erased almost as quickly as it appeared. Even the mountain, it seemed, did not want evidence of him.

They walked. Coral lost all sense of time. The storm was a place as much as a weather, a white country with no borders, and Coral's gift — their beautiful, cursed gift, the thing that had always shown them what maps missed — showed them nothing at all. There was nothing to see. There was only the next step, and the next, and Sable's rope going taut and slack, taut and slack, a pulse between them.

Then the ground fell away.

Not gradually. One moment the slope held; the next it simply wasn't there, and Coral was sliding, tumbling, the rope wrenching their shoulder nearly out of its socket as Sable's weight caught them — or perhaps Coral caught Sable; there was no way to know, in the white chaos, who had saved whom. They fetched up against a wall of drifted snow hard enough to drive the air from Coral's lungs, and lay there gasping, and above them, faint through the roar, they heard Emberlast's cry — a sound like a hinge on the door of a very large, very old house.

And then they heard the other sound.

Beneath the wind. Beneath everything. A thin, carrying note that Coral's body recognized before their mind did: a hunting horn. Not close. Not far. Somewhere in the storm, moving along the contour of the ridge they had abandoned, patient as arithmetic.

"They're sweeping the pass," Sable shouted, pulling Coral upright. Their face was inches away, and even in the white murk Coral could see the calculation running behind their eyes, fast and cold and frightened. "If we keep together, we leave one trail. Big trail. Dragon-sized."

"If we split—"

"If we split, we might live. Some of us." Sable's hand closed on Coral's wrist, hard. "Listen to me. The hollow — I can find it again, I marked it in my head, it's what I *do*. Take the maps and go with Emberlast. She can shield you, hide your heat, buy you a night. I'll draw them. I'm good at being followed in the wrong direction. It's practically a profession."

"No." The word came out of Coral before any thought approved it. "No. We don't split. Every time someone splits in a story like ours, they die apart."

"This isn't a story, Coral. This is a ledger, and right now we're overdrawn." But Sable hadn't let go of their wrist. If anything, the grip had tightened, and there was something in Sable's face that Coral had never seen there before — something naked, quickly buried, but seen. Fear, yes. But fear *for someone else*, which was a currency Sable had always claimed not to carry.

"The hollow," Coral said. "All of us. Now. Argue later."

For one suspended moment, with the horn sounding its patient note somewhere in the white, Sable Quill — liar, betrayer, keeper of secrets Coral did not yet know the shape of — looked like a person about to confess something enormous.

Then the slope above them shifted.

* * *

The avalanche was small, as avalanches go. Later, Coral would learn that small ones kill more often than great ones, because great ones announce themselves and small ones simply arrive.

It came off the shelf above with a sound like the mountain clearing its throat, and then there was no time for anything — no time for words or plans or the argument they had been having about whether to trust each other with their lives. There was only Emberlast's wing, sweeping down out of the storm like a wall of leather and desperation, folding around the three of them as the snow hit.

The world became pressure and white noise and the crushing dark. Coral was tumbled like a stone in a river, slammed, rolled, buried — and then, impossibly, uncovered, dragged, hauled, and there was air, blessed freezing air, and Emberlast's snout shoving snow aside with the tenderness of a creature digging a grave she refused to dig.

Coral coughed. Breathed. Coughed again.

Sable was beside them, snow-crusted, alive, already counting heads with their eyes. "Vey," Sable rasped. "Where's Vey?"

They found him ten yards downslope, buried to the chest, and dug him out, and he was alive too — alive and weeping, great silent sobs that shook his frostbitten frame, and whether he wept for himself or for the promise he'd believed or for the men on the horn he now understood he served, none of them asked, and he did not say.

But the avalanche had given them one gift amid its violence: it had erased the trail. Behind them the slope was a smooth, churned scar of new snow, featureless, trackless. Whatever the riders had been following, they would follow it no further tonight.

And the hollow — Sable found it within the hour, by nose and memory and stubbornness, a shallow cave behind a fallen slab of granite where some long-dead prospector had wintered and died, if the heap of rusted tools in the corner told true. It fit Emberlast badly, the dragon folding herself into the entrance like a hand closing around a candle, her body a living barricade between the storm and the two humans and the broken bait huddled at the back.

They had lost the supplies. All of them. The sled, the food, the oil, the spare rope — somewhere back on the slope, under six feet of new snow, or scattered across a mile of white. What remained was what they carried: a little dried fish in Coral's pack, a flask of spirits in Sable's, the maps against Coral's skin, and the knife at Sable's belt.

Coral sat with their back against the cold stone and divided the fish into four portions with hands that would not stop shaking, and thought about the horn, and thought about the empty pass, and thought about the way Sable had looked at them in the storm — that flash of fear-for-someone-else, quickly buried — and felt the first cold thread of a question they did not want to think slide into their mind:

*How did Vane know which pass? How did he know to sweep the Wethering, of all the passes? Who told him we'd come this way?*

They looked across the dying embers of the tiny fire Emberlast permitted herself — a flame no larger than a cupped palm, hoarded like the last coin of a bankrupt kingdom — and found Sable watching them. Watching them with an expression carefully arranged into blankness, the way a shopkeeper arranges goods in a window.

"You should sleep," Sable said.

"After you," Coral said.

Something flickered across Sable's face — there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. "I'll take first watch."

"You always take first watch."

"Someone has to."

"Yes," Coral agreed softly, and ate their portion of fish, and did not sleep, and watched the storm seal the mouth of the world, and listened to the wind, and underneath the wind, very far away and very patient, the memory of a horn.

Beside them, wrapped in his own misery, Aldous Vey murmured in his fevered sleep. Most of it was nonsense. But near midnight, as Coral sat rigid with cold and suspicion, the man's muttering sharpened into words, clear as a bell across water:

"*...he said the cartographer would keep the sketches close... said to watch the coat... said the sketches were worth more than all of us...*"

Coral did not move. Did not breathe. Across the embers, Sable's eyes were open in the dark, fixed on Coral's coat, where the maps slept against Coral's heart.

And Sable smiled — a small, tired, terrible smile — and said, very quietly, so that only Coral could hear:

"Well. Now we both know what you're carrying."

Outside, the storm went on howling, and somewhere beneath its voice, patient as arithmetic, the hunt waited for morning.
