## 9. Frozen Ledger

The cave found them the way mercy finds the drowning — late, grudgingly, and only because they had stopped being able to choose anything at all.

Sable saw it first: a black seam in the white face of the mountain, half-hidden behind a curtain of icefall that had frozen mid-tumble like a wave caught in amber. They had been walking, if you could call it that, since before dawn. Coral had stopped answering questions an hour ago. That was the ledger Sable kept now, silently, in the place where a smuggler keeps accounts of debts and favors: *Coral spoke last at dawn. Coral stumbled twice on the scree. Coral has not eaten in two days, because there was nothing to eat, because Sable lost the supplies.*

Because Sable had led them into the high passes blind, with Vane's riders behind and the released prisoner's false directions ahead, and then stood in the snow watching the mule bolt with everything they owned strapped to its back except the one thing that mattered, which was each other.

"In here," Sable said, and heard how their own voice had gone strange, thin as ice over water.

The cave went back farther than it showed from outside. The wind followed them in for a few body-lengths and then gave up, sulking against the walls. It was not warm inside. Nothing was warm anymore. But it was *still*, and stillness was a currency worth more than gold up here, where every gust wanted to take something from you — your heat, your breath, your grip on which way was down.

Sable got Coral onto the driest stretch of stone, near the back where the mountain's own slow warmth might seep through, and began unpacking what remained. One blanket, wool, moth-eaten, smelling of mule sweat and salt air from Lowmark, from another life. Half a wheel of hard cheese gone waxy with cold. A stub of tallow candle. Flint and steel. Three strips of dried fish that Sable counted twice, then a third time, as though arithmetic might multiply them.

Three strips. Two people. However many days.

They got a fire going eventually — small, hoarded, the flame no bigger than a cupped hand. Sable fed it pine knots one at a time, rationing light along with everything else. In that stingy glow Coral's face was gray as the stone beneath them. Frost had gotten into the edges of their hair, whitening temples that were far too young for white. Their hands, when Sable took them to chafe blood back into the fingers, felt like things carried up from deep water.

"Talk to me," Sable said. "Anything. Tell me about maps."

Coral's eyes opened a slit. "Maps," they repeated, tasting the word like it belonged to someone else's language.

"You love them. I've seen you love them." Sable worked on the stiff fingers, pressing warmth into them, hating how much of the gesture was apology dressed as care. "Tell me the first one you ever drew."

A long silence. The fire popped. Somewhere above, the mountain groaned under its load of snow, a sound like a giant turning over in sleep.

"The harbor at Lowmark," Coral said at last, voice cracked and small. "I was six. I drew it in charcoal on the back of my mother's laundry list. Every boat wrong. Every pier crooked. But the tide pools—" Something flickered behind their eyes, a coal breathing. "I drew the tide pools where nobody knew they were. Under the north quay. And my mother looked at it and went very quiet and asked me how I knew, and I didn't know how I knew. I just did. She never let me draw again where anyone could see."

"That's why you burn them."

"That's why I burn them." Coral closed their eyes. "Everything I see belongs to other people, Sable. That's the curse of it. I look at a mountain and I'm looking at every exile who ever crossed it, every smuggler's grave, every— " They stopped. Breathed. "Every door."

Sable's hands went still around Coral's fingers.

*Door.* Emberlast's word. The dragon had spoken it in the high pass three nights ago, in her smoke-and-ash voice: *the Spire is a door, little mapmaker, and doors are only holy while they're shut.* Sable had filed the word away the way they filed everything — in the cold ledger, next to debts owed and debts owed *by* them. Because Sable had known for weeks now that the Spire was worth more than gold. Worth more than freedom, even their own. Worth exactly one sister, if the right buyer could be found.

And Coral lay here starving and freezing, trusting them, offering up the story of their gift like bread broken in half, and Sable sat on that knowledge like a man sitting on a corpse.

"Your turn," Coral murmured. "A secret for a secret. Fair trade. Smuggler's rules."

"Smuggler's rules are don't have secrets."

"Liar." But Coral smiled, faintly, eyes still closed, and the smile nearly undid Sable entirely. "You've been carrying something since Lowmark. You check the road behind us more than the road ahead. You count our food like a miser counting coins he owes someone. You—" Coral opened their eyes fully now, and even half-frozen their gaze had that quality Sable both loved and dreaded, the cartographer's gift turned inward, seeing unmapped country. "You flinch when I say Wren's name."

The wind screamed somewhere far above the cave mouth. Sable fed the fire another knot and watched it catch, and thought about lying, and found — for the first time in a life built on lies — that they didn't have the stomach for a good one. So instead they gave a bad one, a cheap one, a coin clipped thin enough to see daylight through:

"I had a sister too. Once."

It wasn't even a lie, exactly. That was the filthiest part. Wren had been more than a sister; she'd been the person who taught Sable to read the tide tables, who held their hair when they drank too much rotgut at fourteen, who'd taken the fall for a cargo of unlicensed tonics so Sable could walk free. *Family looks out*, she'd said, laughing, as the constables took her away. *Go be careful, Quill.* Four years in the crown prison at Dunmarrow now, and every letter that came out of that place came with the same message in her cramped left-slanted hand: *they know what the gifted can do. find the Spire. find the Spire or we all stay forgotten.*

And so Sable had found Coral instead. And traded pieces of Coral's road to Vane's agents, sketch by sketch, checkpoint by checkpoint, telling themselves each time: *this is the last piece. This buys the papers. This frees her and then I confess everything and throw myself on Coral's mercy.*

There had been eleven last pieces.

"I don't want to talk about it," Sable said, which was true, and which was also the closest thing to confession they could afford tonight.

Coral studied them for a long moment. Then, mercifully, unbearably, they reached out and took Sable's hand instead of pressing further. "Fair trade," they whispered. "Some other night."

* * *

Sleep came in shallow tides. Sable dreamed of Lowmark harbor, of the crooked piers and the hidden tide pools, and woke with frost on their eyelashes and a terror in their chest that took a full minute to name: the fire had burned down to a red eye no bigger than a thumbnail.

They fed it the last of the pine knots. Then the candle stub, burning sideways, guttering, filling the cave with a smell like a snuffed chapel. Then, after an hour of staring at it and hating themselves, the corner of the blanket nearest the flame.

Outside, the storm had settled in for the duration. Snow fell with the patience of something being paid by the hour. Through the cave mouth the world was a churn of white and gray, featureless, directionless. Sable knew — because Coral had told them, days ago, drawing lines in the dirt with a stick — that the pass to the unwritten city ran east-southeast from here, below the twin peaks called the Sisters' Teeth. But knowing a thing and finding it under four feet of new snow were different countries.

They ate one strip of fish each. Coral chewed theirs slowly, mechanically, and Sable noticed they were saving the cheese without saying so — breaking off pieces and sliding them toward Sable's side of the blanket when they thought Sable wasn't watching.

"Don't," Sable said.

"Don't what?"

"Ration yourself for me."

Coral looked at them across the dying fire. There was something in their face Sable couldn't read, or could read too well — the expression of someone doing sums. "You've been feeding me your share since the pass," Coral said quietly. "You think I haven't been keeping the ledger?"

Sable said nothing.

"I'm the one with the gift," Coral went on. "I'm the one who can find the Spire. If it comes down to one of us walking out of these mountains—"

"It won't."

"—then it should be me. That's not sacrifice, Sable, it's arithmetic."

"Well, I never was any good at arithmetic." Sable heard their own voice crack on the joke and hated it, hated the glib smuggler's mask slipping over their face at the exact moment they most needed to take it off. "Eat the cheese, Coral. Both of us walk out. That's the whole plan. That's the only version of the plan I'll allow."

Coral almost laughed. It came out as a cough, and then a shiver, and Sable pulled them close under the ruined blanket, back to chest, sharing heat the way they'd once shared watch shifts on the *Salt-Wife*, in the old days, in the easy days before either of them knew what the other was carrying.

Coral's body was trembling in long slow waves, the involuntary shudder of a furnace running out of fuel. Against Sable's shoulder, drowsy, half-gone, they murmured: "When we get out. When this is done. There's something I have to tell you. About the Spire. About what it costs."

"You can tell me now."

"No." A pause. "No, I need... I need to understand it first myself. I saw something, near Emberlast. The Spire doesn't open for people like Vane. It opens for..." Another pause, longer. Sable felt the words forming and dissolving against their neck like breath on glass. "It opens for payment. Everything does. That's the oldest law there is."

Sable stared into the dark and felt those words settle into the ledger beside all the others. *Payment.* They'd assumed the Spire's price would be paid in secrets, in sketches, in information sold to the crown. But if Coral meant something else — something larger — then every sketch Sable had traded wasn't just betrayal. It might be sabotage. It might be handing Vane a key to a lock that would break the world.

Or it might be nothing but a freezing woman's fever-talk, and Sable a traitor for the plainest, stupidest reasons after all: gold, and guilt, and a sister in chains.

"Wren," Sable said softly, testing the name in the dark, watching for Coral's breathing to change. It didn't. They were asleep, or near enough. "Wren, I'm trying. I swear on the Salt-Wife's keel I'm trying. But I don't know anymore whether I'm trying to save her or save myself from what I've done."

The fire died. Sable didn't relight it. There was nothing left to feed it, and besides — some part of them had begun to suspect that they deserved the cold.

* * *

The vision came in the deepest hour, the hour Sable had privately named the hollow hour, when the body's heat is lowest and the mind stops bothering to tell itself comforting stories.

It began as warmth. That was the treacherous part — warmth in a frozen cave, spreading from the point where Coral slept against them, golden and honeyed, like sunlight through amber wine. Sable surfaced from half-sleep into a world transformed: the cave walls had gone translucent, glowing, shot through with veins of light like the Gilded Spire must look, like gold leaf laid over glass. And Coral, still asleep, still shivering faintly, had their eyes moving rapidly beneath their lids, and their lips parted, and light poured out of them.

Not metaphor. Light. Actual light, thin as thread, spilling from between Coral's lashes, pooling on the stone floor in shapes that resolved — Sable's breath caught — into lines. Contours. Roads. A map drawing itself in luminous ink across the cave floor, and at its center a tower, impossibly slender, impossibly tall, rendered in a single unbroken stroke.

Then the tower blurred. The map rippled like water disturbed by a dropped stone. And the vision *turned*, the way a dream turns when something in it decides to look at you —

— and Sable was standing in a courtyard of gray flagstones under a sky the color of dishwater. Chains. The sound of chains, dozens of sets, dragging in ragged rhythm. A column of people shuffling between walls too high to see over, and every one of them wore the gray of crown prisoners, and every one of them moved like the cold had already won.

She was third from the front. Small. Dark-haired, cropped short by prison shears, growing back in uneven tufts that she would have hated. She walked with her wrists crossed in front of her, the way she always did when she was pretending not to be afraid, and she was talking — Sable couldn't hear the words, but she knew the cadence, knew Wren was making the prisoner beside her laugh because Wren could make anyone laugh, that was her gift, the only magic she'd ever needed, and they'd chained her for it anyway—

Her head came up. Across the impossible distance of a dream, her eyes found Sable's, and they widened, and her mouth shaped a single syllable that Sable's heart supplied without hearing:

*Sable.*

The courtyard shattered. Sable came back to themselves gasping, upright, the blanket fallen away, the cave black and bitter and real. Coral was still asleep beside them, breathing shallow but steady, the last threads of light fading from the corners of their closed eyes like sparks going out in ash.

But on the stone floor, faint as breath on a window, the map remained. Lines. Contours. And the tower — the Spire — drawn in a single unbroken stroke, and beside it, in handwriting Sable recognized from a hundred intercepted letters, cramped and left-slanted:

*Third column. East gate. They march at dawn.*

Sable knelt over it until their knees ached, until the words faded, until the stone was only stone. Then they sat back against the wall and pressed both fists against their mouth and shook, silently, the way you learn to cry in prisons and smugglers' holds and other places where sound is death.

Because the vision had settled the argument they'd been having with themselves for eleven sketches and four hundred miles. Not with rhetoric. With arithmetic, Coral's own cold arithmetic: *if it comes down to one of us, it should be the one who can find the Spire.* Wren was alive. Wren was marching east. East was where they were going anyway — and if the Spire truly was a door, a door out of Thornfell, a door out of *everything* the crown had erased, then maybe it was a door wide enough to bring prisoners through. Maybe Sable didn't have to sell Coral's road at all. Maybe they could simply lead everyone — Wren, the prisoner columns, all of them — to the threshold, and pay whatever price the Spire demanded out of their own hide.

Or maybe that was just the lie a traitor tells at three in the morning, the twelfth last sketch, the one that finally lets them sleep.

Dawn came gray and grudging through the cave mouth. Sable divided the last strip of fish unevenly, giving Coral the larger portion, and woke them gently.

"We move today," Sable said. "Storm's easing. And I know which way east is."

Coral blinked at them, frost-lashed, hollow-cheeked, and something in their gaze sharpened — the cartographer reading unfamiliar terrain. "You look like you've seen a ghost."

"Something like that." Sable stood, joints cracking, and looked out at the blinding white slope, where somewhere below, invisible under the snow, an unmapped road waited — and somewhere beyond it, a city that existed only in Coral's sight, and a tower drawn in one unbroken stroke, and a price nobody had named yet. "Coral. When we reach the Spire. Whatever it asks for. Don't pay it alone."

Coral held their gaze a long moment. "That depends," they said quietly, "on what you'd be willing to add to the bill."

And before Sable could answer — before the guilt could climb fully into their throat — the wind shifted, and brought up from the valley below a sound that froze them both where they stood.

Bells. Many bells, iron-toned, arrhythmic. The kind of bells the crown hung on its prisoner columns so the gifted among them could never move unheard.

Coming up the mountain. From the east.
