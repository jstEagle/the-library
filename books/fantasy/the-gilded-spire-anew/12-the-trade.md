## 12. The Trade

The town of Grayhollow had no name on any map worth trusting.

Sable Quill knew it the way they knew most places that didn't want to be known — by the smell of wet peat and tallow smoke, by the way the road narrowed as if embarrassed to arrive, by the three crooked chimneys leaning together like conspirators. It sat in a fold of the Thornfell foothills where the crown's writ went to die quietly, and that was precisely why Sable had come: not for the town itself, but for what passed through it. Letters. Papers. The small mercies of an underground postal system stitched together by smugglers, deserters, and one very reliable widow who kept a candle burning in her window as a signal that the drop was safe.

The candle was lit. Sable's heart performed a small, treacherous leap anyway, because hope had never once done them any favors, and they saw no reason to start trusting it now.

They left Coral and Emberlast camped two valleys back, in a hollow of black pines where the dragon's heat could be mistaken for a spring vent and Coral's gift could keep watch over roads only Coral could see. *Three days,* Sable had said. *I'll get supplies, I'll send word ahead through the passes, I'll be back before you've finished complaining about my cooking.* Coral had looked at them with those mapmaker's eyes — eyes that saw through paper, through stone, through people, apparently — and said nothing at all, which was somehow worse than an accusation.

Sable walked into Grayhollow wearing their second-worst coat and their best lie: a free trader with nothing to declare and less to lose.

* * *

The widow's name was Maren Ostwick, though everyone called her Candle because of the window and no one could remember if that had come first or the flame. Her house smelled of dried lavender and old ink, and she received Sable in the back room where the real business happened, among shelves of mislabeled preserves that were not preserves.

"You look like death's unpaid debt," she said, pouring tea that could have stripped rust.

"I feel like its collateral."

"News first or papers first?"

"Papers." Sable heard their own voice crack on the word and hated it. "Please."

Maren studied them for a long moment, then reached beneath a loose hearthstone and produced a packet wrapped in oilcloth. She set it down between them without letting go. "Before you open that, you should know what it cost to get. And you should know it isn't everything."

Sable's hands went still on the table. "What do you mean?"

"The release order for Wren Quill came through the capital circuit ten days ago. Signed, sealed, legitimate — Inquisitor Vane's own office, which is unusual, they don't usually bother with paperwork for people they intend to forget." Maren's voice was flat, careful, the voice of a woman who had survived thirty years of dangerous kindness by never saying more than necessary. "But there's a condition attached. There always is, with Vane. The order specifies delivery."

"Delivery," Sable repeated. "To Wren?"

"To whoever presents the matching token." Maren slid the oilcloth aside just enough to show a corner of heavy cream paper, a wax seal the color of dried blood, and beneath it — Sable's breath stopped — a strip of vellum bearing a sketch. A mountain pass, rendered in fine confident lines. *Their* lines. One of Coral's route sketches, copied in a hand almost good enough to pass for the original.

The room tilted. Sable gripped the edge of the table.

"He has them already," Sable said. "Some of them."

"He has enough." Maren finally released the packet. "And he wants the rest. The full set. Every sketch, every note, the whole road through the erased valley. In exchange—" she tapped the release order "—your sister walks out of Kettering Prison with clean papers and a pardon stamped so deep even Vane can't unpick it later. That's the trade. That's the whole trade. No haggling, no alternatives. His agent is waiting at the Drowned Bell inn until moonrise tomorrow. After that, the offer burns, and so does the order."

Sable stared at the packet. At the sketch. At the seal.

Somewhere behind their ribs, in the place where guilt had been quietly building its nest for weeks, something shifted its weight and settled deeper.

* * *

They didn't sleep. They sat in the loft above Maren's stable with the packet unopened in their lap for hours, because opening it felt like signing something, and not opening it felt like cowardice, and both felt true.

Wren. Their mind kept returning to her the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Wren at fourteen, teaching herself lockpicking on the family's pantry door until their father gave up scolding and started hiding better locks. Wren at nineteen, caught with a smuggler's ledger and a smile that charmed the magistrate down from hanging to branding. Wren at twenty-three, taken in the first sweep after the Gifted Treason decree — not because she had the gift, but because she'd hidden three children who did, and refused under questioning to give their names. They'd broken her fingers for it. Sable had learned that from a guard who owed them money, and had spent the months since learning what it meant to owe someone more than money.

Coral would understand. That was the unbearable part. Coral, who burned their finest map rather than let it be used to hunt others; Coral, who fed a dying dragon from their own rations; Coral, who trusted Sable with roads that existed only because they believed in them — Coral would understand the love, the desperation, all of it. What Coral would never forgive was the arithmetic. Because Sable had done the arithmetic already, lying awake in snowbound caves and high cold passes, and the sum always came out the same: one sister against a country that didn't exist. One life against a road that led to a door out of the world.

Except now Sable knew it wasn't a door out. Emberlast had told them what the Spire was — the heart of Thornfell, the last living thread of an erased nation, and Coral meant to restore it, meant to spend their gift redrawing borders that kings had drowned in treaty-magic generations ago. And Vane wanted the road to the Spire. Which meant the crown wanted the road to the Spire. Which meant—

Which meant the sketches weren't just a betrayal of Coral. They might be a betrayal of thousands.

Sable put their head in their hands and laughed, low and awful, at the shape of it. Every road out of this led through fire. The only question was whose.

Moonrise was tomorrow. They had until moonrise.

* * *

The Drowned Bell was exactly the kind of inn Sable usually loved — low-beamed, smoke-dark, honest about its dishonesty. Tonight they hated every inch of it. They arrived early, took a corner table with sightlines to both doors, and ordered nothing, because their stomach had closed for business.

Vane's agent arrived at half past eleven, which told Sable something about the man: punctual enough to be dangerous, late enough to seem casual. He was ordinary in the way weapons are ordinary — a gray coat, a gray face, eyes that catalogued exits the way Sable catalogued fences. He sat down across from Sable without asking and placed a leather folio on the table between them.

"Sable Quill." Not a question.

"And you're nobody," Sable said. "That's how this works."

A thin smile. "I'm nobody holding your sister's freedom." He opened the folio. Inside lay the release order, the pardon, the stamped and sealed machinery of mercy — and beside it, fanned out like a gambler's hand, five sketches. Five of Coral's route drawings, copied in that almost-right hand. Sable counted the gaps in their memory of Coral's satchel and felt each missing page like a bruise.

"The Inquisitor is a reasonable man," the agent said. "He admires craftsmanship. He simply requires the complete set. All sketches, all annotations, and anything else the cartographer has produced regarding the Spire. In exchange—"

"I know the exchange. I've been reciting it to myself like a prayer I'm ashamed of."

"Then let's not prolong anyone's evening."

Sable's hand went into their coat. The pages were there — had been there since Grayhollow, since the sleepless loft, since the moment they'd understood that refusing wasn't actually on the menu, only the timing of surrender. They'd told themselves they'd come to negotiate. To stall. To buy time, find another way, warn Coral, do *something* other than the thing they were about to do.

Their hand came out of the coat holding the sketches.

The agent gathered them with the tenderness of a man handling money. He checked each one against his copies, nodded, and slid the folio across the table. "Release order, pardon, travel papers. Valid immediately. Your sister will be processed out of Kettering within the week — faster, if you're curious, than the standard schedule. The Inquisitor keeps his word; it's more efficient than breaking it." He stood, tucked the sketches inside his coat, and paused. "One courtesy, since you dealt fairly. Stay clear of the eastern valleys for the next few days. The Inquisitor's work there will be... thorough."

"What work?"

The agent's smile didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "The kind that requires maps."

He left coins on the table for the drinks Sable hadn't ordered, and was gone into the dark like water finding a drain.

* * *

Sable made it four streets before the shaking started.

They pressed the folio against their chest — Wren's freedom, actual paper, actual seals, the weight of it real at last after two years of letters and bribes and prayers to gods Sable didn't believe in — and shook in the mouth of an alley between a cooper's shop and a shuttered chapel, laughing or sobbing or some hybrid animal sound that belonged to neither. It was done. It was bought. Wren would walk out of Kettering into autumn light, and she'd curse Sable's name for taking so long, and Sable would let her, gladly, forever.

It was done. And somewhere north of here, Coral was sleeping in a pine hollow beside a dying dragon, trusting a road that Sable had just sold.

*Get moving.* The thought arrived cold and practical, the smuggler's voice, the survivor's voice. *Get back to the hollow. Tell Coral. Take the blame, take the rage, take whatever comes — but get ahead of it. Maybe there's still time. Maybe the road can change. Maybe—*

Then Sable crested the lane above the eastern valley, and stopped.

Smoke.

Not chimney smoke. Chimney smoke rises lazy and domestic, gray-white, smelling of bread and peat. This smoke was black and boiling, climbing in fat ropes against the stars, and beneath it the whole eastern slope of the valley glowed a dull furious orange, like a coal bed the size of a village. Because it was a village. Sable could see the shapes of it — rooftops collapsing in slow bright sighs, a barn's silhouette swallowed whole, the church tower standing a moment longer than everything around it before folding inward like a knee.

Grayhollow's eastern valley. The hidden settlements. Everyone knew they were there — the folk who'd fled the decree, the gifted and the shelterers and the ones who simply couldn't prove they weren't, tucked into hamlets that appeared on no tax roll and no honest map. Sable had run cargo to two of them. Had drunk cider in that churchyard. Had taught a girl with frost-bright eyes how to read a compass there, two winters ago, and she'd laughed when north surprised her.

The wind shifted and brought the smell across the mile of dark: pitch and thatch and wool, and underneath it, unmistakably, flesh.

Sable stood in the lane with Wren's freedom papers crushed against their ribs and watched, because looking away felt like a second betrayal and watching felt like the first one finishing its work. Down in the burning, tiny figures moved between the houses — soldiers, unhurried, methodical, going building to building with torches the way a clerk goes line to line down a ledger. Not a battle. An audit. Somewhere in that orange roar were people who had done nothing except exist in the wrong category, and the men killing them had needed maps to find them, and the maps had needed a road, and the road had needed—

No. No, that arithmetic was a trap and Sable knew it, had built the trap themselves out of guilt and grief. Vane had the sketches *before tonight*. The copies proved it. These fires were lit by routes Sable had never drawn, sins Sable hadn't signed for. But knowing that was like knowing the exact chemistry of poison while it worked in your blood. The agent's parting words rang in their skull — *the kind that requires maps* — and every sketch Sable had handed over was a key, and keys don't choose which doors they open, and Sable had handed over a whole ring of them with a smile they couldn't remember making.

The girl with frost-bright eyes. Sable searched the burning shapes for the churchyard, for the cottage where she'd lived with her grandmother, and found only fire where memory insisted walls should be, and that was the moment the night broke something in them that would not, Sable understood dimly, ever be repaired by forgiveness or freedom papers or anything else purchasable.

They turned north. They ran.

The folio banged against their chest with every stride — Wren, Wren, Wren — and the smoke smeared the stars behind them, and the road through the hills unwound ahead in the dark like a sentence Sable already knew the ending of. Two valleys. Three hours, if the legs held. Time enough to reach the pine hollow, to wake Coral, to say the words that would end everything worth having: *I sold the road. I sold it for her. I'd sell it again.*

And beyond that confession, past it, underneath it, one more truth rising like the smoke at their back: Vane had the complete set now. Every sketch. Every annotation. The whole road to the Gilded Spire, delivered into the hands of a man who burned villages as bookkeeping.

Whatever Coral had seen at the end of that road — the door, the country, the restoration of everything erased — Vane would see it too.

Unless Sable got there first, and found some way, any way, to stand in front of what they'd unleashed.

The hills swallowed them running.
