## 13. Confrontation at the Fountain of Names

The fountain sang before I saw it.

That was the thing about the unwritten city — it announced itself in fragments, the way a melody returns to you years after you've forgotten where you learned it. A sound of water where no water should be. The smell of cut stone and something sweeter beneath it, like honey left too long in a sunlit room. Then the square opened up ahead of us between buildings that existed only because I could see them, and there it was: a fountain wide as a threshing floor, its basin carved from white stone veined with gold, its central figure a woman with her arms flung open, water pouring from her palms in silver sheets.

And her name — and ten thousand others — written across every surface.

I stopped walking. Emberlast's breath stirred my hair, warm despite everything, despite the guttering cold that lived in her now like a sickness.

"Names," I said, because my voice needed to do something or I would have wept instead.

I crossed to the basin's edge and knelt. The stone was cool under my fingers, real as anything in Lowmark, real as the maps I used to draw for coin. And there, cut deep and filled with what looked like liquid light rather than water-wear, ran the letters. *Maren Ostreth. Julek Vane-of-nowhere. Sera Ilbin, daughter of Sera Ilbin.* Names upon names upon names, spiraling down into the basin and up the woman's robes and along the rim, so many that they blurred together if I didn't focus, a tide of the erased pressed into permanence by someone who had known this was coming.

"They wrote them down," I whispered. "Before the treaty magic took them. Someone stood here with chisels and wrote down everyone they could."

Sable said nothing behind me. I heard them shift their weight, heard the creak of their pack, and some small animal part of me — the part that had kept me alive since the criers came to Lowmark — noted that Sable had been quiet for two days now. Quiet in a particular way. Quiet the way a person is quiet when they are carrying something heavy and are afraid to set it down.

I should have listened to that part of myself sooner.

* * *

We had come into the city at dusk, through streets only I could perceive, following the pull of the Spire like a fisher follows a line. It rose above the rooftops at the city's heart — I could see it even now, past the fountain's spray, a needle of gilded stone catching light from no sun I could name. Emberlast moved through the ghost-avenues with strange gentleness, her ruined wings folded tight, her claws finding purchase on flagstones that were not there for anyone but us. She had grown quieter as her fire dimmed. Dragons, she had told us, do not fear ending. They fear being forgotten while still burning.

The ghosts did not trouble me anymore. That was the strangest mercy of this place. They walked their erased lives in the amber light — a baker pulling loaves from an oven that had been ash for three generations, children chasing a hoop down a lane, a woman leaning from a window to call someone home to supper. They could not see us. We were the intruders here, the living wandering through a country that had been folded out of existence like a letter never sent.

But the fountain was different. The fountain felt like it was looking back.

"Coral." Sable's voice, low. "We should find shelter before—"

"Say her name."

The words came out of me before I decided to speak them. I hadn't planned the confrontation. I hadn't let myself plan it, because planning meant admitting what I suspected, and admitting what I suspected meant admitting that the last weeks had been built on a foundation I'd mistaken for bedrock.

Sable went very still. In the fountain's spray-light, their face was all angles and shadow, the scar through their eyebrow pale as a signature.

"Say whose name," they said.

"Wren." I stood. My knees ached from kneeling; I welcomed the ache. It gave the moment weight. "Your sister. Say her name, Sable. Here, in this place, where names are the only currency that matters. Say it and mean it, or turn around and walk back down the road you sold."

* * *

I had found the first proof that morning, though I'd been gathering pieces for days without letting them touch each other.

A scrap of paper in Sable's pack when I'd gone to fetch the salt-pork — not hidden well, because Sable had never needed to hide things from me, which was its own kind of wound. A copy of my route sketches, drawn in a hurried hand that mimicked mine badly, with annotations in the margins. Distances. Checkpoints. The valley we'd walked through that existed in no memory but mine. And at the bottom, in a different ink, a single line: *Payment confirmed against delivery. Wren Quill to be released upon verification.*

I had sat with that paper in my hands for a long time while Sable slept ten feet away, their breathing easy, unburdened, and I had thought: *they gave away the road.* Not just the road. The people on it. Every gifted soul who might follow our trail to sanctuary. Every secret Emberlast had trusted us with. The whole unwritten world, priced against one life.

And God help me, I understood it. That was the worst of it. If it had been my sister in those chains — if I'd had anyone at all — I might have done the same. Understanding did not make it hurt less. Understanding made it hurt in a place I couldn't put pressure on.

So I had waited. I had walked beside them all day through the ghost-streets, watching the dead city live its impossible afternoon, waiting until we reached the fountain — because some instinct told me this was where truth belonged. A place inscribed with the cost of erasure. If Sable had traded in names, let them confess among names.

Now they stood at the fountain's edge, and the silence stretched until the water-sound itself seemed loud.

"You went through my pack," Sable said finally.

"Don't." My voice cracked on it. "Don't make this about how I found out. Make it about what you did."

They closed their eyes. When they opened them again, something had gone out of them — the quicksilver charm, the smuggler's smoothness, all the armor they wore like second skin. What was left underneath looked young and terrified and tired in a way that had nothing to do with walking.

"Her name is Wren," Sable said. "Wren Alouette Quill. She's nineteen. She has a laugh like a door hinge and she bites her nails and she was taken in the second sweep of Greyharbor because she can hear lies — actual hearing, they ring in her ears like bells. She was nineteen and they put her in irons and marched her toward the capital and I—" Their voice broke. They steadied it the way you steady a boat in rough water, with visible effort. "I would burn down every unwritten street in this city to get her out. I nearly did. I nearly burned down you."

The words landed like stones dropped into still water. I watched the ripples spread.

"The sketches," I said. "The route. The checkpoints. You gave Vane's people everything."

"Not everything." It came out fast, reflexive, and then Sable flinched at their own defense. "No. No, that's — yes. Everything. I copied your sketches, Coral. All of them. The valley, the passes, the approach to the city. I told them about Emberlast. I told them a cartographer with the sight was leading us to the Spire." They laughed, and it was the ugliest sound I'd ever heard them make. "I told them everything except how to feel about it. That part I kept for myself."

Emberlast rumbled. It was not quite a growl. It was deeper than that, older, the sound of a mountain deciding whether to be offended. She had settled near the fountain's far side, and her ember-dim eyes moved between us like a slow pendulum.

"How long?" I asked.

"Since Lowmark." Sable's hands hung at their sides, curled half-shut. "Since before the pass. Since the night I watched you burn your finest map and run, and I thought — there goes the only person in Thornfell who can draw the way out, and I need them, and I will ruin them, and both of those things are true at once." They stepped closer. I did not step back, mostly because my legs had gone strange. "Every mile we walked, I was measuring it against her. Every kindness you showed me, I spent like coin. And you — you shared your bread with me in the snow cave. You wrapped my hands when they froze. You showed me a city full of ghosts and called it beautiful instead of calling it a prize."

"It *is* beautiful," I said, and my fury found its footing at last, rising like floodwater. "It's beautiful and it's *doomed*, Sable, because of what you did. Vane doesn't want Wren. He wants the Spire. He wants to close the last door on a murdered country and call it justice, and you handed him the map to its heart. Those names—" I struck the fountain's rim, and the impact rang up my arm. "*These* names are the ones he'll erase twice. First the treaty magic, then your sketches. You didn't buy her freedom. You bought her a faster grave."

"I know." Barely a whisper. "I know. I've known since the cave, since I saw her in your vision — chained, Coral, I saw her, and I knew exactly what I'd done and exactly what it would cost and I couldn't stop, because stopping meant choosing, and I am not brave enough to choose. I'm a smuggler. I move goods. I don't weigh souls." They dragged a hand down their face. "I kept telling myself I'd find a way to warn you. To mislead them. To take it back. Liars always keep telling themselves the next lie will be the last one."

The fountain sang. Somewhere in the ghost-city, a bell rang the hour, though there was no bell tower standing anywhere I could see — the sound simply lived here, orphaned, like everything else.

I turned away from Sable. I looked at the names.

*Maren Ostreth,* said the stone. *Julek Vane-of-nowhere. Sera Ilbin, daughter of Sera Ilbin.*

Someone had knelt here once, chisel in hand, knowing the end was coming, writing down everyone they could reach. Not to save them. To remember them. Because remembrance was the only resurrection left when a country was unwritten.

And Sable had sold the remembering to the men with the erasers.

"Walk," I said.

Sable went rigid. "Coral—"

"Walk back down the road. Take the papers, take your sister, take whatever's left of your conscience, and go. I'll finish this with Emberlast. We don't need you. We never needed you. I was *lonely*, and I mistook it for trust, and that mistake ends here." Each word came out clean and cold and cost me more than the whole journey had. I kept my back to them because if I saw their face I would falter, and I could not afford to falter. "You held the door open for Vane. Let him hold one open for you."

Behind me: silence. Then footsteps — one, two — and then nothing, and I thought, *good, go, go,* and hated how much the nothing hurt.

Then Emberlast moved.

* * *

She crossed the square like weather. One moment she was at the fountain's edge; the next her head hung over both of us, vast and patient, her breath washing warm across the inscribed names. Up close, I could see what her dying looked like — the scales along her throat gone grey as old ash, the light in her chest cavity flickering like a lamp running dry. She had perhaps days. Perhaps less.

"Little mapmaker," she said. Her voice resonated in my ribs. "Smuggler. Look at me. Both of you."

I looked. So, I sensed, did Sable, who had stopped mid-retreat like a man walking into glass.

"I am four hundred years old," Emberlast said. "I have seen kingdoms fall properly, with armies and banners, and I have seen this — the falling that pretends it never happened. I knew the ones who cut these names." Her gaze drifted across the fountain, and something ancient moved in her expression, grief wearing the mask of memory. "The stonemason who carved them was named Odile. She worked for nine nights without sleep. On the tenth morning the treaty magic took her, and her chisel fell, and the last name she ever cut was her own daughter's. Half-finished. There." The great head inclined toward a corner of the basin, and there it was: *Lil—* and then blank stone. "I have carried that half-name for three generations. Do you know why?"

Neither of us answered.

"Because loyalty is not a thing you feel," Emberlast said. "It is a thing you spend. Odile spent hers on a chisel and nine sleepless nights, knowing it would save no one, because the spending itself was the point. And you—" the ember-eyes swung to Sable, and I felt Sable flinch like a struck animal— "you spent yours on a sister. Badly. Blindly. At a price you did not understand and cannot now unpay. And you, little mapmaker, you are spending yours right now, on pride dressed as principle, because it is easier to cast off a friend than to forgive one."

"She betrayed us," I said. "She betrayed *you*. Your flame, your door, your dead—"

"Yes." Emberlast did not soften it. "Yes, she did. And I could burn her where she stands, and it would change nothing except the smell. Vane already has the sketches, Coral. My rage will not retrieve them. But there is one thing your betrayal has not yet cost us, smuggler, and one thing your anger has not yet purchased, mapmaker, and both of those things are still in play." She lowered her head further, until one vast eye was level with mine, close enough that I could see myself reflected in it, small and furious and lost. "Vane comes with soldiers. You hold the only gift that can answer him. And he—" the eye rolled toward Sable— "holds the only knowledge of Vane's mind in this square. He bought your road, little smuggler. Which means he trusts his purchase. And a man who trusts his purchase can be sold a lie."

Sable made a sound that was half breath, half sob. "You want me to feed them false routes."

"I want nothing," Emberlast said. "I am dying. Wanting is for creatures with time. I am merely telling you what the ledger says. Loyalty spent badly is not loyalty ended. It is a debt. And debts," and here, impossibly, the old dragon's mouth curved into something like a smile, "are the native language of smugglers."

* * *

The ghost-bell rang the quarter hour. The fountain sang. Somewhere above us the Gilded Spire caught its sourceless light and threw it down across the square, gilding the names, gilding the water, gilding the three of us standing in the wreckage of a friendship that was not yet finished breaking.

I looked at Sable. They looked wrecked — hollowed out, the confession scraped raw across their face, tears cutting tracks through the grime of the road. They had not asked for forgiveness. I noticed that. They had confessed like a man laying evidence before a judge and awaiting sentence, and some stubborn part of me respected the difference.

"If you lie to me again," I said slowly, "even once, even small, even kindly — I will leave you in this city with the ghosts, and I will not look back."

"No more lies." Sable's voice was hoarse. "Only the true kind. Even when they're ugly."

"And Wren — when she's free, she comes to us. She's not a bargaining chip anymore. She's ours to protect. That's the price of staying."

Something moved across Sable's face — relief and terror tangled together, the look of a drowning man thrown a rope he doesn't trust. "She'd like that," they managed. "She'd like you. She hears lies, you know. She'd know you meant it."

"That," I said, "is the only reason I believe you will too."

Emberlast exhaled, long and slow, and the warmth of it rolled over the fountain, over the ten thousand names, and for a moment the water seemed to steam, seemed almost to glow. "Good," she rumbled. "Now that the heart is mended badly, as hearts are — look east, little mapmaker. Tell me what you see."

I turned. Beyond the fountain, beyond the ghost-rooftops, the Gilded Spire stood against a sky that had begun — very faintly, very far away — to fill with dust.

Not cloud. Dust. The dust of many horses, moving fast along roads that only I could see.

Roads I had drawn.

"Oh no," I breathed. "They're already coming."

"Then we have work," said Emberlast, "and very little night in which to do it."
