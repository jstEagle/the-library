## 16. Wren at the Gate

The pass had a name once. Sable could feel it the way you feel a missing tooth — a smooth absence where something used to be. The mountains on either side of them leaned close like conspirators, and the wind that funneled through carried the smell of snow and old stone and, faintly, impossibly, woodsmoke from fires no living person had lit.

Sable crouched behind a shelf of granite with Coral's stolen sketches folded against their chest, and tried not to think about what those sketches had bought.

*Wren's freedom papers.* The words turned over and over in their mind like coins in a gambler's palm, and every time they landed, they came up stained. Sable had traded a route through the heart of an erased country for a signature on a page. They had traded Coral's trust without asking, without warning, without even the decency of hesitation. And then they had watched Vane's agents put a village to the torch — hidden gifted folk, children, an old woman who sang as the smoke took her — and walked away because walking away was what the papers required.

That had been three days ago. Since then Sable had run, climbed, bled, and finally found their way back to Coral, back to Emberlast, back to the unwritten city where the last door in the world stood waiting. They had confessed everything at the Fountain of Names, kneeling in ghost-water while erased names whispered off the stone. Coral had not forgiven them. Coral had *needed* them, which was colder and more useful, and Sable had accepted the difference the way a drowning person accepts a rope with barbs on it.

Now there was only this: hold the pass. Keep Vane's riders out of the high valley long enough for Coral to finish the map. Long enough for Thornfell to be born again or die forever.

Sable checked the knife at their hip, the short bow slung across their back, the eleven arrows remaining in the quiver. Twelve yesterday. Smuggling taught you arithmetic like that — everything counted, nothing replaced.

"You're doing the thing again," said a voice above them.

Sable looked up. Perched on the granite shelf like a gargoyle with opinions was Tam Broadfoot, one of the half-dozen smugglers who had answered Sable's desperate messages over the mountain relay — folk who owed Sable favors, or money, or in Tam's case both, and who had come anyway when they heard the words *the crown is burning gifted villages*. Tam was broad as a doorway and had a face like a dropped pie, and right now that face wore the expression of someone watching a friend walk into weather.

"Doing what thing?" Sable asked.

"The counting thing. The *I sold my soul and here's the receipt* thing." Tam slid down beside them and offered a strip of dried venison. Sable took it mostly to have something to do with their hands. "You did your penance. You knelt in the spooky fountain water. The map-lady didn't gut you. Move on."

"She didn't forgive me."

"No," Tam agreed cheerfully. "But she's letting you stand between her and an army, which is more than most people get. In my experience forgiveness is just need wearing nicer boots."

Sable almost laughed. It caught somewhere in their chest and became something else — not laughter, not grief, but the strange alloy the two made together. Below them, the pass unspooled down toward the lowlands, a gray ribbon of switchbacks between walls of black stone. Somewhere down that ribbon, Vane's column was climbing.

They'd heard the horns at dawn. Not the hunting horns of the checkpoint patrols — these were deeper, slower, the cadence of a column on the march, and beneath them another sound Sable hadn't expected: chains. Hundreds of feet, many of them shackled, keeping time on frozen ground.

Prisoners. The prisoner column, marched out of Lowmark and the capital's dungeons and whatever waystations Vane had filled along the road, brought here to dig the Spire out of its hiding place or die proving it wasn't real. Vane had his maps now — Sable's maps, Coral's gift rendered in ink that could be bought and sold — and he was spending them the way empires spend everything.

"Any sign?" Tam asked.

"Not yet." But Sable's eyes kept going to a spur of rock halfway down the pass, a natural balcony where the switchbacks bent around a shoulder of mountain. If anyone was going to come up that road, they'd come around that bend. And if anyone was going to come up that road *unwillingly*, in chains, with guards —

Sable's hands had gone still on the venison.

Because there was a thought forming, and it was a terrible thought, and it was shaped exactly like hope. Wren had been taken in the first sweep of arrests, two years ago, hauled out of a river village for reading tea leaves too accurately. Wren, who had the small quiet gift of knowing where lost things were — keys, rings, people. Wren, whose letters had arrived through three layers of bribed guards, each one thinner than the last. And prisoner columns were not sorted by crime. They were sorted by usefulness, and the useful ones were marched wherever the Inquisition needed labor or leverage.

If Vane had brought his prisoners here —

"Don't," Tam said quietly.

"I haven't said anything."

"You've said it with your whole spine." Tam sighed, a sound like a landslide deciding against itself. "Look. If she's down there, we'll find her. After. After the pass holds, after the map is done, after the world stops ending. That's the order of operations."

"That's the order of operations for someone who doesn't know what it's like," Sable said, and immediately wished they hadn't, because Tam's face did something complicated. Everyone in the smuggling trade had lost someone to the crown's hunger; that was practically the entry fee. Tam's brother had been taken for mending broken things too well. They never found which mine he went down.

"Right," Tam said. "Fair. But here's my counteroffer: don't die stupid. If you go charging down that road mid-battle, I'll have to explain it to the map-lady, and I'd rather fight the army."

Below them, around the bend of the spur, the horns stopped.

In the silence that followed, Sable heard the thing they would hear for the rest of their life, however long that proved to be: the sound of hundreds of people breathing in unison, the ragged tidal breath of the exhausted and afraid, rising up the pass like weather.

* * *

The column came into view an hour past noon.

Sable lay flat on the granite shelf, glass to their eye — a smuggler's spyglass, brass-cased, older than the war it had been bought during — and watched the Inquisition's rear guard climb the switchbacks. Forty riders, maybe fifty, in the gray-and-silver of Halvard Vane's personal retinue, their livery too clean for the road, their horses too fine. Behind them, roped in files of ten, came the prisoners.

Sable counted files the way they counted everything, automatically, uselessly. Thirty files. Three hundred souls, give or take the ones who'd died on the road. They moved like people who had learned that moving slowly was safer than moving quickly and being corrected. Their clothes were prison-gray gone the color of ash. Some of them stumbled. Some of them held each other up. And every few files, a head would lift and turn toward the high valley — toward the unwritten city, invisible below the ridge line but somehow *known*, the way a compass needle knows north without understanding why — and the guards would strike the heads down again.

Sable's breath fogged the glass. They wiped it with a sleeve and looked again.

File nineteen. Second position, left side.

The figure was thin. Two years of dungeons will do that. The hair had been cut short — prison shears, uneven, practical — and the coat was wrong, all wrong, a guard's castoff with the insignia torn away. But the way the figure walked, weight rolling heel-to-toe like someone perpetually about to break into a run, the way the chin stayed up when every other head bowed —

Sable knew that walk. Had grown up following it through market crowds, had fallen asleep to the rhythm of it on a shared pallet in a Lowmark garret, had memorized it the way you memorize anything you're afraid of losing.

"Wren," they breathed, and the word came out cracked down the middle.

Tam's hand closed on their shoulder. "Don't."

"She's *there*. Tam, she's — file nineteen, second left, she's *alive*, she's—" Sable's voice was doing something embarrassing and they let it, because there was no one left whose opinion mattered enough to hide from. Two years. Two years of letters getting shorter, of bribes getting steeper, of trading piece after piece of themselves down a dark hole hoping the hole would give something back. And here she was, a mile away, close enough to count the stitches on her ruined coat if the light were better.

"And fifty armed riders are between you and her," Tam said. "And the plan requires this pass to hold, and the plan does not include a one-man rescue, and if you break cover now, Vane's scouts see movement on the heights and the whole approach is blown. Coral loses the time they need. Emberlast dies for nothing. Three hundred prisoners stay chained. *Everyone* stays chained." Tam's grip tightened. "I know. I know exactly how it feels. Now be smarter than how it feels."

Sable lay there and shook, quietly, the way a rope shakes when it's holding more than it was rated for.

Because Tam was right, and being right made it worse. The plan — such as it was — depended on the rear guard staying exactly where it was: committed to the climb, focused upward, unaware that the pass above them was held by six smugglers, one dying dragon, and a cartographer scratching lines onto paper with a pen full of the last fire in the world. Every minute the column spent winding up the switchbacks was a minute bought for Coral. Every arrow loosed early was a minute spent.

But the column was climbing *toward* the city. Toward the Spire. Toward Coral, alone at the fountain with ink and arrows falling around them. And Sable was up here, holding a pass, doing the one job that mattered, while Wren — *Wren* — climbed into the jaws of it a mile below.

Duty first. That was the whole of it, the whole ugly creed. Duty first, love later, if later ever came. Sable had watched other people live by that creed and always thought it noble from a distance. Up close it felt like swallowing glass and calling it bread.

Then the world changed.

It started as a sound — a single voice, thin with distance, rising from file nineteen. Singing. A snatch of melody Sable hadn't heard since childhood, a Lowmark dockside song about a girl who stole the moon and gave it back with interest, and the guards shouted at her to stop and she didn't stop, and then another voice joined hers, and another, and Sable sat bolt upright on the granite shelf because they knew what was happening, they had *seen* it happen in taverns and on docks their whole life: a song spreading the way fire spreads through dry grass, person to person, faster than any guard could stamp it out.

Three hundred prisoners began to sing.

"What in the deeps," Tam whispered.

The rear guard wavered. Horses screamed and sidled. An officer's voice cut the air, ordering silence, and the silence lasted four seconds before the song swelled back, louder, uglier, joyous in the way that only the truly desperate can manage. And in the middle of it, file nineteen broke formation.

Not toward escape. Sable's glass tracked her — Wren, it was Wren, it had always been Wren, the set of her shoulders unmistakable even in a dead man's coat — and she wasn't running for the treeline. She was turning *toward the prisoners*, wrenching at the rope on the file beside her, shouting something Sable couldn't hear over the distance and the singing but understood anyway, because some things don't need hearing:

*Now. While they're confused. NOW.*

A guard swung a baton at her. She ducked under it — Sable's heart stopped, started, stopped again — and drove her elbow into his knee, and took his baton, and suddenly file nineteen had a weapon and a purpose and thirty people pressing in behind her.

"They're turning," Tam said, wonder cracking their voice wide open. "The whole column's turning. Look at the front — look at the *front*—"

At the head of the column, the lead files had seized the moment. Chains were coming off — some broken, some unlocked by prisoners who'd spent two years learning locks the way Sable had spent two years learning borders. A supply wagon overturned, spilling tools, and tools in the hands of three hundred desperate people become weapons with remarkable speed. The rear guard, fifty strong, suddenly faced not a column of captives but a mob with nothing left to lose, and mobs with nothing left to lose are the one force in the world that discipline cannot reliably defeat.

And Wren — Wren stood in the churn of it on the third switchback, baton in hand, hair shorn, coat wrong, alive, *alive*, pointing prisoners uphill toward the pass with the absolute authority of someone who knew exactly where lost things were and had just found several hundred of them.

She was looking up. Sable was certain of it, certain the way you're certain of your own heartbeat. A mile of cold air between them, and she was looking straight up the pass, and even at this distance Sable would have sworn — would have bet the last coin of their rotten soul — that she was smiling.

Sable was on their feet before they knew they'd moved.

Tam caught them around the chest. "NO—"

"She's *right there*—"

"And the pass is *here*!" Tam roared, hauling them back behind the granite. Around them the other smugglers had risen, bows drawn, watching the chaos below with the expressions of men recalculating odds. "Sable. *Sable.* Listen to me. She's not trying to reach you. Look at what she's doing — she's driving them UP. She's bringing them to us. To the city. She knows. Somehow she knows what's happening up here, and she's fighting her way *toward the same door you're guarding*, so the worst thing you can do — the single worst thing — is leave this pass open while she's pushing three hundred people through it!"

The logic landed like cold water. Sable sagged against the stone, glass shaking in their grip.

Because Tam was right. Of course Tam was right. Wren wasn't running to Sable. Wren was running a battle. Her sister — her sibling, her blood, her whole reason for every sin of the past season — was a figure in the smoke below, and the space between them was full of riders and blades and a mile of switchbacks, and closing it would cost the pass, and the pass was the throat through which everything lived or died.

*Duty first.*

Sable raised the glass one more time. Found her. File nineteen had dissolved; Wren was everywhere now, at the center of the knot of freed prisoners, directing, shouting, swinging the baton to knock a rider's sword aside and pulling a stumbling old man upright by the collar, all in the same breath. A crossbowman on the flank drew down on her — Sable's whole body lurched — and a burly prisoner stepped into the arrow's path instead, taking it in the shoulder, and the crowd swallowed Wren from view.

Gone. Swallowed. Alive a heartbeat ago and now simply *not visible*, and Sable made a sound they'd deny to their grave.

"Hold the pass," they said, and their voice came out flat and dead and steady, which surprised them both. "Hold it until she brings them up. Then we open the road and get them through. All of them." They turned to Tam, and whatever was on their face made Tam step back half an inch. "Nobody else dies in that column. Nobody. Whatever it costs."

"It might cost the pass," Tam said quietly.

"Then it costs the pass."

Below, the battle churned. The rear guard was rallying — Sable could see the officer re-forming his riders into a wedge, could see the wedge begin to grind into the mass of prisoners, could see the whole fragile miracle starting to come apart. Fifty trained soldiers against three hundred unchained but unarmed and exhausted people, on a narrow road with a cliff on one side. It would be a massacre. It was already becoming one.

Unless the pass opened. Unless someone gave the prisoners somewhere to *go*, uphill, out of the kill-ground — unless the road ahead of them stopped being a wall and became a door.

Sable nocked an arrow. Ten left. Around them, five smugglers did the same, and Tam muttered something that was either a prayer or an insult to the gods of bad plans, and far above — so far above it was barely visible, a thread of gold light at the edge of sight where the unwritten city burned with borrowed fire — the Spire waited.

Sable drew, sighted on the wedge's leading rider, and thought: *Hold on, Wren. Hold on. I'm coming. Just not yet. Not yet —*

The first arrow leapt off the string, and the pass went to war.
