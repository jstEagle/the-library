## 3. Thirteenth Moon Rising

The bells of Vaelmoor began at dusk, as they always did on the eve of the Turning, and Aldric Vane stood at his post beneath the western colonnade of the Sunward Court and counted them the way he counted everything: without expression, without error, and without mercy for himself.

Thirteen strokes. One for each moon of the old calendar, though only twelve hung in the sky tonight. The thirteenth was a bookkeeping fiction, a moon that existed only in temple ledgers and royal ritual—the moon that was owed, not the moon that was. He had stood this post for nine years and had never once thought about what it meant that the kingdom's holiest rite was named after a debt.

Tonight he would think about little else.

The court had gathered in its full plumage. Ambassadors from the Salt League in their lacquered coats, temple prelates in flame-orange whose vestments probably cost more than a border garrison's annual pay, the great houses in silk dyed every shade of ambition. And above them all, at the top of the white marble stair, the King of Vaelmoor—Aldren IV, the Beloved, the Ledger-Keeper's own anointed—waiting to perform the Rite of Turning for the thirty-first time in his reign.

Aldric knew the king. Not intimately—he was Knight-Commander of the Sunward Guard, not a chamber companion—but well enough to have watched a man age across three decades of ceremonies. He knew how Aldren held his shoulders during the long waits: slightly forward, a soldier's impatience worn down by decades of courtly patience. He knew the small cough the king made when the incense was too thick. He knew that Aldren IV, whatever the ballads said, had once been a middling swordsman with excellent footwork and no killer instinct whatsoever, and that he had chosen, early and deliberately, to be a good king rather than a great one, because he'd seen what greatness cost.

That was the man Aldric had sworn his life to. That was the man he expected to see descend the stairs.

* * *

The Rite itself was simple, which was why it had survived eleven hundred years while grander ceremonies rotted into history. The king walked down thirteen steps—one for each moon—and at each step he turned, fully around, to face each quarter of the court. At the bottom he knelt before the Ledgered Flame, the great bronze brazier that had burned in this hall since before the temple was built, and spoke the words of account:

*"What was given is given. What is owed is owed. Let the year be balanced."*

Then he rose, and the Flame flared, and everyone cheered, and the kingdom was officially permitted to begin another year.

Aldric had watched it thirty times. It was choreography. It was comfort. It was the closest thing Vaelmoor had to a heartbeat.

The king began his descent.

One step. Turn. The eastern court bowed; the king's face showed the mild, practiced benevolence Aldric could have drawn from memory. Two steps. Turn. South. Three—

It was at the fourth step that Aldric noticed the shadow.

The colonnade torches threw the king's shadow long across the marble, stretching back up the stairs behind him, and when the king turned at the fourth step, the shadow turned with him.

Half a second late.

Aldric did not move. Did not blink. Thirty years of soldiering had trained him to notice things and file them without reaction—a flicker of movement in treeline, a servant's hands trembling as they poured wine, the particular stillness of a man deciding whether today was the day he drew steel. Notice first. Decide later. Never let your face spend what your mind has earned.

He watched the fifth turn. The shadow followed. Late again—not much, a fraction, the way a reflection lags in disturbed water. At the seventh turn the torches guttered in a draft and the shadows wavered, and Aldric lost it, and hated himself for the relief he felt.

At the ninth turn, the king paused.

It was nothing. A hitch, a breath, the kind of pause any man of fifty-eight might make on a long staircase under the weight of ceremony. But Aldric had spent nine years learning the exact rhythm of this man's public body, and this pause was not in the rhythm. The king stood on the ninth step facing north, and for one full second—long enough for a ripple of confusion to pass through the front rows of the court—he simply *stopped*, the way a clock stops, the way a sentence stops when the speaker has forgotten what he meant to say.

Then he resumed, and completed the turn, and smiled, and the court exhaled, and somewhere a herald recovered his composure and announced the descent of the tenth moon, and everything continued as if the world had not briefly skipped like a needle in a groove.

Aldric's mouth had gone dry.

The king reached the bottom. Knelt. Spoke the words. The Ledgered Flame flared—it always flared; there were mechanisms, Aldric assumed, some temple trickery with oils and bellows—and the crowd cheered, and the thirteenth moon that did not exist was honored by twelve thousand people who had never once asked where its light came from.

And in the flare of the Flame, for just a moment, the king's shadow fell behind him on the marble—cast forward by the brazier, huge and distorted up the staircase he had just descended—and it did not kneel.

The king knelt. The shadow stood.

Then the light settled, and the shadow lay flat and obedient where it belonged, and Aldric told himself he had imagined it, and knew with cold certainty that he had not.

* * *

"You're staring, Commander."

Aldric turned. Serah Doss, captain of the inner watch, stood at his shoulder in her dress grays, holding two cups of the spiced wine that circulated at these affairs. She was the best officer he had and the only one who dared hand him things.

"I'm watching," he said. "There's a difference."

"There is." She passed him a cup. "You've gone the color of candle wax. What did you see?"

"Nothing."

"Liar."

He drank instead of answering. Across the court, the king had risen and was receiving the ceremonial accounts of the year from the Lord High Treasurer—a scroll as long as a table, read aloud in a droning monotone that could sedate a warhorse. Harvest yields. Tariff receipts. The temple's tithe. The numbers of the kingdom, recited so that the year could be closed and sealed.

The king listened with his head inclined. His face was attentive. His eyes—

His eyes were wrong.

Not glassy, exactly. Not vacant. If Aldric had seen those eyes on a stranger he would have thought: tired man, long ceremony, thinking about his bed. But Aldric had spent nine years learning this man's public body, and public bodies lie, and the whole art of guarding a monarch is reading through the performance to the person underneath. And underneath, right now, there was—

Nothing. Not sleepiness. Not distraction. *Absence.* The look of a house with all its shutters closed. The look of a room from which the furniture has been removed but which is still, somehow, arranged.

"Aldric." Serah's voice, low. "You're doing it again."

"Doing what?"

"The thing where you go somewhere I can't follow." She glanced toward the throne dais, then back. "Is it the king?"

He should have said no. He should have laughed, or deflected, or given her one of the dozen harmless explanations available to a knight-commander at a state function. Instead he heard himself say, quietly, "Did you see anything? On the stairs. Anything strange."

Serah considered him for a long moment. She was twenty years his junior and had survived two assassination attempts and one marriage, in that order, and she did not spook easily.

"The pause," she said. "On the ninth step. Everyone's whispering about it. Old man, long night, bad knee—I heard three versions already." She paused. "You don't think it was the knee."

"I think," Aldric said slowly, "that I have watched this rite thirty times, and tonight the king's shadow moved late."

He regretted it the instant he said it aloud. Words spoken become real in a way thoughts never do; they take up space in the world and demand rent. Serah's face went carefully blank—the professional blankness of a soldier being handed something heavier than she'd bargained for.

"Torches flicker," she said. "Marble moves with heat. Eyes are—" She stopped herself. "Eyes are exactly the thing you taught us not to trust. You said a sentry who trusts her eyes dies at her post."

"I did say that."

"So."

"So," Aldric agreed, and drank his wine, and watched the king laugh at something the Treasurer said—a warm, easy, entirely correct laugh—and felt the dread settle into him like cold water finding the seams of a boot.

Because here was the thing he could not say to Serah, the thing he barely admitted to himself: it wasn't the shadow. Shadows lied all the time; light was a coward and a gossip. It was the *pause*. It was the sense, brief and total, that for one second on the ninth step there had been no one home behind the king's face—that the performance had continued without the performer, the way a millwheel keeps turning after the river runs dry.

Thirty-one years of rites. Nine years of watching. One second of absence.

He was getting old, probably. He was tired, certainly. The incense was thick enough to carve.

None of those explanations helped even slightly.

* * *

The summons came at midnight, which told Aldric everything before the messenger opened his mouth.

No one summoned the Knight-Commander of the Sunward Guard at midnight for anything good. Midnight summons came for assassinations, for coups, for fires in the treasury—and, apparently, for whatever had happened on the ninth step. The messenger was a palace page, pale and precise, who informed Aldric that His Majesty requested his presence in the Amber Study, alone and unannounced, and that the Lord Chamberlain had been given instructions that the Commander left the feast "to attend to matters of the guard."

Which was a lie. Which meant the king—or whoever spoke for the king—had prepared a lie in advance, within four hours of the rite, and had built it into the machinery of the household.

Aldric dressed in his undress blacks, checked his blade out of habit, and did not bring it, also out of habit: you did not carry steel into the king's private study unless you wished to make a statement, and statements at midnight were usually the last thing a man made.

The Amber Study sat at the heart of the royal apartments, a round room paneled in honey-colored wood and lit, always, by a single lamp of temple glass. Aldric had been summoned here perhaps six times in nine years—once to plan a funeral, twice to plan wars, three times to be thanked for things the crown could not thank publicly. It was a room for truths that could not survive daylight.

The king stood at the window with his back to the door.

"My lord Vane." He didn't turn. "Thank you for coming. I imagine you were expecting someone else. They usually are, at this hour."

"Your Majesty honors me."

"Hm." The king turned, and in the lamplight Aldric searched his face with a desperation he kept strictly off his features. The same face. The same mild, lined, decent face he had guarded for nine years. Whatever he had seen on the stairs—or hadn't—was not visible now, and that frightened him more than a visible wrongness would have. Wrongness you could fight. This was like guarding a door that looked exactly like every other door.

"You watched me closely tonight," the king said. "On the stairs. You've watched me closely for nine years—you're the only man in this palace who watches me the way a shepherd watches a flock rather than a butcher watches meat, and I have always known it, and I have always been grateful." He folded his hands. "Tell me what you saw."

The question landed like a gauntlet. Aldric weighed his answers in the space of a breath. There was the loyal answer: *nothing, Majesty, a trick of the torchlight.* There was the careful answer: *a pause, perhaps fatigue.* And there was the truth, which a man did not speak to his king at midnight unless the king already knew it.

The king waited. And in the waiting, something in his face shifted—a tension around the eyes, a leaning-forward, almost hunger—and Aldric understood suddenly that the king needed him to say it. That whatever happened on those stairs, the man standing here now had been trapped inside it, aware, unable to stop his own body, and had spent the last four hours wondering whether anyone had noticed.

Whether anyone had *seen him.*

"The shadow moved late," Aldric said quietly. "Half a beat, Your Majesty. On the turns. And on the ninth step—" he hesitated.

"Say it."

"For one moment, sire, you were not there."

The silence that followed was very long. The lamp flame bent in a draft that Aldric could not feel.

"Yes," the king said at last. His voice had changed—flattened, stripped of ceremony, the voice of the middling swordsman with the good footwork, the man underneath the anointing oil. "Yes. I know. I was there and I was not there. Do you understand what I'm telling you, Commander? I stood on my own stairs and watched myself smile, and I could not—" His jaw tightened. He mastered it, the way he'd mastered everything for thirty-one years. "I could not move. It was as if I had been set down somewhere just outside myself, and shown my own ceremony, like a play performed by a very skilled actor wearing my face."

"How long, sire?"

"On the stairs? A breath. Perhaps two." The king crossed to his desk and stood over it without touching it. "But it has been happening for months. Longer moments. Deeper absences. My physicians call it melancholy humors and prescribe rest. My confessor calls it a spiritual dryness and prescribes fasting. My queen—" he stopped, and something raw crossed his face, "my queen has stopped believing either of them, and I cannot tell her the truth, because I do not know the truth. All I know is that I am losing time like a miser losing coin, and tonight, for the first time, I lost it in front of twelve thousand people and one man who counts everything."

Aldric said nothing. His heart was beating hard and slow, the way it did before battles.

"I am going to ask you to do something extraordinary," the king said. "And then I am going to order you never to speak of it. Both orders stand. Do you hear me? Even between us—even here, even now—this conversation ends when you leave this room. As far as the record of this kingdom is concerned, you attended the feast, you reviewed the guard, you went to bed."

"Sire—"

"*Hear me.*" For an instant the king's voice cracked like a whip, and then, horribly, for half a heartbeat, his voice changed again—deepened, smoothed, took on a cadence that belonged to no one Aldric had ever met, a voice like a ledger closing—and said, in perfect calm: "*What was given is given. What is owed is owed.*"

Then it was the king again, gray-faced, gripping the edge of the desk, breathing as if he'd surfaced from deep water.

"You saw that," he whispered. "You saw that too. Gods help me. You saw that too."

Aldric's mouth tasted of iron. Every instinct he owned—the sentry's instinct, the shepherd's instinct, thirty years of them—was screaming the same word, the oldest word in a soldier's vocabulary, older than honor, older than the kingdom: *run.*

He did not run. He had never once run, and he was not going to start in front of his king.

"What would you have me do, Majesty?" he asked.

The king straightened. Slowly, joint by joint, he rebuilt himself—the shoulders, the chin, the mild and practiced benevolence—until he was once more the Ledger-Keeper's anointed, the Beloved, the man on the stairs. Only his eyes stayed open, and unarmored, and afraid.

"Investigate," he said. "Quietly. Without a name attached, without a warrant, without waking the temple or the magisters or my own household. Something is drinking from me, Commander. I can feel it the way a man feels a tapeworm—not the thing itself, only the hunger it leaves behind. Find out what has been eating its king." He managed something that was almost a smile. "And then come tell me. While there is still a me to tell."

"And if I find that the thing doing the eating wears a crown?" Aldric asked. "Or sits closer than a crown? What then?"

The lamp flame stood perfectly still. Outside, far off across the sleeping city, a temple bell rang the hour—one stroke past midnight, the beginning of the day after the Turning, the first day of the new year, the year that would contain the thirteenth moon.

"Then," the king said softly, "you will finally understand why I ordered you never to speak of it."

Aldric bowed, and withdrew, and the door of the Amber Study closed behind him with a sound like a ledger shutting.

In the corridor, alone, he stood for a long moment with his hand pressed flat against the honey-colored paneling, feeling his own heartbeat, confirming to himself that his body answered him, that his shadow, when he raised his arm against the wall-lamp, rose with him—prompt, obedient, half a second early or not at all.

Then he walked out into the dark of the palace, carrying the first dread of his life that had no shape to fight and no wall to hold, and began, silently, to count.

Three days later, a courier from the northern provinces would arrive at the palace bearing routine tax records—and among them, flagged by a customs clerk who did not know why the entry troubled him, a single line item from a mountain parish called Thornfell: *one prayer received, origin unknown, addressed to a god with no registered name.*

By then, Aldric would already be asking his first questions.

And in the Amber Study, after he left, the king stood alone at the window for a long time, watching the twelve moons rise over his city—and did not remember, ever again, having stood there at all.
