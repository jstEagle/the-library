## 4. The Ledger of Small Sins

The audit came on a Tuesday, the way ruin always did—on an ordinary day, in ordinary light, while you were thinking about lunch.

Mirabel knew it was coming before the bell finished ringing. She saw it in the way the under-clerks moved through the scriptorium: too quickly, too quietly, carrying armfuls of fresh ledger-books with their spines still stiff and uncracked. New books meant new columns. New columns meant someone upstairs wanted numbers that hadn't been softened by familiarity.

*Audit,* she thought, and her hand did not so much as tremble as it laid down its quill mid-stroke. Ten years in the Temple of the Ledgered Flame had taught her that much at least: never let them see the arithmetic running behind your eyes.

"Brothers and sisters of the Scriptorium." The voice belonged to Sub-Ledgermistress Corvel, a narrow woman with ink-stained cuffs and the flat gray gaze of someone who had counted other people's sins until counting was all she could do. "By order of the High Ledgermistress, a reconciliation of personal accounts will be conducted this morning. All scribes will present themselves, their quarters, and their private effects for review. Refusal is itself an entry."

*Refusal is itself an entry.* The temple's favorite sentence. Everything was an entry. Every candle burned, every prayer copied, every breath drawn beneath the Ledgered Flame's copper dome went into a book somewhere, and every book balanced against another book, and somewhere at the top of all those balancing books sat Ivenna herself, the High Ledgermistress, whose smile Mirabel had seen exactly twice and remembered both times the way one remembers the moment before a fall.

Around her, forty scribes rose in rustling unison, smoothing robes, composing faces. Mirabel rose with them, and behind her composed face the arithmetic began, fast and cold.

Her cell: one cot, one blanket, three changes of robe, a wooden comb. Clean. She kept it clean because she had always known this morning would come; she simply hadn't known which Tuesday.

Her person: the debt-mark, of course—the Halloran debt, coiled down her left forearm in the temple's own indelible ink, fourteen years of interest rendered as a spiraling column of figures no one but a senior accountant could read. That was fine. That was *supposed* to be there. The mark was the temple's leash, and leashes were not evidence.

The problem was the purse.

It was not in her cell. It was not on her person. It was sewn into the hem of her winter cloak, which hung in the communal cloakroom along with sixty identical cloaks, and the audit would reach the cloakroom within the hour, because Sub-Ledgermistress Corvel was thorough the way water was wet.

Forty-one crowns. Forty-one crowns from the Thornfell runs, folded oilcloth around good silver, enough to buy her mother's farm back twice over if farms could be bought back, which they couldn't, not while the debt-mark held the deed. Forty-one crowns, and if Corvel found them, Mirabel Halloran would be entered into a book from which there was no copying out.

She walked to the cloakroom with the others, unhurried, one scribe among many, the girl who was never noticed. It was her oldest talent. Not beauty, not cleverness—*unnoticeability*, the art of being furniture. In the scriptorium she was competent and unremarkable. In the refectory she took the same seat and ate the same pottage. When the inquisitors had swept the halls two winters past, hunting a stolen reliquary, they had looked directly at her twice and seen nothing worth remembering.

But cloaks were different. Cloaks were searched by touch, and touch did not care how forgettable your face was.

* * *

The cloakroom smelled of wool and camphor and the tallow candles burning in their iron sconces. Corvel stood at the center with two under-clerks and a slate, calling names. One by one, scribes fetched their cloaks and submitted to the patting-down of hems and seams, the turning-out of pockets, the shaking-out of boots.

Mirabel watched the line shorten and did the only thing she could do: she waited for a mistake, or made one.

The mistake arrived wearing Brother Ossian's nervous face.

Ossian was twenty-six and had been timid since the womb, presumably. He copied funerary prayers because he couldn't bear to look at the living long enough to copy anything else. He stammered when addressed by anyone above deacon, blushed when addressed by anyone at all, and had once spent an entire afternoon trying to work up the courage to tell Mirabel she had ink on her cheek. He was, in short, the last person in the Temple of the Ledgered Flame capable of a scheme—and yet when his name was called, his eyes found hers across the room with an expression she had never seen on him before.

It was the expression of a man about to jump.

"Brother Ossian," Corvel said. "Your cloak."

He fetched it. He held it out. And then, as the under-clerk reached for it, Ossian fumbled—magnificently, catastrophically fumbled—dropping the cloak, stepping on it, stumbling backward into the candle sconce.

The sconce tipped. Tallow flame kissed oiled wool, and half the cloakroom erupted into shouting. Under-clerks beat at the fire with their slates. Corvel shrieked for sand. Scribes scattered like startled pigeons, and in the churn of bodies and smoke and screaming, something small and heavy pressed against Mirabel's hand where it hung at her side.

Ossian's hand. Ossian's hand pressing a folded wad of oilcloth into her palm with the desperate firmness of a man passing contraband in a dream.

Then he straightened, apologized to everyone, apologized to the sconce, and let them search his scorched, ruined cloak hem to collar while Mirabel slipped the oilcloth packet up her sleeve and stood very still among the chaos, heart hammering, face blank, *furniture*.

They found nothing in his cloak. There was nothing to find. He had taken her burden onto himself without being asked, without explanation, and now stood dripping apologies while Corvel glared at him with the particular suspicion reserved for people who caused incidents during audits.

"Brother Ossian," Corvel said slowly. "You will report to the Office of Reconciliation this afternoon. An incident during a reconciliation is... irregular."

"Yes, Sister. Sorry, Sister. I'm very sorry."

*Irregular.* In the temple's language, that word was a hook, and hooks were for fish, and fish were for eating. Mirabel understood exactly what would happen in the Office of Reconciliation: questions, gentle at first, then less gentle. Where were you last night, Brother? Why does your account show a purchase of lamp oil you cannot explain? Who are your friends, Brother? What do they say when they think no one is keeping entries?

She had seen it done to others. She had once believed it would only ever be done to others.

* * *

They released the scribes by midday, the audit concluded, nothing found, everything entered anyway—the date, the hour, the incident, the name *Ossian* written in Corvel's cramped hand. Mirabel ate her pottage mechanically. Across the refectory, Ossian sat alone, as he always did, but today he sat alone *differently*: shoulders braced, spoon steady, a man rehearsing.

She could not go to him. Going to him was itself an entry; Corvel was still watching, and Corvel watched the way the Flame's statutes prescribed—always, everywhere, in the name of balance. So Mirabel finished her meal, returned her bowl, and walked the long colonnade toward the copying hall, and let her feet carry her past the Office of Reconciliation just once, just in passing, the way any scribe might pass any door.

Through the open window she heard Corvel's voice, mild as milk.

"—and the lamp oil, Brother. Purchased on the ninth. You've no lamp in your cell. Whom did you buy it for?"

A pause. Then Ossian's voice, thin but level: "For the chapel of Saint Vessia, Sister. The votive stand. It was dark, and I—I didn't want to trouble the sacristan with a requisition."

Another pause. The sound of a quill scratching.

"You'll bring me the chapel's oil ledger this evening, Brother. To confirm."

"Yes, Sister."

There was no chapel oil ledger. There was no votive stand in need of oil; Saint Vessia's chapel burned wax, not oil. Mirabel knew this because she had copied the chapel's inventory twice. Ossian had invented a lie on the spot, a bad lie, a lie with holes in it wide enough to drive a mule cart through—and he had invented it *for her*, a girl he'd barely spoken three hundred words to in six years, most of them about ink.

She kept walking. Her face did nothing at all. Inside, something cracked and shifted, like ice on a river in early thaw.

That evening she found him in the copying hall, working late over a funeral prayer, and set a cup of the good tea—smuggled, ironically, from the Thornfell caravans—beside his elbow without a word.

He stared at it. Then at her. His ears went red.

"I don't know what you mean," he whispered.

"I know," she said. "Neither do I." She sat down across from him, opened her prayer-book, and began to copy, and for a long time the only sounds were quills and breathing and the great bronze clock of the Ledgered Flame tolling the hours above them both.

Finally, so quietly it barely disturbed the air: "Why?"

Mirabel considered lying. She was good at lying; she'd had to be. But he had lied for her first, badly and bravely, and some debts deserved true coin.

"Because I owe money I can't earn honestly," she said, eyes on her page. "And I earn it dishonestly instead. Guiding caravans through Thornfell. Smugglers' roads. That's what the oil money was. That's what your lie covered."

Ossian's quill stopped. She watched him decide, visibly, between horror and curiosity, and watched curiosity win by the width of a hair, because Ossian had spent his whole life losing every argument with his own courage.

"The passes," he said faintly. "At night. You."

"Me."

"That's—" He swallowed. "That's a hanging offense."

"So is helping me. Which you already did." She finally looked up. "Why did you?"

He was quiet for a long moment. When he answered, he didn't look at her; he looked at the funeral prayer under his quill, at the neat rows of *may the Flame keep your account* marching down the page.

"Because you're the only person in this whole temple who's ever treated me like I'm not furniture," he said. "And because—" his voice dropped even lower, "—because I don't think the Flame keeps anyone's account, Mirabel. I think we keep its. I've copied ten thousand prayers and I've never once felt heard. Have you?"

She thought of the ledger-halls, the endless columns, the debt spiraling down her arm like a black vine. She thought of her mother's voice, fourteen years ago: *Sign it, little bird. The Flame is merciful. The Flame balances all things.*

"No," Mirabel said. "I haven't."

Ossian nodded slowly, as if she'd confirmed something he'd suspected for years and never dared write down. Then he dipped his quill and went back to his dead man's prayer, and Mirabel went back to hers, and neither of them said anything else, because in the Temple of the Ledgered Flame the walls had ears and the ears had ledgers.

But when she rose to leave, he caught her sleeve—actually touched her, Ossian, who flinched from handshakes—and pressed something into her hand, folding her fingers over it.

A key. Small, brass, worn smooth. The scriptorium's night-archive, where the sealed prayers were kept: the ones scribes weren't permitted to read, only copy, blind, letter by letter.

"For the lamp oil," he whispered, scarlet to the tips of his ears. "Consider us reconciled."

* * *

Mirabel did not sleep that night. She lay on her cot with the key hidden in the straw ticking beneath her, doing the arithmetic that never stopped, the arithmetic of her life.

Debt principal, incurred by her father's failed harvest and her mother's desperate signature: thirty crowns. Interest, compounded quarterly at the Flame's merciful rate, across fourteen years: four hundred and eleven crowns, eleven bits, and rising. Her wage as a scribe: two bits a week, minus room, minus board, minus the cost of her ink and her robes, which the temple generously deducted. At current rates, the Halloran debt would be paid in full in the year of the Flame 812. She would be seventy-three years old.

Unless she ran. Unless she took the forty-one crowns sewn in her cloak hem and vanished north beyond Thornfell, beyond the Flame's ledgers, into the lawless green where names were optional and debts died with distance.

She had thought about it. She thought about it every night, the way a drowning woman thinks about air. And every morning she stayed, because of the clause—the beautiful, vicious clause on page nine of her contract, the one every debtor signed without understanding: *the debt follows the blood.* Run, and the temple collected from her mother. From her brother Tam, who was nine when she'd last seen him and would be twenty-three now, if he lived. The debt was not a chain on her ankle. It was a knife at her family's throat, and Ivenna held the handle, and smiled her twice-a-decade smile.

So Mirabel Halloran copied prayers by day and guided smugglers by night, and told herself she was surviving, and tried not to hear the smaller, truer voice underneath: *you are waiting. You don't know for what, but you are waiting.*

Near midnight, the temple bells tolled, and beneath their bronze voices she heard—faintly, from the direction of the High Ledgermistress's tower—a sound she had never heard there before. Singing. A single voice, high and strange, singing notes that did not belong to any hymn of the Flame, notes that slid like water over stone.

Then it stopped. Then the tower windows went dark, one by one, like eyes closing.

Mirabel lay in the dark with her hand pressed flat over the debt-mark on her arm, and told herself it was the wind off the mountains.

Three days later, Ivenna summoned her. Not Corvel—*Ivenna*, in the high tower, with tea served in real porcelain and a smile Mirabel had now seen three times, which was two times too many.

"Sister Halloran," the High Ledgermistress said pleasantly, "your penmanship is excellent and your discretion is legendary. Which is why I am assigning you to the sealed collections. Blind-copy work. The prayers of the old rite." She slid a folio across the desk, bound in black leather, stamped with a seal Mirabel had only ever seen on documents that made grown clerics go pale. "You will copy what is written and nothing more. You will ask nothing. You will wonder nothing."

"And if I wonder anyway?" Mirabel asked, before her survival instinct could strangle her tongue.

Ivenna's smile widened by a single degree.

"Then, Sister Halloran," she said, "the Flame will hear you. It hears everything. That is rather the point of it."

Outside the tower, the evening sky over Thornfell was clear and cold, and the moon was rising—thin, sharp, a paring of silver. Mirabel counted moons the way smugglers did, out of habit, marking the dark nights when the mountain roads were safest.

Nine moons to the dark of the month. Nine nights to her next run.

She touched the brass key in her pocket, and walked down into the gathering dark.
