## 6. The Hollow King

The summons came at the hour when candles gutter and honest men sleep.

Aldric Vane had been standing his third watch over the east wing of the palace when the page arrived—barefoot, breathless, a boy of perhaps ten with a lantern that shook in his grip. No seal on the message. No signature. Just four words in a hand Aldric almost didn't recognize: *Come alone. Now. Please.*

The *please* was what frightened him.

In nineteen years of service to the crown of Vaelmoor, Aldric had received a thousand orders and not one plea. Orders came stamped in wax and iron, delivered by men who looked through you as they spoke. A *please* meant the person writing it had forgotten how power worked—or no longer cared. He dressed quickly, buckled his sword out of habit, then unbuckled it again. You did not bring steel to a private audience with your king unless you wanted to die for it.

He followed the page down service stairs he hadn't known existed, through corridors that smelled of tallow and old stone, until the boy stopped before an unmarked door and would go no further. "She said not to say who sent me," the boy whispered, which told Aldric everything he needed to know about who was behind it.

He knocked once. The door opened before his knuckles cleared the wood.

Queen Isolde Vaelmoor stood in the gap, wrapped in a plain gray robe with her hair unbound—a woman he had seen only in state, crowned and jeweled and distant as a star. Up close she looked older than her thirty years and younger than her grief, if that made any sense, and Aldric thought it did, somehow, in the way that exhausted things sometimes look both ancient and newborn.

"Knight-Commander." Her voice was steady. Her hands were not. "Thank you for coming."

"Your Majesty." He bowed. She waved the formality away like smoke and pulled him inside, bolting the door behind them with a sound far too loud for the small room.

It was a sitting chamber, modest by palace standards—a hearth, two chairs, a table set with cold tea neither of them would drink. No guards. No attendants. On the table lay a single sheet of paper covered in the king's handwriting, or something very like it.

"You received my note," she said.

"I did."

"And you came without telling anyone."

"I came without telling anyone."

Something in her shoulders loosened, a fraction of an inch. She sat, then seemed to remember herself and gestured him to the other chair. He remained standing. Some habits were load-bearing.

"Then I'll say it plainly," the queen said, "because I have rehearsed this speech eleven times and every version sounds like madness, so I've decided to stop decorating it." She folded her hands to stop their trembling. "My husband is not my husband. Not entirely. Not anymore."

* * *

Aldric had known Edvard Vaelmoor since the king was a crown prince with mud on his boots and a sword too heavy for him. He had taught the boy to hold a shield properly, had drunk with him the night he won his first tourney, had knelt before him at his coronation and sworn the oath that still lived in his spine like a second bone: *my life for the realm, my blade for the throne, my silence for its secrets.*

He knew the king's face better than his own father's. Which was why, three weeks ago at the Rite of Turning, he had noticed what no one else had—and why he had spent those three weeks telling himself he was wrong.

"The Rite of Turning," the queen said now, watching him with eyes that missed nothing. "You saw it. Everyone saw it. The whole court saw my husband turn the ceremonial wheel and speak the words of renewal, and everyone applauded." She laughed, and there was nothing of laughter in it. "Did you applaud, Knight-Commander?"

"No, Your Majesty."

"No. Because you were watching his shadow."

The fire popped. Somewhere below them, a door closed, footsteps fading into the palace's endless quiet.

"Aldric." She used his name deliberately, stripping rank from it, making it a request between two people instead of a command between two offices. "Tell me what you saw. I need to hear someone else say it, or I will spend the rest of my life believing I invented it."

So he said it. Slowly, because saying it aloud made it real, and real things demanded responses he wasn't sure he could give.

"The king's shadow moved late," he said. "Half a heartbeat, perhaps less. When he raised his arm to the wheel, the shadow followed after—as though it had to be reminded. As though it had been elsewhere and came back." He paused. "And when the light shifted, the shadow corrected itself. Smoothly. Like a man catching a stumble. Shadows don't stumble, Your Majesty. They don't catch themselves. They simply are where the body is, always, without thought."

"And no one else saw."

"Everyone else was watching the king."

"Yes," she said softly. "That's rather the point, isn't it? Everyone watches the king. No one watches what wears him."

* * *

She told him the rest in pieces, the way people tell terrible things—circling, retreating, approaching again from a different angle because the direct road was too well defended.

It had started, she thought, nearly a year ago. Small things first. Edvard had always been a warm man, quick to laugh, slow to anger; a king who remembered the names of scullery maids and asked after their mothers. Then, gradually, the warmth had gone out of him like heat leaving a stone. Not replaced by cruelty—that was the thing she kept returning to, the thing that made it unspeakable. Replaced by *nothing*. He performed kindness now the way a musician plays from memory a song he can no longer hear. The notes were right. The music was gone.

"He wept at his father's death-mass," she said. "Real tears. I checked—I know how that sounds, but I checked, because by then I was checking everything. His eyes were dry. The tears came anyway, on schedule, exactly as grief required. As though somewhere inside him a ledger said *funeral, therefore weeping*, and the ledger was obeyed." Her voice cracked on the word *ledger* and she pressed on before it could break entirely. "And then there are the absences."

"Absences."

"Moments. Seconds. He stops. Mid-sentence, mid-step, mid-bite of food—he simply stops, all of him, everywhere at once, like a clock with the works removed. And then he resumes, precisely where he left off, and if you mention it he looks at you with such gentle concern and asks if *you're* feeling well." She stared into the fire. "I have stopped mentioning it. Do you understand what that means? I have stopped telling my own husband that he disappears in front of me, because telling him accomplishes nothing and frightens him—or frightens whatever answers when he's called."

"How long do the moments last?"

"At first, a blink. Lately—" She swallowed. "Lately, longer. Last week, during council, he was gone for the length of a prayer. Lord Chancellor Hesse spoke to him for the entirety of it, reported on grain tariffs, and the king nodded in the right places and agreed in the right places, and when he came back he turned to Hesse and said, 'Forgive me, my lord, you were saying?' And Hesse, poor man, apologized to *him*."

Aldric felt cold settle into his chest, the particular cold of a soldier recognizing terrain he'd hoped never to fight on. "Your Majesty. Has he been examined? The royal physicians—"

"Are temple-trained," she said flatly. "Every one of them. Sworn to the Ledgered Flame, answerable to the High Ledgermistress, and bound by vows I cannot break and cannot even read. I requested an examination six months ago. The physicians came, smiled, pronounced the king in perfect health, and left within the hour. Since then, requests for examination are... delayed. Lost. Answered with letters asking whether Her Majesty is quite herself." She met his eyes. "They think I'm mad, Aldric. Or they know I'm right, and they want me isolated. I honestly cannot decide which is worse."

"Why me?" he asked. It was the question beneath every other question. "Why not the Marshal? Why not the Lord Protector? Why a knight-commander of the eastern watch?"

"Because of the shadow." She said it simply. "Because you're the only person in this palace who looks at things sideways, at edges, at what everyone else has agreed not to see. Because you stood at that rite and watched a shadow instead of a king, and then you kept your mouth shut—which means you're either loyal or careful, and I've decided I can work with either." She rose and crossed to the window, pulling back the curtain a finger's width. Below, the capital sprawled under a fat moon, the Spire rising at its heart like a gilded needle stitching heaven to earth. "And because you have no ambition. Ambitious men report to whoever can advance them. You report to the truth, or you report to no one. I need one of the second kind."

"That's a dangerous thing to need, Majesty."

"It's the only thing worth needing." She let the curtain fall. "There's more. There is so much more, and none of it makes sense except as pieces of one piece." She returned to the table and touched the sheet of paper lying there—the handwriting Aldric had glimpsed on entering. "Three nights ago, my husband wrote this. In his sleep. Standing at his desk, eyes open, candle unlit, in a dark room. I watched him do it and he did not wake."

Aldric picked up the page. The handwriting was the king's—Edvard's round, careful script, drilled into him by tutors—but wrong in the way a portrait is wrong when the painter has studied the face but never loved it. Every letter correct. Nothing alive in it.

He read it aloud, because the queen clearly needed it spoken by another voice:

*"The accounts approach balance. Nine remain. The vessel stirs in the west. Let the ledgers be kept clean; let nothing be answered that is not paid for; the thirteenth turning will settle all debts."*

Silence held the room like a hand around its throat.

"Nine remain," Aldric repeated. "Nine what?"

"I don't know." The queen's composure finally fractured—not into sobs, but into something worse, a fine trembling like ice before it gives. "I have lain awake for a year building explanations. Illness. Enchantment. A curse from some enemy I haven't identified. And every explanation collapses against that page. *The accounts approach balance.* Aldric, my husband does not write like a sick man. He writes like a clerk. Something is keeping books inside my husband's head, and it is nearly finished."

* * *

He should have refused. Later, in the long accounting of his life, Aldric would return to this moment again and again and ask himself why he hadn't—the oath said obedience, yes, but the unwritten law above the oath said *protect the king*, and what she was asking walked a knife's edge between loyalty and treason. Investigate the king quietly. Watch him. Follow him. Learn what wore him. If he was innocent of all suspicion, she was asking Aldric to spy on his sovereign. If she was right—

If she was right, she was asking him to save him.

"Say it," the queen said, watching him weigh it. "Whatever you're deciding, say it aloud. I've had a year of men deciding things silently around me. I'd like one honest sentence."

Aldric set the page down. "If I do this, and I am discovered, I lose everything. Command, honor, probably my head. Treason doesn't care that I was trying to help."

"Yes."

"If I do this, and I find nothing—if the king is merely ill, merely tired, merely mortal—you will have set a knight-commander spying on his anointed sovereign on the word of a frightened wife."

"Yes."

"And if I do this, and I find *something*—" He stopped. Looked at her. "Then what, Majesty? What exactly does a knight do with a monster wearing his king?"

For the first time all night, Queen Isolde smiled, and it was the saddest thing Aldric had ever seen on a living face.

"Then you come back to me," she said, "and we decide together. That's all I'm asking. Not rescue. Not revolution. Just—one person in this gilded tomb who will look with me instead of away. Everyone looks away, Knight-Commander. The priests look away. The physicians look away. The council looks away. Even I look away, some nights, because looking is unbearable." Her voice dropped to a whisper. "But someone has to look. Someone has to stand in the doorway of the burning house and *count the rooms*, or no one will ever know how many are lost."

Aldric thought of the shadow stumbling and catching itself. Thought of tears on schedule. Thought of a king who had once, at fourteen, hidden a broken bird in his coat for three days trying to mend its wing, and who now wrote in the dark about accounts and balance and vessels.

Pity, he found, was a strange fuel. It burned low and long and it did not make a man reckless—it made him *resolved*, which was far more dangerous.

"I'll need access," he said. "The royal apartments, the privy council records, the physicians' ledgers. Quietly."

"I can give you the first two. The third—" She hesitated, and he understood before she said it. "The medical ledgers are sealed. Temple seal. I've tried twice. The second time, the physician who would have shown me was reassigned to a parish in the Thornfell foothills within the week. A punishment posting, dressed as a promotion."

Thornfell. The name snagged on something at the edge of Aldric's mind—reports, lately, of strange lights in the passes, roads appearing where surveyors swore no roads ran. He set it aside. One mystery at a time.

"Seals can be read," he said. "It's a skill like any other. I knew a man in the border wars who could open a temple lock with a hairpin and a grudge."

"Then take the hairpin," the queen said, "and keep the grudge. You'll need both."

At the door, pausing with his hand on the bolt, Aldric asked the question he'd been carrying since the shadow. "Your Majesty. When he stops—when he goes wherever he goes—do you think part of him knows? Part of Edvard. Trapped in there, watching what's done with his hands?"

Isolde was quiet for a long moment.

"Last month," she said finally, "he sang. Old songs—nursemaid songs, the ones from before the Flame, the ones the temple calls superstition. He didn't know he was doing it. And for the length of each verse, Aldric, he was *present*. His eyes were his own. He squeezed my hand hard enough to hurt, and I swear on every god the temple burned, he was trying to tell me something." She drew a breath that shook. "Then the song ended, and he was gone again, and he thanked me for a pleasant evening as though we'd discussed the weather."

"So he's in there."

"So he's in there." She said it like a sentence being passed. "Which means whatever we do, we must be certain. Because if there is even a flicker of my husband left inside that thing—" Her jaw tightened. "—then it is keeping him alive the way a farmer keeps a cow. And I will not carve up a man to kill his disease until I know which parts are which."

Aldric bowed—the full bow, the one owed to a sovereign, though technically he owed it to her husband—and slipped out into the corridor.

* * *

He did not go back to the east wing. He walked.

The palace at night was a body pretending to sleep: torches breathing in their brackets, sentries pacing their measured rounds, and beneath it all the vast slow pulse of a court that never fully rested. Aldric moved through it thinking about shadows that caught their own stumbles and kings who wept on schedule and a line of handwriting that had no business existing: *the vessel stirs in the west.*

West. Thornfell was west. The mountains where the old gods had once been worshipped, before the Ledgered Flame taught Vaelmoor that divinity was a debt to be managed. He'd served postings there in his youth. The folk in those valleys still whispered to shrines when they thought no temple ear was listening.

*Let nothing be answered that is not paid for.*

He stopped at a window overlooking the inner courtyard and found himself staring, without meaning to, at his own shadow thrown long across the flagstones by the moon. It lay where it should. It moved when he moved. It was, at least, still obediently his.

How many men in this palace could say the same? How many in this kingdom?

Somewhere below, a bell tolled the third hour, and Aldric Vane made his decision the way soldiers make most decisions—not with trumpets, but with a quiet closing of doors behind him. Tomorrow he would request access to the council archives. Tomorrow he would begin reading sealed ledgers with borrowed eyes and stolen hours. Tomorrow he would start counting rooms in the burning house.

Tonight, walking back through corridors lined with portraits of dead kings—all of whom, he realized with a chill that settled deep and stayed, wore the same crown, the same gilded circlet passed hand to hand for nine hundred years—he allowed himself one last comfortable thought before the work began:

He had sworn an oath to protect the king.

No one had ever specified which one.
