## 7. A Debt Written in Skin

The ink was still wet on the last prayer when Ivenna's shadow fell across Mirabel's desk.

It always arrived before she did—that was the trick of it. The High Ledgermistress moved through the scriptorium like a draft under a door, and the candles guttered first, and then the temperature dropped a degree, and then her voice came from directly behind your shoulder as though she had been standing there for hours.

"Sister Halloran."

"High Ledgermistress." Mirabel set down her pen with the deliberate calm of a woman who had nothing in her desk but prayers. Which was true. Everything else lived in her head, or in Sefa's strongbox, or in the hollow beneath the third flagstone in the novices' washroom where she kept a purse of smuggler's silver and a map of Thornfell drawn on oiled silk.

Ivenna did not look at the desk. She never looked at anything so small as a desk. She looked at Mirabel the way an auditor looks at a column of figures—checking for the one that doesn't add up.

"You've been diligent," Ivenna said. "Forty-one prayers copied today. Your hand is steady. Your margins are exact." A pause, thin as a razor laid flat. "Your tallow usage is up."

"The cold," Mirabel said. "My cell faces north."

"Mm." Ivenna's eyes were the color of old ledger paper, cream gone faintly gray, and they held no more warmth than paper either. "Debts accrue interest, Sister Halloran. Even small ones. Even candle-tallow ones. You know this better than anyone in this building."

"I know it best because you taught it to me first."

Something flickered at the corner of Ivenna's mouth—not a smile, but the ghost of where a smile might have been filed away, cross-referenced, and locked in a drawer. "Come with me. There is work for you. Sealed work."

Mirabel's blood went quiet. *Sealed.* In eleven years of copying prayers for the Temple of the Ledgered Flame, she had never once been given sealed work. Sealed prayers were the temple's private stock—the ones bound in black leather with iron clasps, the ones carried down from the archive by clerks who signed their names twice and were searched on the way out.

"Sealed work is for senior scribes," she said carefully. "For proven hands."

"Proven hands are exactly what it requires." Ivenna turned, robes whispering against the stone. "And yours, Sister Halloran, are the steadiest in this building. Come."

* * *

They walked down through the temple's bones.

The Temple of the Ledgered Flame was built like an account book: every floor a page, every corridor a ruled line, everything in its proper column. Pilgrims saw only the top pages—the gilded nave, the eternal flame burning in its bronze brazier shaped like an open palm, the great abacus of iron beads that hung above the altar and clicked softly all night as unseen acolytes counted something no one would name. But below the public floors were the working floors, and below those were the sealed floors, and below those—

Below those, Mirabel had heard, were the original ledgers. The ones written before Vaelmoor had a king. She had been a scribe here since she was fourteen years old and she had never seen them. No one had. That was rather the point of them.

The stairwell down smelled of iron and cold ash. Their footsteps rang differently here—flatter, as if the stone were listening and disapproving.

"You'll want to know why you," Ivenna said. It wasn't a question, and she didn't wait for an answer. "There has been an irregularity. A prayer received at the alms window three nights past. It was logged, catalogued, and destroyed according to protocol. But the destruction was witnessed by someone who should not have been present, and that witness has been asking questions." Her voice stayed perfectly level. "You were on night duty at the scriptorium annex that evening."

Mirabel kept her face smooth. Inside, something turned over slowly, like a body in shallow water. *The dying woman. The lamp that relit itself. The voice like cold water poured down the back of her neck.* She had told herself she'd imagined the shiver. She had not told anyone about the fragment of the prayer she'd palmed from the fire—a curl of vellum no bigger than a thumbnail, hidden now inside the binding of her own prayer book, pressed flat between two psalms about the virtue of paying what one owes.

"I remember the evening," Mirabel said. "An old woman came to the window. She was ill. She left a prayer. We burned it, as we burn all prayers to unregistered gods."

"We do," Ivenna agreed pleasantly. "Because unregistered gods do not exist. Because answered prayers are theft. Because every miracle performed outside the Flame's authority is a debit entered against the kingdom's account, and accounts must be balanced." She stopped at a door banded in black iron. "You believe all of this, don't you, Sister Halloran? You've recited it ten thousand times. *The Flame sees. The Flame counts. The Flame forgives those who pay.*"

"I recite it," Mirabel said. "You taught me to recite it when I was fourteen years old and my family owed you four hundred crowns."

"Yes," said Ivenna. "I did."

She unlocked the door.

* * *

Here is how a child becomes a debt:

Mirabel's father had farmed a stony half-valley in the eastern reaches of Thornfell, and the year she was thirteen, the rains failed, and then the year she was fourteen, the tax assessor came with a temple clerk at his elbow, and the arithmetic was very simple. Four hundred crowns owed. Forty crowns' worth of farm. The difference was entered into the Ledger of Persons as a debt of labor, and debts of labor, per the Doctrine of Just Accounts, could be transferred.

Her father wept. Her mother did not; her mother stood very straight and asked the clerk whether the debt could be assigned to any member of the family, and the clerk said yes, any member of sound body and able mind, and her mother looked at Mirabel's older brother Tam—who was slow, who was sweet, who could not have survived a week inside stone walls—and then looked at Mirabel, who at fourteen already had the steadiest hand in the village because she'd spent her childhood copying out the prices at market for neighbors who couldn't write.

"Her," her mother said. And then, to Mirabel, kneeling, gripping her shoulders hard enough to bruise: "It's four hundred crowns, love. You're clever. Clever people find ways to make numbers smaller. Find a way."

Mirabel had stood in the temple's intake hall while a clerk shaved her hair to the scalp—for hygiene, he said, though she learned later it was so that runaway debtors could be identified at a glance until their hair grew back—and while another clerk inked the debt-mark onto the inside of her left forearm: a column of tiny numerals in temple-formula, each digit a year of servitude, each line a rule she was now bound to keep. The ink was not ordinary ink. It was mixed with ash from the Ledgered Flame itself, and it sank into the skin and did not sit upon it, and when the clerks finished they held her arm near the flame and the mark warmed, just slightly, like a thing alive and patient.

Four hundred crowns. At a debtor's wage of two crowns a month, minus room, minus board, minus the cost of her ink and candles and the tallow she was using too much of—minus, minus, minus—it worked out to thirty-one years.

She was thirty-one years old now, if you counted from fourteen. She was not free. The number had not gotten smaller. Every time she approached the arithmetic of freedom, some new entry appeared: a broken stylus charged against her account, a winter surcharge, an administrative fee for the administrative fee. The ledger was designed, she understood now, the way a snare is designed—not to catch you once, but to hold you forever, tightening a little each time you pulled.

Clever people find ways to make numbers smaller. Her mother had died believing she would. Her father had died not believing anything much anymore. And Mirabel had found a way, all right—she'd found the night roads and Sefa Corrane's caravan and a talent for being overlooked that let her walk silver past temple checkpoints in her scribe's robes—but none of it went into the ledger. None of it made the number smaller. It only bought her secrets, and secrets, unlike debts, could be spent.

* * *

The sealed archive was a round room, and the silence in it had texture.

Shelves rose to a ceiling lost in dark, and every shelf held black-bound volumes behind iron grilles, and every volume bore a wax seal the size of a coin, stamped with the Flame's open palm. Ivenna lit no lamps. Instead she touched the nearest grille and spoke a word Mirabel didn't catch, and the seals along that shelf glowed faintly amber, one after another, like eyes opening.

"These are prayers," Ivenna said, "that were answered."

Mirabel went very still.

"Not miracles of the Flame," Ivenna continued, in the tone of a lecturer addressing a promising student. "The Flame answers nothing. The Flame *counts*. These are prayers addressed to the old gods—to the hundred names the kingdom buried when the first king took his crown. Prayers whispered to stones and wells and dead trees. Prayers that, against all doctrine, were sometimes *answered*." She drew one volume from its grille and set it in Mirabel's hands. It was heavier than it looked. Cold, too, in a way paper shouldn't be. "Every answer is a wound in the world's accounting. Every miracle is value created without payment, and unpaid value rots. So the temple collects these prayers. Catalogues them. Studies where they come from, and why, and how often. And copies them—because a thing must be understood before it can be prevented."

"Why me?" Mirabel asked again, because Ivenna hadn't actually answered.

"Because you are invisible, Sister Halloran. Don't look surprised—you wear it like a garment. People tell scribes things they'd never tell priests, and they tell invisible scribes things they'd never tell anyone. If any irregularity recurs—if any prayer comes to this temple that gets *answered*—I want it witnessed by eyes that see everything and are seen by no one." Ivenna's gaze settled on her, and for the first time Mirabel felt truly examined, the way a jeweler examines a stone for the flaw that will split it. "You will copy these texts faithfully. You will report anything unusual. And you will speak of this room to no one, on penalty of—" she gestured vaguely at Mirabel's arm, at the debt-mark sleeping under her sleeve, "—recalculation."

Recalculation. The word every debtor feared. It meant the ledger reopened, the terms rewritten, the number made larger by whatever margin the temple pleased. Recalculation was how a thirty-one-year sentence became a life sentence became, occasionally, a sentence that outlived the debtor and passed to the next of kin.

Mirabel thought of Tam, slow and sweet, living on charity in a village that barely had enough for itself.

"I understand," she said.

"Of course you do. You understand accounts better than anyone I've ever marked." Ivenna took back the volume, replaced it, and paused at the door with her hand on the iron. "One more thing. The woman at the alms window—the one whose prayer burned. You saw the lamp relight."

It was not a question, and Mirabel's rehearsed denial died somewhere behind her teeth, because Ivenna turned and looked at her with those paper-colored eyes and said, almost gently:

"I'm not accusing you, child. I'm telling you it happens. More often, lately. The old names are stirring, and the temple needs its sharpest eyes on it." A beat. "You may find, in the coming months, that being useful to me is worth more than being free. Freedom is a single transaction. Usefulness compounds."

Then she was gone, and the seals dimmed, and Mirabel stood alone in the dark among a thousand answered prayers, her heart hammering a rhythm she recognized from the night roads: *run, hide, survive.*

But she didn't run. She was a scribe. She picked up the pen Ivenna had left for her, and she opened the first black volume, and she began to copy.

* * *

That night, in her cell, she took out the thumbnail of vellum she'd stolen from the fire three days ago and read it again by the light of a candle she officially did not possess.

The dead woman's handwriting had been a dying spider's scrawl, but the words were clear enough, and they were simple, and they were the kind of thing no doctrine accounted for:

*Ossa-of-the-Underroot, who remembers the small folk: my daughter is lost on the mountain roads. Bring her home. I have nothing to pay. Take what I am instead.*

Take what I am instead.

Mirabel sat with that a long time. Somewhere above her, the great iron abacus clicked and clicked, counting whatever it counted, and somewhere below her feet the sealed archives hummed with their cold amber light, and somewhere in the capital a king she'd never seen sat on a throne beneath a crown that—though she did not yet know it—was eating the answers to prayers like these, meal by stolen meal, to keep something ancient alive.

She pressed the fragment flat between the psalms again and blew out her contraband candle.

In the dark, the debt-mark on her arm felt warm. Not painfully. Just warm, the way a hand feels when someone is holding it a little too tightly, and has been for years, and shows no sign of letting go.

*Find a way,* her mother had said.

Mirabel closed her eyes. Three floors up, in the novices' wing, Brother Ossian was awake too—she knew his habits, the way she knew everyone's—and tomorrow the audit lists would post, and her name would be on them, and her double life would hang by a thread of borrowed courage.

And somewhere out beyond the temple walls, on the cold mountain roads, something old was waking up, and it had started, apparently, by answering the prayers of people who had nothing left to pay.

*So have I,* Mirabel thought, and did not let herself examine what she meant by it, and slept badly, and dreamed of lamps relighting one by one across a dark country, all the way to the horizon.
