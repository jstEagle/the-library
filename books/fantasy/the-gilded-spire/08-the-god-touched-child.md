## 8. The God-Touched Child

Pip had been left behind before. He knew the shape of it the way he knew the shape of his own hands.

It always started the same: a fire going down to coals, voices pitched low in that careful way grown-ups thought children couldn't hear, and then someone—this time it was Doss, who'd taught him to gut a river fish only two months ago—crouching beside him with a face like wet bread and saying, *You stay here, lad. We'll come back for you.*

They never came back for you.

The crew had made camp in a hollow below the Thornfell ridge, under a shelf of grey stone, and Pip sat where they'd left him with a strip of dried mutton and half a skin of water, watching the last of them disappear up the goat trail into the dark. Sefa hadn't even looked at him. That was the worst part. Sefa Corrane looked at everything—every shadow on a pass, every stranger's boots, every coin before she bit it—and she had not once turned her head since the shrine.

Since he'd touched the shrine.

*Don't blame her,* said the singing, faintly, from somewhere behind his ribs. The singing had been there for three days now. It didn't use words exactly. It used feelings shaped like words, the way a cloud could be shaped like a bear if you squinted. *She is small, and you are large now. Small things fear largeness they cannot count.*

"I'm not large," Pip told it, out loud, because talking to it out loud was better than letting it talk inside where it echoed. "I'm ten."

*You are a door,* said the singing, gently. *Doors are not large. But everyone wants to know what's behind them.*

Pip pulled his knees up to his chest. Above him the sky was doing something wrong. He'd noticed it first at the shrine, when the old stones lit up blue-white under his palm like a lamp being fed, and he kept noticing it now: the stars near him seemed brighter than the stars far away. Not much. A hand's width of sky, maybe, where the light bent toward him instead of past him. Like the dark was leaning in to listen.

That was what Doss had seen. That was why they'd gone.

Because at supper, when Pip had laughed, three sparks had jumped off his fingers and hung in the air over the fire like little golden flies, and Brother-something—the temple man they'd carried as cargo, the one wrapped in grey with the frightened eyes—had taken one look and started praying so fast he choked on it, and Sefa had said one word, quiet as a knife going into a sheath:

*Out.*

So here he was. Left behind, again, with the mutton and the water and the singing, on the flank of a mountain full of roads that were waking up, wearing light that people could smell.

He'd heard them talking about that, before they left. The temple men. How the inquisitors had dogs—not real dogs, worse, men trained until their noses were dogs' noses—who could catch the scent of an answered prayer on the wind. How anything touched by the old gods gave off a smell like lightning-struck cedar, and how anything that smelled like that got burned, because burned things couldn't smell anymore.

Pip sniffed at his own wrist.

He smelled like lightning-struck cedar.

"Oh," he said, very quietly, to nobody. "Oh, that's bad."

* * *

He didn't stay in the hollow. Staying put was for people who believed *we'll come back for you*, and Pip had stopped believing that around the time his mother handed him to a caravan master in exchange for passage south and didn't watch him go. Instead he did what he was good at, which was follow.

Following was simple if you paid attention. Boots left prints; hooves left divots; fire left ash and ash held its shape till morning. The crew's trail went down the eastern slope, along the streambed, toward the low road—the smuggler road, the one that wasn't supposed to exist, the one that glowed faintly now when Pip walked on it, which he tried not to think about.

But about two miles down, the trail split in a way trails shouldn't. Most of the boot-prints went east, toward the river crossing and the flatlands beyond. One set—narrow boots, long stride, walking fast, walking alone—went west, toward the little market town huddled under the mountain's shoulder where the temple bell rang the hours.

Pip crouched over those prints for a long time.

He knew whose they were. Everyone in the crew knew about the scribe, the one who guided them through the passes at night and vanished by day, the one Sefa called *our ghost*. Mirabel Halloran. She had eyes the color of pond ice and a way of standing that made you forget she was there, which Pip had thought was a magic trick until he realized it was just practice. She never talked to him much. Once, though, on a cold night above the tree line, she'd caught him shivering and wordlessly dropped her own cloak over his head and walked away before he could say thank you, and Pip had decided, in the serious way children decide things, that she was the best person he had ever met.

Also—and this mattered more, right now, sitting in the dark smelling like a struck match—she worked in a temple. Temples were where the burning people lived. If anyone knew how to stop a boy from smelling like lightning-cedar, it would be someone who spent her days copying prayers.

Or she'd turn him in. Pip considered this honestly, the way he considered everything, because his mother had taught him that hope was expensive and you should count your coin before spending it. She might turn him in. Temple people burned god-touched things; he'd heard the crew say so a dozen times.

But she'd given him her cloak.

Pip stood up, hitched the water skin onto his shoulder, and turned west, following the narrow boot-prints down toward the lights of the town, while behind him the singing rose up warm and pleased as a cat settling into a lap.

*Good door,* it murmured. *Find the one who sees without looking.*

* * *

Mirabel Halloran's house was hard to find and easy to enter, which Pip learned by waiting across the lane from it for four hours in the cold, watching her come home through the back gate of the temple district with her hood up and her shoulders curved inward like a drawn bow, and then watching her check the street twice, and then slip inside a narrow grey house wedged between a chandler's and a wall.

Getting in was easier. There was a gap under the shutters big enough for a determined cat or a small, thin boy, and Pip had been described by three different caravan masters as *smaller than his ration*, so in he went, landing soft as spilled flour on a floor covered in paper.

Paper everywhere. Stacked paper, rolled paper, paper weighted down with ink pots and river stones. It smelled of ink and tallow and, underneath, faintly, of the temple's incense—that hot metallic smell the Ledgered Flame burned in all its halls, the smell of accounting.

Pip hid behind a stack of prayer-ledgers taller than he was and waited.

She came in maybe twenty minutes later. He heard the bolt slide, the banked stove stirred, the small domestic sounds of a person alone. Then the sounds stopped.

"You're breathing," Mirabel said, to the room, in a voice like cold water over stone. "Behind the ledgers. Third stack. You breathe like a rabbit."

Pip considered denying it. Then he stood up, because hiding from someone who'd already found you was just lying with your body.

Her knife was already out. Of course it was. She held it low and casual, the way Sefa held hers, and her pond-ice eyes went over him in one sweep—his face, his hands, his empty scabbard-belt, the crew's badge stitched crooked on his collar.

"Pip," she said. "Sefa's boy."

"Not her boy." His voice came out smaller than he wanted. "Nobody's boy."

"Then why are you in my house at midnight?"

"Because—" And here was the thing Pip had rehearsed, walking through the freezing streets, the whole speech, the whole argument, *please don't burn me, I can be useful, I can carry things, I can read a bit*—and it all fell apart the moment he opened his mouth, because he was tired and cold and left behind, and what came out instead was the truth, raw and stupid:

"They left me. Because of the shrine. Because I touched it and it lit up and then I started—" He held up his hand, the one that had touched the stone, and turned it over, and there it was, plain as dawn in the dark room: a faint glow under his skin, tracing the lines of his palm like rivers on a map, blue-white and patient. "I started doing *that*. And I can't make it stop. And the temple men say they can smell it on me. Smell it like smoke. And I didn't know where else to go."

The silence went on so long Pip counted his heartbeats. Eleven. Twelve. Thirteen.

He watched Mirabel Halloran's face do something complicated. He watched survival move through it—he saw it clearly, even at ten, even terrified; he saw the exact moment she calculated what harboring him would cost, what the inquisitors would do to a debt-marked scribe caught sheltering god-touched goods, and he watched that calculation finish and sit there in her eyes like a coin on a scale.

And then he watched her lose.

Not dramatically. That was the strange thing. She didn't sigh or soften or do any of the things mothers in stories did. She just closed her eyes for one breath, the way a gambler closes their eyes before pushing in the last of their money, and when she opened them the knife was gone and she was crossing the room and grabbing his glowing wrist, hard, turning his palm toward the candlelight with a scribe's frown, like the problem wasn't a child but a miswritten line in a ledger.

"This is resonance," she muttered. "Old binding-scripture. I've copied descriptions of this. They keep them in the sealed cases." Her thumb pressed his pulse point; the glow flared and settled. "Does it hurt?"

"No. It sings."

"It *sings*."

"Like water," Pip said. "Cold water. Under stones."

Something crossed her face at that—something old and quick, a flinch, gone before it finished arriving. She let go of his wrist and stepped back and Pip watched her do the other thing, the human thing, the thing that had nothing to do with ledgers: she looked at him properly. At the mud on his knees. At the way his hands were shaking now that the danger was passing. At the place on his cheekbone where Doss had cuffed him a week ago for dropping a coil of rope.

"When did you eat last?" she asked.

"...Yesterday."

She closed her eyes again. This time it looked less like gambling and more like prayer, and Pip would learn later, much later, that Mirabel Halloran did not pray, and that this was the closest thing to prayer she ever did: the closing of eyes before an act of self-destruction freely chosen.

"Sit down," she said. "There's bread. Don't touch anything that looks important, which is everything." She was already moving, pulling a kettle, dragging a stool, and then she stopped mid-stride and turned around and pointed at him with a severity undermined entirely by the fact that she was handing him bread while doing it. "Understand me, Pip. I am not kind. I am not brave. What I am is a person with a debt-mark on her arm who has spent eleven years learning exactly how to be invisible, and you are a lantern walking into my house. Do you know what happens to me if the inquisitors find you here?"

"No."

"Neither do I. That's the problem. Nobody's ever been stupid enough to find out." She raked a hand through her hair. "Sefa left you. Sefa, who doesn't leave *cargo*."

"She was scared," Pip said quietly. "I made her scared. I'm sorry."

"Don't apologize for existing. It's a bad habit and it makes you easy to cheat." She set the bread in front of him, and the kettle on the hook, and then she stood there in the middle of her paper-filled room with her arms folded, and Pip ate, and the singing in his ribs hummed contentment, and outside the window the town bell tolled the third hour of morning.

"Why did you take me in?" he asked, mouth full. It was the question that mattered. Grown-ups lied about it constantly.

Mirabel looked at him for a long moment. Pip had the feeling she was deciding whether to lie, and he felt oddly honored that she seemed reluctant to.

"Because you followed my footprints," she said finally. "Out of every trail on that mountain, you picked mine. And I've spent my whole life making sure nobody picks mine." She turned away, busying herself with the stove, and her voice came out lower, almost to herself. "I suppose some part of me wanted to know what it felt like. Being chosen."

"The gods chose me," Pip offered. "I didn't pick them either."

"Yes, well." She snorted—a real snort, surprised out of her. "Welcome to Vaelmoor. Nobody picks anything here. Things get written on you and you spend the rest of your life deciding what to do about the handwriting." She glanced back at him, at his glowing hand resting on her table, and her jaw tightened with decision. "Tomorrow I'll copy down everything the sealed texts say about resonance-vessels and god-touch. Tomorrow we figure out how to hide you. Tonight—"

The knock came at the door.

Three slow strikes. Evenly spaced. The kind of knocking that wasn't asking.

Pip's glow flared bright enough to throw shadows, and Mirabel killed the candle with her bare hand in one motion, and in the sudden dark he heard her whisper, steady as ledger-lines:

"Get behind the ledgers. Breathe like a mouse."

Through the window, in the lane below, torchlight moved. Two figures in grey, and between them a lean man in temple black with a nose lifted slightly to the night air, reading it, the way you'd read a page.

Reading the smell of lightning-struck cedar, drifting out from under a chandler's shutters.

"Inquisitor Merek," Mirabel breathed, so softly Pip nearly missed it. "They're already in town."
