## 9. Inquisitor's Arithmetic

Dain Merek believed in numbers the way other men believed in gods.

Numbers did not weep. Numbers did not lie, though they could be made to lie by careless hands—and it was the careless hands he hunted. In nineteen years of service to the Temple of the Ledgered Flame, he had learned that every heresy left a residue in the accounts: a candle unaccounted for, a page missing from a registry, a tithe that arrived a copper short. Sin was arithmetic. You only had to do the sums honestly.

The prayer of Marla Vetch had not been done honestly.

He sat in his cold office above the Inquisitorial Archive with her file open before him—a thin file, insultingly thin—and turned the facts over like coins he suspected of being clipped.

Marla Vetch, dyer's widow, sixty-one years old, three months behind on her tithes. Admitted to the Temple infirmary on the ninth of Emberfall with a wasting lung. On the eleventh, she had been visited by a scribe who took down a final prayer—the standard mercy offered to the dying, their last words recorded and burned so that no stray devotion might escape into the world unaccounted. On the twelfth, before the prayer could be conveyed to the burning brazier, something had happened.

A lamp had relit itself. A voice had spoken. And a woman who should have died quietly had instead died *loudly*, screaming words that three witnesses—two nurses and an under-prior—had been sworn to forget.

They had not forgotten. They never did. That was the trouble with miracles: they were the one commodity that refused to stay on the books.

Merek closed the file and looked at the single line that mattered, copied out in his own small, exact hand:

*Prayer recorded by scribe on duty, scriptorium rotation, evening of the 11th.*

No name. The infirmary log listed the scribe as "rotation duty," which was a way of saying no one had bothered to write down who had held the dying woman's hand. Somewhere in this temple, a scribe had taken down a prayer to a forgotten god—a prayer to *Vess of the Cold Water*, if the fragments screamed by the nurses were to be trusted—and had then walked away and said nothing.

Either the scribe was complicit. Or the scribe was terrified. Or the scribe had heard nothing at all and the miracle had simply chosen them as its inkwell.

In Merek's experience, the third possibility was the most dangerous. The innocent were always the best vessels.

* * *

The scriptorium at midmorning smelled of ink, tallow, and forty bodies sweating over their desks in disciplined silence. Merek stood in the doorway a moment before entering, because standing in doorways was part of the work. People noticed an inquisitor the way sheep noticed a wolf at the fence line—even when the wolf did nothing but stand there, the flock rearranged itself around the fact of him.

He watched the rearrangement happen. Most heads bent lower. One novice dropped his pen. A brother near the window went very still, the stillness of a man pretending to be absorbed in his work while listening with his whole spine.

And one scribe—a woman, dark-haired, plain-robed, seated at the third desk from the far wall—did not react at all.

She kept writing. Her pen moved in long, even strokes. Her shoulders stayed level. If she had registered his presence at all, she had filed it somewhere beneath her notice.

Merek marked her. Not as suspicion—suspicion came later, after the arithmetic—but as an entry in the ledger. *Scribe, female, unremarkable.* He had learned long ago that the unremarkable ones were worth watching precisely because they worked so hard at it. Fear made people loud or silent, and silence, sustained too perfectly, was its own kind of noise.

"Brother Prior," he said to the scriptorium's overseer, a soft-bellied man named Elutheran whose hands smelled permanently of gall, "I'll need your roster for the evening of the eleventh."

Elutheran produced it with the eagerness of a man hoping cooperation would be mistaken for innocence. Merek read down the column of names. Nine scribes on rotation duty that night. Seven were accounted for elsewhere during the critical hour—supper, chapel, latrine breaks attested by witnesses who would swear to anything to avoid being interesting.

Two remained.

Brother Ossian Fenwick, age thirty-four, eleven years in service, no marks against his name, no distinctions either. The kind of scribe who copied ten thousand prayers and was remembered by none of them.

And the second entry made Merek's pen pause against the paper.

*Mirabel Halloran. Debt-bound. Transferred obligation, Halloran family estate, year 741.*

Debt-bound. He knew the type. Children sold into temple service against their families' ledgers, raised in the shadow of the Flame, bound tighter than any novitiate because they had no family to run to and no name to hide behind. Good workers, usually. Careful. Watchful. And always, always aware that their entire existence hung on a thread of arithmetic—which made them either the most loyal servants the temple had, or the most desperate liars.

There was no middle ground with debt. He had written a monograph on the subject. It had been well received.

"I'll begin with Brother Fenwick," he said.

* * *

Ossian Fenwick sweated the way other men bled.

Merek watched it bead along the young man's hairline within the first minute of questioning, watched his hands find each other in his lap and grip, watched his eyes travel everywhere except Merek's face. They sat in a small interview room with one lamp, one table, two chairs. Merek had deliberately chosen the room without the window. Confessions grew better in the dark.

"You attended Marla Vetch on the evening of the eleventh," Merek said. It was not a question, so Ossian didn't answer it, just nodded in a jerky, birdlike way. "Tell me what she prayed for."

"She—she prayed for forgiveness. For her sins." Ossian's voice cracked on *sins*. "Standard form. I recorded it in the usual manner."

"The usual manner." Merek let the words sit. "And what happened after you recorded it?"

"Nothing." Too fast. Far too fast. "I—I sealed the prayer, as is proper, and conveyed it to the burning chamber, and—" His throat worked. "And that was all."

"That was all." Merek opened the file and laid three sworn statements on the table, faceup, in a neat row. "Under-prior Halvard says the lamp in the infirmary cell relit itself after it had guttered out. Nurse Cettia says the dying woman spoke with a voice that was not her own and called on Vess of the Cold Water by name. Nurse Brannick says the prayer parchment was found *unburned* in your custody, Brother Fenwick, and had to be burned twice before it would take the flame."

Ossian stared at the statements as if they were scorpions. "I don't—those aren't—they're mistaken—"

"Three witnesses, mistaken in identical ways?" Merek folded his hands. "Brother, I am going to offer you something I rarely offer. I am going to tell you what I already know, so that you may measure your next words against it. I know the prayer was answered. I know the answer was violent enough that the temple burned the body quickly and quietly. I know the prayer passed through your hands. What I do not yet know is whether you are a fool, a coward, or a heretic—and I will be honest with you, Brother Fenwick, the difference between those three categories determines whether you leave this room tonight, leave this temple in chains, or leave this world on the pyre."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below them, the great ledger-hall bells rang the noon count, and Ossian flinched at the sound as though the numbers themselves had accused him.

"I didn't know," he whispered finally. "I swear it on the Flame, Inquisitor, I didn't know what she was praying to until—until it was already happening. She asked me to write it down and I did, it's my duty, and then the lamp and the voice and I was so frightened I just—I finished the record and I sealed it and I told myself it was nothing, that I'd imagined it, because if it wasn't nothing then—"

"Then you had failed in your office," Merek said quietly. "You had carried an answered prayer through the halls of the Flame and delivered it to the fire late. Yes. I understand exactly what you told yourself."

Hope flickered in Ossian's wet eyes. "Then you—you believe me?"

"I believe fear makes men slow," Merek said. "And slowness, in your position, is a sin of negligence rather than intent. Negligence can be forgiven. But there is a question remaining, Brother Fenwick, and your answer to it will decide everything." He leaned forward. "Who else touched that prayer? Who else saw it, handled it, read it, before it reached the fire?"

"No one. No one, I sealed it myself—"

"Someone countersigned the conveyance. The seal requires two marks. Whose mark is second?"

Ossian's mouth opened. Closed. Merek watched the young man's mind race through his options like a rat through a burning granary, and saw the moment the rat chose the wrong wall.

"I don't remember," Ossian said.

Merek sighed and rose. "That is a pity, Brother Fenwick. Because I have the conveyance ledger here, and the second mark is legible, and when I compare it to the scriptorium roster, I suspect I will find it belongs to someone who was sitting very calmly at her desk this morning while I stood in the doorway."

He gathered his papers. At the door he paused, and without turning, said: "You will remain available. Do not speak to anyone about this interview. Do not speak to *her*. The Flame sees all debts, Brother Fenwick—including the ones incurred in small kindnesses. Those interest fastest of all."

Behind him, he heard Ossian's breath go out of him like a punctured bellows.

* * *

Mirabel Halloran sat where he had seen her sit that morning, third desk from the far wall, copying what appeared to be a funerary rite in a fine, even hand. Around her, the scriptorium murmured with the shush of pens. Merek pulled a chair from the neighboring desk—uninvited, unhurried—and sat down across from her.

She looked up. Her eyes were brown and unremarkable and met his with exactly the right degree of polite attention: neither servile nor bold. Perfectly calibrated. He noted the calibration.

"Scribe Halloran."

"Inquisitor." She set down her pen and folded her hands, waiting. Not asking why he was here. Asking why he was here would have been natural; not asking was a choice, and he marked it.

"You attended a dying woman in the infirmary on the evening of the eleventh. Marla Vetch."

"Yes."

"What did she pray for?"

"For mercy," Mirabel Halloran said. "For forgiveness of her sins. She was frightened. She talked a great deal—dying women often do—and some of it was prayer and some of it was just talk. I recorded the prayer according to form and sealed it."

"And after you sealed it?"

"After I sealed it, I returned to the scriptorium and completed my shift." A small pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table. "It was a long shift. I don't recall much else about it. Dying women blur together, Inquisitor, if you attend enough of them. I'm sorry. I know that sounds cold."

"It sounds honest," Merek said, and watched her. "Nurse Brannick says the prayer parchment resisted burning. That it had to be burned twice."

"I wouldn't know. I wasn't present at the burning."

"Nurse Cettia says the woman invoked Vess of the Cold Water."

"Then the nurse has a better memory than I do." Nothing moved in the woman's face. Not a blink out of rhythm, not a swallow misplaced. "Marla Vetch talked for nearly an hour. She named half the old calendar of saints and several saints I suspect she invented. If she named forgotten gods among them, I wrote down what was required by form and let the rest go. The dead are entitled to their rambling, I think. We'll all ramble soon enough."

It was a good answer. It was, Merek thought, a *beautiful* answer—it gave him nothing, gave him a plausible flaw of memory, gave him a small confession of irreverence to make the larger denials credible, and wrapped the whole of it in a weary humanity that invited sympathy. He had interrogated priests, generals, and one arch-heretic who had burned three villages to cover his tracks, and none of them had lied with this economy.

Which meant, of course, that she was lying.

Truth was clumsy. Truth rambled, overshot, defended details that needed no defense. This woman's answers were cut to size, each one fitted to his question like stones to an arch. Only guilt built that neatly—or long practice at surviving scrutiny, which in a debt-bound scribe amounted to the same education.

"You're debt-bound," he said, conversational now, almost gentle. "Transferred obligation. Your family's account, settled onto your skin at fourteen. That must teach a person careful habits."

Her expression did not change, but something behind it did—a shutter drawing back a finger's width, showing him a glimpse of a room he was not meant to see. Rage, maybe. Or grief. Or simply the animal attention of prey that has heard the wolf step off dry leaves onto stone.

"It teaches arithmetic, Inquisitor," she said. "Everything here does."

"Yes," Merek agreed. "It does." He stood, smoothed his coat, and let the silence hang a beat longer than comfort allowed. "Thank you for your time, Scribe Halloran. No doubt our paths will cross again. The Flame keeps thorough records, and I keep more thorough ones."

He felt her eyes on his back as he crossed the scriptorium, and for the first time in nineteen years, Dain Merek had the distinct, unwelcome sensation of being the one entered into someone else's ledger.

* * *

That evening he spread the day's harvest across his desk and did the sums.

Item: the second countersignature on the Vetch conveyance belonged to Mirabel Halloran. Ossian Fenwick had lied about not remembering, which meant he was protecting her, which meant either affection or fear, and both were levers.

Item: Scribe Halloran claimed not to recall the prayer's contents, yet the conveyance record showed she had handled the parchment for the full sealing—both marks, both wax impressions, the prayer passing through her hands twice. One did not forget what one held twice. One especially did not forget when what one held had later refused to burn.

Item: the infirmary log showed she had remained in the cell four minutes after the sealing, alone with the dying woman, when protocol required immediate withdrawal. Four minutes. Long enough to hear a voice like cold water. Long enough to answer it.

Item, and this was the item he turned over longest: in nine years of auditing the scriptorium's output, Scribe Halloran's work was flawless. Not excellent—flawless. Every margin measured, every rubric exact, every prayer copied to tolerance. And flawless work, produced day after day by a woman with no talent for devotion and no visible love of the Flame, was not piety.

It was camouflage. The best camouflage he had ever audited.

Merek dipped his pen and wrote, in his small exact hand, a single line in his private book:

*H. — watches without appearing to watch. Lies like breathing. Recommend full surveillance. Recommend quietly.*

He underlined *quietly* twice. Because here was the arithmetic that troubled him most, the sum that would not resolve: the temple had burned Marla Vetch and her prayer within hours, swift even by inquisitorial standards, and the order for that swiftness had come from above his head—from the High Ledgermistress's own office, signed before his report was filed. Someone had known the prayer was answered before he did. Someone had wanted it buried faster than investigation required.

And now he was ordered to investigate, slowly, visibly, with two scribes as obvious suspects—as if he were meant to catch something. Or meant to be seen catching it.

Merek set down his pen and looked out his narrow window at the temple spires, black against a sky where the moon hung a shade too thin, a shade too pale, like a coin worn past counting.

He had spent his life believing that sin was arithmetic and that honest sums would save the world. But lately the columns would not balance. Lately the errors ran upward, toward offices with locked doors, and the deeper he audited the Flame, the more it resembled not a temple but a ledger with certain pages torn out—and torn pages, in his long experience, were never torn by accident.

Somewhere below, in the scribes' dormitory, a light still burned in one window. Third floor. He had checked the housing rolls that afternoon: the corner cell belonged to Mirabel Halloran.

She was awake. Of course she was awake. Prey that lies like breathing does not sleep well.

Merek closed his book, and in the morning he would request the sealed archive—every prayer the temple had suppressed in the last hundred years, every miracle devoured and written off as ash. Because if Mirabel Halloran was hiding something, she was hiding it inside a pattern, and patterns, unlike people, could not lie.

The first pattern he intended to pull was simple: how many prayers, in how many years, had been answered—and who, each time, had signed the orders to burn them.

He suspected he already knew whose signature he would find.

He suspected, too, that finding it would be the last honest sum of his career.
