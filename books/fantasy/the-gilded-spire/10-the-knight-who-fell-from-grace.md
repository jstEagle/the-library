## 10. The Knight Who Fell From Grace

Aldric Vane had broken into the royal archive at the hour when even the candles were asleep.

He told himself it was not breaking in. He was Knight-Commander of the Vaelmoor Guard; his seal opened half the doors in the palace. But the medical ledgers were kept behind a door his seal did not open—a door of black iron with no keyhole he could find, only a slot for wax impressions from the Office of Accounts—and so on the third night after the queen's whispered plea, he stopped telling himself anything at all and simply picked the lock with a stiletto borrowed from a man he'd once hanged.

The lock was temple work. It surrendered anyway. Everything surrendered eventually to patience and a steady hand; that was the whole of soldiering, if you stripped away the banners.

Inside, the air smelled of dust and something else—something faintly sweet, like honey left too long in a hot room. Aldric lit no lamp. He had spent thirty years learning to see in the dark, first as a boy poaching rabbits in the Weald, later in border wars where light meant arrows. He moved between the shelves by touch and memory, fingers walking the spines until he found what the queen had described: *Royal Medical Ledgers, Reign of Aurel IV, Sealed by Order of the High Ledgermistress.*

Eleven volumes. One for each year of the king's decline.

He carried them to the reading table beneath the narrow window where moonlight fell like a blade of milk, and he opened the first, and he began to read.

* * *

The ledgers were not what he expected. He had imagined physicians' notes—the king's fevers, his tremors, the wasting of his hands. Instead they were accounts.

Not metaphorically. Literally. Columns of figures in three different hands, dated by mooncount rather than month, recording quantities of something the scribes called simply *yield*. Beside each entry, a source designation: *Votive, Greater. Votive, Lesser. Pilgrim Gratitude, Mass Rite. Intercession, Verified.* And beside those, in a column headed *Disbursement*, numbers that grew steadily larger year over year, drawn against a single account whose name made Aldric's skin crawl though he could not have said why:

*The Crown Reserve.*

He turned pages faster. The king's symptoms were there, buried in the margins like contraband—*subject reports gaps; subject's shadow observed desynchronizing during Rite of Turning; appetite for ceremony increasing; duration of lucidity decreasing*—but always the margins, never the body of the text. The body of the text was arithmetic. The king was dying, and someone was keeping books on it.

In the eleventh volume, the most recent, the handwriting changed. It became small and precise and beautiful, the hand of someone who had been trained since childhood to make letters like engraved silver, and beside one entry—*Thirteenth Moon approaches; reserve critical; vessel candidates under review*—that hand had written a single word in the margin, pressed so hard into the paper it had nearly torn through:

*Soon.*

Aldric sat very still in the dark and listened to his own heartbeat, which sounded suddenly loud, suddenly mortal.

That was when the lamps came up.

They came all at once, a dozen flames blooming along the walls without any hand lighting them, and Aldric knew before he turned around that this was temple fire, the cold gold flame that burned without heat, because the cold had already reached him, sliding down his collar like a finger of river water.

"You read quickly," said a voice behind him. "For a soldier."

He knew the voice. Everyone in Vaelmoor knew the voice of Inquisitor Dain Merek, though few heard it at this range and lived long enough to remark on it. Aldric turned slowly, hands open and empty, the way you turned when surrounded—not because surrender was in his nature, but because there were six temple wardens in the room behind Merek, and the door he'd picked was now flanked by two more, and heroics in an archive were just a way of dying somewhere inconvenient.

"Commander Vane," Merek said. He was a narrow man, gray as ash, with the patient eyes of a man who had spent his life counting other people's sins and found the work satisfying. "You are found in a sealed archive, in violation of a Writ of Silence signed under the king's own authority, holding eleven volumes of crown-confidential record." He smiled. It was a small smile, economical, like everything else about him. "I confess I expected better tradecraft."

"The lock was poor," Aldric said.

"It was excellent. We replaced it last week." Merek drifted closer, and the wardens drifted with him, a constellation around a dead star. "You understand my curiosity. A knight-commander with thirty years of blameless service, decorated twice, mentioned in the Songs of the Ford—why would such a man throw it away over some ledgers?"

Aldric weighed his answers the way he weighed ground before a battle. He could lie. He was not bad at lying; command taught you to tell men they weren't going to die in ways that let them believe it. But Merek would have prepared for lies. And something in Aldric—something older than his oath, something from the boy who poached rabbits—was tired, bone-tired, of saying the things that kept the peace.

"The king is being eaten," he said. "And your temple is keeping the books."

The silence that followed had texture. One of the wardens shifted. Merek's expression did not change at all, which was itself an answer, and both of them knew it.

"There it is," Merek said softly. "You know, I hoped you'd say something foolish like that. It simplifies the paperwork." He gestured, and a warden stepped forward with a folded document bearing the triple seal of Crown, Temple, and Court Magistry. "By authority vested under the Concord of Flame and Throne, Aldric Vane, Knight-Commander of the Vaelmoor Guard, is hereby stripped of rank, title, and command, declared unfit by reason of religious delusion, and remanded to exile in the northern territories, there to remain for the term of his natural life or the king's recovery, whichever comes first."

"Religious delusion," Aldric repeated.

"You saw a shadow move late," Merek said. "And instead of consulting a physician, you consulted heresy. That is delusion, commander. The dangerous kind—the kind that reads." He stepped aside, clearing the path to the door. "Your effects have been packed. Your sword stays. Exiles don't carry steel north; the road does what the road does, and we prefer you arrive unarmed."

"And if I refuse?"

Merek looked genuinely puzzled by the question, which was the most frightening thing about him. "Refuse what? You're a soldier. You follow orders. These happen to be orders." He paused at the door, and for just a moment the economized face flickered into something almost human—almost sympathetic. "Go north, Vane. Get old. Get drunk. Forget the shadows. Most men who learn what you learned die of it within the year, and the ones who survive are the ones who stop asking. Be one of those. I'd take no pleasure in your file crossing my desk again."

"I believe you," Aldric said. "That's the worst part."

* * *

They marched him out at dawn, which he supposed was a courtesy—an execution of reputation deserved its audience. The courtyard of the Guard barracks was full. His knights stood in ranks, and not one of them met his eye, and he understood that too; Merek would have made it clear that grief was permitted but loyalty was not. Ser Brandt, his second, twenty years at his right hand, stood at the front with his jaw working like a man chewing glass, and when Aldric passed close enough, Brandt said, very quietly, "The ford," which was nothing, which was the first word of the song they'd sung together after the battle at the Graywater Ford, and it meant *I know, and I am ashamed, and I will remember*, and Aldric walked on with his spine straight because thirty thousand hours of drill will hold a man up when nothing else will.

They took his cloak at the gate. They took his ring. They gave him back a plain wool coat that smelled of someone else's sweat and a purse of twelve coppers, which was either an insult or a joke, depending on how far north you thought a man could walk on twelve coppers.

The road out of the capital ran past the Temple of the Ledgered Flame, and as the escort marched him by, the great bronze doors stood open and the faithful streamed in for morning accounting, and the bells rang the hour in their pattern of nine—nine strokes for the nine moons, the tenth stroke for the debt unpaid, the eleventh for the debt forgiven, the twelfth for the ledger closed. There was no thirteenth stroke. There had never been a thirteenth stroke. Aldric had lived forty-six years in Vaelmoor and never once wondered why the bells stopped at twelve, and now he wondered it with a hunger that felt like the beginning of a new life.

*Good,* he thought, as the city walls fell away behind him and the road bent north into country he'd only seen on maps. *Wonder is the first ration of every campaign. Start hungry. Stay hungry.*

* * *

The north took three weeks to break him down and rebuild him into something simpler.

The first week, he was still Knight-Commander Vane in his own head, and he walked like it, and innkeepers in the river towns took one look at his bearing and charged him double. The second week, his boots gave out and his coin ran low and he learned to sleep in hay barns and eat what the hedgerows offered, and somewhere around the ninth day he caught himself checking the position of the sun out of habit and realized he had nowhere to be at any hour ever again, and the realization hit him like a blow to the chest—he actually staggered, alone on a rutted road with geese watching him from a fence, staggered like a man struck.

Because here was the thing he had never told anyone, the thing he had buried so deep even he had lost the map to it: he had never wanted to be a knight. He had wanted to be free. The knighthood had been the price of the orphan boy's bread, the barracks' roof, the shape and meaning of every day since he was nine years old, and he had paid it so long and so faithfully that he'd forgotten the payment wasn't the same as the wanting. Thirty years of service, and the service had become the self, and now the self had been confiscated at a gate along with the cloak and the ring, and underneath it—

Underneath it there was a man who liked the smell of rain on dust. Who whittled badly. Who had once, at nineteen, watched a hawk ride a thermal above the training yard for ten full minutes and thought, *that*, and then buried the thought because knights do not stand in yards watching birds.

The third week, he watched a hawk again, for as long as he pleased, standing in the middle of the Thornfell approach road with his pack on his shoulder and no one in the world authorized to give him orders.

It should have been misery. It was, in part. His knees ached. His pride ached worse. At night he dreamed of the king's flat glassy eyes and woke with Merek's soft voice in his ears—*be one of those*—and answered it aloud to the darkness: *no*. The queen's face haunted him too, the terror she'd tried to hide behind court composure, the way her hand had shaken as she gripped his wrist. *Find out what's happening to him. Please. Before whatever is happening finishes.*

He was walking toward Thornfell with no sword, no rank, and no plan, and the honest truth was that disgrace had taken everything except the one thing he needed: he was still the only living soul outside the sealed rooms who had read those ledgers and understood them. Whatever was coming—whatever the thirteenth moon meant, whatever *vessel candidates under review* portended—he was carrying it north in his head like a dispatch that must not fall into enemy hands.

And then, on the twenty-third day, the road began to glow.

* * *

It happened at dusk, in the deep fold of the Thornfell passes where the mountains closed in like a held breath. Aldric had made camp early in the lee of a wayshrine—one of the old roadside markers, knee-high, worn featureless by centuries of weather, the kind the temple called *superstition stones* and ordered defaced wherever found. This one had escaped the chisels, maybe because it stood in country too wild for chisels. He'd built a small fire against its base, more for the company than the warmth.

He was chewing the last of a dried sausage when the stone began to shine.

It started as a pulse, faint as a heartbeat under cloth—warm gold light seeping from the shrine's carved seams, brightening, dimming, brightening again, keeping time with something. With something. Aldric froze, sausage halfway to his mouth, every soldier's instinct screaming that light in the mountains at night meant trouble, and every newer instinct, the one born in a moonlit archive, whispering: *watch. Learn. This is the thing they burn people for.*

The light steadied. And up the pass, perhaps a mile distant where the road switchbacked through a saddle of black rock, another light kindled in answer—a second shrine, waking, glowing—and then, farther off, a third, faint as a star fallen into a valley, and the whole dark spine of Thornfell was suddenly stitched with small fires that no one had lit.

Aldric got to his feet slowly. His heart was hammering, and to his own surprise it was not fear doing the hammering. It was recognition. The ledgers' columns had called it *yield*; the margins had called it nothing at all; but here it was, unaccounted for, unbilled, burning free in the high passes like coins spilled from a thief's torn purse.

The old roads, the maps had called certain vanished routes through these mountains. Roads that appear on no survey and obey no gradient, that the temple's cartographers marked with a symbol meaning *do not chart*. He'd assumed it was superstition, or smuggling lore, or both.

But shrines did not light themselves. Someone was moving through Thornfell tonight, someone the stones remembered, someone the mountain was announcing mile by mile like watchfires calling in a border raid.

Aldric banked his fire, shouldered his pack, and did the thing that thirty years of discipline and one week of freedom agreed upon without debate: he followed the lights.

The trail led him up the switchbacks to the saddle, where the wind cut sharp and the second shrine blazed like a hearthstone. Below it, in the shadow of the rocks, he saw them: mules, heavily laden, standing quiet in a hollow. Wagons under oilcloth. And figures—half a dozen, maybe more, moving with the economy of professionals, and among them a woman at the head of the column, broad-shouldered, dark-haired, studying a scrap of paper by the shrine's glow as though the paper were telling her secrets.

She looked up before he made a sound. Smugglers always did; he'd hunted enough of them to know. Her eyes went from his face to his empty sword-belt to his exile's coat, and he watched her perform the same arithmetic he would have performed, quick and cold and complete.

"Well," she called across the hollow, her voice carrying easily in the thin air. "Either you're the world's worst bandit, or the temple finally threw away something interesting." She folded the paper away. Behind her, the shrine pulsed once, slow and warm, like a sleeping thing turning over. "Which is it, soldier?"

Aldric Vane, knight-commander of nothing, commander of himself at last, walked down into the light.

"My name is Aldric," he said. "And I need to talk to whoever reads those roads—because I think I know why they've opened. And I think the kingdom has less time than anyone believes."

The woman's eyes narrowed. Somewhere behind her, a young voice—high, eager, wrong somehow, ringing faintly like a struck bell—said, "Sefa. Sefa, look at his shadow."

And every head in the hollow turned to look at the place where Aldric's shadow lay stretched long and strange across the glowing stone—stretched, he saw with a chill crawling up his neck, a half-second behind the man who cast it.
