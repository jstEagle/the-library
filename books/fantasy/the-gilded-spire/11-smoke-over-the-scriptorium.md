## 11. Smoke Over the Scriptorium

The summons came at the ninth bell, written in Ivenna's own hand, which meant it was not a summons at all but a sentence being read aloud in advance.

*All scribes to the courtyard of purification at the tenth bell. Attendance will be recorded.*

Mirabel folded the note along its creases until it was small enough to disappear between her fingers, and it did—her hands had always been good at making things vanish, ink-stained things especially—and she sat back down at her desk and finished copying the psalm she was working on, because whatever else happened, her quota would be checked, and quotas were the one law of the Temple of the Ledgered Flame that even fire respected.

Around her, the scriptorium emptied in silence. Forty desks, forty scribes, and not one of them spoke. They didn't need to. The word *purification* traveled through the room like cold air under a door, touching every ankle, and everyone understood what it meant: someone had been found wanting. Someone's prayers had been weighed and measured and judged unclean.

Brother Ossian paused at her desk on his way out, his round face gray as wet paper.

"Mirabel." His voice was barely more than breath. "The archive vaults. Third sublevel. They've been carrying crates out since dawn."

"I know," she said, because she did. She'd seen the carts at first light, tarped and guarded, rolling toward the courtyard while she'd been grinding ink for the morning shift. She'd told herself it was ordinary—paper stock, damaged ledgers, the endless housekeeping of an institution that measured everything. She hadn't believed it then either.

"They say it's the Merek inquiry," Ossian whispered. "Everything touched by the answered prayer. Everything from the old collections. All of it."

"All of it," Mirabel repeated. The words tasted like ash already.

Ossian hovered a moment longer, wringing his hands the way he did when he wanted to say something brave and knew he wouldn't. Then he went, and Mirabel sat alone in the great room with the smell of iron-gall ink and tallow smoke, looking down at the psalm she'd just finished—a hymn to the Ledgered Flame, praise for the god who counted all debts and forgave none—and thought about a dying woman in the charity ward whose scribbled prayer to a forgotten name had made a lamp burn like a piece of the sky.

She had kept a fragment of that prayer. A corner of the page, no bigger than her thumbnail, folded into the lining of her left boot where the debt-mark on her forearm couldn't see it. She had told herself it was evidence. Insurance. A scribe's habit of preserving source material.

She had known, even then, that she was lying.

Now the temple was going to burn everything like it, and Mirabel Halloran discovered something in herself that frightened her more than any inquisitor: she did not want to let it happen.

* * *

The courtyard of purification was a square of white flagstone behind the main sanctuary, ringed by colonnades so that the faithful could watch without being splashed. In its center stood the Purifying Brazier, a bowl of black iron wide enough to bathe a child in, though it had never been used for anything but paper. The Ledgered Flame did not burn people. It burned what people wrote, which the temple considered a mercy, and which Mirabel had come to understand was simply a tidier kind of violence.

By the time she took her place among the ranks of gray-robed scribes, the braziers were already lit—three of them, dragged in for the occasion—and the crates were stacked beside them like cordwood. Two dozen crates. She tried to count them the way she counted everything, automatically, the smuggler's arithmetic running under the scribe's: two dozen crates, each holding perhaps three hundred pages, call it seven thousand prayers. Seven thousand voices addressed to gods the temple had decided were dead, or worse, alive.

Inquisitor Dain Merek stood at the edge of the courtyard with his hands clasped behind his back, watching the scribes assemble with the patient attention of a man counting coins. He had been in the temple for eleven days now, and in those eleven days he had interviewed every scribe twice, sealed the third sublevel, and made Brother Fenwick weep openly during matins. He wore the plain charcoal vestments of his order with no insignia, because inquisitors were not supposed to be men, only functions. He looked at Mirabel once, briefly, the way a clerk looks at a column of numbers to confirm the sum, and moved on.

She had lied to him beautifully four days ago. She hated how easily it had come.

High Ledgermistress Ivenna ascended the steps of the sanctuary porch, and the courtyard went still in the particular way that stillness happens around her—not reverence, exactly, though the younger scribes felt reverence; the older ones felt weather. She was sixty and straight-backed and dressed in ledger-black trimmed with gold thread arranged in columns of figures, so that her hem was literally an accounting. Her eyes swept the assembled scribes, and Mirabel felt the sweep pass over her and deliberately not stop, which was worse than stopping.

"Children of the Flame," Ivenna said. Her voice carried without effort; it always had, as if the acoustics of every room rearranged themselves out of respect. "You are here to witness correction. Not punishment—correction. There is a difference, and you will learn it, because the difference is the whole of our faith."

She gestured, and two acolytes brought forward a single page, displayed on a board like a relic.

"This prayer was recovered from the charity ward collection. It invokes a name that does not appear in any sanctioned canon. It was answered." A pause, precise as a ledger line. "Understand what that means. Every prayer answered is power spent. Power belongs to the Flame. When a dead god answers, it is theft. When we permit the theft, we are complicit in it. This—" she touched the page with one finger, almost tenderly, "—is contraband. It is also a wound. And wounds are cauterized."

They fed it to the brazier. It flared green-white for half a heartbeat—the color of the old answering, the color of cold water and lamplight relighting itself—and then it was orange, ordinary, temple-fire, and gone.

A sound went through the scribes. Small. Involuntary. Mirabel heard it clearly because she was making it too: a soft intake of breath, forty throats, the sound of people watching something true be destroyed.

Then the crates were opened, and the work began.

* * *

They burned them by the armful. That was the part Mirabel had not prepared for—not the ceremony of it, the ceremony was nothing, ceremony was the temple's native language—but the sheer industry. Acolytes hauled pages from the crates and passed them down lines of hooded novices who fed the braziers without reading them, because reading was forbidden, because to read a condemned prayer was to be contaminated by it. The flames roared. Ash rose in gray banners and drifted over the colonnade, settling on the shoulders of the watching scribes like a first snow.

And the pages fought back.

Not all of them. Most burned like any paper, curling obediently into black lace. But some—Mirabel watched, unable not to watch—some resisted. A page would catch, flame halfway across, and gutter, and flare again, and for a moment letters would stand up out of the burning like bones through skin. Names. She caught fragments of names in languages she'd never been taught, syllables that seemed to arrive slightly before the sight of them, the way thunder arrives after lightning except here the order was reversed, sense first, sound after. One page, burning, sang. Three notes, high and thin, cut off when the wind shifted.

Beside her, Sister Brava crossed herself with the Flame's sign—fist to heart, open palm outward—and whispered the Litany of Accounts. Down the rank, other scribes did the same. The whispering spread like the fire itself.

Mirabel did not cross herself. She kept her hands folded in her sleeves, and inside her sleeves her hands were shaking, and she understood with terrible clarity that she was not shaking with fear.

She was shaking with anger.

Seven thousand prayers. Seven thousand people who had knelt in the dark with their griefs and their hopes and written them down and sent them toward heaven, and heaven had *answered*, and the answer had been confiscated and weighed and now fed to a furnace because the wrong god had picked up. Because the Flame's accounts required that all devotion flow one direction, into the crown, into the capital, into whatever vast arithmetic the temple served at its summit. These weren't documents being destroyed. They were letters being intercepted. Letters from the poor, mostly—the charity ward collections, the roadside shrines, the rural parishes where people still remembered the old names the way you remember a grandmother's lullaby without knowing what the words mean.

*I have copied ten thousand prayers,* she thought, watching a page with a child's handwriting vanish. *Ten thousand, and I never once asked where they went.*

The line of acolytes brought a new crate, pried it open, and began passing its contents down. And because the universe has a sense of humor that runs to cruelty, this crate held the roadside shrine collections—Thornfell collections, mountain prayers, the small gods of passes and springs and standing stones, the pantheon of her other life, her night life, the roads she walked when the temple slept. She recognized the hand on the topmost bundle. Old Mother Cass of the High Pass chapel, dead six years, whose prayers Mirabel had catalogued as a novice and privately mocked for their rusticity.

Mother Cass's prayers went into the fire, and the fire turned green-white, and stayed green-white longer than it should have, and Inquisitor Merek took a step forward, frowning at the flame like a merchant frowning at short weight.

That was when Mirabel saw the loose page.

It had slipped from a bundle—an acolyte's grip, hurried work, the paper sliding free and drifting down behind the second brazier, into the shadow between the iron bowl and the flagstones. No one saw it. Everyone was watching the flames, and the flames were behaving strangely, and Merek was giving quiet orders, and the ceremony's perfect choreography had developed a hitch.

The page lay in the shadow, six feet from Mirabel's place in the rank.

She did not decide. That was the thing she would remember afterward, the thing she would turn over at night for years: there was no decision, no weighing of risk, no smuggler's calculation of odds. There was a gap in the world—a moment when the acolytes' backs were turned and the novices were flinching from a flare-up and Merek's attention was fixed on the abnormal flame—and her body simply moved, the way water moves downhill, the way she moved through crowds unnoticed, the talent that had kept her alive through two lives deployed now for the first time in service of something instead of against everything.

Three steps. She had stumbled—that was the trick, the oldest trick, the clumsy-scribe stumble, a catch of the toe on uneven flagstone, a gasp, a hand thrown out to break the fall. Her sleeve swept the ground. Her hand closed on the page and slid it up inside her robe, against her stomach, and she was up again, apologizing, blushing, steadied by the novice next to her, laughing shakily at her own clumsiness while somewhere behind her ribs her heart hammered out a smuggler's count: *nobody saw, nobody saw, nobody—*

Merek was looking at her.

Not long. A glance, two seconds, three. His eyes were pale and unhurried and they rested on her the way a hand rests on a door to feel if it's locked. Then the flames surged—another resistant page, another flare of that impossible green—and his attention was pulled away, and the moment closed over like water.

But she had seen his face in those three seconds, and what she'd seen there was not suspicion exactly. It was inventory. He was adding her to a list, and lists, in the Temple of the Ledgered Flame, did not get shorter.

* * *

She should have destroyed the pages that night.

That was the sane course, the survivor's course: wait for the dormitory to empty, feed the stolen pages to her candle, watch them curl, done. Three pages—because in the hours after the burning, alone in the scriptorium finishing her quota with hands that would not quite steady, she had counted what her sleeve had gathered, and it was three. Three pages torn from different bundles at different moments, her body having worked with a thief's efficiency while her mind stood aside in shock. One was Mother Cass's—a prayer for safe passage, invoking a name rendered in the old mountain script, the characters shaped like footprints. One was a fragment of a litany, its header burned away, its remaining lines a rhythm of invocation that made the lamp above her desk flicker in time as she read. And the third—

The third was the answered prayer. Or a sister to it. The same cramped, dying hand as the woman in the charity ward, the same desperate scrawl, and at the bottom, where the temple's auditors had stamped CONDEMED in red across the invocation, the ink of the stamp had refused to take. It sat on the surface of the page in beads, like water on oil, like the paper itself declining to acknowledge the verdict.

She sat in the empty scriptorium holding that page, and the candle flame leaned toward it, gently, repeatedly, like a plant toward sun.

Burn it, said the part of her that had survived fourteen years of double life. Burn it and be invisible again. You have a debt. You have Ivenna's eye on you. You have Merek counting you into his sums. You cannot afford to be the person who steals from a pyre.

And underneath that voice, older and quieter, the voice she'd been hearing since the lamp relit itself in the charity ward: *You have copied ten thousand prayers and never sent one up for yourself. What did you think you were saving your soul for?*

She folded the pages small. She did not burn them.

* * *

The mark woke on the third night.

Her debt-mark occupied the inside of her left forearm—a ledger-line scar, fine as script, transferred to her skin at fourteen when the Halloran family's obligations were formally assumed by their last surviving member. For ten years it had been exactly what it appeared: a scar. Raised, pale, faintly iridescent in certain lights the way scars are, and dumb. Dead tissue. The temple's signature on her body, permanent and silent.

She woke past midnight with her arm burning.

Not hurting—burning. There was a distinction, and her body insisted on it with total authority: this was heat without injury, warmth radiating from the scar tissue outward, and when she threw back the blanket and turned her arm to the window's moonlight, she saw the lines of the debt-mark glowing.

Faintly. A gold so deep it was nearly amber, moving beneath the skin like light under ice, tracing the ledger-lines in slow pulses. Pulse, fade. Pulse, fade. A rhythm. She lay very still and watched it, and slowly, with the detached horror of a scribe proofreading her own flesh, she recognized the rhythm.

It matched the flicker of the candle leaning toward the stolen pages, which she had hidden, wrapped in oilcloth, inside the false bottom of her ink chest.

She got up. She crossed the dark dormitory aisle—eleven sleeping scribes, the sound of rain beginning against the shutters—and knelt at her chest and unwrapped the oilcloth, and the pages gave off a light of their own now, barely, a breath of green-white along their edges, and her scar pulsed in answer, pulse for pulse, like two lamps hung in separate windows signaling across a distance.

*What are you?* she thought—at the pages, at the mark, at whatever circuit had opened between them. *What did you put in me?*

No answer came. But the glow steadied, and warmed, and for one absurd moment the sensation in her arm was not pain or fear at all but something embarrassingly close to recognition. The feeling of a hand taken. Of being *read*.

She wrapped the pages away. The glow faded to nothing within minutes, leaving her kneeling in the dark with rain on the shutters and her heart going like a courier's hooves.

In the morning, the scar looked ordinary. Pale. Silent. But she caught herself, three times before the noon bell, pressing her thumb against it as if checking a coin for clipping. And each time, beneath the dead-feeling skin, she thought she felt the faintest residual warmth. An ember that had learned to wait.

* * *

On the fifth night after the burning, Brother Ossian came to her desk after compline, moving with the careful casualness of a man carrying nitroglycerin, and set down a returned manuscript for rebinding. Inside its boards, folded flat, was a scrap of newsprint from the capital gazette.

She read it with the pages of the binding open over it like a screen. A brief item, buried on the fourth page: *Knight-Commander Aldric Vane, formerly of the King's Own, stripped of command following irregularities in the royal household records. Exiled to the northern garrisons. Effective immediately.*

Northern garrisons. Northern roads. Thornfell.

Mirabel looked at the item for a long time. Around her the scriptorium settled into its evening rhythms—candles snuffed, desks squared, the great room breathing its smell of ink and stone—and her arm lay quietly on the desk, scar-side down, warm as a held secret.

She had stolen from a pyre. She had chosen, with her own skin, and her skin had accepted the choice and begun to change to fit it. And now the capital was sending its discarded knights north, along roads that were hers, toward mountains where wayshrines had started to glow.

She folded the newsprint away and reached for her pen.

There was a caravan leaving for Thornfell in four days. She intended to be on it.
