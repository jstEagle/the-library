## 13. The Crown's Hunger

The fire was small and shielded, the way Sefa Corrane insisted every fire be built on roads that should not exist—a ring of stones, a screen of cut pine boughs, the light killed before it could climb higher than a man's knee. Aldric Vane had spent twenty-two years sleeping beside fires that were meant to be seen. Beacon-fires. Signal-fires. The great watch-blazes of the garrisons, tall as three men, roaring defiance at the dark.

This was the opposite of everything he knew. This fire apologized for itself.

He sat across it from Mirabel Halloran and watched her not-watch him back. She had a way of looking past a man, or through him, or at some middle distance where his face dissolved into the general landscape, and he understood now—had understood since the pass, since she'd read those impossible roads like a priest reads scripture—that this was not rudeness. It was armor. She made herself unremarkable the way other people made themselves loud.

"You said you'd tell me what I was exiled for," she said. "Or rather, what the king is. You've been circling it for two days."

"I've been deciding how to say it without you laughing me off this mountain."

"Oh, I don't laugh much," Mirabel said. "It costs too much."

Behind her, at the edge of the firelight, Pip sat with their knees drawn up and their eyes enormous. The child had not spoken since the pass—not since Merek's hounds had come howling down on them and Sefa had put herself between the company and the crossbows. They'd carried Sefa out on a litter of cloaks, blood soaking through in slow dark pulses, and left her below with four of Corrane's crew who had volunteered—or been volunteered—to keep her alive until they could bring a herb-woman up from the low valleys. Pip had watched the whole thing from behind Mirabel's skirts, if a smuggler could be said to have skirts. Aldric suspected the child had watched everything, always, and that watching was the whole of what Pip did.

"Start anywhere," Mirabel said. "But start."

Aldric looked into the small apologetic fire and found, to his surprise, that the words came easily. He had been carrying them so long they had gone soft inside him, like coins kept too long in a fist.

* * *

"I was eleven years old when my father took me to my first Rite of Turning," he said. "Every knight-commander's heir goes. It's tradition. The king renews his oath before the assembled orders—the Crown swears to the Kingdom, the Kingdom swears to the Crown. Very old words. Older than the temple, though the temple likes to claim otherwise." He turned his hands toward the heat. "I stood in the Great Hall for three hours while the procession arranged itself, and then the king came in wearing the Gilded Crown—you've seen it on the coinage—and I remember thinking, even at eleven, that he looked tired. Not kingly-tired. Tired like a man hauling something uphill."

"And now?"

"Now I know he *was* hauling something. We all were. Every prayer answered in Vaelmoor for nine hundred years has been hauled uphill by that man." Aldric heard his own voice go flat and strange. "You asked once why the temple burns prayers to the old gods. Why the Inquisition exists. Why a faith would spend centuries stamping out miracles instead of making them. Everyone asks eventually, and everyone gets the same answer: heresy, corruption, false gods, dangerous power. And it's all true, the way a lie can be true. But it isn't the *reason*."

"The reason?"

"The crown-spirit." He said it plainly, because there was no other way to say it, and watched Mirabel go very still. Even Pip leaned forward an inch. "The first kings didn't conquer Vaelmoor with swords alone. Swords were everywhere in those days. What they had was something else—something they caught, or bound, or bargained with—I've read fragments of three different accounts and no two agree. A god, a demon, a hunger older than names. Whatever it was, they bound it into a crown of gold and iron and they put it on the head of the king, and they made it part of the succession, and they called it the blessing of the realm. And then—" he paused—"they discovered what it ate."

Mirabel's voice came out carefully level. "What does it eat?"

"Prayer. Answered prayer specifically. Faith that gets results. When a farmer prays for rain and rain comes, when a mother prays over a fever and the fever breaks—somewhere in that moment, power moves through the world, and the crown drinks it. That was the design. The first kings took a wild, hungry thing and gave it a regulated diet. Every miracle in the kingdom became tribute. Every answered prayer became a tax." He laughed, and hated the sound of it. "You copy prayers for a living, Mistress Halloran. Have you ever wondered why the Temple of the Ledgered Flame is so obsessed with ledgers?"

"I've wondered many things about the temple. Mostly at night."

"Because the temple isn't a church. It never was. It's a *collection agency*. Nine hundred years ago, the first Ledgermistresses worked out the truth: you can't let faith flow freely to the old gods, because every drop that reaches them is a drop the crown doesn't drink, and a starving crown-spirit doesn't stay politely bound—it thrashes. So they built a system. One god, one crown, one authorized channel. All prayer routed through the Flame, all miracles weighed and audited and approved, all unauthorized divinity burned as heresy." His jaw tightened. "They didn't suppress the old gods out of piety. They suppressed them out of *arithmetic*. Every shrine they torched was a competitor closed. Every saint of the old ways erased was a meal redirected. The kingdom runs on one economy of grace, and the crown holds the monopoly, and the temple collects."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the pines with a sound like distant surf, and for a moment Aldric thought he heard something else underneath it—something almost like singing—but it passed, or he imagined it, and he pressed on because if he stopped now he might never start again.

"There are records," he said. "Sealed ledgers in the royal archive, the ones I was reading when they stripped my command. Accounts going back to the founding. Do you know what's in them? Not battles. Not laws. *Yields.* Prayers received, prayers granted, power harvested per season, per province, per year. Drought years marked in red. Plague years marked in gold—the crown feeds magnificently during plagues, did you know that? Thousands of desperate prayers, all of them answered just often enough to keep hope alive, all of it flowing up through the Flame into the crown." He looked at her. "Your High Ledgermistress knows. Whoever sits in Ivenna's chair has always known. The temple's true scripture is a feeding schedule."

Mirabel had not moved. But Aldric saw her hands, folded in her lap, tighten until the knuckles stood white as candle-wax, and he saw her glance flick—just once, involuntary—toward the child at the edge of the light.

"And the king?" she asked. "The man under the crown. What does nine hundred years of—of being a drinking vessel do to him?"

"It hollows him." The words came out of Aldric quieter than he intended. "Not all at once. The binding takes a healthy man and spends him across a lifetime. First the shadow goes—I told you about the Rite, the half-second delay. Then the eyes, sometimes. Then whole minutes, hours, days where the man simply isn't there and something else is using his face. The spirit keeps him alive because a dead host ends the binding, and it is very, very motivated to continue existing. It patches him. Pours stolen grace into the wounds. The kings of Vaelmoor live long and die empty." He swallowed. "King Edren is thirty-eight years old. He has worn the crown since he was nineteen. There are mornings, according to his own physicians' sealed notes, when he wakes and does not remember his wife's name until noon."

Pip made a sound. Small. Almost nothing—a breath shaped like a word and then swallowed. Aldric looked over, and the child was staring into the coals with an expression far too old for that face, and he thought: *the child hears it. Gods know how, but the child hears something.*

"And the thirteenth moon," Mirabel said. "You mentioned it before. On the road. You said the pages we stole spoke of it."

"They did. And I'll tell you what I understand, which is less than I'd like. The binding was made at the Spire—your Spire, the gilded one, the temple's mother-house. Once a century, roughly, the moons stack in a pattern the old astronomers called the Thirteenth Turning, and on those nights the original binding weakens. Thin walls, thin nights. The spirit strains against its chain, and the crown drinks harder to compensate, and the temple works triple shifts routing prayer to keep it fed. Most Turnings pass without incident. But the accounts I read—" he hesitated, and then decided that a man already exiled had nothing left to protect by lying, "—the accounts I read show the current Ledgermistress preparing for this Turning differently than any in recorded memory. Massive transfers. Sealed caravans of consecrated goods moving toward the capital. Prayer-quotas doubled across six provinces. She's stockpiling. Either she expects a feast or she expects a famine, and either way, whatever's coming, the temple intends to meet it with the crown fed fat."

"Or," Mirabel said slowly, "she intends to *change the terms*."

"That," Aldric admitted, "is the question that got me exiled."

* * *

He told them the rest in order, because order mattered; he had learned in the army that horror delivered out of sequence becomes mere sensation, but horror delivered in order becomes understanding, and understanding was the only weapon he had left to give these two.

He told them how he had found the medical ledgers—how a routine audit of palace provisioning had led him to discrepancies, how the discrepancies had led him downward, floor by floor, lock by picked lock, into the sealed sub-archive where the truth of nine hundred years was kept in neat columns and careful script. He told them about the succession records, how each dying king's last months showed the same pattern—accelerating hollowing, frantic feeding, and then, always, a page torn out. Nine hundred years of torn pages. Someone, in every generation, had decided the full account must never balance.

He told them about the night he'd been caught. The quiet, almost gentle efficiency of the magisters. No shouting. No trial. Just a letter of exile signed in the king's name—which was to say, possibly, in the crown's name—ordering Knight-Commander Aldric Vane north to Thornfell, of all places, the one corner of the kingdom where the old roads still breathed and the temple's grip went numb. He had wondered, walking north with his command stripped and his honor forfeit, whether it was mercy or disposal. Now, sitting on this mountainside between a smuggler-scribe and a glowing child, he suspected it had been neither. He suspected someone in the capital had wanted exactly this: a disgraced soldier dropped onto the board like a piece nobody remembered owning.

"Or the crown simply made an error," he said. "It's been doing that more lately. The hollow kings forget things. Perhaps whoever ordered my exile forgot why they were doing it halfway through the sentence." He shrugged. "Either way, here I am. The kingdom's garbage, washed up on your road."

"You're not garbage," Pip said.

Both adults turned. The child had not spoken in two days. Their voice was thin and clear as a bell struck once, and in the firelight their eyes held that faint luminescence Aldric had been pretending not to notice for a week—the light of a wayshrine, the light of something waking.

"You're a knight," Pip said. "Knights are stories. Stories aren't garbage. Stories are *food* too, but the good kind." The child hugged their knees tighter. "That's why the bad thing in the crown eats prayers. Because prayers are stories you mean. And the old gods used to eat them too, but they shared. The bad thing doesn't share."

Silence held the little camp. The fire cracked. Far away, down the black shoulder of the mountain, Thornfell waited with its impossible roads, and beyond Thornfell the capital, and beyond the capital the Spire, gilded and hungry, counting its accounts in the dark.

"How do you know that?" Mirabel asked gently. "About sharing?"

Pip considered the question with the gravity of a scholar. "The shrine sang it to me," they said. "Before the bad men came. It sang the whole song. I only remember the ending." The child's voice dropped to a whisper. "The ending says: *what was taken can be carried, but only by one who owes, and only where the debt began.*"

Aldric felt the words land somewhere in his chest like a stone dropped down a well. He looked at Mirabel Halloran—debt-marked, temple-branded, a woman whose entire life was a ledger of owed things—and saw her looking back at him, and neither of them said anything at all, because there was nothing yet to say.

The child yawned, laid their head against a rolled cloak, and was asleep within minutes, breathing softly, a faint gold shimmer pulsing at their temple in time with some distant heartbeat.

Mirabel banked the fire. Her movements were precise, economical, the movements of a woman who had performed ten thousand small acts of survival without ever once being thanked for them. When she spoke, she did not look at Aldric.

"One who owes," she repeated. "And only where the debt began."

"The Spire," Aldric said. "If I'm right—if any of this is right—the binding was made at the Spire, and the Thirteenth Turning is coming, and the woman who runs the ledger is preparing something. Which means we have a choice. We run. North, west, over the border—there are places even the crown's arithmetic doesn't reach. Or—" he gestured at the sleeping child, at the stolen pages wrapped in oilcloth inside Mirabel's coat, at the burn scar on her hand that glowed faintly in the dark like a coal refusing to die—"we go south. Into the teeth of it. Toward the one building in Vaelmoor where every lie of the last nine hundred years was written down and locked away."

Mirabel was quiet for a long time. When she finally answered, her voice was steady and cold and absolutely certain, and it frightened him more than anything the crown-spirit could have done.

"Aldric. I have spent my whole life paying debts to a thing that eats people. You're telling me the debt itself is the weapon." She pulled her coat tighter around the sleeping child. "We go south."

Far to the south, past the passes, past the plains, a gilded spire caught the rising moonlight—and somewhere beneath the gold, something ancient turned over in its sleep, and began, very slowly, to count.
