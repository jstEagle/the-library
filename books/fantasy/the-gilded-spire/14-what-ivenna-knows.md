## 14. What Ivenna Knows

The accounts did not balance.

Ivenna Corvane, High Ledgermistress of the Temple of the Ledgered Flame, Keeper of the Ninth Seal, servant of the Crown Above Crowns, sat in her office above the grand accounting hall and looked at the discrepancy for the fourth time that morning. It was small. It was always small. That was what made it interesting.

Three candles answered in the lowlands parish of Vess. Two were recorded. One was not.

She dipped her pen and wrote the figure again, slowly, as if writing it more carefully might make the numbers agree with one another. They did not. Somewhere in the kingdom, a prayer had been answered off the books—a miracle consumed without a corresponding entry—and the Ledgered Flame did not tolerate unrecorded meals any more than a merchant tolerated unrecorded coin.

She closed the ledger and pressed her palm flat against its cover, feeling the warmth beneath the vellum. The Flame was warm even when no fire burned near it. It had been warm for three hundred years, ever since the first High Ledgermistress had signed the Concordat in ink mixed with ash from the king's own hearth. *We will keep your accounts,* the Concordat said. *You will keep our kingdom.* A fair trade, on its face. The crown-spirit fed on answered prayer—on divinity, siphoned and swallowed before it could take root—and the temple grew fat and powerful brokering the transaction. Every miracle the old gods tried to grant became a meal for the thing in the crown. Every meal kept the king alive. Every year the king lived, the temple's authority deepened like a root.

Ivenna had understood this since she was nineteen years old, kneeling in this same office, swearing herself to the Ninth Seal while the previous Ledgermistress explained the arithmetic of salvation. She had not wept. She had not prayed. She had asked, very reasonably, what happened to clerics who refused.

*They are entered,* the old woman had said. *In the red column.*

There was a red column in every ledger Ivenna kept. Most pages never touched it. Some pages lived there.

She rose and went to the window. Below, the scriptorium spread out in its long hall of desks, forty scribes bent over their copying like a field of pale flowers all turned toward the same sun. From this height they were beautiful, in the way that ordered things were beautiful. Rows. Columns. Each head bowed at the correct angle. Each producing the correct number of lines per hour, prayers copied for the faithful who would never know that most of what they received was blank paper dressed in holy language—that the true prayers, the ones with weight, the ones the old gods might actually hear, were copied only under seal, only by hand-picked scribes, only so the Flame could catch them mid-flight like moths in a jar.

That was the part novices never grasped, the part that broke the clever ones: the temple did not suppress the old gods out of piety or fear. The temple *farmed* them. A god answered a prayer; the answer passed through the world like light through glass; and somewhere between the asking and the arriving, the Flame drank it. The faithful got their candle lit, their fever broken, their rain—small answers, rationed answers, answers the crown-spirit allowed because a starving field fed no one. The surplus went up. Always up. To the Spire. To the crown. To the hollow, hungry majesty that had ruled Vaelmoor for three centuries without once aging past the face on the oldest coins.

And now something was leaking.

Ivenna turned back to her desk and drew out a slim folder tied with black cord—the folder she had been building for eleven days. Inside: an inventory of anomalies. A lamp relit itself in the hospice ward before a dying woman's scribbled prayer could be collected and burned. The woman herself had died in the subsequent purification, which was standard, but the prayer fragment had not been recovered from the ash, which was not. A wayshrine in Thornfell pass had been found glowing by persons unknown—smugglers, likely, though smugglers did not concern her except as vectors. And in the scriptorium's eastern row, where the sealed prayers were copied, attendance records showed a pattern of small irregularities clustered around two names.

Brother Ossian Fenwick. Scribe, third rank, eleven years of service, no disciplinary marks, donations exactly to the required amount every quarter—not a copper more, which was itself a kind of signature. A timid man. Careful. The careful ones were either innocent or guilty; the reckless ones were merely stupid.

Mirabel Halloran. Scribe, third rank, debt-bound, transferred liability of the Halloran family obligation at age fourteen, current balance outstanding: a sum so large it functioned as a life sentence. Copying speed exceptional. Error rate negligible. Visibility—here Ivenna paused, pen hovering—visibility anomalous. The girl had a talent for being overlooked that Ivenna had catalogued years ago and filed under *assets*, because a scribe who attracted no attention was useful for certain transports, certain deliveries, certain errands that required a person to be functionally invisible.

Assets could become liabilities. It happened constantly. It was, in fact, the entire history of the red column.

The question was which column Mirabel Halloran belonged in, and Ivenna did not yet have enough figures to enter her anywhere. So she would gather them. She was patient. Patience was simply interest accruing.

She rang the small brass bell on her desk, once.

The door opened. Brother Cadmus, her under-clerk, a gray man with gray hands and the eyes of someone who had stopped being curious about anything decades ago, stood waiting.

"Send for Brother Ossian," she said. "Tell him the quarterly audit requires his testimony regarding page-count variances in the eastern rows. Nothing alarming. Reassure him."

"Shall I reassure him truthfully, mistress?"

Ivenna almost smiled. "Reassure him convincingly. Truth is a matter for the ledger."

Cadmus bowed and withdrew. She watched him cross the accounting hall below, threading between the desks of junior clerks who tallied tithes and indulgences and the market price of absolution, and she felt the familiar cold satisfaction of machinery working as designed. People thought evil required appetite, required cruelty, required some hot and howling thing at the center. Nonsense. Evil was a well-kept ledger. Evil was knowing exactly what everything cost and being willing to pay it in other people's coin.

* * *

Brother Ossian arrived within the hour, which meant he had probably been sitting in the cloister pretending to read since dawn, waiting to be summoned, dreading it. He stood in front of her desk with his hands folded and his shoulders drawn up around his ears like a man expecting weather.

"High Ledgermistress." His voice cracked on the second word. He cleared his throat. "Mistress."

"Sit, Brother Fenwick."

He sat on the edge of the chair, spine rigid, knees together, a man occupying as little of the world as possible.

"You copied sealed prayers in the eastern rows on the ninth, eleventh, and sixteenth of last month," Ivenna said, opening the folder without hurry. "Your counts show a variance of four lines on the ninth, seven on the eleventh, and—" she let the pause stretch until he shifted "—eleven on the sixteenth. Explain the variance."

"I—mistress, the sixteenth, there was an interruption, a novice spilled ink, and I had to recopy the—"

"The novice log shows no spill on the sixteenth."

Silence. Ossian's hands had gone white where they gripped his knee. Ivenna watched him with the detached pleasure of an auditor watching a number reveal itself. He was lying badly, which meant he was lying about something that frightened him more than she did. Interesting. Very few things frightened people more than she did.

"Brother Fenwick," she said, and softened her voice by a single degree, which was more terrifying than shouting, "do you know what happens to prayers after you copy them?"

"They—they go to the sealing vault, mistress. For archival."

"And before that?"

His throat moved. "Before that they are... inert. Mistress. Ink on vellum."

"Ink on vellum," Ivenna repeated. She rose, walked to the cabinet behind her desk, and removed from it a single sheet of paper, which she laid before him. On it, in her own precise hand, was a transcription: the hospice prayer, reconstructed from the inquisitor's report. *Lady of the Cold Water, my daughter walks the roads alone and the roads are dark. If you can hear what the Flame cannot keep, keep her.* Below it she had written the date, the parish, and in the margin, in red ink: *ANSWERED — UNRECORDED — ONE (1) LAMP.*

Ossian stared at the page. His lips parted. Whatever he had expected, it was not this, and Ivenna marked the exact quality of his shock—the way his eyes went first to the words of the prayer, then to the red notation, then to her—with the care of a clerk entering figures.

"A prayer was answered in this temple's hospice," she said quietly, "and the answer never reached the crown. Do you understand what that means, Brother Fenwick? Somewhere in the chain between a dying woman's desperation and the king's table, a meal was stolen. The Flame does not forgive theft. The Flame does not forgive *leakage*. And the inquisitor assigned to trace this leakage has narrowed his inquiry to the eastern rows." She sat down again, folding her hands. "To the eastern rows, Brother Fenwick, where you have variances in your counts and a face like a man carrying stolen goods."

"Mistress, I swear by the Flame, I never—I copy what I'm given, I seal what I'm told to seal, I would never—"

"I believe you haven't stolen anything." She let him sag with relief for precisely two seconds. "I believe you may know who has."

"I don't—"

"Sister Halloran."

The name landed in the room like a dropped weight. Ossian flinched as if struck, and Ivenna watched the whole war happen behind his face: loyalty against terror, friendship against survival, the arithmetic every creature in this temple eventually performed. She gave him time. Time was free. Everything else had a price.

"She's my friend," he whispered finally. Which was, Ivenna noted, neither confirmation nor denial, and therefore both.

"Friendship is not a crime, Brother Fenwick. It is simply an expense." She opened a different ledger—one bound in green, the color of mercy, the color of money—and turned it toward him. "Here is yours. Eleven years of service. Modest debts, modest means, a widowed mother in Harrowgate whose rent is paid, I notice, by quarterly remittances from the temple almonry. A kindness extended to you at some cost to the temple. Costs accumulate, Brother. Interest accrues. And here—" she touched the next line "—is what your account becomes if the inquisitor's inquiry finds leakage in the eastern rows and traces it to a scribe who concealed knowledge of it. Concealment is complicity. Complicity enters the red column."

Ossian made a sound that was not quite a word.

"But." She turned the page. "If a loyal brother of the Flame were to observe carefully—to *report* carefully—on the conduct of Sister Halloran, her movements, her associations, her... irregularities... then his account would read differently. Service rendered. Debt relieved. His mother's rent guaranteed for life. Perhaps a posting somewhere quiet and green, far from inquisitors and red columns." She closed the ledger gently. "I am not asking you to betray anyone, Brother Fenwick. I am asking you to keep accurate records. You are a scribe. Accuracy should be your religion."

He was crying now, silently, the way timid men cried, tears without sound, as if even grief feared being noticed. Ivenna waited. She had all night. She had, in a sense that few living people understood, all the time in the world—the crown did not age, the Flame did not gutter, and the ledger never closed.

"If I refuse?" he asked at last, barely audible.

"Then tomorrow Inquisitor Merek interviews you instead of me. He is less patient than I am, and he has instruments." She paused. "And your mother's remittance ends this quarter. The almonry is reviewing its accounts. These things happen."

Ossian bowed his head. When he spoke, his voice was flat and dead, the voice of a man signing something. "What do you want me to record?"

*Everything,* she thought. *Every glance, every errand, every secret she trusts you with. Bring me the whole account, and I will balance it.*

Aloud, she said: "Her comings and goings. Her associates outside the temple. Anything she says about the sealed prayers, the old gods, the shrines. Report to me directly—never to Merek, never to anyone else. Come at the evening bell, three days hence. Tell no one, including her, especially her." She slid a small slip of paper across the desk: a receipt, blank but countersigned, worth more to a frightened man than gold. "A token of the temple's confidence. Keep it on your person."

He took it with shaking fingers, thanked her in a voice like ash, and left.

Ivenna remained at her desk a long moment, listening to his footsteps fade down the stair, and permitted herself the smallest exhale. It was done. The trap was not the slip of paper, nor the threat, nor even the promise—it was simpler and crueler than any of those. The trap was Ossian himself. Because Mirabel Halloran was, by every report, a careful woman, and careful women trusted exactly one kind of person: the harmless ones. The timid ones. The men who flinched and stammered and occupied as little of the world as possible.

She would tell Ossian everything. And Ossian, poor gray mouse, would carry it all to Ivenna's desk in neat little bundles of guilt, and never understand until the end that he had been chosen for this role precisely because he loved her.

Betrayal was not a passion. Betrayal was bookkeeping. You identified the asset closest to the liability, and you debited it.

* * *

That evening, alone, Ivenna unlocked the bottom drawer of her cabinet and took out the object she consulted perhaps twice a year: a sheet of black vellum, older than the temple, older than the Concordat, given to the first High Ledgermistress by the thing in the crown as a token of their arrangement. No ink wrote on it. Instead, when she pressed her palm to its surface and spoke the Ninth Seal's name, figures surfaced in it like fish rising in dark water—the crown's own accounting, the great ledger of the kingdom's soul.

She pressed her hand flat and whispered.

The vellum shivered. Numbers rose: the total miracles devoured this season, the crown's current hunger measured in souls' worth of divinity, the projected shortfall before the thirteenth moon. The shortfall was growing. The old gods were waking faster than the Flame could drink them—shrines lighting across Thornfell, prayers slipping the net, whole villages whispering to forgotten names in the dark. The crown was hungry, and hunger made it impatient, and impatience made it careless, and careless crowns historically ended badly. There had been a king before the first king. The black vellum remembered him. The black vellum remembered what the first king had paid to bind the thing that wore his face, and Ivenna had read those figures often enough to know that the payment had not ended. It was ongoing. It was *her*.

Below the totals, one final figure surfaced, unbidden, in letters that glowed faintly red:

*ANOMALY — SCRIPTORIUM EAST — ONE (1) SCRIBE — DEBT-MARKED — WATCH.*

The crown had noticed Mirabel Halloran too.

Ivenna sat back, and for the first time that day, something like genuine feeling crossed her face—not fear, nothing so vulgar, but the sharp alertness of an auditor who realizes the discrepancy is larger than the column allows. A debt-marked scribe. Marked skin, marked soul, already half-written in binding-ink. If the crown wanted her watched, then the girl was not merely a leak. She was potentially a vessel, or a key, or a fuse—and objects of that category appeared in ledgers only twice: once as assets, and once, afterward, in the red column.

Three days, then. Let Ossian bring her his bundles of guilt. Let Mirabel Halloran run her little errands and light her little fires and believe, as the invisible always believed, that no one was counting.

Everyone was counting. Counting was the whole of the law.

Ivenna returned the black vellum to its drawer, snuffed her candles one by one, and stood a moment in the dark above the sleeping accounting hall, listening to the Flame breathe in its vault below—warm, patient, hungry.

"Balance," she said softly, to the darkness, to the crown, to the ledger that never closed, "is coming."

Far away in the capital, in a gilded room where a king sat motionless on a throne too large for his shrinking body, something ancient turned its attention north, toward Thornfell, toward a temple, toward a girl with ink in her skin—and began, with terrible diligence, to count.
