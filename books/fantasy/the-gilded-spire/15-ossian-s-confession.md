## 15. Ossian's Confession

The candle in Brother Ossian's cell had burned down to its last finger's width, and still he could not make the ink dry on the page before him.

Not because the ink was slow. Because his hand would not stop shaking.

*Subject: Scribe Mirabel Halloran. Third report of the moon.*

He had written her name eleven times now and scraped it out eleven times, and the vellum was wearing thin as skin in the corner where the letters kept trying to be born. Inquisitor Dain Merek had given him until the morning bell. Merek had given it kindly, which was worse than giving it cruelly. Cruelty you could hate. Kindness sat in your chest like a swallowed stone.

*"You are not betraying her, Brother Ossian,"* Merek had said that afternoon in the interrogation room that smelled of tallow and old smoke, folding his pale hands on the table between them as though they were two merchants agreeing on a price. *"You are saving her. The Ledgermistress has already marked her. If her file arrives at the Flame incomplete—if it arrives with a friend's testimony attached, a friend who noticed nothing amiss—she will simply be reassigned. Quietly. She will copy prayers in some coastal scriptorium until she dies of salt air and boredom. That is the gentlest ending available to her, and you are the only one who can write it."*

And Ossian, who had spent thirty-one years being the sort of man no one ever needed to lie to, had looked down at his own hands—ink-stained, careful, harmless hands—and heard himself say, *I'll need time to observe her properly. A report built too fast looks like fabrication.*

Merek had smiled at that. Had actually smiled, warm as a hearth.

"Good," he'd said. "A forger's instinct. I knew you had one in you."

That was four days ago. The morning bell was now less than an hour away, and Ossian had finally understood, in the slow way that timid men understand things—completely, and far too late—that there was no version of this report that saved anyone. He had read the files Merek let him see. He had seen what happened to scribes whose files were *complete*. The reassigned ones did not die of salt air. They died in the undercroft, or they did not die at all, which was somehow worse, and their names were struck from the rolls so cleanly that even the memory of having known them felt like a sin to confess.

Mirabel Halloran had covered for him once. Three years ago, when he had mis-copied a line of the Litany of Accounts—a single transposed figure, *nine hundred ninety-one* where the original said *nine hundred nineteen*—and the audit bell had rung for him, and he had stood before High Ledgermistress Ivenna trembling like a wet cat while his whole life narrowed to a point. And Mirabel, who barely spoke to him, who owed him nothing, had stepped forward and said, "Begging pardon, Ledgermistress, but Brother Ossian copied from my draft, and the error is mine." She had taken the correction mark herself. Taken the debt-penalty against her own already-crushing account, a penalty of six months' labor, so that a stranger would not be flogged with shame.

He had never asked her why. He had been too much of a coward to even thank her properly. He had only watched her after that—the way she moved through the scriptorium like water moving through cracks, unnoticed, unnoticeable, always alone—and understood that she had done it precisely because she knew how the machine worked, knew exactly what a small kindness cost and exactly how little it bought, and had paid anyway.

Tonight he was going to pay her back. Not with a report. With everything else he had.

Ossian set down the pen. Then, with the deliberateness of a man defusing something, he blotted Mirabel's twelfth name into oblivion, ground sand over the whole ruined page, folded it small, and fed it to the candle flame. It went up green-ish, the way treated vellum does, and he watched every curl of it die.

Then he took out a fresh sheet, and wrote, in his small, precise, unremarkable hand—the hand of a man who had never once been suspected of anything, which was the only weapon he owned:

*Mirabel —*

*They know about the answered prayer. They have traced it to your desk by the residue in the ink-well; Merek showed me the assay. They believe you kept a fragment. They are right, aren't they? You must burn it tonight, whatever it is, wherever you've hidden it. The archive will be sealed and searched at first light—I heard Merek instruct the under-wardens myself. There is a fire scheduled. I don't know all of it. I know enough to know that you cannot be found inside it or near it.*

*I am going to give them a report naming me instead of you. By the time they understand it is false, you should be beyond the walls. Go north. Don't go to anyone you love—they will use them. Don't go anywhere you've been twice.*

*You covered for me once and never asked why. I'm not asking either. Some debts aren't meant to be collected. They're meant to be paid forward.*

*Burn this.*

*— O.*

He sanded it, folded it into a tight square the size of a communion wafer, and pressed his thumbprint in wax on the fold—not any official seal, just his own print, the one mark of himself he had ever made on the world.

Getting it to her was the impossible part. The dormitory corridors were patrolled on quarter-bells since the inquiry began; Mirabel slept in the junior scribes' wing, three floors up, behind a door Merek had warded with an assay-charm that tasted the air for foreign ink. Ossian could no more reach her room tonight than he could fly off the belfry.

But Mirabel Halloran was never in her room at night. Everyone knew that, and no one knew it, because noticing things about Mirabel was not a habit anyone had formed. She was the gray sister of the scriptorium, the one whose name you forgot mid-sentence. She took the night-copy shifts no one wanted. She walked the cloister at odd hours "clearing her head."

So Ossian did the bravest and simplest thing of his entire gray life: he went to the scriptorium itself, at the dead hour past midnight, and sat down at her desk, and waited for her to come collect her pens.

* * *

She came at the second hour, silent as spilling water, and stopped dead in the doorway when she saw the candle burning at her own station.

"Brother Ossian." Her voice was flat, watchful. He had never heard her use her real voice indoors before. Up close, in bad light, he saw for the first time how tired she was—not the ordinary tiredness of scribes but the deep-set weariness of someone carrying weight in places no one could assay. Her left cuff was buttoned wrong. Under it, faintly visible when she moved, the skin of her wrist glowed a dull ember-orange, like a coal wrapped in linen. He didn't ask. He was suddenly, horribly sure he didn't want to know yet.

"You shouldn't be here," she said.

"No," he agreed. "Neither should you. That's rather the problem." He slid the folded square across her desk. "For you. Read it and burn it, in that order, quickly."

She didn't touch it. "What is it?"

"A confession. Two, if you count the one I'm about to make out loud." His voice cracked and he hated it and kept going. "Inquisitor Merek came to me four days ago. He wants reports on you. Weekly. Detailed. He gave me a choice—write them, or join the file myself." Ossian laughed, a small broken sound. "As if those were different outcomes. As if cooperation ever saved anyone in this building. I've read the archives, Mirabel. I copy the ledgers of the condemned. Do you know what happens to the cooperative ones? Their entries end with the word *grateful.*"

Her eyes flicked to the paper, then back to his face, and something shifted in them—wariness turning to a kind of grim arithmetic. He watched her calculate him: threat, fool, trap? He stood very still and let her look, because he had decided that if this was the last honest act of his life, it would be fully honest.

"Why tell me?" she said at last. "You could write the report. Name me. Save yourself. Merek rewards the compliant—I've seen his promotion lists."

"Yes," Ossian said. "And then I would spend the rest of my life copying prayers to gods we starve, knowing exactly what I sold and exactly what it bought, and the ink would know it too. Ink knows, Mirabel. Anyone who's spent thirty years copying holy text knows that. Every lie I ever wrote sits differently on the page than truth. The letters lean." He managed something that was almost a smile. "I refuse to spend eternity leaning."

She picked up the note then. Read it fast, the way smugglers presumably read contracts—with her fingers as much as her eyes. When she reached the part about the fire, her face did something complicated and settled into decision, the way ice settles before it becomes water.

"The archive," she said quietly. "It's tonight, isn't it. That's why Merek moved the search up."

"I believe so, yes."

"And you're going to walk into the inquisitors' net and tell them the answered-prayer trace leads to you."

"I'm going to tell them," Ossian said, "that I have been conducting an unsanctioned study of pre-Ledger liturgical forms, that I accessed restricted prayers using your access-seal because yours is senior to mine, and that any residue in your ink-well is therefore mine. It has the advantage of being nearly true. I *have* been studying forbidden texts for years—quietly, at night, out of—" he faltered, then finished, "—out of hunger. For something true. I suspect you know the appetite."

For a moment neither of them spoke. Somewhere above them the temple bells turned the half-hour, bronze and indifferent, counting out the kingdom's accounts.

"Ossian," Mirabel said, and her voice had changed—lower, rougher, the voice of the woman under the gray sister, the woman who guided caravans through mountains in the dark. "They will take you to the undercroft. You understand that. Whatever you've imagined—they will go past questions."

"I have imagined it," he said. "Every night since Merek smiled at me. I find that imagination, like ink, behaves better if you face it directly." He straightened his robe with fussy precision, one last small vanity. "I am a coward, Mirabel Halloran. I have been one for thirty-one years, and I know the animal thoroughly—it flinches from pain, but it flinches worse from becoming the thing it fears. Let me be brave for one hour. It's the only hour I'll get, and I'd rather spend it well than long."

Something glistened in her eyes and was gone before he could name it.

"There's a drainage grate behind the archive's east stack," she said abruptly, all business now, and he realized with a strange joy that she was planning, that she had accepted him as an accomplice and was spending skill on his behalf. "Old pilgrim tunnel, pre-Ledger. It runs under the ash-chute. If the fire goes the way these fires go, the wardens vent the heat east—the grate will be hot but passable. I need you to delay the sealing of the archive by a quarter-bell. Trip the alarm in the west transept. Anything."

"I can do anything," Ossian said, "except be important. Fortunately, unimportant is exactly what the job requires."

The ghost of a smile crossed her face—the first he'd ever seen on her. "Brother Ossian. When this is over—"

"Don't finish that sentence," he said gently. "Sentences like that are how they catch people. Just go. And Mirabel—" he hesitated, then said the thing he had carried for three years, cheap and late and necessary, "—thank you. For the nine hundred nineteen."

She stared at him. "You knew?"

"Ink knows," he said. "Go."

* * *

The west transept alarm went off at the third hour, a small fire in a devotional candle-rack, quickly smothered, loudly investigated. Ossian stood among the responding wardens with his heart hammering and his face arranged into anxious harmlessness, and counted the quarter-bells as the archive's sealing was delayed, delayed, delayed.

At the fourth hour, Inquisitor Merek found him in the scriptorium, sitting calmly at his own desk, the false confession laid out squared to the desk's edge like a prepared tax roll.

"Brother Ossian." Merek's smile was gone. In its place was the other face, the assay-face, pale and patient. "Scribe Halloran's station shows fresh disturbance. Candle wax that isn't hers. Your thumbprint wax on a burned document fragment recovered from her grate-ash. You wouldn't know anything about that."

"I would, actually." Ossian rose and offered the confession with both hands, the way one offers a relic. "Everything is in there. I ask only that you read it fully before deciding what I am."

Merek read. Ossian watched him read, and discovered that the terror he had rehearsed for four nights did not arrive; in its place was a strange, floating clarity, like the moment after a fever breaks. *This is what courage feels like,* he thought. *It feels like nothing. Like clean paper.*

"This names you," Merek said slowly.

"It is accurate," Ossian said. "Check every claim. You'll find I've anticipated your checks. I've had thirty-one years of practice anticipating what powerful men want to hear." He folded his hands. "The scribe Halloran is guilty of nothing but diligence. I used her seal. Arrest me."

Merek studied him for a long moment with those colorless eyes, and Ossian saw—saw with perfect terrible lucidity—the exact instant the inquisitor decided not to believe him, and the further instant where he decided to pretend to believe him anyway, because a confession in hand was worth more than a suspicion without one, and because somewhere in the Spire's great accounts, a quiet middle-aged scribe was an acceptable rounding error.

"Bind him," Merek said to the wardens. "Gently. He's cooperative." And to Ossian, almost tenderly: "We'll want to discuss your study of pre-Ledger texts at length, Brother. There's a room below that's very good for discussion."

"I imagine there is," said Ossian.

As they took his arms, he thought of Mirabel moving through the dark behind the archive stacks, quick as water, carrying her ember-lit wrist and her stolen truths toward the north road, and he thought: *paid forward,* and the undercroft stairs, when they came, seemed almost like a descent into sleep.

* * *

Mirabel smelled the smoke before she reached the east stacks.

The archive fire was supposed to start at dawn—a controlled burning, wardens present, the old-god prayers fed to the Ledgered Flame's purifying hearth in the sanctioned manner. But someone had jumped the schedule. Someone with keys and fear, or orders, or both. The east range of the archive was already breathing smoke through the shelf-gaps, and the wardens who should have been sealing doors were hauling buckets with the frantic incompetence of men doing a job they hadn't rehearsed.

Which meant the vault of restricted prayers was open.

Which meant, she realized, heart slamming, that Ossian's diversion had worked too well—the west transept alarm had pulled wardens from the archive before the sealing, and now the fire was loose in a building full of the only true things the temple owned, and she had perhaps a hundred breaths.

She could run. Every survival instinct she possessed, honed across ten years of double life, screamed *run*—north, now, alone, before the smoke became search-parties. Pip was waiting in the culvert with the bundle of food she'd left, a small god-touched shape humming in the dark, trusting her.

Instead her feet took her into the smoke.

Later she would tell herself it was strategy: the pages in the restricted vault were the map to everything—the crown, the thirteenth moon, the debt written in her skin. But in the moment it wasn't strategy. In the moment it was Ossian's voice saying *some debts aren't meant to be collected*, and a lifetime of watching good pages burn because powerful men feared what was written on them.

The restricted vault's iron door hung ajar, heat shimmering off it. Inside, shelves of vellum and bark-paper curled at their edges, and the old prayers—prayers to Vael the Road-Mother, to Hessa of the Nine Springs, to the nameless god of the thirteenth moon—lay in their thousands like sleeping birds.

Mirabel stuffed her satchel with both hands, tearing pages free in fistfuls, choosing by instinct, by the faint warmth some of them gave off against her burned palm. The glow under her cuff had become a blaze now, answering the fire, answering the prayers, and she could hear—faintly, terribly—something in the smoke that might have been singing, or might have been the roof timbers.

A beam groaned overhead. The doorway behind her filled with orange light.

She ran, satchel heavy against her spine, out through the ash-chute corridor, down to the drainage grate, hauling it open with scorched fingers as the heat breathed over her shoulders like a living thing. Behind her the archive roared; ahead, the pilgrim tunnel ran cold and black toward the river and the north road and the small humming shape of a child who believed she was coming.

Pip looked up when she dropped into the culvert, ash-gray and singed and shaking, and said simply: "You went back for the words."

"Somebody had to," Mirabel managed. "They keep burning them."

Pip considered this with the grave solemnity of the very young and the very strange, then reached up and took her burned hand, and where the child's fingers touched, the pain went quiet—not healed, but *listened to*, the way a storm listens to a lullaby.

"They'll know it was you," Pip said. It wasn't a question. Children like Pip didn't ask questions they could smell the answers to.

"Yes," Mirabel said. Behind them, above them, the temple of the Ledgered Flame was burning its own memory, and somewhere beneath it a quiet man was paying her debt with his body, and somewhere in the smoke and the shouting, Inquisitor Dain Merek was already assembling the hunt. "They'll know it was me. So we'd better be fast, and we'd better be gone."

She shouldered the satchel of stolen gods, took Pip's hand, and ran north into the dark, while behind them the sky over the temple turned the color of a page consuming itself.
