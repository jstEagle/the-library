## 16. The Smuggler's Oath

The vote was called at dusk, the way Sefa Corrane's mother had called every hard vote in her day: with a knife laid flat on a board and everyone's hands where they could be seen.

She'd chosen the shrine clearing for it, which some of her crew thought was sentiment and others thought was strategy, and Sefa—being honest with herself in the way she permitted herself exactly once a day, usually over the first cup of bitter pine tea—knew it was neither. It was because the wayshrine was still glowing. Faintly, like a banked coal under ash, that pale gold light that had no business surviving three hundred years of neglect. And she had learned something about her people in the weeks since Pip touched the stone and heard singing: they didn't argue well near it. The light made them quieter. More honest. It was very hard to lie standing in old-god territory, and Sefa Corrane had built her whole life on knowing when people were lying.

So: dusk, the shrine, the knife on the board.

Twelve of them sat in a rough ring on fallen logs and overturned supply crates. Twelve, counting her. Not counting the four who weren't crew—not yet, not ever, depending on how the stones fell tonight. Those four stood apart near the fire pit, close enough to hear everything, which was the point. Mirabel Halloran stood with her arms folded and her face arranged into the particular blankness Sefa had come to think of as her ledger-face: nothing owed, nothing owed to her, accounts closed. Aldric Vane sat on a stone like a man sitting on a stone, which after three weeks of practice he'd almost gotten good at. The knight still moved like a drawn bow. Pip sat between Mirabel and the fire, legs swinging, watching the vote with the enormous patience of children who have learned that grown-up ceremonies are mostly noise.

And Ossian—no. Ossian wasn't here. Ossian was back in Vaelmoor City, in a cell or a grave or worse, and Sefa caught Mirabel looking at the empty space where a thirteenth person should have been, and looked away first.

"Right," Sefa said. She kept her voice level, the voice she used for negotiating tolls with bandit captains who outweighed her by half again. "You all know why we're here. I'll not dress it up. We've got four mouths that aren't ours. One of them is a scribe the Temple of the Ledgered Flame wants badly enough to burn an archive over. One of them is a knight-commander who walked out of the capital with sealed papers in his coat. One of them is a child who glows when the moon's right and smells like—" she gestured vaguely, "—like rain on hot stone, if you're an inquisitor. And one of them is a mule."

"The mule stays," said Brannick immediately. Brannick loved the mule.

"The mule was never in question." Sefa turned the knife a quarter-turn on the board, an old signal, meaning *speak now or hold your peace forever*. "Here's what sheltering them costs. Merek has our descriptions. He has the boy's description best of all, and he's got temple writ behind him now, real authority, not just his own spite. Every checkpoint from here to the Spire road will have his name on it next to ours. Any one of us taken can buy their own neck by selling the rest. That's the arithmetic. I won't pretend it's kinder than it is."

Silence. The shrine hummed its low note, the one you felt in your teeth more than your ears.

"Say the other part," said Wren. Wren was her rope-and-rigging woman, sixty years old, missing two fingers to a bad knot in her youth, and the only member of the crew who'd been with Sefa longer than Sefa had been captain. "Say what happens if we turn them out."

Sefa looked at her. Wren looked back, unblinking, and Sefa understood that this was the trap her oldest friend had laid for her, gently, in front of everyone. Because Sefa had already voted, hadn't she? By bringing them here. By feeding them. By letting Pip sleep in the wagon with the good blankets. The vote was a formality, and Wren knew it, and Wren wanted it said aloud anyway so nobody could later claim they hadn't known what turning them out meant.

"Fine," Sefa said. "If we turn them out, the inquisitors take the scribe and the knight within a week—the roads are crawling, there's nowhere for folk like them to run. They take the boy inside of two days, because a god-touched child alone is a lantern in a dark field. And then Merek works us over one by one anyway, because we fed them, we carried them, we're in his book regardless. Turning them out doesn't save us. It just means we hang for less."

"That's cheerful," muttered Doss.

"It's true, which is better." She let that sit. "Now the other side says their piece. Tam?"

Tam was twenty, quick with a blade and quicker with an opinion, and he'd been against taking fugitives since the night Mirabel first appeared out of the Thornfell passes with a knight in tow. He stood, and Sefa gave him credit for standing; most men argued harder sitting down.

"My brother's in the Flame's service," Tam said. "Third bell-ringer at the Harrowgate temple. If we're marked as god-shelterers, they don't just take us. They audit him. They audit everyone we've ever drunk with. You know how it works—you've seen it done to the Ferro family, and the Ostmarks, and half the riverfolk last spring. It's not our fight. The king's crown, the gods waking up, whatever's eating prayers—I didn't sign on for any of it. I signed on to move cargo and stay breathing." He sat down. "That's my piece. No malice in it. Just arithmetic."

"Arithmetic," repeated a voice from the fire, dry as old paper. Everyone looked at Mirabel. She unfolded her arms. "Forgive me. Occupational habit. But if we're doing sums, do them honestly. Your brother gets audited either way, Tam. Inquisitor Merek traced an answered prayer to my scriptorium desk within nine days. Nine. Do you imagine he hasn't already pulled the caravan manifests out of Thornfell? Your name is in his book because you hauled me over the pass in month one. Sheltering me doesn't add a crime. It only adds a reason."

"You don't know that."

"I copy ledgers for a living," Mirabel said. "I know exactly how a man like Merek keeps books. Everything gets entered. Nothing gets forgiven. The only question anyone in this clearing gets to answer tonight is whether their page says *accomplice* or *witness*." She paused, and something moved behind her eyes that Sefa couldn't read—grief, maybe, or guilt, the look of a woman carrying a debt that wasn't on any ledger. "I'd rather none of you paid anything for me. That's also true. But I can't unsign what's written."

"Gods' teeth," Doss said into the silence, "the scribe argues like a toll-keeper."

"She *is* a toll-keeper," Sefa said. "We've established this. All right. Hands."

* * *

They voted the old way. Knife flat on the board, and each hand placed palm-down beside it for yes, withdrawn for no. No names spoken aloud—names could be tortured out of a man, but a memory of hands around a fire was softer evidence, and soft evidence kept crews alive.

Brannick: yes, instantly, before Sefa finished asking, because Brannick had adopted Pip somewhere around the second week and had stopped pretending otherwise. Wren: yes, slow and deliberate, pressing her ruined hand flat like a seal on wax. Doss: yes, with a curse, "but if the mule suffers one indignity—" Yes, Doss, the mule is fine. Little Cat, their scout: yes, and Sefa noted she didn't hesitate, though Little Cat hesitated over everything, including breakfast. The Marlow twins: one yes, one no, which was somehow the most Marlow thing possible, and they glared at each other across the fire while everyone pretended not to watch. Old Hetty, their cook, who had buried a husband and two sons to temple audits in the Ferro purges: yes, and her hand came down on the board hard enough to make the knife jump, and she said nothing at all, and the nothing was louder than Tam's whole speech.

Then Tam. His hand hovered. Sefa watched him look at Pip—at the boy, who was watching him back with those too-old eyes, no fear in them, just a kind of terrible patience—and Sefa saw the exact moment Tam understood that a child would remember, forever, which hand withdrew.

His palm hit the board. "Against my better judgment," he said. "Yes."

Last, Rennick, the twins' cousin, quiet Rennick who never voted first and rarely voted loudly. He looked at his hand for a long moment. Then he withdrew it entirely and folded his arms.

"No," he said.

The clearing went still. Eleven to one. Close enough, by the old reckoning—a crew needed unanimity for exile, but only a majority for shelter—but a no was a no, and it hung in the air like smoke.

"Rennick," Sefa started.

"Captain." He said it respectfully. That was the worst of it; there was no anger in the man, only fear wearing respect's clothing. "I've got a daughter in Lowmark. Her mother's family are chapel-folk, proper ones, tithing ones. If my name comes up in a god-sheltering crew, they don't just lose me. The girl loses her whole house. I'll carry cargo, I'll stand a watch, I'll even swing a blade if it comes to that. But I'm not swearing an oath to it. An oath is different. An oath follows you."

Sefa nodded slowly. She'd expected two noes, maybe three. One was a mercy, and she knew it, and she hated that she knew it.

"Fair spoken," she said, and meant it. "No one swears unwilling in my crew. That's law older than me."

And then Pip stood up.

* * *

He'd been so quiet through the whole vote that Sefa had half forgotten him, which was a mistake she suspected everyone in that clearing had made and would go on making until the day the boy did something none of them could explain away. He stood by the fire, small and thin, seven years old or eight or possibly ninety, and he looked at Rennick without a scrap of judgment, which somehow made it heavier.

"He's right," Pip said. "About oaths."

"Pip," Mirabel began softly.

"No, listen." The boy's voice had that quality it got sometimes, that resonance, like a wet finger on a glass rim, like the shrine itself had borrowed his throat to speak through. The glow under his skin brightened, faint gold, and the wayshrine behind them answered—it always answered him—humming up from its banked coal-smolder to a clear, steady light. Every face in the clearing turned toward it. "Oaths follow you. That's what they're *for*. My gran said—" he stopped, and Sefa saw him reach for a memory and find it, and hold onto it with both hands. "My gran said an oath is a door you lock from the inside. Nobody can make you swear one. But once it's sworn, nobody—including you—gets to pretend it isn't there."

He looked around the ring. "I'm not asking anybody to swear to me. I'm asking you to swear to each other. There's a difference. The gods can tell the difference." A pause, and then, with the devastating simplicity of childhood: "So can I."

Nobody spoke. The shrine sang its single note. Somewhere up-slope a nightbird called and got no answer.

It was Wren, of course, who broke it. Wren, who had been a road-woman before Sefa was born, back when the old laws were laws instead of stories. She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and faced the company.

"There's a road-law," she said. "Older than the Ledgered Flame, older than the kingdom, older than the gilding on that spire they're so proud of. My grandmother swore it and hers before her, back when the shrines were lit on every mile-stone and a traveler was a traveler no matter whose colors they wore." She held up her maimed hand, the two stumped fingers, like a relic. "*No one is turned away from a lit fire.* That's the whole of it. No fine print. No exceptions for kings or inquisitors or frightened men with daughters in Lowmark. You light a fire on the open road, you've made a promise to every soul walking under the sky. Break it, and the road remembers. The road always remembers."

"The road," Sefa said quietly, "is awake now. We've all seen it."

"We've all seen it," Wren agreed. "Which means the old law isn't a story anymore. It's a term of contract. And I say we swear it properly, here, at a lit shrine, with witnesses that count." She nodded at the wayshrine, at Pip, at the dark watching them from between the trees. "All in favor of the oath, stand."

Eleven stood. Rennick stayed seated, head bowed, and no one looked at him with contempt, because contempt was cheap and these were smugglers, who understood better than any priest alive that fear was not a sin but a tax, and everyone paid it differently.

Mirabel stood last. Sefa watched her do it—watched the scribe weigh it, visibly, the way she weighed everything, this woman who had spent her whole life being invisible and was now, page by page, choosing to be seen. When Mirabel finally rose, she rose like a woman signing something she couldn't unread.

"Then it's sworn," Sefa said. She picked up the knife.

* * *

Her mother had taught her the words, and her mother had learned them from a woman who'd walked the roads before the Flame ever put a bell in a tower, and the words were plain because old things are always plain—that was how you knew they were old. New oaths come dressed up. Old ones come naked.

Sefa pricked her thumb on the point, one bead of blood, and pressed it to the flat of the blade.

"By fire lit and road remembered," she said, "no one under my watch is turned away. Not from bread, not from blanket, not from blade's protection. What the fire shelters, I shelter. What the road carries, I carry. Sworn at a waking stone, under an honest moon, with these my witnesses." She turned the blade outward, handle first, to the company.

They came one at a time. Brannick, grinning, blood and all. Wren, solemn as a funeral. Doss muttering about the mule's rights. The twins, both of them now, the dissenting twin caving with theatrical disgust while his brother crowed. Little Cat, fast and silent. Old Hetty, who kissed the blade before she bled on it, which nobody had done in Sefa's lifetime, and which made the hair stand up on Sefa's arms. Tam, jaw set, doing the brave thing he'd already decided to do. Then Aldric Vane, the disgraced knight, who knelt—actually knelt, the dramatic fool—took the knife in both hands like a chalice, and swore the words in a voice that cracked on *road remembered*, and Sefa pretended not to notice because knights deserved that much dignity.

Then Mirabel Halloran. She took the knife, and before she cut, she looked at Sefa across the fire, and said, quietly, so only the captain heard: "You know what this makes you, legally speaking. Under temple law, an oath-sworn confederacy harboring a marked vessel is—"

"Scribe." Sefa held her eyes. "I've been a criminal since I was fourteen. Tonight I'm just becoming a *loyal* one. Cut your thumb."

Mirabel almost smiled. Almost. It was the closest thing to a smile Sefa had gotten out of her in three weeks, and she counted it a victory worth more than the vote. Blood on steel. Words said. Done.

Pip went last, of course. He refused the knife—"I don't bleed on purpose, it makes the stones excited"—and instead pressed his small palm flat against the wayshrine itself, and the light flared, gold flooding the clearing like sunrise arriving twelve hours early, and the boy said the words in a language none of them knew and all of them understood, and every tree on the hillside seemed to lean in.

When the light faded, Rennick was standing.

He hadn't sworn. He stood there with his fists at his sides, looking wretched, looking young, looking like a man standing at a door he couldn't quite bring himself to lock from the inside. And Sefa—because she was captain, and because her mother had raised her right despite everything—walked over to him and put a hand on his shoulder.

"You eat at my fire tomorrow," she said. "Oath or no oath. That one's mine, and it doesn't need a vote."

Rennick's throat worked. "Captain, I—"

"Sleep on it. The road's patient. Turns out the gods are too." She squeezed his shoulder once and left him to it, because a man pushed into an oath was a man holding a knife at your back, and a man left standing in the cold might yet walk to the fire on his own feet.

Behind her, the crew was already moving—Hetty hauling the big kettle out, Brannick showing Pip how to skewer a dough-bread on a green stick, Wren and the twins arguing amiably about watch rotations, Aldric standing a little straighter than he had in weeks, Mirabel sitting down beside the boy with her arm around him like she'd been doing it all her life. Like a family, Sefa thought. Chosen, not given. The only kind that holds.

She was lifting the kettle off Hetty when Little Cat dropped out of the dark at the clearing's edge, silent as falling snow, and the look on the scout's face killed the celebration before a word was spoken.

"Road-watch," Little Cat breathed. "South approach, two miles out. Lantern signals—temple pattern, military spacing." A pause. "And captain—they're not coming up the road. They're coming up the *old* road. The one that shouldn't exist."

The wayshrine hummed. Around its base, Sefa noticed, the ancient carvings had begun, very faintly, to glow.

"They know we're here," Pip said, into the sudden silence. He wasn't afraid. That was the worst part. He sounded almost sorry for them. "They've known since we swore."
