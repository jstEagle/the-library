## 17. Mooncount Nine

The safehouse was a smuggler's idea of a temple: a collapsed watchtower in the shoulder of Thornfell, its upper floors open to the sky, its lower rooms dry and dark and smelling of tallow, wet wool, and Sefa's terrible pipe-weed. Rain had been coming down for two days, drumming on the broken vaulting above them like a clerk's fingers counting out hours. Mirabel counted along with it without meaning to. She had spent eleven years of her life counting things she did not want to count.

Four moons left. She had written the number three times in the margin of her thoughts before she caught herself doing it and made herself stop.

* * *

They gathered around the fire in the tower's ground room because there was nowhere else to gather, and because Pip had asked—asked with that particular gravity the child sometimes wore, like a coat too heavy for such a small frame—if they could look at the pages again tonight. The stolen pages. Three sheets of vellum pulled from a burning archive while the skin of Mirabel's right hand cooked beneath them, and which now glowed faintly at night from under her wrappings, a soft amber pulse she kept hidden inside her sleeve the way other people hid knives.

Aldric sat with his back to the wall, as he always did, his ruined knight's cloak around his shoulders and his sword across his knees even though no one had threatened them in nine days. He read the way he did everything: slowly, completely, as if the words might change if he blinked. Sefa sprawled opposite him, boots up on an old barrel, turning her knife over and over in her fingers—a habit that meant she was bored, or worried, or both. Two of her crew played dice in the corner with the studied loudness of people pretending not to listen. Pip sat closest to the fire, cross-legged, chin on fists, watching the pages the way other children watched festival lanterns.

Mirabel spread the three sheets flat on a board across her knees and let the firelight do its work.

The first page was liturgy—the old kind, pre-Ledger, the kind the temple called *unaccounted speech* and burned on principle. A call-and-response for something called the Turning of the Year's Last Door. The second was worse: a fragment of doctrine, half-eaten by fire, describing what the first kings had done "in the age of the hollow crown." The third page was the reason none of them had slept properly in a week.

It was instructions.

*The binding shall be loosed only where it was wrought: at the Spire, upon the seat of the first king, when the thirteenth moon completes its turning.*

*It cannot be killed. It can be carried.*

*Let a vessel be found who takes it willingly, for unwilling flesh will not hold it, and the chain must be forged of consent or it shatters and the thing goes free.*

*The vessel's life is the length of the chain. While the vessel lives, the spirit is bound. When the vessel dies—*

The rest of the line was gone. Burned away in the archive fire, a ragged brown edge where the sentence should have ended. They had argued about that missing ending for two nights. Aldric thought it meant the binding broke on the vessel's death. Sefa thought it meant the vessel simply died with it, clean, like snuffing a candle. Pip, who heard things in old stone that no one else could hear, had said nothing at all, only looked at Mirabel for a long moment with those too-old eyes, and then looked away.

Mirabel had not offered an opinion. She was a scribe; she knew better than anyone how much weight hung on a single missing word.

"Read it again," Aldric said. Not a request. He'd been saying it every night, and every night she obeyed, because repetition was how you caught lies—in texts as in people.

"*The binding shall be loosed only where it was wrought,*" she read, keeping her voice level, clerical, the voice she used for copying prayers she didn't believe in. "*At the Spire, upon the seat of the first king, when the thirteenth moon completes its turning.*"

"Which is four moons off," Sefa said, around her pipe stem. "Mooncount thirteen. We've got—" she counted on scarred fingers, "—nine days into this moon, then three more moons after. Say a hundred and ten days, if the roads behave."

"The roads won't behave," Aldric said. "Merek knows we're in Thornfell. And the Spire sits in the middle of the most garrisoned city in Vaelmoor."

"So we're going to walk into the heart of the kingdom, past the temple that wants me flayed, past the inquisitor who wants all of us in chains, and up the Spire itself," Sefa said, "to unbind a god-parasite off the king's head. Wonderful. I've run worse cargo."

"You have not," said one of the dice-players, without looking up.

"I have not," Sefa conceded, "but I've been paid worse."

Pip laughed, a small bright sound, and the tension in the room loosened by exactly that much. Mirabel turned the third page over, though she knew perfectly well what was on the back—nothing, just water-stains and the ghost of an older script bleeding through—and used the motion to buy herself three seconds.

*Let a vessel be found who takes it willingly.*

She had known what it meant since the second night. She suspected Aldric had too, because Aldric noticed everything and had stopped meeting her eyes during the readings three nights ago. But knowing a thing and saying a thing were different economies, and Mirabel Halloran had survived her whole life by understanding which debts were spoken aloud and which were carried in silence.

*Unwilling flesh will not hold it.*

That was the trap in the text, wasn't it? That was the elegant, temple-shaped cruelty of it. You couldn't force someone. You couldn't order a soldier to do it, couldn't condemn a prisoner to it, couldn't sacrifice some poor conscript on an altar of necessity. It had to be given. Freely. Which meant that somewhere in this room, in this ragged company of liars and fugitives and children, someone had to *choose*—

"—and that's why the tax ledgers matter," Aldric was saying, and Mirabel realized she had missed half a minute of the conversation. "If we go in as pilgrims they'll search us twice. As merchants, once. As a tax caravan under temple seal, they'll wave us through and thank us for our service."

"You've thought about this," Sefa said.

"I've had a hundred and ten days' worth of reasons to think about it."

"No," Sefa said. "You've had nine days. Same as us. You've just been thinking longer each day." She knocked out her pipe against the hearthstone and stood, joints cracking. "Enough. Pages aren't going anywhere. Mooncount doesn't move faster because we stare at it. Pip, bed. Everyone else, watches as posted."

The crew shuffled toward their bedrolls. Aldric gathered his sword and paused beside Mirabel's chair, and for a moment she thought—feared—he was going to say it, right there, say *the vessel, Mirabel, someone has to be the vessel*, and she would have to stand in front of all of them and lie with her face while her hand burned under its wrappings.

He only said, quietly: "Your hand. Let me see it before you sleep."

"It's fine."

"That's what you said six days ago, and then you fainted mending a bridle."

"That was the road fever," she said. "Everyone had it."

"Pip didn't."

She looked up at him. He was a disgraced knight-commander with a traitor's brand waiting for him in the capital, a man who had lost everything in the space of an afternoon and rebuilt himself into something harder and stranger—and still his eyes held that same stupid, stubborn care that had gotten him exiled in the first place. Reading sealed ledgers. Asking forbidden questions. Caring about a king who might already be beyond saving.

"In the morning," she said.

He held her gaze a moment longer, and she saw it there again—the thing he wasn't saying, sitting behind his teeth like a coin held under a tongue. Then he nodded and went to take first watch.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She climbed instead, up the ruined stair of the watchtower, careful on the wet stone, until she came out under the open vault of sky where the roof should have been. The rain had thinned to mist. Through it, low over the eastern ridgeline, hung the moon—fat, silver, eight nights from full. Mooncount nine, by the old reckoning the temple pretended it had never used.

Somewhere east and south, past four ranges and a hundred leagues of guarded road, the Spire stood gilded against the night, and inside it a man who had once been a king sat wearing a crown that ate miracles. And somewhere in that city, Inquisitor Dain Merek was doing arithmetic of his own, and High Ledgermistress Ivenna was balancing columns, and neither of them ever slept, she was sure of it. People like that didn't sleep. They tallied.

Mirabel unwrapped her hand.

The burn had healed wrong—or right, depending on how you looked at it. Where the fire had kissed her skin, the mark that remained was not scar-tissue but script: fine, looping characters in a faded gold-brown, circling her palm and wrist like a border drawn around a page. Old-god script. She couldn't read it. Pip could, a little, and had gone very quiet the one time Pip traced it with one small finger and whispered, *"It says the debt is transferable."*

Transferable. Everything in this kingdom came down to that phrase eventually.

She flexed her fingers and watched the mark pulse, faintly, in time with the moonlight. Below her, in the tower, she could hear the small sounds of the company settling: Sefa's crew muttering over dice, Aldric's boots on the stair as he walked his watch, Pip's breathing gone slow and deep. Her people. Hers, though she had never in her life been permitted to own anything, and would certainly not be permitted to own this.

*Let a vessel be found who takes it willingly.*

Here was what she knew, in the ledger of her own heart, in the column she never showed anyone:

Aldric would volunteer. Of course he would. He was a knight; it was the whole architecture of him. He would kneel on that throne-room floor and offer himself with that grave, hopeless honor of his, and the binding would refuse him—she was almost sure it would refuse him, because he would be offering partly out of duty, and duty was not willingness, duty was just another debt, and the chain had to be forged of *consent*, freely given, no creditor attached. He would fail. And then he would spend whatever was left of his life believing he hadn't been good enough.

Sefa would laugh at the whole idea and then try to do it anyway, drunk on courage, and the binding would refuse her too, because Sefa Corrane wanted to live—wanted it fiercely, greedily, wanted wine and roads and her crew grown old around her—and wanting to live was not a flaw, but it meant her yes would always have a *no* hiding inside it.

And Pip—

Her stomach closed like a fist. No. The text said *willingly*, and a child's willingness was worth nothing, was a horror, was the exact crime the temple committed when it transferred a family's debt onto a fourteen-year-old girl and called it lawful. If it came to Pip, she would burn the world down first. She was surprised to find how calm she felt about that. Not rage. Arithmetic.

Which left, if you ran the numbers honestly, the way she'd been trained to run them since she could hold a pen:

One candidate. One scribe with a debt-marked hide, whose whole life had been spent being overlooked, underestimated, invisible—whose entire talent, her one genius, was going unnoticed by power. And here was the final joke of it, the joke so bitter she almost laughed aloud into the mist: the crown-spirit *saw*. It perceived through every ward and veil in the kingdom. The one creature in Vaelmoor built to escape its notice would become, if the pages spoke true, the one creature it could never stop noticing. Forever. Its voice in her ear until the day the chain snapped taut and—

And what? That was the missing line. That was the burned edge. Nobody knew what happened when the vessel died, and she had sat through two nights of argument letting them theorize because as long as the sentence was unfinished, she didn't have to finish it for herself.

Below her, Aldric's boots paused on the stair. She felt him looking up through the broken vaulting, feeling for her the way he always did, and she stepped back from the edge of the roofline so he'd find the tower room empty when he finished his round.

There would be time to tell them. There had to be. On the road, maybe, somewhere between here and the capital—some cold morning when the fire was lit and the mood was steady and she could lay it out in ledger-code, debits and credits, and make them understand it wasn't sacrifice, it was *arithmetic*, and arithmetic was the one language she'd never been allowed to stop speaking.

Or maybe she'd tell no one. Maybe she'd walk into the Spire with a plan folded up inside her sleeve next to the glowing hand, the way she'd walked into every dangerous room of her double life, and let them all discover afterward what she had decided for them. That was its own sin. She knew that. She had watched the temple commit it for eleven years—deciding for people, in their name, on their behalf, balancing their accounts without their consent—and she had sworn, in the smoking dark of the archive with her hand on fire, that she would never be the temple.

But the alternative was telling Aldric tonight, and Aldric would argue, and Sefa would argue louder, and Pip would look at her with those old eyes and already know, and the argument would eat days they didn't have, and meanwhile Merek was closing and the moon was filling and the kingdom was running out of miracles to steal.

Nine days into the ninth moon. A hundred and ten days, if the roads behaved.

She wrapped the hand again, hiding the gold-brown script under linen, and started down the stairs to lie to her family with her face, which was, she reflected, the one skill the temple had actually taught her well.

On the landing below, she stopped. Pip was awake—of course Pip was awake, sitting up in the nest of blankets by the cold hearth, watching her come down the stairs with the firelight catching the faint luminescence that never entirely left the child's skin these days.

"You counted," Pip said softly.

"What?"

"Just now, on the roof. Your lips moved. You were counting." Pip tilted her head. "You always count when you're scared. Brother Ossian said so. He said you count your steps and your coins and your sins, and the number's always smaller than the truth."

Mirabel crouched down to the child's level. Up close, Pip's eyes had that depth to them, that well-water darkness, and Mirabel felt—as she always did—the strange sensation of being *read*, thoroughly, by someone a quarter her age.

"What are you afraid of?" Pip asked.

The honest answer sat in Mirabel's chest like a swallowed coal. Four moons. A vessel willing. A chain forged of consent. A missing line at the bottom of a burned page.

"Roads," she said, and smiled the smile that had fooled inquisitors. "I'm afraid of roads. Now sleep, little one. We march at dawn."

Pip lay down obediently. But as Mirabel turned to climb to her own corner, the child's voice followed her up the stairs, small and clear and terrible:

"The stone sang about you today," Pip said. "It says the vessel is already chosen. It says she just hasn't told anyone yet."

Mirabel did not answer. She climbed the rest of the stairs in the dark, laid down without removing her boots, and stared at the broken ceiling until the mist outside began to pale toward dawn—counting, counting, counting, and the number was always smaller than the truth.

Tomorrow they would break camp and turn south, toward the capital, toward the Spire. Tomorrow the mooncount would tick to ten, and Aldric would ask about her hand again, and she would show him, because a lie needed feeding to stay alive—and tomorrow, maybe, or the day after, or on some rain-slick pass with Merek's hounds behind them, she would finally open her mouth and say the thing the stone already knew.

But not yet. Not tonight. Tonight the secret was still hers, and a scribe learns early that the last thing you own is the last thing you surrender.

Four moons. She could carry it that long.

She'd carried heavier.
