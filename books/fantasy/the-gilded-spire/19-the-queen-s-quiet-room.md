## 19. The Queen's Quiet Room

The summons came sewn into the hem of a beggar's coat.

Aldric had been three days in the capital, moving between safehouses Sefa Corrane's people kept like a spider keeps webs—dusty, unremarkable, strung across the city in patterns only she could read. He had grown a beard, traded his knight-commander's bearing for a carter's slouch, and learned to flinch correctly when temple wardens passed. It was a strange education for a man who had once commanded four hundred swords, and he was not certain he was learning it well.

The coat arrived folded in a delivery of lamp oil to the chandler's shop on Cooper's Row where he was pretending to inventory tallow. Inside the lining, stitched with thread so fine it might have been an accident of manufacture, was a scrap of linen no larger than his palm.

On it, in a hand he knew—had signed orders beneath, had knelt before while it held a seal—were five words:

*The quiet room. Midnight. Come alone.*

He read it twice, then fed it to the chandler's furnace and watched the linen curl and go to ash. *Come alone* was a problem. Mirabel Halloran would never permit him to go anywhere alone again; he had seen what happened to people who went places alone in this kingdom. But some doors did not open for two.

* * *

In the end he compromised the way soldiers compromise: he told her a version of the truth shaped to pass inspection.

"The queen wants to see me," he said, in the back room above the chandler's shop, where Pip sat cross-legged on the floor sorting buttons by color and Mirabel stood at the window doing her trick of being furniture even though there was nothing to hide behind but curtains.

Mirabel turned from the window slowly. In the lamplight her face was all sharp angles and banked fire, and Aldric had learned in their weeks on the road that the sharper the angles got, the more carefully you should tread. "Alone," she said.

"She asked for alone."

"And you're going to tell her no."

"I'm going to tell her I'll come alone," Aldric said, "and then I'm going to spend an hour making sure that's true." He laid out the plan the way he'd once laid out battle dispositions: the route through the tanner's quarter, the timing against the night watch rotations Merek's men kept, the signal—a shutter closed twice—if things went wrong. "If I don't send word by dawn, take Pip north and don't look back."

"Those are terrible instructions," Mirabel said. "I've never followed good ones either, so we'll get along." She looked at him a long moment, and whatever she saw in his face seemed to settle something. "You know she could be bait. The whole court could be bait. That thing in the crown felt us in the tunnels under the city. If it can reach through the king, it can reach through her."

"I know."

"You're going anyway."

"I served her husband for nineteen years," Aldric said. "I knelt when they crowned him. Whatever else is true, there's a woman in that palace who has been alone with a monster for longer than any of us have been running from one. If she's asking for help, I owe her the courtesy of finding out if it's real."

Pip looked up from the buttons. The child's eyes had that faraway shine they got sometimes, as if listening to music no one else could hear. "She cries at night," Pip said simply. "The lady in the tall house. She sings the old songs so the crying stops, but the crying doesn't stop. It just goes somewhere deep."

The room went very still.

"You've heard her?" Mirabel asked quietly.

Pip shrugged, the shrug of a child who did not understand that what they said could rearrange the world. "She sings wrong on purpose. So the thing upstairs won't listen. But sometimes it listens anyway." Pip went back to the buttons. "You should bring her something warm. People who cry that long forget to eat."

* * *

Midnight found Aldric standing in a servant's corridor that smelled of beeswax and cold stone, guided by a page who could not have been more than twelve and who never once looked at his face. The palace of Vaelmoor was a body he knew from the outside—the great hall, the throne room, the barracks of the Kingsguard—but its veins were foreign to him, passages behind tapestries, stairs built for carrying coal, doors that existed in no plan he'd ever been shown. He understood suddenly that the palace had been designed by people who expected to be besieged by their own guests.

The quiet room was small and low-ceilinged, tucked into the oldest part of the keep, and it held almost nothing: a table, two chairs, a cold hearth, and Queen Isolde Vaelmoor.

She was not what he remembered. Nineteen years ago she had been a bride of seventeen, all polish and protocol, a princess from the coastal kingdoms married north for grain treaties. Now she was thin in a way that spoke of deliberate rationing rather than circumstance, gray-streaked hair pinned without ornament, wearing a servant's wool dress with a queen's posture. Her eyes were the worst part. They were the eyes of someone who had been awake for years.

"Ser Aldric." Her voice was low and even. "Or should I say *former* Ser Aldric. The paperwork on your disgrace was very thorough. I made them show it to me."

"Your Majesty."

"Don't." She raised a hand, and for a moment the gesture was pure command, the voice that had commanded a household through nine years of quiet horror. "There is no Majesty here. There is no rank in this room. Sit down, Aldric, before your knees give out. You always hid exhaustion badly. Even at the northern musters."

He sat. Up close he could see the tremor in her hands that she kept folded in her lap to disguise it, and the faint shadows of old bruises at her collarbone, long healed, long hidden.

"You sent for me."

"I sent for the man who noticed." She leaned forward. "Four years ago. The Rite of Turning. You saw my husband's shadow move late, and you were fool enough to write it down in your observation log instead of forgetting it like everyone else who has ever seen it. Do you know how many people have seen it, Aldric? How many guards, physicians, priests? I have counted eleven in nineteen years. Ten of them are dead or disappeared. You were merely exiled, which means someone wanted you alive, which means someone thought you were more useful disgraced than dead." A thin smile. "I have wondered for years whether that was luck or design. I've stopped caring. You came."

"I came."

"Then let us waste no time, because we may have very little." She drew a breath, and when she spoke again the evenness cracked, just once, like ice over deep water. "My husband is still inside. I need you to understand that first, before anything else, because everything else depends on it. He is still *there*. Buried, starved, caged—but there. I have proof. I have nineteen years of proof. And every person I have ever shown it to is gone."

"Tell me," Aldric said.

* * *

She told him in the flat voice of a woman giving a military report, because that was the only way she could tell it.

"It feeds at the Rites. You know that much, I think—you saw the shadow lag because the body was being *used*, worn like a glove. But here is what the ledgers will never show you. It cannot hold him all the time. It is strong during ceremonies, strong when prayers are being answered somewhere in the kingdom—it swells on them, I can feel the whole palace change, the air goes thick like before a storm—and then it thins. And in the thinning, he surfaces."

"How often?"

"Minutes. Sometimes only seconds. Once, years ago, nearly an hour—he woke in the garden at dawn and didn't know where he was, and I got him inside before the attendants came, and he wept into my shoulder and asked me why his hands smelled of ink." Her jaw tightened. "I have learned to farm those moments. I have become a harvester of seconds of my own husband's mind. Do you understand what this court has made of me? I am a woman who hoards crumbs and calls it a marriage."

"What brings him up?" Aldric asked. "What calls him to the surface?"

Isolde was quiet for a moment. Then she rose, crossed to the wall, and pressed a stone that clicked inward with the softness of long practice, revealing a niche. From it she took a small object wrapped in wool, and unwrapped it on the table between them.

It was a music box. Old, plain, its paint worn to bare wood—no gilding, no royal mark. A peasant's toy.

"He bought this before I met him," she said. "When he was crown prince, traveling incognito through Thornfell, before anyone taught him what he would have to stop being. There was a shrine singer in a mountain village. He paid her with a silver button and she gave him this, and a song." Isolde's fingers rested on the box without winding it. "The spirit hates it. Do you understand? It *hates* it. When the song plays, the king comes up fighting, like a drowning man following light through water. The magisters call these episodes seizures. The physicians dose him until he sleeps. The High Ledgermistress suggested, twice, delicately, that the object be removed from my keeping." Her eyes lifted to Aldric's, and they were terrible and bright. "Twice. And both times I reminded her, very politely, that a queen's grief is one of the few properties a crown cannot audit."

"The old songs," Aldric said. "That matches what we've seen. The child with us—Pip—they sing things that make walls remember themselves. In the tunnels under the city, the burn-mark on Mirabel's arm lit up like a signal fire, and something miles away felt it. We think it was the crown-spirit. We think it *turned toward her*."

"Then you are already known." Isolde said it without drama, the way one confirms weather. "Good. Then you'll believe the rest faster." She finally wound the music box, one turn, and set it on the table.

The melody that crept out was simple, modal, older than any hymn Aldric had ever heard in a temple. It sounded the way mountain air tastes. And he watched the queen watch it, and he understood that she was not listening to the music. She was watching the door, the walls, the space around them—for some sign that the thing upstairs had heard.

Nothing stirred. The song played itself out and died into silence.

"That's all it takes," Isolde said softly. "Thirty seconds of a shepherd's tune, and the most closely guarded prisoner in Vaelmoor claws his way toward the sound. They can't stop the songs entirely—do you know why? Because the songs are load-bearing. The binding that holds the spirit in the crown was woven with them. Suppress the old music completely and the binding frays, and the thing gets *loose*, and loose is worse than leashed. So the temple burns prayer pages and silences singers in the villages, but the palace keeps its little stock of tolerated lullabies, dosed like medicine." She laughed, briefly, and it was the worst sound Aldric had heard in years. "Everything in this kingdom is a dosage. Everything is arithmetic. I have lived nineteen years inside an equation, Aldric, and I am so tired."

"Your Majesty—" He stopped himself. "Isolde. Why now? Why summon a disgraced knight you haven't spoken to in four years? What changed?"

For the first time, the queen hesitated. She looked at the dead hearth as if reading something written in the ash.

"The thirteenth moon," she said. "The turning. You know about it." It wasn't a question; word had reached even her, through channels she clearly declined to name. "Every nineteenth year, the Rite of Turning becomes the Rite of Renewal, and the renewal requires... a harvest. I have read the sealed accounts—I have spent nineteen years learning to read sealed accounts, it is remarkable what a woman can do when no one thinks she can read at all. This time the harvest is meant to be large. There is a child involved. God-touched. The Ledgermistress has been hunting it since the tunnels." Her gaze came back to him, iron under ash. "And there is a scribe with a debt-mark who carries stolen pages in her skin. I know about the burn-mark, Aldric. I know what glows on her arm. I know what it means, better perhaps than she does, because I have read the oldest accounts of the first binding. The vessel. The willing carrier. The chain made of a life."

Aldric's blood went cold. "She hasn't told us what the pages say. Not all of it. She reads ahead of us and closes the book and smiles and changes the subject."

"Of course she does." Isolde's voice gentled, and it was somehow worse than the iron. "Because she already knows. People who intend to sacrifice themselves develop a particular way of holding a cup of tea. I have watched myself do it for nineteen years." She stood, and straightened, and the servant's dress became armor. "So. Here is what I can give you, and it is not much, and it is treason of the highest order, and I give it gladly. Listen."

* * *

She gave him the servant tunnels.

Not the ones they had used coming into the city—those were common knowledge to the palace guard, swept weekly. These were older. Pre-dynastic, she said, cut by the builders of the Spire itself before the first king ever wore a crown, back when the Spire had been something other than a treasury and a cage. The palace sat on the Spire's roots like a barnacle on a hull, and the old builders had left ways through the bone.

"There is a door in the undercroft of the east laundry," she said, tracing the route on the table with a fingertip, leaving a faint line of damp on the wood. "Behind the third row of washing coppers. It opens onto a stair that descends past the cellars, past the foundations, into a gallery the current architects don't have plans for. The gallery runs beneath the processional way directly to the Spire's root-chambers. The temple believes those chambers are solid rock. They are not. Nothing in this kingdom is what it appears to be—that is rather the entire disease, isn't it?"

"Why give this to us?" Aldric asked. "Why not use it yourself? Nineteen years, Isolde. You could have walked him out through those tunnels any night he surfaced. Small hours, one lucid minute at a time, a hundred nights—"

"Because he cannot leave." She said it simply, and the simplicity was the horror. "The binding anchors through the crown, and the crown anchors through the throne, and the throne anchors through the Spire itself. Move him beyond a certain distance and the spirit tears free of its leash and takes him apart looking for purchase. I consulted, early on, in code, with a physician who understood bindings. He explained it to me and then he disappeared, which was instructive. My husband dies the moment he leaves the Spire's reach—or worse, the moment he leaves it *empty-handed*, with the spirit still hungry and loose." She met Aldric's eyes. "Whatever your scribe intends to do at the heart of it, it must be done *inside*. On the thirteenth turning. There is no other night, and there is no other place. I am not giving you an escape route, Ser Aldric. I am giving you a road in. Whether there is a road out is a question I stopped asking years ago."

The words hung in the cold air of the quiet room. Aldric found himself thinking of Sefa Corrane lying wounded in a smuggler's cache north of the city, of Ossian in an inquisitor's cell, of Pip asleep against Mirabel's shoulder on bad nights, humming fragments of songs that made the candle flames lean in to listen. All the roads he had walked out of honor and into exile had led here, to a basement room and a queen in a washerwoman's dress handing him a map drawn in water.

"There's something else," Isolde said. "A condition."

"Name it."

"When it's done—whatever is done, binding, burning, freeing, failing—when the thirteenth moon sets, I want my husband's body brought to me. What's left of him. What's *him*." Her voice did not break. It had clearly been trained out of breaking decades ago. "Nineteen years I have shared a bed with a monster and saved crumbs of the man. I am owed the man. Even if it's only an hour. Even if it's only enough time to sing the song right, with him awake to hear it."

Aldric bowed his head. "If it can be done, it will be done."

"I know," she said. "That's why I chose you. Not because you're loyal—you proved you aren't, thank the gods, loyalty is what built this cage. I chose you because you're the kind of man who writes down what he sees, even when writing it down destroys him." She almost smiled. "Go. Before the watch rotation turns. And Aldric—one more thing."

She took the music box and pressed it into his hands, closing his fingers over the worn wood.

"If Mirabel Halloran tells you she's the only one who can carry it," the queen said, "you tell her that a woman who has spent nineteen years watching a willing vessel rot from the inside knows exactly what 'willing' costs. And you tell her that the kingdom does not need another martyr. It needs a smuggler. Make her cheat, Aldric. Make her find the third option. That's what smugglers do, isn't it?" She stepped back, and the queen was gone from her face, and only the tired woman remained. "Now get out of my house before I commit another act of treason and start crying."

* * *

He was three streets away, beard pulled up, carter's slouch restored, when the bells of the temple rang the hour before dawn. In his pocket, the music box knocked gently against his hip with each step, like a small stubborn heart.

At the chandler's shop, the shutter was dark, but the back-room window glowed faintly at its edges. He climbed the stair and pushed open the door and found them waiting—Mirabel at the table with a half-mended shirt she had clearly not mended a single stitch of, Pip curled in the corner with one eye open, which was how the child slept in cities.

"Well?" Mirabel said.

Aldric set the music box on the table between them. Then he told them everything—the tunnels, the thirteenth moon, the harvest, the anchor that would not let the king leave, the queen's condition. He told it all except one piece, and then, because he was the kind of man who wrote down what he saw, he told that too.

"She knows what the pages say," he finished. "About the vessel. About what a willing carrier becomes. She says you already know, and that you've known since Mooncount Nine." He met Mirabel's eyes and did not look away. "She also says the kingdom doesn't need a martyr. It needs a smuggler. She says to make you find the third option."

Silence. Pip sat up slowly in the corner, and the child's eyes had gone wide and luminous, fixed not on Aldric but on the music box, as if hearing something still playing inside it.

Mirabel looked down at the worn wooden box for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was steady, and that steadiness frightened him more than anything the queen had said.

"Then we'd better go steal a king," she said, "before the temple counts us out of existence."

Outside, far above the sleeping city, the moon was waning toward its thirteenth turn, and somewhere in the Spire's gilded dark, something ancient turned over in its sleep—and began, patiently, to count.
