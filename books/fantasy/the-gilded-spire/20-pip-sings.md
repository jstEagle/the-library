## 20. Pip Sings

Pip had learned early that the dark was not empty.

The dark was a thing with ears.

Down in the servant tunnels beneath the capital, where the torchlight only reached as far as Aldric's arm could throw it and the stone sweated cold, Pip pressed one palm flat against the wall and listened the way other people looked. The stone was old. Older than the temple. Older than the king's grandfather's grandfather. And it was *lonely*—that was the thing nobody understood about old stone, that it got lonely, sitting in the dark for hundreds of years with nothing to remember out loud.

"You're humming again," Mirabel whispered behind her.

"Wasn't."

"You were. The same three notes you've been doing since we came down the ladder." A pause, and then, softer: "Where'd you learn them?"

Pip shrugged, though Mirabel couldn't see it in the dark. "The shrine sang them first. I just kept them. Like keeping a pebble in your pocket." She turned her hand over against the wall. "The walls know it too. They're just rusty."

Ahead, Aldric's torch guttered and steadied. The knight moved like a man walking into his own funeral—shoulders squared, jaw set, every step placed as if the floor might be lying to him. Sefa would have made a joke about that. Sefa was two days' ride north with a wound wrapped in boiled linen and an oath holding her together, and Pip missed her so much it sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

"The queen's map said four turnings," Aldric said. "Left at the cistern fork, down past the wine cellars, and the tunnel runs under the Spire's foundation."

"The queen's map is thirty years old," Mirabel said. "And this tunnel—" Pip felt Mirabel stop beside her, felt the scribe's fingers find the same place on the wall where the stone changed, went from dressed block to something rougher, older, "—this tunnel doesn't end where she thinks it does."

"How do you know?"

"Because Pip's been humming at it for half a mile and it's been humming back."

*It had been.* That was the marvel of it. Every step deeper, the stone's answering hum had grown a little louder, a little less shy, like a dog hearing its name after years of shouting. Old gods didn't speak the way the Ledgered Flame spoke—all ledger-lines and accounting-words and prayers that came back stamped RECEIVED. Old gods hummed. They remembered in melodies, and if you gave them back even three notes of what they'd lost, they leaned toward you like a fire leaning toward wind.

"I can wake it," Pip said. "I think. If I sing the whole thing."

Aldric turned. In the torchlight his face was all hard planes and tired eyes, but there was something else in it too—the look of a man watching a child put her hand on a blade. "The whole what?"

"A lullaby." Pip hugged her arms around herself. The tunnel was cold and the cold came up through her boots like the mountain was trying to get a grip on her ankles. "The one from the shrine. It's not really words, not the way you mean words. It's more like... when your mam used to sing to you and you couldn't say what the song was about, only how it made you feel safe. The gods made songs like that for people before the temple taught everyone to count instead."

Mirabel was quiet a moment. Pip could feel her thinking; Mirabel thought loud, even when she didn't talk, like a ledger being balanced somewhere behind her eyes.

"If you sing," Mirabel said slowly, "and the walls answer—and they will, because everything old in this kingdom has been waiting for someone to ask—then every ear tuned to the old ways hears it. Including whatever's wearing the king."

"Then we shouldn't sing," Aldric said flatly. "We should move fast and quiet, like smugglers."

"We've been moving fast and quiet for six days," Mirabel said. "And the servant tunnels don't go through to the Spire anymore, do they, Pip? They've been walled up. Bricked. Because the temple sealed every door that remembered anything." Her voice did the thing it did when she was working arithmetic on someone's cruelty. "We don't need a door. We need the walls to open one."

"They'll want to," Pip said. "They're so *tired*, Mirabel. Nobody's sung to them since before your gran's gran."

Silence. The torch hissed. Somewhere far above, muffled by forty feet of stone, the city went about its evening—cart wheels, a watch bell, the thin thread of a temple choir practicing the Rite of Turning. Even that sound arrived flattened and gray, like everything the Flame touched.

Then Mirabel laughed—a short, startled sound, no humor in it at all.

"Listen to me," she said. "Planning to wake dead gods under the nose of the thing that eats them. Six months ago I was copying prayers about honest weights and measures." She touched Pip's shoulder. Her hand was warm and ink-stained and it shook, just slightly, which Pip pretended not to notice because Mirabel hated being noticed. "Sing, little one. But sing quiet. Make it a secret between you and the stone. Can you do that?"

Pip could. Secrets were the first thing the shrine had taught her, back when the singing started and Sefa's crew still looked at her like she was weather they hadn't planned for. You didn't shout a god awake. You leaned close to the world's ear and you *told* it.

She found her note the way you find a stair in the dark—with your body, before your mind. The lullaby had no beginning you could point to; you just stepped into the middle of it, the way you'd been stepping into the middle of it your whole life without knowing. Three notes, then a falling fourth, then the long held tone underneath like water under ice.

She sang it soft. Softer than breathing. She sang it *into the wall.*

* * *

The stone woke like a cat stretching.

That was the only way Pip could ever describe it afterward, though she tried many times. Not an earthquake, not a rumble—nothing so crude. It was a shift in weight, a settling, the whole tunnel suddenly aware of itself the way a sleeper becomes aware of their own limbs. Dust sifted down through the torchlight, gold-flecked, turning slowly, like the air itself had decided to shine.

"Oh," Pip said, because there wasn't another word.

"What—" Aldric began.

The wall opened.

Not broke. *Opened*—the great rough blocks sliding apart along seams no eye could see, grinding with a sound like a giant clearing its throat, and beyond them lay a room full of sleeping light.

Pip stepped through before anyone could catch her sleeve. She heard Mirabel hiss her name and didn't stop, because the room was singing back now, low and layered, a hundred small voices braided into one, and no power in Vaelmoor could have made her wait.

It was a chapel. Small—smaller than any temple chapel, no bigger than Sefa's cabin on the wagons—but it was built in curves, the ceiling arched like the inside of a shell, and every surface was carved. Not with the Flame's ledgers and tally-marks. With hands. Hundreds of carved hands, palms open, lining the walls from floor to ceiling, and resting in each stone palm was a relic.

A bell with no clapper, green with age. A cup that held darkness instead of wine. A comb of white wood. A child's shoe, bronze, worn through at the toe. A mirror that showed only fog. Seeds in a clay jar that rattled when Pip breathed near them—rattled *in rhythm with her breath*, she realized, and her skin prickled from wrist to crown.

"They're not dead things," she whispered. "They're sleeping things. Like seeds in winter." She turned, and the torchlight caught Mirabel in the doorway, and Pip saw—

"Mira. Your hand."

Mirabel looked down at her right hand, at the burn scar across her palm where she'd snatched three pages out of the temple fire, at the mark that had glowed faint as banked coals on bad nights ever since.

It was blazing.

Not burning her—Pip could tell the difference, had learned the difference from the shrine—but lit from underneath the skin, lines of gold crawling up her wrist like script writing itself, and every carved hand in the chapel was turning toward her. Slowly. Stone grinding on stone. Hundreds of open palms pivoting, offering their sleeping treasures to a scribe who stood frozen in the doorway with light pouring out of her hand like she'd dipped it in dawn.

"It knows me," Mirabel said. Her voice came out strange, flat, the voice she used when the numbers frightened her. "Why does it *know* me?"

"Because you took something out of the fire," Pip said simply. "Fire eats remembering. You stole remembering back. The old ways mark their thieves." She grinned despite everything, because it seemed a wonderful kind of theft. "You're not a thief, though. You're a keeper now. There's a difference. The shrine told me. Keepers glow."

"That's—" Aldric had come through behind them, torch high, and Pip watched him take in the chapel with a soldier's eyes and lose the war against wonder anyway. His torch flame bent sideways, toward the relics, like all fire wanted to be here. "*Saints' bones,*" he breathed. "This is beneath the Spire. Beneath it. They built their gilded throne on top of *this* and bricked it shut."

"They always do," Mirabel said. "Build on top. Seal below. Put the pretty face upstairs and bury everything that remembers downstairs." She flexed her glowing hand, winced, curled her fingers closed. The light leaked between them regardless. "Pip. How long does this last? The waking?"

Pip considered the chapel honestly, listening to its hundred sleeping voices. "For the room? Long. Rooms hold songs well." Then, because lying to Mirabel was like lying to a scale—it always weighed out in the end—"But the singing travels. Sound goes where it wants. And the thing in the crown has ears everywhere the Flame built fires."

Aldric's head snapped around. "You said sing *quiet*."

"She did," Pip said. "And the walls carried it anyway, because walls love her more than they fear the crown. That's not anyone's fault. That's just what happens when you feed starving things." She reached out and laid her small hand over Mirabel's blazing one. The gold light wrapped around both their fingers like warm honey. "It's okay, Mira. Being seen isn't always bad. Sometimes it's the first good thing that happens to you."

Mirabel looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind her eyes—fear, yes, but underneath the fear, something hungrier and older, the same longing Pip had seen the first night she'd followed her home through the rain. The longing to matter.

"Being seen," Mirabel repeated quietly, "is how everything I love gets taken away."

"Not always," said Pip, who was seven years old and therefore knew everything. "Sometimes it's how it gets *kept*."

* * *

Miles above and east, behind nine locks and a wall of sanctified glass, something ancient opened its eyes.

It had no eyes, properly speaking. It had attention, which was worse—for attention, once given, did not blink.

It had felt the tremor come up through the roots of the city like a struck bell: an old chord, a forbidden chord, a lullaby that predated the first king and the binding and the great Accounting that had saved the world by starving it. For a moment—three heartbeats of the dying man it wore—the thing in the crown *remembered*. Warmth. Hands. A valley full of voices singing crops ripe and rivers clean and children lucky. It remembered being loved in quantities beyond counting, before counting was invented as a cage.

Then the lucid flicker passed, and the hunger closed over the memory like water, and the crown-spirit did what it had done for nine hundred years.

It consulted the ledger.

*Imbalance,* it noted, in the language without words. *Below. In the sealed places. A singer. A carrier of unlicensed grace.* And threaded through that entry, flagged in the old red script it reserved for debts that threatened the whole book: *the marked one. The scribe who steals. The hand that burns.*

It had dismissed her before—an insect, an error, a rounding mistake. Errors could be corrected at leisure.

But errors did not wake chapels beneath the throne. Errors did not carry old-god fire in their skin and walk upright, smiling, forging papers, conning inquisitors. Errors did not gather knights and smugglers and god-touched children around them like sparks gathering to a rising flame.

The thing in the crown turned its whole vast attention, slow as a glacier, toward a point beneath its own foundations. It felt the shape of her now—Mirabel Halloran, debt-marked, fire-marked, marked in ways even it had not yet finished reading—and it felt, for the first time in centuries, something adjacent to caution.

It summoned its hound. It wrote new powers into the hound's commission with strokes of absolute black. *Bring me the vessel they are making,* it instructed. *Or bring me her hands.*

Far below, in a chapel full of opening palms, Mirabel Halloran shivered and looked over her shoulder at the empty tunnel, at the dark that had suddenly acquired a direction, a weight, a *gaze*.

"Mira?" Pip whispered.

"Someone just looked at us," Mirabel said. Her blazing hand had clenched into a fist, light streaming through her fingers like blood through bandages. "Something very old and very hungry just found us on its ledger. We have minutes. Maybe less."

Aldric was already moving, torch forward, blade loose. Above them, faint through forty feet of stone, the palace bells began to ring—not the hour, not the Rite, but the alarm-pattern that every soldier in the city knew in their marrow.

The chapel's hundred carved hands, all together, closed gently around their relics—as if tucking them in, as if saying goodbye.

Pip took Mirabel's burning hand, and they ran.
