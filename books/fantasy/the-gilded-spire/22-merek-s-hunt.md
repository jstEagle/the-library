## 22. Merek's Hunt

Inquisitor Dain Merek had learned long ago that the gods did not speak to men like him. They spoke to scribes, to children, to dying women with scribbled prayers and no one left to mourn them. To Merek they said nothing at all, which he had come to regard as a professional courtesy. A man who heard voices could be deceived by them. A man who heard nothing had only arithmetic.

And the arithmetic was finally coming due.

He stood on the shoulder of the Spire road where it narrowed above the Greywater falls, wind pulling at his grey cloak, and watched the mountain do what mountains did best: make liars out of maps. Three days of pursuit through Thornfell, three days of cold camps and dead informants, and now—there. Smoke, thin as a needle, rising from a hollow below the ridgeline where no honest traveler would bother with a fire.

"They've stopped," said Brother Calloway beside him, breath steaming. The young acolyte was shivering. Merek had stopped shivering years ago; he suspected it was one of the prices of the work, paid in installments so small you never noticed the account emptying.

"Stopped is not caught." Merek raised two fingers, and behind him thirty temple wardens and six crown magisters went still as cut stone. "They'll move again before moonrise. The smuggler woman knows these passes better than her own sins. We take them in the narrows, where the road forgets it's a road."

"And the child?"

Merek looked down at the boy-shaped absence in his ledger. *Pip. No surname. God-touched.* The pages of his report had begun to smell faintly of honey and old rain since the shrine incident, which was not a thing paper should do, and Merek had taken to keeping that particular page wrapped in lead foil like a poison.

"The child is the priority," he said. "The Halloran woman second. The knight third—he's only a man, whatever he used to be. The smugglers are cargo."

He did not say what the thing in the capital had told him, because it had not used words. It had used *pressure*, a weight behind his eyes like a thumb on wet clay, and an understanding that arrived fully formed the way a bruise arrives: *bring me the small bright one. Bring me the vessel-candidate. Bring me the imbalance.*

Merek had served the Ledgered Flame for twenty-two years and had never once been given an order by anything without a mouth. He found, examining himself honestly, that he felt no horror about this. Only a kind of grim satisfaction. At last, someone in this kingdom who knew exactly what it wanted.

* * *

The company made their mistake at dusk, and their mistake was kindness.

Merek saw it unfold from the ridge through a warden's far-glass: the fugitives' fire, banked low in the hollow, and around it five figures—the disgraced knight moving with a soldier's economy even stripped of his command, the scribe-woman Mirabel Halloran hunched over something that glowed faintly against her palm, the smuggler captain Sefa Corrane laughing at something, and the child, the god-touched child, turning slow circles with his arms out while motes of light gathered around him like moths that had forgotten to be afraid.

*Kindness,* Merek thought. *They stopped for the child's sake. He wanted to see the stars.*

It would have been easy to despise them for it. He found instead that it sat strangely in his chest, watching the little circle of light in all that dark—a family, or the shape of one, assembled from broken pieces the way beggars assemble a coat from other men's castoffs. Merek had been taken into the Flame at nine years old when his mother sold him to settle a debt of four crowns. He knew precisely how such families were priced.

He gave the signal.

The wardens came down the scree in silence, boots wrapped in wool, and the magisters walked behind them with their hands full of quiet. The plan was simple and therefore, in Merek's experience, doomed to complication: seal the hollow with a silence-circle so no prayer, no song, no old-god whisper could carry; take the child first; let the rest surrender in the face of overwhelming numbers.

For the first ninety seconds, it worked perfectly.

Then the boy looked up at the ridge—at Merek, specifically, across four hundred yards of darkening rock—and smiled, and said, in a voice that carried impossibly over the wind as if the wind were merely carrying it for him:

"He's sad. The grey man is the saddest one here."

Every head in the hollow turned toward the ridge. The ambush died in the space of a breath.

"Light take it," Calloway whispered.

"No," Merek said, already moving. "*Move.*"

* * *

What followed was not a battle. Battles had lines and drums and some pretense of order. What followed was a stampede of desperate people through terrain that hated everyone equally, and Merek pursued it with the cold patience of a man following a blood trail.

The company split at the river fork—Halloran and the knight driving uphill toward the old Spire road, the smugglers falling back along the water with the child between them. Corrane fought the way she presumably ran cargoes: dirty, fast, and with a total absence of honor that Merek noted with something adjacent to respect. She put a knife into a warden's thigh, a boot into another's teeth, and shouted orders in smugglers' cant that turned six frightened people into one moving animal.

But there were thirty of them. There were always more of them. That was the whole architecture of the temple—one inquisitor, thirty hands, and behind the thirty hands an institution with a thousand years of practice at being inevitable.

They cornered the smugglers at the narrows above Greywater falls, where the road pinched between cliff and drop and the river roared loud enough to swallow thought. Corrane put her back to the rock, blade out, blood sheeting down her left arm from a crossbow bolt she hadn't bothered to remove, and grinned at Merek across the closing ring of wardens.

"Inquisitor," she called over the water. "You look like a man doing sums."

"I am."

"How's it going?"

"Poorly," Merek admitted. "You're an unbalanced entry, Captain Corrane. Hand over the child and I'll enter you as a fine instead of a forfeiture."

She laughed—actually laughed, red teeth and all—and glanced back once at the boy pressed into the rocks behind her. Something passed across her face that Merek recognized despite himself, because he had seen it once in a cell block mirror after his mother's boat left without her: the expression of a person measuring what they were worth against what someone else was worth, and finding the sum unacceptable.

"You know what the old road-law says," Corrane said. "No one gets turned away from a lit fire. Not by us. Not tonight."

"Road-law is not law."

"It's the only kind that ever held," she said, "when the real law came to burn your house."

Then she moved—not away from the wardens but *into* them, into the narrowest point of the narrows, planting herself in the gap like a keystone pulled loose, and Merek understood one full second too late what she intended.

She wasn't fighting to win. She was fighting to *be the gap*.

"Take her!" Merek snapped. "Around her—"

But the narrows allowed nothing around her. Two abreast, that was all, and Sefa Corrane had spent twenty years learning every way a body could fill a space. She took a sword-cut across the shoulder and gave back a knife to the ribs. She took a pommel to the jaw and hooked a warden's ankle and sent him screaming over the edge into the white water. She laughed the entire time, ugly and magnificent, and behind her the boy Pip was tugging at Mirabel Halloran's sleeve—she had come back down, of course she had, kindness again—and Halloran was shouting something at Corrane that the river ate.

Merek pushed through his own wardens, drawing the lead-lined page from his coat, and held it up, and the words on it writhed, and he spoke the phrase the thing in the capital had pressed into him like a seal into wax.

The world *leaned*.

It was not magic as the magisters practiced it, tidy and contractual. It was older and hungrier. The sound of the river bent toward Merek. The light bent. And Sefa Corrane, mid-swing, staggered as if the mountain itself had shifted its weight against her, and the warden's blade found her side, deep, deep enough that Merek heard her breath change key.

She went down against the rock. Got up. Went down again.

Halloran screamed her name—a raw, cracked sound, nothing like a scribe—and the knight Aldric appeared out of the dark above like vengeance with a sword, cutting a lane to the fallen captain, hauling her up over his shoulders while Halloran and the boy scrambled past them both onto the high trail.

"Seal the pass!" Merek roared. "Nothing follows them up! Nothing!"

But the mountain was already doing his work for him. The high trail beyond the narrows climbed into country where horses failed and litters crawled, and Corrane was bleeding out the rhythm of her own heart, and every minute they spent carrying her was a mile the others gained.

Merek stood over the wrecked smuggler captain as the wardens bound her wounds just competently enough to keep her alive—for interrogation, for leverage, for the ledger—and looked up the black throat of the pass where four fugitives had become three-and-a-half.

Calloway came up beside him, pale. "We have her. We have the pass. But the child—"

"I know."

"The Spire road forks at the Hanging Stair. If they reach the stair before we—"

"They won't." Merek wound the lead foil back around the page. His hand, he noticed distantly, was not entirely steady, and he examined that too, filing it away the way he filed everything: *witnessed, weighed, entered.* "Because the child will slow them down."

Calloway frowned. "How, Inquisitor?"

"Because the child won't leave her." Merek looked down at Sefa Corrane, unconscious now, her grin finally gone, her blood making a black shine on the stones. "That's the flaw in everything they've built out there in the dark, brother. Every one of them keeps stopping to save each other. It's why they'll lose."

He believed it as he said it. That was the terrible part, he would think later. He believed it completely.

* * *

They caught the boy at moonset on the Hanging Stair, and it went wrong in a way Merek would spend the rest of his life failing to explain cleanly.

The stair was a shelf of ancient stone bolted to the cliff face by hands older than the temple, and the company had nearly reached its top when the wardens' lanterns closed the bottom. Halloran and Aldric were fifty feet ahead, hauling each other upward. The boy was between them and the wardens, climbing well for a child, quick as a squirrel—but quick was not fast enough, and Merek watched the geometry resolve with the dispassion of a man reading a column of figures: the boy would be reached. The others could not come back for him without dying. The equation balanced.

Except the boy stopped climbing.

He turned around on the narrow stair, over a drop that would kill a grown man, and looked down at the lanterns, and Merek felt the air change the way it changes before summer lightning. The boy wasn't afraid. That was the thing Merek had not prepared for, though he should have. Everyone was afraid. Fear was the load-bearing wall of the entire temple, the mechanism beneath every tithe and confession and audit. This child looked down at thirty armed servants of the Ledgered Flame with an expression of mild, sorrowful curiosity, like a man watching ants dismantle something beautiful.

"You're the sad one," Pip said, finding Merek's face among the lights. "I said before. You're the saddest one here."

"Hold," Merek said quietly, and the wardens held.

"Why are you sad?" The boy cocked his head. Around him, faint as breath on glass, the air began to glow. "The stone remembers you, grey man. It says you used to sing. Before. It says you had a mother who sang."

"Silence him," Calloway breathed. "Inquisitor, the power—it's waking the *stone*—"

Under Merek's boots, ancient and indifferent, the Hanging Stair hummed.

"Bind him," Merek said. "Gently. He's worth more unmarked."

The wardens surged up the stair, and the boy raised one small hand, and the humming became a chord, and two wardens sat down abruptly on the steps as if their strings had been cut—not hurt, Merek saw, just *done*, asleep mid-stride like tired children. The magisters began shouting counter-phrases. Light crawled up the cliff face in veins of gold. Somewhere below, the river forgot how to be a river for three full seconds and hung in the air like a held note.

And through all of it the boy kept looking at Merek, and the glow kept growing, and Merek understood with cold clarity that none of his instruments could touch this—that the Flame had spent a thousand years burning prayers precisely so it would never have to meet one face to face.

So he did the only thing left in his ledger. He took out the lead-wrapped page, unwrapped it, and let the thing in the capital speak through him.

"*Small bright one,*" he said, in a voice that was his and profoundly was not, flat and vast as a vault door. "*Your song is owed. Your song has always been owed. Come and pay.*"

The glow guttered. The boy swayed on the stair, eyes going wide and glassy, and for one heartbeat Merek saw terror arrive in that small face—the first fear, the first proof that under the god-light he was still nine years old and alone on a cliff—and Merek felt something twist in his chest that he refused to name.

The wardens took him then. Hands and hood and chains inscribed with binding-script, and the light went out of the air all at once, and the mountain was only a mountain again, and the river remembered how to fall.

From the top of the stair, thin with distance and rage, Mirabel Halloran's voice came down the dark: *"PIPPIN!"* And then Aldric's arm around her, dragging her away, because staying meant dying and dying meant the boy's capture bought nothing.

Merek stood on the humming stone with the sleeping child in his arms, and waited for triumph to arrive.

It did not. What arrived instead was a memory, unbidden and unwelcome: himself at nine years old, chained in a wagon, watching his mother's house recede, while a temple clerk entered him in a ledger as *one boy, value: four crowns, debt settled.*

He had become the wagon. He understood that now, standing in the dark with the warm weight of a god-touched child breathing against his shoulder. Somewhere in twenty-two years of faithful arithmetic, the clerk and the boy had changed places, and no one had audited the transfer.

"Set up camp," he said, and his voice came out level, which cost him more than the climb had. "Wounded go down at first light. The prisoner goes with me."

"And the others, Inquisitor? Halloran and the knight? They'll follow us to the Spire."

"Yes," Merek said. He looked up the pass, toward the distant gilded peak catching the last moonlight like a blade catching a lie. "They will. Count on it. Build it into the sums."

He handed the boy to Calloway and turned away before anyone could see his face, and behind him, faint under the wind, the old stone sang one more line of its endless remembered song—and Merek, who heard nothing from any god, heard that.

He did not report it. Some entries, he told himself, a man was permitted to keep off the books.

The march to the Spire began at dawn, and every mile of it, Merek knew, was already paid for.
