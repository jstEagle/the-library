## 23. The Price of Survival

The cave smelled of pine smoke and blood.

Mirabel knelt beside Sefa and did the arithmetic she hated most—not coin, not pages, not prayers, but flesh. The wound ran from the smuggler captain's hip to her ribs, a long red seam where Merek's blade had opened her like a ledger. Someone had packed it with moss and bound it with a sash, but the binding was already soaked through, dark as spilled ink.

"Stop staring at it," Sefa rasped. Her face was gray as old tallow, but her eyes were still sharp enough to cut. "It's not going to improve under observation."

"Nothing improves under observation," Mirabel said. "That's why I became invisible."

A wet laugh that turned into a cough. "There's my girl."

*I'm not your girl,* Mirabel wanted to say. *I'm the reason you're bleeding.* She said nothing. She took Sefa's hand instead, and found it cold, and felt something in her chest crack along a fault line she hadn't known was there.

Behind them, deeper in the cave, Aldric stood at the entrance with his sword drawn and his back to the firelight, watching the pass they'd fled down. Watching the road where Pip wasn't. Where Ossian wasn't. Where the screams had faded into mountain silence an hour ago, and no one had spoken since.

* * *

They had run. That was the truth of it, stripped of all its better words. Merek had come out of the treeline with temple hunters at his back and new power rolling off him like heat off a forge—the crown-spirit's attention made flesh—and Sefa had looked at Mirabel once, just once, and shouted the order that would define them all:

*"Run, you idiots! I'll hold the pass!"*

And they had run. Mirabel, who could have stayed. Who knew twenty ways to hide in plain sight, who had spent her whole life being overlooked, who might have slipped through Merek's grasp the way she slipped through everything. She had run because Sefa told her to, and because running was what she did best, and because some coward's arithmetic in her head had whispered: *you're the one who can read the roads. You're the one the Spire needs.*

The same arithmetic, she realized now, sitting in the dark with Sefa's blood cooling on her hands, that had let her walk away from the burning woman in chapter two of her own damnation. The same voice that always, always concluded: *survive first. Grieve later. You can grieve forever; you can only die once.*

She was so tired of that voice.

* * *

"We go back," Mirabel said.

The word came out before she'd decided to say it, which was how the truest things usually left her. Aldric turned from the cave mouth. In the firelight his face was all planes and shadow, the disgraced knight's face, the exile's face, and behind it she could see him doing his own arithmetic.

"No," he said quietly.

"They have Pip. They have Ossian."

"I know."

"Merek will take them to the Spire. Every hour we—"

"Every hour you spend running back down that pass is an hour Merek expects you to spend," Aldric said. "He cornered us there because he *wanted* you to come back for them. That's not a rescue, Mirabel. That's a second net."

"And if we don't?" She was on her feet now, and didn't remember standing. "If we just keep climbing toward the Spire with two of ours in his cells? What are we then?"

"Alive," Aldric said. "Which is more than they'd be if we throw ourselves after them."

"That's easy for you to say. You've already lost your honor. I haven't—" She stopped. The words had gone somewhere crueler than she intended, and she saw it land, saw the old wound in him open a finger's width. "I didn't mean that."

"You meant some of it." He sheathed his sword and came to sit across the fire from her, close enough that she could see the exhaustion carved around his eyes. "Good. Be angry. Anger keeps you moving. But be angry at the right target." He nodded at Sefa. "She made a soldier's choice. She'd make it again. You know she would."

"I know she would," Mirabel said, and her voice broke on it. "That's what I can't stand. Everyone keeps choosing to bleed for me. Ossian, who couldn't lie to save his life, lied to save mine and got taken for it. Sefa held a pass against an inquisitor with the crown-spirit's blessing on him. And here I am, alive again, *again*, walking away while other people pay my debts."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the pines with a sound like turning pages.

"Is that what you think this is?" Aldric asked. "Your debt?"

"What else would you call it?"

"I'd call it a war," he said. "And in a war, people get wounded. People get taken. Not because of you, Mirabel. Because of the thing in the crown that has been eating this kingdom for three hundred years. You didn't start this. You're just the first person in centuries stubborn enough to finish it."

"But I *let* them," she whispered. "Ossian offered to take my place in the net and I let him. I could have argued. I could have—"

"You could have what? Tied him up? Carried him over your shoulder through a burning archive?" Aldric shook his head. "I've commanded men for twenty years. I've sent them to die with letters home in their pockets. Let me tell you the one lesson that cost me everything to learn." He leaned forward, firelight catching the gray in his beard. "The question is never whether people will suffer for the cause. They will. The question—the only question—is how many, and whether the suffering buys anything. A commander who won't ask that question kills everyone. A commander who asks it honestly sleeps badly and saves most of them."

"Then ask it," Mirabel said. "Ask it right now. How many people are acceptable losses, Knight-Commander Vane? For the kingdom. For the Spire. Give me the number."

He was silent for a long time.

"That's the trap of the question," he said finally. "There is no number. There's only the next choice, and the next, and doing your best to make each one worth what it costs. Anyone who gives you a number—" his jaw tightened, "—anyone who gives you a number is Ivenna. That's her whole religion. Balancing lives like columns in a ledger. We are not fighting to become better bookkeepers than she is."

Mirabel looked at her hands. Sefa's blood had dried in the creases, dark lines like writing she couldn't read.

"Then what am I fighting to become?"

"Somebody who stops paying for things with other people," Aldric said gently. "Starting with yourself."

* * *

Sefa slept, or pretended to. The crew kept watch in shifts—two of the six who'd made it up the mountain, hollow-eyed, flinching at every sound. Mirabel sat apart with the stolen pages spread on her knees, the ones she'd pulled from the temple fire at the cost of her burned hand, and read them by moonlight through the cave mouth.

*Unbound only at the Spire. By a vessel willing to carry it. On the thirteenth turning.*

Vessel. She'd known since Mooncount Nine what the word meant. She'd known and said nothing, hoarding the knowledge like a coin she couldn't bear to spend, telling herself she'd decide when the time came. But the time was coming whether she decided or not, and now the price had been paid in advance—by Ossian, by Sefa, by a child who sang to walls because the walls sang back.

How many acceptable losses?

One, said the arithmetic in her head. One, at the end, if it must be. Me.

But even that felt like a lie—a smuggler's trick she was playing on herself, dressing up despair as sacrifice. Because wanting to turn back wasn't strategy. It was guilt wearing armor. If she went back and died in Merek's cells, Pip would still be caged, Ossian would still be broken, Sefa would still be bleeding, and the crown-spirit would still be eating the world one answered prayer at a time. Her death would buy nothing. It would just be one more entry in Ivenna's ledger, and Ivenna had taught her exactly how much entries were worth.

*You were never invisible,* she thought suddenly, and didn't know where it came from. *You were waiting. And now you're here, and waiting is over.*

"You look like a woman making a decision," Aldric said from the darkness. He'd moved without a sound; old soldiers did.

"I look like a woman who hasn't slept."

"Both things can be true." He settled beside her, close enough to share warmth without crowding. Below them, far down the valley, a light moved—small, distant, deliberate. Lanterns. Temple lanterns, sweeping the lower passes in a pattern she recognized from the other side of too many smuggling runs. Searching. Not for them.

For stragglers. For witnesses. For anyone who might carry word of what happened at the pass.

"They'll move the prisoners at dawn," Mirabel said. "Merek won't risk keeping them in the field. He'll take them to the Spire by the king's road, fast, before word spreads." She traced the route in her mind—she could see it the way she saw all the old roads now, glowing faintly behind her eyes since the wayshrine, since Pip's song woke something in the mountains. "There's a shepherd track that cuts the switchbacks above Greywater Ford. If they travel heavy with prisoners, we can be ahead of them by noon tomorrow."

Aldric studied her. "We're not going back for them."

"No," Mirabel agreed. "We're going ahead of them. Into the Spire." She folded the stolen pages and tucked them inside her shirt, against the burn scar that pulsed faintly, warm as a second heartbeat. "Merek wants me to come charging down that pass to die for my people. So I won't. I'll walk in the front gate instead—or near enough—and I'll trade myself for Pip when the moment's worth trading. Not before. Not out of guilt. Out of *arithmetic*, the good kind. The kind where I choose the price instead of letting it be chosen for me."

"That's a dangerous plan."

"It's the only kind I have." She almost smiled. "I've been underestimated my whole life, Aldric. It's the one advantage nobody can take from me. Merek thinks I'm a scribe who got lucky. Ivenna thinks I'm a debt-mark with legs. The crown-spirit thinks I'm an imbalance to be corrected." She looked at him, and for the first time since the pass, since the screaming, since the running, her eyes were steady. "Let them all keep thinking it. Right up until it costs them."

Below them, the lanterns swept on, patient as tide. Above them, past the ragged clouds, the twelfth moon was waning toward its dark.

Four nights, now. Maybe five.

Sefa stirred by the fire and murmured something in road-cant—an old smuggler's blessing, *may your road rise lit*—and Mirabel rose to check her bandages, and the cave settled into the long, cold business of waiting for dawn.

In the morning they would climb. Ahead of the enemy, into the teeth of the kingdom, toward a tower full of cells and ciphers and a child who needed someone to stop surviving long enough to start saving.

Mirabel closed her eyes and, for the first time in years, prayed—not to the Ledgered Flame, not to the waking gods, but to whatever listened in the space between.

*Let me be worth what it cost.*

Far away, in a cell built of old-god stone, something small and bright heard her, and began to sing.
