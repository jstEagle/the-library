## 25. Cells and Ciphers

The cell smelled of cold iron and old stone, and Pip had decided—somewhere between the third hour and the third day, time being a slippery thing down here—that the stone was the important part.

They had taken the boy's boots at the door. They had taken the cord from his hair and the little bone whistle Sefa had carved for him and the smooth river pebble he'd carried since Thornfell because it fit his palm just right. They had taken everything except the one thing they didn't know he had, which was the song.

Pip sat cross-legged on the pallet with his eyes closed and listened to the walls.

Everyone else heard walls. Walls were walls. But Pip had learned, in the wayshrine under Thornfell, that listening was a kind of seeing, and what he heard now made his heart beat slow and steady instead of fast and frightened, because the walls here were not temple stone. Not entirely. The temple had built its prison on top of something older, the way ivy builds itself on an oak, and underneath the mortar and the iron and the inquisitors' careful cruelty, the old stone was still there. Still sleeping. Still humming, very faintly, like a hearth gone cold but not yet dead.

*Wake up,* Pip thought at it. *Please.*

The stone did not answer. Not yet. But it shifted, the way a sleeper shifts when someone says their name across a room.

He smiled to himself, small and secret, and began to count.

* * *

Counting was how he kept the fear in its box.

Sefa had taught him that, though she'd never said it out loud. He'd watched her do it on bad crossings, when the pass was iced and the mules were screaming and half the crew wanted to turn back. She would stand at the head of the line and count aloud—stones, switchbacks, breaths—and by the time she reached some arbitrary number she'd chosen for no reason anyone could see, the worst of the panic had passed through her like weather.

So Pip counted the things the cell gave him.

Fourteen stones wide, eleven deep, if you measured by the old stones underneath rather than the new bricks laid over them. One door, iron-banded, with a slot for food and a grate for watching. One lamp, high in the wall, burning oil that smelled of the temple's cheap tallow. One pallet, one blanket thatched with straw that poked through, one bucket, one drain in the floor that whispered to itself when water ran somewhere far below.

And one neighbor.

He knew about the neighbor before he ever heard him, because the old stone told him. There was a hollow beyond the east wall—a second cell, close enough that their floors shared a foundation—and in that hollow was a heartbeat. A quick, anxious heartbeat, the kind that never quite slowed all the way down even in sleep.

On the fourth day—or maybe the fifth; the lamps never changed—the heartbeat's owner spoke.

"Pip?"

Pip's whole body went still. He knew that voice. Soft, apologetic, always half-swallowed, as if the speaker expected to be interrupted by someone more important. Brother Ossian. The timid scribe from the scriptorium who had once covered for Mirabel's arithmetic and blushed to the roots of his hair doing it. The man who had warned them, who had taken Mirabel's place in the inquisitors' net while smoke ate the archive behind him.

"Brother Ossian?" Pip pressed his palm flat against the east wall. "Is that you? Are you—are you *here*?"

"I'm here." A shaky laugh came through the stone, thin as paper. "Cell nineteen, I think. Or twenty. They don't tell you. I've been—I've been counting the meals to keep track. Eleven meals. So four or five days, depending on whether they feed us twice on holy days."

"They fed me six times."

"Oh." A pause. Pip could practically hear him recalculating, the way he used to watch Ossian recalculate ledger columns, lips moving. "Then either we're in different wings, or they're feeding you less because you're small, which is—" His voice went tight and angry, an anger so unaccustomed to itself that it sounded almost surprised. "That's not right. You're a child. Whatever else they think you are, you're a *child*, and there are rules, even in—"

"In prisons?" Pip asked.

"In *everywhere*," Ossian said fiercely, and then seemed embarrassed by his own volume, because his voice dropped back to its usual murmur. "I'm sorry. I'm not good at this. At talking. I'm good at copying things out and keeping my head down and I have done both for thirty years and look where it got me."

"Where did it get you?"

Another pause. When Ossian answered, his voice had gone quiet in a different way—not timid, but careful, like a man walking on ice.

"I did one brave thing, Pip. One. I stood up in front of Inquisitor Merek and lied, badly, to buy Mirabel time, and they took me the same hour. And do you know what I felt, sitting in the cart on the way here?" He laughed again, and this time the laugh wobbled. "Relief. Thirty years of keeping my head down, and the moment it was finally off my shoulders, I felt *light*. Isn't that a terrible thing?"

"No," Pip said. "I think it's true, though. That's how it works. The heavy part isn't the chains. It's deciding."

Silence from the other cell. Then, softly: "How old are you, Pip?"

"Nine. Nearly ten."

"And you know that, and I'm learning it at forty-six." A long exhale. "All right. All right. We're neighbors, then. If you need anything—if you hear anything—knock. Three times means danger. Two means... I don't know. Two can mean 'I'm still here.'"

"One means thank you," Pip said. "That's the smuggler code. Sefa taught me. Well—she taught everyone else. I just listened."

"Of course you did." Ossian's voice warmed. "You listen the way I read. All the way to the bottom of the page."

* * *

They came for Pip on the seventh meal.

Two guards, faces hidden behind smoked-glass visors—the inquisitors' answer to whatever it was they thought he was, some contagion of the old gods that could be caught through the eyes. They chained his wrists with iron wrapped in prayer-cloth, inked with verses that stung where they touched his skin, and marched him down corridors that the old stone sang about as he passed.

Because that was the thing. The deeper they took him, the more the old stone woke.

It wasn't the singing yet. Not the full song he'd heard at the wayshrine, the one that had poured into him like light through a cracked window. This was smaller. A hum here. A warmth there. Once, passing a junction where two ancient foundations crossed beneath the newer floor, Pip felt the stone *notice* him—a great slow attention turning toward him like a whale turning in dark water—and the guard's grip tightened because Pip gasped.

"What is it?" the guard demanded.

"The floor's warm," Pip said, which was true, and let them think it was nothing.

They brought him to a room with a table and a lamp and Inquisitor Dain Merek, who sat with a ledger open in front of him and a pen that never seemed to touch the page. Merek had a face like a well-kept account: balanced, precise, nothing wasted. He looked at Pip the way Mirabel had once described auditors looking at ledgers—as if the child were a column of numbers that refused to sum.

"You touched the wayshrine at Thornfell," Merek said. "You heard it sing. I want to know what it said."

"It said hello," Pip said.

Merek waited. Pip waited better. Nine-year-olds, Pip had discovered, were excellent at waiting, because their whole lives were waiting—for meals, for grown-ups to finish talking, for the world to make sense—and unlike grown-ups, they weren't embarrassed by it.

"The shrine is a relic of a suppressed cult," Merek said at last. "Suppressed relics are inert. Stone does not sing. Therefore either you're lying, or something is using you to lie. I haven't decided which interests me more." He turned the ledger around. On the page was a drawing—a spiral, broken in seven places. "This is the cipher found scratched into your cell wall. Except you've been in that cell five days, and the scratches are older than five days. Which means previous occupants left it. Do you know what it is?"

Pip looked at the spiral and his heart did something complicated, because he *did* know, or half-knew, the way you half-know a lullaby sung to you before you could talk. Seven breaks. Seven sleeps. The old gods hadn't died when the first king bound them—they'd been put to sleep, folded into the land itself, into standing stones and wayshrines and, apparently, the foundations of the temple's own dungeons. The spiral was a map of the sleeping. Every break was a place where the dream thinned.

"It's a game," Pip said. "Children play it. You draw the spiral and you take turns breaking it and the last one to break it loses."

Merek studied him for a long moment. Then he turned the ledger back and wrote something—one line, unhurried—and Pip understood with a chill that whatever he'd just given away, however small, had been entered into an account somewhere, and accounts in Vaelmoor had a way of coming due.

"Take him back," Merek said. And then, as the guards moved: "Boy. The stone doesn't love you. It uses whoever's nearest. Remember that, when it starts whispering."

But walking back down the corridor, chained and tired and scared in the ordinary human way, Pip smiled at the walls, because Merek had it exactly backwards. The stone didn't use him. It *asked*. That was the difference between the old gods and the temple, and Pip was nearly certain it was the most important difference in the world.

The old gods knocked. The temple picked locks.

* * *

That night—he chose to call it night, when the corridor lamps were trimmed low and the guards' footsteps grew lazy—Pip sat against the east wall and put his mouth close to the stone.

"Ossian?"

A rustle. "I'm here. Two knocks earlier—you knocked two. Are you all right?"

"I'm fine. Merek showed me a drawing." Pip traced the spiral on the wall with his finger as he spoke, and felt the old stone beneath shiver, faintly, like a dog hearing its name in another room. "Seven breaks. I think—I think each break is a place where one of the old gods is sleeping closest to the surface. And I think this dungeon is built on one of them."

"Why would the temple build a prison on—"

"Because it's strong," Pip said simply. "Old-god stone holds. Doesn't crack, doesn't shift, keeps the cells cold and the walls sound. The builders probably forgot why it worked so well. People forget." He thought of Mirabel saying that once, her voice bitter: *Forgetting is the temple's favorite miracle.* "But the stone remembers. And it's been listening to me."

Another silence. When Ossian spoke again, his voice had changed—still soft, but steadier, the tremor packed away somewhere.

"Pip. What are you planning?"

"Nothing yet. But I want to try something, and I want you to tell me if it's dangerous, because you know their rules better than anyone. Better than they do, maybe. You copied the sealed prayers, didn't you? Before?"

"Yes." A breath. "Yes, I did. For eleven years. I know what's in them. I know what the temple is afraid of, because I spent eleven years writing down its fears in beautiful script." A dry, wondering laugh. "Do you know, I never once thought of it that way until just now."

"Good," Pip said. "Then here's what I noticed. The prayers they seal—the ones that get answered—they're all addressed to gods with seven names. And the cipher has seven breaks. And when I touched the wayshrine, the song had seven parts, except only one part was awake enough to sing alone." He pressed his palm harder against the wall. "I think the breaks aren't wounds. I think they're *doors*. And doors open from the inside if somebody on the outside knows the knock."

"Pip." Ossian's voice dropped very low. "If Merek finds out you can wake even a fraction of—"

"He already knows I'm dangerous. That's why I'm in a cell instead of a grave. He wants to understand me before he spends me." Pip had learned that word from Sefa too, and it fit Merek like a glove. "So we have time. Not much. But some."

"And what would waking the stone even do? Open your door? The hinges are iron and the frame is blessed oak and—"

"Not the door." Pip closed his eyes. Beneath his hand, faint as a pulse in a wrist, the old stone answered his attention. Warmth. Attention. A vast patience that had been dreaming for three hundred years and was beginning, slowly, deliciously, to surface toward his voice like a swimmer toward light. "The drains. The water runs somewhere below us—did you know that? Old channels, older than the temple. If the stone wakes enough to remember its own shape..." He grinned in the dark, and it was Sefa's grin, borrowed and slightly too big for his face. "Then the drains remember where they go."

Silence. Then Ossian, sounding dazed: "You want to escape through the plumbing."

"I want to escape through the *memory of the plumbing*. There's a difference. The difference matters."

A laugh broke out of the other cell—startled, rusty, real—and Pip heard it catch and change into something wetter, and understood that Brother Ossian was crying, quietly, the way people cry when hope arrives after they'd stopped budgeting for it.

"I'm sorry," Ossian managed. "It's only—I've been counting meals, Pip. Eleven meals. I'd started making peace with the arithmetic. And now a nine-year-old is proposing sedition via drainage, and I find I—" He blew out a breath. "I find I want to live again. It's very inconvenient."

"One means thank you," Pip said, and knocked once on the wall.

After a moment, one knock came back.

* * *

He began that same night, one whisper at a time.

Not the song—not yet. The song was loud, and loud things brought guards, and guards brought Merek with his ledger and his patient pen. Instead Pip did what he did best: he listened, and then he repeated, softly, barely voiced, the smallest fragments the stone offered him. A word of the old tongue that meant *water remembers*. A phrase that meant *the door sleeps facing inward*. Little ciphers, traded back and forth between his mouth and the stone's slow mind, each one sinking into the walls like rain into drought-cracked earth.

By the third such night, the corner where he slept stayed warm after the corridor lamps went low.

By the fifth, the drain in his floor had stopped whispering to itself and begun, very faintly, to hum along.

And by the seventh, when Pip laid his whole palm against the east wall and breathed the oldest fragment he knew—the one that meant *wake gently, you are safe, you are safe, you are safe*—the stone beneath the entire cell block sighed.

It was not a sound the ears caught. It was a sound the bones caught. Somewhere far above, Pip knew, lamps flickered. Somewhere in the temple's grand accounting hall, a quill skipped. And somewhere in a tower room, Inquisitor Dain Merek looked up from his ledger with the expression of a man whose columns had just refused, for the first time in his career, to balance.

In the dark of cell nineteen-or-twenty, Ossian whispered, "What was that?"

Pip lay back on his warm corner of the floor, heart hammering, grinning at the ceiling.

"That," he said, "was the first knock from the inside."

And far below the cells, deeper than the drains, older than the temple, something vast turned over in its sleep and began, syllable by syllable, to remember its own name.
