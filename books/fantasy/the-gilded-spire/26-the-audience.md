## 26. The Audience

They took her chains off in the antechamber, which frightened Mirabel more than the chains had.

Chains meant a prisoner. No chains meant something the Spire wanted to present—polished, composed, disposable. Two guards in gilded half-armor flanked her, and between them walked Inquisitor Merek, his boots silent on marble that had been polished by four centuries of kneeling servants. He had not spoken since the exchange at the pass. He didn't need to. His silence had the texture of a ledger column waiting for its final figure.

The antechamber itself was an argument made in gold. Every surface gleamed—the work of men who believed that wealth was proof of divine favor, and that enough of it could blind even the gods. Mirabel had copied prayers about humility in a scriptorium that smelled of tallow and mouse droppings while this room drank the taxes of three provinces. She let herself feel the fury, because fury kept her spine straight.

"Rules," Merek said quietly, as they paused before doors tall enough to admit a siege engine. "You will speak when addressed. You will kneel when bidden. You will not look directly upon the crown."

"And if I do?"

"Then you will learn why we don't." He glanced at her, and there was something almost like respect in it, or perhaps only curiosity—a man studying a sum that refused to balance. "You are either the bravest woman I have arrested or the most foolish. The crown has eaten braver."

The doors opened.

* * *

The throne room of Vaelmoor was cold.

Not the honest cold of stone and draft, but a cold with intention behind it, like the air above an open grave. Banners hung motionless despite the high windows being open. Courtiers lined the walls at precise intervals, each one standing exactly where a chalk mark must have told them to stand, each one wearing the fixed smile of people who had learned that looking away was fatal and looking too long was worse.

Mirabel kept her eyes on the floor as she walked. It was a discipline she'd perfected over fifteen years: the scribe's walk, head bowed, hands quiet, a person shaped so thoroughly into unremarkability that eyes slid off her like rain off oiled cloth. She counted paces instead of faces. Forty-two steps from the door to the dais. She knew because she had memorized the floor plan Pip had drawn from memory after their flight through the tunnels, and because counting was what she did when terror needed somewhere to go.

"Kneel."

She knelt.

Silence stretched. Then footsteps—slow, uneven, the gait of a man whose body was being worn rather than inhabited—and a voice came from above her, resonant and wrong, like a bell struck underwater.

"You are the scribe."

It wasn't a question, but it demanded an answer. "I am, Your Majesty."

"The one who carries the old ink." A pause, long enough for her heart to stumble. "The Ledgered Flame writes everything down. Did you know that? Every prayer. Every debt. Every sin. It believes that what is written cannot be unwritten." The voice drifted closer, and she felt the temperature drop around her like a hand closing. "But you have been writing things it does not know about. That is an imbalance."

She kept her gaze on the marble. In its polished surface she could see a reflection, warped and wavering: a tall figure in ceremonial robes, and above it, a crown that seemed to drink the light rather than reflect it. She looked away from the reflection immediately—but not before she saw something else.

The figure's shadow lay wrong on the floor. It moved before he did.

"I asked you a question once already," the voice said, and now there was an edge of something beneath the resonance—impatience, maybe, or hunger. "Why did you come to my city?"

"To deliver a tax caravan, Your Majesty." The lie came smooth, worn soft from use. "And to settle accounts."

"Accounts." Something in the tone shifted—a flicker, a hitch, like a needle skipping on a groove. "Yes. Accounts. There is always—" The voice stopped. Started again, lower, rougher, a different voice entirely, cracked and human and exhausted beyond measure. "There is always another entry."

Mirabel's breath caught.

She knew that voice. Not the man—she'd never met King Edvard IV, no more than she'd met the moon—but she knew that *sound*. The sound of a person surfacing from deep water, gasping, disoriented, drowning in their own body. She'd heard it in Ossian's stammer when the inquisitors passed too close. She'd heard it in Sefa's crew-boy Tam, waking screaming from dreams of the pass where Merek's crossbows had found his shoulder.

The courtiers hadn't moved. Hadn't blinked. Either they hadn't noticed, or noticing was a skill they had amputated years ago.

"Your Majesty," she said carefully, "the temple sends its regards, and its ledgers."

"Ledgers." The word came out flat, mechanical. The monster again. "The Flame keeps the kingdom's books. The Flame has always kept them. Four hundred years of perfect balance." The footsteps circled her, slow. "And yet entries keep appearing that were never authorized. Prayers answered outside the system. Shrines waking without permit. A child who sings to stones." The voice bent low, near her ear, and the cold of it frosted her skin. "Where is the child, scribe?"

"With friends."

"Friends." A dry sound that might have been laughter in a healthier throat. "Everyone believes in friends until the audit comes."

And then—again—that skip. That terrible, beautiful hitch, like a lamp guttering back to life. When the voice came again it was quieter, frayed, and it said, with unmistakable urgency:

"*Count the candles.*"

Mirabel froze.

* * *

There were forty-one candles burning in the throne room. She knew because she'd counted them on the way in, out of habit, out of the scribe's compulsion to tally everything—forty-one candles in iron stands along the walls, plus the great flame at the altar behind the throne, which was not a candle but might as well have been, because it burned and it was counted and it was *entered*, everything entered, everything balanced—

Forty-one candles. But the throne room's official inventory, which she had copied twice in her years at the Temple of the Ledgered Flame, specified forty.

One extra candle. One unauthorized entry.

Her mind raced through the cipher the way Ossian had taught her, back in the scriptorium, when they'd been supposed to be copying psalms and had been trading forbidden games instead. Ledger-code: the secret shorthand of temple accountants, designed to hide discrepancies from casual readers, a language of debits disguised as devotions. *An extra candle* was a specific phrase in that code. It meant: *there is a discrepancy here that I am not permitted to name aloud.*

He was speaking to her in the only language the thing wearing him couldn't read.

She made herself breathe. Made herself say, in the bored tone of a functionary reciting inventory: "The throne room holds forty candles, Your Majesty, per the register of the reign's ninth year. If one burns unregistered, it should be reported to the Under-Chamberlain for correction."

A silence. Then, softly, in that other voice—the human one, buried deep, threaded through with something that shook with relief and terror in equal measure:

"Yes. Yes, precisely. Report it. Report everything." A breath. "You read the old notation. Who taught you?"

"A brother of the scriptorium, Your Majesty. Timid. Fond of margins."

"Ossian." The name came out like a wound reopening. "They took him. I heard them take him. I could do nothing. I can do *nothing*—" The voice fractured, and for one horrible moment Mirabel heard what lived underneath the king: not majesty, not power, just a man alone in a dark house with something vast wearing his skin, screaming silently for years.

Then the cold rushed back in, and the resonant voice resumed mid-thought, seamless, as if the interruption had never occurred: "—and so the imbalance must be corrected. You understand, scribe. All accounts must balance."

"Of course, Your Majesty."

"You will be held until the thirteenth turning. There is a rite. There is always a rite." The footsteps retreated toward the dais. "The Flame requires a vessel. The vessel requires preparation. You will find our hospitality adequate."

Mirabel bowed her head lower. And in the space between one heartbeat and the next, she took the risk she'd been weighing since the candles.

"Your Majesty," she said, "one matter remains outstanding. An old entry. From before the current administration."

The cold paused.

"There is a debt," she continued, keeping her voice level, scribe-flat, "incurred under the reign's second year, carried forward, never discharged. The holder of that debt wishes to know if it is still honored."

She was speaking of the queen. Of Aldric's whispered reports, of the quiet room behind the tapestries, of a woman who still played old songs on a lute in the dark and watched her husband's face for flickers. She was asking: *is there anything left in there that remembers her?*

The silence went on so long that Merek shifted behind her.

Then the human voice came back—and this time it didn't sound like a man surfacing. It sounded like a man using the last of his strength to hold a door shut against a flood, knowing the door would not hold, knowing the flood would win, and choosing, with everything that remained of him, how to spend his final moments upright.

"Honored," he said. "Tell her—" A shudder ran through the tall figure, visible even from Mirabel's lowered vantage; the shadow on the floor writhed, straining against its owner. "Tell her the songs still reach me. Tell her I hear them from very far away, down where it's dark, and they are the only—" The voice cracked like ice. "The only warm thing."

"Your Majesty," Merek said warningly.

"Don't save me."

The words dropped into the throne room like a stone into a well. Mirabel's head came up before she could stop herself—directly toward the crown—and what she saw in that instant seared itself into her forever: the king's face, gaunt and gray above the collar of state, and his eyes, which were for one single heartbeat *his own*—wet, terrified, lucid, pleading—and behind them, far back in the depths, something enormous turning its attention toward the surface like a leviathan rising toward light it had been denied.

"Don't save me," the king repeated, and now the resonance was flooding back in, drowning the man, but he fought it, he fought it with the last coins of himself, each word a separate act of will: "*Save what's left.*"

The eyes went flat.

The cold closed over the throne room like water over a drowned lantern. The figure straightened, robes settling, and when it spoke again the voice was whole and hollow and had never been anything else.

"The audience is concluded. Take the scribe below. See that she is comfortable. We have such plans for her, and the moon is nearly full."

Guards took her arms. As they turned her toward the doors, Mirabel looked back—one last glance, the kind of glance that gets people killed—and saw the king standing alone on his dais among his motionless courtiers, crowned and robed and utterly absent, a lit window in a house where everyone had died.

*Save what's left.*

She carried those three words out of the throne room like contraband, hidden in the same place she'd hidden stolen prayers and smuggler's routes and every other dangerous treasure of her double life: in the one vault no inquisitor had ever managed to search.

Her own stubborn, debt-marked, unforgivably hopeful heart.

* * *

They put her in a cell that was, insultingly, comfortable.

A real bed. A basin. A window—narrow, barred, but a window, set high in the Spire's western face, through which she could see the sky going amber over the capital. They wanted her healthy. They wanted her calm. A vessel, she thought, was probably like wine: you didn't pour it into the cup broken.

She sat on the bed and pressed her palm flat against her debt-mark, feeling the old ink warm beneath the skin, and she let herself shake. Just for a minute. Just long enough to honor the terror, the way Sefa had taught the crew to honor a storm—acknowledge it, then sail through it.

*Don't save me. Save what's left.*

What was left? A queen playing lullabies to a ghost. A knight in exile holding a gatehouse with smugglers and a boy with a wounded shoulder. A timid brother in a cell somewhere below her feet, who had traded his freedom for hers and never once complained. A god-touched child locked in these same depths, singing to stones that remembered being holy.

That was what was left. That was the kingdom, really—not the banners and the gold and the forty-one candles burning lies in a dead man's hall. The leftovers. The overlooked. The people the ledgers rounded down.

Mirabel had spent her whole life being rounded down. She knew exactly what they were worth.

She looked at the amber light fading in her high window, and she began, silently, in the ledger-code of timid brothers and desperate scribes, to compose her next entry.

Below her, faint as a heartbeat, she thought she could hear singing.

Someone—or something—was waking up the walls.
