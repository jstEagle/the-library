## 28. The Smuggler's Trick

The cell they gave her was a scribe's cell, which was the first mistake.

Mirabel knew it within an hour of the door closing. The Spire did not waste space; every chamber in this mountain had been built for a purpose, and a room with a writing ledge, an inkwell carved from black stone, and a stool of exactly the right height was not a prison. It was a workstation. Someone—Merek, or Ivenna, or the thing wearing the king's face—expected her to be useful. Expected her to copy. Expected her to sit down at that ledge and do what she had done every day for eleven years: put ink where she was told to put it.

She sat on the floor instead, back against the cold wall, and waited for the moon to come through the high slit of a window.

Because there was one thing about ink that no ledger-keeper in Vaelmoor seemed to remember, and it was this: ink was not obedience. Ink was a language, and languages could be spoken by anyone who learned them. The temple taught its scribes that marks on skin were chains. The temple had never once asked what the marks were *made of*.

Her debt-mark ran from the inside of her left wrist up past her elbow, a lattice of fine black lines like frost grown under the skin, each stroke a year of the Halloran debt, each flourish a penalty, each small crossbar a name of a clerk long dead who had signed her family into servitude. She had copied it onto herself at fourteen, guided by Ivenna's own hand, weeping, while the High Ledgermistress recited the words of binding in the old formal cadence.

And here was the thing Mirabel had understood only recently, only since the burn on her palm had started glowing, only since the old roads opened and the wayshrine sang: the cadence Ivenna used had not been the temple's. It had been older. Borrowed. A binding is a binding, whatever god it serves—and bindings have seams.

Sefa had taught her that, years ago, over a smuggler's fire in Thornfell, mending a torn sail by lantern light. *Every knot has a seam, Mira. That's how you know it was tied by hands. Hands get tired. Hands make choices.* Sefa was lying somewhere below the pass now, gut wound packed with moss and prayer, alive because Aldric had carried her three miles through snow. Mirabel turned the thought over like a coin she couldn't spend yet and made herself look at it plainly: Sefa had held the pass so that Mirabel could be taken, because Mirabel had walked out onto that road and offered herself for a child. Every knot has a seam. Every price has a payer. She intended to make sure this particular transaction did not clear.

Above her, through the window slit, the twelfth moon rose—waning, gibbous, one night from setting. Tomorrow the thirteenth would begin its turning. She had until then.

She got up, crossed to the inkwell, and dipped two fingers.

* * *

The first thing the ink did was hurt.

She had expected that. What she hadn't expected was the sound—a low harmonic hum, like a wet finger circling a glass rim, rising from her own arm as the fresh ink met the old mark. The debt-lattice woke. Lines she had carried for eleven years as dead weight suddenly had direction, had *flow*, and she could feel them the way you feel a river under ice: moving, patient, going somewhere.

Going *inward*. Toward her heart, she realized, and laughed—a short, ugly laugh in the empty cell—because of course. Of course the binding ran to the heart. That was where debts lived. That was where the temple had always collected.

But flow could be redirected. Any smuggler knew it: you didn't dam a river, you cut a channel. She pressed her inked fingers to the wall beside the door and drew—not a symbol she'd been taught, but a symbol she'd stolen, one of the three pages pulled burning from the scriptorium archive, one she had copied onto the inside of her own forearm in mirror-script where no auditor would think to look. The old gods' script did not describe things. It *instructed* them.

"Open," she whispered, in the cadence Ivenna had used at fourteen, the cadence of binding, reversed.

The lock did not open. The lock *dissolved*—the iron simply forgot, for the length of one heartbeat, that it had ever been a lock, and the door drifted ajar on hinges gone soft as wax. Beyond it, the corridor stood empty, lit by guttering flame-lamps that burned without flicker, the way everything burned in the Spire: too steady, too obedient, wrong.

Mirabel flexed her left hand. The debt-mark glowed faintly now, a network of dim silver threads, and where the glow touched the stone walls, hairline veins of older light answered—old-god stone, all through this mountain, sleeping in the bones of the building like seeds under snow. The crown-spirit had built its fortress on top of a graveyard of divinity and never noticed, because the spirit counted power and the stones held none. They held something else. They held *memory*.

"Right," she said softly, to no one, to herself, to Sefa lying bleeding in a smuggler's camp and Ossian sitting in some cell being brave for the first time in his life. "Let's go rob a temple."

* * *

She moved the way she moved through Thornfell at night: weight on the outer edge of the foot, breath timed to the lamps' slow pulse, eyes reading the corridor the way she read a page—for structure, for repetition, for the place where the pattern broke. Two turns brought her to a junction where a bored acolyte-guard stood picking at his fingernails with a stylus. She stopped in the shadow of an archway and considered him, and considered the strange new fact of her own visibility.

For eleven years her talent had been absence. People looked at her and their gaze slid off, a scribe-shaped nothing, a girl with ink stains and no opinions. But the debt-mark was awake now, and awake things were *seen*. She could feel it—the guard's attention snagging on her like cloth on a nail. Invisibility was spent. She had traded it, in the dark of the archive, for this glow, this hum, this terrible conspicuousness.

Fine. She'd never been invisible anyway. She'd been *unremarked*, which was different, and unremarked was a skill, and skills did not die just because the conditions changed. You didn't stop guiding caravans because the road flooded. You found the ford.

She let the guard see her.

He turned, startled, hand going to his baton—"You're meant to be in your cell"—and she was already talking, already moving, the patter flowing out of her smooth as smuggled silk: "Meant to be, yes, but the Ledgermistress wants the vessel inventory reconciled before the thirteenth turning, and she said bring the third-key ledger from the annex, and if I'm late she'll want to know why you delayed me, so." She held out her marked arm as proof, let the glow do its work. "Third-key ledger. Annex. Move."

It should not have worked. He was armed; she was a prisoner; the arithmetic was all wrong. But the Spire ran on ledgers, on the assumption that everyone in it was either obeying or being punished, and a glowing debt-mark issuing instructions fit neither category and therefore short-circuited him entirely. He stepped aside. He even pointed. "Left, then down the spiral."

"Blessings of the Flame," she said piously, and walked past him, and did not breathe properly again until the spiral swallowed her.

Down. Always down. The cells were beneath the accounting floors, because the Spire kept its prisoners the way it kept its records: filed, ordered, close at hand for reference. She passed doors with small brass plaques—numbers, not names; the Spire did not believe people deserved names—and counted under her breath the way Pip counted stars, and on the ninth door she heard it.

Singing. Very quiet. A child's voice doing something impossible to a lullaby, bending notes that had no bends, and around the doorframe, seeping through stone that had no business letting anything through, thin ribbons of gold-green light moved like weed in current.

"Pip," she breathed, and put her palm flat to the door.

The old script on her forearm warmed. She drew the opening-stroke, reversed Ivenna's cadence, and felt the binding in her skin reach out through her hand into the door like roots through soil. The iron sighed and went soft. Inside, a small figure sat up on a cot in a nest of blankets, eyes huge and luminous in the lamplight, and behind those eyes something ancient and delighted looked out and recognized her.

"You came back," Pip said. "The stones said you'd come back. They've been saying it all day, they're very chatty when you listen, nobody here listens, they're all counting."

"I'm here." Mirabel crossed the cell and gathered the child against her, one fierce squeeze, feeling the tremor in the small body that Pip would never admit to. "Are you hurt?"

"They asked me questions. I answered with songs. Merek got very angry." Pip's chin lifted, and there was Sefa in that gesture, Sefa in miniature, defiant on a burning road. "Songs aren't answers."

"No," Mirabel agreed gravely. "They're better." She glanced at the corridor, then back. "Can you keep doing it? The songs? Because I need these walls to forget they're walls."

Pip grinned, and it was not entirely a child's grin. "They're already forgetting."

* * *

Ossian was four cells down, and he was not singing.

He was sitting exactly as Mirabel had seen him sit for eleven years in the scriptorium: spine straight, hands folded, expression composed into a mildness so complete it functioned as armor. When her light spilled through his dissolving door he looked up, and his composure cracked all at once, like ice off a branch in spring.

"Mira." His voice came out ruined. "You shouldn't—I told them nothing, I want you to know, I told them nothing, but they were going to—there are procedures, there are *instruments*, and I'm not brave, I never was, I only—"

"You warned me." She knelt in front of him and took his folded hands, unfolded them, held them. Ink-stained fingers, bitten nails, a scribe's calluses matching her own. "You had every reason not to and you did anyway. That's the definition of brave, Ossian. I'm sorry it took me this long to say it."

He stared at her, and then, with the terrible dignity of a man trying not to weep in front of a child, said: "Your mark is glowing."

"It's been a strange week." She hauled him to his feet. He swayed; they had not fed him well. "Can you walk?"

"I can walk. I can also, if it comes to it, talk." A ghost of his old timid humor. "I've spent my whole life being overlooked, Mira. Apparently it's a family talent."

"It's a guild," she said. "We have oaths and everything. Come on."

* * *

The corridors above the cells were the problem.

Below, the old-god stone slept in patches, waking wherever Pip touched or sang, and the bindings dissolved like sugar in warm water. Above—up through the spiral, across the accounting floors, toward the servant tunnels the queen had described—they would pass into newer construction, crown-stone, spirit-fed, stone that had never known any god but the thing in the gilded dark. There the ink would not work. There they would need the other set of skills, the ones Mirabel had paid for in bruises and near-hangs across a decade of nights.

She stopped at the mouth of the spiral and laid it out for them the way she'd lay out a route for Sefa's caravan: quick, concrete, no sentiment. "Two guards at the record hall, but they rotate on the bell, and the bell is due. After the record hall there's a service stair behind the tax shelves—Ossian, you've dusted those shelves, tell me I'm right."

"Thursdays," he said faintly. "There's a gap behind the seventh shelf. I used to hide there during audits."

"You hid during audits for *years* and never once mentioned it?"

"It seemed impolite."

Pip made a sound that might have been a laugh or a hiccup. Mirabel found she was grinning, wild and exhausted, and let herself have it for exactly one second before the bell rang far above, bronze and toneless, and the rotation began.

"Now," she said. "Walk like you belong. That's the whole trick. Nobody stops anyone who walks like they belong—it's the hesitant ones they question. Eyes forward. Bored faces. We are three clerks carrying an urgent reconciliation, and we are *deeply* tedious."

They went.

And it worked, God help her, it worked, because every skill she owned finally had its hour. The guard at the record hall glanced up as they passed and Mirabel gave him a nod of pure bureaucratic weariness, the nod of a woman who had reconciled forty thousand entries and resented every one, and he looked away first. Ossian found the gap behind the seventh shelf without hesitating, and the service stair beyond it smelled of dust and lamp oil and eleven years of Thursdays. Pip hummed under their breath, barely audible, and where the humming touched the stairwell walls the old veins of light brightened, and twice a locked door ahead of them simply stood ajar before they reached it, as if the mountain were holding doors open for the child the way a courtier holds them for a king.

"The stones like me," Pip confided, climbing.

"The stones have excellent taste."

At the top of the service stair, through a grate, they saw it: the grand accounting hall, vast and candlelit, where clerks moved between towering shelves of contracts like ants in a cathedral of paper. And at the hall's far end, on a dais beneath the Ledgered Flame itself, stood a figure in grey-and-gold, still as a drawn line, head bowed over an open book.

Ivenna.

Mirabel's hand closed slowly into a fist. The debt-mark flared hot against her skin, answering something in her blood, and for one dangerous moment she imagined walking down there, into the light, and saying everything.

"Don't," Ossian whispered, reading her as easily as she read contracts. "Not yet. Not for anger. Anger spends badly, Mira. You taught me that—you just didn't know you were teaching it."

She exhaled. Turned away from the grate. "Anger keeps," she said. "Come on. The tunnels. We have a gatehouse to open and a knight to embarrass."

* * *

The servant tunnels took them east and down, narrow passages where the air tasted of snowmelt and the walls were oldest of all—pre-Spire stone, pre-crown stone, stone that remembered the mountain before any king claimed it. Here Pip stopped needing to sing quietly. The child walked with both palms brushing the rock, and the rock *answered*, light running ahead of them like dawn chasing night down a valley, and the tunnels widened as they went, as if the mountain were making room.

They were nearly at the tunnel mouth—cold wind, real wind, smelling of pine and freedom—when the light ahead of them stuttered.

Once. Twice. Then steadied, but changed: redder, warier, the color of a banked fire deciding whether to become a blaze. Pip stopped, head cocked, listening to something Mirabel couldn't hear.

"What is it?" Mirabel crouched to the child's level. "Pip. Talk to me."

"The stones are scared," Pip said slowly. "Not of us. For us." Small fingers gripped her sleeve. "Something's coming up the mountain road. A lot of somethings. And the gatehouse—" Pip's luminous eyes lifted to hers, wide and certain. "The gatehouse is singing too. Somebody's there. Somebody's holding it. He's loud. The stones say he's very loud and he won't sit down."

Mirabel rose, heart slamming, and looked out through the tunnel mouth at the night below the Spire—where, impossibly, absurdly, gloriously, torches were moving along the ramparts of the outer gatehouse in patterns no garrison would ever use, and a horn sounded three short blasts that every smuggler in Vaelmoor knew, because it wasn't a military signal at all.

It was Sefa's crew's signal for *the road is ours; come home*.

Aldric. Against orders, against arithmetic, against every sane calculation—he'd taken the gatehouse with a handful of wounded smugglers, on the last night of the twelfth moon.

"He bought us the door," she breathed. "Now we'd better go sell it back to them at a profit."

Behind them, deep in the Spire, a bell began to ring—not the rotation bell, not the audit bell, but the great alarm, the one that woke the whole mountain. Ahead, the thirteenth moon was rising over Thornfell's teeth, thin and white as a blade's edge.

Mirabel Halloran took her family's hands and ran toward the noise.
