## 29. Mooncount Twelve

The twelfth moon hung low and swollen over the Spire's eastern wall, so close to full that its light seemed less like illumination than like a wound in the sky. Aldric Vane crouched in the shadow of a fallen watchtower, three hundred paces from the gatehouse, and counted the men on the battlements.

Six. He knew four of them by name.

Sergeant Brannoc, who had once carried Aldric's shield when he took an arrow at Redford Bridge and had wept with relief when the wound proved shallow. Corpors Tam Weller, who owed his commission to Aldric's testimony before the promotion board. The twins, Hesk and Oren Marsh, who had been boys of fifteen when Aldric first drilled them in the courtyard at Caer Dun, teaching them how to hold a spear without trembling.

He had trained half the garrison himself, back when he was Knight-Commander of the Royal Guard, back when honor was a thing you could hold in your hand like a sword hilt and know its weight.

Now he crouched in smuggler's leathers with a stolen blade and planned to fight his way through men who would still call him *ser* if they saw his face.

"You're doing the thing again," Sefa Corrane said beside him. She was propped against the tower's broken masonry, her wounded side bound in layers of linen that had long since soaked through to rust-brown. Her color was wrong—gray beneath the tan—but her voice was steady as a ledger line. "The thing where you look at people like they're already dead."

"I'm counting exits."

"You're writing their eulogies." She shifted, winced, kept talking anyway because Sefa Corrane did not know how to stop talking any more than she knew how to follow an order. "There's a difference. I've buried enough crews to know."

Aldric didn't answer. Below them, in the rocks, the rest of the crew waited: six smugglers, two of them barely more than children themselves, armed with knives, crossbows, and the particular desperation of people who had burned every bridge behind them. Mirabel, Pip, and Ossian were already inside the mountain—had gone down through the servant tunnels two nights past, into the dark, toward whatever waited at the Spire's heart. The plan required time. Time required the gatehouse to stay closed. Closed required someone to hold it.

Someone, Sefa had said, looking directly at him with her bloodied bandages and her insolent, unbreakable eyes. *You were their commander. You know how they think.*

*I was,* he had said. *Past tense.*

*Past tense,* she'd agreed, *is exactly what makes you useful.*

"The moon sets in under two hours," Aldric said now. "When it clears the ridge, the thirteenth turning begins. Whatever Mirabel is doing down there, she'll need the garrison distracted. Confused. Looking outward instead of down."

"And your plan for accomplishing this miracle?"

"Walk up to the gate," Aldric said, "and knock."

Sefa stared at him. Then, slowly, painfully, she started to laugh—a dry, cracked sound that turned into coughing that bent her double. One of the younger smugglers moved to help her; she waved him off with a hand that shook only slightly.

"Knock," she wheezed. "Gods preserve me. I'm dying next to a madman."

"Madness is just arithmetic nobody else has done yet," Aldric said. "They won't open the gate for an army. They won't open it for a siege. But they'll open it for me—or at least hesitate long enough for the crew to take the wall. Brannoc served under me for eleven years. When he sees my face, he'll hesitate. And hesitation is a door."

"Hesitation is also a crossbow bolt," Sefa said. "In case your arithmetic missed that sum."

"It didn't." He looked at her, really looked—the gray lips, the hand pressed to her side, the stubborn spark that refused to gutter. "You should stay here. You can barely stand."

"I can lean dramatically," she said. "It's a skill. Besides—" and here her voice lost its edge of jest, went quiet and hard as river stone, "—my crew is going over that wall. I don't sit out. That's not how the oath works. You swore it too, knight. Lit fire, no one turned away. You don't get to leave anyone behind, including yourself."

Aldric looked up at the moon. It was beginning its slow descent toward the ridge, dragging the night after it. Somewhere beneath his feet, in corridors carved before the first kings, a scribe and a god-touched child and a timid man who had traded his freedom for theirs were walking toward a thing older than the kingdom.

"Very well," he said, and stood, and drew his sword.

* * *

The walk across the open ground was the longest hundred paces of Aldric Vane's life.

He did not hurry. Hurrying was for men who feared arrows, and fear showed, and soldiers could smell fear the way sharks smelled blood. So he walked at parade pace, spine straight, sword sheathed, hands empty and visible, straight up the switchback road to the Spire's outer gate as though he had every right to be there—as though he were still Knight-Commander of the Royal Guard and this were merely an inspection.

"HALT!" The challenge came from the wall. A crossbow cranked. "Name and business!"

"Sergeant Brannoc," Aldric called back, pitching his voice to carry, warm and clear in the cold air. "Eleven years you carried my shield. Have you forgotten my face already?"

Silence on the wall. He heard it spread like frost—the murmur passing from man to man, the name traveling, *Vane, it's Vane, the traitor Vane—*

Torches flared. A face appeared above the parapet: weathered, bearded, familiar as his own reflection. Brannoc stared down at him, and Aldric watched the war move through the sergeant's eyes—duty and memory tearing at each other like dogs.

"Ser Aldric." Brannoc's voice was careful. Flat. The voice of a man reading from a script because the script was all that stood between him and feeling. "You were exiled. Your return is forbidden by order of the Crown Magisters. Surrender your weapon and submit to arrest."

"My weapon stays mine," Aldric said. "But I'll submit to nothing, old friend, and we both know you don't want me to. Open the gate, Brannoc. What's coming tonight doesn't concern you. Let it pass."

"What's coming—" Brannoc leaned over the parapet, and now his voice cracked, the script abandoned. "What's coming is *you*, ser. With smugglers. Armed. On the night the Magisters sealed the mountain. Do you know what they told us? Traitors and god-cultists, come to desecrate the Spire. They said you'd lead them." His jaw worked. "I said no. I said, not Ser Vane. Not him. I *vouched* for you."

And there it was—the knife Aldric had known would come, slid between ribs by no enemy but by his own reflection in a loyal man's eyes.

"I know," Aldric said quietly. "I'm sorry, Brannoc. I vouched for the king once, too. We were both wrong."

"Open fire!" a new voice shouted from the inner wall—one of the Magisters' own men, a captain in black and silver whose name Aldric had never bothered learning. "Kill the traitor!"

"No one fires!" Brannoc roared back, wheeling on the captain, and for one suspended heartbeat the gatehouse garrison stood pointed at itself, crossbows wavering between two commands—and that heartbeat was all Aldric needed.

He whistled, sharp and short.

The crew came over the wall like smoke.

Grappling lines that Sefa's people had spent a day rigging sang taut; shadows poured up the rock face on the gatehouse's blind side, moving with the silent competence of people who had spent their lives going where walls said no. The first sentry never made a sound. The second managed half a shout before a smuggler's arm locked around his throat. Crossbows clattered onto stone. By the time the black-clad captain had gathered his wits, there were eight armed strangers on his battlements and his own garrison standing between them, uncertain, weapons half-raised and pointing nowhere.

"Stand down," Aldric said, loud enough for everyone. "All of you. This ends one of two ways, and only one of them lets you see morning."

"Traitor!" the captain spat. He drew his sword, and six royal soldiers found themselves caught between his order and their own commander's silence. "Brannoc! Arrest him, or share his rope!"

Brannoc looked at Aldric. Looked at the captain. Looked at the moon, sliding inexorably toward the ridge, and at the strange, still quiet that had settled over the mountain—the same quiet, Aldric realized, that Pip's songs left behind them, the hush of old stone remembering.

Then Sergeant Brannoc of the Royal Guard did the most soldierly thing Aldric had ever seen him do.

He grounded his spear. Butt on stone, point to the sky. The oldest signal in the army's book: *I yield this ground.*

"Brannoc, you *dog*—" the captain began, and got no further, because Corporal Tam Weller grounded his spear too. Then the Marsh twins. Then another, and another, a soft percussion of wood on stone rolling down the battlements like rain beginning, until only the captain and his five Magister guardsmen stood rigid in a forest of surrendered arms.

"You always said," Brannoc said to Aldric, and his voice was rough, and his eyes were wet, and he did not care who saw it, "that a soldier's oath was to the kingdom, not the man wearing it. Eleven years, ser, you hammered that into us. So which of us is the traitor tonight? The man holding the gate for the crown's pet magicians? Or the man holding it for the kingdom?"

Aldric's throat was tight. "Neither," he said. "That's the lesson, Brannoc. There are no traitors in this yard tonight. Only men choosing whom to serve. Choose well."

The captain snarled and lunged—not at Aldric, but at the nearest surrendered soldier, at Brannoc, sword rising in pure spite—and Aldric moved without deciding to, the way he had moved his whole life, body answering duty faster than thought. Steel rang. Three passes, fast and brutal, and then the captain's sword spun away over the parapet and Aldric's point rested against the man's throat, trembling, hungry for the kill.

Everyone watched. The captain closed his eyes.

Aldric lowered the blade.

"Bind them," he said hoarsely. "The Magisters' men. Gag them, lock them in the guardroom. No wounds. No deaths." He turned to his former soldiers, standing among the smugglers who had expected to fight them, and found he could not make the words grand. They came out plain, worn smooth like stones carried a long way. "I spent my whole life believing honor was obedience. That if I held the line, held the oath, held the *man*, I was a good soldier. Tonight I learned the line was drawn around the wrong thing." He sheathed his sword. "Hold the gate. Not for me. For whoever walks out of that mountain at dawn."

Brannoc saluted—the old salute, fist to breastplate, the one they hadn't used since Aldric's disgrace—and thirty voices answered with the crash of spears grounding in unison, and the sound rolled off the mountain like thunder giving way.

* * *

Later, when the gatehouse was secured and the moon had touched the ridge, Aldric climbed to the highest point of the wall and looked east.

Sefa came to stand beside him, moving carefully, one hand pressed to her bandaged side. She said nothing for a long while. Below them, smugglers and royal soldiers shared a watchfire with the wary, exhausted camaraderie of men who had expected to die fighting each other and had been spared the trouble.

"They'll come," Aldric said finally. "The Magisters. Reinforcements from the capital. Maybe worse than reinforcements." He touched the hilt of his sword. "We bought hours. Perhaps a day. No more."

"Hours are currency," Sefa said. "Ask any smuggler. Spend them right and they're worth a fortune." She followed his gaze to the setting moon, its light thinning now, thinning, the sky behind it going the deep bruised violet of approaching dawn. "One more night after this one. Then the thirteenth turning."

"Then whatever Mirabel means to do, she does it tomorrow."

"Aye." Sefa was quiet a moment. "She never told you, did she. What the vessel has to be."

Aldric went still. "No. Did she tell you?"

"She told me nothing. That's how I know." Sefa's laugh was soft and bitter. "People about to live tell you everything. People about to pay a bill go quiet and start saying goodbye in small ways. Giving away their rations. Mending other folks' cloaks." She looked up at him. "She's been doing it for a month, knight. Haven't you noticed?"

He had. He had noticed and told himself it was nothing, told himself a dozen comfortable lies, because noticing fully would have meant acting, and acting would have meant admitting what the stolen pages said, what *vessel* meant, what a willing carrier's life became.

"She'll try to shut us out," Aldric said. "Tomorrow. She'll try to carry it alone."

"Then we'll be very rude about it," Sefa said. "Found family doesn't mean much if you let it martyr itself politely." She stuck out her hand, gray-lipped and steady. "Watch the gate with me till sunrise, knight?"

Aldric took it. Her grip was cold and strong.

Below them, far beneath the mountain's roots, something vast shifted in its sleep—and every torch in the gatehouse guttered at once, all together, like lungs drawing a single breath.

The twelfth moon slipped below the ridge.

The thirteenth night had begun.
