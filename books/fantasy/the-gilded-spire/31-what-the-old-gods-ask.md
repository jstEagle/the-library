## 31. What the Old Gods Ask

We have been called many things.

In the first years of the world we were called by no name at all, because there was no one to call us—only the mountain and the rain and the slow green patience of things that grow without being asked. Then came the shepherds, and they named us for what they needed: the God of the Thaw so the lambs would come, the Lady of the Ledgered Flame's opposite, the Quiet Brother who kept the roads, the Nine Who Sing Beneath. We answered because answering was our nature, the way water answers a channel cut into stone. A prayer spoken truly is a door, and we have never been able to leave a door unopened.

Then the first king bound us. He did not kill us—nothing can kill what was here before the killing was invented—but he folded us small, each god into a stone, each stone into the roots of the Spire, and he took our power the way a man takes milk from a goat, patiently, twice a day, forever. He made a thing to hold it. The thing wore his crown. The thing has worn every crown since.

We have watched from under the mountain for nine hundred years. We have felt every miracle siphoned away mid-answer, every prayer snuffed like a lamp pinched between wet fingers. We have heard the temple teach that we are dead, that we were never real, that the old gods are stories told by smugglers and hill-wives, and we have lain in the dark beneath Thornfell and thought: *let them believe it. Belief that costs nothing is worth nothing.*

But now the thirteenth moon is rising, and the stones are cracking, and we are waking, and we are hungry in a way we have not permitted ourselves to be hungry in nine centuries.

And there is a woman standing in the heart of the Spire with something ancient written on her skin, and she is refusing us.

* * *

Understand how we see her.

You see a woman: brown-haired, ink-stained, twenty-six years old, tired in the marrow. You see the debt-mark curling up her left arm like black ivy, the fresh burn across her palm glowing faintly gold, the scribe's calluses, the smuggler's flat watchful eyes.

We see more. We see the shape of her soul the way you might see the shape of a key held up to candlelight—not its ornament but its function, the thing it was made to open. Mirabel Halloran is a key. She has been a key all her life; she simply was never told what lock. Every year of copying prayers, every night of guiding wagons through passes that should not exist, every lie told beautifully to an inquisitor, every coin hidden in a hem—these were not detours. They were the cutting of teeth. The mountain does not make keys quickly.

She stands now in the Spire's innermost chamber, the room the first king built to hold us, though he built it to look like a treasury and smell like one too, all gilt and cold marble, because kings believe that greed is the best disguise for fear. Around her: the knight Aldric Vane with his sword drawn and his honor hanging off him in tatters he has stopped trying to mend. The child Pip, whose light we know better than their own mother knew it, singing low and steady the lullaby that makes walls remember they were once hillsides. The wounded smugglers above, holding a gatehouse against men who were once their countrymen. And below, in the deep places, the crown-spirit thrashing in its dying cage, the old king flickering inside it like a man knocking on the inside of a coffin lid.

The thirteenth moon is four hours from full. The chamber's windows—there should be no windows this far down, but the Spire has always grown strange architecture when it pleases—show a sky going the color of a bruise healing.

Mirabel has just learned the truth. The knight told her, or perhaps she read it in the stolen pages, or perhaps—this is likeliest—she knew already, the way she knows which pass will flood and which prayer will be answered. The spirit cannot be destroyed. It can only be carried. And the vessel's life becomes its chain.

She has said yes. She has not yet said it aloud, but we hear the yes forming in her the way you hear a storm forming over the sea, pressure dropping, birds going quiet. She has done the arithmetic. She is very good at arithmetic; they trained her for it from the age of four. One life against a kingdom. One debt against ten million. She has decided to pay.

This is where we must speak to her. This is where we must ask her to stop.

* * *

We do not speak as you speak. When we come to a mortal, we come as everything at once—the thaw and the frost, the road and the rockfall, the lamb and the wolf that takes it. Mortals have described our voices as cold water, as singing, as the sound a forest makes when it decides something. Pip once tried to explain us to Sefa Corrane and gave up after a while and said only, *they're loud, but kind of polite about it,* which is as accurate a description as any priest has managed in nine hundred years.

So we gather ourselves out of the cracked stones of the Spire's foundations, out of the wayshrine in Thornfell that a boy relit, out of every sleeping relic in the hidden chapel, and we pour ourselves gently into the chamber, and we say:

*Mirabel Halloran.*

She does not flinch. We note this with approval. Most mortals fall down. Aldric Vane falls down, actually, one knee cracking against marble, because he is a soldier and soldiers know when something larger than themselves has entered the room. Pip turns toward us with a smile like sunrise through fog. But Mirabel only lifts her chin and looks into the place where our voice comes from, and says, "I'm listening."

Good. Listen, then.

*We have watched your kingdom for nine hundred years,* we tell her, and the chamber fills with the smell of rain on hot stone, of spring thaw, of woodsmoke and turned earth. *We watched the first king bind us. We watched him build the thing in the crown. We watched the temple rise around the theft like scar tissue around a splinter. We watched them burn the prayers. We watched them burn the pray-ers. We watched a woman last winter—a washerwoman, dying of the lung-rot, praying to a god her grandmother half-remembered—and we reached for her, because reaching is what we are, and the crown-spirit ate the answer before it touched her, and they burned her alive in the courtyard for the crime of having asked.*

Mirabel's jaw tightens. She was there. We know she was there. That is partly why we chose this moment.

*We have waited a long time to be able to offer anyone anything,* we say. *Here is what we offer.*

The chamber changes. The gilt peels back like bark off a dead tree, and beneath it the Spire shows its true face: living stone, veined with light, breathing. And beyond the windows—we let her see beyond the windows—the whole of Vaelmoor spreads out under the rising moon, every city, every farm, every road, every temple, rendered in perfect miniature the way we see it, the way we have always seen it, with terrible loving clarity.

*Burn it,* we say.

The word lands in the chamber like a dropped bell.

*Not cruelly. Not wastefully. We are not the fire the temple taught you to fear—we are the fire that clears the field so the green can return. Let the crown-spirit starve. Let the temple's ledgers rot unread. Let the kingdom go back to the mud it crawled out of, and in a hundred years, two hundred, there will be villages again, honest ones, shrines again, true ones, prayers again, answered ones. No more hollow kings. No more children sold into debt. No more Ivennas balancing the accounts of miracles devoured. A clean page, Mirabel Halloran. You of all people understand the mercy of a clean page.*

We show her what we mean, because showing is kinder than telling. We show her the Thornfell valleys green past any memory, the wayshrines lit on every hill like a constellation brought down to earth. We show her children born free, learning songs instead of sums. We show her the old gods walking openly again, tending the world the way shepherds tend flocks, and we confess to her—for we are honest, even now, especially now—that part of what we show her is our own longing. Nine hundred years under the mountain. Nine hundred years of being eaten. We want our world back. We want it so badly that we have dressed the wanting in justice, and we are not entirely sure where one ends and the other begins.

*You would not need to carry it,* we say softly, and here our voice goes gentle as snowfall, because this is the knife of the offer and we will not pretend otherwise. *The vessel dies with the binding. But if the kingdom burns, the binding burns with it. Everything ends together. No one pays alone. Is that not fairer? Is that not cleaner than one woman chained forever to a starving god?*

* * *

The silence afterward lasts eleven seconds. We count. We count everything; it is a habit we caught from the mortals, or they from us, it hardly matters anymore.

Aldric Vane gets up off his knees. He looks at Mirabel, then at the vision of the burning-clean kingdom, and his face does something complicated—because part of him wants it, we can see the wanting, the same wanting we feel, the soldier's fantasy of burning the corrupt institution to its foundations and starting over. He has lost his command, his brothers-in-arms, his name. Fire has a certain appeal to men who have lost things to institutions.

Pip stops singing. The child looks up at us with those old-god eyes, and says, "That's not what the song says," and keeps singing.

And Mirabel Halloran walks to the window—the impossible window, the one looking out over all of Vaelmoor—and she puts her burned hand flat against the glass, and she looks at the miniature kingdom with the same expression she wears over a ledger: total, merciless attention.

"Show me the roads," she says.

We show her the roads. Every cart track and pilgrim path and smuggler's cut, glowing faintly now as the old network wakes, the veins of the world remembering their pulse.

"Show me the people on the roads tonight."

Ah. There it is. We could refuse—we are old enough and proud enough to refuse—but we promised honesty, and honesty is a debt we intend to pay. So we show her: the night caravans, the late walkers, the inn at Threeford with its lamps still lit, the shepherd on the high pasture counting his flock by moonlight, the temple novices asleep in their cells who are guilty of nothing but obedience, the washerwomen, the tax clerks, the children. Ten million small flames, none of them asking to be put out for the sake of the page they're written on.

"You're doing the thing they did," Mirabel says quietly. "Ivenna. The temple. All of them." She turns from the window. Her eyes are dry, which somehow is worse than tears. "They look at people and see numbers. Entries. Costs that can be written off. You're looking at a kingdom and seeing a ledger that needs balancing, and fire is just your version of red ink." She holds up her marked arm, the black ivy of the debt-mark catching the moonlight. "I've spent my whole life being a line item. Being the cost someone else calculated. I know exactly what it feels like when the arithmetic doesn't include you. So no."

*The kingdom is rotten—*

"The kingdom is *people.*" Her voice cracks, just once, and she pushes through it. "Rotten institutions, rotten ledgers, rotten crowns—burn all of that, gladly, I'll hold the torch. But you don't get to burn the people to punish the system. They didn't sign the contract. Most of them don't even know there *is* a contract." She steps closer to the center of the chamber, closer to us, and we feel her the way a riverbed feels the flood—small, mortal, and absolutely immovable. "Justice that serves the dead pages instead of the living is just cruelty with better handwriting. I've copied enough handwriting to know."

*It will cost you everything,* we warn her, and we do not soften it, because she deserves the truth at full weight. *The binding will hurt in ways we cannot describe and you cannot yet imagine. It will not end. The thing in the crown is nine hundred years of stolen divinity and grief, and it will live in your skin, and some nights it will scream, and some nights—worse—it will make sense. You will never be invisible again. Every inquisitor, every magister, every god-touched child within a hundred miles will feel you coming. You are trading one debt-mark for another, except this one cannot be forgiven, because you will be the creditor and the debtor both.*

"I know," Mirabel Halloran says.

*You cannot know.*

"I've been carrying something in my skin since I was fourteen." She touches the debt-mark, almost tenderly, the way you'd touch an old scar. "It's heavier than you think and lighter than you'd believe. I can carry one more thing. What I can't carry—" and here her voice finally breaks properly, and Aldric moves toward her and she waves him off, "—what I can't carry is Pip growing up in ashes. Or Sefa dying for nothing. Or Ossian in a cell somewhere knowing I chose the clean page over the people on it. You're offering me a way to not be a vessel. I'm telling you I'd rather be a vessel than a coward with good reasons."

* * *

We are quiet for a long time.

Mortals think gods are offended by refusal. Mortals are wrong. We have been refused before—rarely, memorably—and each time it has been the finest thing we witnessed in that age. A god can be worshipped by millions and learn nothing. But one woman standing in a gilded room, refusing paradise because paradise was priced in other people's blood—that teaches us something no hymn ever has.

Still. We are old, and we are frightened—yes, gods frighten too, we frighten most of all when we are waking after nine hundred years in the dark—and so we try once more, the way a merchant tries one final price:

*There will be no second offer. If you bind it, you bind it until you die, and you do not know when you will die, and neither do we. The chain is permanent. The whispers are permanent. Are you certain, Mirabel Halloran, daughter of a burned farm, scribe of the Ledgered Flame, guide of roads that should not exist?*

She smiles. It is not a happy smile. It is the smile of a woman who has just finished a long division and gotten a whole number, ugly but exact.

"No," she says. "I'm not certain. I'm certain about maybe three things in my whole life, and this isn't one of them. But certainty is just another word the powerful use. What I am is *willing.* Turns out that's the only currency that matters tonight." She rolls up her sleeve, and the debt-mark gleams, and beneath it, faintly, the burn from the stolen pages glows gold. "Besides. You said it yourself—you can't destroy it, only carry it. Well. I've been carrying debts since before I could write. Consider this the last entry."

Above them, muffled through miles of stone, the twelfth moon begins to set, and somewhere on the gatehouse walls Aldric's former brothers-in-arms are advancing, and somewhere in the cells below Pip's whispered songs are waking stones older than kingdoms, and somewhere in the deep of the Spire the crown-spirit feels the old gods gathered close and screams against its cage with the fury of a thing that remembers being worshipped.

The thirteenth moon is three hours from full.

We withdraw—not in anger, never in anger, but the way a tide withdraws to let a ship leave harbor—and as we go we give her the only gift left to give: we open every old road in Vaelmoor at once, all of them, the whole sleeping network blazing awake beneath the kingdom like veins of dawn, so that whatever happens in the next three hours, help can reach the Spire, and word can flee from it, and no door in this mountain will stay closed against the people coming through it.

And we leave her one sentence, pressed into the gold glow of her burned hand like a seal into wax, to be read when she needs it most:

*You were never a line item, Mirabel Halloran. You were the margin note that saves the page.*

Below, the crown-spirit begins to sing back at Pip's lullaby—a broken, hungry, nine-hundred-year-old song—and the thirteenth moon clears the peaks, and the unbinding begins.
