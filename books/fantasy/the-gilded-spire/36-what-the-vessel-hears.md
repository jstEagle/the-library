## 36. What the Vessel Hears

The spirit had opinions about breakfast.

*That bread is stale,* it said, the moment Mirabel bit into her morning heel of loaf at Sefa's fire. *You are chewing on disappointment. I ate better offerings in the age before your kind learned to count.*

"Then you're welcome to starve," she said around the mouthful. "I've eaten worse. I've eaten worse on purpose, when the alternative was nothing."

*I was a god,* it said, with the weary grandeur of a nobleman forced to dine in a stable. *I received the first fruits of empires. Virgins wept with joy at my altars. Now I am carried about in a smuggler's skin like coin in a cutpurse's boot.*

"You're not even a whole god anymore. You're what's left after the temple skimmed you for four hundred years." She swallowed. "Which makes us even, because I'm what's left after the temple skimmed me too."

A pause. She had learned to feel its pauses now, the way a sailor feels a shift in wind—a pressure against the inside of her thoughts, a settling of weight behind her eyes.

*That,* it said finally, *was almost clever.*

"I have my moments."

The fire crackled. Somewhere beyond the camp, Pip was teaching Ossian—released, hollow-eyed, slowly mending—to whistle a three-note phrase that made the nearest wayshrine stones hum in sympathy. Aldric was sharpening a blade he no longer needed to sharpen, because his hands didn't know what to do when they were empty. Sefa sat propped against a wagon wheel with her bandaged side and her ledger of routes open on her knee, pretending not to listen to Mirabel talking to herself.

They all pretended not to listen. It was a kindness, and like most kindnesses in this company, it was also a fiction everyone agreed upon.

*Your captain watches you,* the spirit observed. *She wonders if I will take you. If one morning you will simply stop being Mirabel Halloran and become only a door that something walks through.*

"Sefa worries about everything. It's how she stays alive."

*And do you wonder?*

Mirabel turned the question over the way she'd once turned over coins to check for shaving—by feel, by weight, without letting it see her face. "Every day," she said quietly. "But wondering isn't the same as fearing."

*No,* the spirit said, and there was something in its voice she couldn't name—something old and tired and almost gentle. *It is not.*

* * *

The negotiations had begun in earnest three weeks after the binding, on the road south from the Spire, when Mirabel woke at midnight to find the spirit attempting to move her left hand.

It wasn't violent. That was the unsettling part. Her fingers simply flexed, slow and curious, like a child testing a glove. She watched them move in the moonlight with a detachment that would have frightened her more if she hadn't already been so thoroughly frightened by everything else.

"No," she said.

*Yes,* it said, and moved her thumb in a small circle.

"I saved your existence, such as it is. You were going to be unmade with the king. I gave you a roof. That makes this my house, and in my house, we don't drive the cart."

*I was bound by kings older than your kingdom's founding. Do you imagine your smuggler's rules will hold me?*

"I don't imagine anything," Mirabel said. "I count."

She sat up, wrapped her blanket around her shoulders, and did what she did best: she laid out terms.

"Here's how it works. You get to talk whenever you want—I can't stop you anyway, and honestly, four hundred years alone in a crown sounds like hell, so consider conversation part of your board. You get to see everything I see, hear everything I hear. Also unavoidable. In exchange: no moving my body. Not a finger. Not while I sleep, not while I dream, not ever. And no pushing at my thoughts when I'm working. I guide roads for a living. If you make me step off a cliff because you're bored, we both die, and you go back to being nothing. Is that the trade you want?"

Silence. Long enough that she began counting her own heartbeats to measure it.

*You negotiate like a woman who has been sold before,* it said at last.

"I was sold at fourteen. It taught me the value of things." She pulled the blanket tighter. "So. Terms?"

*Terms,* it agreed, and she felt something settle into place—not a chain, exactly, but an understanding. A contract written in neither ink nor blood but in the plain arithmetic of two creatures who needed each other and knew it.

*One amendment,* it added. *When you pray—and you do pray, little scribe, even now, even after everything—you will pray aloud. I wish to hear what you ask for.*

"Why?"

*Because I was a god who heard ten thousand prayers a day, and then I heard none for four centuries, and I find I miss it.* A pause. *Also, some of your prayers are insulting, and I enjoy them.*

Mirabel laughed—a short, surprised bark that woke Pip, who mumbled something about shrines and went back to sleep.

"Deal," she said.

* * *

The cruel whispers came mostly at night, and mostly when she was weakest.

*Your mother sold you for less than a season's grain,* it murmured once, as she lay staring at the stars through a gap in the canvas. *Do you hate her?*

"She was starving. The temple set the price. I hate the ledger, not the hand that signed it."

*A convenient distinction. Cowards make it often.*

"Probably." She rolled onto her side. "Doesn't make it wrong."

*Your knight loves you. He will not say it. He counts his words like a miser counts coin, and every one he doesn't spend on you is a coin he hoards out of fear.*

"Aldric and I are colleagues."

*You slept with your back against his back for six nights on the Spire road. Soldiers do that with people they trust. Lovers do it with people they—*

"Goodnight," Mirabel said firmly.

*He dreams about the day you took the spirit into yourself. In his dream he reaches you one breath sooner, and you never have to carry me at all, and in the dream he wakes up weeping every time, and he tells no one, and—*

"*Goodnight.*"

But she lay awake a long while after that, listening to Aldric's breathing two fires away, and hating the spirit, and being grateful to it, and understanding that both of those things could be true at once—the way a debt and its payment could live on the same page.

Other nights it was wise, and that was worse, because wisdom from the thing wearing her skin felt like theft.

*You flinch when children look at your mark,* it said, watching through her eyes as a farm girl stared at the black filigree crawling up Mirabel's wrist. *Why? You saved them all. Their queen owes you their peace.*

"Children look at monsters. I know what I am."

*You are not a monster. I am a monster. You are a woman doing arithmetic with her life, which is different, though I grant the difference is subtle at a distance.* A pause. *Also, the child is staring because your mark glows when I am pleased, and I am pleased, because she has mud on her face and it reminds me of offerings children used to bring me—clay figures, badly made, given with total seriousness. I had forgotten that. You let me remember things. I had not expected that.*

"What things?"

*Everything. Your world is so small and so loud. Bread and blisters and arguments about whose turn it is to cook. When I wore the crown, I saw only power—prayers rising like smoke, souls tallied, hunger measured in kingdoms. Down here I see what the prayers were actually for. A mother asking for her son's fever to break. A farmer asking for rain. They were never praying to a god, little scribe. They were praying to someone to notice.* Its voice went quiet. *I devoured ten thousand years of that longing and never once understood it until I rode in the skin of a woman who copies other people's prayers for a living.*

Mirabel lay very still.

"That," she said finally, "is the saddest thing anyone has ever said to me."

*I contain multitudes of sadness. It comes with the territory.*

* * *

By midwinter they had worked out a system, and like all systems Mirabel built, it looked like chaos and ran like clockwork.

The spirit got its spoken prayers. It got first refusal on any song Pip sang near her (it hated most of them; it loved the lullabies with a ferocity that embarrassed them both). It got to comment on everything, endlessly, and Mirabel got to tell it to shut up, endlessly, and somehow between those two pressures they had carved out something that was almost companionship, if companionship could exist between a woman and the ancient parasite chained to her soul.

In return, Mirabel got counsel. Unwanted, unsentimental, occasionally monstrous counsel—but counsel nonetheless.

It caught a cheat at cards by reading the man's pulse through her palm. (*His heart stutters when he looks at the queen card. Take him for everything.*) It remembered the old roads from the inside, from the time before they were closed, and its directions had gotten Sefa's crew through two passes the maps called dead. (*Turn north where the stone leans. The mountain remembers shortcuts your kind has forgotten.*) And twice—only twice—it had warned her of danger with a flat, cold certainty that brooked no argument: *Riders on the ridge. Not soldiers. Worse. Move now.*

Both times, she'd moved. Both times, she'd lived.

"You realize," Sefa said one evening, watching Mirabel argue with empty air about the best route through the Greywater ford, "that you've domesticated it."

"I haven't domesticated anything. We've come to a commercial arrangement."

"You haggle with it over dinner."

"It haggles first. I'm defending my interests."

"You named it."

"I have not named it."

"You called it 'your majesty' yesterday. Sarcastically, but still. That's a name. That's a relationship."

Mirabel opened her mouth to object and found, to her horror, that she had nothing. The spirit, inside her, radiated smugness like a stove radiates heat.

*Tell your captain,* it said, *that sarcasm is the highest form of prayer.*

* * *

On the last night of winter, camped in the shadow of the Thornfell wayshrine—Pip's shrine now, lit and tended, a beacon for every traveler on the lawless roads—Mirabel sat alone at the fire and did the thing she had promised.

She prayed aloud.

It was a strange prayer, halting, addressed to no one in particular—or perhaps to everyone: the waking gods in their stones, the dying ember of divinity behind her ribs, whatever else was listening in the dark between stars.

"I don't want much," she said. "I never learned how to want much. I want the crew safe through spring. I want Ossian to stop flinching at bells. I want—" She stopped. Started again. "I want to believe the mark doesn't make me less. I want to walk into a town and have a child look at me and see a person who guides roads instead of a warning. And I want—" her voice dropped, went rough, "—I want the ones we lost to have meant something. Sefa's side will heal, but the others won't come back, and I keep the ledger of them, I keep it every night, and the numbers don't balance, they'll never balance, and I need someone to tell me that's allowed. That you can save a thing and still grieve what it cost."

The fire popped. The wayshrine hummed its low, patient note.

And the spirit—the ancient, devouring, half-erased thing that had worn a crown for four hundred years and a smuggler for one—said, quietly:

*Allowed. Grief is the interest paid on love. Any ledger that shows no grief has simply recorded no love, and I would rather carry yours than the clean books of the temple that starved me.*

Mirabel blinked hard at the fire.

"That was almost kind."

*Don't tell anyone. I have a reputation.*

She laughed, wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, and was still laughing when the wayshrine's hum changed pitch—deepened, quickened, became something almost like speech.

Pip came running from the shrine with wide eyes and light leaking between their fingers where they'd touched the stone.

"Mira," the child breathed. "The gods are talking. All of them at once. And they're saying—" Pip swallowed. "They're saying someone is coming up the Thornfell road. Someone who carries a debt-mark like yours. Someone who shouldn't exist."

The spirit went utterly silent inside her—for the first time since the binding, truly silent—and Mirabel felt it turn, slow and vast, toward the dark northern pass, and recognized the feeling at last for what it was.

Fear.
