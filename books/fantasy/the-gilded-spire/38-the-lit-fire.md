## 38. The Lit Fire

Pip had learned that fire was a kind of language.

Not the way the temple said it, all smoke and punishment and accounts to be settled. Real fire. The kind that lived in a hearth and meant *stay*. The kind Sefa kept in the galley stove of the *Wren's Tooth* and fed every morning like a stubborn old dog. The kind Mirabel carried now behind her eyes, quiet and heavy, the thing she never talked about but which Pip could feel humming under her skin whenever they sat close — a second heartbeat that wasn't hers.

The shrine at Thornfell had been dark for three hundred years. Pip knew because the stones told them. Stones were terrible gossips once you knew how to listen, and Pip had been listening since the night they'd touched this very shrine and heard singing come out of it like water out of a cracked jug.

Now the singing was louder. Now there were people coming up the pass to hear it.

"You're going to burn your sleeves," Mirabel said, crouched beside them on the frost-stiff grass. She had her hood up even though it wasn't raining, because Mirabel always had her hood up, but Pip could see the corner of her mouth doing the thing it did when she was pretending not to be pleased about something.

"They're Sefa's sleeves," Pip said. "She gave them to me."

"That's worse, not better."

"The gods gave me these hands." Pip held them up, palms out. The light was already starting in them, that pale gold-green glow like foxfire, like the underside of leaves when the sun came through. "These are just borrowed too."

Mirabel went quiet the way she did when Pip said something true without meaning to. It happened a lot. Grown-ups spent so much time saying things that weren't true that the true ones landed on them like thrown rocks.

Below them, the camp was waking. Tents and wagons strung along the old road like beads on a string — more than Pip had ever seen gathered in one place outside a city. Pilgrims with walking staffs wound in ribbon. Smugglers with knives hidden in their boot-tops and hymns hidden in their pockets, because you couldn't sing the old songs openly yet, not everywhere, not even after everything. And the ones in between, the pilgrim-smugglers, who carried relics in false bottoms and prayers in code and who laughed loudest at the fires at night because they understood best what it cost to carry something holy through a country that wanted it dead.

Sefa stood at the center of it all with her arms crossed and her gray braid whipping in the wind, arguing with a fat merchant-priest about tolls. Arguing with joy. Pip could tell it was joyful arguing because Sefa's hands were moving and nobody had drawn a weapon.

"Is she going to be angry?" Pip asked. "About the fire?"

"Pip." Mirabel pulled her hood back. Her hair was longer than it used to be, and there was a streak of white in it now that hadn't been there before the Spire, and her debt-mark — the great coiled ledger-scar that ran from her wrist to her collarbone — caught the early light and threw it back silver instead of black. It did that sometimes. The thing inside her had moods. "You relit a god's beacon on a law older than the kingdom. If anyone's angry, they're wrong, and Sefa knows it."

"But she likes being right."

"She likes being *paid*," Mirabel said. "Being right is just how she collects."

* * *

They came down together, and the crowd made room for them the way water makes room for a stone — not quickly, not all at once, but completely.

That was new. A year ago Pip had been a stowaway nobody claimed, small enough to hide in a grain hold, quiet enough that Sefa's crew forgot to feed them half the time. Invisible, like Mirabel used to be. But invisible wasn't the same as gone, and the old gods had opinions about forgotten children. They'd poured themselves into Pip like wine into an empty cup, and now when Pip walked past, people touched their foreheads. Not to Pip. Pip had figured that out early and it mattered. They were touching their foreheads to whatever looked out through Pip's eyes.

It should have been frightening. Mostly it was just lonely, in a strange sideways way, being a window everyone prayed at.

Except here. Here it wasn't lonely at all.

"Pip!" Tam the tinker waved from beside his wagon, where he'd set up his little traveling forge. He'd walked four hundred miles to be here, he said, because his grandmother used to hum an old-god tune while she worked and he'd never known until the shrines woke why it had made him feel safe. "You'll do the lighting, then? Proper? With words?"

"With words," Pip promised.

"And the song?"

"And the song."

Aldric was there too, standing at the edge of the gathering the way he always stood at edges, like a man who hadn't decided if he was allowed inside yet. He'd refused his reinstatement twice now. Pip had heard him tell the queen's messenger, very politely, that he was done guarding thrones and would rather guard roads. He wore no colors anymore, just a plain sword and a patched cloak, and pilgrims kept asking him to bless things, and he kept explaining, red-faced, that he'd been exiled by one temple and fired by another and really wasn't qualified.

Pip thought that was exactly what made him qualified.

"Little one." Aldric nodded to them. His voice was still rough from the wound Merek's crossbow bolt had given him at the pass, though that was two seasons gone. "Cold morning for miracles."

"Best morning for miracles," Pip said. "Cold mornings, you can see your breath. It looks like the gods are breathing with you."

Aldric blinked. Then he laughed — short and surprised, the way he always laughed, like the sound had escaped custody. "Did you learn that from a book or make it up?"

"Made it up. Books don't know anything the stones don't."

* * *

The wayshrine stood where the old road crested the pass: five standing stones around a fire-bowl of green-black metal, carved over every surface with script so old that even the temple's oldest sealed archives had only fragments of it. Pip could read it now. Not all of it. But enough.

*Whoever comes cold shall be warmed.*
*Whoever comes hungry shall be fed.*
*Whoever comes hunted shall be sheltered.*
*No one is turned away from a lit fire.*

The road-law. Sefa had invoked it a year ago with a knife on the table and a crew vote hanging by two voices, and it had saved Mirabel's life, and Aldric's, and Pip's own. Pip had asked the stones once whether the law came from the gods or the smugglers, and the stones had sung back something that Pip translated, roughly, as: *What's the difference?*

Sefa climbed the last stretch of the path and stopped in front of the shrine, arms still crossed, looking at it the way she looked at a cargo manifest she suspected was lying to her.

"Well," she said. "Three hundred years dark, and now we're going to make it everybody's problem again."

"It was always everybody's problem," Pip said. "Everybody just pretended it wasn't."

"Don't get clever with me, sprout. I've got merchants to fleece and a priest to overcharge." But her hand came down on Pip's head anyway, ruffling their hair, rough and brief and warm. That was how Sefa hugged. Like she was paying a debt and resented the interest.

The merchant-priest — Brother Callow, round and sweating and genuinely delighted to be somewhere his superiors couldn't see him — cleared his throat. "In the old days," he offered, "the lighting was done at moonrise. For the, ah. The symbolism."

"In the old days," Sefa said, "the lighting was done whenever somebody damn well needed a fire, or the law didn't work." She looked at Pip. "Somebody cold coming up the pass right now?"

Pip listened. Not with ears. With the other thing.

Down the southern switchbacks, small and far, a knot of travelers was struggling through the snow-dusted scree. Two adults, one limping. A child. An old woman being carried. They were hours out, maybe less, and the weather was turning, and they were carrying someone who wouldn't survive the night in the open.

"Yes," Pip said. "Someone's coming. Someone who needs it lit *now*."

Sefa grinned, sharp as a filleting knife. "Then we do it now. Road-law." She turned and bellowed down the slope, "OI! Wood on the bowl! All of you! Move like your supper depends on it, because it does!"

* * *

They built the fire together, and that was the part Pip would remember longest, later. Not the flame. The building.

Tam brought split oak from his wagon. Two sisters who'd walked from Vaelmoor City itself — real pilgrims, poor as dust — carried brush in their aprons. A smuggler named Wren, who'd once run guns for a warlord and now ran medicine north across the passes, laid her own emergency kindling, waxed and precious, into the bowl without a word. Aldric split logs with his sword because he'd left his axe in a ditch somewhere in the capital and refused to explain why. Sefa directed traffic like a harbor pilot in a storm. Ossian — thin, gray, half-blind now, freed in the collapse and never quite warm since the cells — stacked the wood in careful, perfect rows, because Ossian had been a scribe for thirty years and could not help making even chaos orderly.

And Mirabel knelt at the edge of the bowl with her marked arm bare to the air, and the thing inside her leaned toward the shrine like a dog toward an open door, and for one moment her whole body shivered with the effort of holding it still.

"I'm fine," she said, before anyone asked.

"You're not," Pip said gently.

"No," Mirabel agreed. "But I will be. Light it, sprout."

Pip put both hands into the bowl, into the heart of the stacked wood, and sang.

It wasn't a big song. That was the secret nobody at the temple had ever understood — the old gods weren't in the thunder and the burning bushes. They were in lullabies. In work chants. In the tune grandmothers hummed over washtubs. Pip sang the wayshrine awake the way you'd wake a sleeping friend: quietly, kindly, with a hand on the shoulder.

The stones began first. A low hum, felt in the teeth. Then the carvings filled with light, gold-green running through the old script like dawn through a valley, word by word by word, three hundred years of dark peeling away. The crowd's murmur died. Somewhere a woman started crying and didn't bother hiding it.

And then the wood caught.

Not like normal fire. It burned tall and steady and the color of foxfire, throwing no smoke, giving heat like an embrace, and its light reached down the whole length of the pass so that the road below lay visible for miles, every switchback lit like a staircase in a lantern.

*A beacon,* the stones sang through Pip's bones. *Lit and kept.*

"By whose authority?" called out an old man near the front, half-joking, half-not, because some habits outlive empires.

Pip looked at Sefa.

Sefa stepped forward, planted her boots, and delivered it like a tariff notice:

"By the road-law! Whoever comes cold shall be warmed. Whoever comes hungry shall be fed. Whoever comes hunted shall be sheltered. **No one is turned away from a lit fire** — not by king, not by priest, not by weather, not by price!" She jabbed a finger at the crowd. "That law saved my crew when half of us voted to let fugitives freeze. It's older than your kingdoms and it'll outlast the next lot too. This fire is lit under it. Anyone got a problem with that can take it up with the mountain."

Nobody took it up with the mountain.

* * *

The travelers came down off the switchbacks as the light died toward evening — the limping man, his wife, the child, and the old woman carried on a door torn off its hinges. Frost in her eyebrows. Breath shallow as paper.

Pip met them at the fire's edge and took the old woman's hand, and the warmth went through, and her eyes opened, and the first thing she said — the very first thing, before thanks, before sense — was:

"Oh, I know that song. My mother sang it. I thought everyone had forgotten."

"We almost did," Pip said. "But forgetting isn't the same as gone. We keep learning that."

They fed everyone. Of course they did. Sefa's cooks produced stew from nowhere, the way smuggler cooks always did, vast quantities from mysterious supplies, and Brother Callow blessed the pots with a prayer so garbled by official doctrine that Pip had to fix it halfway through, gently, and Callow just laughed and said, "Thank you, I've been saying it wrong for twenty years, it feels much better this way."

Night settled in. The fire held. People sang — first the safe songs, then, one by one, emboldened, the old ones, each voice joining like a stitch closing a long tear. Aldric danced with one of the pilgrim sisters and was terrible at it and glowed anyway. Ossian fell asleep sitting up with his back against a waystone, and someone put a blanket over him, and Pip saw Mirabel stand over him a long moment, her face doing something complicated, before she tucked the blanket's edge in with two fingers.

Later, Pip found Mirabel at the shrine's edge, apart, watching the fire with the expression she wore when the thing inside her was talking.

"What's it saying?" Pip asked.

Mirabel was quiet a moment. "It remembers this place. Before the binding. It says..." Her jaw worked. "It says it used to be the one who kept the fire. That this was its shrine, once. That the first king didn't destroy the old gods — he *hired* them, and then chained them, and billed them for the chain."

Pip considered that. Considered the ancient, terrible, grieving thing coiled in their friend's skin, which had eaten a kingdom's miracles and murdered a king by inches and which had also, last winter, quietly taught Mirabel a smuggling cipher neither of them would name aloud.

"Does it want the fire back?" Pip asked.

"No." Mirabel's mouth twisted. "It wants to *warm its hands* at it. Same as everyone else." She huffed something that was almost a laugh. "I told it it could listen to the singing. Just listen. It's... quieter tonight than it's been in months."

"Because it's home," Pip said simply. "Even chains remember home."

Mirabel looked down at them then, really looked, and her eyes were wet in the firelight, and she didn't say anything at all, which from Mirabel was a whole speech.

* * *

Past midnight, when the crowd had thinned to sleepers and the fire burned low and gold, Pip climbed onto the flat top of the tallest waystone and sat cross-legged, listening.

The stones were singing again. Not the waking-song. Something else. Something with a shape to it, a direction, like a finger pointing.

North. Past Thornfell, past the high passes, into the deep country where the maps went blank and the old roads ran wild. There was a shrine there — no, more than a shrine. A gathering. Old-god territory waking all at once, a whole valley lighting up like the script on these stones, and underneath the singing, faint but unmistakable, a note of distress.

Something up there was calling. Not to the gods.

To the vessel. To the mark on Mirabel's skin. Calling it *home*, the way the crown-spirit had once been called home, and the tone of the call was not entirely friendly.

Pip's hands went cold despite the fire.

Below, Mirabel slept against Sefa's wagon with her marked arm wrapped close, and the mark gleamed faintly in the dark, silver answering the distant north, and Pip realized with a lurch that the beacon they'd lit tonight was visible for a hundred miles —

— and that everything for a hundred miles could see it too.

Including, perhaps, whatever was singing from the deep country, in a voice like cold water and old grief, getting closer with every verse.

Pip hugged their knees and watched the fire and did not wake anyone. Not yet. Some news kept better till morning.

But the stones sang on, and the child listened, and far to the north, something turned over in its long sleep and began, slowly, to climb.
