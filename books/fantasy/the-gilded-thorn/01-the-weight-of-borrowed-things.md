## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Things

The soldier was dying in the alley behind the Tallowman's Rest, and he was smiling about it.

That was the first thing I noticed—the smile. Men die in Vess in a hundred ways, and most of them go out ugly: gasping, cursing, bargaining with gods who stopped listening twenty years ago. This one lay in a puddle of his own making with a knife-wound under his ribs that had already soaked through his coat twice over, and he was smiling at the sky like it owed him money and had just paid up.

I crouched beside him out of habit, not mercy. In my trade you learn to tell the difference between a man who has minutes and a man who has hours, because the harvest is different. Minutes means the memories are bright and close to the surface, easy to lift, still warm from living. Hours means they've gone down into the cellar of themselves to wait, and you have to dig.

He had minutes.

"Easy," I said, which is what I always say, though it's a lie both ways. Nothing about it is easy, and I am not easy either. "I'm going to help you."

"Help," he said. His voice was a wet rag wrung thin. "Nobody helps."

"Everyone gets helped once." I took his hand. His fingers were cold as coins. "What's your name?"

"Corvan." He said it like he was checking whether it still fit. "Corvan Hale. Seventh Lances."

"Well, Corvan Hale of the Seventh Lances. You're going somewhere better than this alley, and I'm going to walk you there. But the road costs a toll." I turned his hand over and pressed my thumb into his palm, where the skin is thinnest, where the remembering lives closest to the surface. "Think of your happiest hour. The one you'd keep if you could only keep one."

He didn't fight me. That's the part people don't understand about my trade—they think there's struggle, screaming, some great wrenching theft. There isn't. Dying men *give*. They're so tired of carrying it all, and here comes a girl with soft hands saying *let me take just this one*, and they hand it over like a coat on a hot day.

His happiest hour came up through his palm and into mine like water through a cloth.

A summer evening. A courtyard behind a garrison house. A woman laughing—his wife, dark-haired, flour on her cheekbones—and a daughter of maybe three years old chasing a hen across the flagstones while he sat on the step with a beer going warm in his hand and thought, with the whole simple certainty of a man who does not yet know how the world works: *this is enough. This is everything. If I died tonight I would die full.*

I held it the way you hold a bird—firm enough not to lose it, gentle enough not to crush it. Then I did what I always do. I wrapped it. Folded the edges in, tucked the corners, sealed it in wax and a scrap of his own coat-lining so it would know its owner. A charm. One hour of a man's life, bottled against the dark.

"There," I said, and pressed his hand closed around nothing at all. "You'll feel lighter now. Everyone does."

"What did you take?" he asked. Not angry. Curious, the way dying men are curious about everything except death.

"The weight," I said. "Just the weight."

He smiled again—that same smile—and I left him in the alley with his empty hands and his minutes, and I walked away with an hour of beer going warm and a hen squawking and a woman with flour on her face, and I did not look back, because looking back is how thieves get caught, and also because if I looked back I would have to think about what I do, and I have made a very careful life out of not thinking about what I do.

* * *

Vess at night is a city pretending to be something else.

By day she's gray and lawful and grimy, all customs-houses and curfew-bells, a port town that grew rich off other people's wars and never quite washed the blood from under her fingernails. But after dark she puts on her market face, and the night market along the Weepers' Canal is the truest thing about her—stalls lit by fat candles, fish-smoke and frying onions, fortune-tellers who are frauds sitting three spaces down from fortune-tellers who are not, and everywhere, threaded through it all like wire through meat, the quiet commerce of things that aren't supposed to exist anymore.

Because magic is outlawed in Vess. Has been for twenty years, ever since the Fall—though ask anyone what fell, exactly, and watch their faces go smooth as pond ice. Ask them what came before the Fall and they'll tell you: before the Fall, there was the Fall. History in this country starts with its own wound. Nobody remembers why the bells toll on the ninth day of autumn. Nobody remembers why there's a national day of mourning with no name attached to it. People just feel sad one day a year, the way you feel rain coming in your knee, and don't ask questions, because asking questions in Vess is how you meet the Hollow Men.

So we sell our wares quietly. Memory-charms, luck-knots, grief-easing tinctures, love-salves that work exactly as well as the buyer wants them to. The stall I share—rented, technically, from a woman named Bess who believes I deal in perfumes—is wedged between a candle-dipper and a man who sells birds that can repeat anything they hear, which in Vess is either a novelty or a death sentence depending on what they overhear.

"Evening, thief," said the candle-dipper, whose actual name I've never learned but who answers to Wick.

"Evening, arsonist."

"That was one fire, and it was *candle-adjacent*."

I set out my tray. Six charms tonight, each one wrapped in wax and colored thread—red for joy, blue for sorrow eased, black for memories someone wanted gone so badly they paid me double. The soldier's hour went in the center, red thread, and I confess I looked at it a moment longer than the others. It was a good harvest. Clean, whole, unspoiled by fear or pain. Hours like that are rare. Most people's happiest moments have rot somewhere in them—a debt unpaid, a door slammed, a name they shouldn't have said. His was pure. Beer going warm. Hen. Flour-faced wife. *This is everything.*

"You're brooding," Wick said. "You only brood when you've got something good. Brooding's how you price it."

"I'm not brooding. I'm appraising."

"Appraise faster. You've got a customer."

She was a big woman in a dyer's apron, hands stained violet to the wrist, and she smelled of vinegar and indigo and the particular desperation of someone who has been crying recently and professionally concealed it. She didn't browse. She came straight to my tray like a woman walking into cold water.

"The red ones," she said. "Which is strongest?"

"Depends what you need it for."

"My mother died in the spring." She said it flatly, rehearsed, a speech she'd given herself until it wore smooth. "And I can't—I can't stop seeing the sickroom. Every time I close my eyes. I just want one night where I remember her *before*. Before the bed, before the—"

"Joy," I said, and set the soldier's hour in her palm. "One hour, whole and clean. A summer evening. A family. You'll live inside it till morning, and when you wake, the sickroom will be quieter. Not gone. Quieter."

She stared at the little wax lump like it might bite her. "Is it stolen?"

Everything is stolen. That's the entire trade. "It's *secondhand*," I said. "Like everything worth having."

"How much?"

I told her. She winced, then paid it, because grief makes spendthrifts of us all, and she walked away clutching it in her violet fist like a coal she was afraid to drop and afraid to let cool.

Wick leaned over. "Sold the good one first again. You always sell the good one first. Business advice, free of charge: save the best for last, build the evening's momentum—"

"If I hold onto it too long I start thinking about it," I said. "Thinking about it ruins the price."

"Thinking about what? It's not yours."

"No," I said. "It's not."

And that, dear reader of my private thoughts, is the whole rotten heart of my profession, laid out between a candle-stall and a canal: none of it is mine. Three years in Vess, and every memory I own came secondhand. I know what a wedding feels like—four weddings, actually, from four different brides and one extremely enthusiastic groom. I know the taste of a mother's cooking I never ate, the sound of a lullaby no one sang to me, the specific pride of a man watching his son walk for the first time. I am a museum of other people's best days, curated by a thief, attended by no one.

Here is my rule, the only rule, the one I carved into myself the way you carve a notch into a doorframe to mark how tall you've grown: *never steal your own past.*

Not because it's forbidden. Because it's impossible. Because when I reach back into myself for something to sell, I find—nothing. A room with the furniture gone. Three years ago I walked into Vess with no history, no name I trusted, no face in my mind older than the city gates, and I have learned to live in that emptiness the way you learn to live in a house with a hole in the roof: you put a bucket under it and stop looking up.

The bucket, in my case, is work. Harvest, wrap, sell. Harvest, wrap, sell. Keep the hands busy and the eyes forward and never, ever look up at the hole.

* * *

The market thinned past midnight, the way markets do, candle by candle. I was wrapping the last of the black-thread charms—a merchant who'd paid me to remove his memory of signing a contract he regretted; the guilt stayed, funnily enough, only the details went, so now he felt guilty about a sin he couldn't name, which I consider my finest irony of the season—when I felt it.

Not heard. Felt. The particular pressure of being watched by someone who is very good at it.

I didn't turn. I finished the knot, tucked the charm away, and began packing my tray with the unhurried movements of a woman with nothing to hide and nowhere urgent to be. Under the counter, my hand found the knife I keep for reasons I tell myself are culinary.

Two figures at the mouth of the lane. Tall. Gray-cloaked. Standing too still, the way nothing natural stands too still. The candle flames near them had gone quiet—not blown out, just *attentive*, leaning toward the two figures like flowers toward a sun that gives no light.

Hollow Men. Inquisitors. The ones who erase practitioners—though "erase" is the word the proclamations use, clean and bureaucratic, and the word in the alleys is different. The word in the alleys is that they eat what you remember, and leave the rest of you standing there with holes in it, greeting your neighbors with a smile and no idea why your own children flinch from you.

They hadn't moved toward me. That was the strange part. They stood at the lane's mouth like men checking a compass, and one of them turned his head—slowly, mechanically, a head on a hinge—and looked not at me but at my tray. At the empty space in the center of it where the soldier's hour had been.

Then the taller one raised one gray finger and pointed. Not at me.

Past me. Down the canal. Into the dark beyond the market, where the lamps gave out and the tenements leaned together like conspirators.

I packed the rest of my tray in four seconds flat, blew out my candle, and slipped into the crowd with my heart going like a drum in a parade. Whatever they were pointing at, I had no intention of being nearby when they found it. I cut through the fish-stalls, down the steps by the Weepers' Bridge, into the labyrinth of lanes behind Tanner's Row where even the watch doesn't bother, and I was three streets gone and congratulating myself on a clean escape when I reached my door and found it standing open.

I always lock it. Locking doors is the first lesson of a life with no past—you guard what little you have, because you can't replace any of it.

Inside, my single room was exactly as I'd left it: cot, table, the loose floorboard under which my earnings sleep, the jar of wax and thread. Except the air was wrong. Cold, somehow, in early autumn. And in the middle of my floor, mud-caked, barefoot, shivering in a shift that had once been white, stood a child of maybe six or seven years old, looking at me with eyes the color of deep water.

And when I opened my mouth to say *who are you, how did you get in here, get out*—

The child spoke first. Two words, in a language I have never heard in my life, that made every charm in my tray hum like struck glass.

And worse—far worse—some small, buried, traitorous part of me understood every syllable.
