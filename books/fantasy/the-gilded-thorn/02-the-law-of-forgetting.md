## 2. The Law of Forgetting

There are three laws in Vess, though the city only bothers to write down one of them.

The first is written on every gate and tollhouse in letters tall enough to read from a moving cart: *No working of the art, upon pain of unmaking.* The second is unwritten but universally understood: don't get caught doing it anyway. The third — the one that actually keeps people alive — is mine: never steal your own past.

I was thinking about all three laws the morning they took Old Sefa from the dye-house on Candle Lane, because laws are like weather in Vess. Everyone talks about them, nobody can do anything about them, and when they turn bad, you find out who left their shutters open.

* * *

Let me back up, because if you're new to Vess — and everyone in Vess is new to Vess, in one way or another; nobody's family goes back more than twenty years, a fact the city wears like a borrowed coat that doesn't quite fit at the shoulders — you'll want to know how we got here.

Twenty years ago, there was a war. Or there wasn't. That's rather the point.

What the archives say — and Vess's archives are very proud of themselves, all vellum and wax seals and clerks with ink-stained fingers who will show you any document you like for a small fee — what the archives say is this: twenty years ago, the kingdom of magic fell. Not a country called anything. Just *magic*, capital-M, an abstraction, a plague, a fire that burned itself out. The great practitioners were destroyed. The art was outlawed across the nine free cities. Peace and reason reigned ever after.

Ask the archives *where* the kingdom of magic was, and the clerks smile at you with the particular patience of men who have answered this question before. It was everywhere, they say. It was nowhere specific. Maps of the period are conveniently vague, borders drawn in watercolor washes that could be coastline or could be cloud.

Ask them what the war was fought over, and they'll tell you: over magic itself. Ask who won, and they'll tell you: we did, obviously, or we wouldn't be keeping the archives.

Ask them what stood in the valley beyond the Salt Fen, north by northwest, three weeks' hard ride from Vess, and the clerk's smile will freeze by exactly one degree, and he will inform you that there has never been anything in the valley beyond the Salt Fen, that the Fen marks the edge of the settled world, that beyond it is marsh and then waste and then nothing worth a mapmaker's ink.

And here is the thing — here is the thing I have spent twenty years not thinking about, which is a full-time occupation requiring considerable discipline — here is the thing: when he says *nothing*, some small animal part of your brain agrees with him. Eagerly. Relieved, even. Because the alternative to *nothing* is a question, and questions in Vess have teeth.

The man who signed most of those archives — the histories, the treaties, the handsome new edition of the atlas with its confident blank space past the Fen — is Aurel Vane. He was a young scholar then. He is Lord Protector now. He did not conquer anything, according to his own documents. He merely *corrected* the record. And a corrected record, it turns out, is stronger than any army, because you cannot march against a thing that officially never happened.

That's the first law's true shape, underneath the lettering on the gates. It isn't *no magic*. It's *no remembering*.

* * *

Which brings us, by an unpleasant road, to the Hollow Men.

They aren't called inquisitors officially — officially they're "Record Wardens," which sounds like men who guard shelves — but nobody calls them that unless a Warden is standing within earshot, and even then only the brave or the stupid. They're called Hollow Men because of what they do and, some say, what they are.

A Hollow Man comes to your door dressed in grey so undyed it seems embarrassed to be a color at all. He does not arrest you. Arrest implies charges, trial, record. A Hollow Man simply stands in your doorway and looks at you, and his eyes do something eyes shouldn't do — they *draw*, the way my charms draw, except instead of taking a happy hour from a dying soldier, they take everything. Your mother's face. Your wedding day. The name of the street you grew up on. The fact that you had a name. He drinks you down to the rim, and what's left walks around, eats, sleeps, answers questions correctly, and cannot tell you why it cries at certain smells.

They call it erasure. The victims are called the hollowed. There are perhaps two hundred of them in Vess, living in a kind of gentle fog along the river, doing piecework, smiling at strangers. They seem peaceful. That's the cruelest part. Whatever the Hollow Men leave behind has no grief in it, because grief requires a before, and there is no before. Only a smooth, warm, endless now, like a stone worn round by water.

People say the Hollow Men themselves were once practitioners who erased too much and drank their own selves empty. People say a lot of things. In Vess, saying things is the last legal art form.

* * *

Now. Old Sefa.

Sefa dyed cloth on Candle Lane, and she'd been doing it since before I arrived in the city, which made her, in local terms, practically geological. She was a broad woman with forearms like ship rope and a laugh you could hear through two walls, and she dyed her silks colors that didn't exist anywhere else — a blue she called duskwater, a red like the inside of a secret. Customers came from three districts over. Her prices were outrageous. Nobody bargained, because bargaining felt like insulting a priest.

Here is what nobody said out loud: no mordant, no mineral, no beetle-shell on earth produces duskwater blue. Sefa's dyes were worked. Small workings, quiet ones, the kind you can do with a word under your breath and a lifetime of practice — color-singing, the old women would have called it, back when old women could call things anything. She'd been doing it for decades. The Wardens had walked past her shop ten thousand times.

And then, on a grey morning in the month the river smells of mud, a Hollow Man stopped walking.

I saw it happen. I was coming back from the night market with my coin purse light and my feet heavy, and I stopped at the corner of Candle Lane because Sefa was hanging out her silks and the duskwater caught the light the way it always did, like a piece of evening someone had cut out and kept. Beautiful. Stupid of me to look. You should never look at beautiful illegal things in daylight; it draws attention, yours and otherwise.

The Hollow Man was already in the lane. I hadn't seen him arrive. They're like that — you never see the first one, you only ever see him *already being there*, as if the street grew him overnight. Grey robe, grey hood, hands folded. He stood in front of Sefa's silks for a long time. Long enough that I counted my own heartbeats and lost track twice.

Then he reached out one hand — slowly, almost tenderly — and touched the hem of a duskwater scarf.

And the color went out of it.

Not faded. *Went*. Like a candle pinched. The silk stayed silk, good silk, but the blue was gone, and where the blue had been there was nothing, a absence-shaped hole in the cloth that my eyes kept sliding off of, the same way they slide off the valley past the Salt Fen.

Sefa came out of the dye-house wiping her hands, saw him, saw the scarf, and — I will give her this, and it cost her everything — she did not run. She straightened up to her full impressive height and looked the Hollow Man in the hood, and she said, in a voice that carried to me at the corner and probably to the harbor:

"Twenty years I've hung these out. Twenty years. Where were you?"

The Hollow Man tilted his head. Under the hood there wasn't much — I caught a glimpse, just a glimpse, of a face like a thumbprint in wet clay, features present but *uncommitted*, a face that had been looked at too long by other faces until it forgot its own argument. When he spoke, his voice was soft and dry, like pages turning.

"Madam," he said, "there has never been colored silk on this lane."

"Yes there bloody well has," said Sefa, "you grey son of a—"

He touched her wrist.

I want to describe it properly, because I've thought about it every day since, and description is the only revenge I'm likely to get. He didn't grab her. He rested two fingers on the pulse point of her wrist, the way a physician might, the way a lover might, and he *drank*. I know that's what it was because I do a version of it myself, and recognition is a horrible thing. I take one hour, carefully, leaving the seams intact. He took the bolt of cloth whole.

She didn't scream. Screaming requires fear of what's coming, and what came was the opposite of coming — it was *un*-coming. Her face went slack in stages, like a house going dark room by room. First the anger drained out of her, then the confusion, then the recognition of the crowd that had gathered, then — I watched this happen, I watched it and did nothing, remember that, file it away with the rest of my debts — then the knowledge of what silk was. She looked down at her own hands, purple-stained to the elbow, and turned them over with mild curiosity, as if they belonged to a stranger whose name she was trying politely to recall.

The Hollow Man released her wrist. She swayed, smiled at him — *smiled* — and wandered toward the river, where the hollowed go, following some current invisible to the rest of us.

He gathered up her scarves, all of them, folded them neatly, and walked away. The duskwater blue did not come back. By afternoon, the dye-house had a new tenant, a cooper, who complained about the smell and knew nothing of the woman who'd worked there, and neither did anyone else, and by winter the only person in Vess who remembered Old Sefa was me.

Which, I assure you, is its own special punishment. Being the sole custodian of a dead woman's memory, when memory is contraband. Every time I thought of her I committed a crime. I committed it daily. I recommend it.

* * *

After Candle Lane, I changed my habits, and I want to be honest about the fact that my changes were coward's changes, survival changes, nothing noble. Noble people in Vess end up smiling at the river.

I stopped selling charms with any color in them. Joy, I learned, has a texture the Wardens can taste — happiness leaves residue the way spice lingers in a pot — and residue is evidence. So I narrowed my stock. Small comforts only. The smell of bread from a childhood kitchen. The weight of a sleeping cat. One perfect ordinary Tuesday. Nothing bright enough to sing, nothing old enough to have a homeland in it.

I started wearing gloves, even in summer, because a charm works best skin-to-skin and I didn't want to give any passing Warden an accidental taste of my inventory. I moved my cache of charms out of the floorboards — too obvious, everyone hides things in the floorboards — and into the one place in my rooms no searcher would think to look: inside the hollow leg of the table I eat at alone. There's a joke in there somewhere about loneliness as security measure. I haven't found it yet. Give me time.

And I added a fourth law to my private three, right beneath *never steal your own past*: *never let them see you remember.*

Because that's the real test, I'd come to understand. The Hollow Men don't hunt practitioners, not really — practitioners are just commerce, and commerce flows, and Vane's people have never minded commerce. What they hunt is *witnesses*. Anyone who carries a memory older than twenty years. Anyone whose insides contradict the archives. Sefa wasn't taken for the dye-work. The dye-work was the pretext. She was taken because she'd been on Candle Lane for thirty years, because her body was full of a Vess that predated the correction, because somewhere in her was a time before the blank space on the map, and blank spaces must be kept blank at any cost.

The city is full of such people. Full of them. Every graybeard who mutters about the good old days without being able to say *which* days. Every woman who hums a lullaby in a language she calls nonsense. Every refugee-shaped hole in the population that everyone politely steps around. Vess is less a city than a wound that agreed to stop bleeding, and the Hollow Men walk its streets with their soft dry voices, keeping the scab on.

So: vigilance. Eyes down, trade narrow, gloves on, table-leg cache, and a new route home from the market every week, varied like a drunkard's walk, because paranoia, I've found, is just foresight with anxiety.

It was working. For months, it worked. I got so good at being forgettable that I half-believed it myself — Elodie Oyelaran, no history, no residue, a woman shaped like a gap in a ledger. Some nights I'd sit at my table with its hollow leg and feel almost safe, and almost-safe in Vess is the richest feeling available, believe me.

And then, on a night in late autumn, with frost on the windows and my charms humming their small contented hums on their shelf —

someone knocked at my door.

Three knocks. Soft. Wrong. Nobody knocks at my door. Nobody *knows* my door.

I blew out the candle, put my hand on the table leg, and listened.

The knocking came again, patient as snowfall, and beneath it — faint, impossible, in a voice too small to belong to anything but a child — a sound I hadn't heard since a life I don't remember:

words. Actual words. In a language that made every charm on my shelf wake up and shine.
