## 3. A Knock at the Wrong Hour

There are three hours in Vess when a knock at your door means nothing good. The first is just before dawn, when the watch makes its rounds and the Hollow Men ride behind them like shadows that learned to wear coats. The second is midday, when respectable people are awake and only trouble knocks. The third is any hour at all, if you're me.

So when the knocking came — three small raps, polite as a debt collector — I did what any sensible practitioner does. I blew out my lamp, pressed my palm flat against the floorboards, and listened with everything but my ears.

The house told me what it knew: one heartbeat on the other side of the door. Small. Fast as a sparrow's. No iron, no salt, none of the tricks inquisitors carry to burn a thief of memories out of her own walls. Just a small wet heart going like a drum at a funeral nobody wanted to hold.

I should have left it knocking. That's the honest version of this story, the one I'd tell if I were a better liar. I have survived in Vess for three years by being exactly as kind as is profitable and not one drop more. Kindness in this city is a coin purse left open on a market stall; someone will take it, and they won't say thank you.

But it was raining the way Vess rains — sideways, spitefully, like the sky had a grudge — and the knock came again, softer this time, almost apologetic, and I thought: *it's a child, or something wearing a child, and either way it will be gone by morning if I don't open the door.*

That last part was a lie I told myself, and I want it on the record that I knew it was a lie even as I told it. Some things, once they knock, don't leave. You can bar the door, you can move cities, you can sell your own name to a stranger and buy a new one back at discount. They still knock. You just stop hearing it for a while.

I opened the door.

* * *

The child stood in the rain like it had been poured there and hadn't yet decided to run off. Mud to the knees, mud to the elbows, mud in its hair in long streaks, as though it had crawled through something rather than walked. Its eyes were the color of tea left too long, and they looked at me with an expression I recognized from the mirror on bad nights: the look of someone who has been running so long that stopping feels more dangerous than anything chasing them.

"You're not from the Lamplight Ward," I said. "Nobody from the Lamplight Ward has ever been that muddy. We're proud of our filth here. We curate it."

The child said nothing. Rain dripped off its chin. Behind it, the alley gurgled and stank of the tanneries, and somewhere a drunk was singing a song about a fisherman's wife that got filthier with every verse.

"Wrong door," I tried. "Whatever you're selling, I'm not buying. Whatever you're fleeing—" I stopped, because that was the wrong thing to say to those eyes. "Go to the Sisters of the Drowned Bell. They feed strays."

The child opened its mouth.

What came out was not Vessian. It wasn't Low Trade, which every gutter-rat in this city speaks before their own mother's tongue. It wasn't High Trade, or the clipped court-Vessan the magistrates use when they want to sound expensive. It wasn't anything. It was sounds like water over stones, like wind through a reed bed, like a word I had never heard and somehow, horribly, already knew.

And my charms sang.

I need you to understand what that means. A memory-charm is a quiet thing. It sits in its glass like a firefly in a jar, dim until you crack the seal, and then it burns whatever hour I stole into your head for as long as the wax holds. They do not hum. They do not sing. In three years of making them, through cold snaps and heat waves and one memorable night when Maro's cellar flooded and I had to fish forty memories out of standing water, not one charm has ever made a sound.

Every charm on my shelf began to hum at once. Low, sweet, harmonizing — forty stolen hours of other people's happiness rising together like a congregation finding the hymn. The dying soldier's afternoon in the orchard. The baker's daughter's wedding dance. A fisherman's last sunrise before his lungs gave out. All of it singing to this mud-caked child in a language made of rain.

The child's eyes went wide. It looked at my shelves the way starving people look through bakery windows — not with greed, exactly. With grief.

"No," I said, to the charms, to the child, to whatever god handles emergencies in the Lamplight Ward. "No, absolutely not. Those are inventory."

The child took one step inside without asking, which should have been the moment I put it back out in the rain. Instead I stood there like an idiot while it dripped on my floorboards, and the humming swelled, and I felt something in my chest turn over — that old, stupid, dangerous thing that lives under the ribs and doesn't care about survival rates.

It was tenderness. Uninvited. Unprofitable. Tenderness, arriving at my door at the worst possible hour, wearing mud.

* * *

Here is what I know about children in Vess, because I make it my business to know things that keep me alive: there are perhaps four hundred of them in the Lamplight Ward, most of them quick, all of them hungry, and every single one speaks enough Trade to beg, bargain, or lie. A child who cannot ask for bread in the local tongue is not from here. A child who cannot ask for bread in *any* tongue I know is not from anywhere I know.

And a child whose presence makes stolen memories sing like choirboys is not a child the world has agreed to acknowledge.

I dried it off — him, I decided eventually; there was a stubborn set to the jaw that read male to me, though I'll admit the mud complicated the assessment — with a rag I keep for exactly no purpose, because I have never before had a guest. He let me scrub the worst off his face without flinching, which told me he'd been handled roughly before, or not at all, and couldn't decide which was worse. Under the mud his skin was brown as mine, though a different brown — mine is the warm bronze of the southern coast, his something older, cooler, like river stones.

"Name," I said, tapping my own chest. "Elodie. El-o-die." I tapped his chest. "You?"

He watched my mouth with terrible concentration. Then he answered, and it was that language again, two syllables, soft, and my charms hummed louder, and one of them — the soldier's orchard, the one I'd sold twice and bought back once because I couldn't stand to let it go — actually glowed. Faintly. Like a coal remembering fire.

"Stop that," I told it. Charms don't listen. Neither did he.

I fed him because a fed stray is easier to think around than a starving one, and also because watching him eat hurt something in me I preferred intact. Bread, hard cheese, the heel of a honey-cake I'd been saving for a night when existing required compensation. He ate like food might be revoked — fast, careful, both hands — and between bites he kept looking at the shelves. At the charms. His lips moved sometimes, silently, reading them, though there was nothing written on the glass to read.

Around midnight he fell asleep sitting up, mid-chew almost, the way children and soldiers sleep when the body finally overrules the mind. I carried him to the cot in the corner — my cot, technically, though I've slept in the chair for three years because lying down invites dreams and dreams invite questions I can't afford — and covered him with the good blanket, the one with more patches than blanket.

Then I sat across from him and watched him breathe and conducted an audit of my life choices.

Item: I had admitted a stranger past my threshold. Item: the stranger was a child who spoke a dead language — and I use the word *dead* advisedly, though I didn't know how dead yet, God help me. Item: my merchandise, my livelihood, my entire defensive architecture of small glass hours, had reacted to him like he was a struck bell and they were the ringing. Item: item: item.

Item: I had not thrown him out.

I am very good at throwing things out. Feelings, mostly. I've had practice. Three years ago I arrived in Vess with nothing — no name anyone recognized, no history anyone could check, not even, if I'm honest about the shape of it, much of a past I could point to. What I had was hands that could lift an hour out of a willing skull without leaving a scar, and a fence named Maro who looked at those hands and saw profit, and a city that had outlawed exactly what I was and therefore guaranteed me a seller's market.

My rule — the rule, the whole of my ethics compressed to five words — is *never steal your own past.* Not because it's forbidden, though it is, catastrophically, by law and by common sense. Because I looked inside myself once, early, carefully, the way you probe a sore tooth, and found... rooms. Closed doors. A hallway where the wallpaper should be. Whatever childhood I had, somebody had gotten to it before I did, and prying at those doors felt like prying at a wound that had finally stopped bleeding. So I built a trade out of everyone else's yesterdays and called it a living, and I told myself the hollow feeling was just hunger, and hunger can be fixed with bread.

The child slept. The rain kept up its argument with the roof. And the charms hummed on, quieter now, a lullaby pitched to him alone, and I sat in my chair and felt dread and tenderness sitting on either shoulder like a pair of disputing angels, and I understood — with the clarity that only comes at the wrong hour — that I was already lost.

Because here's the arithmetic of Vess, and I do it quickly: a child who appears out of nowhere, speaking nothing, trailed by weather and silence, is either a trap or a catastrophe. Traps are set by people. Catastrophes are set by history. And in this city, twenty years after the Fall — after whatever it was that ended the old world and started the law of Forgetting, after which magic became a hanging offense and memory became contraband — history is the more dangerous of the two.

If the Hollow Men found him here, they wouldn't ask questions. Questions imply answers, and answers imply records, and records are the one thing the inquisitors exist to prevent. They would simply erase him, the way you erase a chalk mark, and then they would erase me for having harbored him, and then they would erase the neighbors who heard the humming, and by morning the Lamplight Ward would have one fewer crooked tenement and no one would remember why the corner lot smelled faintly of rain.

Dread said: *carry him out tonight. Leave him at the Sisters' door. Let him be someone else's catastrophe.*

Tenderness said nothing at all. Tenderness just looked at the small shape under the patched blanket, at the mud still crusted under his fingernails, at the way his hand had curled closed around nothing — around something, maybe, some object long since lost, held in a fist that hadn't gotten the news — and tenderness stayed. Tenderness, I have learned since, is the least reasonable force in the world. It doesn't argue. It just refuses to leave, like a stray at your door in the rain.

I banked the lamp. I checked the wards on the windows — salt lines, mostly superstition, but superstition is cheaper than alternatives. I put the soldier's orchard charm in my pocket instead of on the shelf, because it had glowed and glowing draws attention and I was not ready to examine why I protected it like that.

And then, because I am a professional and professionals plan, I decided: one night. He stays one night. In the morning I feed him, walk him to the Sisters of the Drowned Bell, hand him over with a lie about finding him in the market, and return to a life of manageable sins.

That was the plan. I want it noted that I had a plan.

Somewhere past the smallest hour, half asleep in my chair, I heard him speak again — not awake, not to me. A dream-word, or a prayer, or a name. Two syllables, soft as the rain, and every charm on my shelves turned toward him in the dark like flowers toward a sun that hadn't risen yet.

And under my breastbone, in the locked rooms where my own past should be, something knocked.

Three small raps. Polite as a debt collector.
