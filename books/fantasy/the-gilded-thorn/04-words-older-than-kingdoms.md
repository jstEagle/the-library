## 4. Words Older Than Kingdoms

The child slept in my chair.

Not the bed—I'd offered the bed twice, with all the warmth of a woman offering a stranger her own grave—but the chair, curled up like a comma in a sentence nobody had finished. Mud had dried on her in layers, geological strata of road and river and who-knew-what, and she smelled like wet wool and something underneath that I couldn't name. Something green. Like a forest that hadn't been cut down yet.

I sat across from her with a cup of chicory going cold and watched her breathe, because apparently this was my life now. A woman who made her living taking things from other people's heads, sitting vigil over a head I couldn't get into at all.

That was the part that kept me awake, if I'm honest. Not the mud or the midnight knock or the way she'd looked at me like she'd been looking for me specifically, which was its own flavor of nightmare. It was the silence behind her eyes when I reached out—habit, reflex, the little brush against the surface of a mind that tells me what a person dreams about. With everyone it's like touching water. Ripples, colors, the shape of their wants. With her: nothing. Not a locked door. Not an empty room. Nothing, the way a hole in the ground is nothing. The way a place where a house used to be is nothing.

My charms knew more than I did. That was the second thing keeping me awake.

All night they'd been humming. Not the market-hum, the cheap little shiver I work into them so buyers feel like they got their coin's worth. This was deeper, a resonance in the bone of them, like tuning forks left near a bell that never stops ringing. I'd laid them out on the table to shut them up and instead they'd hummed louder, and the child, half-asleep in the chair, had smiled at them. Smiled, the way you smile at music you recognize from somewhere you can't name.

So I did what any sensible woman does at four in the morning with an impossible child and a table full of singing trinkets. I made tea I didn't drink and started cataloguing.

* * *

Here's what I know about languages, which is more than most thieves but less than I pretended: every tongue has a grain. Vessish runs smooth and slippery, built for contracts and lies. The dockside cant has a gravel to it, all edges. The hill-folk speech from the eastern valleys curls at the ends, apologetic. When someone speaks near me, even in words I don't know, I feel the grain of it, the same way you can tell silk from rope by touch alone.

The child's language had no grain I recognized.

That should have been the end of it—a gutter-tongue, some border dialect, a family thing passed down in secret the way families do when the world turns hostile. Vess is full of those. Half the city prays in a language the temples don't sanction, and the other half swears in one the magistrates pretend not to hear.

But it wasn't the sound that snagged me. It was what happened to my charms when she spoke.

The first word she said that morning—she was awake before me, standing at my window watching the chimney smoke of the tanneries rise, and she turned and said one word, pointing at the smoke—was *senneth*. Or close to it; my ears wanted to reshape it into something Vessish, and I had to hold them still to hear it plain. Smoke. She meant smoke. I know she meant smoke, the way you know a thing you have no business knowing.

And every charm on that table rang like a struck glass.

I picked one up—the soldier's hour, the one I hadn't sold yet, the dying man's last good afternoon—and held it while she watched. Warm. Warmer than body heat has any right to make glass. And inside it, through the shell of it, I could feel the memory shifting, turning toward her like a plant toward light.

Memories don't do that. Memories are goods. They sit where you put them. That's the whole basis of my trade, the entire architecture of my life: memory is inventory, inventory is inert, and inert things can be weighed, priced, and sold without anyone getting hurt worse than they already were.

This one leaned toward her like a dog hearing its name.

"Say it again," I told her.

She said it again. *Senneth.* And this time I heard more than smoke. Underneath the word there was a whole weather system of meaning—chimney smoke, yes, but also hearth-smoke, and the smoke of burning fields, and the sweet gray ribbon of it rising off a harvest fire, and a mother's voice saying *come in out of the senneth, little bird,* and—

I put the charm down. My hands were shaking.

Because I had never heard that word in my life. I would swear that on anything you liked, on the bones of whoever you hold holy. And yet I had understood it—not guessed it, not inferred it from context, but *understood* it, the way you understand your own name. The meaning had been sitting in me already, folded up small in some drawer I didn't know I had, and the sound of the word had simply opened the drawer.

There are two explanations for a thing like that. One is that I'm losing my mind, which is a real possibility in my line of work—you spend enough time handling other people's pasts, you start finding theirs mixed into yours, like hairs in a borrowed coat. The other explanation was worse, and it was standing at my window with mud on her feet, waiting for me to catch up.

* * *

I tried the practical route first. I always do. Feelings are for after the facts are counted.

"Where are you from?" I asked, in slow, clear Vessish, the way you talk to foreigners and drunks.

She tilted her head. Answered in her own tongue, three words, soft as anything, and pointed east.

East is the Marches. East is burnt ground and garrison roads and, past all of it, the sea. Nobody comes from east of the Marches. There's nothing east of the Marches except what the maps call the Salt Fen, which is a polite cartographer's way of saying *here be nothing, stop asking.*

"Your name?"

Another word. One syllable and then two, quick, like a birdcall. I couldn't hold onto it. It slid off my memory the way rain slides off waxed cloth, and I want to be clear that this has never happened to me. Names are the easiest thing in the world to keep. I've carried the names of forty strangers' dead mothers in my head for years, cargo I never asked for and can't put down. But this child's name wouldn't stick. It was as if the word itself had been oiled.

Except—one moment. One moment when she said it and looked straight at me, and for half a heartbeat it stuck, and what stuck was not a sound but a shape, a feeling, a small bright terrible thing like a coal wrapped in cloth. And under the feeling, unbidden, unearned, rose a single word of my own:

*Wren.*

I don't know where it came from. I don't know why a bird's name should live in the same hidden drawer as a language I've never learned. But she heard me say it—whispered it, really, barely aloud—and went absolutely still, the way animals go still when the trap closes, and then nodded once, solemn as a magistrate, and that was that. Wren. Some things you don't choose. They arrive and refuse to leave, like children at wrong hours.

* * *

By midmorning I'd run out of practical routes. So I did the thing I never do. I opened my own stock.

Under the floorboard beneath my bed there's a loose plank, and under the plank there's a tin box, and in the box there are eleven charms I have never sold and will never sell. Not because they're precious—they're dregs, mostly, fragments too thin to carry a price, scraps of other people's days that came to me stuck to better goods, the way burrs come home on a dog. I keep them for the same reason misers keep broken coins. Proof of the trade. Proof of the rule.

The rule being: never steal your own past.

Everyone in my business knows the rule. Maro taught it to me my first week, holding my wrist hard enough to bruise, his face closer than faces ought to come. *You'll be tempted,* he said. *Someday you'll find a piece of yourself in someone else's stock, and you'll think, just one look, just to know who I was. Don't. A thief who opens her own safe stops being a thief and starts being a ghost.* I didn't understand him then. Three years later I understand him perfectly and obey him anyway, most days, with difficulty.

But these eleven weren't mine. They were leftovers, orphaned fragments, and I told myself—lied to myself, with excellent craftsmanship—that examining them was due diligence, inventory management, anything but what it was.

I laid them out in front of Wren.

She looked at them the way you'd look at a table of food after a long fast. Not greedy. Hungry in a way that has nothing to do with appetite. Her hands came up off her lap and hovered over the charms without touching, and the humming in the glass rose until my teeth ached with it, and I said, "Go on, then," though I don't know what I thought she'd do.

She touched the third one from the left.

It shattered.

Not broke—*shattered*, the way ice shatters, all at once and everywhere, and the memory inside it didn't spill out the way spilled memories do, puddling and fading. It stood up. It filled the room the way light fills a room when you open shutters, and for one second I wasn't in my rented room above the chandler's at all.

I was standing in an orchard.

Trees in rows, heavy with fruit I knew the name of—*alveth*, the word arrived whole, alveth, a plum-gold fruit that bruises if you look at it wrong—and the light was late-summer light, thick as honey, and someone was laughing, a woman, and the laugh pulled my head around before I could think, and I saw—

Nothing. The vision tore away like a page snatched from my hand, and I was back in my room, on my knees, I noticed, with glass dust in my palms and blood coming from somewhere minor, and Wren was crouched in front of me with her hands hovering near my face, not touching, her eyes enormous.

The orchard was gone. But the word stayed. *Alveth.* And under the word, a country.

I want to describe what happened next properly, because it deserves proper description, and because if I make it sound tidy you won't believe it, and I need someone to believe it. What happened was that a word opened in me like a door opening in a wall I'd lived against all my life without knowing the wall was hollow. Behind the word *alveth* there was a tree. Behind the tree, rows. Behind the rows, a valley. Behind the valley, a kingdom, and the kingdom had a name, and the name was—

Ashkar.

I said it out loud and the remaining ten charms sang.

Ashkar. Ashkar, Ashkar, the word ringing down corridors of me that shouldn't exist, and here is the vertigo, here is the bottomless part: I know the name. Everyone in Vess knows the name, the way everyone knows the names of the old kings and the founding of the city and the Fall. Twenty years ago, the histories say, Ashkar fell. There was a war. There was a usurper—Aurel Vane, may his name itch forever—and the war ended and Ashkar fell and that was that, a closed book, a finished story, one of the facts of the world like the color of the sky.

Except.

Except that when I reached for the history of it—the battles, the borders, the kings, the reasons, the *shape* of the thing—there was nothing there. Not forgotten-nothing. Forgotten things leave holes with edges. You can feel the outline of what you lost, the way your tongue finds the gap where a tooth was. This was different. This was a hole with no edges, a gap that had been smoothed over, painted, papered, a wall where the tooth used to be, and painted so long ago and so well that only a madwoman would ever run her fingers along it and notice the seam.

Twenty years of histories, and I couldn't remember a single fact about Ashkar except its fall. Couldn't picture its flag. Couldn't name its capital. Couldn't say who fought whom or why, and neither, I realized with a cold sliding feeling, could anyone else I'd ever met, and no one had ever found that strange, and I had never found that strange, and that—that was the strangest thing of all. A whole country, erased down to a single sentence, and the sentence repeated so often it had worn grooves in all our minds: *Ashkar fell, Ashkar fell, Ashkar fell.*

Fell from what? Fell how? Fell *where?*

Point east, the histories say, vaguely, toward the Marches, toward the Fen, toward nothing. Ask a scholar and he'll recite the fall and stall on everything before it, the way a man recites a prayer in a language he doesn't speak. I'd done it myself a hundred times. We all had. The whole country was a word we knew and a thing we couldn't see, and the child kneeling on my floorboards speaking its dead tongue was the first living proof I'd ever touched that the word had ever pointed at anything real.

* * *

Wren let me sit there shaking for a while. She was patient about it, in the way of someone who has waited a very long time for much larger things.

Then she got up, crossed to the middle of the room, and crouched down over the bare boards, and I thought she was going to be sick, honestly, and I moved to fetch the basin, and she caught my sleeve. Not hard. Just enough. *Watch.*

She spat on her palm—child's ritual, older than any teaching—and began to draw.

Lines. Straight ones first, sure and unhesitating, no false starts, the way you draw a thing you've drawn ten thousand times or a thing that's drawn itself into you deeper than practice goes. Then curves. Then a lintel, a threshold, hinges. I know joinery; I grew up—or didn't grow up, which is the problem—in a city of doors, and I know a door when I see one being drawn.

It was a good door. That's the detail that undid me. Not a child's scribble of a door, not a rectangle with a knob. A door with ironwork I recognized, scrollwork in the corners, a keyhole shaped like a thorn. Somebody had built this door once. Somebody had hung it and walked through it daily, and this child had known it, and now it existed nowhere in the world except in charcoal-mud on the floorboards of a memory-thief's rented room.

When she finished, she sat back on her heels and looked up at me, and she spoke. Six words, maybe seven. Slowly. Deliberately. The way you speak to someone you're trusting with something breakable.

And I understood her.

Not all of it—there were gaps, words I felt the shape of but couldn't open, drawers still stuck. But the spine of it came through clean, arriving in me in Vessish as if translated, as if the language had been waiting in me all along for someone to give it breath:

*This is the door. It is the last one. It opens from the inside, and I am the key, and I cannot turn it alone.*

The room went very quiet. Even the charms stopped humming. Outside, the tannery carts rattled past, and the chandler below dropped something and swore, and the ordinary morning went on being ordinary, and I knelt on my floor staring at a drawing of a door in a language of lines, in a room that suddenly felt like the inside of a question.

Because here's what I hadn't said yet, what I was still refusing to say, what I would go on refusing to say right up until refusing stopped working: the door she drew was familiar. Not the ironwork—though God help me, that too. The *place*. The light in the drawing, the angle of the sun she'd shaded with the side of her hand falling across the threshold—it fell the way light falls in the orchard. The orchard from the shattered charm. The orchard I had no business remembering.

The last door in the world, and some buried piece of me knew exactly which side of it I used to stand on.

Wren watched me work it through. She was good at watching; she'd had practice, I suspect, at watching people fail to understand. Then she reached out, took my right hand—my thief's hand, my scarred palm—and turned it over, and pressed her small muddy thumb into the center of it, hard, over the heel of my lifeline.

And the skin warmed under her thumb like a charm waking up, and somewhere far down under twenty years of paint and paper and smoothed-over walls, something knocked.

Once. From the inside.

I looked at the door on the floorboards. I looked at the child. I thought about Maro, and the rule, and the tin box under the loose plank, and every careful invisible year I'd built in this city out of other people's leavings.

Then I blew out the lamp, though it was morning, because some conversations you want dark for, and I said the only sensible thing left to say.

"I know a fence who's going to hate this."
