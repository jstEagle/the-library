## 5. The Fence Who Raised Me

Maro kept his shop in the Crook, that fold of Vess where the streets bent like guilty consciences and the city watch had learned, over the years, to lose their way. You could stand at the mouth of Tanner's Lane and swear you'd walked it a hundred times, and still come out somewhere you hadn't meant to be. The Crook protected its own. It was the only neighborhood in Vess with any sense of loyalty, and even its loyalty was mostly inertia.

Maro's shop sat at the crookedest part of the bend, behind a door painted the green of old pond water. Above it hung a sign with no words on it—just a brass key, polished bright, which was Maro's joke, because he sold nothing that opened anything. He dealt in charms, in bottled hours, in the small stolen weather of other people's lives. I brought him my goods; he set the prices; he took his cut, which was generous, and taught me the trade, which was priceless, and lied to me, which I would learn later, but that morning I still believed every word out of him like scripture.

I had known him since I was nobody. That's not poetry. I came to Vess three years ago as an actual nobody—a girl with no past she could name, no accent she could place, no memories older than a fog. Maro found me sleeping in the alcove behind his shop, and instead of turning me out or turning me in, he fed me stew and asked me one question: *What do you remember?*

Nothing, I told him.

*Good,* he said. *Clean slate. Best kind of thief.*

He taught me how to lift a memory without tearing it, how to cradle a stranger's happiest hour in your palms like a soap bubble, how to bottle grief so it didn't leak. He taught me the rule I lived by—the only law I kept above Vess's law: *never steal your own past.* Not because it was forbidden. Because it was impossible. A person can't pickpocket themselves. Your own memories sit too deep, grow roots into everything else. You'd have to take the whole self to get at them.

I used to think that rule was wisdom. I've since learned it was also a warning.

That morning, Wren was asleep on my cot when I left—or pretending to sleep, which for a child who'd appeared out of a rainstorm speaking a dead language amounted to the same thing. I'd tucked the charcoal rubbing of the floor-drawing inside my coat, against my ribs, where I carried things I didn't want to look at directly.

The rubbing itself had been an act of cowardice dressed up as method. I couldn't bear to keep staring at the drawing on my floorboards—that door, drawn in a child's sure hand, with a keyhole shaped like nothing I'd ever seen and yet shaped exactly like something I almost remembered. So I'd laid paper over it and rubbed charcoal across until the image lifted free, and then, because I am a professional and professionals don't burn evidence they don't understand, I folded it up and carried it to the one man in Vess who understood everything worth understanding and most things that weren't.

The walk through the Crook did nothing to settle me. Morning there is a gray, fish-smell affair, gulls arguing over the midden heaps, the tanners' vats breathing their sour breath across the lanes. But under the ordinary stink something else moved today—a wrongness I couldn't name. My charms hummed in their case at my hip. All of them. At once. A low chord, like a struck bell heard through water.

Charms hum when they're near their kin. Near memory. Near *source*.

They'd been humming since the child arrived.

* * *

Maro was at his counter, doing what he always did at that hour: weighing nothing in particular on scales built for weighing things that had no weight. Memory has mass, if you're trained to feel it—an hour of joy weighs more than an hour of boredom, a first love outweighs a decade of Tuesdays—but the scales were theater. Maro weighed with his hands. The scales were for customers, who trusted brass more than they trusted flesh.

He looked up when the bell over the door clattered, and his face did its usual thing: the slow crease of a smile that never reached the eyes, because the eyes were already counting what I'd brought and what I'd cost him.

"Elodie." My name in his mouth always sounded slightly like an appraisal. "You're early. Either business is good or business is bad."

"Business," I said, "is strange."

"Strange is expensive." He set down the weights. "Expensive is good."

I locked the door behind me. I drew the shade. These were theatrics I'd learned from him, and he raised an eyebrow at both, and the eyebrow said: *this better be worth the drama.*

"It's about a child."

"Children are cheap," he said. "Unless they're rare."

"This one speaks a language that makes my charms sing."

The smile stayed on his face but something behind it went very still, the way a pond goes still when a heron's shadow crosses it. "Show me."

I unfolded the rubbing on the counter and smoothed it flat with both hands, and I watched my mentor—the man who had taught me that nothing in this trade should ever surprise you, that surprise is just ignorance wearing its Sunday clothes—I watched him go pale.

Not thoughtful-pale. Not calculating-pale. I know all of Maro's colors. This was the pale of a man seeing a debt come due, the pale of a gambler watching the one card he'd buried turn up in someone else's hand.

His fingers hovered over the paper without touching it. The drawing: a door, tall and narrow, its frame worked in lines that might have been script or might have been thorns. And around the frame, in that same sure child's hand, a border of small marks—not letters, not quite. More like the *idea* of letters, the way a footprint is the idea of a foot.

"Where," Maro said, and his voice had dropped half an octave, "did you get this?"

"A child drew it. On my floor. In charcoal she found in my own hearth."

"A child." He said the word like he was testing whether it would hold his weight. "What child? Whose?"

"That's rather the question, isn't it. She turned up two nights ago, mud to the eyebrows, wouldn't speak Vessian, wouldn't eat until I gave her the bowl unwatched. And when she started talking—" I tapped the paper. "My charms hummed, Maro. Every charm in my case. Like they knew her voice."

"They didn't hum."

"They hummed."

"Elodie." He folded the rubbing closed with two fingers, careful, the way you close a dead man's eyes. "Listen to me, and listen the way I taught you to listen, which means hearing what isn't said as well as what is. There are languages that predate the law. There are languages that were made illegal not by decree but by erasure—you understand? Not banned. *Unwritten.* And a child who speaks one isn't a child. She's evidence. Evidence walks on legs, eats your food, sleeps on your cot, and when the Hollow Men find her—and they will find her, they always find her—they won't take her away. They'll take her apart, gently, page by page, and then they'll take everyone she touched, and everyone those people touched, backward through the city like a fire through a hayrick. Starting with the fence who harbored her. Starting," he said, "with me."

"You don't know that."

"I know exactly that." For a moment the mask slipped entirely and underneath it was something I'd never seen on Maro's face: fear, naked and undignified, an old man's fear with wet eyes behind it. "Twenty years ago there was a country. You don't remember it. I don't remember it. Nobody remembers it, and that is not an accident, that is *policy*, and the policy has teeth, and the teeth have names, and the names carry warrants. Whatever that child is, she is a splinter of something that powerful men paid oceans of gold to make never-have-been. And splinters work their way out, Elodie. They fester. They surface. And when they surface, everything around them gets lanced."

"So we hide her better."

"*We?*" He laughed, and it was the worst sound I'd heard all week. "There is no we. There is a fence and a thief, and a fence survives by knowing what not to stock. You want to know what happens to fences who stock the unwritten? Ask—" He stopped himself. His jaw worked. "Ask anyone. You can't. That's the point."

I looked at the folded rubbing between us on the counter, this little rectangle of paper with a door drawn on it, and I felt the vertigo again, the one that had been living in my chest since the night the child knocked. Because here was the thing I hadn't said aloud, the thing I'd carried against my ribs along with the paper:

When I'd laid the charcoal over the drawing, my hands had known the motions. Not guessed them. *Known* them—the angle of the stroke, the pressure, the way you work from the center outward—and my hands had learned that trade from no one, because I had no one, because I had no past, except apparently I did, except apparently my past was a door drawn by a mud-caked child in a language that made my life's work sing like a choir.

"Maro," I said quietly. "When I copied it, my hands remembered how."

He went white to the lips. Actually white, the blood leaving like a tide going out.

"Then you're not harboring evidence," he said. "You *are* the evidence. Both of you. Oh, you stupid, brave, catastrophic girl."

"What do I do?"

And here is the thing I want written plainly, because for years afterward I turned it over like a coin worn smooth, looking for the face where it wasn't counterfeit: he answered fast. Too fast. The answer was already in his mouth before my question finished leaving mine, which meant it had been waiting there, which meant some part of Maro had known what he'd say from the moment he saw the paper, maybe from the moment I walked in, maybe longer, maybe twenty years longer.

"Drown her."

The word fell into the shop and lay there.

"The canal," he said, softer now, gentler, the gentleness worse than the word. "Behind the tanneries. It's deep and it's quick and the current takes things out toward the fen, and nothing that goes into that water comes back with a name. No body, no inquest, no Hollow Men asking questions with hot irons. You tell no one. You bury the drawing. You burn the floorboards she marked. And in a month you'll be selling charms again and this will be a bad night you had once." He reached across the counter and put his hand over mine, and his hand was shaking, and I had never once seen Maro's hands shake. "I know what it costs. I'm asking anyway. Because the alternative costs more, and it costs *everyone*, and cruelty done small and early is mercy done large and late. That's not cynicism, girl. That's arithmetic."

I pulled my hand out from under his.

"You taught me the trade," I said. "You never taught me drowning."

"I taught you survival. Drowning is a chapter of it." His eyes were wet and hard at once, which shouldn't be possible and was. "You think I say this because I'm cruel? I'm saying it because I've seen what the alternative looks like. I've seen whole *streets* emptied. People you'd have sworn were solid, gone, and their neighbors can't even mourn them because they can't remember mourning. That's what the Hollow Men leave. Not bodies. *Gaps.* And gaps fill with whatever the government prefers you to believe."

"And if I don't?"

"Then run. Take her and run, tonight, now, and never come back to Vess, never send word, never let your shadow fall across my door again. Because if you stay—if you keep her under my sky—one day soon there'll be a knock at that green door, and it'll be polite, and it'll be patient, and it will be the last conversation either of us ever has." He straightened, and the mask came back down, and the merchant returned, and somehow that was the most frightening thing of all: how quickly he could reassemble himself. "Now get out of my shop. You were never here. I never saw that paper. And Elodie—" I paused at the door, hand on the latch. "Whatever you decide," Maro said, "decide it alone. Don't come back here with her. Don't come back here with questions. The man you knew this morning doesn't exist anymore, and neither does the favor he owed you."

The bell over the door clattered behind me, cheerful and idiotic.

* * *

I stood in Tanner's Lane with the fog coming off the canal and the tannery vats breathing their sourness, and I did not go home right away, because home had a child in it, and I needed to be honest with myself about what Maro had asked before I looked her in the face.

Here is what I found, when I went digging in myself the way I'd dig in a stranger: I did not feel horror. I felt *recognition*, which is worse. Because some cold practical corner of me—the corner that prices other people's joy by the dram—had already run the arithmetic before Maro spoke. One child against a city. One muddy, silent, impossible girl against everyone whose names I knew. The math was clean. The math was monstrous. And the fact that the math came so easily to me frightened me more than the Hollow Men did.

But underneath the math, lower than the cellar floor of me, something else sat up when Maro said *drown her*, and it did not do arithmetic. It did something I had no trade-name for. It simply refused. Flat, total, wordless refusal, the way the body refuses poison—not decided, just *known*.

And beneath even that, deeper, where I keep nothing because I thought I had nothing: a smell. Wet charcoal. Smoke and blossom. An orchard burning somewhere far away, and someone's hand in mine, small, sticky, gripping hard.

I had never smelled it before in my life.

I stood in that lane until the fog soaked through my coat, and then I did what Maro should have predicted, what any student of his should have seen coming from a mile off: I did the opposite of what I was told, for reasons I couldn't defend, on faith I didn't own.

I went home.

The door of my room stood the way I'd left it. Inside, Wren was awake, sitting cross-legged on the floor beside the spot where the drawing had been, though I'd scrubbed the boards twice and the charcoal ghost still showed if the light came slantwise. She looked up at me. And she held out her hand.

In her palm lay a scrap of charcoal.

She didn't speak. She didn't need to. Her eyes said it plain as Vessian: *you went to ask someone about us. What did they say?*

And I, who lie professionally, fluently, for coin—who can lift a dying soldier's best hour and sell it back to him with a straight face—looked at that child and heard myself say, in a voice I barely recognized as mine:

"We're leaving tonight."

Wren closed her fist around the charcoal. Then, slowly, she smiled—the first time I'd seen her do it—and pointed one small finger at the ghost of the door on my floorboards, and then east, toward the fen, toward the edge of every map Ilsa Voss had ever been fired for drawing.

Outside, far off across the rooftops of Vess, a church bell began to ring the wrong hour.

And my charms, all of them, began to hum.
