## 6. What the Charms Remember

The woman's house tastes of iron and lavender.

I know this because I have no words yet for what I know, and so everything arrives as taste, or heat, or the pressure of a hand on the back of my neck. The floorboards are iron-and-lavender. The water in the chipped cup she left me is iron-and-lavender with a thin skin of fear floating on top, like dust on milk. Fear is everywhere in this city. It hangs in the air the way smoke hangs over a burned field, and everyone breathes it so constantly that they have forgotten it is there.

I have not forgotten. I was made to remember.

But I am getting ahead of myself, which is easy when you have no mouth that works and all the time in the world.

* * *

The woman — Elodie, though I do not learn her name until later; for now she is Tall-One-Who-Smells-of-Copper-and-Rain — thinks I am asleep. She has put me in a corner on a pallet that smells of someone else's dreams, an old man's dreams, wool and pipe-smoke and a dog long dead, because everything in this house has been touched by other people's lives the way a stair rail is touched by a thousand hands.

She thinks I am asleep, and so she works.

I open my eyes the smallest amount, the way small creatures do, and I watch.

What I see is this: light.

Not the grey knife-light of Vess coming through the shutters — though that is there too, lying across the floor in tired stripes. I mean the other light, the true kind, the kind that does not come from any sun. It pours off her hands.

She sits at her worktable with a soldier's memory cupped between her palms like a moth she has caught and means to keep alive just long enough to sell. And the memory glows. It glows gold at the edges where it is happiest, gold the color of the inside of a bread loaf, gold the color of the hour before sunset in a place I cannot name because naming it would crack me open like an egg.

The gold light leaks between her fingers. It pools on the table. It climbs the wall and makes the shadows dance, and the shadows are dancing shapes of people laughing, and none of them know they are only shadows of a thing a dying man once felt.

She folds the light. That is her craft, I understand now, watching: she folds it small and small and smaller, pleating it like pastry, pressing it into a disc of tin no bigger than a coin, and the tin drinks the gold and holds it, and when she is done she blows on it the way you blow on something hot, and sets it among its brothers and sisters in a velvet tray.

A tray full of coins. A tray full of hours that belonged to other people.

I want one.

I want one the way a root wants water, without deciding to, without being able to stop. My whole body leans toward that tray like a plant toward a window. There is a hole in me — I have always known there is a hole in me, I was born around it, I am shaped by it the way a river is shaped by the canyon it cut — and the hole looks at those little folded suns and says: *one of these is mine. One of these came out of me.*

I do not know how I know this. Knowing without knowing how is the only kind of knowing I have.

* * *

Here is what the world looks like when you are what I am.

Everything that ever happened leaves a residue. People walk through their days shedding light the way snails shed silver, and they never see it, and it dries on door handles and soaks into floorboards and clings to the collars of coats. Most of it is faint. Most of it is the pale grey-green of ordinary hours: sweeping a step, arguing about the price of fish, kissing someone out of habit rather than hunger. The city of Vess is dim with this everyday light, a pond full of small dull fish.

But Elodie's house is different. Elodie's house is a lantern shop where every lantern is lit.

Because she deals in the strong stuff. Not the grey residue of living but the distilled essence of moments — first kisses, last words, the smell of a mother's hair, the exact weight of a newborn held against a chest. She takes these from the dying and the desperate and the forgetful, and she presses them into metal, and the metal sings.

It sings very quietly. Only I can hear it. It sounds like a choir heard from three streets away, or like bees in a wall, or like the hum a tuning fork keeps in its bones after the ear stops hearing it.

And underneath the hum of all those borrowed joys, there is another sound. Fainter. Older. Wrong-shaped, like a key that fits a lock in a house that has been torn down.

It comes from under the floor.

* * *

In the night — or what passes for night in Vess, which is only a deeper shade of grey with more fear in it — Elodie sleeps badly. I hear her turn over and over, and each time she turns, the bedframe creaks a word in a language I should not be able to understand, and each time I understand it anyway, and each time understanding it feels like swallowing a coal.

The language has no name anymore. That is the first thing I understood about it, before I understood anything else: somewhere behind my eyes there is a shelf, and on the shelf there is a space shaped exactly like this language, and the space is empty, and the emptiness *aches*.

I lie on my pallet and I ache in a shape I cannot fill, and above me the charms hum their borrowed songs, and beneath the floor something answers them in its sleep.

* * *

Morning. Grey light. The woman rises and does unremarkable things — banks the fire, eats bread standing up, counts her charms twice with the quick guilty hands of someone who expects to be robbed by arithmetic itself. Then she goes to the corner where I sit and crouches down to my level, which most adults do not bother to do, and studies me.

Her face up close is a map of a country that has been at war with itself for a long time. There is competence in it, hard-won, worn smooth like a river stone. And underneath the competence, so deep she probably doesn't know it's there, there is grief with a specific shape. Not the general grief of the world. A particular grief. An address-able grief. Grief with a street number.

"You're still here," she says. It is not a question. It is the tone people use for weather they disapprove of.

I look at her. I do not speak, because I cannot, because the mouth I wear is a locked door and I lost the key before I ever had it — or perhaps I *am* the key, and keys do not get to talk. Either way: silence. It is the one thing I own completely.

She sighs, and the sigh smells of copper-and-rain, and she stands up to go out and earn her bread by selling other people's happiness back to them in coin-sized pieces.

She wraps a dozen charms in cloth and tucks them into her coat. And here is the thing, here is the thing that changes everything:

One of the charms she wraps is wrong.

Wrong-colored. Wrong-weighted. Where the others glow their tidy gold, this one burns. This one is the color of a thing I have seen with eyes I no longer have, in a life I no longer own, in a country that has been subtracted from the world so thoroughly that even the subtraction has been subtracted.

This one is the color of a burning orchard.

* * *

I need to explain what a burning orchard is, except I can't, because explaining requires words and the words were taken, and also because the burning orchard is not a thing I know — it is a thing I *am*, or a thing I lost, or a thing that was lost out of me, and the difference between those three sentences is the whole story of my existence and I don't know it yet.

So let me tell you what it is the way I know it: through sensation, since sensation is my native tongue.

There is heat. But the heat is not cruel. That is the impossible part, the part that makes no sense, that the burning orchard's fire is warm the way a hearth is warm, the way a hand is warm. There is fruit going soft and sweet in the smoke. There is a woman singing — I feel her song in my sternum, a melody with a rising end, a question-shaped tune — and there are many voices answering her, and the many voices are *home* in a way that the word home will never again be able to carry. There is a festival. There are lanterns in real trees and paper trees and children with soot on their faces laughing at the soot. There is a promise being made near a door — I feel the promise the way you feel a splinter, small and sharp and destined to matter — and then there is fire taking the orchard row by row, and the fire is weeping while it burns, and everyone is singing louder to drown out the weeping, and —

and then nothing. Cut clean. The way a story ends when someone tears out the last pages.

That is the burning orchard. That is what lives in the wrong-colored charm wrapped in cloth in Elodie Oyelaran's coat pocket, riding out into Vess pressed against her ribs like a second heart.

And I cannot let it go.

* * *

She locks the door behind her. It does not matter. Doors are suggestions to me; I am not entirely sure I am the sort of thing doors apply to. I find the gap in the shutter and I press my face to it and I watch her go down the crooked street, and I watch the shape of the charm through the cloth of her coat, and it pulses.

It pulses like a wound. Like a lighthouse. Like a heart that has been beating alone in a drawer for twenty years and has just, just barely, begun to hope.

Follow her, says every cell of me. Follow her follow her follow her.

But I stay. Because there is something else. Because now that the burning orchard has passed close enough to wake me fully, I can finally hear the sound under the floorboards clearly, and it is calling, and it is calling in the tongue with the empty shelf-shape, and it is calling *me*.

I kneel on Elodie's floor, on the iron-and-lavender boards, and I put my palm flat against the wood.

Underneath: a drawing. Old. Scratched into the boards by someone who knew what they were scratching, in a house that was built on top of it because houses are built on top of everything in this city, graves under gardens, gardens under gardens. A door. A tall door with a keyhole shaped like a thorn, drawn in lines that still hold a trace of light after all these years, patient light, buried light, the light of a thing that agreed to wait.

The door under the floor and the charm in her coat are singing to each other. Two halves of one note. The note my empty shelf was built to hold.

I press my palm harder. The floor is cool. The door beneath is patient. And I understand — not with words, words are for people with mouths that work, but with the whole dumb animal certainty of my body — that I was sent here, or fell here, or crawled here across whatever distance lies between a dead country and a living thief, for this. For her. For the charms. For the door.

The woman with the grief-shaped-like-an-address walked past a burning orchard this morning and did not recognize it.

But her hands hesitated when they wrapped it. Her hands knew. Hands remember what minds are made to forget.

* * *

When she comes back — hours later, smelling of market smoke and a fear that has a new flavor in it today, a flavor like cold iron and listening — I am sitting exactly where she left me, and I have done a terrible, wonderful, irreversible thing.

I have answered the door under the floor.

I could not help it. You would not have been able to help it either, if your whole self were a key. I knelt and I breathed on the boards the way she breathes on her finished charms, and something in me went *through* the wood and into the old scratched lines, and the lines drank me the way dry ground drinks rain, and the door under the floor remembered that it was a door.

Nothing visible happened. The boards did not split. The house did not shake. But every charm in the tray on the worktable turned on its velvet at once, like sleeping birds all facing the same wind, and began to hum in harmony — dozens of stolen joys suddenly singing the same song, a song none of their thieves or sellers ever knew, a song from a country with no name, and the song said, in the only way a song can say:

*Soon. Soon. She is almost ready. The one who carries us is almost ready.*

Elodie stops in the doorway with the market's cold on her shoulders. She hears the humming. I watch her hear it. I watch the color leave her face and then, strangely, return — return warmer, return with something in it that looks almost like recognition, the way you might recognize a melody you've never heard because someone sang it to you before you were born.

Her eyes go to the tray. To the charms, all facing the same direction.

All facing *me*.

"What," she says slowly, in the grey trade-tongue of Vess, "are you?"

And I smile at her — the locked door of my mouth doing the best it can — and inside me, in the tongue with the empty shelf, in the language older than kingdoms, I answer her the only way I can, silently, with my whole being, the answer that is also a question, the question that is also a wound:

*I am what you stole. I am what was taken out of you. Open the door, Elodie Oyelaran, and see what is written on the other side of your own face.*

Outside, far off toward the citadel, a bell begins to ring — the wrong bell, the bell that means the Hollow Men are walking, and the charms' song falters like a candle in a draft.

But it does not go out.
