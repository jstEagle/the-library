## 7. Hollow Men at the Market

The night market of Vess opened the way a wound opens: slowly, reluctantly, and with everyone pretending it didn't hurt.

I'd been setting up my stall in Gullmarket for three years, long enough to know which lanterns flickered honest and which had been hexed by vendors who thought a little glamour was beneath the law's notice. It wasn't, technically. Nothing was beneath the law's notice anymore. But the Watch had better things to do than arrest a fishwife whose mackerel gleamed a shade too silver, so the small magics survived like weeds between flagstones—ugly, stubborn, and everywhere if you knew where to look.

My stall was honest work, or as close to it as my trade allowed. Charms strung on waxed cord: a grandmother's Sunday afternoons, a first kiss behind a tannery, the smell of bread from a bakery that had burned down before its owner was born. Ordinary memories, harvested clean and sold back diluted. Enough to make a homesick dockhand weep into his ale. Not enough to interest anyone official.

That was the rule that kept me alive, and I'd never once broken it: sell small. Sell borrowed joy, not stolen history. A charm could hold an hour of someone's happiness without leaving fingerprints. Anything older than that—anything with roots—carried soil, and soil carries scent.

Wren sat behind me on an upturned crate, wrapped in one of my spare cloaks, doing the thing Wren did best: being invisible. Three days under my roof and the child had learned Vess's first lesson faster than most adults manage in a lifetime. Don't look at anyone. Don't be looked at. Exist at the edges.

Except Wren couldn't quite manage it tonight, because the charms were humming again.

It was faint—a vibration more felt than heard, like a fly trapped inside amber. The memory-charms nearest the child trembled against their cords, and twice now a customer had picked up a vial, frowned at it, and set it down with the vague discomfort of a man who has touched something warmer than it should be. I'd moved Wren to the crate where the lantern light fell least, but light wasn't the problem. The problem was that everything I made near that child came out *strange*.

"Stop that," I murmured, not turning around.

Wren said nothing. Wren never said anything in any tongue I understood, though sometimes at night I woke to hear the child talking softly to the dark in that liquid, rolling language—the one that made my fillings ache and my charms hum and some locked room behind my ribs rattle on its hinges.

Business was steady. A cooper bought his wife a charm of her mother's kitchen; I'd lifted it off a dying woman in the Salt Ward two winters back and it had never failed to produce tears. A young guard paid too much for courage—he wanted a memory of standing firm, and I happened to have one from a soldier whose name I never learned. I took his coin and told myself the familiar lie: *this is mercy, this is medicine, no one is hurt that isn't already hurting.*

And then the crowd parted.

People part for Hollow Men the way water parts around a dead thing floating downstream. No one decides to move. They simply find themselves elsewhere, and later they can't say why.

He wore the grey coat, of course. Grey coat, grey gloves, grey hat with a brim pulled low—not to hide his face but to discourage anyone from wanting to see it. Underneath all that grey he was unremarkable in the way of well-made furniture: medium height, medium build, a face you'd forget while looking at it. His name, when he gave it to my neighbor the fruit-seller, was Sef.

"Inquisitor," she breathed, and her apples might as well have been stones for all the commerce they did after that.

He worked down the row methodically. Not raiding—*shopping*. That was the horror of the Hollow Men in market settings; they didn't kick over stalls. They browsed. They picked up wares, turned them over, asked quiet questions in voices like dust settling. Half the row would be closed by morning anyway, because fear is contagious and merchants are sensible people.

He reached the charm-seller two stalls down from me, a sweating man called Doss who dealt in secondhand courage and recycled grief. Sef held a vial up to his lantern, and I watched Doss's face go through the five stages of a merchant meeting an inquisitor: denial, bargaining, flattery, despair, and the special gray acceptance of a man calculating how much stock he can carry while running.

I should have packed up then. Three years of survival instincts screamed it—*fold the table, pocket the coin box, take the child, walk away slow.* But running draws the eye, and Wren couldn't run fast, and there was still a chance—one thin chance—that a Hollow Man browsing Gullmarket had nothing to do with me.

Then he was at my stall.

Up close, the grey was wrong in a way I couldn't articulate. Too uniform. Like it had been dyed in a single batch, by the same hands, for every man in the order. He lifted a vial—the cooper's purchase, no, wait, that one was gone—no, the baker's daughter's wedding day, a pretty little thing, pale gold and harmless.

"How much?" His voice was mild. Mild as milk.

"Four coppers." My hands were steady. I made them be steady. "Wedding day, sir. Very gentle. Good for sleeping."

"Mm." He turned it in his grey gloves. And then, because the gods hate a comfortable thief, he did the thing no customer ever did: he uncorked it and held it beneath his nose.

Charms aren't meant to be tasted raw. That's the whole point of the dilution—you drink the memory secondhand, through oil and prayer and a drop of wine, so it passes through you like weather instead of taking root. Tasting a concentrate straight is crude. Painful. Only professionals did it, and only for one reason: verification.

His nostrils flared. Just slightly. Just enough.

Something in my chest went very cold and very quiet, the way a house goes when a floorboard creaks at night and you stop breathing to listen.

"This one's old," he said.

"They're all old, sir. Secondhand memories. That's the trade."

"No." He set the vial down with terrible gentleness. "Old means recent to someone else. This—" his gloved finger tapped the glass, once, "—this has *soil* in it. Roots. There's a word caught in the sediment of this memory, charmer. A word in a language that doesn't exist. Do you know what happens to people who traffic in languages that don't exist?"

"I sell happy hours," I said. "Weddings. Kitchens. Whatever language they're crying in, it's Vessish."

He smiled. I want to be clear about that smile: it held no malice whatsoever. That was the worst part. Hollow Men didn't hate their work. You can survive being hated. What you cannot survive is being *processed*.

"You'd be surprised," he said, "what languages are crying in."

Behind me, on the crate, the hum rose. All the charms at once, a chord like a wet finger on glass rims, and Wren stood up.

I grabbed the child's wrist. "Not now," I breathed, "sit *down*—"

But Wren wasn't looking at me or at Sef. Wren was looking past us both, at the lantern above my stall, and the flame had gone strange—leaning toward the child like a plant toward sun, stretching, going tall and white and *attentive*. Around the market, other flames began to lean. Forty lanterns, fifty, all bending in their glasses toward one mud-caked kid on a crate, and the crowd noticed, because crowds always notice fire behaving badly.

Sef turned.

I've replayed the next ten seconds perhaps a thousand times since, and in every version I'm grateful for different details. I'm grateful that Doss chose that exact moment to knock over his own display in a panic, because the crash covered my movement. I'm grateful that the crowd's attention was on the lanterns and not on the grey coat turning. Mostly I'm grateful that Wren, whatever ancient thing the child was, still had a child's hand, and a child's hand fits in mine.

I swept the coin box off the table into my satchel, seized the charm-cords in a fistful—let the cooper's wife mourn, let the whole inventory go, glass and gold and three years of careful stock—and pulled Wren off the crate into the dark behind my stall, between the fish-barrels, into the gap where Gullmarket's vendors pissed and dumped brine and where no respectable lantern leaned.

"Halt" was all Sef said. One word, mild as milk. It landed on my spine like a hand.

We ran.

Not out the main way—out the gullet, the drainage cut behind the fish stalls where the cobbles were greasy with scales and the stink would follow me for days. Behind us I heard the crowd's noise change pitch, that particular collective gasp of Vess citizens witnessing an arrest, followed by silence, followed by the sound of grey boots not hurrying, which was worse than hurrying. Hollow Men don't chase. They *proceed*. They know something most prey animals don't: that in Vess, eventually, everything proceeds toward them.

We came up out of the cut by the tannery sluice, soaked to the knee, Wren wheezing, my satchel banging my hip with the lovely stupid music of stolen coins. I hauled the child up Tanner's Stair, three flights of rotting steps, along the drying-loft catwalk where the hides hung like flayed ghosts and the reek covered our trail better than any spell.

At the far end of the catwalk I stopped, bent double, and listened.

Boots below. Unhurried. Two sets—I cursed silently; one I might have risked doubling back past, but two meant a coordinated sweep, which meant Sef hadn't come alone, which meant I'd been right about the soil and the roots and the word in the sediment.

Ashkaran. The word was Ashkaran. The residue in my charms was Ashkaran, which was impossible, because Ashkar was nothing, nowhere, a hole in every map, and yet the child spoke it nightly to my dark and my charms hummed and a Hollow Man had *tasted* it off my stock and now two sets of grey boots were proceeding up Tanner's Stair with the patience of men who get paid either way.

Wren tugged my sleeve. Pointed—not at the stairs, not at the street. At a door.

There was no door there. I knew that doorframe intimately; I'd sheltered under it during rains. It opened onto a brick wall, bricked up decades ago, a dead end the pigeons owned. Wren pointed at it again, insistent, and made a small urgent sound, and then put a small muddy palm flat against the wood.

The door opened onto a room.

Not the wall. A room—lamplit, low-ceilinged, smelling of tallow and old paper, and I did not question it, because questioning is for people with time. I pulled Wren through and the door swung shut behind us and became, when I turned around, a wall with a bricked-up frame, pigeon-droppings and all, exactly as it had been for thirty years.

The room was somebody's study. Books everywhere, a cold stove, a chair with a coat over it. On the desk lay charts, half-drawn maps, and a compass rose inked with a care so loving it hurt to look at. And in the corner, frozen mid-reach for a candlestick, stood a broad-shouldered woman with a cartographer's squint and a knife already half-drawn, staring at us like we'd fallen out of the sky.

Which, as far as she could tell, we had.

"Don't scream," I said. "I'll explain. Mostly. Some of it. The parts I understand, which is none of it."

Her eyes went from me to Wren to the wall-door and back. She was, I would learn, not a woman easily rattled—but no one trains for children arriving through solid brick trailing the smell of the sea and the tannery.

"Elodie," Wren said suddenly—clearly, impossibly, in perfect Vessish, the first word the child had ever spoken in any tongue I knew.

Both the woman and I stared.

"Elodie," Wren said again, and pointed one grimy finger at the maps on the desk—at the empty spaces where coastlines should be—and then pressed that same finger flat against my chest, over the locked room behind my ribs, over the place where the humming lived.

Outside, faint through brick and mortar, I heard grey boots reach the top of Tanner's Stair. Pause. Proceed away, unhurried, patient as winter.

The woman found her voice. "You brought a Hollow Man's quarry through my wall," she said, "and the child knows your name."

"Yes," I said. "Also, I think I owe you rent."

* * *

We waited out the night in Ilsa's study—I got her name eventually, pried loose between accusations and tea she made in a pot crusted with old ink. She didn't ask many questions, which I distrusted profoundly until I realized why: she wasn't shocked enough to ask them. People who have seen the impossible ask questions. People who have *expected* the impossible just pour the tea.

She watched Wren the way a scholar watches a manuscript she's afraid to touch. And when the child slept at last, curled in her reading chair with my cloak for a blanket, Ilsa looked at the empty spaces on her own maps—at the gaps where her life's work said land should be—and said quietly, almost to herself:

"I charted a coastline there once. Twenty years ago. Inked it myself, soundings and all. Then one morning the charts were blank and the Admiralty told me I'd been drinking." Her jaw tightened. "They retired me for insisting otherwise."

"What was it called?" I asked. "The coastline."

She looked at Wren sleeping, and at me, and I watched her decide to trust me the way you decide to jump a wide gap: all at once, and terrified.

"That's the wrong question," she said. "Ask me what the *word* was. The one your inquisitor tasted. Because I've spent twenty years remembering a single syllable of it, Elodie, and tonight—" she nodded at the child, at the humming charms spilled across my table, "—tonight is the first time it hasn't felt like madness."

I didn't answer. I couldn't. Because she was right—it was the wrong question, and the right question was the one pressing its small muddy palm against my sternum even in sleep, asking, in a language older than kingdoms, something I could almost—

Almost—remember.

By dawn, I knew two things with absolute certainty.

The Hollow Men had my scent now, and they did not give up a scent.

And somewhere in this city, in Maro's warm and crooked heart, there was a vault—and in that vault, I suddenly knew with a certainty that arrived like a blow, were bottles with my name on them.

I shook Wren awake. "Time to go meet my father-in-all-but-blood," I said. "Let's find out what he knows about doors."
