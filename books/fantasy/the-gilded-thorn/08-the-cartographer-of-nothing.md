## 8. The Cartographer of Nothing

There are three kinds of people in Vess who will talk to you before dawn: priests, thieves, and the ruined. I was two of the three, and I knew where to find the third.

Ilsa Venn lived above a chandler's shop in the Tallow Quarter, where the air always smelled faintly of rendered fat and apology. The whole street leaned east, as if the buildings themselves were tired of standing straight after twenty years of holding up a city that had forgotten what it was leaning away from. Her window glowed at that hour—candlelight, not lamp-oil, because candles you can blow out quickly when someone knocks.

Someone knocked. It was me.

She opened the door on the chain, one eye visible, gray as old pewter. "Market's closed."

"I'm not selling."

"Then you're buying, and I haven't got anything worth the coin." But her eye had already dropped to my satchel, and I watched her do the arithmetic every ruined person does—the quick, hungry calculation of whether the stranger at your door is your salvation or your end.

"Maro sent me," I lied, because it was faster than the truth.

The chain came off so fast it nearly sang.

* * *

Her rooms were a museum of a profession that no longer existed. Maps hung everywhere—not maps of Vess or the free cities or any of the trade routes that kept the world fed and fat, but maps of nothing. Blank parchment with careful borders. Charts of coastline that dissolved into empty space halfway across, as if the cartographer had simply run out of world. One enormous sheet behind her worktable showed a country drawn entirely in dotted lines, the way you'd sketch a ghost.

"You're the mapmaker," I said. "The one they struck off the guild rolls."

"They struck me off for drawing accurately." She poured tea without asking if I wanted any; it was that kind of house. She was younger than her voice—forty, maybe, with ink stains up both forearms like failed tattoos—and she moved through her cluttered rooms with the economy of someone who'd stopped expecting visitors. "The guild called my charts 'speculative fictions.' The magistrates called them sedition. Both agreed on the burning."

"And you kept drawing."

"I stopped sleeping instead. It seemed the better trade." She sat down across from me and looked at me properly for the first time, and I felt it the way you feel a physician's hands—clinical, thorough, slightly unpleasant. "You're the memory-thief. Maro's girl. He talks about you when he's drunk, which is to say, always."

"What does he say?"

"That you have no past and excellent manners, and that the second is more frightening than the first." She smiled without warmth. "So. What does a thief of other people's yesterdays want with a woman who draws places that don't exist?"

I set Wren's rubbing on her table.

I'd made it that morning while the child slept—a charcoal impression of the door Wren had drawn on my floorboards, pressed paper against wood until every line came up true. I'd told myself it was evidence. Proof, if I needed proof, that I hadn't dreamed the whole impossible week. But standing in Ilsa Venn's candlelit room, watching her look at it, I understood I'd really come for something else. I'd come because everyone else had told me to drown the child, forget the child, run from the child, and some stubborn splinter in me wanted one person—one—to look at that drawing and say something other than *destroy it*.

Ilsa went still.

Not startled-still. Still the way water goes still before it freezes. Her eyes moved over the lines—the frame, the arch, the keystone shape at the top that wasn't quite any symbol I knew—and her breathing changed. Slowed. I've spent years reading faces across a market stall, watching for the flicker that says *this charm is real, this grief is fresh*, and I know every species of human reaction. This was none of them. This was recognition so complete it looked like grief.

"Where," she said quietly, "did you get this?"

"A child drew it. On my floor. In charcoal."

"A child." She said the word carefully, like someone testing ice. "And this child speaks?"

"Not our tongue. Something else. Old." My mouth was dry. "It makes charms hum, Ilsa. Silverwork that's held a hundred memories, and it hums when the child talks."

Ilsa stood up. Walked to the great map behind her worktable—the one drawn in dotted lines—and put her finger on a place where the dots were densest, near a coast that faded into blankness like a sentence that trails off—

"There," she said. "Tell me what you see."

"Nothing. Dots."

"Look harder. What shape do the dots make?"

I looked. And looking, I felt the vertigo again, the same sick lurch I'd felt when Wren first spoke—that sense of standing on a stair that isn't there. Because the dots did make a shape. A coastline. An estuary. A river delta branching like a hand spread flat against the sea. I could see it, the way you can see a face in fire, except this wasn't pareidolia. The pattern was deliberate. She had drawn a country using only absence, the way a sculptor makes a horse by carving away everything that isn't horse.

"It's a country," I said slowly. "But there's no name on it."

"No." Her voice had gone very soft. "There never is. That's the trick of it, Elodie. That's the whole filthy trick." She turned to face me, and her eyes were bright and terrible. "Twenty-two years ago I was a journeyman cartographer with a commission from the Guild of Wayfarers: survey the northeastern coast, chart the trade approaches, note harbors. Standard work. I sailed out of Corvess harbor with two assistants and a hold full of ink." She laughed, one dry note with no humor in it anywhere. "We found a coast that wasn't on any of our charts. A green coast, rich, with cities—I saw towers, Elodie, white towers with red roofs, I saw terraced orchards going up hillsides like stairs for giants. There were ships in the harbor. People waved to us from the walls."

"And?"

"And we anchored overnight to take soundings, and in the morning my assistants remembered nothing. Not the coast, not the towers, not the waving people. They looked at their own notes—soundings, sketches, bearings taken in good light—and they said, *what is this? Who wrote this? Why is it in my hand?*" She picked up her teacup, discovered it was empty, and set it down again with exaggerated care. "One of them wept. The other threw his journal into the sea. I kept mine. That was my crime, you understand. Not the drawing. The keeping."

"The Fall of Ashkar," I said. The words came out of me the way words come out of a sleepwalker—flat, automatic, a phrase I'd heard a thousand times in a city built on the phrase. *Since the Fall of Ashkar. Since the Fall. Twenty years since the Fall.* Every law, every curfew, every burning of a hedge-witch's herbs—all of it dated from the Fall of Ashkar, the catastrophe so terrible that even its name survived only as a marker, a date, a wound everyone agreed to stop touching.

"Yes," Ilsa said. "Everyone knows the Fall of Ashkar. Ask anyone what fell, and watch their face. Go on. Describe it to me. What happened at the Fall of Ashkar?"

I opened my mouth.

Nothing came out.

I tried again, reaching for it the way you reach for a word on the tip of your tongue, and felt the same sensation I'd felt staring at the dotted map: a shape, an outline, a weight of something enormous just past the reach of my mind—and then smooth blankness, like a wall where a door should be. My palms actually prickled. The scar on my right palm—the old one, the key-shaped one I'd had since before I could remember having anything—itched like a healing wound.

"You see?" Ilsa said gently. "You know the date. You know the word. You know that something fell. But ask yourself *what Ashkar was*—a kingdom? A republic? On a river or the sea? Its neighbors? Its king? Its language?" She spread her hands. "Twenty years, and no ballad, no chronicle, no ruin, no refugee descendants, no border treaty, no grave. Conquered countries leave all of those. Conquered countries bleed into the conqueror—names, recipes, grudges, songs. Ashkar left nothing. Ashkar left less than nothing. Ashkar left a hole shaped like itself and a single word to mark the edge of the hole."

"Vane conquered it," I said. "Everyone knows Aurel Vane rose during the—"

"During the Fall. Yes. And what was he before?" She didn't wait. "No one knows. What did he conquer *with*? No one knows. Where are the veterans of that campaign, the war-brides, the occupation records?" She was pacing now, between her hanging ghosts of maps, and her voice climbed. "Elodie, I have spent twenty years doing the only thing a cartographer can do with a hole: measuring its edges. And the edges are wrong. Do you understand? Erasure leaves ragged edges. Burn a library and ash remains. Sack a city and ruins remain. Kill a people and orphans remain. But this—" she slapped the dotted map, "—this is clean. This is *written*. Someone took a pen to the world and wrote Ashkar out of it, the way a clerk strikes a debt from a ledger, and the ledger closes up smooth behind the strike like water behind a stone."

The room felt colder. Or I felt smaller. Hard to say which.

"You're saying it wasn't conquered."

"I'm saying conquest is a knife, and this was an eraser." She stopped pacing. Looked at me. Looked at the rubbing. "Now tell me about the child."

So I told her. The knock at the wrong hour. The mud, the silence, the language that wasn't a language so much as a weather system—syllables that moved through my rooms and made my charms ring faintly, like distant bells. The drawings. The door on my floorboards, drawn over and over, always the same door, always with that keystone mark. The way the child looked at me—not the way children look at strangers, wary or wheedling, but the way you look at a house you used to live in.

When I finished, Ilsa was quiet for a long time. Then she went to a locked drawer, unlocked it with a key from around her neck, and brought out a leather folio gone soft as cloth with handling.

"My original notes," she said. "From the coast. From the morning my assistants forgot." She opened it. Sketches. A harbor. White towers, red roofs. Terraced orchards climbing hillsides like stairs for giants. And at the bottom of one page, copied in a careful hand: a symbol. A keystone mark.

My skin went cold from the scalp down.

"That's it," I whispered. "That's the mark on the door. That's what the child draws."

"I know." She traced it with one fingertip, not quite touching. "For twenty years I thought this was a sigil of whatever nation I'd found—their sign for a city gate, maybe, or a god, or a dynasty. But I've had two decades to think, and here's what I think now." She looked up. "I don't think it marked a door, Elodie. I think it *was* one. I think what I saw from that ship was the last morning of a country that was being closed like a book, and I think what I sketched was the cover."

"The Last Door," I said. I hadn't meant to say it aloud. The words arrived in my mouth fully formed, wearing familiarity like a borrowed coat—words I had no right to know, and knew anyway, the way Wren's language hummed in silver I'd never tuned. "Why do I know that phrase? Ilsa, why do I know things I shouldn't know?"

"Because you're a thief," she said, "and somewhere in that satchel of yours, or in that head of yours, is something that remembers. Memory doesn't die when it's stolen, girl. It just changes owners. And erasure—" her mouth twisted, "—erasure has never once, in all my measuring, been perfect. There are always scraps. A word here. A scar there. A child, perhaps, who walked out through the closing door before it shut."

Outside, the Tallow Quarter was waking. Carts rattled past below; the chandler's boys began the day's first rendering. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a world that had agreed, smoothly and completely, to be exactly the size it was.

And I stood in a room full of maps of nothing, holding a charcoal rubbing of a door, and felt hope arrive in me like a blade—terrible hope, hope with an edge, the kind that cuts the hoper. Because if Ilsa was right, then everything was wrong. The laws were wrong, the history was wrong, the twenty years of fear my whole life was built on were founded on a forgery. And if everything was wrong, then the child speaking the dead tongue in my back room wasn't a curse or a trap or a stray.

The child was a survivor. Maybe the only one.

And something in me—something older than my three years in Vess, something that predated my own memory the way bedrock predates the house built on it—leaned toward that child like a plant toward light.

"There's a route," Ilsa said suddenly, half to herself, already pulling a fresh sheet toward her. "If the Door exists—if it's where I think it is, in the fen country northeast, where my charts go blank—then there's a route. Through the Burnt Marches, along the old salt road. It would take—"

"Ilsa." I put my hand flat on her table. "Slow down. I came here to learn what the drawing means. I didn't come to—"

"To what? To believe?" She looked at me with those pewter eyes, and beneath the ruin and the ink stains I saw, briefly, the journeyman who had once stood on a ship's deck watching towers wave goodbye. "Girl, you came here because you've already believed. You believed the moment the child spoke and your silver rang. Everything since has just been you looking for permission." She dipped her pen. "Consider this permission. Now sit down and tell me everything about that child, because if even half of what I suspect is true, then the most dangerous object in the world is currently sleeping in your spare room—and every Hollow Man in Vess would burn this city down to get it."

I thought of Wren asleep under my roof. Small. Mud-caked. Humming in a dead tongue.

"Hurry, then," I said. "Tell me how to keep a miracle hidden."

Ilsa's pen paused over the blank page.

"That," she said, "is the one thing no map can teach you."

* * *

I left before full light, taking a copy of the keystone mark and a promise: Ilsa would chart the route to the fen country, the old salt road, and send word through Maro within the week. At the door she caught my sleeve.

"Elodie. One more thing." Her grip was stronger than her frame suggested. "The child draws doors. Think about what that means. Doors don't get drawn by people who are inside. Doors get drawn by people who remember being outside them—or inside them—when they mattered." She released me. "Whatever that child is, it chose your floorboards. Out of every hearth in Vess, yours. Ask yourself why a miracle knocks on a thief's door."

I walked home through streets going gray-gold with dawn, past shuttered stalls and early bakers, and the question rode my shoulder the whole way like a second shadow.

Why mine?

I had no answer. But my scarred palm itched the entire way home, and when I let myself in, Wren was awake, sitting cross-legged on the floor beside the charcoal door-drawing, waiting. The child looked up at me, pointed one small finger at the mark on the boards, then at my right hand—at the scar shaped like a key I'd carried all my life without knowing what it opened.

Then Wren said a word in that humming, ancient tongue, gentle as an invitation, and though I had never learned it, I understood it perfectly:

*Soon.*
