## 9. Three Years of Small Charms

People in Vess assume I have a past. They assume it the way they assume the river flows downhill and the tax collector has a cousin on the city council—automatically, without interest, without ever once checking. A woman my age must have a mother somewhere, a village, a first kiss, a reason she flinches at church bells. The whole economy of gossip depends on it.

I let them assume. Assumptions are free, and I've never been able to afford anything else.

The truth is that I arrived in Vess three years ago with nothing behind my eyes but fog. Not amnesia—I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I had. Amnesia is a wound. What I had was an absence, smooth as the inside of a bottle. I knew how to speak, how to count, how to pick a lock I'd never seen before. I knew the names of things. I did not know why I knew them, or who had taught me, or what it felt like to be taught. I stood at the South Gate with mud on my boots and a hole where a childhood should be, and the gate guard asked my name, and I said "Elodie," because it was the only word in my mouth that sounded like mine.

He waved me through. That's Vess for you. They'll wave through anyone who isn't carrying magic, and they can't see what's missing from a person, only what's leaking out of her.

* * *

The first winter, I nearly died of ordinary things. Cold, hunger, the particular cruelty of a city that charges you for the privilege of sleeping indoors. I worked the fish docks until my hands split, then a dye-house until my lungs burned blue, then nothing, because there was no work left for a girl with no references and no story.

It was in that nothing—a doorway off Tanner's Row, rain coming down like the sky held a grudge—that I met Maro.

He found me going through his rubbish, which was embarrassing, and then he watched me talk a drunk dyer out of a memory he didn't want anyway—the man kept reliving the day his brother drowned, and I told him, without knowing how I knew, that grief could be lifted out like a splinter if you knew where the skin was thin. I didn't know what I was saying. It came out of the hole in me the way water comes out of a cracked jug: not because you pour it, but because it's there.

Maro took me upstairs, fed me eel pie, and asked me one question. Not *who are you*. Not *where are you from*. He asked, "Can you do it again?"

That was Maro in a sentence. He never once asked about my past in three years. I used to think it was kindness. Later I wondered if it was recognition—one hollow person knows another the way a lock knows its key.

* * *

Here is how the trade works, since you're wondering, and since explaining it keeps me from thinking about other things.

Every strong feeling leaves a residue. Joy especially—it soaks into a person like wine into bread. Most people carry their residues around their whole lives, hoarding even the ones that rot them. But a thief with the knack can ease a memory loose, whole, and leave the person lighter. Sometimes they thank you. The dying thank you most often; they're tired of carrying.

Once it's out, the memory keeps. Fold it into wax, press it with a sigil, and you have a charm. Crack the seal and the memory plays—not in your head exactly, more against it, like light through a window. For an hour or so you are someone else's best day.

There are rules. Maro gave me three, and I've added a fourth of my own.

One: never take pain unless it's offered. Pain-memories keep badly. They sour the batch, and worse, they sour you.

Two: never sell the same memory twice. The dead don't complain, but the living do, and the living have cousins.

Three—and here Maro's voice goes quiet, the way it does when he means it—never steal your own past. If a broker ever offers you a vial with your name on it, walk away. Whatever's in there was taken for a reason.

My fourth rule I made myself: never look too long at the fog behind your own eyes. Fog is patient. It waits for you to stare.

* * *

I got good. That's the shameful part, how fast I got good. Three months after Tanner's Row I was lifting memories clean enough that the donors didn't stagger. Six months, and I could tell a memory's vintage by its weight in my palm—a soldier's homecoming sits heavier than a baker's morning, a wedding runs bright and quick like mercury.

By the second year I had a stall in the night market between a candle-seller and a woman who claimed to sell luck, which is a scam, whereas what I sold was real, which is arguably a bigger scam. My charms went for good coin. Lovers bought each other first meetings. Grieving mothers bought an hour of a child's laughter—never their own child, that would be unbearable, but some child, any child, the generic sound of small joy. Old men bought mornings. Everyone in Vess, it turned out, was hungry for a life that wasn't theirs, and I was the woman with the ladle.

I told myself I was a mercy. Mostly I even believed it.

* * *

But here's what nobody tells you about living on other people's joy: you develop a connoisseur's palate and an empty stomach. I could describe four hundred happy hours in loving detail. Sunlight on a lake where a father taught a daughter to skip stones. The smell of a kitchen on a festival morning, cardamom and woodsmoke. A boy running through an orchard with stolen plums in both fists while someone laughed behind him—

No. Not that one. That one isn't mine either. None of them are mine. That's rather the point.

Some nights I'd crack a defective charm—one with a hairline flaw in the wax, unsellable—and let the memory wash over me just to feel something arrive from outside my own skull. It never stayed. By dawn it had drained back out of me like bathwater, leaving the tub of myself exactly as empty as before. Maro caught me doing it once. He didn't scold me. He just looked at me across the workshop with an expression I couldn't read, and said, "Careful, girl. Borrowed light shows you the shape of the dark."

I thought he was being poetic. Maro is often poetic when he's avoiding something.

* * *

Why didn't I go looking? You're wondering that too, aren't you. A woman with no past, in a city full of memory-brokers, and she never once went shopping for herself?

Because of the rules. Because of Maro's third rule, delivered in that quiet voice. Because somewhere under the fog was a certainty with no evidence attached: *if you find it, it will cost more than you have.* And because—inversion of inversions—I liked the fog. Or I needed it. When you have nothing behind you, every direction is forward, and no one can make you go back. Invisibility isn't just what Vess does to the poor. For me it was a coat I'd sewn myself, stitch by stitch, and worn so long I'd forgotten I was cold.

A woman came to my stall once, early in the second year, holding a charm I didn't remember making. She said, "This is yours, I think. It was in a lot I bought at auction." And she set it down among my wares and walked away before I could answer.

I didn't touch it for six days. It sat in my lockbox like a spider I'd agreed to cohabit with. On the seventh night I held it over a candle, meaning to melt it, and the heat made the wax sweat, and through the sweat I saw—for half a heartbeat—a color. Orange-gold. Firelight, or fruit, or sunset on a wall I almost remembered.

I snuffed the candle and buried the charm in the river mud under the pier, and I did not sleep, and in the morning I put a new sigil on my stall sign and raised my prices, and told myself the shaking in my hands was the cold.

That's the thing about chosen blindness. You have to keep choosing it, daily, like a vow. And every day the choosing gets harder, because the fog doesn't stay still. It thins. Little shapes move in it—edges of doors, hemlines of dresses, the back of someone's head turning toward you just before you wake.

* * *

And then, last week, a child knocked on my door caked in river mud, speaking a language that made my charms hum in their box like bees, and drew a door on my floorboards with a finger, and the fog didn't thin.

It tore.

I haven't slept properly since. I keep seeing the orchard—that orange-gold, the same color from the buried charm, the same color that lives now behind Wren's eyes when the child looks at me like I'm a word on the tip of the world's tongue. I keep hearing syllables I shouldn't understand and understanding them anyway, which means the language didn't come in through my ears. It came up from underneath. From the fog. From whatever the fog is *for*.

So tonight I did the thing I swore I'd never do. I dug the charm out of the river mud. It's on my table right now, dripping, the wax scarred but whole, humming faintly in time with the sleeping child on my cot.

Maro's third rule says walk away.

But Maro also went pale as whey when I showed him the rubbing of that door, and ordered me to drown a child, and Maro has been wrong before—he was wrong about the eel pie, he was wrong about the Corvane job, he was wrong about me, if being wrong about someone means failing to predict that she'd stop obeying.

Tomorrow I'll take the rubbing to someone else. There's a mapmaker, Ilsa, disgraced, who supposedly charts places that stopped existing—which is nonsense, obviously, except that everything I believed was nonsense has started knocking on my door lately, sometimes literally.

Tonight, though, tonight I sit with the charm in my cupped hands, mud and all, and I say aloud, to the fog, to the child, to whoever stole three years of me and left the rest:

"I'm coming to collect."

The charm hums. Under the pier, under the city, under twenty years of somebody else's careful silence, something answers.
