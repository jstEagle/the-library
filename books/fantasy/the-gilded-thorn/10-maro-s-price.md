## 10. Maro's Price

There is a particular arithmetic to guilt, and I have spent forty years becoming fluent in it. Most men think of guilt as a debt, something you pay down in installments—confession here, charity there, a coin slipped to a beggar on the anniversary of your worst day. Amateurs. Guilt isn't a debt. It's inventory. You store it, you rotate it, you keep it out of the light so it doesn't spoil, and above all you never, ever let anyone audit the ledger.

I am telling you this so you understand that when I sold Elodie Oyelaran to the Hollow Men, I did not do it because I am a monster. I did it because I am an excellent bookkeeper.

* * *

Let me tell you about my shop, since everyone always wants to know about the shop. Maro's Curiosities, third door past the tannery row in Vess's low quarter, where the smell of rendered fat covers a multitude of sins and the rent is paid in cash and silence. To the casual customer: brass instruments of dubious origin, moth-eaten tapestries, jars of things pickled in vinegar that may or may not have once had eyes. To the knowing customer: charms. Memory-work, bottled and stoppered and priced according to sweetness.

And to one customer in particular—the kind who arrives without arriving, whose footsteps make no sound because sound has agreed to look the other way—to him, the shop was simply a place where a man might be found who knew where other men could be found.

The Hollow Man came on a Tuesday. They always come on Tuesdays. I don't know if it's doctrine or scheduling, but twenty years of living under Vane's law teaches you the rhythms of the thing that hunts you. Tuesday, market day, when the stalls are up and the alleys are crowded and a man can slip into a shop without being remarked upon. The Hollow Men are punctual the way death is punctual: not eager, not late, merely inevitable with good timekeeping.

He stood in my doorway for a long moment before entering, which was its own kind of message. *I could stand here all day. I could stand here until you notice me. Notice me.*

"Maro," he said. His voice was like paper being folded. "You're difficult to find."

"I'm a shopkeeper," I said. "We're famously stationary."

Something moved beneath his hood—not a face, exactly, more like the memory of one. The Hollow Men eat memory, you understand. Their own first, professionally speaking. An inquisitor fresh from the Academy still has eyes, a mouth, expressions. One who has been at the work for decades has only a hood, and suggestions. This one had been at the work a long time. When he tilted his head, I felt my own memories lean away from him, like cats avoiding a dog.

"You deal in recollection," he said.

"I deal in curiosities."

"You deal in recollection," he repeated, with the patience of a man who has never once been interrupted successfully, "and three nights ago, a charm passed through the night market carrying residue of a dead language."

I kept my face doing what shopkeepers' faces do: pleasant, faintly bored, already calculating his change. Inside, my heart performed a maneuver I can only describe as dropping through several floors of a building and landing in the cellar among the pickled things.

"Ashkaran," he said.

"There's no such language," I said, and this was true, officially true, true in every archive and every schoolbook and every gravestone in Vess. There had never been a country called Ashkar. There had never been a Fall. There was only the Unification, twenty years ago, when Aurel Vane brought peace to lands that had always been peaceful, and anyone who said otherwise was a madman or a traitor, and both categories were handled by the gentleman currently dripping silence onto my floorboards.

"No such language," he agreed pleasantly. "And yet."

"And yet," I said, because arguing with a Hollow Man is like arguing with a hole in the world; the hole wins by continuing to exist.

"The charm was traced to a seller. A woman. Young. Works the night market under no name worth remembering." He paused, and I want you to appreciate that pause. He was giving me room. He was letting me see the shape of the trap before it closed, because the Hollow Men believe—and I think they're right—that a man who betrays willingly gives better information than a man who betrays under duress. Duress makes liars of us all. Willingness makes accountants.

"She has a teacher," he said. "Someone who taught her the trade. Someone who taught her the rules. Never steal your own past—isn't that the rule? Such an odd rule. So specific. Rules like that come from somewhere, Maro. Rules like that come from a wound."

The cellar of my heart grew very cold and very full of vinegar.

"What is it you imagine I can do for you?" I asked.

He reached into his robe and set something on my counter. Not money. Money would have been insulting—or rather, money would have been honest, and honesty was not on the menu. It was a small glass vial, empty, stoppered with wax the color of dried blood.

"Do you know what we do with practitioners we take?" he asked. "We don't kill them. Killing wastes the goods. We drink them. Everything they were, everything they remembered, every kindness and cruelty and afternoon nap—it goes into us, and they walk away hollow, and eventually they lie down somewhere and forget to get up." He turned the vial slowly with one finger. "This vial is for her. But it doesn't need to be used today. Or this week. Vials keep."

"You're threatening her."

"I'm offering you a schedule," he said. "There is a difference. The difference is you."

* * *

After he left—the door didn't even open for him; he simply stopped being in my shop, which is a trick I hope never to grow accustomed to—I sat for a long time among my curiosities and took stock of my inventory, both kinds.

Here is what the world knows about Maro: fence, fixer, the man who taught half the low quarter's cutpurses which pockets hold faith and which hold only keys. Here is what Elodie knows: that I found her three years ago, mud-poor and memory-light, a girl with a thief's hands and a hole where her childhood should have been, and I fed her, and I trained her, and I gave her the rule that keeps her alive. *Never steal your own past.* She thinks I gave her that rule out of wisdom. Out of care.

She's half right. It is wise. And I do care about her, in the way a man cares about the finest piece in his collection, in the way a miser cares about the coin he'll never spend. Care is not the same as clean hands. Nothing in my life has ever been the same as clean hands.

Here is what nobody knows, what I have kept rotated and out of the light for twenty years:

I was there. At the border. Before the Fall, before the unwriting, back when there was a country called Ashkar and a border post called Grey Ford where a bored guard named Maro checked carts for smuggled wool and thought mostly about his gambling debts.

They came to me in the last autumn. Memory-thieves, though I didn't have the word then. Foreigners with soft voices and softer smiles, and they offered me gold—more gold than a border guard sees in ten years—for one simple service. Look the other way at night. Let the carts through unexamined. That's all. What was in the carts? Bottles, they said. Preserves. Fruit from the orchards.

Fruit from the orchards. Gods forgive me, that's almost funny. It was the orchards themselves they were taking. The memories of them. Whole villages sold their happiest hours to these people, bottle by bottle, and I waved the carts through and counted my gold and told myself it was commerce, harmless commerce, a victimless trade in nostalgia.

Then Vane came. And I learned what the bottles were *for*. He wasn't buying memories the way you buy apples. He was buying a country—one hour, one joy, one grief at a time—until there wasn't enough of Ashkar left holding itself together, and the whole nation came apart like wet paper, and the maps burned, and the archives rewrote themselves, and the name *Ashkar* became a sound that meant nothing anywhere in the world.

Except in me. Because I was there, and I remembered, and remembering was suddenly the most dangerous crime in existence.

So I did what any sensible criminal does. I went to the new authorities and I purchased absolution. There are brokers for such things, even now, even in Vess—men who can take a memory out of your head and seal it in glass. I sold them my guilt. All of it. The border post, the gold, the carts, the country coming apart while I counted coins. Twenty years of my life, sealed in wax and stored in a vault, and in exchange I walked out into the bright new Unification a man with a gap in him shaped exactly like treason, comfortable as an old boot.

That's the secret, if you're wondering, of how men like me sleep at night. We don't have less conscience than other men. We have *inventory*.

* * *

Three days. That's what I gave myself. Three days to find another way, because I want it on the record—even if the record is just this, just me talking to the inside of my own skull—that I looked for another way.

I considered warning her. Considered it seriously, lying awake in the room above the shop, listening to the tannery vats bubble their eternal digestion. Warning her meant running her out of Vess ahead of the hunt, and hunted girls run toward the people who taught them, and she'd run straight back to me, and the Hollow Man would be standing in my doorway again, and this time he wouldn't bring an empty vial. He'd bring two.

I considered killing him. Be serious, Maro, I told myself. You're sixty-one years old and your weapon of choice is haggling.

I considered confessing. Actually sat down one evening with ink and paper to write it all out—the border, the bribes, the vault, the whole rotten ledger—and got as far as *My name is Maro and twenty years ago* before I stopped. Because here's the thing about confession: it doesn't erase the debt. It just publishes it. And the Hollow Men read everything published.

Besides—and this is the part I keep circling, the part that tastes worst—there was the girl herself to consider. Elodie. If they took her, drank her, left her hollow on some roadside, she'd feel nothing. That's the mercy of it, the obscene mercy. The woman they'd destroy wouldn't be the woman I know. She'd be an empty dress with her walking rhythm. Whereas if I gave her up cleanly, quickly, pointed them true and stepped back—no, wait. That's not the consideration. That's the sales pitch. Let me try again.

The consideration is this: they weren't only going to ask about her. A Hollow Man who smells Ashkaran in the market starts pulling threads, and every thread in the low quarter runs through my shop eventually. If I stonewalled him, he'd start drinking *my* neighbors, *my* contacts, one thread at a time, until he found the pattern, and the pattern leads to a border guard named Maro, and the border guard's guilt sits in a vault that would crack open like an egg. My buried crime wouldn't stay buried. It would be exhumed, examined, and entered into evidence, and the man who let the thieves in—the man whose greed helped unmake a nation—would die for it publicly, in the square, as a lesson.

Whereas the girl. The girl was one thread. Cut one thread, save the fabric.

I've heard worse reasoning from better men. I've *made* worse reasoning for better men. That's the thing about betrayal with a human motive: it never feels like betrayal from the inside. From the inside it feels like survival. It feels like arithmetic.

* * *

On the fourth day, the Hollow Man returned, and I gave him my answer.

"She works the night market," I said. "East side, near the fountain with the broken saint. She wears a grey coat with a tear in the left cuff—she won't mend it, says it's lucky. She sells under the name 'the Quiet Girl.' She lives alone, third floor, the crooked house off Tanner's Lane with the blue door." I made myself say the blue door. I made myself remember teaching her to pick the lock of that same door when she was seventeen and had lost her key, laughing, both of us crouched in the dark like conspirators, which is precisely what we were, though neither of us knew against whom.

The Hollow Man absorbed it all without writing anything down. They don't write. Writing is memory outside the body, and they prefer their meals internal.

"And the teacher?" he asked. "The one who taught her the rule?"

"Gone," I said. "Dead, probably. People in this trade don't retire."

It was the first outright lie I'd told him, and he let it pass, which frightened me more than any question. A Hollow Man lets a lie pass only when the truth is already in his possession, or when the lie is more useful than the truth. I couldn't decide which was worse.

He rose—he didn't stand so much as become vertical—and paused at the threshold that would not open for him.

"You're wondering if you've done the right thing," he said. Paper folding. "Don't. Right is a word for people with intact histories. You did the *survivable* thing. In my experience, that's the only verb most men conjugate."

And then he was gone, and the shop smelled faintly of cold cellars, and I stood behind my counter surrounded by other people's stolen joys and felt absolutely nothing, which is the finest proof of craftsmanship there is. I had built this emptiness myself, joint by joint, and it held my weight perfectly.

* * *

That evening I did three things, and I will report them honestly because a ledger is worthless unless it's complete.

First, I opened a bottle of plum brandy I'd been saving for a celebration that never materialized, and I drank a third of it, and I did not taste it.

Second, I took down a charm from the back shelf—one of Elodie's early pieces, clumsy work, a soldier's memory of his daughter learning to walk, sold back to him at cost because he was dying and I was feeling expansive that decade—and I held it up to the lamp and watched the light move through it like something swimming, and I put it back, and I did not smash it, and I have no defense for why.

Third, I wrote a note. Four words. I addressed it to the crooked house with the blue door, and I paid a runner double to deliver it fast and ask no questions, and the four words were:

*Run. They know. —M.*

Yes. I know. I hear it too—the absurdity, the obscenity of it. I sell the girl to the wolves and then send her a map of the wolf den. Call it conscience. Call it cowardice wearing conscience's coat. Call it the oldest trick in the guilty man's book: betray them, then warn them, and let yourself believe the warning cancels the betrayal, the way a payment cancels a debt. It doesn't. I know it doesn't. The ledger doesn't care about your feelings. The ledger only records that on the same day, in the same hand, you did both things, and both things count.

But here is what I know about Elodie Oyelaran, better than the Hollow Men know it, better than she knows it herself: she reads people the way she reads locks. And she knows my hand. She's seen my notes a hundred times—prices, warnings, the occasional gruff *don't be stupid* after she's done something stupid. She'll read those four words and she'll know two things instantly: that the danger is real, because I don't waste words, and that it came *through* me, because I signed it.

She'll know I betrayed her. And she'll run anyway, because staying is death, and she'll run *well*, because I trained her, God help me, I trained her better than I trained anyone, and the chase across the rooftops of Vess will be a masterpiece of everything I ever taught her, every trick and shadow-line and door-code, all of it spent now on escaping the consequences of my survival.

And part of me—the part that isn't the bookkeeper, the small drowned part that still remembers a border post at dusk and a cart full of bottled orchards rolling past my lantern—part of me hopes she makes it. Hopes she runs so far and so fast that the Hollow Men lose the thread, lose the trail, lose interest, and the whole ugly transaction dissolves into the fog of Vess like it never happened.

But I've been in this business too long to believe in hoping as a strategy. Hope is inventory too, and mine is nearly out.

Because there's one more thing, the thing I haven't written in any ledger, the thing I only admitted to myself somewhere around the bottom of the brandy bottle: the Hollow Man asked about the teacher. And I lied. And he *let it pass*.

Which means that somewhere in his paper-folded voice and his suggestion of a face, he already knows. He knows the teacher exists. He knows the teacher is sitting in a shop on tannery row drinking plum brandy and calling it dinner. He let me keep my little lie the way a cat lets a mouse keep its dignity—briefly, and only because the ending is already decided.

He wasn't finished with me. He was never going to be finished with me. Selling Elodie wasn't the price of my safety. It was the *down payment*.

And so, on the fifth morning, when I unlocked my shop and found the empty vial sitting in the middle of my counter—wax seal unbroken, placed there by no door that opened, waiting patiently among the curiosities like a jar asking to be filled—I understood at last what kind of arithmetic I'd been doing all along.

Not survival. Not betrayal. Just inventory, rotating, out of the light.

And somewhere across the city, in a crooked house with a blue door, a girl was reading four words in my handwriting, and the rooftops of Vess were about to learn what I'd taught her.
