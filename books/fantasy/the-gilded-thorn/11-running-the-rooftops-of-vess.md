## 11. Running the Rooftops of Vess

The Hollow Men came for us at the tanner's gate, which was rude of them, because I had specifically chosen the tanner's district on the theory that no one with a functioning nose would follow us there.

I had been wrong about a great many things that week. This was only the latest.

"Elodie." Sef's voice carried across the yard, flat as slate. He stood in the gateway with two of his brothers behind him, their grey coats buttoned to the throat, their faces wearing that terrible patience of men who have eaten enough memories to know exactly how this scene goes. "You have something in your keeping that does not belong to you."

"That's my entire business model," I said. "Everything I own belongs to someone else."

Wren's hand found mine. Small fingers, mud still in the creases of the knuckles. The child didn't look at the inquisitors the way other people did — not with fear, and not with the careful blankness of someone pretending not to be afraid. Wren looked at them the way you look at weather.

Sef took one step forward, and I felt it happen: a pulling sensation behind my eyes, like a hook set gently in the soft part of me. He was tasting. Reaching into the air around us for residue, for the flavor of what we'd touched. And Wren — Wren was standing right there, radiating whatever Wren radiated, which apparently tasted like a language that had been dead for twenty years.

I did the arithmetic very fast. There were three of them, one gate, and a yard full of curing hides and lime pits. The math said drown yourself in the lime pit now, save everyone the trouble.

Then Wren pulled my hand, and we ran.

* * *

Here is something they don't tell you about Vess: the city is two cities stacked on top of each other and pretending otherwise. There's the street city, all cobbles and commerce and the law, and then there's the roof city, which is where people like me live when the street city gets ideas. The roof city has its own geography, its own borders, its own unwritten constitution, the first article of which is *don't fall*.

I had run the roofs since I was fifteen years old. I knew every gap between the dyers' lofts and the granary, every loose tile on the chandler's row, every gutter that would hold a person's weight and every one that would hold a lie about holding a person's weight. When I went up over the tannery wall with Wren's hand in mine, I thought: *I know this country better than anyone alive.*

The first wrong thing happened at the chimney stacks above Cooper's Lane.

There is a gap there — four feet, maybe five, between the cooper's roof and the sailmaker's loft. I've crossed it a hundred times. You take a running start off the low stack, you land on the loft's ridgeline, you keep moving. It is not elegant but it is reliable, and reliability is the whole religion of rooftops.

I jumped. Wren jumped with me, hand in mine, and we landed —

— in the sailmaker's *workroom*. Inside it. Through the roof, or rather not through anything at all; one moment my boots were finding the ridge tiles and the next we were standing between the benches, dust motes hanging in lamplight, a half-stitched jib sagging over its frame three feet from my nose. No sound of breaking tile. No startled apprentice. Just the room, warm and ordinary, as if rooms were places you could simply arrive.

Wren looked up at me. Wren's face said nothing, because Wren's face never said anything, but the child's grip tightened on my hand, and I felt — I don't know how else to put this — I felt the child *notice*, the way you notice a stone in your shoe. A small discomfort. An apology, almost.

"Don't," I whispered, though I hadn't decided yet whether I meant *don't apologize* or *don't do that again* or *don't exist so loudly*.

Down through the workroom stairs, out past a sailmaker who blinked at us like we were smoke, back up onto the street-side roofs. Behind us, somewhere below, the grey coats moved through Cooper's Lane with the unhurried certainty of men who could smell us.

Because here was the problem, and I understood it even as my lungs started to burn: I could outrun the Hollow Men. Anyone can outrun the Hollow Men; they don't hurry, because hurrying is for people who might lose. What you cannot outrun is what Wren *was*. Every step the child took left a mark on the world, a taste in the air, a hum in the bones of buildings. Sef wasn't chasing us. Sef was *reading* us, following the trail of impossible language the way I followed the smell of fresh bread down a market row.

We needed distance. Distance was the one commodity the roofs couldn't sell me tonight.

* * *

We crossed the dye-works by the plank walkways, where the air turns colors — saffron near the turmeric vats, a bruised violet near the logwood — and I felt the second wrong thing before I saw it.

It feels like walking into a draft. That's the closest I can come. A coolness across the face, a sense of a door opening somewhere nearby, except we were forty feet above any door.

"There," Wren said.

Not a word. Wren didn't speak — not Vessian, not anything I'd heard. But the child made a sound, a small urgent note in the throat, and pointed, and I understood it anyway, which should have frightened me more than it did. Everything about Wren arrived in my head pre-translated. I'd stopped asking why sometime around the third impossible thing.

What Wren pointed at was a door. Standing alone on the dye-works rooftop. A plain door, oak, iron-hinged, painted the faded green of every cheap door in Vess, framed and everything, frame and lintel and all, planted on the tarred gravel of the roof with absolutely nothing behind it but sky.

Now. I want to be clear that I am a professional. I have seen charms misfire. I have seen a memory charm sold twice give both buyers the same dead mother and cause a small riot outside the Lamplight Market. I have seen drunk men swear the moon followed them home. But doors do not grow on roofs, and children who cannot speak do not point at them with the weary familiarity of someone pointing out a shortcut.

"It opens onto your house," I said stupidly. "Doesn't it."

Wren turned the handle. The door swung open onto a kitchen — a kitchen with a low black stove and a bowl of pears and a window looking out on rain, and none of it was anywhere in Vess, and some deep animal part of me recognized the shape of that window before my mind caught up and slammed the recognition shut like a lid.

My chest hurt. Actually hurt, a hot wire pulled tight under the sternum.

"No," I said, louder. "No shortcuts. Shortcuts are how inquisitors find you. We go the long way, the honest way, the way that involves my knees hurting tomorrow."

I dragged Wren away from the door and the child came without fighting, but as we left I heard — I would swear on my own grave, which at the rate I was going wouldn't be long delayed — I heard the faint sound of rain on that window, and someone humming. A tune. Half a tune. A tune I almost knew.

We ran.

* * *

The exhilaration, when it came, surprised me. I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the one luxury I've never been able to steal.

There is a particular joy in flight done well. Your body stops being a thing you operate and becomes a thing you *are*; the gaps between rooftops stop being gaps and become rhythm; the whole city unrolls beneath you like a map drawn by someone who loved it. I was good at this. Twenty years old and I was the best there was at the one skill nobody respectable has ever wanted, and for six or seven rooftops — leaping the chandlers' row, sliding down the awning post above the fish market, Wren on my back with arms locked around my neck and a weight like a bird's — for those few rooftops I was flying, and the terror underneath was just fuel, and I laughed. Out loud. On a roof. In Vess. With the Hollow Men below.

Wren laughed too. A breathy, rusty sound, like a hinge remembering oil. First laugh I'd ever heard out of the child, and it cost me more concentration than the jump off the granary did, because some traitor part of my brain wanted to stop and *savor* it, and savoring is death at speed.

Then we came over the clocktower square, and the exhilaration curdled.

They had not followed us up. Of course they hadn't. They had done something smarter. They had gone to the edges.

Three grey figures stood in the square below, spaced wide, faces tilted up, and they weren't searching. They were waiting. And on the far side of the square, on the roofline I needed to reach, two more stood already — already *there*, coats stirring in the wind, and I understood with a cold drop in my stomach that Sef had guessed our line. Not tracked it. *Guessed* it. Because Sef had bought memories once — I'd sold him one, months ago, a small pleasant thing about a river — and somewhere in what he'd bought or tasted tonight he'd learned how a thief thinks, and a thief thinks in rooflines, and there are only three ways across the clocktower square, and he was standing in all of them.

"Right," I breathed. "New plan. The plan is: be somewhere else."

But Wren was looking at the clocktower itself. At the belfry. At the little door at its base that I knew for a fact was kept locked, because the bell-ringer's guild paid good money for a lock that actually worked, and I had considered stealing it once and decided even I had standards.

Wren walked to it. Turned the handle. The door opened — unlocked, oiled, swinging inward like it had been waiting — and inside was not a stairwell.

Inside was Maro's back room. The counting room behind the Curious Hour, with its ledgers and its lamp and its smell of ink and old paper, and Maro himself sitting at his desk with a cup of tea halfway to his mouth, staring at us through a doorway that existed nowhere in the architecture of Vess.

For one long moment, three impossible things held perfectly still: the child, the doorway, and the fence who raised me, with tea suspended an inch from his lips and an expression on his face I had never seen before and have spent every night since trying to forget.

It wasn't surprise. Maro doesn't do surprise; surprise is for amateurs. It was *recognition*. He looked at Wren the way a man looks at a debt he'd hoped was dead.

"Elodie," he said quietly, and it wasn't a greeting. It was the tone you use for a diagnosis.

Behind us, boots on gravel. Sef's voice, closer now, unhurried, terribly polite: "There's no shame in stopping, mistress Oyelaran. Everyone stops eventually. It's rather the point of walls."

Wren stepped backward through the doorway, pulling me, and I let myself be pulled — into Maro's counting room, into the smell of ink, into the one place in Vess I had sworn never to bring trouble, dragging the trouble in behind me like a wet cloak. The green door swung shut on the belfry and became, when I spun around, a shelf of ledgers against a wall that had always been a wall.

Maro set down his tea. He looked at Wren. He looked at me. And he said, in the voice he used for pricing things that shouldn't have prices:

"Sit down, girl. Both of you. And tell me why the Hollow Men are reading your name off the wind — and why that child smells like a country that never happened."

Outside, high above the clocktower square, the bells began to ring the hour, though no one had climbed to ring them, and every one of the six notes came out wrong.
