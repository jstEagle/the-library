## 12. Ilsa's Unfinished Map

Ilsa Vantrek lived above a pickle shop in the Sump, which is the sort of detail that teaches you everything and nothing about a person. The stairs smelled of brine and old paper. The door had no lock, only a sign that read KNOCK AND BE ANSWERED OR GO AWAY, which I took to mean the lock was her.

I knocked. Something heavy shifted inside. Then: "If you're from the Guild, I burned your survey last week and I'd burn you too."

"I'm not from the Guild."

"If you're from the tax office, the pickles are the landlord's."

"I'm not from the tax office."

A long silence, the kind a person fills by deciding whether to be interested or to keep being a door. "Then who in Vess's grey name are you?"

"Elodie Oyelaran." I said it plainly, because with mapmakers you learn quickly that names have edges, and if you flinch when you say yours they notice. "Maro said you were the best chartist he ever fenced maps for. He also said you'd probably threaten me. So far he's two for two."

The door opened on a chain. One eye appeared, grey as wet slate, framed by hair the color of dishwater going on silver. The eye looked at me, then down at Wren, who was doing what Wren did best, which was exist quietly enough to make people nervous.

"You brought a child into the Sump," the eye said.

"The child brought itself. I'm mostly luggage."

The chain rattled off. The door swung wide, and there was Ilsa Vantrek in a dressing gown embroidered with compass roses gone threadbare at the north, holding a walking stick she clearly didn't need but enjoyed brandishing.

"Inside, then. Mind the charts. Step on one and I'll know which one, and I'll grieve about it out loud for a very long time."

* * *

Her rooms were not rooms so much as an argument between cartography and hoarding. Maps hung from every wall—some of them beautiful, inked in three colors; some of them wrong in ways that looked deliberate; some of them of places I couldn't name, which unsettled me more than I let show. A table under the window held a half-eaten supper and a half-finished coastline. Shelves sagged. Somewhere a kettle muttered to itself like an old man at a council meeting.

Ilsa pointed her stick at a chair until I sat in it. She pointed it at Wren, hesitated, and instead pulled a second stool over with her foot.

"Children don't sit still," she said, as though reporting a defect in the species.

Wren sat perfectly still. Ilsa blinked at this, decided it was somehow my fault, and went to see about the kettle.

While her back was turned I studied the walls. And there—between a chart of the Amber Straits and something that might have been a fantasy of the northern ice—I saw it. Not a map. A rubbing. Charcoal on cloth, the lines soft and smudged, and underneath them unmistakable even in bad light: a tall narrow arch, a keyhole shape, the same proportions as the drawing pressed into Wren's floorboards.

My mouth went dry in a way that had nothing to do with fear and everything to do with its cousin.

"You've seen it before," I said.

Ilsa didn't turn around. The kettle got poured. Steam climbed. When she finally spoke her voice had changed register, dropped all its theater, gone flat and careful, like someone walking on ice and testing each step.

"Seen what?"

"You know what. The door."

She set two cups down harder than cups deserve. "That," she said, "is a rubbing of a carving on a tomb in the Old Quarter. A nobody's tomb. A merchant's wife. People put doors on tombs, girl. It means passage. It means grief. It doesn't mean anything else."

"It's identical to a drawing a mute child scratched into my floor with a fingernail."

Now she looked at me properly, and behind the slate of her eyes something moved, quick and frightened, like a fish under ice.

"Identical," she repeated.

"To the line weight. To the proportions. To—" I stopped, because saying *the way it hums* would sound mad, and I needed her sane. "To the exactness of it. Nobody draws a tomb-door twice, that exactly, by accident."

Ilsa sat down across from me. She wrapped both hands around her cup though it was too hot, the way people do when they want their hands occupied so their face can lie freely. For a while the only sound was the kettle settling and, distantly, the pickle shop below closing up, bolts sliding home.

"How old is the child?" she asked.

"I don't know. Seven? Eight? It turned up at my door four nights ago speaking gibberish."

"It's not gibberish."

The room went very quiet. Even the kettle seemed to hold its breath.

"Say that again," I said.

Ilsa set down her cup. She rose, went to a shelf, and began moving things aside with the deliberation of someone unearthing something they'd sworn they'd buried deep enough. What came out was a folio, leather gone green with damp, tied shut with a bootlace. She set it on the table between us but didn't untie it.

"Before I tell you what I know," she said, "you're going to tell me why Maro sent you to me. Because Maro does not send anyone anywhere unless sending them profits him, and he especially does not send anyone to *me*, because we are not friends."

"He didn't send me. I found his name in your ledger—"

"My ledger was stolen eight years ago."

"—in his vault," I finished. "Along with a lot of other things. Including, I think, a piece of my own life. He raised me after that. Taught me the trade. Fed me and used me, in roughly equal measure. Two days ago I showed him a rubbing of that door and he told me to drown the child."

I watched her take that in. Watched the word *drown* land somewhere old in her.

"Drown," she said softly. "Yes. That would be Maro's advice. Maro has always believed the past is a well you drop things into and never hear splash." Her thumb moved along the bootlace without untying it. "You came here against his instruction."

"I came here because drowning children is a line I haven't crossed yet, and I wanted to know why everyone keeps standing on the far side of it pointing at me."

Something in her face eased—not softened, eased, like a fist deciding not to be one. She untied the bootlace.

* * *

What was inside the folio was not one map but many, layered like leaves, and every one of them was unfinished. Coastlines that trailed off into blank vellum mid-stroke. Roads that simply stopped, the ink thinning as though the pen had run dry or the hand had lost conviction. Mountain ranges sketched and abandoned. In the margins, in a younger, fiercer version of Ilsa's handwriting, notes crowded each other: *survey incomplete — the village of ___ will not stay drawn — asked the harbormaster, he laughed — asked again, he did not laugh, he wept and did not know why.*

"There were nine of us," Ilsa said. She'd taken up a position by the window, back half-turned, watching the street below rather than me. "Guild chartists. We called ourselves the Nine Compasses, which sounds grander than we were—we met in a tavern and argued about projection methods. But we were good. We were mapping the borderlands east of the Salt Fen, twenty years ago. Before."

"Before what?"

"That's the question, isn't it. That's the whole question." She touched one of the trailing coastlines with one finger, gently, the way you touch a scar. "We were surveying a country called Ashkar."

I waited for the word to mean something. It didn't. It landed in me like a stone into deep water—no bottom, no echo, just gone—and yet some animal part of me flinched at the splash I never heard. I gripped the arms of the chair.

"Ashkar," I said. Testing it.

"You felt that." She turned. "Don't pretend you didn't. Everyone feels it. That's how I knew, all those years ago, that it wasn't madness. You can't feel a hole. A hole is where feeling stops. But we all felt the *edges* of it, like a tongue finding a missing tooth. There was a country east of the Fen. Every road led toward it. Every trade route bent around it like water around a stone. And when you tried to draw it—" she spread her hands over the unfinished maps, "—the ink wouldn't hold. Not magic. Ink holds fine for anything else I've ever drawn. It's that the *place* wouldn't hold. As if the world had been told to forget it and the world is a very obedient student."

"And your colleagues? The other eight?"

"Gone. Scattered. Two died—one fell from a height, one drowned in a dry riverbed, which I recommend not thinking about too hard. The rest stopped mapping entirely. Took up farming, tailoring, respectable work. One of them begged me to stop, the last time I saw him. He held my wrists and cried and said, *Ilsa, whatever is east of the Fen, the world has agreed it isn't there, and agreements keep people alive.*" Her mouth twisted. "He was right. Look what agreeing got him—alive, and hollow, and afraid of a word."

"And you?"

"I kept drawing." She said it without pride, which made it worse. "For years I kept trying. And then one night, eleven years ago now, I drew the whole thing. Every road, every river, every town. A complete map of Ashkar. I remember finishing the last stroke and thinking, *there, you stubborn woman, done.*" She paused. "And then I woke up the next morning and the parchment was blank. Not erased. Blank. As if I'd never put ink to it at all. And I couldn't remember a single name from it. Couldn't remember whether the capital was called anything. Couldn't remember my colleagues' faces from the survey season. I remembered that I had drawn a map. I couldn't remember what was on it."

The room tilted, very slightly, the way it does when a floorboard settles—or when your understanding of the ground does. On the stool beside me, Wren had gone rigid, listening with their whole small body, the way they listened to my charms.

"So the country's gone," I said slowly, working it through aloud because saying it kept it from swallowing me. "Not conquered. Conquered places get renamed. They appear on maps under new letters. This is—" I reached for it and found the word waiting, absurd and precise. "Unwritten."

"*Unwritten,*" Ilsa repeated, tasting it. "Yes. That's the word I settled on too, in the end, alone, with no one to check it against. Someone didn't defeat Ashkar. Someone *edited* it. And whoever holds the pen—" she looked at me, and for the first time the fear in her was naked, "—girl, do you understand what that means? It means the histories in every library are missing a chapter and no librarian feels the gap. It means the free cities' archives say the east is empty fen and empty fen is what they'll defend against. It means armies could march out of nowhere, because *nowhere is where Ashkar used to be.*"

"I know," I said quietly. "Maro says war's coming. He won't say how he knows. I believe him."

"Believe him. And believe this: whatever your child is, it isn't from here. Look at it."

I looked. Wren was staring at the folio—at the trailing coastlines, the abandoned roads—with an expression I had no right to recognize and recognized anyway. It was homesickness. I'd seen it a thousand times in the memories I lifted and sold: the soldier's ache for a farm, the widow's ache for a kitchen, always pointed at a place that still existed, however far. Wren's longing pointed at these broken lines the way a compass points north.

"Wren," I said softly.

The child stood. Crossed to the table. And with one finger, careful as a surgeon, touched the point where the longest road ran off the edge of the oldest map—the easternmost line, the one that thinned into nothing.

Where the fingertip rested, the vellum darkened. Just slightly. As if heat were rising through it. And beneath the blank space beyond the road's end, faint as breath on glass, lines appeared: a curve of shore. The ghost of a harbor. Gone again in the space of a blink.

Ilsa made a sound I'd never heard a grown mapmaker make. She grabbed the folio's edge to steady herself.

"Do that again," she whispered.

But Wren had already withdrawn the hand and was looking at me, and the look said, plain as speech: *you know what that was. Say it.*

"The Salt Fen," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me. "Ashkar was east of the Salt Fen. The child is showing us where."

* * *

Ilsa spent the next hour doing what she was born to do, which was argue with geography. She pulled charts—old ones, guild ones, smuggled ones—and overlaid them, weighted their corners with pickle jars and teacups, and talked the whole time, fast and low, a lecture delivered to herself with me permitted to overhear.

"The Fen runs salt-flat for forty leagues, hard as kiln brick in the dry season, which ends—hmm. Ends soon. Rains come and it goes to soup knee-deep for a hundred miles. So if you go, you go now or you wait a year." Jar. Cup. "Two routes. Northern route follows the old drovers' road through the Thornmark—but the Thornmark's been leased to Vane's grazing lords since the Fall, which means Vane's riders, which means checkpoints every league, and you—" the stick jabbed at Wren, "—do not look like a grazier's kin. Southern route crosses the Burnt Marches. No checkpoints because no one wants it. No water either, or none you'd drink, and it's eleven days' walk if the weather holds and a graveyard if it doesn't." Another jar. "Both routes converge on the Fen's southern shore, where the old ferry crossing was. Was. Nothing there now but reeds and, presumably, the memory of a ferry."

"And past the Fen?"

"Past the Fen," Ilsa said, and stopped arranging jars. "Past the Fen is the blank part of every map I own. Past the Fen is where my ink gave up. If there's a door in the world that leads back into a country that was unwritten, it'll be there. At the center of the hole. Where the tooth was pulled." She sat down heavily across from me. "That's where your child drew you to, thief. Whether it meant to or not."

I looked down at Wren, who was watching me with those patient, mud-brown eyes, and I thought about the floorboards, and the humming charms, and Maro's white face saying *drown it*, and the word *home* I hadn't learned yet but already half-understood.

"Why me?" I asked, and it wasn't rhetorical; I genuinely wanted the answer. "Vess is full of hedge-witches and charm-mongers. Why does a child walk out of nowhere and land on *my* doorstep?"

"Maybe it didn't land," Ilsa said. "Maybe it was aimed. Or maybe—" she hesitated, and I watched her decide to say the true thing instead of the kind one, "—maybe it walked past a hundred doors looking for the one that opened. Doors know their own, girl. Ask any mapmaker. Thresholds are the oldest magic there is, older than kingdoms, older than writing. Everything else gets rewritten. Doors just open, or they don't."

The kettle had gone cold. Neither of us moved to warm it. Outside, the Sump was settling into evening—carts groaning, gulls arguing over the pickle barrels, the ordinary noise of a city that believed its own history.

I thought about the route. Eleven days through the Burnt Marches. Checkpoints or drought. War coming out of the east like weather. A child who couldn't speak the language of anywhere. And me—a thief with borrowed skills, no past worth the name, and exactly one talent: taking what belonged to others and making it portable.

It was, I reflected, almost a qualification.

"When does the dry season end?" I asked.

Ilsa blinked. "You're asking about seasons."

"I'm asking about seasons."

"Nine days. Perhaps ten, if the sky's feeling generous, which historically it hasn't."

"Then I leave within the week." I heard myself say it and felt, strangely, no dread—only a click, deep and internal, like a tumbler falling into place in a lock. "Through the Marches. To the southern shore. To wherever the child leads."

"Elodie." Ilsa leaned forward, and her voice dropped to something raw. "Twenty years ago eight sensible people told me to stop asking questions about the east, and every one of them was right by every measure except the one that matters. If you cross that Fen, you may find a door. You may find nothing. You may find the thing that unwrote a nation, and it may notice you the way a man notices a fly. Is there anything in your life worth that?"

I almost laughed. It came out as something smaller.

"Ilsa, I sell other people's happiest hours in a night market so I can afford a room where nobody knows my name. I have one friend and he sold my childhood and told me to drown a baby. The child on that stool is the first creature in three years that chose my door." I reached over and put my hand on Wren's shoulder; the small body leaned into it, trusting, immediate, and that lean went through me like a coal through paper. "So yes. There's something worth it. It's sitting right here, and it's the only thing in Vess that remembers a country the whole world forgot, and if I don't carry it east, no one will."

Ilsa looked at me for a long moment. Then she did something I didn't expect. She pulled a fresh sheet of vellum from the drawer, uncapped a bottle of the good iron-gall ink—the ink she'd refused to waste for eleven years, I'd have bet my charms on it—and began to draw.

The Burnt Marches first, from memory: ridgelines, a dead river, the wells that were poison and the three that weren't, marked with tiny careful crosses. The southern shore of the Fen. The reeds. And then eastward, her pen slowing, the strokes growing lighter, until at the edge of the sheet the line simply faded, as if the road ahead were being drawn in smoke.

She stared at the fading edge. Her jaw tightened.

"It's starting again," she said quietly. "The closer I draw to it, the less it holds." She looked up, and there was something terrible and bright in her face—the look of a woman who has waited eleven years for permission and finally given it to herself. "So I'm coming with you. Don't argue. Someone has to hold the map, and someone has to remember what was on it when the map stops holding, and I am very, very good at remembering things that insist on being forgotten."

Outside, the gulls went up all at once, screaming, and somewhere in the direction of the city gates a bell began to ring—not the hour, not the alarm, but the third bell, the one that meant strangers at the walls in numbers.

Ilsa and I looked at each other over the unfinished map.

"Well," she said. "That's either the army Maro promised us, or it's someone who already knows where you slept last night. Either way, girl—finish your tea. We leave tonight."
