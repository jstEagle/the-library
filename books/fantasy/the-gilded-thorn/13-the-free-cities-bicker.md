## 13. The Free Cities Bicker

### I. Delegate Cassian Voss, Speaker for the City of Ondrel

I have sat on the Concord Council of the Free Cities for eleven years, and in that time I have learned three things: never trust a delegate who brings his own ink, never schedule anything after the midday meal, and never—*never*—ask the Archivists to confirm something urgent.

Yet here we were, the ninth hour of debate on the question of whether Aurel Vane was marching an army toward us, and the Archivists had finally delivered their finding.

"There is no army," announced Archivist Pellam, a thin man with the complexion of someone who had not seen weather since childhood. He held up a ledger as though presenting a relic. "The census rolls of the Vane Protectorate list standing forces at twelve thousand men. Twelve thousand, delegates. A garrison force. Sufficient for border patrol and tax collection. There is no field army. There can be no field army."

"Then what," said Delegate Amara of Sennet, "is burning its way through the Corrow Pass?"

"Pillagers," said Pellam.

"Tens of thousands of pillagers? Moving in column? With supply wagons and siege train?"

"Large pillagers."

I will grant that Amara kept her voice level. I would not have. I had seen the refugees myself, two weeks past, when I rode out to inspect the eastern toll stations—the ones that no longer collected tolls because there was no one left east of them to collect from. They came in families, in twos and threes, in long straggling lines that looked from a distance like the land itself was unraveling into thread. And here is the thing I could not get out of my head, the thing I said now, standing, because sitting felt dishonest:

"They cannot tell us why they fled."

The chamber quieted. Delegates are like cats; they smell drama and pretend indifference.

"I spoke to forty of them," I continued. "Farmers, a miller, a woman who kept a shrine—I don't know which god, she couldn't say either. Not one of them could name the village they came from. They knew their own names. Their children's names. The shape of their grief. But ask them *where*, and they look at you the way you look at a word you've written so many times it stops meaning anything. One man told me he was homesick. I asked him for home. He wept. That was his whole answer."

"The trauma of flight," said Pellam mildly, "is well documented to disorder memory."

"The trauma of flight does not delete *place names*, Archivist."

"Stress does strange things to—"

"Three villages," I said. "Brenholt, Callowmere, and—" I stopped. I had written them down. I had read them aloud to my clerk on the road. I reached for my notes and found the page, and found the space where they should be, and found instead a small ink blot shaped like nothing at all.

Two names. I had been certain of three.

Pellam watched me with an expression I want to call sympathy but was probably patience. "Delegate Voss. You have been riding hard roads and speaking to frightened people. It is natural that—"

"Bring me the survey rolls," I said. "The eastern districts. Every farmstead between the Corrow and the toll stations."

"We have done so," said Pellam, and here, I will swear before any god still taking attendance, his voice softened by a single degree, which from Pellam was a scream. "The districts you describe are listed as common pasture. Uninhabited. They have been uninhabited since the founding surveys."

### II. Odile Ferran, Junior Scribe, Archives of Sennet

The trouble with being a junior scribe is that you are invisible, and the further trouble is that everyone knows it, and the final trouble—the one that keeps you awake at night—is that invisibility is a kind of key.

Senior Archivist Pellam did not know I was in the map room when the delegation from Ondrel sent their request down. He did not know I heard him instruct his assistant to pull the founding surveys, nor that I heard the assistant come back white-faced twenty minutes later saying the founding surveys for the eastern districts were missing, all of them, every copy, across every archive in Sennet, and that the gaps in the catalogues were themselves gone—smoothed over, renumbered, as if the shelves had always held exactly the scrolls they held now.

Pellam said, very quietly, "Check the duplicates."

"They're gone too, sir."

"Check the *duplicates of the duplicates*."

"Sir, there's a pattern. The gaps aren't random. Everything missing is from the same period. Twenty years ago. And everything missing is—" the assistant swallowed— "it's all the same shape, sir. Like pages cut out around a silhouette. Whatever was taken, it was taken *around* something. Something in the middle that they left, or couldn't reach."

I am a junior scribe. My duties are copying, filing, and fetching. But I grew up on a farm, before my parents sold it and moved inside the walls, and I remember—I *remember*—that our nearest neighbors were the Hollis family, who kept bees, and whose honey my mother traded for wool every autumn. I remember the taste of it. I remember being stung and crying and being given honeycomb to suck until the crying stopped.

This morning I went to find where the Hollises had lived. I asked the district clerks. I consulted the tax rolls. I walked the eastern road until my boots gave out.

There is pasture there. Good grass, ungrazed, going to seed. No bees. No farmstead foundation. No fence posts, no well, no burned rafters—nothing, not even the *absence* of a house, which any country child can find in her sleep. Just grass, lying smooth as a made bed.

And here is the worst part, the part I have told no one, because telling it would cost me my post and possibly my reason: I can no longer fully picture my mother's face when she tasted that honey. I remember that she smiled. I remember the smile the way you remember a story about someone. The rest is going soft at the edges, like fruit.

Something is eating the world, and it has excellent table manners.

### III. Lord-Commander Brecht Hale, Retired, Ondrel Militia Council

They laughed at me. That's the short version. The long version is that they laughed at me *in order*, which is somehow worse.

First the merchants' faction laughed, politely, the way you laugh at an old soldier who has begun confusing his campaigns. Then the trade delegates laughed, less politely, because fear is bad for quarterly projections. Then young Delegate Amara—who I had expected better of—laughed hardest of all, though I noticed her laugh came a half-second late, the laugh of a woman checking whether the room was safe before committing to it.

"Garrison levels," I said, "are a record. Records can be edited. Armies cannot. Look out your window."

"My window faces the grain exchange," said the Ondrel trade delegate.

"Then look at the roads. Look at the refugee columns. An army that eats a country doesn't need to be in your archives, madam. It needs to be in your fields, and by the time it's in your fields, the archives won't matter, because there will be no one left to read them."

"Commander Hale." This was Pellam himself, who had traveled from Sennet for the session, carrying his ledgers like a man carrying his own skeleton. "You are asking this council to act on the basis of rumors, refugees, and—forgive me—a feeling."

"I am asking you to act on the basis that *every archive in the Free Cities agrees perfectly*," I said. "Have you ever known records to agree perfectly? I fought in the Border Wars, Archivist. I have read after-action reports from both sides of the same skirmish. Men who stood ten feet apart could not agree on whether it rained. And you want me to believe that twenty years of census data across six rival cities, compiled by clerks who hate each other, matches without a single discrepancy? Perfect agreement isn't accuracy. Perfect agreement is a *signature*. Someone signed this."

The chamber murmured. For a moment—one moment—I thought I had them.

Then the Ondrel delegate said, "Even granting your theory, Commander, what would you have us do? Raise levies against an army that, by your own argument, our records say does not exist? On what authority? With what taxes?" And there it was, the true engine of the Free Cities, the machine beneath the marble: not disbelief, but *cost*. It is expensive to prepare for a war. It is free to disbelieve in one.

"Cheaper than the alternative," I said.

"Is it?" said the trade delegate. "Show me the alternative. Show me one document, one witness with a name and a place, one piece of evidence that would stand in this chamber. Give me something I can bring to the guilds."

I thought of the refugee with the empty hands and the full eyes. *Homesick,* he'd said. Homesick for nowhere. What document could he sign? What place could he name?

"No," I admitted. "I can't give you that. All I can give you is this: when the columns arrive—and they will arrive—you will stand in this chamber and say, *no one warned us.* And you will be wrong. You were warned. You simply preferred the paperwork."

I resigned my commission that evening. Retired men make poor prophets, but I intend to be a very good one anyway. I have bought a horse and started east, toward the burn line, because someone ought to see it with eyes that are still permitted to believe them.

### IV. Amara of Sennet, Delegate, in Her Private Rooms

My father taught me that the first lie of any tyranny is small and administrative. He was a customs officer under the old regency, before Vane, and he used to say: *Amara, no one seizes a country with soldiers alone. Soldiers are loud. Seize the ledger first, and the soldiers become unnecessary—the country will argue itself into submission.*

I thought of him tonight because I cannot sleep, and because today I voted—yes, I confess it, I voted—with the skeptics, and the vote passed, and the Free Cities will not raise levies, and somewhere east of the Corrow Pass an army that does not exist continues to march through districts that were never inhabited toward cities that have already decided, in advance and in writing, that they cannot be conquered.

Here is what I did not say in the chamber. Here is what I will perhaps never say aloud.

Twenty years ago, when I was nine, my mother sang me a lullaby. I remember the melody perfectly. I remember her voice, low and rough at the edges from the winter cough she never shook. I remember that the song had words, many words, verse after verse, and that I loved it more than any song before or since.

I cannot recall a single word of it.

Not one. I hum it sometimes, in the dark, and my mouth shapes itself around syllables that aren't there, and the melody goes on and on, patient as water, waiting for language that has been removed from me the way a tooth is removed—cleanly, completely, leaving only the ache of the absent thing and the tongue returning, again and again, to the gap.

Tonight I hummed it, and for the first time, at the end of the third verse, I heard—not remembered, *heard*—a single sound. One syllable, surfacing like a drowned body rising.

It sounded like *"Ash."*

Ashkar. The word is in none of our chronicles. I have checked; I spent the night checking, in the restricted stacks, with a lamp I was not entitled to. The name appears nowhere. And yet my mother's lullaby knows it, and my mouth knows it, and some animal cellar of my heart has been keeping it warm for twenty years like a coin sewn into a coat.

Someone erased a country. Someone is erasing the evidence of the erasing. And the free cities—the proud, rich, quarrelsome free cities—are sitting in a circle reading perfectly consistent books while the author of those books sharpens his pen.

Tomorrow I will stand before the council again. I will not bring documents. Documents are his territory; he wins there. I will bring my mother's lullaby, and I will sing it, badly, in front of six delegations and the Archivist Pellam, and I will watch their faces when they realize they know the tune.

Every one of them will know the tune.

I am certain of it the way you are certain of a door in a dark house: not because you can see it, but because you have walked this hallway your whole life, and tomorrow, God willing, someone is finally going to open it.

* * *

Far to the east, in a city that still believed itself merely poor rather than doomed, a thief with a child in tow slipped out through the night market's last light, carrying a dying soldier's happiest hour wrapped in wax paper—and behind her, at the edge of hearing, the first outriders of an army no archive had ever named crossed the river at Corrow Ford, unhurried, unopposed, and utterly unrecorded.
