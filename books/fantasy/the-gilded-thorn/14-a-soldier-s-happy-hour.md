## 14. A Soldier's Happy Hour

There are rules to my trade, and rule one has always been this: never sell a memory back to the person it came from.

It sounds like ethics. It isn't. It's arithmetic. A man who remembers his own happiness twice will come looking for the thief who made him remember it wrong, and dying men have nothing left to lose but grudges. I built three years of quiet survival on that rule, and I meant to die by it too—old, rich, and unremembered, which is the only way anyone in Vess gets to go.

But the soldier was still at the Hospice of Saint Vellum when I went back for him.

I told myself it was business. The charm had been sitting in my inventory for eleven days, and charms decay like fish; a happy hour left too long in a glass vial goes sour at the edges, all its edges, until what you're selling is less a memory than an argument about one. Nobody buys sour joy. So yes. Business. Purely.

The Hospice of Saint Vellum occupies a former granary near the Tallow Docks, which tells you everything about how Vess values the people who bleed for it. Grain fed the city once; now the building holds soldiers instead, stacked in cots like sacks, and the smell is roughly what you'd expect from that substitution. The Sisters who run it charge by the hour and pray for free, which is the only honest exchange rate I've ever encountered.

I paid for a visitor's token with coins I'd earned lifting other people's best hours. There's a symmetry there I didn't examine too closely.

He was in the far row, by the wall where the damp comes through, because the men nearest the windows pay extra and he clearly hadn't. He looked worse than he had at the market square, if worse can be measured in a face that was already mostly gone. The fever had moved in like a tenant who intends to renovate. His eyes were open, though, fixed on the ceiling beams as though they owed him something.

I stood at the foot of the cot and did the thing I never do: I hesitated.

"Visiting hours ended," he said, without looking at me. His voice was gravel steeped in brine. "Or they haven't started. One of those."

"They're flexible here." I came around to the side. Up close he was younger than his voice—young enough that the fever hadn't finished with him yet, only mostly. "You bought something from me. Eleven days ago. In the market."

His eyes came down off the beams and found me, and I watched recognition arrive and then get politely shown out again. Sick men don't hold on to faces. "Don't recall."

"You wouldn't. That was rather the point." I set the vial on the blanket between us, and the little light inside it shifted, amber turning toward gold, the way it does when it senses its origin nearby. "A hour in an orchard. Late summer. Someone laughing."

Something moved behind his face—not expression exactly, more like weather. "That was real?"

"It was someone's. I couldn't tell you whose, before you ask. It doesn't work that way. I take what's loose and I bottle what I take." All true, in the way that half-truths are true, which is to say technically and uselessly.

He looked at the vial for a long time. Then he said, "You've come to buy it back?"

"To give it back."

"That's not how markets work."

"No," I agreed. "This isn't a market."

Here is what I had not told myself when I decided to come, or rather here is what I had dressed up in the costume of business and sent out the door ahead of me: eleven days ago, when I lifted that hour from him, I had felt it leave. I always feel them leave—a small tug behind the sternum, like pulling a splinter from somewhere that shouldn't have skin—and usually I feel nothing about feeling it. But this one had fought me. Not him; I don't think he even knew what was happening, thought maybe he'd dozed off in the sun against a cart wheel. But the memory itself had clung, the way some of them do, the ones that are load-bearing. You learn to tell the difference between a memory a person can spare and one their whole architecture rests on. I'd taken a load-bearing one and sold it anyway, because a load-bearing memory fetches triple, and rent was due, and I had rules instead of a conscience precisely so that rent could always be paid.

And then a child had walked into my house speaking a dead language, and drawn a door on my floorboards, and hummed along to a burning orchard I carried in my own blood, and suddenly every clean little rule I owned had developed cracks, and through the cracks I could see the shape of what I actually was: a woman who sold dying men their own happiness by the hour.

So. Conscience. It arrives the way weather does. You can't schedule it and you can't invoice it.

"Drink it or wear it," I said, because I am incapable of tenderness without scaffolding around it. "Wearing's better if you want it slow. Drink it if you want it all at once, but you'll cry, everyone cries, and the Sisters will think it's the fever."

"How much?" His hand had already drifted toward the vial. Hands know things before heads do.

"Nothing."

"Nothing." He tried the word out like a coin he suspected of being clipped. "Nobody in Vess gives nothing."

"I'm nobody in Vess. It works out."

He laughed—or started to, and the laugh collapsed into coughing, and for a moment I thought we'd be done here early, and I felt something adjacent to panic, which was also new, also unwelcome, also filed under *weather*. When he'd finished, he picked up the vial with both hands, the way you hold something holy or fragile or expensive, and asked, "What is it? Truly. What did I lose?"

"An hour," I said. "Late summer. You're standing under trees heavy enough to be embarrassed about it. There's cider. There's a woman—dark hair, freckles across her nose like someone flicked a brush at her. She's laughing at something you said, and you can't remember what you said, and it doesn't matter, because the point of the hour isn't the words. The point is that in that hour, you are absolutely certain the war is over and you survived it, and she's certain too, and the orchard agrees with both of you."

The ward had gone quiet around us. Or I'd stopped hearing it. One of those.

"That sounds," he said carefully, "like a memory of a time before a battle."

"Yes."

"Then it's not mine. I mean—" he turned the vial, watching the light swim. "I haven't been in an orchard since I was nine. This is someone else's hour."

And there it was, the flaw in my beautiful plan, the crack running straight down the middle of my grand gesture: he knew his own past well enough to recognize a stranger's happiness when it was handed to him in glass. I had stolen a dying man's happiest hour and sold it to another dying man, and now I stood between them holding nothing, offering nothing, being nothing much at all.

"It fits," I said. "That's the cruelty of it. Happiness isn't tailored. It hangs loose on anybody."

He was quiet a while. Down the row, a man moaned a name over and over, a woman's name, or a country's, impossible to tell.

"If it's hers," the soldier said finally, "she'll be wanting it back too."

"She's dead." The word came out flat and factual and I hated it instantly, because how would I know? Because the memories I lift carry their endings with them, folded up small in the corners—that's part of why they sell, that's the gilding on the thorn: every charm is proof that joy survives its own conclusion. The woman in the orchard was dead. The hour itself had told me so, the way old letters tell you things their writers never meant to say. "I'm sorry. I don't usually know that either. This one I knew."

"Then it's an orphan," he said. "An orphaned hour." He unstopped the vial. "Do I drink it?"

"Slowly. It's stronger than it looks. Most things that survive alone are."

He drank.

* * *

I have watched a hundred people put on borrowed joy. It's the merchant's part of my trade, the performance side: you watch the face change and you name your price while the face is still soft. I have seen hard men weep at strangers' weddings, seen grief-bent women laugh with a laughter that belonged to somebody's dead brother, seen a customs officer experience a child's first snowfall and wave my smuggled goods through without a glance. I used to find it funny. I used to find it profitable, which is funnier.

I had never watched someone drink back an hour that wasn't theirs.

He didn't weep. That was the first strange thing. He sat very straight in his cot, hands flat on the blanket, and his breathing changed—it slowed, deepened, found some older rhythm—and the fever-flush in his cheeks settled into something warmer, more ordinary, like a lamp turned down to a livable brightness. And then he smiled. Not the cracked, grateful smile of a man given a gift. A different smile entirely. The smile of a man who is *there*, under trees, in late summer, listening to a dark-haired woman laugh at something wonderful he can't quite recall saying.

For the space of perhaps a minute, the Hospice of Saint Vellum held two people: a dying soldier in a damp cot by the wall, and a living man in an orchard that no longer existed anywhere except inside him.

Then it passed through him, the way borrowed light passes through glass, and what it left behind was—

I expected gratitude. Gratitude I had budgeted for. What I got was worse.

He opened his eyes and looked at me, and his face was wet, and he said, "Oh."

"Oh," I agreed, brilliantly.

"You live like that?" he said. "Carrying those. Selling them."

"It pays the rent."

"That's not what I asked."

No, it wasn't. I considered several answers—the wry ones, the deflecting ones, the little armored sentences I keep polished for exactly these occasions—and found, to my considerable irritation, that none of them would fit through my teeth. "Sometimes," I said. "When I'm careless. Which is often. Carelessness is cheaper than caution, in my line."

"What was her name?" he asked.

"The woman in the orchard? I don't know. Names don't come loose. They're stitched in too tight."

"Pity." He looked at his hands. "She seemed like she'd have had a good name. Something you'd want to say a lot."

The ward swam a little. I blamed the smell, the tallow smoke, the general atmosphere of the place, which could make a stone melancholy. "I should go."

"Wait." He caught my sleeve—caught, not grabbed; a dying man's grip is surprisingly specific—and pressed coins into my hand before I could stop him. Three coppers, hospital-worn, warm from his pocket. "Payment."

"There's no charge."

"Then it's a tip." His eyes had the orchard still in them, a residue of gold. "For the errand. Somebody ought to pay for the errand. She'd have wanted—I think she'd have wanted—" He stopped, confused by his own pronouns, grieving a woman he'd never met for reasons he couldn't articulate, and I stood there and let him, because I deserved every second of it.

"Buy something with it," I managed. "Something useless. Useless things are the only honest purchases left in this city."

I made it four steps before he said my name.

I don't use my name in the trade. I'm *the magpie girl*, I'm *the charm-wife*, I'm whatever the market needs to call me so it doesn't have to wonder whether I have a mother. But eleven days ago, drunk and generous with his last coin, he'd asked, and I'd been tired enough to answer, and now:

"Elodie." He said it gently, like setting something down. "Whoever she was—the one the hour came from—thank her for me. If you ever find out where she is."

I told him I would.

I have never lied so completely to anyone, and I include the Hollow Men, whom I have lied to professionally.

* * *

Outside, the Tallow Docks were doing their evening imitation of honesty: gulls screaming over fish guts, stevedores cursing in six languages, the light going bronze and merciful across the water. I walked fast, because walking fast feels like purpose, and I needed to feel like anything other than what I was.

Three coppers in my fist. A tip. For the errand.

Here is the arithmetic I could not stop doing, walking those docks with my collar up: I had charged him eight silver for that hour. Eight silver for the last good thing a stranger would ever feel, sold to a man who'd spend it dying. And the hour itself—the original, the true one—belonged to a dead woman whose name was stitched in too tight to steal, who had stood under her trees on the last ordinary afternoon of her life without knowing that in twenty years her happiness would be currency in a city she'd probably never visited, changing hands, accruing interest, paying some thief's rent.

Some thief's. Mine. Say it plainly, Elodie, just once, in the privacy of your own skull: *mine.*

I had always told myself my trade was mercy adjacent. I took what people could spare. I gave joy to people drowning in the opposite. The market stall was a form of alms with overhead. It's remarkable what you can believe at two in the morning with rent due and a vial of somebody's wedding day warming your palm.

But I had felt that orchard hour fight me. Load-bearing. And I'd pried it loose anyway, and the man it came from—had I even noticed him? I tried to remember the taking, which is a thing I almost never do, remembering being bad for business. A man against a wall near the fountain, feverish, alone. Had anyone come looking for him? Would anyone? In Vess, the answer to that question is so reliably *no* that asking it is practically a prayer.

And underneath that arithmetic, a colder one, the one I'd been refusing to read since the night a mud-caked child drew a door on my floorboards: *I have a vault of my own.* Somewhere in this city sit bottles with my name on them—my childhood, my mother's voice, my country, my whole self, sold off before I had the wit to know what I was losing. I walk around Vess with a hole where a life should be, and I fill other people's holes for coin, and I call the arrangement fair because it keeps me fed.

The soldier had thanked me. That was the worst of it. He'd tipped me for the privilege of borrowing a dead woman's joy, and he'd meant it, and his gratitude had landed in me like a stone in still water, and the ripples are still going.

Conscience, I'm told, is what you feel when your actions and your story about your actions stop matching. By that measure I'd been due for years. Wren just pulled the thread.

* * *

I got home after dark, let myself in through the window because doors are for people with nothing to hide, and stopped dead two steps inside.

Wren was sitting cross-legged in the middle of the floor, exactly where the chalk door was drawn, and every charm on my shelf was lit.

Not glowing—*lit*, lit from within, each vial a small stained-glass window, throwing colors on the walls that I had never put there: orchard gold, sea-green, festival red, a hundred hues for a hundred hours that had never belonged to me. The room looked like the inside of a lantern. It looked like the inside of a memory.

Wren wasn't touching any of them. Just sitting there, hands in lap, watching the shelves with that terrible patient attention, and as I came closer I saw that the lights were moving—flickering in sequence, shelf to shelf, like something reading.

"Wren," I said, and my voice came out smaller than I intended. "What are you doing?"

The child looked up. Those eyes, gray as slate rain, and behind them something vast trying to fit into a very small face.

Then Wren raised one hand, slowly, and pointed—at the third shelf, the good shelf, where I kept the strongest stock—and the charms there flared all together, and out of the flare rose a sound.

Not speech. Not quite music. A chord, if a chord could be homesick: the sound of my entire inventory resonating at once, harmonics I had no instruments for, and threaded through it—faint, unmistakable, impossible—one word in a language that died twenty years ago and that I understood anyway, the way you understand your own heartbeat, without learning, without trying, without permission.

The word was *home*.

Every bottle on my shelf heard it. Every bottle answered.

And I stood in my own doorway, three coppers still clenched in my fist, and understood that whatever Wren was, my charms had known it longer than I had—because they had just sung to it in Ashkaran, every one of them, even the ones lifted from Vess-born thieves and dockside drunks. As if the language weren't dead at all. As if it had only been sleeping in the things people carry.

As if it had been sleeping in *me*.
