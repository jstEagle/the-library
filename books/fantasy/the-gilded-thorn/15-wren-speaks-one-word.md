## 15. Wren Speaks One Word

The word came at dawn, which was wrong on two counts.

First: Wren did not speak. In the eleven days since the child had appeared mud-caked at my door, Wren had produced exactly three sounds—a hum that made my charms shiver in their jars like bees dreaming of spring, a laugh with no voice behind it, and once, at two in the morning, a sob so old it seemed to have been waiting in that small chest since before either of us was born. I had built my whole trade on reading what people didn't say. The dying soldier whose happiest hour I'd lifted hadn't said a word about his daughter either; he'd just held her name in his mouth like a stone he couldn't swallow. So I knew silence. Wren's silence was different. Wren's silence was full.

Second: dawn was when I slept, or performed the theater of sleeping. Thieves and memory-brokers keep the hours of owls and apologists. Dawn in Vess is for the honest and the doomed.

But there it was—grey light through the shutters, dust turning slow circles like it had nowhere better to be, and Wren standing in the middle of my floor in one of my spare shirts that fit like a tent fitted for a smaller tent, pointing at the door drawn on the floorboards.

The drawing had not faded. That should have told me something right there. Everything fades in Vess. Ink, dye, memory, resolve—the city eats them all with the same indifferent appetite. But the lines Wren had scratched into my floor eleven days ago were as black and sure as the hour they'd been made. I'd walked over them, swept around them, considered prying up the boards and burning them, and every morning they looked freshly drawn, as if the charcoal remembered itself.

Wren pointed at it again. And said:

*"Doran."*

I want to be precise here, because precision is all I had left after what happened next. The word wasn't shaped like any word I knew. It wasn't Vessian, which I speak with the accent of someone who learned it late and resents it. It wasn't the gutter-trade pidgin of the night market, or the High Cantile the magistrates use to say nothing at great length. It was none of the languages I have ever heard, and I have heard most of them, because hearing is how you survive in a market where everything—including you—is for sale.

And I understood it completely.

*Doran.* Home. Not house—I have sold enough soldiers' memories to know the difference between *house* and *home*, the way one is walls and the other is the smell of bread through those walls at an hour that means safety. *Doran* meant home the way a wound means the thing that cut you. It meant home with a homesickness folded inside it, the way my charms carry their griefs folded inside their joys. It meant home the way a word means something when it has been said ten thousand times by ten thousand mouths and then never again by anyone.

I understood it the way you understand your own name. Not learned. Known.

The vertigo hit me somewhere below the ribs, in the place where I keep the fear I don't look at directly. Because understanding is evidence. You cannot comprehend a language you've never encountered unless the comprehension was put in you—and there are only two ways anything gets put into Elodie Oyelaran. Someone teaches it to you, or someone sells it to you, or—

Or you already had it, and it was taken.

I sat down on my stool rather harder than dignity allows. Wren watched me with those eyes that were too dark and too old and somehow familiar in a way I'd been refusing to examine, the way you refuse to examine a bill you suspect you can't pay.

"You said a word," I told the child, because talking steadies me even when the words are stupid. "You said *doran*. And I know what it means, and I don't know why I know what it means, and that is—" I laughed, and it came out wrong, thin as counterfeit coin. "That is not possible."

Wren tilted their head. Then, patiently, the way you'd speak to someone who was being very dense about something obvious, Wren said it again.

*"Doran."*

And this time the word came with weather.

I don't know how else to describe it. The first time had been meaning without texture—a translation, clean and cold. The second time the word arrived dressed. It came with heat on its shoulders and smoke in its hair. It came with the smell of fruit cooking somewhere, sweet going to bitter. It came with a sound underneath it like wind through branches heavy with things ready to fall.

An orchard. Burning.

My hands started shaking, which annoyed me professionally. A thief whose hands shake is a thief who gets caught, or worse, a thief who gets caught being caught. I pressed my palms flat against my knees and breathed the way Maro taught me—four counts in, seven out, the rhythm that keeps a man steady while he lifts a stranger's last good afternoon out of him like drawing poison.

"Where did you learn that word?" I asked Wren.

Wren pointed—at me.

"No," I said. "No, no. You've got it backwards, little one. I'm the one who takes. I don't give. Nothing comes from me to anybody. That's not how the trade works. I'm a well, not a fountain. You draw up out of me, you don't pour into—"

Wren crossed the room—three steps; the room is not large—and took my face in both hands. Small hands, warm, absurdly certain. And Wren looked into my eyes the way Ilsa looks at maps, the way Maro looks at ledgers, the way I look at people when I'm deciding what of theirs I can carry away without them noticing it's gone.

Then Wren said, very softly, in that dead language that lived in my skull without paying rent:

*"Elodie."*

Not doran this time. My name. My name—which I chose myself, incidentally, three years ago, off a list in my own head of names that sounded like nobody's, because the girl who arrived in Vess had no name she could recall and needed something to sign the ledger with. Elodie. I'd picked it because it sounded like a woman who had a grandmother. And here was this mute child saying it in Ashkaran—or saying whatever Ashkaran shape of it existed before I invented the Vessian version—and it fit the original like a key fits the lock it was cut for.

The floor tilted. Or I did. One of us.

* * *

Here's the thing about stolen goods: they're quiet. That's the whole art of it. When I lift a memory, I take it whole and sealed, and it sits in its vial like a fly in amber, and it does not whisper to me. I don't know what's in the soldier's charm except what he showed me on his face while I took it. I don't taste the merchant's wedding day. I am, professionally speaking, a courier of other people's interiors. The packages stay closed.

So when I say that the language woke up in me—not arrived, *woke*, like something that had always been there and had merely been lying still—I want you to understand what that implies about where it had been lying.

It had been lying in my stolen memories. Mine. The ones I'd never dared open because rule number one, the only law I ever kept: never steal your own past.

Three years ago I came to Vess carrying nothing but a scar on my palm shaped like something I couldn't identify and a head full of fog where a childhood should be. I assumed the fog was damage. Grief, maybe, or the particular mercy some minds perform when what happened to them is more than they can hold. I built my rules around that assumption. Never steal your own past, because if I went looking for what I'd lost, I might find it—and finding it would mean knowing who took it, and knowing who took it would mean a war I could not win with a knife, a stool, and a talent for making people feel briefly wonderful.

But fog isn't empty. Fog is just water pretending to be absence.

And if Wren—one word, twice spoken—could make the fog light up from inside like a lamp behind linen, then the language wasn't lost. It was stored. Bottled. Filed away in the same vault-quiet dark where I kept forty other people's happiest hours and my own unopened childhood, and it had been waiting—patient as charcoal that won't fade—for someone to say the word that turned the key.

I got up. I got up because sitting down had become unbearable, because my body had decided before my mind did that we were going to do something about this.

"Wren," I said, and crouched to the child's level, and made my voice gentle, which it rarely is, "I need you to say more words. Any words. Say the word for bread. Say the word for—"

Wren shook their head. Firmly. And pointed at the door on the floorboards again, and then—this is the part I keep returning to, the part that has been sitting in my chest for two days now like a swallowed coal—Wren pointed at my palm. The left one. The one with the scar.

The scar shaped, I saw now, saw finally, like a door-key.

I had carried it for twenty years. I had traced it a thousand times in the dark, the way you touch a tooth that aches. I had shown it to exactly one fence, who had gone pale and told me to drown a child. And it had never once occurred to me—never—that a key is not a symbol. A key is a tool. A key is an object with a job. Keys do not exist for doors that were never locked, and locks do not exist for rooms that were never worth keeping.

Somebody had marked me. Marked me young, marked me permanently, marked me with the shape of the thing I was apparently supposed to find. Which meant somebody, twenty years ago, had believed I would need to find it. Believed it hard enough to spend a child's skin on the certainty.

"I don't remember getting this," I whispered.

Of course I didn't. I didn't remember anything. But not remembering, I understood now—with the beautiful terrible clarity of a thief recognizing another thief's work—was not the same as it never happening. Somebody had done to me what I do to others. Somebody had stood over a child and drawn out her hours one by one, sealed them, bottled them, sold them or kept them or burned them, and left the vessel walking around with a key scarred into her hand and no idea what it opened.

The Hollow Men erase practitioners. That's what everyone knows, what the proclamations nail to every post: magic is outlawed, forgetters ride at dusk, confess or be hollowed. But the Hollow Men *eat*. Everyone says eat. Sef—if it was Sef who came to my stall, if that grey appetite in a magistrate's coat tasted Ashkaran in my charms—they take memories into themselves. They consume.

This had been different. This had been tidy. Surgical. Commercial, even. Somebody had harvested me and left the rest intact, functioning, deniable. That's not hunger. That's inventory.

And there is exactly one person in Vess who deals in inventory. Who has vaults under his counting-house. Who raised me, taught me the four-count breath, fed me, lied to me with the tenderness of a father, and went the color of tallow when I showed him a child's drawing of a door.

Maro had told me to drown Wren. I had thought, at the time, that it was fear. Fear of the Hollow Men, fear of attention, fear of the old man's careful life cracking open. Reasonable fear. I'd almost forgiven it before I'd even left his shop.

But fear doesn't make a man pale at a drawing. Guilt makes a man pale at a drawing.

Fear runs from the thing. Guilt recognizes it.

"Doran," I said aloud, testing it in my own mouth, my own voice—and the room responded. Not dramatically. Nothing flew, nothing glowed. But every charm in my jars turned toward me at once. Forty small sealed lights, forty strangers' joys in glass, and they all leaned, the way wheat leans after wind, toward the sound of a word from a country that officially does not exist.

They recognized it. Of course they did. They've spent years pressed against my skin, against my blood, against whatever I am. If the language lives anywhere outside Wren's mouth, it lives in the residue of me—in the sediment I've left in everything I've touched. My thefts had been marinating in my own erased past this whole time, and I'd been selling pieces of Ashkar to half of Vess without knowing it, wrapped in soldiers' happiness and merchants' weddings, seasoned with a dead kingdom like salt in a stew.

No wonder the inquisitor stopped at my stall. No wonder he went still as a struck bell.

And no wonder Maro went white. If anyone in Vess knew what Ashkaran residue smelled like in a charm—knew it the way a man knows the smell of his own crime—it would be whoever helped empty the country in the first place.

I looked at Wren. Wren looked back, patient, ancient, five years old, waiting for me to catch up. The child had walked across a drowned kingdom's grave to my door. Had drawn a door on my floor. Had said one word and cracked my whole life open like a jar.

"You're not lost," I said slowly. "You're not even a stray. You're a trail. You're breadcrumbs. Somebody sent you to the one person in Vess who could follow you home—because following you home means following *me* home."

Wren smiled. Small, crooked, gap-toothed. Familiar. Gods help me, familiar—the smile pulled at muscles in my own face that knew the shape, that had worn it before, in a life I couldn't reach.

I stood. I went to the shelf where I keep the charms I don't sell—the failures, the fragments, the ones too broken or too strange to move at market. Among them, wrapped in oilcloth because it unsettles me, sits a scrap of vellum I bought years ago from a mad cartographer: a rubbing of coastlines that match no chart, labeled in a hand that shakes, *here the map stops agreeing with the world.*

Ilsa. Ilsa of the unfinished maps. The disgraced woman who charts places that stopped existing.

If my past is the map—and it is, I felt it click into place the moment Wren spoke, the certainty settling over me like a coat that fits—then I need someone who can read terrain that isn't there anymore. I need someone who knows what happens to a country when it's unwritten, and where the writing goes, and whether a bottle of stolen childhood can call it back.

I took down my coat. I counted my coins, my charms, my exits. Wren fetched the ragged blanket without being asked, the way children do when they've already understood the shape of the day before the adults finish arguing with it.

At the door I stopped. Looked down at the charcoal lines that would not fade.

"Doran," I said once more, quietly, like locking up. Like a promise. Like a key turning in a lock I couldn't yet see.

Behind me, in the jars, forty small borrowed lights leaned toward the sound—and far across the city, in a counting-house above a vault full of stolen childhoods, I would have bet my hands that an old man felt the word land too, and reached for a ledger, and began, with shaking pen, to sell me.
