## 16. Digging in the Memory Market

The Gullet opens at midnight, which is fitting, because everything sold there should only be handled after dark and with gloves.

Most people know the night market on Chandlers' Row — the pretty one, with lanterns shaped like fish and vendors who call their wares in rhymes. That's where tourists buy charm-bottles of a stranger's first kiss and go home feeling briefly loved by someone they'll never meet. The Gullet is what's underneath Chandlers' Row, one flight of rotten stairs down, where the brokers trade in bulk: grief by the pound, childhoods by the cask, whole decades decanted into jars that used to hold pickled eels.

I'd been selling there for three years. I had never once gone looking to buy.

Buying is different. Selling is a transaction; buying is an admission. The moment you hand over coin for someone else's remembered life, you're confessing that your own isn't enough. And mine — I'd always told myself — was simply *short*, not insufficient. A warehouse fire, a dead family, a boat to Vess, Maro's crooked hand pulling me out of the harbor water. That was the story I had. It fit in a thimble. I'd never wanted to look too closely at why.

But Wren had drawn a door on my floorboards in Ashkaran, and the word for it had risen in me unbidden, like a bubble from deep water, and bubbles come from somewhere.

So at half past midnight I put on my least memorable coat, pocketed every coin I owned, and went down into the Gullet to buy back my own childhood.

* * *

The trick with brokers is that they're all liars, but they're liars with ledgers. Memory is inventory. Inventory has provenance. Somewhere in the Gullet there existed a record of who had sold me my past — because someone *had* sold it, or discarded it, or traded it away, and three years ago a frightened girl with no name she trusted had stood in this same stinking dark while a broker said, *here, take these, they were going cheap.*

I started with Doss.

Doss runs the stall nearest the stairs, which means he pays the most rent, which means he's either the most successful or the most bribed, and with Doss it's both. He's a soft man built like a dropped sack of flour, with rings on every finger except the ones he uses for counting, and when he saw me coming he arranged his face into the expression of a beloved uncle.

"Little Thorn," he said. "Heard you'd gone respectable."

"I sell charms to tourists now. It's practically a guild trade." I kept my voice bored. Bored is armor down here. "I'm shopping tonight, not selling."

That got his attention. Brokers love a buyer the way spiders love a fat fly — but they also get curious, and curiosity in the Gullet is a disease you catch from other people's questions.

"Looking for anything particular?"

"Provenance," I said. "Three years ago. A lot came through here — a girl's childhood, maybe ten years' worth, sold loose or in vials. Fire imagery. A harbor. I want to know who consigned it."

Doss's rings clicked against each other as he folded his hands, which was his way of telling me the next number out of his mouth would be offensive. "Records cost."

"I know what records cost."

"Then you know records about *old lots* cost double. Nobody keeps good books on things people are trying to forget, little Thorn. That's the whole business model." He leaned in, flour-soft and smiling. "And a girl's childhood? Sold loose? You know how many girls' childhoods pass through this market? Half of Vess would match that description if you squint."

"Would they?" I let a little of the cold through. "Because I'd have thought a ten-year lot, unbroken, with that much grief in it, would be rare enough to remember. Even for you."

Something flickered behind his eyes. Not guilt. Recognition. He *did* remember.

"That lot," he said slowly, "wasn't sold loose."

My heart did something unprofessional. "Go on."

"It was bought whole. One buyer. Paid over the odds, which should have been my first warning, because nobody overpays for a stranger's childhood unless—" He stopped himself, and I watched him decide, in real time, how much of me he was willing to endanger for the price of my silence later. "Unless they already know whose it is."

"Doss."

"Don't say names down here." His voice dropped to almost nothing. "You want the ledger entry, it'll cost you, and you want it *unwitnessed*, it'll cost you double, and even then I'm only telling you because whoever bought that lot scares me more than you do, and I've met you angry."

"Flatterer."

"The buyer didn't come in person. Sent a runner with a sealed chit. Chit bore a mark I've seen exactly once before and hoped never to see again." He pulled a scrap of paper toward him, wet his pencil stub, and wrote something. Then he covered it with his palm and looked at me with genuine, floury sorrow. "You're not going to like it."

"I haven't liked anything since Tuesday. Show me."

He lifted his hand. On the paper was a small, precise symbol: a circle with a keyhole cut through its center.

Maro's mark. The mark he pressed into wax on every honest deal and every dishonest one, the mark I'd known since I was fourteen years old and shivering in his back room, the mark that meant *safe* the way a cage means safe.

"He bought my childhood," I said. My voice sounded very far away. "Maro bought my childhood."

"Three years ago," Doss said. "Same season you turned up on his doorstep with nothing but saltwater in your lungs. Funny thing, timing." He slid the paper toward me. "Take it. No charge. Consider it a gift, and consider what I'll want in return to be the privilege of you never mentioning you were here."

I took the paper. My hands were steadier than I deserved.

"One more thing, little Thorn." Doss's face had gone carefully blank, the way men's faces do when they're deciding whether to warn you off a cliff or watch you walk off it. "The runner who brought the chit — I asked around after, quiet-like. Found her. She said the delivery wasn't to Maro's shop. It was to his *vault*. Down past the tanneries, under the old counting-house. And she said—" he grimaced, "—she said the door was warm. Like something inside was breathing."

* * *

I didn't go straight to the vault. I want that on the record, wherever records go when nobody keeps them. I walked the long way, up through the waking streets, past the bakers lighting their ovens, and I made myself do arithmetic instead of feeling things.

Arithmetic: Maro raised me. Fed me, taught me the trade, gave me a room above his shop and a rule — *never steal your own past* — that I'd obeyed like scripture without once asking why he'd made it a law. Arithmetic: three years ago a girl arrived in Vess with no memories and a broker's lot containing exactly those memories went up for sale, and Maro bought the whole thing at over the odds. Arithmetic: he'd had them all along. Every birthday. Every mother. Every burning orchard. Sitting in a vault under the tanneries while I ate his bread and called him the closest thing to a father the city allowed.

Counter-arithmetic, because I owed him that much: maybe he bought them to protect them. Maybe he'd been keeping them safe for me, waiting until I was ready, waiting for some threshold of trust that I'd somehow never crossed. Maybe the rule — *never steal your own past* — was a lock on a door he meant to open gently, someday.

Maybe. But Doss had said the door was warm, and doors don't get warm from kindness.

Wren was asleep on my floor when I got home, curled against the wall beneath the chalk door, one hand open on the boards as if holding something invisible. When I stepped inside, the child's eyes opened — no startle, no blink, just opened, like the child had been awake the whole time behind the sleep.

"I have to go out again," I said, uselessly, in Vessian.

Wren looked at the chalk lines on the floor. Then at me. Then the child did something new: reached out and touched the floorboards twice, deliberately, the way you'd knock.

*Knock,* said the gesture. *It's a door. Doors are for knocking.*

"Not yet," I said, and heard my own voice crack on the second word, and decided not to examine that.

* * *

The old counting-house by the tanneries has been empty for twenty years, which in Vess means it's full of something worse than tenants. The tanners' yards stink so thoroughly that the Watch doesn't patrol them — no one lingers, no one witnesses — and the building hunches behind them like a debtors' debtor, windows boarded, walls stained with rain and older stains.

Maro's vault was underneath. I'd known that much for years, the way you know things about family that you never say aloud. Twice a month he'd disappear for an evening and come back smelling of stone dust and lamp oil, and if I ever asked, he'd say *inventory*, and I'd let it lie, because everyone in the memory trade keeps one room they don't show anyone. Mine was the space behind my own eyes. His was apparently literal.

Getting in was insultingly easy, which should have frightened me more than it did. The boarded side door yielded to a pry-bar I'd brought for exactly this betrayal, though I hadn't known whose. Inside, dust lay thick as snowfall, undisturbed except for a single line of footprints leading to the cellar stairs — Maro's prints, recent, going down and not coming back up.

The stairwell was warm.

Not metaphorically. The air thickened as I descended, took on a temperature, a faint animal warmth, like walking into a mouth. My charms felt it before I did — the two I carry always, a soldier's courage and a bride's forgiveness, tucked against my ribs — and they began to hum, softly, the way they'd hummed when Wren first spoke.

"Oh," I said quietly, to no one. "Oh, you have got to be joking."

The cellar door was iron, and it was locked, and the lock was good, but locks are just opinions and I've spent three years disagreeing with them professionally. It took me four minutes and one skinned knuckle. Beyond it, lamplight leaked yellow under a second door, and from behind that door came a sound I will hear for the rest of my life however long or short that turns out to be:

Humming. Dozens of bottled memories, humming in chorus, low and layered, the way a struck bell keeps singing after the strike. And threaded through the hum, faint but unmistakable, a melody in a language I shouldn't know and somehow did — the same rising phrase Wren had spoken on my doorstep the first night.

Ashkaran. Something in that vault was singing Ashkaran.

I put my ear to the wood. Under the humming, another sound: footsteps. Slow. Pacing. The rhythm of a man who has been pacing for a long time and has stopped expecting to stop.

I could have knocked. Some part of me — the part Maro fed, the part that still tasted his bread and his lies together — wanted to knock, wanted him to open the door and explain, wanted the counter-arithmetic to win.

But I'd seen what happened to the neighbor in the next building when the Hollow Men came. I'd learned what happens to people who wait politely outside doors in this country.

I picked the second lock.

The vault beyond was smaller than the humming promised — one room, stone, shelves from floor to ceiling, and on the shelves, hundreds upon hundreds of bottles, vials, jars, each one glowing faintly with the light of somebody's life. Childhoods in rows. Grief in casks. And in the center of the room, on a workbench under a single lamp, a cluster of perhaps thirty vials set apart from the rest, arranged with terrible care, each labeled in Maro's cramped hand.

Each labeled with a date. Dates spanning ten years. Dates ending the winter I came to Vess.

My dates.

And standing among them, caught mid-pace, lantern in one hand and my entire stolen childhood reflected in his wet eyes, was Maro.

For a long moment neither of us moved. The bottles hummed. The lamp flickered. Three years of borrowed trust hung between us like smoke.

"You found the ledger," he said finally. Not *how did you get in.* Not *it's not what it looks like.* Just that, flat and tired, the voice of a man whose last card had played itself.

"Cost me a favor from Doss," I said. "Which he gave free, which tells you how scared he is. Of you? Or of whatever's making this room sing?"

Maro set the lantern down. His hands, I noticed, were shaking — Maro's hands, which had never shaken, not when the Hollow Men walked into his shop, not ever. "Elodie. Listen to me very carefully. Whatever you think those are—"

"They're mine." I stepped toward the workbench. "Ten years of mine. Bought three years ago, the same season a girl fished out of the harbor conveniently couldn't remember her own name. You've had them the whole time. In a warm room. Under the tanneries." My voice was doing something dangerous and steady. "Why does the room sing, Maro?"

"Because I couldn't destroy them," he said, and the words came out of him like a tooth coming out, bloody and relieving all at once. "God knows I tried. The first year. There are things in a person's childhood that other people will pay *anything* to make sure stay forgotten, and yours—" He stopped. Looked at the vials. Looked at me. "Yours aren't just memories, girl. I've been in this trade forty years. I know stock. And what's in those bottles isn't stock. It's a *keyhole*. And there are men in this city who can feel it humming from across the river."

The warmth in the air pressed closer. On the shelf beside me, one vial glowed brighter than the others — amber light, orchard-colored — and inside it, faint as breath on glass, I could see a small dark-haired girl standing under burning trees, and she was not screaming.

She was singing.

"Who else knows?" I whispered.

Maro's face did the thing faces do when the answer is the worst possible answer. Behind us, upstairs, in the tannery silence, a board creaked under a weight that hadn't been there when I'd pried the door.

"Too late for that question," Maro breathed. "They followed you, girl. Or they followed me. Either way—" he snuffed the lantern with his bare hand, plunging us into the humming dark, "—we're both in the vault now."
