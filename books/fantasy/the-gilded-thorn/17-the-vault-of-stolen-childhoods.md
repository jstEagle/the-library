## 17. The Vault of Stolen Childhoods

Maro's house had always smelled of tallow and old paper and the particular sweetness of memories kept too long in glass — a smell like fruit going soft in a locked pantry. I had catalogued that smell for eleven years. I knew it the way other people knew their mothers' voices.

Which was why it was so strange to be crouched behind his rain barrel at midnight, counting the ways I could get into his cellar.

There were three. The front door, which was barred and warded with a charm I had sold him myself, back when I was nineteen and stupid enough to sell my best work to the man who would one day use it against me. The kitchen stair, which creaked in a language only Maro spoke fluently. And the coal chute, which was narrow, filthy, and guarded by nothing but a rusted grate and my own dignity.

I chose the chute. Dignity is for people who aren't being hunted by Hollow Men.

The grate came away with a squeal I felt in my teeth, and then I was sliding down into darkness on my back like something being buried alive in installments. Coal dust rose around me in a soft black bloom. Somewhere below, the city's night sounds went on — a drunk singing three streets over, a cart, the bells of the Saltmark tower tolling the hour wrong because they'd been doing it wrong for twenty years and no one remembered the correct time anymore.

I landed in the coal bin, stood, and brushed myself off out of habit even though there was no light to see by and no one to see me.

*You've robbed fences before,* I told myself. *This is just a fence you love.*

That was the problem, of course. Every lock I'd ever picked belonged to a stranger. Strangers don't look at you from inside the dark. Strangers don't have your name stitched into the lining of their coat pockets, the way Maro had once sewn mine into a courier satchel when I was fourteen, saying *so you never lose yourself, little thief*, which I now understood to be either the kindest or cruelest thing anyone had ever done for me, depending on what I found at the bottom of this staircase.

Wren was above, tucked into the loft of Ilsa's map shop with instructions to stay absolutely silent and absolutely still, two commands the child received with the serene confidence of someone who had never once obeyed either. I'd left them a charm — a borrowed memory of warm bread, harmless, soothing — pressed into their small palm. If I didn't come back by dawn, Ilsa would take Wren east. That was the arrangement. I hadn't said aloud what "if I didn't come back" meant. Some arrangements are better left unspoken, like wills and apologies.

I struck no light. I didn't need one. Eleven years of moving through Maro's cellar in the dark had written its floor plan into my feet: twelve steps down, turn left, past the drying racks where charms hung like smoked fish, along the shelf of bottled griefs — those sold poorly; nobody wants to buy someone else's sorrow, which tells you everything hopeful about people and nothing true — to the iron door at the far wall.

The door I'd never been allowed through.

*Cellar's for stock,* Maro always said. *Vault's for valuables.* And he'd smile, and I'd smile, and neither of us would say what we both understood: that I was stock too, once. A child delivered to his door with empty eyes and no history, worth exactly as much as the trade he could teach her.

The lock was new. Of course it was new. He'd changed it after the night Sef tasted Ashkaran in my charm, after word went out that the girl with the quiet hands had gone strange, gone loud, gone *visible*. Maro had always taught me that visibility was death in Vess. He'd changed the lock the way a man changes the locks on a house where a daughter has become a liability.

I knelt before it and listened the way Maro had taught me to listen — not with ears but with the flat of my hand, feeling the tumblers' intentions. New lock, but an old make. Vessan ironwork, the kind the guilds had been forging since before the Fall, back when the guilds forged things for purposes other than fear. It wanted a key, and I didn't have one, but it also wanted to be flattered, and flattery I could do.

Three minutes. Four. My knees ached against cold stone. Sweat ran coal-black down my temple.

Then something gave — a tumbler turning over with the reluctant click of a decision regretted even as it's made — and the door swung inward on hinges that had been oiled recently and well.

Maro took care of his valuables.

* *

The vault was smaller than I'd imagined, and I had imagined it often. All these years I'd built it in my head as a cathedral — high shelves, endless rows, the whole architecture of Maro's empire laid out in glory. Instead it was a room barely bigger than my own, low-ceilinged, windowless, lit by a single lamp of green witchfire that burned without heat and threw no shadows, so that everything in it seemed to float slightly, unanchored, like objects photographed underwater.

Shelves lined every wall. On the shelves stood bottles.

Hundreds of them. Small ones, wine-dark, stoppered with wax the color of dried blood. Each bore a tag in Maro's precise, upright hand — the handwriting of a man who believed that anything worth stealing was worth labeling. Dates. Prices. Provenance codes. And, in most cases, a single descriptive line, because Maro understood that memory, like any commodity, sells better with good copy.

*Ivy-covered gate, summer, the smell of rain coming.*
*A mother humming, off-key, unbothered.*
*First snow seen from a sickbed window.*
*Being forgiven.*

I walked the rows slowly, reading tags, and the green light made the bottles glow faintly from within, each one holding its little captive dawn. This was the trade, laid bare. This was what I did to people, arranged neatly by vintage. I had always told myself my work was gentle — that I took only the happiest hours, that a man dying in a gutter could spare one golden afternoon better than he could spare blood. Standing here, I saw the arithmetic differently. Every bottle was a person's best moment, decanted, corked, priced. Every bottle was someone walking the rest of their life with a hole shaped like joy.

And somewhere in this room was me.

I found the section by accident, or by instinct, or by whatever sense had been leading me since a mud-caked child knocked on my door speaking a dead tongue. The tags in this row were older — the ink faded, the wax cracked — and the dates clustered around three years ago, the year I'd come to Vess, though I hadn't known until recently that I'd *come* rather than simply *been*. The provenance codes were different here too. Not Vessan. The letters were angular, dense, beautiful in a way that hurt to read, and I recognized them the way you recognize a face across a crowded market — not from memory, since I had none, but from Wren's drawings on my floorboards.

Ashkaran script.

My hands were shaking. I noted this with the detached interest of a craftsman observing her tools misbehave.

The first bottle I lifted held something that moved like water and burned like sunset — amber shot through with red, swirling, restless. The tag read:

*Orchard, burning. Child, age six. Price: see ledger.*

*Burning.* They'd labeled it *burning* as if that were the memorable part, as if the fire were the feature and not the wound. But even through the glass, even before I uncorked it, I could feel it — warmth, yes, and smoke, but underneath both, apples. Ripe apples and dry grass and someone calling a name across rows of trees, and the name was —

It wasn't Elodie. I knew that with total certainty. Elodie was the name Maro gave me, the name for the girl with no past, the name that fit like a coat bought for someone else. The name called across the orchard was shorter. Softer. It ended in a vowel that doesn't exist in Vessan.

I set the bottle down before I dropped it.

* *

Here is what no one tells you about temptation: it doesn't arrive looking like temptation. It arrives looking like justice.

I had rehearsed this break-in as theft. In and out, quick hands, no sentiment — the same discipline I brought to any job. But standing in the green dark among the bottled childhoods, holding my own six-year-old self in my cupped palms, I discovered that theft requires a certain distance from the goods, and I had none. This wasn't merchandise. This was the answer to a question I'd been carrying so long I'd mistaken it for my skeleton: *who was I before?*

I wanted to uncork it right there. I wanted to drink my own history standing up in a robber's cellar like a drunk with a stolen bottle, consequences be damned. Three years of waking at night reaching for something that wasn't there. Three years of watching clients weep over returned joys while I stood outside the whole human ceremony of remembering. Three years of being competent and invisible and alone, and here, *here*, in a bottle with a price tag, was the cure.

My thumb found the wax seal.

Stop.

Maro's voice, in my head, in the tone he used for apprentices about to ruin good stock: *Patience, little thief. Greed is how they catch you.*

But it wasn't greed. That was the thing I couldn't explain to Maro's ghost-voice, and wouldn't have tried. Greed wants more. I wanted only what was already mine — which is, I suppose, the oldest justification in the world, and the exact one every thief in this room had probably whispered to themselves over someone else's bottled happiness. The thought arrived unwelcome and stayed: *how am I different?*

Because it's mine, I answered. Because it was taken from a child who couldn't consent. Because —

Because the soldier in the gutter hadn't consented either. Because the widow whose wedding-day I'd lifted hadn't consented. Because I had built a whole ethics of *gentle taking* that amounted to choosing which wounds I was willing to see.

I stood in the vault of stolen childhoods and finally understood that I had been robbing houses my entire career, and this one was simply the first with my name on the deed.

None of which changed what I did next.

I'm not going to pretend otherwise. You can have your epiphanies and keep your hands, too; that's the dirty secret of revelation. I wrapped the orchard bottle in cloth from my pocket, careful as a midwife, and I took four more from the row — the ones whose tags made my chest ache without my knowing why. *A festival, lanterns on water.* *A woman's hands braiding hair.* *A promise made at a door.* *Home, defined.* Five vials. Five fragments of a country that had never existed and a girl who had never been anyone else.

The fifth bottle was different from the others. Smaller. The liquid inside didn't swirl; it sat perfectly still, and it wasn't amber but a deep, patient blue, like evening before stars. Its tag had no description at all. Just the date — the same date as the others — and a single word in Maro's hand, underlined twice:

*Key.*

I stared at it until the green light swam. Key. Key to what? Maro had known. Maro had known three years ago, when he'd bought a traumatized child's memories off some broker and filed one vial under *key* instead of under a feeling. He'd known what I was carrying. He'd known what I'd lost. And he'd raised me anyway, fed me anyway, taught me the trade anyway — keeping the answer to my life on a shelf ten steps from where I slept, priced and waiting.

Something in my chest went very quiet and very cold, the way it does just before a charm takes hold, except no charm had touched me. This was all mine. This coldness was homegrown.

I took the blue vial too.

Six bottles in my satchel, wrapped and muffled, weighing almost nothing and everything. I turned for the door.

* *

That's when I saw the ledger.

It lay open on the vault's single desk, because Maro was many things but never careless with anything except, apparently, the assumption that I was still the obedient creature he'd made. The page facing up was recent — the ink still sharp. A list. Names, dates, sums. And down the right-hand column, in entries spanning months, a client code I recognized because I'd seen it scratched on the wax seals of charms sold through intermediaries I'd never met: the mark of the Inquisition.

Maro had been selling to the Hollow Men. Not just information — *stock*. Bottled memories, purchased by the men who ate them. And the most recent entry, dated nine days ago, listed a single item with a provenance code I knew intimately, because it was mine, because I'd coded my own work for years:

One charm, sourced O.O. Residue: pre-Fall dialect. Buyer: Inquisitor Second Class. Note: *client requests further product from same source.*

He'd been feeding me to them in doses. Selling my craft — maybe selling pieces of *me* — to the men who hollow people out, one taste at a time, letting them develop the appetite slowly, the way you fatten a goose. All those years of *visibility is death, little thief*, and the whole time he was the one holding the lamp.

I should have left. Every instinct Maro had ever installed in me screamed leave — take the vials, go, vanish, you have what you came for. But my feet carried me to the desk instead, and my hand turned the ledger pages backward, backward, three years, until I reached the first entries in Ashkaran-coded provenance. The acquisition record. The bill of sale for a child's entire past.

The seller's code meant nothing to me. The buyer's column held one word, and the word was *M.*, and beside it, in the notes margin, in Maro's tidy hand, a line that I read four times because my mind refused it the first three:

*Full lot. Seller says the girl watched the orchard burn and remembers nothing. Verify blankness before payment. If she ever recovers the lot, the Door business becomes a problem. Keep the key vial separate. She must never connect the child to the fen.*

The fen.

Ilsa's unfinished map bloomed behind my eyes — the Salt Fen, the place where Ashkar stood, the route we were meant to walk. Maro knew about the fen. Maro knew about the Door. Maro had known for three years, had sat across from me at a thousand dinners, had sewn my name into a satchel, had said *so you never lose yourself* — while keeping me lost on purpose, deliberately, profitably, because a girl with no past asks no questions and a girl with no past can be kept.

The green witchfire flickered. Just once. Just slightly.

I froze with my hand flat on the ledger, and in the silence I heard it — beneath the drunk singing, beneath the wrong-tolled bells, beneath the sound of my own blood — a thin, rising note from the corridor above. A charm waking. Not one of Maro's cheap market wards; I knew those the way I knew my own heartbeat. This was guild-work, expensive, patient, the kind of alarm that didn't ring bells but sent whispers — straight to the ear of whoever had paid for it.

And there were only two people in Vess who'd want to know the moment someone opened the vault of stolen childhoods.

I looked down at the ledger, at the ink still sharp enough to smear, and understood that the page had been left open on purpose. That the desk sat exactly where a fleeing thief's eye would find it. That the newest lock, the oiled hinges, the unlocked-from-outside feel of the whole night had the shape of a hand inviting me in.

Either Maro had grown careless in his old age.

Or Maro had wanted me to see the ledger, wanted me to take the vials, wanted me running — because a thief caught in the act needs a fence to save her, and a fence who saves her owns her forever. Or worse: because the Hollow Men's client had requested *further product from same source*, and the easiest way to deliver the source was to let her run somewhere predictable, weighted down with six bottles of her own past, slow as a woman carrying water.

The whispering note climbed higher. Somewhere above me, a door opened — the front door, the barred one, the one with the ward I'd sold him myself — and boots came down the kitchen stair with the unhurried tread of men who owned the building, the street, and the outcome.

I killed the witchfire with a pinch and a word, and the dark closed over me like water, and I stood in the vault of stolen childhoods holding my own life in a satchel, listening to the footsteps count down the stairs.

Twelve steps. Turn left. Past the drying racks.

Coming toward me.
