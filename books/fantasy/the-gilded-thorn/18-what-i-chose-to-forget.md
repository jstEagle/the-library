## 18. What I Chose to Forget

The vial was smaller than a sparrow's egg and heavier than my whole life.

I want to be precise about that, because precision is all I had left at that point. The vault of Maro the Magnificent—fence, father-figure, professional liar with excellent taste in candlesticks—was a long room under his counting house, lined floor to ceiling with niches, and every niche held glass. Bottled joy. Bottled grief. Bottled Tuesdays, if you could find a buyer for them. Three years I'd run errands through this room, carrying other people's pasts like a courier carries letters she isn't allowed to read.

And there, on the third shelf from the door, in a niche marked only with a wax seal shaped like a moth, sat my childhood.

I knew it the way you know your own name spoken by a stranger in a crowd. Not the label—Maro hadn't bothered labeling it, which should have told me something about how much he valued me—but the vial itself. The glass had gone faintly amber, the way charms do when they've been sitting near strong memory too long. It hummed against my palm, low and constant, like a hive thinking.

The alarm would trip the moment I lifted it. I knew that too. Maro's vault wasn't guarded by men; men can be bribed, and Maro had been bribed so many times he'd developed an immunity, like a horse to snake venom. No, the vault was guarded by the bottles themselves. Disturb one without the owner's key and every charm in the room woke up screaming—not sound exactly, but the magical equivalent, a shriek of displaced memory that any Hollow Man within three miles would feel in his teeth.

So naturally I picked it up.

Let me explain my reasoning, such as it was. My reasoning was: twenty years ago a country died, and everyone forgot it had ever lived, including me, apparently, because I had *lived* there. My reasoning was: a mute child had walked out of the rain speaking the dead tongue and drawn a door on my floorboards, and the door had opened onto a room that smelled of woodsmoke and someone singing. My reasoning was: I had spent three years selling strangers their own happiness back to them at four coppers a charm, telling myself it was honest work, honest as anything in Vess, while the entire missing half of me sat in a niche in my foster-father's cellar gathering dust.

My reasoning, in short, was not reasoning at all. It was hunger. But I've never claimed to be a philosopher. Philosophers don't survive night markets.

* * *

The lifting itself took less time than this sentence. Glass off the shelf, wrapped in my scarf, into the satchel with Wren's spare shirt. The shriek started before my fingers cleared the niche—a rising whine, then a chorus, hundreds of bottled voices waking at once, confused and furious, memories shouting *where am I, where am I* in the wordless way memories do.

I ran.

Maro's counting house had two exits, which is one more than most fences bother with, because most fences aren't cowards with foresight. I went out the back, through the tallow-smelling kitchen where old Sefa dozed over her ledger, up the stairs, across the roof of the dye-works next door with its reek of mordant and its view of half of Vess laid out below me like a spilled jewelry box. Behind me the whine faded, swallowed by the city's ordinary noise. Ahead of me, somewhere south, the Hollow Men were already turning their eyeless faces toward Maro's street.

They'd come for him first. That thought should have made me feel worse than it did. I filed it away to feel later. I've always been good at filing things; it's how you survive in Vess. Feel it Tuesday. Or never.

* * *

Wren was waiting where I'd left her—at the shrine to Saint Ovelin of the Broken Compass, a squat little alcove between a chandler's and a pawnshop, where the saint's stone face had been worn smooth by centuries of desperate people touching it for luck they didn't believe in. She sat cross-legged on the step with her knees drawn up, watching the street with the patience of something that had waited longer than streets.

She saw the satchel and stood. Didn't reach for it. Just looked at me, and then at the bulge under the canvas, and her whole small body leaned toward it like a plant toward light.

"Not yet," I said. "Not here."

Here was a public street in a city where magic was outlawed and I was now, officially, a thief of state evidence or whatever the Hollow Men called it when they wrote their reports, which presumably no one ever read, since reading requires remembering and remembering is what they eat. Still. Habits keep you alive.

We went up. Vess is a city of roofs the way a swamp is a city of mud—you don't love them, but they'll carry you where the streets won't. I'd learned the rooftops in my first year, back when I still flinched at heights, and now they were as familiar as my own hands. We crossed the chandler's, the cooperage, the long sagging spine of the tannery sheds, and came down at last in the ruin behind the old glassworks—an abandoned kiln yard where the walls were thick enough to swallow sound and nobody had cared about anything in thirty years.

I built a fire. Small, shielded, mostly for light. And then I sat with the vial in my cupped hands and did not open it for a long time.

Because here's the thing nobody tells you about stolen goods: once you know they're yours, they change weight. For three years I'd handled charms like merchandise—cool, quick, professional. This thing in my hands was warm as a sleeping bird and it was beating, or seemed to be, and I sat there in the kiln yard with a mute child watching me and a stolen country humming against my palms, and I was afraid.

Not of the Hollow Men. Not of Maro, though God knows I would be soon enough. I was afraid of what was in the bottle. Because I had built myself from scratch, see. Elodie Oyelaran, arrived in Vess three years ago with no past, no family, no name anyone recognized—and I had made that into a kind of freedom. Nobody's daughter. Nobody's debt. A woman with empty pockets and clean hands, or as clean as a thief's get. If I opened that vial and found nothing—if the amber glass held fog and static and the ordinary dark of a mind that had simply never been anywhere—I wouldn't lose a memory. I'd lose the story I'd been telling myself. The one where I was *from* somewhere. The one where the ache I carried had a shape and a name.

"Elodie." Wren said it—the only word she ever said, my name, in that voice like a bell rung underwater—and put her hand flat on the vial.

The humming stopped. Everything stopped. The fire, the wind, the distant bells of the Hour of Anchors. The world held its breath like a thief at a window.

Then the vial opened—or rather, it came undone, the stopper lifting without my touching it, and the memory poured out and up and around us like smoke that had decided to become a place.

* * *

You want to know what a recovered memory feels like? It doesn't feel like remembering. Remembering is thin, secondhand, a painting of a room. This was the room.

Heat first. Late-summer heat, orchard heat, the air thick with ripening fruit and the dust of the road beyond the wall. I was small—I knew I was small the way you know things in dreams, absolutely and without evidence—and I was running between rows of trees heavy with something like plums but darker, purple-black, with skins that shone. Someone was behind me. Not chasing. Following at a walk, laughing, letting me win.

"Mama says six," a voice said—my voice, a child's voice, higher than I remembered voices going—"but Mama counts slow."

"And who counts true?" the man behind me asked. His accent was one I'd never heard and understood perfectly, the way you understand your own heartbeat.

"The priest. The priest says seven. Seven days till the Door-feast."

"Seven days." He caught me up, swung me onto his shoulders, and I got my first clear look at his face, and the world tilted sideways and kept tilting.

He was young. Younger than I am now. Dark-haired, broad through the shoulders, with a scholar's ink stains on his fingers and a farmer's calluses underneath them, and a face that was—there's no honest way to say this—kind. Open. The kind of face that expects to be liked and usually is. He wore a plain wool coat with the collar turned up against the evening, and around his neck, on a cord, hung a key. An iron key, old-fashioned, with a bow shaped like a thorn blossom.

I knew that face.

I have spent three years in Vess learning every portrait of Aurel Vane that hangs in every government office, because knowing the usurper's face is survival—it's on the coins, for pity's sake, stamped on the currency we steal and spend. The coin-face is stern, gray-bearded, carved by committee. The face above me in the orchard was twenty years younger and bore no beard, but the bones were the bones. The set of the jaw. The particular way his eyes crinkled, which on the coins looks like wisdom and in life, up close, in the gold light of a dying summer, looked like delight.

Aurel Vane carried me on his shoulders through an orchard in a country that does not exist, and I laughed, and he laughed, and neither of us knew yet what he would become.

That was the moment my identity shattered, if you want the beat-by-beat. Not with horror. With confusion. Because the memory didn't stop, and the memory didn't make him monstrous. Memory after memory unspooled from that single vial—vials are dense, Maro bought compression charms wholesale—and in every one of them he was the same. Teaching me letters under the plum trees, his finger tracing shapes in the dirt. Arguing gently with my mother about whether the Door should ever be opened, his hand resting on the thorn-blossom key. Standing in the rain the last night, saying goodbye to my parents at our gate, and—this is the part I will be turning over until I die—looking down at me, six or seven years old, hiding behind my mother's skirts, and saying:

"She won't remember any of this. That's the mercy of it. Let her be new."

He arranged it. Or he consented to it, agreed to it, signed whatever needed signing—my parents handing their daughter out of a dying country the way you pass a candle through a closing door. And the man who did that, the man whose face was wet with rain and something else, was not yet a monster. He was just a man holding a key to a country's soul, and I have to believe—he must have believed—that drinking a nation was better than losing it. That if he swallowed Ashkar whole, kept it safe inside himself, then even unwritten, it would endure. Somewhere in the dark of him, maybe it even felt like devotion.

Devotion. There's a poison that comes in a loving cup.

* * *

The memory ended the way memories end—all at once, mid-breath, the orchard folding up like a fan. I came back to myself sitting in the cold kiln yard with ash on my hands and tears on my face and no memory of having started crying.

Wren was pressed against my side. She had watched the whole thing, I realized—she'd seen it with me, or through me, her head tipped back, her eyes wide and reflecting fires that weren't in our little flame. When I looked down at her she spoke. Not her one word. A flood of it—Ashkaran pouring out of her fast and urgent, her small hands shaping the grammar of a dead kingdom, and the terrible thing, the vertiginous, floor-dropping-out-from-under-me thing, was that I understood every syllable.

*You saw him. You saw him before. Tell me—was he good then? Was he good before?*

"I don't know," I said, and heard my own voice come out in Ashkaran too, unbidden, rusty as an unused hinge. "Wren. I don't know what he was. I know what he is."

But even that was a lie, or half of one, because what I knew now was worse than either answer. I knew that monsters are made, not born, and I knew the workshop. I knew that the man who unmade my country had once swung me on his shoulders and called me *little thief* affectionately, because I'd pocketed a plum, and I knew that some part of him—the part that said *let her be new*—had done what he did believing it was protection.

And underneath all of it, quieter, colder, a thought I couldn't shake:

*He has my whole past. All of it. Every year of Ashkar, every festival, every name—he drank it. Which means somewhere inside Aurel Vane, tangled in with everything else he's swallowed, there is a version of me. A child in an orchard. He remembers my childhood better than I do.*

I looked at my hands. They were shaking. In the firelight the scar on my right palm showed pale—the old scar I'd had since before Vess, since before everything, the one shaped vaguely like a key that I'd always assumed was from some ordinary accident in an ordinary life that had never existed.

It wasn't an accident scar. I knew that now the way you know your own name. Something had been given to me, or taken from me, at a door.

Wren tugged my sleeve. Pointed east, toward the river, toward the Salt Fen none of the maps admitted to. Then she pointed at the satchel, at the empty space where the vial had been, and then—she took my scarred hand in both of hers, turned it over, and traced the shape of the scar with one deliberate finger.

Door. Key. Door.

"Yes," I said. "I know. I'm going."

And then, because the night wasn't finished ruining me, I heard it: boots on gravel, at the mouth of the kiln yard, and a voice I knew—dry, tired, and terribly gentle—the voice of Inquisitor Sef, who had bought a charm from me not ten days ago and tasted something in it that should not exist.

"Elodie Oyelaran," he called, conversationally, the way you'd greet a neighbor over a fence. "Don't run. I only want to ask you about the orchard."
