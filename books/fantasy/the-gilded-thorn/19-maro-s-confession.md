## 19. Maro's Confession

There is a particular smell to gold that has been paid for a betrayal. Most people will tell you gold has no smell at all, and most people have never been paid to open a gate they swore to keep shut. I have. It smells like sweat and river water and the faint sweetness of the wax seals on a courier's satchel — because it was a courier who brought it, riding up to the Ashkaran border post on the last night of the world, with coin enough to buy my signature on a piece of paper that said *no one came through here*.

I was twenty-six years old and I had been a border guard for four years, which is to say I had spent four years freezing on a wall in the eastern marches watching nothing happen, being paid almost nothing to watch it, and dreaming of Vess. Everyone dreamed of Vess then. The city of lamps, the city of contracts, the city where a man could be anyone he said he was and no one would check the ledger twice.

The thieves who came through my gate that night were not the romantic kind. No swashbuckling, no masks, no clever dialogue by moonlight. They were tired men and women with ledgers of their own, ledgers full of names of people who had agreed — *agreed*, they kept insisting, and I believed them, God forgive me, I wanted to believe them — to sell an hour or a year of their lives for bread money. They called themselves harvesters. They called it a trade. They had documents with signatures and everything, because even theft, done at scale, wants paperwork.

They offered me a third share to look elsewhere. I asked for half. That is the detail I cannot scrub out, the one that comes back at three in the morning wearing its little hat: not that I took their gold, but that I haggled. A man who haggles over treason has already told himself a story about what he is, and the story is *businessman*.

They poured the coin into my helmet because I didn't have a bag big enough. I remember the weight. Nine pounds, give or take. I have lifted a lot of things since — crates, coffins, a child out of a flood channel once, though she'd dispute the word *child* — and none of them have ever weighed what that helmet weighed going down the wall stairs two at a time so the sergeant wouldn't see me staggering.

* * *

Here is what I did not know, standing at my little gate with my ill-gotten helmetful, waving through the wagons: they weren't just harvesting. They were the advance party. Aurel Vane's people, though the name Vane meant nothing to anyone yet except as a rising star in some ministry nobody respected. The memory-thieves were the siege engines. You don't need to burn a country if you can make it forget itself. Ashkar didn't fall. Ashkar was *edited*, and I held the pen for one night, and I was paid nine pounds in coin for the privilege.

By morning the wagons were gone east and the paperwork said nothing had come through, and within a season the paperwork was all there was. Not just mine — everyone's. Maps went soft in the middle like fruit going bad. Archives developed gaps you could drive a legion through. Old men who'd traded in Ashkar markets couldn't say what they'd bought there, only that they had a strange ache when they looked at certain rivers. And me? I remembered everything, because I hadn't sold anything. Yet.

That was the trap, you understand. That was the elegant, beautiful, monstrous part of it. The thieves left a card behind, tucked into my pay chest like a tip. An address in Vess. *If the remembering becomes inconvenient.*

It became inconvenient within the month. I would wake up hearing a language I couldn't name, singing in my own voice. I would taste orchard smoke. I would stand in the barracks yard and feel the enormous, grinding pressure of a secret that had no edges — because a secret about a country is too big to keep; it doesn't sit in your head, it sits on your chest, and it breathes for you, badly.

So I went to Vess. And I made my first purchase.

* * *

Let me tell you how memory-selling works from the buyer's side, since everyone romanticizes the thief and nobody writes about the man in the chair. There is a room above a dye shop in the Tanners' Quarter, and there is a broker named Sallow-Neck Ferro who has hands like a midwife and eyes like a bill collector, and he sits you down and says, "What are we taking?"

You don't get to choose precisely. Memory isn't a shelf of jars; it's a woven thing. You can point at a knot and say *this one, the guilt*, and Ferro will nod and smile and take hold of the knot, but thread runs through the whole cloth. I wanted the gate, the wagons, the helmet of coin. What came out also took: my mother's lullabies (she used to sing in Ashkaran; I lost the words and kept the tune, which is a cruelty I wouldn't wish on a tax collector), the name of my first sergeant, the taste of a specific plum, the reason I flinched at wax seals.

And the guilt itself. Oh, the guilt went beautifully. I remember the moment it lifted — it felt exactly like setting down the nine pounds of coin, that same unclenching in the shoulders — and I remember thinking, with the lightness of a man newly robbed, *why did I come here again?*

That's the joke of it. That's the whole rotten joke. Erasure doesn't leave you clean. It leaves you light, and lightness without a reason is its own kind of weight. For twenty years I have walked around Vess feeling like a man who has forgotten why he's apologizing, apologizing anyway. I overtip. I feed stray cats. I raised a thief's brat off the street because something in me howled at the sight of her hungry, and I never asked myself too closely *why her, why that howl*. You don't ask questions of a hole. The hole asks them of you, and it never likes your answers.

* * *

Elodie. Let's talk about Elodie, since we're confessing.

She came to me at nine years old, mud to the eyebrows, holding a dead sparrow in her cupped hands like it was an offering and she was very small god. She'd heard — gods know how, she heard everything, that child had ears like a debt collector — that I dealt in charms. She wanted to trade the sparrow's last flight for bread.

"It's a bad trade," I told her. "A bird's hour of dying isn't worth bread."

"It's not dying," she said. "It's flying. Last thing it did was fly."

I gave her the bread and kept the charm and wore it myself for a year, and on grey mornings I'd press it to my palm and feel a small brown body doing the one magnificent thing it knew. Then I taught her the trade properly, and I taught her the rules, and the first rule I taught her was the one rule I invented specifically so that she would never, ever end up like me:

*"Never steal your own past."*

She thought it was craft wisdom. Don't poach your own memories, girl, the charm comes out flat, no market for secondhand grief you already lived. All true. All beside the point. The real reason is that I have seen what a person looks like after they've sold themselves off in pieces, and I couldn't do it. Couldn't watch her do it. Call it the one honest act of my life, dressing cowardice up as mentorship.

And now she has gone and found a child that speaks a dead tongue, and drawn a door on my floorboards — well, on hers, but news travels — and suddenly every hole in me has started asking questions in unison.

Because she brought me a rubbing of that drawing. Charcoal on paper, pressed off the boards of her floor, and I looked at it and my hands shook before my head knew why. My body recognized it. Some scrap of thread the brokers missed, some splinter of the knot, sat up in me and *screamed*. I've stood at that door. Or one like it. On the far side of a gate in the eastern marches, twenty years ago, there was a checkpoint building, and inside the checkpoint building there was a door painted with that exact arch, that exact keyhole-scar, because it was a standard pattern, because Ashkar stamped its doors the way Vess stamps its coins, and I walked past it every day for four years and I let the wagons through it and I—

I ordered her to drown the child. You'll want to know that, since we're being thorough. I looked at a charcoal rubbing of a door and told a woman I love like the daughter of my sins to drown a mud-caked child in the canal, and I dressed it up in urgency — *they'll come for you, they'll come for both of you, it's mercy, it's strategy* — and every word was true except the ones that mattered. The truth underneath was simpler. The truth was: *if that child is what I think it is, then what I did is real again, and I cannot afford for it to be real, and I will pay anything to make it unreal, including you.*

Twenty years ago I paid gold to forget. This time I'm paying differently.

This time I'm paying in people.

* * *

The Hollow Men's man came at dusk, the way their men always do, as if fear keeps office hours. Not the faceless horror of the stories — the stories undersell it, actually, because a Hollow Man in person is polite. Terribly polite. His name was Sef, and he had the stillness of furniture and the eyes of a man reading a menu, and he sat in my back room among the charm-stock and drank the tea I gave him because refusing would have been impolite, and he said:

"We're told you have knowledge of anomalous residue. Ashkaran-class."

"There's no such class," I said. "Ashkara—"

"Don't," he said, mildly, and the mildness was worse than a knife. "Don't finish that word in my presence unless you want this conversation to become a different conversation."

I finished my tea instead. We understood each other.

He wanted Elodie. He wanted the child. He wanted addresses, habits, escape routes — and here is the thing I need you to understand, the thing that makes this a confession rather than a complaint: he didn't threaten me. He offered. A clean slate. Full excision, professional grade, better than Ferro's dye-shop work. *Whatever it is you're carrying, Mr. Maro, we can carry it away.* And I sat there knowing exactly what he meant, knowing he knew, knowing that the Hollow Men always know, that's their whole terrible genius, they recruit from the guilty because the guilty are already halfway hollowed —

and I gave him the address.

I gave him the address the way I signed the paper at the gate: quickly, before the weight of it could reach my shoulders. Same motion. Same man. Twenty years between, and the hand remembers.

* * *

Afterward I did what I always do. I worked. Counted stock, sealed jars, polished the little brass scales until they lied convincingly about being honest instruments. And somewhere around midnight I went down to the vault — my vault, under the shop, the one with the good locks, the one where I keep the inventory that isn't for sale — and I stood in front of the shelf where I keep the bottles I should have destroyed decades ago.

Three of them. Ashkaran glass, the old make, faintly green. Harvest stock from the wagons. Payment-in-kind, the thieves called it, tossing me my third share in bottled hours because coin was getting heavy. I never opened them. I never dared. They've sat there glowing faintly, like embers that won't admit they're dead, for twenty years, and every time business brings me down here they hum at me, softly, in a language I paid to forget.

Tonight they were loud.

Tonight, when I put my ear to the shelf — and I don't know why I did that, call it the hole asking its question — I heard, unmistakable, coming out of the smallest bottle like steam out of a kettle:

A child laughing. Orchard smoke. A festival drum. And under it all, thin as wire, a voice singing in Ashkaran, and the voice was young, and it was singing a lullaby, and some buried shard of me knew the words, knew them the way you know a scar, and the lullaby was—

It was the one my mother sang. It was the one I lost. It was in *that bottle*, in harvest stock, meaning it wasn't mine at all, meaning either someone else's mother sang it too, in that country, the way mothers do, everywhere, the same seven notes passed hand to hand like bread —

or meaning the bottle was mine, and the thieves took more from the border guard than I ever knew, and I have spent twenty years missing a mother whose face I traded for a clean ledger and can no longer picture.

I broke the seal. I'm telling you I broke the seal because there's no version of this confession where I pretend I hesitated. I drank it standing up, greedy, like a man drinking after a desert, and the orchard bloomed, and the drums, and a woman's hands braiding someone's hair, and a gate, my gate, seen from the other side, from *inside* the country, on the last bright afternoon before the wagons came, and I was in it, I was in both sides of it, thief and guard, and the whole memory folded closed around me like a door swinging shut—

—and upstairs, faintly, sweetly, the alarm bells in my stockroom began to ring, because someone had tripped the ward on the case where I kept the one bottle I never shelved: the vial I bought three years ago from a harvester who didn't know what he had, the vial labeled in a dead tongue, the childhood of a mud-caked girl with sparrow hands, the burning orchard, *her* burning orchard, which I purchased and hid and told myself I was keeping safe for her, and which she has just stolen back through my best lock while I stood downstairs drinking my own mother's voice.

The bells rang, and I stood in the dark with orchard smoke in my mouth, and I understood, finally, with total clarity, what I had sold tonight and to whom, and what was climbing her stairs right now with polite feet.

I ran for the ladder.
