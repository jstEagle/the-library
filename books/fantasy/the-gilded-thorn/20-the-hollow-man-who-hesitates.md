## 20. The Hollow Man Who Hesitates

There is a rumor about the Hollow Men that we have no hearts. I'd like to correct the record, for accuracy's sake: we have hearts. They simply no longer contain anything, in roughly the way a house that has burned to its foundations still technically contains rooms.

My name was Sef. Is Sef. The Order took my surname when they took everything else, on the principle that a man with one name is easier to file. I have been an inquisitor of Vess for eleven years, which means I have eaten more memories than most people will ever *have*, and I can tell you the truth nobody wants printed: memory tastes like nothing at all when you take it by force.

That's the trick of it. The priests say sin weighs on the soul. It doesn't. It just leaves room where there used to be flavor.

* * *

The summons came at dusk, as summons do — never at a civilized hour, because the Order believes comfort breeds doubt, and doubt breeds questions, and questions breed exactly the kind of thinking that gets men like me unmade from the inside out.

Inquisitor-General Corvane received me in the Hall of Ledgers, which is what the Order calls the room where it keeps its conscience: forty shelves of wax tablets recording every extraction performed since the Fall. Forty shelves. Twenty years. Do the arithmetic and then don't, because the arithmetic is the kind that makes a man look at his hands.

"Sef." Corvane didn't look up from the tablet he was reading. He never looks up. I once spent an entire briefing studying the top of his head and wondering if he could taste my curiosity, and decided he could, and stopped wondering things in his presence. A useful discipline. Most of my inner life now happens elsewhere, in a small rented room behind my eyes where Corvane has no jurisdiction. "You know the Oyelaran woman."

"Elodie Oyelaran. Memory-thief. Operates the night market under the sign of the—"

"I know her trade. What I want to know is why she's suddenly interesting."

I told him what we had: reports of a child speaking an unregistered tongue. Charms humming with residue that made three junior extractors sick. A fence named Maro who had, two nights ago, sold us her address with the trembling eagerness of a man paying off a debt he couldn't name. All of it true. None of it complete, because I had also read Maro's statement four times and noticed what wasn't in it — the way he'd flinched at the word *Ashkaran*, as though the word itself were contraband. But you don't volunteer observations to Corvane. You let him think he's extracted them.

"A dead language," Corvane said, and here he did look up, and I wished he hadn't. There are lamps in Vess that burn without oil. Corvane's eyes are that kind of light — illumination with no source, warmth with no fire. "There are no dead languages, Sef. There are only languages we haven't finished killing."

He gave me the writ. Kill the thief. Take the child alive. And bring back any charms in her possession unopened, because the residue interested the General Office, and the General Office being interested in anything is how cities end up on lists that later turn out never to have existed.

"You hesitate," Corvane said, as I turned to go.

I hadn't. I had paused. There is a difference, and eleven years ago I would have known it instinctively, and tonight I had to construct the distinction deliberately, brick by brick, like a man rebuilding a wall while someone watches.

"No, General."

"Mm," said Corvane, who tasted lies the way other men taste salt.

* * *

Here is what the recruitment pamphlets don't tell you about becoming Hollow. They make it sound like surgery: the bad memories removed, the grief excised, the fear cauterized. A clean wound. What actually happens is closer to what happened to Ashkar, if half the rumors I'm not supposed to have heard are true — not removal but *unwriting*. The extractors don't cut the memory out. They drink it, and what remains isn't absence. Absence would be honest. What remains is a smooth surface where the texture used to be, and you spend years running your thumb over it, trying to find the seam.

They took my war. That was the pitch, anyway: you've seen things a man shouldn't see, son, and we can make it so you haven't. I was nineteen, a conscript from the river provinces, and I had done two years of things I drank myself stupid to forget anyway, so the offer sounded like mercy.

What I didn't understand was that they don't take just the bad. Grief isn't a single thread you can pull; it's woven through everything. They took the war, and the taking took my mother's face — because her face lived in the last letter she sent me, which lived in the memory of reading it, which was soaked in the same blood-water as everything else from those years. They took the war, and took with it the taste of the bread I ate the morning I enlisted, and the name of the girl who kissed me at the depot, and whether or not I had ever been afraid.

I found out later — everyone finds out eventually, in the quiet hours, pressing on the smooth places — that I had volunteered for something before the war too. There's a hollow in me shaped like a door. I've never learned what stood behind it. That's the cruelty of the craft, if you want to know: the Order doesn't just eat your memories. It eats your knowledge of what it ate.

We are called Hollow Men because of what's missing. We should be called something worse, because of what was taken *on purpose*, and by whom, and for whose table.

* * *

Elodie Oyelaran's lodgings were empty when I came for her, which surprised no one — Maro's information was two days old, and thieves keep lighter feet than fences keep promises. But her work remained. She'd left in haste, and haste is generous to inquisitors.

The room was small and ruthlessly ordered, which told me things the dossier hadn't. A woman who lives alone with one chair, one cup, one blanket folded to military corners is a woman who decided long ago that owning things is the same as losing them. On the table: a lockbox, forced open already — she'd taken what mattered — and beside it, dropped or discarded, a single charm.

Now. A word on charms, since you're going to judge her and I'd rather you judged her accurately. A stolen memory, properly worked, becomes a small glass thing you wear against the skin, and when you need what it holds — courage for a funeral, joy for a marriage that frightens you, an hour of being loved by someone now dead — you crack the seal and breathe it in. It's commerce. Ugly commerce, maybe, but consider what Vess sells legally: soldiers' futures, indentured decades, children's labor measured in candle-hours. Elodie Oyelaran sold happiness by the hour, and her customers came back, and in Vess that constitutes a moral advantage.

The charm on her table was warm. That should have been my first warning. Charms cool once their maker stops tending them; this one sat in the cold room radiating heat like a held breath.

Protocol said catalog and seal it. My hands said otherwise. Eleven years of protocol versus one moment of the old hunger — and I want to be honest, since honesty is the one luxury the Order left me: I was hungry. You live long enough as Hollow and you develop appetites that have no object, a thirst with no water attached, and the only thing that ever quiets it is tasting what you used to be.

I cracked the seal and breathed.

* * *

An orchard.

Not the word — the *place*. Sunlight coming down green-gold through leaves so heavy with fruit they bowed like courtiers. The smell of windfalls rotting sweet in the grass, that particular sugar-rot that means late summer and abundance beyond counting. Dust on bare feet. A woman's voice calling a name — a child's name — from somewhere uphill, in a language I did not know and yet understood the way you understand a lullaby, which is to say completely, which is to say it bypassed the ear entirely and went straight into whatever is left of me that the Order didn't get.

And fire.

The memory was double-exposed, the way strong ones sometimes are, stamped twice by feeling. Underneath the orchard ran its ending: smoke, the trees going up like torches, the sweetness of the rot turning acrid, small feet running, the voice calling the name again and again and then not calling it, and the specific silence after a voice stops that is louder than any sound I have catalogued in twenty years of listening to people lose themselves.

I stood in Elodie Oyelaran's empty room with an open charm in my hand, and I wept.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have: I did not remember anything. That's the horror. The tears came from nowhere, from the smooth places, from hollows I didn't know I had — my body responding to someone else's grief the way a struck bell responds, ringing at a frequency it was cast for long ago. Somewhere in the burned foundations of me there was an orchard-shaped cavity. I had never known it existed. I knew it now, the way you know a missing tooth: by the tongue finding the gap.

The child's name. The voice called a child's name, and the memory reached toward it and I reached with it, and there was nothing there. Not forgotten. *Removed.* Someone had drunk that name out of me, and drunk the knowing-of-it, and drunk the drinking-of-it, leaving a hollow shaped exactly like a burning orchard in a country that does not appear on any map.

Ashkaran.

The residue reports had said the word and I had filed the word. Now the word opened in me like a door.

* * *

I sat on Elodie Oyelaran's one chair for a long time. The charm cooled in my palm. Outside, Vess went about its evening — cart wheels, a drunk singing, the bells of the Lawquarter striking the hour that meant honest people were indoors and the Order was awake.

Let me tell you what I understood in that chair, because it arrived all at once, the way floodwater arrives.

The Order sent me because I am reliable. Reliable means smooth — no seams, no textures, nothing left in me that could snag on sentiment. They looked at the hollows in Sef and saw a clean instrument. What they failed to account for is that a hollow is not empty. A hollow is a shape. And shapes can be *matched*.

Elodie Oyelaran had been to the same surgeons. I knew it the way you know a fellow survivor of a shipwreck — not by anything she'd say, but by how she'd hold herself near deep water. No past. One chair. A rule, Maro's file mentioned it, her rule, repeated around the market like scripture: *never steal your own memories.* Everyone thought it was professional ethics.

It was terror. She knew there was nothing there to steal.

And the child — the mud-caked child speaking a murdered tongue, the child who made charms hum, the child the General Office wanted alive and unopened — that child was walking around Vess carrying in its small body everything the rest of us had drunk. I understood that too, sitting there, the way you understand thunder after the lightning: the Order wanted the child not because it was evidence but because it was *contents*. The last undrunk drop, as the old rumors put it, though I'd always taken the rumors for the fever-talk of men whose imaginations had been extracted along with their sins.

Corvane's eyes. Lamps that burn without oil. I had always assumed that was power.

Power is just appetite that won.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic I promised not to do earlier, done now, in the dark of her room, with her grief cooling in my hand.

Twenty years since the Fall. Forty shelves of ledgers. Say each tablet records thirty extractions — a conservative count; the purges after the Fall went through whole districts like threshers. Call it some tens of thousands of hollowed citizens, each one carrying cavities they cannot name, each one walking through Vess pressing their tongues against the gaps. And every one of those gaps is shaped like something. An orchard. A harbor town. A festival of lights on water. A mother's voice calling a name.

Vane didn't conquer a country. He *catered*. He set a table for himself out of an entire nation, and the nation's own neighbors helped clear the plates, and the neighbors' neighbors signed the writs, and men like me — men who had been given mercy as children and never asked what the mercy cost or who paid for it — men like me carried out the dishes.

I am not innocent. I want that on the record too, since I'm apparently keeping one tonight. I have eaten memories by force. I know exactly what force-tasting is like: nothing, ash, a transaction. Whatever tenderness I felt toward Elodie Oyelaran's charm was not absolution. It was just the first time in eleven years I had tasted anything at all.

But there is a difference between a man who burns a library and a man who was handed a torch before he was old enough to ask why, and I intend to spend what's left of me finding out whether that difference matters to anyone besides me.

* * *

The report I filed that night was accurate in every particular except the one that mattered. Thief fled, I wrote. Premises secured. One charm recovered, residue consistent with the samples already logged. Subject's destination unknown.

All true. And between the lines, invisible to anyone who hasn't spent a decade learning to write reports that lie by omission: *I know where she's going. I know what the child is. And I have not decided what I am.*

Corvane will taste the omission eventually. He tastes everything. I have perhaps days — less, if the General Office opens the residue logs and notices which extractor accessed the orchard sample twice.

So. Decisions.

There's a version of me that goes back to the Hall of Ledgers, kneels, confesses the hesitation, and asks to be emptied further. They'd oblige. They'd drink the orchard-hollow right out of me, smooth over the last seam, and I'd walk out of that room unable even to miss what I missed. That is the version of me the Order built, and he is reliable, and he is smooth, and he is very tired.

There's another version. He's standing in a thief's rented room holding a dead woman's — no. Holding a living woman's happiest hour, which she stole from a dying soldier, which she was perhaps planning to return, because that's the kind of thief she turns out to be, God help us both.

The second version needs to find her before the Order does. Which means moving tonight, ahead of the hunt, carrying nothing but a warrant I no longer believe in and a hollow in my chest shaped like home.

I sealed the charm and put it in my coat, next to the heart that supposedly isn't there.

Then I did the one thing no Hollow Man has ever done, as far as the ledgers record, in twenty years of forty shelves:

I left the lamp burning behind me, so they'd think I was still working — and walked out into the dark, hunting the only two people in Vess who might know what I'd lost.
