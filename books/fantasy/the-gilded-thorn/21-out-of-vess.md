## 21. Out of Vess

There are three ways to leave a city that doesn't want you to leave, and I had tried all of them at least once in my career as a person of interest.

The first is the front gate, which is for people with papers, money, and the kind of face that officials believe. I have none of these. The second is the river barge route, where you pay a smuggler to hide you among casks of pickled eel, and I can tell you from experience that pickled eel has a smell that outlasts even a Hollow Man's memory-eating. The third is the sewers, and I would rather be eaten.

So naturally, on the worst morning of my life, all three were closed to me, and we went over the wall.

"You're certain," Ilsa said, for the fourth time, standing in the shadow of the tannery at the base of Vess's eastern rampart. She said it the way mapmakers say things—flatly, like she was checking a coordinate. "You're certain they've reached the gates."

"I'm certain Sef found an Ashkaran charm in my work," I said. "I'm certain Maro sold me. And I'm certain that when the Hollow Men want someone badly enough, they don't come alone. They come with soldiers, and the soldiers belong to—" I stopped myself before the name. Saying Aurel Vane's name aloud in Vess felt like tapping on the glass of something enormous and asleep. "Them. The vanguard. Whatever you want to call the column of men who burned three villages between here and the border while their archives insist those villages never existed."

Ilsa looked up at the wall. It was forty feet of mortared panic, built after the Fall by a government terrified of exactly the thing now walking toward it. There were drainage grates near the top, rusted shut since before I was born. Above them, the parapet walk, and above *that*, the grey morning sky, thick as unwashed wool.

"And the child climbs?" Ilsa asked.

I looked down at Wren, who was crouched at the base of the wall doing what Wren did, which was touching things. Wren's small fingers rested against the stone, and I swear—the light did something. Not much. A shiver, like heat off a road. Like the wall remembered being a cliff, or wanted to.

"Wren," I said, "can you climb?"

Wren looked up at me. Those eyes—I'd stopped trying to describe them to myself, because every description came back wrong. Old. That was the only word that stuck. Eyes like a door left open onto a room you'd lived in once.

Wren nodded once and began climbing the sheer stone the way other children climb stairs.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about fleeing your life: it takes surprisingly long to pack.

I had three years of belongings in that garret above the dye-works, and when the moment came to leave them forever, I took a satchel, two knives, a coil of rope, and—after a full minute of standing in the middle of the room feeling like an idiot—nothing else. My charms stayed in their case on the shelf. Forty little glass vials of other people's happiness, glinting in the early light like a jeweler's idea of a conscience.

"Leave them," Ilsa had said, when I hesitated.

"They're worth more than this building."

"They're also traceable. Every vial has a taste. You told me yourself the inquisitor tasted Ashkaran in one." She'd said it gently, which was worse. Ilsa was not a gentle woman; gentleness from her meant she thought you were about to do something stupid and wanted you to know she'd predicted it.

She was right, of course. She usually was, in the specific infuriating way of people who have already lost everything and therefore find loss very easy to recommend. So I left them, all forty, and the last thing I saw of my home was that shelf of borrowed joy catching the dawn, and I thought: *somebody will steal these, and somebody will buy them, and for a few hours some stranger in Vess will feel happy without knowing why, and maybe that's the closest thing to a legacy I get.*

Then Wren tugged my sleeve and pointed at the floorboards, where the child had chalked a door weeks ago. The chalk mark was gone—scrubbed, rained through, forgotten—but Wren traced its outline in the air anyway, and looked at me, and made the sound. The one word. *Home,* in a language that had no right to exist, in a voice that had never used it for anything but true.

"All right," I told the child. "All right. We're going."

* * *

The climb was easier than it should have been, and I mean that in the way that frightened me most.

Stone does not cooperate. Stone especially does not cooperate with a thief carrying a child on her back and a disgraced cartographer wheezing behind her. And yet handholds appeared where I remembered none—cracks widened, mortar crumbled just ahead of my grip, and twice I put my weight on ledges that I could have sworn were not there, and held.

Wren rode my back with arms around my neck, humming. Not a tune I knew. A tune I *almost* knew, the way you almost know a word on the tip of your tongue, and every time the humming touched the stone beneath us, the stone listened.

"Don't," I whispered into the child's ear, halfway up. "Please. Small. Stay small. If anyone sees—"

Below us, faint across the rooftops, came the bells.

Not the alarm bells. The *other* bells—the ones Vess rang only twice in my lifetime. Once for the death of a Lord-Protector, and once for nothing anyone could name, a ringing that lasted six hours and that the archives later recorded as having never happened.

This was that second kind. A wrongness bell. The city itself, confused about what it was hearing.

I hauled myself onto the parapet and looked east, past the tanneries and the corpse-fields and the salt marshes, to the causeway road that ran from Vess to the rest of the world.

Dust. A column of it, wide as a river, moving toward the gates.

And at its head, riding under no banner at all—which was worse than any banner, because banners belong to countries and this belonged to a man—rode the vanguard. Maybe four hundred lances. Maybe more, hidden in the dust. They moved with the terrible unhurried confidence of an army that has been told, by men whose job is knowing things, that there is no army coming, and therefore nothing at this gate needs defending except a thief, a mute child, and a woman who draws maps of nowhere.

"They're a day out," Ilsa panted, arriving beside me. "Maybe less. Cavalry moves faster than—"

"That's not cavalry for the siege. Look at the order of march. That's a spearhead. That's a force built to take one gate fast and hold it while the real weight comes up behind." I had grown up around soldiers' memories; I knew the shape of a column the way Maro knew the shape of a ledger. "They're not here to conquer Vess. Conquering comes later, after the archives finish explaining that Vess surrendered peacefully generations ago. This is here for *us.*"

"For you."

"For what I'm carrying." I shifted Wren higher on my back. The child's chin dug into my shoulder, warm and heavy as a sleeping coal. "Which means every gate, every barge, every sewer grate in this city belongs to them by nightfall. Which means we're leaving now, over the wall, into marshland, with whatever we can carry. Any objections?"

"Several," Ilsa said. "None actionable."

That, I decided, was the most honest sentence spoken in Vess that day.

* * *

The descent nearly killed us, because the wall cooperated going up and betrayed us going down, and I have a theory about that which I don't like. I think Wren understood climbing. Walls, doors, thresholds—those the child understood in the bones, in whatever passed for bones in something that used to be a whole country's soul. But descending, leaving, *exiting*—the child fought it, quietly, the way a plant fights frost, and the stones stiffened under our hands until Ilsa slipped and I caught her wrist and we hung there for ten horrible seconds above thirty feet of tannery yard, three women and a secret, dangling.

Then Wren, pressed against my back, made a small frustrated sound, and the stones relented all at once, like a fist unclenching, and we dropped the last eight feet into the mud and lay there breathing.

"Never again," Ilsa announced to the sky.

"We'll do it again in an hour. The marsh path forks at the drowned chapel, and if we take the wrong fork we're walking into the fen blind."

"The *fen.*" Ilsa sat up. Mud slid off her shoulders. Something crossed her face—not fear exactly. Recognition. "Elodie. The Salt Fen. You know why the route goes through it."

"Because it's empty and nobody guards nothing."

"Because it isn't empty. Because it's the one place in the world that was never properly emptied." She wiped her hands on her coat and failed entirely. "Twenty years ago something happened there that the archives can't describe because describing it was the first casualty. I charted the coast afterward, do you understand? I sailed down it. There is a stretch of shoreline sixty miles long where the water is wrong and the charts go blank and my own hand refused to write what I saw. They stripped my license for it. Called it fabrication." She laughed, short and awful. "It wasn't fabrication. It was the first honest map ever confiscated in Vess."

I looked at her. I looked at the child, who was watching the eastern wall with an expression I had learned to read as patience, which is a strange expression for a child and a familiar one for a kingdom.

"And the Door," I said. "The Last Door. It's really there. In the fen."

"I don't know what's there. Nobody knows what's there. That's rather the point." Ilsa stood, joints cracking like green wood. "But I know where the blank space is on every map in the world, and I know that your child hums at walls, and I know that when I look at that fen on a chart, I feel homesick. I haven't lived anywhere in fifteen years. I shouldn't feel homesick for a place that isn't on the map." She looked at me, and her eyes were wet, and she seemed furious about it. "Homesickness for nowhere. That's what's left of a country, girl. That's what he couldn't drink. That's what's calling us east."

* * *

We crossed the corpse-fields at a dead run, because there was no elegant way to do it and because the mud slowed everything equally, which I suppose is a kind of justice.

Behind us, the bells kept ringing their wrong note. Ahead of us, the marsh opened up in its grey-green miles, stitched with reeds and the skeletons of boats that had been dragged ashore to die. Somewhere north, a heron lifted off, unbothered, the last creature in the world having a normal morning.

Halfway across, Wren started humming again, louder, and I felt it in my teeth—that almost-tune, that shape of a song with the middle missing—and I noticed something I should have noticed weeks ago. The reeds leaned toward the child. Just slightly. Just the way grass leans toward sun, or the way a crowd leans toward a stage.

"Wren." I stopped. Ilsa stumbled past, then doubled back. "Look at me. We need to talk about being quiet."

Wren looked at me. The humming stopped. The marsh went so silent I could hear my own heart filing complaints.

"When we get to the fen—if we get to the fen—you're going to do something big. Aren't you. Something I can't help with and Ilsa can't draw and no one can stop." The child's eyes didn't change, which was answer enough. "And the men behind us are coming because they can feel it too. Vane can feel it. Every mile we run east, we're dragging his attention behind us like a torn sack."

I crouched. I took the small cold hands in mine. Mud, chilblains, a scar on one palm shaped like nothing yet—like a key waiting to decide what it unlocked.

"I need you to know that I'm not taking you somewhere," I said. "Whatever happens at that door, whatever it costs—it's yours to choose. Do you hear me? I've stolen enough in my life. I'm not stealing a country's ending just because it happens to be holding my hand."

For a moment Wren's face did something old and vast and unbearably sad, and then it folded back into a child's face, and the child squeezed my fingers—once, hard, deliberate—and pointed east.

Toward the fen. Toward the blank space on every map. Toward home.

"Yeah," I said, and my voice cracked like a boy's, and I let it. "Yeah. Let's go home."

* * *

We reached the drowned chapel as the sun finally tore through the wool of the clouds, and that was when I heard the horns behind us.

One note. Then another, answering. The vanguard had reached the gates of Vess—or close enough to announce itself—and the sound carried flat across the marsh like a blade laid on a table. Behind me, faint with distance, I could see the smoke starting to rise from the eastern quarter. Not burning yet. Just cook-smoke, camp-smoke, the domestic noise of an army settling in for a stay.

They weren't chasing us. That was the detail that turned my blood to fen-water. They hadn't ridden us down or sent riders flanking along the causeway. They'd simply arrived, and sat down, and begun to exist between us and every road home, patient as arithmetic. Because they knew something I was only now letting myself know:

There was no coming back. Whatever happened in the Salt Fen, the woman running east across the marsh with a stolen childhood and a borrowed compass-heading was not going to be the same woman who could have walked back into Vess and haggled over charms in the night market. The city was closing behind me like water over a dropped coin.

"Elodie," Ilsa called. She'd climbed onto the chapel's half-sunk roof and stood there with her satchel of maps and instruments, silhouetted against the light, looking less like a disgraced mapmaker than like a signpost someone had raised at the edge of the world. "The fork. Left branch follows the old sea-wall. Right branch—" She paused. I watched her check a chart, watch her hands hesitate over the blank space, watch her make the choice anyway. "Right branch goes off the maps. Completely. Past the last thing anyone bothered to record."

I looked back once at Vess—its towers, its bells, its forty vials of borrowed joy sitting on a shelf for whoever stole next—and then at Wren, small and mud-caked and humming, pointing east into the blankness with the certainty of a soul that had been pointing east for twenty years.

I took the right fork.

Behind us, very far away now, the bells changed their note—from wrongness to something almost like a warning, or almost like a farewell, depending on what you believed cities could feel—and the marsh closed around the three of us, and the maps gave out, and we walked on.

The last thing I heard from the world of recorded things was a horn, blown long and low, like a man clearing his throat to say a name he'd been told didn't exist.

Then the reeds took even that.
