## 22. Road Through the Burnt Marches

The Burnt Marches earned their name honestly, which is more than you can say for most things in this country.

Three days out of Vess and my boots had already surrendered. They'd been good boots once—stolen from a merchant who never noticed their absence because I also stole his memory of owning them—but the road between Vess and the free cities is a conspiracy of flint and mud that forgives nothing. Wren walked beside me without complaint, which was somehow worse than complaining. A child who doesn't complain on a forced march has either made peace with suffering or expects something worse to come, and I didn't want to know which.

Ilsa limped ahead with her map case strapped across her back like a shield. She'd redrawn our route twice since the gates, muttering about roads that weren't where her old charts said they should be, or were where no chart said anything at all. The world, it seemed, was still a little embarrassed about Ashkar, and kept shifting its furniture when nobody looked.

"Water," she said, which was her way of saying *rest*, which was her way of saying *I'm sixty years old and if we don't stop I will lie down in this ditch and become a landmark*.

We stopped. There was a stream, or what passed for one—the Burnt Marches water ran grey with ash that had been falling, I realized slowly, for twenty years. Nobody had thought to mention it. Ashkar burned, and the ash went somewhere, and somewhere was here, sifting down season after season onto land that had forgotten why it tasted of cinders.

Wren knelt at the stream's edge and cupped water in both hands but didn't drink. Just watched it. The way the child watched things had started to unnerve me—not the watching itself, but the quality of attention, as though Wren were listening to the water rather than looking at it.

"Don't drink that," I said, and then felt like an idiot, because we'd been drinking it for three days and there wasn't exactly an alternative. "I mean. Boil it first."

Ilsa snorted. "With what? Our last fire cost us half a day and a barn we had to leave in a hurry."

She wasn't wrong. We'd slept the first night in a farmer's hayloft, paid for with a charm—a memory of a wedding, borrowed sweetness—and left before dawn when the farmer's wife stood in the yard staring east with an expression I couldn't read and couldn't shake.

That expression was the thing about this journey I hadn't prepared for. I'd expected soldiers, checkpoints, hunger. I hadn't expected the faces.

* * *

You could tell the refugees by how they walked. Not the lame ones, not the ones carrying everything they owned in bundles on their backs—you could tell them by the *hesitation*. Every mile or so, one of them would stop mid-stride, turn around, and look back the way they'd come with an expression like a man who's left his house and can't remember whether he locked the door. Except it wasn't the door they were trying to remember. It was the house. And there was no house. There was just the flat grey horizon and the ash-sifted fields and whatever nameless thing had gotten into their feet and told them to run.

An old man shared our fire the second night—or rather, he sat near our fire, because none of us had anything worth sharing and everyone knew it. He had a cart with one wheel and a goat that had given up on dignity entirely.

"Fleeing the war?" I asked him, because that was what you asked.

"What war?" He said it genuinely puzzled, not defensive. "There's no war. My son's a clerk in Vess. He writes me every month." He patted his coat pocket, where letters presumably lived. "But I woke up Tuesday and I couldn't stay. Couldn't tell you why. Felt like—" He worked his jaw. "You know when you dream of a place? Someplace warm. You wake up and it's gone, and the whole day tastes wrong? Like that. Been tasting wrong for twenty years, I suppose. Just Tuesday it got loud."

"And your wife?"

He looked at me like I'd asked him something obscene. "Never married."

"You said—"

"I know what I said." He frowned at his own hands. "Funny thing. Feels like I'm missing somebody. Twenty years of feeling like I'm missing somebody, and there's nobody to miss." He laughed, a dry sound with no floor under it. "Doctor in Vess called it melancholia. Charged me two crowns to call it that."

I gave him a charm. Not one of my good ones—one of the dregs at the bottom of my satchel, a memory of a summer rainstorm over a wheat field, secondhand and slightly frayed. He held it against his palm and his face did something complicated: relief and grief arriving at the same door, each blaming the other.

"That's it," he whispered. "That's the taste. That's what's been missing." Then, sharper, almost angry: "What *is* that? Where's that field?"

"Wheat country," I said. "South, maybe. Doesn't matter."

"It matters." He closed his fist around the charm. "It matters and I can't say why, and that's the worst part, girl. Grieving something you can't name. It's like hunger with no mouth."

I didn't sleep much after that.

* * *

By the fourth day the road had thickened with them. Families, mostly, walking east toward the free cities with the particular grim patience of people who have decided that motion is better than standing still even if motion has no destination. And all of them, every single one, carried that same look—the turned head, the backward glance, the hunger with no mouth.

A woman with twin boys on her hip told me she'd left her village because the well water had started tasting of smoke. Another man said birds wouldn't land in his fields anymore. A girl of maybe fifteen, walking alone with a shawl over her head like a widow, said simply: "I keep dreaming of a door." She couldn't describe it. She'd tried. Her hands sketched shapes in the air and failed, over and over, like a tongue searching for a bad tooth.

Wren walked among them and they parted for the child without knowing they were doing it. I watched a family shift aside on the narrow road, making room, and then glance at each other wondering why they'd done it, why their chests had gone tight, why the smaller of the two boys had started crying and couldn't stop.

Because that was the other thing. People cried on that road constantly, and nobody knew why. You'd pass a grown soldier sitting on a milestone with his helmet in his lap, weeping silently, and he'd wave you past with an embarrassed gesture: *nothing to see, no wound, no reason, move along.* Collective grief with the grief surgically removed, leaving only the shape of it, the way a burn scar holds the shape of the flame.

"They feel it," Ilsa said quietly, beside me. "Twenty years ago, when it happened. Everyone in this country felt something tear, and no one could find the wound, so they all decided they were imagining it." She adjusted her map case. "They're not imagining it. They're just not allowed to know what it is."

"Ashkar," I said. The word still felt strange in my mouth, like a key that fit a lock I couldn't see.

"Ashkar," she agreed. "And every one of these people lost something in the Fall, whether they ever saw its shores or not. You don't have to remember a country to mourn it. Mourning's older than memory. Ask any dog whose owner died before it was born."

"That's not comforting, Ilsa."

"I'm not a comforting woman. I'm a mapmaker. I draw the world as it is, and lately the world as it is has been giving me hives."

* * *

We crossed the river at a ferry crossing that charged triple because of the crowds, and on the far bank the character of the country changed. Greener. The ash thinned. But the people didn't change—they got *worse*, if anything, because here the villages were fuller, and fullness meant more turned heads, more backward glances, more doors opening at dusk as families stepped out to stare east at nothing.

In one village, a whole street of them stood outside their houses in the rain, facing east, saying nothing. I stopped to ask a woman what they were waiting for and she looked at me with genuine confusion.

"We're not waiting for anything," she said. "We just came out."

"Why?"

"I don't know. Did you hear something?" And then, before I could answer, her face crumpled and she pressed her hand over her mouth and said, muffled: "Oh, gods. Why am I crying? I'm sorry. I never cry. Please don't look at me."

Wren took her hand. Just walked up and took it, small fingers lacing through hers, and the woman's sobbing slowed, steadied, stopped. She looked down at the child with an expression I recognized because I'd worn it myself, that first night at my door: dread and tenderness fighting for the same chair.

"Yours?" she asked me.

"Yes," I heard myself say, and discovered it wasn't entirely a lie anymore, which was a problem for another day.

"Keep them close," the woman said. "Whatever's coming. Keep them close." She didn't know what was coming. Nobody knew what was coming. The archives said nothing was coming; the archives said the eastern marches had been empty grazing land since time immemorial. But her body knew, the way a body knows a storm before the clouds arrive, some pressure behind the ears of the soul.

Vane's strategy, I was beginning to understand, was beautiful in the way that terrible things are beautiful. He hadn't just erased a country. He'd erased the *evidence of erasure*. The free cities would sit in their councils reading archives that said all was well, while their citizens wept in the streets for reasons no physician could name. You cannot rally a nation against a threat that officially does not exist. You cannot even *fear* properly. Fear needs a name. These people had only the ache.

* * *

The fifth night we sheltered in a ruined watchtower—Ilsa swore it appeared on no map she'd ever drawn, which by now felt less like an error and more like a message—and I counted our supplies by touch in the dark. Half the charms gone, spent on passage and bread and silence. Three vials of my own recovered memories, wrapped separately, which I touched less often than I told myself I wouldn't touch them at all.

The orchard vial was the worst. I'd taken it out once, on the second night, just to hold it, and the glass had been warm. Memory-charms go cold when they're empty. This one was warm as a sleeping bird, and I'd put it away fast, heart hammering, because warmth meant it wanted something. Warmth meant it remembered being mine.

Wren appeared beside me in the dark. The child had a talent for appearing—no footsteps, no warning, just presence, like weather changing.

"Sleep," I said.

Wren shook their head and pointed east, through the broken window of the tower, at the horizon. I looked. Nothing. Grey dark, starless, the usual.

Then, faintly, so faintly I might have invented it: a glow. Not fire-glow. Something steadier, warmer, like lamplight behind a very distant curtain. It pulsed once, twice, and was gone.

"What is that?" I whispered.

Wren looked up at me, and in the dark the child's eyes caught light that wasn't there, and for a moment—not the first time, but the strongest yet—I felt the vertigo of recognition, the sense that I was looking at something I'd known once, long ago, in a life I'd sold off in pieces. Wren's hand found mine and pressed my palm, right over the scar shaped like a door-key, and the scar *ached*, deep and sweet, like a bruise remembering its birth.

"Hungry," Wren said. One of the child's few words, and always the same one, and I'd assumed it meant food.

Watching that horizon, holding that aching hand, I understood it meant something else entirely. Everything else. The child was hungry the way the old man with the goat was hungry, the way the crying woman was hungry, the way the whole road of turned heads and backward glances was hungry. Homesick for nowhere. Starving for a country that had been eaten.

And getting closer to it every step we walked.

Below us, on the road we'd left at dusk, torchlight moved. A rider, traveling fast and alone, heading east along the same line we followed. I watched until the torch vanished, and told myself it meant nothing. Roads run east; travelers ride them.

But I'd spent six years reading people the way Ilsa read maps, and something about the set of that torch—the purposeful bob of it, the pace that neither hurried nor wandered—put a cold finger on the back of my neck.

Someone was following the same hunger we were. Or following *us*.

I didn't sleep. I sat with Wren's hand in mine and the orchard vial warm against my ribs, and watched the east, and waited for morning to tell me which kind of trouble had found our trail.
