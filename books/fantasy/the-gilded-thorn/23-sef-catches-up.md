## 23. Sef Catches Up

I have killed eleven practitioners. I can recite their names because the Order requires me to, carved into a slate I carry against my chest like a second heart. Eleven names. I used to think the list was a burden. Lately I suspect it is a leash.

The twelfth name was supposed to be Elodie Oyelaran.

I followed her out of Vess by scent, which is not a thing I could explain to my superiors without ending up on someone's list myself. Memory leaves residue — the Order taught us that much truth wrapped in enough lies to make it serviceable. A stolen joy smells like rain on hot stone. A stolen grief smells like iron. The woman ahead of me on the burnt road smelled of both, and of something else besides, something the Order had no word for and therefore pretended did not exist.

It smelled like an orchard in September.

* * *

Three days out of Vess, I stopped pretending to myself that this was pursuit.

Pursuit has a shape. You close distance. You plan the kill. You rehearse the report you will file, the clean bureaucratic language that turns a person into an incident. *Subject resisted. Subject was erased. Order restored.* I had done all of that. I had camped within sight of their fire two nights running and watched the child sleep with her mouth open and the mapmaker talk in her dreams about contour lines, and I had rehearsed nothing at all.

Here is what nobody tells you about being a Hollow Man: we are called hollow because we eat memory, but the eating goes both directions. Every memory I consume to feed the Order's ledger takes a bite out of me too. Not the eaten one — mine. My own past thins like broth stretched over too many bowls. I am thirty-four years old by the count of my teeth, and I remember perhaps nine years' worth of living, and most of those are training.

I know how to kill a man six ways before breakfast. I do not know my mother's face. I do not know if I had a mother. There is a scar on my knee shaped like a crescent moon and no memory attached to it, just the scar, sitting there like a sentence missing its verb.

Somewhere in the ledgers of the Order there may be a vial with the rest of me in it. Or there may not be. That is the particular cruelty of our trade: we cannot even be sure what was taken.

So when I say I caught up with Elodie Oyelaran on the third morning, understand that she caught up with me first. She had been leaving signs for days — small ones, deliberate ones, charms hung on branches at my eye level, each one holding a scrap of some stranger's happiness. A wedding dance. A dog retrieving a stick. A boy's first taste of honey off his own fingers. She was feeding me, the way you'd feed a strange dog, testing whether it bites.

I ate every one. I told myself it was evidence collection.

* * *

She was waiting for me at the ford of a dead river, standing in the cracked grey bed of it with the child behind her and the old mapmaker leaning on her walking staff like a woman who intended to watch whatever happened next with great professional interest.

"You've been following us since Vess," Elodie said.

"Since before Vess," I said. "Since the market."

"And you haven't reported."

"I report everything." This was true. It was also true that my reports had grown vague as scripture. *Subject travels west. Subjects appear unremarkable.* Somewhere in the Order's archive my reports were getting thinner and thinner, until eventually they would read like a man describing a dream he was trying to forget.

"You're supposed to erase us," she said. "That's the job. Hollow Men don't chat at riverbeds."

"No," I agreed. "They don't."

The child stepped out from behind her. Wren. The Order had a file on the child three lines long, which for the Order was practically a biography. *Unregistered practitioner-adjacent. Linguistic anomaly. Priority.* Three lines, and the last line underlined twice, and I had carried that file across half a country wondering why my hands shook whenever I touched the paper.

Wren looked at me the way no one looks at a Hollow Man — without fear. With recognition, almost. Like looking at a locked door and knowing what's behind it.

Then the child said a word.

I won't write it here. I can't; the sound doesn't survive translation any more than a smell survives a description. But it went through me like cold water through a hot stone, and somewhere in the thin broth of my memory something rose to the surface and broke open, and I was — briefly, unbearably — five years old, standing in an orchard, holding someone's hand, and the trees were heavy with fruit and the light came down gold through the leaves and someone was singing, and I knew — I knew the way you know your own name — that this was home.

Then it was gone. Of course it was gone. That's the whole trick of us.

But I was on my knees in a dry riverbed, and the slate with eleven names had fallen from my chest, and Elodie Oyelaran had not run. She crouched down in front of me instead, at arm's length, wary as you'd be near a wounded animal, and asked:

"What did you see?"

"Nothing," I said. "Nothing. There's nothing to see. That's what we are."

"That," she said, "was the most frightened 'nothing' I've ever heard."

* * *

I should have killed them at the ford. Write that down somewhere. Inquisitor Sef had them at his mercy at the ford of the dead river and chose instead to sit on a stone like an old man and answer questions, which is the beginning of every traitor's story, and I tell it now so that whoever reads the record understands I knew exactly what I was doing.

She asked me about the orchard. I said I didn't remember an orchard. She asked me why I was crying, and I discovered that I was, and that discovery frightened me more than anything the Order had ever shown me in the black rooms beneath Vess.

"I ate a charm," I said finally. "On the road. One of yours. Honey. A boy tasting honey." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Do you know what I tasted underneath the honey? Ashkar. Residue. The market charm was contaminated — that's why I was sent after you. And when the child spoke just now, the contamination — opened. For a moment I—"

"Home," Elodie said quietly. "You saw home."

"I don't have a home. Nobody has a home. Ashkar doesn't exist. I've read the histories. There was never any such country. There was a border dispute, some rebellion, the usual—"

"There was an orchard," Elodie said. "There were festivals. There was a language. There was a door." Her voice did something complicated on the word *door*. "You've been drinking other people's memories for years, Inquisitor. Has it never occurred to you to wonder where your own went?"

"The Order doesn't take from its own."

"The Order," said the mapmaker, Ilsa, from her stone, with the dry precision of a woman reading coordinates aloud, "bottles its recruits' childhoods at induction. Standard practice since the founding. It keeps you obedient. A man with no past will follow any order, because orders are the only story he has." She looked at me over her spectacles. "You didn't know?"

I did not answer. There was nothing to answer with. Nine years of memories, and not one of them mine, and all this time I had believed the thinness of my life was my own failing — that I had simply been born shallow, born hollow, born less. That the Order had seen the emptiness in me and given it a purpose.

They hadn't seen it. They'd made it. And they'd put the rest of me in a bottle in a vault somewhere beneath Vess, filed between a heretic's confession and a tax ledger, and I had spent fifteen years guarding the vault door from the outside.

"Do you want to know the worst part?" I heard myself say. "I volunteered. I remember volunteering. I remember thinking it was an honor." My hands were shaking. "Why would anyone volunteer for that? What was I running from that being empty seemed like the better offer?"

Nobody answered. The wind moved down the dead river, carrying dust that might once have been a riverbank, or a road, or a kingdom, depending on which version of the world you'd been sold.

Wren sat down beside me on the stone. Just sat down, the way children do, shoulder almost touching mine, and hummed something — three notes, four, a fragment of a melody with no beginning and no end — and my chest hurt in a place I didn't have a name for.

"All right," I said, to the river, to the child, to whatever was left of the man who'd walked into that riverbed carrying eleven names. "All right. Ask me what you're going to ask me."

Elodie crouched again, level with my eyes.

"What was Ashkar?" she said. "Before it was unwritten. Tell me everything you felt at the ford. Every scrap. Don't polish it. Don't make it useful. Just tell me true."

And because I had nothing left to lose but the emptiness itself, I told her.

* * *

I told her about gold light through leaves. About a song whose words I couldn't hold but whose sadness I could, the specific sadness of a festival's last night. About hands — someone's hands, broad, scarred at the knuckle, holding mine. I told her the air had tasted like ripe fruit and woodsmoke and the end of summer, and that when the vision broke I had felt more bereaved than I had ever felt killing, and that I did not know who or what I was bereaved of, only that the loss was older than my service, older than my name, older than the slate.

When I finished, none of them spoke. The child leaned her head against my arm, briefly, like a bird settling, and then moved away, and I let myself believe it meant what I wanted it to mean.

"So it's true," I said. "Ashkar was real."

"Ashkar is real," Elodie said. "It's just been made unavailable." She stood, brushed dust from her knees, and studied me with the appraising look of a woman doing arithmetic. "The Order sent twelve of you after us. Twelve?"

"Eleven still functional."

"And the others — will they hesitate like you did?"

"No." I thought of my colleagues, my brothers-in-emptiness, and could not lie. "No. They'll kill you at the first opportunity. I'm the flaw in their design, not the pattern."

"Then you know what happens next," she said. "You go back, or you come with us, or you die here on this riverbed, and honestly the last one's the kindest option available."

"Seductive," I said. "But I'd like to see the Door first."

Something flickered across her face — surprise, suspicion, and under both, hope, which is the most dangerous currency in any country and the only one I've ever been rich in.

"Why?" she asked.

Because I wanted to stand in front of the last true thing left in a world of rewritten paper. Because I wanted to see the face of whatever had been done to me, done to all of us, and watch it open. Because nine years of borrowed life had taught me exactly one lesson worth keeping: that emptiness recognized emptiness, and that two hollow things knocked together made a sound like a bell.

What I said was: "You have a vial problem, a route problem, and an army problem. I'm a solution to one of those. Possibly two."

"She's taking the murderer," Ilsa observed, to no one.

"He hesitated," Elodie said. "In twenty years, no Hollow Man has ever hesitated." She looked at me a moment longer, weighing something heavier than trust — the thing you use instead when trust isn't available. "Sef. That's your name? Your real one, not your designation?"

"It's on the slate. It's all I have."

"Then walk behind us," she said, "where I can see you. And Sef — if the Order catches us, and you get the chance to choose again—"

"I'll hesitate," I said. "It's becoming a habit."

* * *

We walked west that afternoon, four people and a ghost of a country, down the spine of the burnt marches. Behind us, far back on the road, I could feel them coming — my colleagues, my eleven brothers, moving with the patient certainty of men who have never once wondered what was taken from them.

I did not tell Elodie how fast they travel. There would be time. There is always less time than there is.

But that night, when the others slept, I took out the slate and looked a long while at the eleven names, and then, beneath them, in the space reserved for the twelfth, I wrote my own.

---

Elodie

Here is a joke I did not expect to live long enough to appreciate: the safest place to hide from a wolf is inside the wolf.

I kept him behind us on the road, exactly where I could see him, which is also exactly where he could see us, and I spent the whole afternoon deciding whether that was strategy or stupidity. With Maro, betrayal had worn a friendly face for twenty years, which meant I'd learned to check everyone's face for the knife. Sef didn't bother hiding his. He wore his knife openly, on his hip and in his history, eleven names deep. There was something almost restful about it. You always know where you stand with a man who tells you he's killed people. It's the ones who say *I'd never hurt you* that you have to watch.

Still. I checked his face anyway. Old habits keep you breathing.

He walked behind us with the slate against his chest and the child drifting nearer to him by degrees, the way water finds a crack. By evening Wren was walking beside him instead of behind him, matching his stride, and Sef had the expression of a man discovering a room in his own house he'd never known was there.

"Don't," I murmured to him, when Wren took his hand to cross a gully.

"Don't what?"

"Don't get attached. Things that get attached to Wren tend to—" I gestured vaguely at the horizon, where the burnt marches smoldered faintly even at dusk, like a country trying to remember fire. "Things happen. Doors happen. Sacrifices happen."

"Is that what happened to you?" he asked. Too perceptive by half, this one. Hollow Men should come with a warning label. *Contents may see straight through you.*

"What happened to me," I said, "is that I'm going to give everything back."

He was quiet for a dozen steps. Then: "Give what back? To whom?"

"To a door, Sef. To a country. To a child who used to be a kingdom and got folded up small enough to fit through the cracks of the world." I laughed, because if I didn't I'd cry, and crying in front of an inquisitor seemed like poor operational security. "You wanted to know what Ashkar was. Ask me again in a week. The answer's going to cost me, but I'll have it."

"And you're sure it's worth it?" He said it carefully, not mocking — genuinely asking, the way a starving man asks whether bread tastes good. "Worth more than yourself?"

I thought about the orchard. I thought about the vials still waiting somewhere in Maro's vault, my mother's laugh, the promise made at a door, all the pieces of a girl named Oyelaran that I'd been carrying around the edges of for three years like a dress that almost fits. I thought about Wren's hand in mine at the threshold that was still two hundred miles away, and about how the closer we got, the lighter I felt, and how frightening it is to feel light when you know the lightness is you being spent.

"Yes," I said. "Ask me again at the Fen, though. I reserve the right to be a coward at the last minute."

"Noted," Sef said, and for the first time since I'd met him, he smiled. It was a terrible smile, crooked and rusted, clearly out of practice. "For the record, thief — I've eaten eleven people's happiest hours, and not one of them was about being brave. They were all about being ordinary. Suppers. Weddings. Dogs." He looked west, toward the Fen neither of us could see yet. "Whatever's at the end of that road, I find I'd like my hour to be this one. Walking behind a thief and a ghost, going to commit the largest act of treason in recorded history."

"That's very sentimental for a Hollow Man."

"I'm full of honey," he said gravely. "A boy tasting it off his own fingers. It's ruined me."

* * *

We made camp in the lee of a fallen watchtower that Ilsa insisted, tapping her fading map, had once marked the Ashkaran border. The stones still bore a carving — worn nearly smooth, but Wren knelt at it in the dark and traced it with one finger, humming the festival-song, and where the child's finger passed the grooves darkened, sharpened, remembered themselves. Letters. I couldn't read them. Neither, I think, could Wren anymore. But the stone could.

Behind us, south, the horizon pulsed once, softly, like a heartbeat. Army-fires, miles of them, or something worse. Sef stood watch and didn't pretend otherwise.

"They'll reach the Fen two days behind us," he said. "Maybe less. Vane himself travels faster than his army — he doesn't sleep, or doesn't need to. If he senses the Door stirring..." He didn't finish. He didn't have to. I'd felt it too, for days now: a pulling sensation behind my sternum, like the world inhaling.

"Then we walk faster," I said.

I lay down beside Wren in the shadow of the tower, on ground that had belonged to a country for twenty years by belonging to no one, and just before sleep took me, the child pressed something into my palm and closed my fingers over it.

I opened my hand. A pebble. Grey, ordinary, river-smooth — except that in the starlight I could see, faint as breath on glass, the shape of a key burned into its skin.

Below us, deep under the earth, something turned over in its sleep and began, very slowly, to wake.
