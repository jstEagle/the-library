## 24. The Orchard Again

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that only thieves and grieving people understand, and by the time we made camp in the hollow beneath the burnt aqueduct, I had become fluent in both dialects.

The vials sat in my pack like a nest of sleeping wasps. Nine of them. Nine small glass promises that Maro had kept locked away for three years behind wards and lies and the particular silence of a man who knows exactly what he's holding. I'd taken them from his vault along with my own name, more or less, and now they rattled against each other every time I walked, a sound like tiny teeth.

Sef had gone to scout the road east. Ilsa was asleep sitting up, which is a skill mapmakers develop and never lose, her unfinished map spread across her knees even in dreams. And Wren sat beside me in the dark with the patience of someone who had been waiting twenty years and could wait twenty more.

"Right," I said, to no one in particular, because saying things out loud makes them less like hallucinations. "Let's see what I paid for."

Here is what nobody tells you about stolen memory: it doesn't come free when you take it back. Every charm in my coat was somebody's joy — a baker's pride in his first loaf, a bride's whole afternoon of being loved, a fisherman's boyhood summer rendered down to a thumb-sized warmth you could press against your heart and *feel*. That was my trade. That was how I ate. And the Door, or whatever passed for its lock-keeper in the architecture of impossible things, wanted payment in exactly that coin. True memory, freely given. It didn't care whose.

So each vial I opened would burn through a charm's worth of someone else's happiness to light the way into mine. I had counted my charms twice, then a third time hoping the number would change out of embarrassment. It hadn't. Nine vials. Eleven charms. Two spare joys, if nothing went wrong.

Nothing ever goes wrong. That's practically Vess's city motto.

* * *

I started with the smallest one, because I am a coward about the important things and always have been.

This vial was pale green, the color of early apples, and it hummed against my palm the way Wren's voice made my charms hum — except this was mine, tuned to me, and the recognition went through me like a needle finding an old scar. I took one of the charms out — the fisherman boy's summer, warm as bathwater — held both in my cupped hands, and did the thing Ilsa had described, the thing the rubbing on Maro's floor had hinted at: I let them touch. Memory against memory. Payment against debt.

The charm went first. Not violently — it didn't shatter or scream. It just *opened*, the way a window opens, and the summer poured out of it and caught fire in the air between my hands, blue-white and brief, a whole childhood's worth of sunlight spent in four seconds. The fisherman's boy would wake tomorrow and feel a small smooth place where that summer used to be, and he wouldn't know what was missing, only that some mornings the sea looked gray for no reason.

I'm sorry, I thought. I'll say it properly someday, if the world survives long enough for apologies to matter again.

And then the green vial bloomed.

A kitchen. Not grand — low ceiling, smoke-blackened beams, a table scarred with knife-marks and candle-wax. A woman stood at the hearth with her back to me, and she was singing, and the song was in Ashkaran, and I understood every word without understanding how, the way you understand your own heartbeat.

*Little thief,* she sang, *little light, steal the stars but come home by night—*

She turned around.

I don't remember deciding to fall apart. It happened the way a wall happens to a boat — suddenly, totally, with water everywhere. Because it wasn't just any woman. It was the face I saw in polished bronze and still water and the rare honest moments when my reflection forgot to be a stranger. Older than I remembered, because I had been remembering a child's version, but the same. The same crooked smile. The same eyebrow that went up on one side when she was amused, which was apparently where I'd gotten mine, which meant I'd been carrying her around on my own face for twenty years like a letter I couldn't read.

"Mama," I said. My mouth knew the word in Ashkaran before my mind knew it in Common. "*Mamai.*"

She couldn't see me, of course. Memory is not a visitation; it's a haunting that runs on schedule. She wiped her hands on her apron and called toward the garden door — *"Elodie! Wash your feet before you track the orchard in!"* — and there it was, my name, in her voice, with the accent I'd lost and never known I'd lost, the vowels rounded like river stones.

Then the memory folded itself up, corner to corner, and was gone, and I was sitting in a burnt hollow under an aqueduct with wet cheeks and empty hands and one fewer vial.

Wren's hand found mine in the dark. Small. Certain. The child squeezed once, the way you squeeze someone to say *I'm here* without spending words, because words were still mostly a country Wren visited rather than lived in.

"She sang," I managed. "About me. About — stealing stars." A laugh came out of me sideways, cracked down the middle. "I've been a thief since before I could walk. It's *hereditary.*"

Wren leaned against my shoulder. Together we watched the last blue sparks of the fisherman boy's summer drift up through the broken arches and go out among the stars, which, I noted bitterly, had not cost anyone anything and were therefore showing off.

Eight vials left. Ten charms.

I reached for the next one before I could talk myself into rationing my own past like bread in a siege.

* * *

The second vial was amber and loud — I could feel it buzzing before I even touched a charm to it, like a jar with something festive trapped inside. This one cost me the baker's loaf, that simple fierce pride, and I watched it burn gold and thought, absurdly, *he really did make a beautiful loaf, I hope someone tells him so.*

Then: drums.

A town square strung with lanterns made of pierced tin, throwing wheeling constellations across the cobblestones. Musicians on a wagon — fiddles, a drum big enough to ride, a flute played by a man standing on one leg for reasons the memory did not explain and I did not question. And everywhere, *everywhere*, people dancing in the loose chaotic way of people who have done this every year of their lives and will do it every year after, who dance not because it's an occasion but because it's Tuesday-shaped and the harvest is in.

A festival. Our festival — Ashkar's, mine, though the memory wore no label. I knew it the way you know your own bed by falling into it.

And there, at the edge of it all, perched on a rain barrel because she was too small to see over the crowd, a girl of maybe six with braids coming undone, clapping wildly off the beat, wearing a paper crown that had gone soft with sweat. Her crown. My crown. There was a smear of honey cake down the front of my dress and I remembered — God help me, I *remembered* — that I had won that crown in a contest I didn't win so much as refuse to stop participating in.

An old man with white eyebrows danced over to the barrel and bowed to me with tremendous ceremony, as though I were visiting royalty, and held out his hand, and six-year-old me put one small foot in it and was swung down into the crowd and whirled until the lanterns became ribbons and the ribbons became the sky.

The feeling that came with it — I can't render it honestly in Common. The nearest word is the one Sef asked about on the road, the homesickness-for-nowhere that drove the refugees out of the Burnt Marches. Except this was the other side of it. Not missing a home you can't name. *Having* one, so completely that you don't notice it, the way you don't notice air until someone takes it away.

The memory closed. The bakery boy's pride guttered out above us, and I felt the loss of it like a draft from a door left open — his joy, spent on mine. One for one. That was the exchange rate now, and the market had never been crueler.

"Two," I whispered. Seven vials. Nine charms. "Wren, if I start laughing or weeping uncontrollably in the next hour, the correct response is to hit me with something soft."

Wren considered this gravely and patted my arm, which I chose to interpret as agreement.

* * *

I should have stopped there. Any sensible smuggler stops when the cargo starts mattering. But the third vial was different from the moment my fingers closed around it — heavier than glass should be, cold despite sitting all day against my body, and it *pulled*, gently, the way a tide pulls, like it had somewhere to be and considered me a reasonable route.

I almost saved it. I want that on the record, for whatever record survives: I almost saved it for morning, for better light, for courage.

Instead I spent the spinner-weaver's wedding joy — the whole golden afternoon of being loved — and the cold vial drank it down like a drunkard at a funeral and opened onto:

Rain. Evening rain, warm, the kind that steams off stones. A doorway — not a grand one, a plain wooden door at the end of a lane, paint peeling, a copper knocker worn bright by hands. And two people standing under the eaves beside it, sheltering, not quite ready to knock.

My mother. Younger than in the kitchen, younger than I had ever seen her, with rain in her hair and a look on her face I recognized from mirrors on bad days: terrified and certain at the same time, the expression of a woman about to do something irreversible.

Beside her, a man. Tall, dark-skinned, with laugh lines already carved deep, holding a bundle wrapped in oilcloth the way you hold something that is either very precious or very dangerous, possibly both. He was speaking low and fast in Ashkaran, and the memory gave me his words whole:

*"Once it's done, there's no undoing it. The Door doesn't open twice, Elodie. Whatever we hide behind it stays hidden — from him, from everyone. Even from us, if it comes to that. Are you sure?"*

My mother — younger, braver, soaked to the skin — touched the copper knocker and did not use it.

"I'm sure," she said. "Vane drinks memory, Dario. You've seen what he is now. If he ever gets inside our heads—" She stopped. Steadied herself. "We give them to the Door instead. Freely given, freely kept. Better the Door holds our childhoods than that monster eats them."

"And the girl?"

"The girl remembers nothing," my mother said, and her voice broke on it, one clean crack like ice on a pond. "That's the point. That's the *price*. She gets to grow up safe and whole and unhaunted, and she'll hate us for it, and I will deserve every ounce of that hatred, and I will pay it gladly if it keeps Aurel Vane's mouth shut."

The man — Dario — looked down at the oilcloth bundle and laughed, quietly, the way people laugh when the alternative is worse.

"You know she'll find them anyway. She's yours. She'll pick every lock in creation before she's twenty-five."

"God," my mother said fervently, "I *hope* so."

The door opened — I never saw who opened it, the memory kept that face deliberately dark, some guardian's discretion — and the two of them stepped inside with their bundle of bottled childhoods, mine among them, and the memory ended the way a book ends when someone slams it.

I sat very still for a long time.

The rain sound lingered longer than the rest. Rain does that; it's stubborn. And under it, my thoughts moved slowly, heavily, like carts through mud.

They hadn't sold my memories. They hadn't abandoned me. They had *hidden* me — hidden my past inside the one vault in the world Vane couldn't drink, and the price had been me. A daughter traded for a daughter's safety. And then Ashkar fell anyway, or was unwritten anyway, and the plan and the country and the parents all went into the dark together, and I grew up in Vess picking locks, fulfilling prophecy without knowing I'd been assigned one.

"They *chose* this," I said aloud. My voice sounded strange to me, scraped thin. "Maro didn't steal my childhood. They gave it away. To keep it from *him*." And then, because grief and fury share a bloodstream in me: "Mom, I take back every curse. Most of them. Sixty percent."

Wren was staring at me with an intensity that had weight to it, and I realized the child had felt the memory too — felt it the way the charms hummed, the way the road bent near Wren, everything about Wren being slightly more real than the world around it. Wren's lips moved. No sound. But the shape was unmistakable, and I understood it without learning it, the way I understood the lullaby, the way I understood everything now that the doors inside me were opening:

*Dario.*

The child knew the name. Knew the man. Which meant —

Which meant the bundle hadn't held only my memories. Which meant the Door had been keeping more than one family's secrets. Which meant Wren, the last undrunk drop of Ashkar's soul, might have been *in that room*, in whatever form a soul takes when it's being hidden in oilcloth.

"Wren," I said carefully, "what else did my parents put behind that door?"

The child looked at me with eyes that were suddenly, terribly old, and did not answer, and outside the hollow, far down the dark road, something began to glow — not campfire orange, not lantern yellow, but a cold white light moving fast, the light of men who eat memories for a living and had finally stopped needing to search.

Because you don't have to find a thief, I realized, my hand closing around the remaining vials. Not when the thief has spent all night lighting signal fires made of joy.

They were coming straight for us.

* * *

I woke Ilsa with my boot, which she later told me was the traditional method and she respected it. "Hollow Men," I said. "East road. Minutes, maybe less."

Ilsa rolled her map, already awake, already calculating. "The fen road?"

"The fen road."

"And your memories?"

I patted the pack. Seven vials. Eight charms, after tonight's spending. Enough to finish opening them, if the Door kept its side of the bargain. Not enough to survive what was chasing us, unless survival turned out to be the sort of thing you could pay for with someone else's happiness.

Which, I reflected grimly, swinging Wren up onto my back and running, was increasingly the only currency I had left.
