## 25. Vane's Version

They call me the Usurper, which is flattering and inaccurate. A usurper takes a throne. I took something considerably larger, and the throne was simply what was left over afterward, like crumbs on a plate after a very good meal.

Let me tell you how it actually happened, since history—my history, the one I wrote with my own hands—will never be honest about it.

Twenty years ago there was a country called Ashkar. You don't remember it. That's not an insult to your education; it's a compliment to my appetite. Ashkar sat where the Salt Fen sits now, between the Burnt Marches and the sea, and it was old in the way that mountains are old—not impressive at a glance, but load-bearing. Its people had a language that predated every kingdom around it, songs that made stone listen, and a Door. One Door. The last true exit from the world as it is written.

I know all of this intimately because I ate it.

Not conquered. Not burned. *Drank.* There is a difference, and the difference matters, though no one left alive is equipped to appreciate it. An army can take a city and leave the memory of it standing, a wound the world remembers and eventually scars over. But if you drink a thing—if you swallow it whole, every song and street name and grandmother's curse, every child's first word in the old tongue—then there is nothing left to remember *with*. The country doesn't fall. It becomes unverifiable. It becomes a rumor with no witnesses. It becomes, in the most literal sense available to us, nothing.

I was not born a monster, whatever the free cities' underground pamphlets claim. I was born ambitious, which is worse, because monsters are at least honest about their hungers.

I was a scholar once. Can you imagine? Aurel Vane, archivist of the Royal Collegium, cataloguing the birth-records of duchies that would outlive their dukes. I spent eleven years among documents, and documents teach you a terrible lesson: everything ends, but paper endures. Kings die; their signatures remain. Loves fail; the letters survive them. Memory is the only true currency, and everyone spends it carelessly, letting it rot in skulls that will be dirt within a century.

I decided to stop being careless.

The drinking itself—I won't pretend it was easy. Ashkar's soul did not go down like wine. It went down like a river of fire, and for three days I lay in the Door's antechamber while a nation poured itself into me, screaming in a language only I would ever hear again. My hair went white at the temples. My shadow, I'm told, behaves strangely now, arriving late or leaving early, as if even it isn't sure it belongs to me. Small prices. In exchange: I became untouchable. Not armored—untouchable. There is a distinction. Armor can be breached. But you cannot fight a man who exists inside your own certainty. Every soldier who raised a blade against me found his hands remembering obedience he'd never learned. Every priest who cursed me discovered his liturgy had a hole in it shaped exactly like my name.

You cannot assassinate a man who has already happened to you.

* * *

People assume power is the point. People are small. Power was merely the practical arrangement—the scaffolding I erected so that no one would ever have time to ask questions. A busy empire does not wonder what lies under its foundations.

No. The point was permanence. I had watched the Collegium's oldest texts crumble. I had watched my mother forget my father's face in her final year, then forget mine, then forget forgetting, smiling at the wall with the serene blankness of the truly finished. Everything we are is held by threads so fine that one small worm—one fever, one blow to the head, one usurper with an appetite—snaps them forever.

So I became the thread. All of them. Ashkar lives in me now, complete and curated. When I close my eyes I can walk its streets. I know the festival of the First Thaw, when they floated candles down the irrigation channels until the whole valley looked like a river of stars. I know the smell of their orchards in late summer—smoke and honey, because they burned their prunings at dusk and the sweetness came from somewhere deeper than fruit. I know a lullaby whose third line has no translation in any living tongue, because the tongue it belonged to died in my throat.

I am not their conqueror. I am their *archive*. The last library of a nation, bound in one arrogant spine.

And here is the part I admit to no one, not even at night, not even to the mirror, which flatters me professionally: some nights I open the archive just to visit. I sit in my tower above the capital with a glass of wine I cannot taste anymore—nothing tastes like anything when you've drunk a country; flavor was one of the prices—and I walk the streets of Ashkar in my head. I pass the orchard outside Vess-gate. I hear children singing the untranslatable lullaby. And I feel—

Well. Whatever I feel, it stays behind my ribs where it belongs. An archive does not grieve. An archive *preserves.*

* * *

Which brings us to last night, and why I am awake, and why my hand keeps drifting to the window as though the horizon might confess something.

For twenty years, the archive has been quiet. Dead things are quiet; that is their principal virtue. Oh, occasionally a fragment would surface unexpectedly—a dream of white doors, a sudden fluency in a dead alphabet when I was exhausted—but those were settling sounds, the house creaking on its foundations.

Last night, the archive *stirred.*

I want to be precise, because precision is the last scholarly habit I've kept. At the third hour past midnight, I was reviewing grain tariffs—power is mostly tariffs, another thing the pamphlets get wrong—when every memory of Ashkar in my possession turned toward something. Not woke. Turned. The way a field of wheat turns toward the sun, all at once, without wind. Twenty years of swallowed songs and streets and sorrows, and every single one of them leaned in the same direction, south and west, toward the Salt Fen, like iron filings finding a magnet they'd been told didn't exist.

I dropped the tariff ledger. I, who have watched executions without blinking, dropped a ledger of *grain tariffs*, and stood in my tower shaking like a man hearing his own name called from a drowned ship.

Something is knocking on the inside of my collection.

I rode to the fen's edge two years ago, after the first faint tremor—a candle-flicker in the deep archive, easily dismissed. Nothing there. Salt flat to the horizon, unmarked by any map worth the ink, because I had seen to it personally that no map worth the ink marked anything. The Door was sealed. I had sealed it myself, in the days after the drinking, with wards woven from stolen certainty, and seals do not simply—

Except. Except that seals are made of memory, aren't they. Every ward is a remembered promise, every lock a remembered fear. And I have recently learned—I learned it through a tremor in the archive, through a sourness in the wine of my hoard—that pieces of Ashkar escaped me. Fragments. Droplets off the ladle, so to speak. A childhood here, a festival there, scattered into the world in vessels I did not account for. Bottled. Sold. Traded in the night markets of a city built on my blind spot.

Someone has been carrying pieces of my meal around in their pockets for twenty years. And now someone—or something—is trying to pour them back.

* * *

This morning I summoned Sef.

The Hollow Men are my finest work, if I'm permitted vanity, and I permit myself everything. Ordinary inquisitors erase practitioners; my Hollow Men erase *thoroughly*. They are men who sold me their own pasts in exchange for purpose, walking questions with no answers inside them, and therefore incorruptible—there is nothing in them to corrupt. Sef is the best of them. Empty as a bell and twice as resonant.

"Find the residue," I told him. "Ashkaran traces in the memory-trade. Charms, brokers, fences. Someone in Vess is moving fragments of what's mine."

He tilted his head, that birdlike tilt of a man consulting a shelf with nothing on it. "Yours, lord?"

"Mine," I said, and let him hear the weight in it. "Every scrap. Every drop. If you find the vessel carrying them, you will bring me the vessel intact. Do you understand? Intact. Whatever it looks like. Whoever it looks like."

He bowed and went, and I stood at my window watching him cross the courtyard, a small dark absence in the shape of a man, and I felt the archive stir again beneath my ribs—that slow, collective turning toward the south and west—and I confess something that will never appear in any history I write:

I am afraid.

Not of armies. Armies are arithmetic. Not of assassins; you cannot kill a man who is already inside your killers' certainties. I am afraid the way a miser is afraid, the way a dragon sleeping on its hoard is afraid: because something wants it back. Because ownership, I am beginning to suspect, was always the wrong word for what I did. You can steal a purse. You can even steal a life. But a *country*—a country is not property. A country is a belonging, and belongings, unlike possessions, belong to themselves.

Somewhere in Vess, in a night market that smells of tallow and rain, someone is selling back the hours I swallowed. Somewhere a child is speaking a language that exists nowhere but inside my body. And the Door—the Last Door, the one I sealed with the bones of the word *forever*—is remembering that it was never mine to shut.

Very well. Then I will do what I did before. I will go to the fen ahead of my armies, because some meals you must attend personally. I will stand at the threshold of everything I drank, and if the thief comes—as thieves always come, drawn to the vault they've heard whispered about—I will make her an offer no reasonable person refuses.

I will give her back her past. All of it. Every stolen year, bottled and gleaming, in exchange for her walking away and letting my archive sleep.

It is, after all, the only currency I have ever spent well.

And if she refuses—if she chooses a dead country over her own remembered name—then I will learn, at the Door of everything, whether a man who has drunk a nation can be made to give it up.

I find, setting down this account, that my hands are steady and my shadow has arrived early for once, waiting by the door like a servant who knows something I don't.

South and west, the archive turns.

I am coming home to defend it.
