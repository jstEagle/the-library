## 26. The Fen That Isn't There

The Salt Fen announced itself by refusing to exist.

We'd been walking toward it for two days—Sef limping on a splinted ankle he refused to discuss, Ilsa navigating by a map that was mostly blank space and apology, Wren riding my shoulders with the patient weight of a child who had once been something much larger and remembered it in their bones. And then the ground simply stopped agreeing to be anything.

Not water. Not salt crust. Not the grey scrubland we'd crossed since dawn. Just a pale shimmer, like heat-haze in winter, stretching to a horizon that kept rearranging itself when you weren't looking directly at it.

"That's it," Ilsa said, in the voice of a woman reading her own death warrant and finding the handwriting nice.

"That's what?" Sef asked. "There's nothing there."

"There's *less* than nothing," Ilsa said. "That's the point. Look at your feet."

I looked. My boots stood on cracked white earth, ordinary enough, the kind of ground that would taste of salt if you licked it. But ten paces ahead, the earth didn't crack or continue or end. It just stopped being asked to perform. The world out there was an unpainted wall, and someone had forgotten to even hang a canvas on it.

"Twenty years ago this was the richest delta in the world," Ilsa said. "Rice terraces up the hillsides. Salt harvested in pink pyramids. The city of Ashkar sat where that—" she gestured at the shimmer, at the absence, "—that *nothing* is. Four hundred thousand people. Canals. A library that made Vess's look like a tavern's back shelf." She said it flatly, the way you recite facts you've been punished for knowing. "And when they unwrote it, they unwrote the ground too. The fen isn't hiding Ashkar. The fen is the hole Ashkar left."

Wren tugged my hair. Not hard. Just a small insistence, fingers curling in the strands above my ear, the way they did when words were too heavy and touch had to carry them instead.

"I know," I said. "I feel it too."

And I did. That was the worst part. All the way across the Burnt Marches I'd felt it building—a homesickness with no home attached, an ache shaped exactly like a place I couldn't name. The refugees we'd passed on the road had it too. They couldn't say why they were walking south, only that they woke at night grieving for somewhere they'd never been. I'd thought it was the war coming. It wasn't. It was this. It was four hundred thousand erased lives pressing against the inside of the world like voices against a window, and every one of us with an Ashkaran-shaped hollow in our chest could hear them humming.

Even me. Especially me. A woman with no past of her own, carrying other people's memories in glass vials against her ribs, standing at the edge of a country-sized memory that had been stolen from everyone at once.

"Well," Sef said, and spat. "I've spent eleven years erasing people for the Order, and I have to say, this is the first time I've seen the paperwork done properly."

"Sef."

"What? It's well-made. Look at the seams." He pointed at the horizon, where the shimmer met the sky in a line so clean it hurt. "You can't even see where the lie starts. That's craftsmanship. Terrible, monstrous craftsmanship, but—" He caught my expression and shut his mouth. Eleven years as a Hollow Man teaches you to admire the work. Three weeks traveling with us had taught him to hate himself for admiring it. Progress, of a kind.

Ilsa unrolled her map on a flat stone, weighting the corners with salt chunks. The route she'd drawn—the one she'd spent three sleepless nights reconstructing from half-remembered charts and a smuggler's song and, gods help us, a child's drawing—ended here, at a mark she'd inked as a circle with a door drawn inside it. Beyond the circle, the parchment was blank. Not empty. Blank. She'd told me once there was a difference: empty meant nothing had been written; blank meant something had been taken away.

"The approach runs straight through," she said. "Due east, maybe three miles to where the Door should stand. If it still stands. If it can stand, given that the concept of *standing* may not apply in there." She rubbed her eyes. Ink stained her fingers to the second knuckle; she'd been writing all night, and I'd noticed the writing fading as fast as she wrote it. Proximity to the fen was eating her knowledge, bite by bite. By now she probably couldn't have told you the capital of Vess, which, frankly, no great loss, but still.

"And if it doesn't stand?" Sef asked.

"Then we've walked three miles into nothing for nothing, and Vane's army finds our bodies whenever the world remembers to look for them." Ilsa folded the map with hands that weren't quite steady. "Anybody want to say something sensible and turn back?"

Wren slid off my shoulders before I could answer.

They walked to the edge of the shimmer—that pale, wrong nothing—and stopped, head tilted, listening to something. The wind had died an hour ago. There was nothing to listen to. But Wren listened anyway, the way you listen at a door when you're not sure whether anyone's home, and then they took one step forward onto ground that wasn't.

And the air parted.

I don't have better words than that, and I've spent three years selling words to people who'd lost theirs. The shimmer split along a seam, slow as a curtain drawn back by a hand we couldn't see, and behind it wasn't more nothing. Behind it was *light*—not sunlight, though it was gold, not firelight, though it moved—but the light you get behind your eyelids when you remember something so hard you're almost there. Wren stood in the seam of it, small and mud-brown and utterly unafraid, looking back at us over one shoulder with an expression I'd learned to read over these weeks: *come on, then. What are you waiting for?*

"Oh," Ilsa whispered. "Oh, it's *still here.*"

"Of course it's still here," I said, and my voice came out strange, thick, like I was speaking around something lodged in my throat. "You don't erase a country. You just convince everyone to stop looking at it. It's been here the whole time, waiting for someone to—"

To what? Remember? Believe? Walk through a curtain of air because a mute child told us to?

I'd spent twenty years—no. Three years. I'd spent three years in Vess learning that memory was currency, that it could be bottled and sold and stolen and drunk, that the powerful got fat on other people's pasts while the poor sold their childhoods by the hour. Aurel Vane had built an empire on that arithmetic. He'd eaten a whole kingdom to make himself untouchable, and the world had signed off on the transaction because the alternative was unbearable.

But Wren had just shown me the flaw in the ledger. You can steal everything a country remembers about itself. You cannot steal what the country *is*. It waits. It hums under the skin of the world. It sends a child to a thief's door in the middle of the night and refuses to leave.

"Elodie." Sef's hand was on his knife—not drawing it, just resting there, an old habit from a man relearning what his hands were for. "The seam. It's not staying open."

He was right. Even as I watched, the edges of the parted air were knitting closed again, gold light thinning to a thread, Wren standing in a gap that was narrowing by the heartbeat. The fen wanted its nothing back. Or something in the fen did—or something beyond it was pulling the curtain shut from the other side, gentle as a parent closing a door on a sleeping child.

"Wren!" I was moving before I decided to, boots crunching salt, lungs full of air that tasted of nothing at all. "Wren, get back—"

But Wren didn't come back. Wren pressed both small palms against the closing seam, and the light flared, and I swear on every charm I ever stole that I heard it—a sound like a held breath finally released, like forty years of silence breaking, like a word in a language I shouldn't know and somehow did:

*Ashkar.*

My own mouth shaped it. I never taught myself that word. No one in Vess had spoken it in twenty years because no one in Vess knew it existed. But it came up out of me like water from a well I'd forgotten I owned, and with it came a smell—orange blossom, woodsmoke, rain on hot stone—and a sound, distant, of bells, and a feeling so large and specific that it knocked me to my knees on the salt crust:

*Coming home late. Lantern light. Someone waiting.*

I didn't know whose lantern. I didn't know whose door. The memory wasn't mine—it couldn't be mine, I had no memories, that was the entire architecture of my life—but it lived in me the way Wren's language lived in me, planted there by whatever I'd lost, growing wild in the dark.

The seam held. Wren's arms trembled with the effort of holding it, and I saw now that the light wasn't just light—it was pressure, the weight of an unwritten country leaning against the thinnest possible membrane of the world, and a seven-year-old body was the only thing keeping it propped open.

I got up. I ran. Ilsa was shouting something behind me—coordinates, warnings, maybe a prayer—and Sef was shouting something else, and none of it mattered, because Wren was swaying, and the seam was singing, and I put my hands next to those small ones against the curtain of the world.

It was warm. Gods, it was warm. Like pressing your palms against a window in winter with a fire on the other side. Like touching the cheek of someone feverish. Like the orchard—

Like the orchard burning.

I snatched my hands back. Then, hating myself, put them back. Because the warmth wasn't the fire. The fire was one memory, one vial, one hour of one terrible day. This was everything else. This was the whole country, alive under the burn scar, pushing toward the light.

"It needs a key," Ilsa said, arriving beside me, breathless, grey-faced. Her voice had gone strange too—half wonder, half terror. "That's what the old charts said. The Door doesn't open for force. It opens for—" She stopped. Swallowed. "For true memory. Freely given."

"Then we're rich," I said, and heard how hollow it sounded. "I'm carrying two dozen charms. A soldier's happiest hour. A baker's wedding. A fisherman's daughter's first—"

"No." Ilsa's hand closed on my wrist. Her grip was iron; three weeks of hard road had rebuilt her soft mapmaker's hands into something else. "Look at it, Elodie. Really look."

I looked. Through the seam, past Wren's silhouette, I could see—faintly, the way you see the bottom of a river through moving water—shapes. Rooftops. Terraces. A tower. The ghost of a city holding its breath, waiting to be real again.

"You can't buy your way through that door with other people's joy," Ilsa said quietly. "The Door knows the difference between a memory you stole and a memory that's yours. It always has. That's why Vane couldn't drink his way to godhood cleanly—he's full to bursting, but none of it's *his*, and the Door won't take stolen goods." She turned to me, and her eyes were wet, and I understood with a lurch that she wasn't looking at me anymore. She was looking at the shape of what I'd have to do. "Whatever key opens this thing, Elodie, it has to be yours. Truly yours. Given freely. Which means—"

"Which means I need my memories back." The words came out flat, factual, terrible. "All of them. The ones Maro bottled. The ones I've spent three years pretending I didn't want."

The seam flickered. Wren sagged, and I caught them under the arms, hauling them back from the edge before the curtain could close on empty air. They weighed almost nothing. They were shaking. When I lifted them, they pressed their forehead against mine, and I felt it again—that hum, that vast patience, that grief the size of a nation wearing the face of a tired child.

*Soon,* I promised them, in a language I'd never learned. *Hold on. Soon.*

Behind us, Sef had gone very quiet. I turned, Wren on my hip, and found him staring at the seam with an expression I'd only seen once before—the night he'd told us about the purchased past sitting in his chest like a stone, the memories of a man he used to be, bought and paid for by the Order, none of them his.

"It's beautiful," he said, and his voice broke on the second syllable. "Gods forgive me, it's the most beautiful thing I've ever seen, and I helped build the machine that buried it."

"Yes," I said. "And now we're going to dig it up. All of it. Starting with mine."

Ilsa was already spreading her fading map on the salt, marking our position with hands that shook. East, she said. Three miles through the nothing, following the seam Wren could hold open only in pieces. And somewhere ahead, a Door that wouldn't accept stolen goods—which meant that before we reached it, I would have to go back to Vess in my mind, back to Maro's vault, back to the bottles of my own childhood, and take back what was mine.

And I already knew—I knew the way you know the last line of a story before you've read it—that taking them back would cost more than I'd ever stolen in my life.

Wren tightened their arms around my neck.

Together, we stepped through the curtain.
