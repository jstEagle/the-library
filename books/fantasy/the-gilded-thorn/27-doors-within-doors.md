## 27. Doors Within Doors

The air inside the Fen's curtain was warm.

That was the first wrongness, and I want it on the record that I noticed it. We had crossed a salt marsh in late autumn—mud to the knee, wind that found every gap in a coat like it had been paid to—and then Wren had walked forward into nothing and pulled the world open like a curtain of grey gauze, and now we stood in air that smelled faintly of woodsmoke and ripening apples. Warm as a kitchen. Warm as somewhere someone loved you.

Ilsa stopped just past the threshold and looked back. The fen was still there behind us, grey and flat and reeking, but seen through the curtain it looked dimmed, like a room with the shutter half-closed.

"It doesn't want us leaving," she said.

"Everything worth entering has that quality," I said. "Maro's counting house. The Vess tax office."

"This isn't a joke, Elodie."

"No," I agreed. "It's worse than that. It's architecture."

* * *

The Door itself sat at the center of a hollow that shouldn't have existed, because nothing existed here—that was rather the point of the place. But there it was: a door standing alone in a circle of pale grass, no wall around it, no house, no frame beyond its own timber. Old oak gone silver-grey with weather, iron hinges furred with rust, and carved across its face—a hand's width at a time, worn nearly smooth by centuries of hands or centuries of weather or both—the shape of a key.

Not a picture of a key. A *shape*, the way grief is a shape. You knew what it was the way you know a face in a crowd before you can say why.

Wren walked straight toward it. I caught the child's shoulder and felt something pass through my palm—not heat, not cold, a kind of attention, like being read by a very patient librarian who already knows which book you are.

"Don't touch it yet," Ilsa said sharply. "Gods. Don't either of you touch anything until we understand the rules. Places like this always have rules."

"You've seen one before?"

"I've seen maps of one." Ilsa crouched, pulled her satchel around, and began laying out her tools with the grim ceremony of a surgeon. "And I've read the accounts of the cartographers who came looking for Ashkar after the Fall. Three expeditions. Two came back empty-handed. One came back with empty *heads*—alive, walking, answering questions, but nothing in them. Not names, not their mothers' faces. Just polite vacancy." She looked up at me. "The Door takes payment, Elodie. That's the rule. Everything here is a transaction."

"I'm familiar with transactions," I said. "It's been my entire adult life."

* * *

We made camp in the lee of the Door itself, which Ilsa protested and I overruled, on the grounds that if the thing wanted to eat us it could do so while we slept regardless, and I'd rather be eaten warm than cold. Wren sat cross-legged facing it, the way children sit before fires, and hummed. The humming was in Ashkaran. I didn't know how I knew that. I knew it the way I knew the warmth, the way I'd known the word *home* when Wren finally spoke it on the rooftops of Vess—not learned, just *there*, sitting in me like furniture left by a previous tenant.

My stolen memories. My bottled childhood. Every vial I'd cracked open between Vess and here had put another piece of Ashkar back inside me, and every piece fit like it had never left, because it hadn't—it had only been carried by someone else, sold and resold, passed hand to hand through three years of night markets until it came home to roost in the one thief dishonest enough to steal it back.

I turned my palm up. The scar sat there, thin and pale, shaped like a door-key. I'd had it since Vess. Since before Vess. I'd told people it was a rope burn.

"The key," I said aloud, because saying things aloud makes them smaller. "It wants a memory. True, freely given. Ilsa, tell me I'm wrong."

Ilsa didn't look up from her fading map. "You're not wrong. The old accounts call it *the tithe*. A door between what-is and what-was-unmade can't be opened with force or cunning. It opens with a gift. Something true, given freely, without taking it back." She blew gently on the ink; it vanished even as she wrote, and she wrote faster. "Which raises an interesting question about your inventory."

"My inventory is stolen goods," I said. "Every charm I own. Every memory I lifted from some dying soldier or grieving widow or drunk apprentice who'd have traded his whole childhood for a hot meal. I stole them. Freely given they are not."

"So the Door won't take them."

"The Door will take them." I heard my own voice go flat, the market-voice, the voice I used when the price was bad news. "That's what worries me. Look at it, Ilsa. Look at this whole place. Ashkar wasn't destroyed. It was *unwritten*—drunk down by Aurel Vane and locked behind this door so thoroughly that two kingdoms' worth of archives forgot it ever existed. This Door isn't a barrier. It's a *lockbox*. And lockboxes don't care where the key came from. They care that it fits."

I held up my palm again. The scar caught the strange warm light.

"And mine fits. Because they're true memories. Stolen, yes—but true. The soldier's happy hour was true. The orchard was true. My mother's face—" My voice did something embarrassing and I let it, because there was no one left to perform for. "My mother's face was true. The Door will open for them. And when it does—"

"When it does, they're spent," Ilsa finished quietly. "Unmade. Forever. You don't get them back, Elodie. That's what freely given means. You give them *away*."

* * *

Night came, if night was the word for it—the light through the curtain dimming to a deep amber, the grass glowing faintly like the last coals of a fire. I took out my remaining charms and laid them on my coat in rows, the way I used to lay them out in the market: little glass stoppered vials, each holding a curl of light that moved like smoke underwater.

Thirty-one charms. I counted twice. Thirty-one pieces of other people's joy, hoarded against a rainy day that had finally arrived with a vengeance.

And among them, wrapped separately in cloth because I couldn't bear to mix it with stock, the ones that were mine. The orchard. The festival. The promise made at a door by a woman with my jaw and someone else's eyes. Fragments of a childhood I'd sold at seventeen to buy passage into a city that erased me, and bought back piece by piece at ruinous cost, and now—

"Now," I said to Wren, "here's the arithmetic."

Wren looked up. The child's face in the amber light was calm in a way no child's face should be, and I understood—I finally let myself fully understand—that I wasn't explaining to a child at all. I was explaining to a country. To the last undrunk drop of a nation's soul wearing the shape of something small enough that no inquisitor would bother hunting it.

"Vane drank Ashkar," I said. "All of it. Every mind, every song, every name. Except you. Except whatever was hidden in you. When the Door opens, all of that comes back—the land, the language, the people, everything he swallowed. But the Door needs a key, and the key is memory given freely, and the only memories in the world that remember Ashkar clearly enough to turn the lock are—" I gestured at the rows of glass. "These. Mine. Bought back with three years of theft and every coin I ever earned."

Wren reached out and touched one vial—the orchard, of course, always the orchard—and the light inside leaned toward the small fingers like a plant toward sun.

"If I use them," I said, "they burn. All of them. I get to see my mother's face one more time and then never again. I get to stand in the orchard once more and then it's ash forever, even in my head. I'll walk into a restored country that I gave everything to remember, and I won't remember why." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "There's a joke in there. Maro would find it. The fence who raised me would appreciate the structure of it: the thief pays for the goods with the goods themselves."

Wren said nothing. Wren almost never said anything. But the child took my scarred hand and pressed it flat against the ground, against the pale grass, and I felt it again—that patient reading, that terrible tenderness—and beneath my palm the earth was *humming*. Faintly. In Ashkaran.

It was singing. The ground itself was singing, muffled, like voices through a wall, like a whole nation pressed behind a door with its mouth to the crack, waiting.

"They're in there," I whispered. "They're alive in there. Aren't they? All of them. Everyone Vane drank. He didn't kill them. He *kept* them."

Wren nodded once.

And that was the moment the sacrifice crystallized, if you want the honest accounting—not at the threshold, not in the giving, but here, on the grass, doing sums. Because I had built my whole self out of the belief that my memories were *mine*, hard-won, reclaimed, the one thing in my life I'd refused to sell. Never steal your own past, I'd told myself for three years, and meant: never surrender your own past. And now the choice wasn't between keeping it and losing it. It was between keeping it and letting a million mouths finish their song.

A country, or a childhood.

I had spent three years learning to survive without belonging. I had gotten very good at it. I'd told myself it was freedom.

It was inventory. That's all it had ever been. Inventory, hoarded by a frightened girl who thought that if she just held enough of other people's happiness she might eventually feel some herself.

* * *

I woke before dawn—or the amber equivalent—to find Ilsa standing at the Door with her palm flat against the wood, and her face wet.

"It answered me," she said, not turning around. "I asked it what was behind it and it showed me. Half a second. A harbor. Ships with painted eyes on the bows. A woman calling a name I almost—" Her voice broke and rebuilt itself. "My grandmother sang a lullaby with words nobody in Vess recognized. Scholars said she was mad, or foreign, or both. Elodie, I think I'm *from* here. Third generation unremembered. I charted places that stopped existing because some part of me was trying to draw my way home."

"Ilsa—"

"Don't." She wiped her face briskly, furiously. "Don't be kind to me right now, I haven't got the margin for it. Here's what matters: I checked the old accounts again, all night. There's a line in the third expedition's record—one sentence, half burned—that everyone dismissed as madness. *The door counts what you carry.*" She turned to look at me, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "It counts, Elodie. It knows what you have. It knows what you're willing to spend. And I don't think it accepts partial payment."

"Partial payment," I repeated.

"To open a whole country, you'd need—" she gestured at my rows of vials, at the wrapped bundle, at everything. "All of it. Every charm. Every recovered memory. Not the thirty-one. The thirty-one and the bundle and whatever else surfaces when the first ones start burning, because burning memories *call* to buried ones, and yours will come running like dogs to a whistle, and you'll have to feed them all to the fire or the Door stays shut and we die here in the warm with the Hollow Men arriving behind us."

I looked at my life laid out in glass.

Thirty-one strangers' joys. One mother's face. One orchard. One promise at a door.

Somewhere far behind us, back through the curtain, through the fen, through the burnt marches, I could almost hear the sound I'd been hearing in nightmares since Vess: boots. An army coming to make sure the lockbox stayed shut, and its keeper fat and untouchable on the strength of what he'd drunk.

"All of it," I said.

"All of it."

I picked up the soldier's charm—the very first one, the dying man's happiest hour, the hour by the river with the girl whose name I'd known once. I'd carried it three years. It was the closest thing I had to proof that I was capable of carrying something gently.

I weighed it in my palm, and it was so light, and it was the heaviest thing I had ever held.

"Well," I said. "Then we'd better decide fast. Because I think I hear—"

The curtain at the edge of the hollow shivered.

And through it, thin and grey and patient, came the smell of cold iron.
