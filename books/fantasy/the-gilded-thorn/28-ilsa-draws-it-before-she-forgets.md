## 28. Ilsa Draws It Before She Forgets

The first thing I lost was the name of the bird.

There was a bird on the fen — a grey, long-legged thing that stood in the shallows on one leg and regarded me with the patience of a creditor — and I had known its name once, I was almost certain of it. Not just *heron*, which is a word anyone can have. The other name. The one the marsh-folk used, the one with the soft middle consonant that my old master Corvin used to say when he was drunk and feeling educational. I reached for it and found the shelf where it should have been swept clean, dusted, arranged for a customer who never came.

I told myself it was age. I am fifty-one, my knees have opinions about weather, and forgetting is the natural tax on a long life spent memorizing coastlines. I told myself that all morning while I sketched the fen's edge in my book — the reed-beds, the drowned causeway, the way the water lay flat as hammered tin — and then I looked down at what I'd drawn and realized I had labeled the reed-beds twice and the causeway not at all.

That was not age. Age misplaces things. This was *removing* them.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about being a mapmaker: you are not drawing the world, you are arguing with it, and the world always wins eventually. I learned that young. Corvin used to say a map is a promise the land never agreed to keep, and he laughed when he said it, right up until the year the Laughing Coast stopped laughing. Right up until the charts went wrong.

I was twenty-three when I charted a harbor that no longer existed. That sounds like madness, so let me be precise: I stood on a cliff above open sea with my instruments out, and I drew a harbor — curving piers, a lighthouse, warehouses with red tile roofs — because I could *see* it, plain as the sun, and because my instruments insisted it was there, and because three fishermen who had rowed me out swore they'd sold mackerel on those piers since boyhood. And then the Admiralty checked the older charts and found nothing. No record. No ruins under the water when divers went down. Just my drawing, and three fishermen who by winter could no longer remember why they'd been so sure.

They called me mad. Then they called me disgraced, which is more official. Then they called me nothing at all, because in Vess a woman without a guild stamp is barely a noun.

For thirty years I assumed the fault was mine. That is the cruelty of erasure — it doesn't need to argue with you. It only needs to wait until everyone else agrees you were wrong, and then your certainty becomes your shame, and you carry it like a stone in the boot until you forget there was ever a time you walked without limping.

Then Elodie Oyelaran turned up at my door with a mute child and a rubbing of a door drawn in charcoal on floorboards, and asked me if I knew what Ashkar was, and I said yes before I remembered that I wasn't supposed to know anything of the kind.

* * *

Now we were camped at the edge of the Salt Fen, where Ashkar had stood — or hadn't; the grammar of the thing defeats me even now — and the air out over the water had begun to behave like something with an opinion.

It started small. Elodie's fire wouldn't take on the first night; the flames kept bending toward the fen as if leaning to listen. Wren sat facing the water the whole evening, motionless, and the child's shadow fell *toward* the fen instead of away from the fire, which I noted in my book and then crossed out, because some observations make the ink feel unsafe.

By the second morning, the fen had eaten the name of the bird.

By noon, it had taken the capital city of Serevan from me — not the place, I've never been there, but the *fact* of it, the shape of its river delta that I'd copied into forty different charts, gone, a hole shaped like knowledge. I tested myself the way Corvin taught us: recite the free cities in order of founding. Vess, Callowmere, Brant, Serevan—

Four. There were four. I counted again and felt the number slide under my hand like a fish. Five? I wrote down *five cities* and stared at it and understood, with the cold professional clarity that has been the only constant in my life, that I was watching myself become less.

So I did what I have always done when the world argues with me. I sharpened a pen and started keeping score.

* * *

The trouble with ordinary ink is that it trusts the paper. You put a line down and the line stays down; that is the entire contract. But the ink I mixed that afternoon — lampblack, salt from the fen itself, and a spoonful of tallow I'd been saving against emergencies, which this emphatically was — that ink did not trust the paper. That ink wrote the way a man walks across ice he doesn't trust.

I began with the approach, because the approach was the thing most likely to kill us.

The Salt Fen looks flat. That is its first lie. From the shingle where we camped it presents itself as a mile of shallow water and reed, unremarkable, the sort of terrain a cartographer notes in two lines and forgets. But Wren had walked out onto it that first evening — walked straight off the shingle and across the surface of the shallows as though the water were a rumor the child declined to believe — and where Wren stepped, I had watched the light *bend*. Not reflect. Bend. As if the air out there were a curtain someone had gathered aside and let fall behind.

So I mapped what I could see and what I could infer, and I wrote the inferences in the fading ink so I would know which parts of my own map to distrust:

*Shingle ends at low-tide mark. Beyond: water that is not water — depth reads as six inches by line-and-weight, but the line comes back dry. Do not trust soundings. Reed-beds end in a clean arc, too clean, as if cut. The arc is a boundary. The child crosses it freely. We do not.*

The words were still wet when they began to go. Not smearing — *thinning*. The lampblack lifted off the page like breath fading off glass, letter by letter, starting from the ends of words inward, so that *boundary* became *bounda* became *bou* became a faint grey ghost and then nothing, and the paper showed no stain where it had been. Clean. Unwritten.

I watched my own sentence die and felt something in my chest do the same.

* * *

"You're doing the face," Elodie said.

She'd come up beside me without sound — she does that, moves like a debt collector — and stood looking down at my book with her arms folded and her jaw set at the angle that means she has decided something and is waiting for the world to catch up.

"What face?"

"The one where you're losing an argument with geography."

"Ah. My resting expression." I turned the book so she could see the ghost-words. "Tell me what you read there."

She bent close. Her eyes tracked left to right, then again, slower. "There's... there's writing. Mostly. Some of it's—" She stopped. I watched her arrive at it, the way I'd arrived at it, the slow horror of a person realizing the ground is moving. "It's going. While we watch."

"While I watch, at any rate. You may still be able to read the ghosts. Which raises the interesting question of whether the fen eats knowledge or merely borrows it, and I confess I don't love either answer." I pulled the book back and smoothed the page. "Elodie. I need to tell you what's happening to me, and I need you to remember it, because in three days' time I may not be able to."

"That bad?"

"I lost a city today. Serevan, possibly. Possibly it was never called Serevan. Possibly—" I made myself stop; the sentence was unraveling faster than I could speak it. "The Door is unwriting outward. A radius, growing. We're inside it now. Everything I know about this place, everything I ever charted or heard or guessed about Ashkar, is being collected. Or deleted. I genuinely cannot determine which, and the distinction may matter enormously."

"Why would it matter?"

"Because deletion leaves nothing. Collection leaves a *debt*." I tapped the page where the word *boundary* had died. "Corvin had a theory, near the end, when his own charts started coming up blank. He said the places didn't stop existing — they were *filed away*. That somewhere, someone kept the ledger. And a ledger, Elodie, implies a librarian. And librarians—" I smiled, and I'm told it isn't a comforting smile, "—librarians charge fees."

Wren was standing at the waterline, ankle-deep in shallows that should have soaked the child's boots, staring out at nothing with an expression I had last seen on Corvin's face the day before he walked into the sea.

* * *

That night I worked by candlelight and lost arguments by candlelight.

This is what I managed to save, in the fading ink, racing my own mind like a woman bailing a boat with a thimble:

The approach runs along the drowned causeway — I could see its bones under the false water, a road of black stone running straight out toward the center of the fen, and I knew in my bones it was a road because roads have a look about them even when drowned, a confidence of line. Halfway along, the causeway passes through the clean arc of the reed-line, and past that point my instruments gave up entirely: the needle spun, the plumb line hung crooked, and when I sighted through my azimuth ring toward the fen's heart I saw — I am recording this exactly as I saw it, because in a week I will doubt it and I want the evidence in my own hand —

I saw a door.

Not metaphorically. A door, freestanding, upright, in the middle of empty water a mile out. Old dark wood, iron-bound, with a lintel carved in a style I recognized the way you recognize a face across a crowded room — and the recognizing hurt, physically, a pull behind the eyes, because the style had a name and the name was being taken from me even as I looked at it. I got the compass bearing down. North-northeast, four degrees east of true. I got the distance by pacing the geometry backward from the shoreline: roughly a thousand and two hundred paces.

I did not get the name of the carving style. By the time my pen moved to write it, the space where it lived was already swept.

And here is the detail I keep circling back to, the one that wakes me between the watches: the door was closed. And beneath its foot, on the threshold stone, there was a keyhole. And the keyhole was not empty-shaped. It was shaped like a scar.

Like the scar on Elodie Oyelaran's palm, which I have seen often enough these past weeks to draw from memory — a thin line, door-keyed, curved at the shoulder like a question asking to be answered.

I wrote that down too. In the good ink. The permanent ink. Some things deserve to survive even if I don't.

* * *

On the third morning I could no longer read my own earlier books.

Understand what I'm saying. I opened the satchel volume — thirty years of fieldwork, every chart I ever smuggled out of the guild archives, every coastline I ever argued into existence — and the pages were full of my own handwriting and it meant nothing. Elegant lines, confident labels, and behind them a wall of fog. I held the book upside down for a full ten seconds before the wrongness of it registered, and when I turned it right way up, I laughed. What else do you do? I laughed until Elodie came running, and then I showed her the book and she went pale and took it from me gently, the way you take scissors from a child.

"How much is gone?" she asked.

"The past, mostly. The general shapes remain. I know I was a mapmaker. I know I was disgraced, though the reason has gone pleasantly vague — I find I've decided it was somebody else's fault, which is very restful, I recommend it." I flexed my fingers, testing their memory of the pen. "The present is holding. Today I can hold. Ask me tomorrow."

"Can you still get us to the Door?"

I thought about lying to her, and discarded it; she steals memories for a living, she'd smell the forgery. "Yes," I said. "While I'm standing here. Because I wrote it down this morning in the salt-ink, and the salt-ink is still legible — barely — and because Wren knows the way in the way water knows downhill. But Elodie, listen to me. Every hour we spend inside this radius, I lose another room of the house. Yesterday it was cities and birds. This morning I tried to recall my mother's face and found a beautifully rendered blank where she ought to be. I drew her once, you know. When I was nine. Badly, but I drew her." My voice did something I didn't authorize. "The Door is taking my payment in advance."

"Then we go now." She said it the way she says everything, flat and final, a coin placed on a counter. "Today. Now. Before it takes whatever's next."

"And what happens," I asked, "when we reach the threshold and the keyhole needs a mapmaker, and the fen has already invoiced me down to the last coastline? What if I stand in front of that door and can't remember what doors are *for*?"

She was quiet for a moment. Out on the water, Wren turned and looked at us — at me, specifically, and the child's face had an awful gentleness in it, the gentleness of someone who knows exactly how much something costs and is letting you choose anyway.

"Then I'll carry you," Elodie said. "You drew us this far. Whatever's left of you gets through."

I have been paid in gold, in guild honors I never received, in one truly memorable kiss on the Laughing Coast before the laughing stopped. Nothing ever paid me like that sentence. I turned back to my book and kept writing, fast, while the words still meant.

* * *

We broke camp within the hour. Elodie carried the gear; I carried the book; Wren walked ahead across the shingle toward the arc of cut reeds, and where the child stepped, the light bent aside like a curtain, and I followed the bend.

I want it recorded that I mapped as I went. Even now. Even knowing each line would fade before I finished the next. Bearing north-northeast. Depth false. Stone true. The causeway holds. The causeway holds.

Halfway out, the fog in my head reached the room where I keep arithmetic, and I watched numbers go the way of birds and mothers — I looked at my own pace-count and saw marks instead of quantities — and I gripped the book and said aloud, to whoever keeps ledgers in this drowned country: *"A thousand two hundred paces. You'll not have that one. It's written."*

And the ink faded under my finger even as I said it, and I laughed again, because what else, and Elodie caught my elbow and steadied me, and ahead of us the door stood up out of the false water, closer now, iron-bound and patient, its keyhole catching light like a scar catching sun.

Behind us — far behind, back along the drowned road, at the fen's grey edge — I heard a sound I knew from thirty years of war-charts and never once hoped to hear at my back: the cadence of many boots, marching in step, coming on fast.

I did not turn around. Turning costs paces, and I am down to so few.

"Inquisitors," Elodie breathed, and her hand found Wren's shoulder.

"No," I said, reading the rhythm of it, the last piece of professional knowledge I owned, spending it like a dying woman spends gold. "Too heavy for Hollow Men. Too regular. That's soldiers, girl. That's an army's vanguard — Vane's vanguard — and they've found the fen."

The curtain of light parted ahead of us. The Door waited. Behind, the drums of the unmaking world came on.
