## 30. Wren's True Name

The Door was smaller than I had imagined, which felt like a joke the world was playing on me. Twenty years of whispers, of Ilsa's fading ink, of a child drawing it on floorboards in a dead language — and here it stood at the heart of the Salt Fen like a kitchen door left unlatched. Grey stone, worn smooth by salt and time and whatever time meant out here, where nothing grew and nothing decayed and even grief seemed to have been pickled.

Wren put one small hand flat against it.

I had seen that gesture before. In my recovered memories — the ones I'd burned through like a spendthrift burning her inheritance — I had seen a woman with my mother's hands press her palm to a door just this way, saying goodbye, or hello, or something in between. The gesture lived somewhere below memory, in the marrow. That should have told me everything. I wasn't ready for it to tell me everything.

"Wren." My voice came out strange in all that white silence. "Maybe we shouldn't—"

But the air around the child had already begun to change.

* * *

You have to understand what Wren looked like before that moment, because after it, I couldn't hold the picture anymore, the way you can't hold water in a fist no matter how tightly you squeeze.

A mud-caked child of perhaps seven years, hair the color of river clay, eyes too old and too dark for the small face they occupied. A child who hadn't spoken except once, who had drawn doors on floors and hummed songs that made charms sing back. Who had followed me through Vess's rooftops and the Burnt Marches and the bickering councils of men who couldn't remember why they were afraid. Who slept curled against my back on cold nights with the particular weightlessness of someone not entirely committed to being solid.

That's what I mean. Even then, there were signs. Wren never got hungry in quite the right way. Never bled when scraped, only wept salt — actual salt, I realized now, standing in a fen made of it. And doors — doors behaved oddly around Wren. Opened onto wrong rooms. Stuck, then swung wide as if remembering an obligation.

Ilsa had said it weeks ago, laughing over her unfinished map: *"Doors like this child. Doors remember."*

I had thought she was being poetic. Ilsa was never poetic. She was a cartographer of nothing; precision was her religion.

* * *

It started with the light.

Not sunlight — the Fen had a grey, sourceless illumination, like the inside of a pearl. This light came from under Wren's skin. It began at the hand pressed against the Door and spread outward along small veins like dawn creeping across a map, filling in coastlines. I watched the light travel up one arm, across a shoulder, and I did what any sensible person does when confronted with the impossible: I stepped backward into empty air and nearly fell.

"Elodie."

Not Ashkaran. Not the wordless sounds, the humming, the pointing. Words. Wren's voice was high and clear and utterly unfamiliar, because I had never heard it used before, and yet the accent was one I knew in my teeth, from a vial, from a burning orchard, from a mother's mouth saying goodnight in a tongue the world had been made to forget.

"You're speaking," I said, brilliantly. Three years of surviving Vess on wit, and that was my contribution.

"I am remembering how," Wren said. "There is a difference."

The light reached the child's face then, and I saw — I will swear this to whoever asks, though no one will ask, though soon perhaps no one will remember me to ask — I saw the face begin to come apart. Not horribly. Not like flesh tearing. Like a painting drying in reverse, layers lifting, and beneath each layer another face, older faces, younger faces, a farmer's weathered cheeks, a bride's painted brow, a soldier mid-laugh, ten thousand faces layered like leaves in a book, none of them Wren's and all of them somehow, unmistakably, home.

"No," I said. "No, no — stop it, whatever you're doing, stop—"

Because I understood, then. All at once, the way understanding comes when it can do the most damage. The child at my door in chapter three of my ruin — the knock at the wrong hour. Mud-caked, mute, impossible. No one would hunt a child. No army searches for a beggar. No inquisitor tastes a stray's memories and finds anything worth reporting. If you were the last undrunk drop of a murdered country's soul, and Aurel Vane had swallowed everything else — every archive, every elder, every song, every name — where would you hide?

In the smallest vessel you could find. In a shape no one would look at twice. In a child too dirty and too quiet to be worth the trouble of erasing.

"You're not a child," I whispered.

The light flared, and for one terrible instant the face beneath all faces looked directly at me — vast, sorrowful, ancient beyond cruelty, gentle beyond sense — and then it folded itself back down, layer upon layer, until only Wren remained, small and shaking, tears cutting clean lines through the dust on those clay-colored cheeks.

"I don't know what I am," Wren said, and the voice cracked like a real child's voice, which was somehow worse. "I was everyone. Then I was almost no one. Then I was small enough to hide." Small hands pressed against the Door. "He drank us, Elodie. He drank us down to the lees. This is what's left. Me. A puddle he didn't see."

* * *

I want to be honest about what I felt, because honesty is the only currency I have left that isn't counterfeit.

My first feeling was terror. Pure, animal, self-preserving terror. Because if Wren was Ashkar's soul — the whole drowned nation compressed into a child's shape — then the Door needed more than stolen memories burned as kindling. It needed a key, and Ilsa's fading map had been explicit about that much: *a key of true memory freely given.* And what is a soul, if not the truest memory there is? What would opening the Door actually cost?

My second feeling came half a heartbeat later and annihilated the first.

Tenderness. Devastating, unmanageable tenderness — the kind that doesn't ask your permission. Because Wren was still standing there with a hand on the Door, still crying salt, still *small*, still looking up at me with the same expression from the night at my door: the expression of someone who has nowhere else in the world to knock. Twenty years of being a puddle in hiding. Twenty years of watching the world walk over the place where your body used to be, hearing people say *there was never anything there* — and the first thing this remnant of a nation did, the very first thing, was find its way to a memory-thief's garret and refuse to leave.

Why me? I hadn't asked. I should have asked.

"You knocked on my door," I said. "Three years ago. Before any of this. Why me?"

Wren wiped their face with their sleeve, leaving streaks, and gave me the ghost of the smile I'd spent three years learning to read.

"Because part of you was ours," Wren said simply. "The orchard. Your mother's voice. The promise at the door. You carried pieces of us inside you, Elodie, wrapped up and sold and resold, but *carried*. When everything else of us went silent—" the small hand pressed harder against the stone, "—you were the loudest thing left. I followed the sound."

Every charm I'd ever lifted. Every hour of joy I'd siphoned and bottled and sold in Vess's night market. I had thought myself a parasite all these years, a tick on the body of other people's happiness. And all the while, without knowing it, I had been a reliquary. A walking jar of preserved homeland. The loneliest woman in Vess had been, without knowing it, the most crowded.

I sat down in the salt. There didn't seem to be anywhere else to put myself.

* * *

Behind us, Sef lay where he had fallen at the threshold, buying us minutes with his hesitation and then his blood. Ilsa was further back, scratching at her last sheet of paper with a stylus that was already forgetting her, ink vanishing as fast as she wrote it, mapping our approach for whoever might come after — though who comes after the end of the world's best-kept secret, she hadn't specified. And ahead of us, somewhere past the Fen's grey horizon, Aurel Vane was coming. Ahead of his own army. He would feel the Door stirring the way a drunk feels a bottle in the next room.

So there was no time. There is never time. But I made some anyway, because Wren was crying, and I discovered I would burn down the arithmetic of the world before I hurried a crying child.

I crossed the salt and knelt beside them and did the only thing I knew how to do with another person's pain: I held out my hands and let them take what they needed.

Wren fell into me like a house falling. All that weight — a nation's worth — and yet so slight, so careful, the way you're careful when you've spent twenty years afraid of being noticed. They gripped my coat with both fists. Their tears soaked salt into my collar, and each one stung like a tiny accusation.

"I'm frightened," Wren said into my shoulder.

"You're a country," I said. "Frightened is reasonable."

A wet, hiccupping laugh. "Is that what you tell yourself?"

"Constantly."

We stayed like that while the grey light pulsed gently around us, the Door waiting with the patience of stone, which is to say forever. And I thought about the choice that was assembling itself in front of me, patient as the Door itself. To open it, I would need true memory, freely given. Mine were stolen goods — stolen, ironically, from myself, by brokers and fences and a border guard named Maro whose confession was still a fresh wound. But Wren — Wren was the truest memory in existence, undrunk, ungiven, hidden.

And I understood, with the tenderness still lodged in my chest like a splinter, that the Door would not take Wren by force. Nothing about Ashkar had ever been taken gently; that was the whole tragedy. The key would have to be offered. And offering it meant—

"Don't make that face," Wren said, pulling back to look at me. Those too-old eyes, and beneath them now, faintly, like a watermark, the vast sorrowful face of everyone. "I know what you're thinking."

"Do you?" My voice betrayed me. Voices are unreliable narrators.

"You're thinking you'll find another way. You're always finding another way — it's what thieves do." Wren touched my cheek with one small salt-crusted hand. "Elodie. Listen. Being poured out isn't the same as dying. I was everyone once. Do you know what it's like to be *everyone*? You can't love anyone. You can only love all, and loving all is like eating fog." The small face crumpled and reformed, determined. "But these three years. These three years I was *someone*. I was yours. I learned hunger — real hunger, for bread, not souls. I learned what it means when you check on me at night and pretend you weren't. I learned—" the voice wavered, "—I learned what a door sounds like when it opens for you instead of against you."

"Wren—"

"So when I give myself back," Wren said, "it won't only be a country coming home. It will be me, getting to be everyone again *knowing* what it is to be one. Nobody else in the history of anything gets to know both." A pause. The grey light steadied, gathered, began to pool in the small cupped hands like water finding its level. "And maybe — maybe — if the Door is kind, and doors have been kind to me, mostly — maybe what's left over, the last little bit, the drop that remembers being yours — maybe that gets to choose a shape afterward."

"Choose," I repeated. My throat had gone tight as a fist around a stolen coin.

"A shape," Wren agreed gravely, in the tone of a child explaining something perfectly obvious to a slow adult. "Everyone gets a body eventually. Even puddles."

And that's when I finally, properly, broke. Not loudly. Thieves don't cry loudly; we've trained ourselves out of it, because noise attracts Hollow Men and landlords alike. Just salt running down my face to join the fen's, two kinds of the same water meeting, indistinguishable. I held Wren's face in my hands — the clay-cheeked, too-old, mud-and-miracle face — and I kissed the top of that river-colored head the way my mother had kissed mine in an orchard that was currently ash, in a country that was currently nothing, twenty years and one lifetime ago.

"Whatever you become," I said, "whoever you choose to be — you'll know where my door is."

"It's not really your door," Wren said. "It's going to be everyone's door. That's rather the point."

"Sentimental," I managed.

"I learned from a professional."

* * *

Then the light rose.

It came off Wren's skin in slow ribbons, gold-white, impossibly warm in all that grey, and it did not stream toward the Door so much as *recognize* it — the way a river recognizes the sea, the way a word recognizes the mouth about to speak it. Wren stood straighter than I'd ever seen a child stand, arms slightly away from the body, letting go, and the face beneath all faces surfaced one last time and looked at me — not vast now, but grateful, and young, young in a way nothing that old should be able to look —

— and the first ribbon of light touched the Door, and the stone began, very softly, to sing.

It sang in Ashkaran. Of course it did. And I understood every word without learning any of them, because the language lived in the vials I'd burned and the bones I'd carried and the child I was losing, and it sang:

*We were here. We were always here. Remember us by living.*

The light poured. Wren diminished. Smaller and brighter and smaller, a candle burning down to its purpose, and I knelt in the salt with my hands out like a beggar at the feast, catching nothing, holding nothing, giving everything I had, which was attention — the oldest gift, the only one I'd never known how to steal.

The last of the light — one final drop, golden, trembling, stubborn as a weed through pavement — hovered where a child had stood.

And then it did not hover.

It came to me. It pressed itself, warm and small and insistent, against my chest, above my heart, exactly where a child curls when carried, and it stayed.

Behind us, far off across the fen, the grey horizon flickered — a smudge of movement, disciplined and wrong, the vanguard shimmer of an army, and ahead of it, moving faster than any army, a single figure walking with the unhurried confidence of a man who drinks countries.

Aurel Vane had arrived early.

The Door sang on, half-open, hungry for the rest of its kindling, and the warmth against my chest beat like a second heart, and I stood up out of the salt with empty hands and turned to meet him.
