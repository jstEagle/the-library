## 31. Vane Arrives Early

I have always been early. It is a vulgar habit, I know — the sort of thing that marks a man as having once been poor, or once been afraid, or both. Emperors arrive late. Kings arrive fashionably late. But I was neither of those things when I learned to count the minutes between my father's footsteps and my mother's screaming, and so I have never trusted a door I did not reach before it could be opened against me.

The Salt Fen arrived before I did, in a sense. Or rather, it failed to. That was the trouble with the place. Twenty years ago I had stood at its edge and watched a country the size of my ambition simply stop being there — not burned, not drowned, not salted in the old honest way, but *unwritten*, the way a clerk strikes through a ledger line he has decided never happened. The fen had rushed in to fill the absence like water filling a boot-print, and for twenty years it had done nothing but sit there, flat and gray and smelling of old iron, while every mapmaker in the world drew it as a smudge and every archive recorded that no such smudge required explanation.

Now the smudge was humming.

I felt it three days out from Vess, in my teeth, the way you feel a storm or a lie. By the time my vanguard crested the last ridge of the Burnt Marches, I knew what it was: something in the fen was *remembering itself*. And there is only one thing in this world that can make dead country remember, and she was standing at its edge with a child's hand in hers and a disgraced cartographer at her shoulder, looking exactly as tired and stubborn as the reports said.

I left the army at the ridge. Armies are for countries that still exist. What remained of this negotiation required only me, my voice, and the considerable inventory of other people's lives I carry behind my eyes.

You should understand something about me, since history will inevitably get it wrong — history being, these past two decades, less a record than a garden I prune personally. They will say I destroyed Ashkar out of cruelty. Cruelty is so *inefficient*. No. Ashkar died because it was the last country on earth whose memory I could not edit. Every other nation, I had rewritten gently, over years — a treaty amended here, a famine relocated there, a king's bastardy quietly promoted to legitimacy. But Ashkar's people remembered themselves the way stone remembers weight. Their songs were load-bearing. So I did what any careful man does with a wall that cannot be moved around: I drank it.

All of it. Every harvest song, every grudge, every grandmother's recipe for honey-bread, every name of every child born under their crooked little kings. I took a country into myself the way a man takes a draught of wine, and I have been drunk on it for twenty years. Do you know what it is to remember an entire nation? To wake some mornings tasting salt bread you never baked, grieving mothers you never met, loving a language you outlawed? It is magnificent. It is unbearable. It is the closest any man has ever come to being a god, and I would do it again before you finished asking.

But gods, I have learned, can be unmade by their own relics. And walking toward me across the gray flats was a relic wearing mud-caked boots.

* * *

Elodie Oyelaran. I knew her file the way I knew everything now — which is to say, completely and uselessly. A memory-thief, one of the small vermin who survived my prohibition by trading in the one commodity I hadn't thought to nationalize. Three years in Vess, no recorded past before them, which interested me more than her crimes. People with no past are either liars or wounds, and wounds are useful.

She stopped ten paces away. Good instincts. Close enough to talk, far enough to run. The child did not stop at all — walked right up to the invisible line where my presence began to press and stood there staring up at me with eyes the color of the fen, and I confess the sensation was deeply unpleasant. Being looked at by that child was like being audited by your own conscience, if your conscience had been raised by wolves and spoke a dead tongue.

"You're shorter than the statues," she said. In Vessian. Flat. Unimpressed.

"There are statues?"

"There were," said the cartographer, Ilsa, from behind. "In Ashkar. You tore most of them down yourself, the week after."

I smiled at her, because it costs nothing and unsettles people who expect rage. "Madam Ilsa. Your maps were better before the revision. I mean that as a compliment."

"Take your compliment and drown in your fen."

Charming. I turned back to the thief. Up close she had the look I remembered from the border guards' reports — the ones I'd drunk along with everything else — the look of a girl who had been carried out of a burning orchard and had decided, with the particular ferocity of children, never to be carried anywhere again.

"You know what I am," I said. "So let us skip the part where you pretend otherwise and I pretend to be offended. I've come to make you an offer, and I'd like to make it before my army arrives and makes everything louder."

"I don't bargain with men who eat countries."

"A curious objection from a woman who eats evenings." I let that land. Her jaw tightened; good, she was listening. "Yes, I know your trade. Don't blush — theft is only commerce conducted without paperwork, and I have abolished most of the paperwork. Here is my offer, and I'll make it only once, because I am early, but I am not patient."

I reached into the place where I keep what I have taken. It is not a place, precisely. It is more like a cellar with no floor, and I went down into it and came back up holding her.

Her childhood. All of it. The orchard, the mother with flour on her wrists, the festival with the paper lanterns and the boy who stepped on her foot during the reel, the promise made at a door — I had all of it, had swallowed it whole with the rest of Ashkar, and it sat inside me like a jewel in a fat man's pocket, barely noticed, occasionally admired.

I held it out the only way I could: in words, in the shape of knowing. I watched her face as I described her sixth birthday — the honey-bread, the crooked little crown her father made from willow twigs, the word her mother used for *darling* that exists in no living tongue. I watched the color leave her and come back wrong.

"That's mine," she whispered.

"It is," I agreed pleasantly. "It has been mine for twenty years, along with several million other somethings. Which brings us to terms." I gestured at the fen, at the faint wrongness in the air where a Door was deciding whether to exist. "Walk away. Take the child — I don't even want him, he's a husk, a rind, the drop I couldn't reach — take the mapmaker, take whatever scraps of yourself you've recovered, and go live somewhere the war won't find you. In exchange, I give you back everything. Not fragments. Not vials. The whole of it. Your mother's voice. Your name as it was said before anyone taught you to flinch at it. Every year they erased, restored, entire, free of charge, because I am feeling generous and because generosity frightens people more than threats."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I drink the Door before it opens, the way I drank everything else, and you will spend the rest of your life almost-remembering, which I'm told is worse than forgetting. Also my army arrives within the day, and they are less conversational than I am."

The child made a sound then. Small. Not a word — or not a word in any language I had bothered to keep. He took her hand.

And here is the thing I had not planned for, the small ugly fact I will admit to no one but this page: looking at that child's hand in hers, I felt something shift in the cellar of myself. Something knocking. Something that had been patient for twenty years and was patient no longer.

I kept my smile fixed. I am very good at that. But I made the offer again, softer, the way you speak to a horse you don't want spooked, and I waited for her answer with a god's composure and a man's racing heart.

* * *

He knew my birthday.

That was the thought that kept circling as Vane's voice wound down and the fen wind pushed cold fingers down my collar. Not the armies, not the threat, not even the impossible fact of the usurper standing alone on the flats like a man come to collect a debt — but the birthday. The willow-twig crown. The word Mama used, the one I'd spent three years trying to buy back one stolen charm at a time, the word that lived in the bottom of the orchard vial like sediment.

He'd said it correctly. Accent and all.

That was the horror of Aurel Vane, I realized, watching him smile his reasonable smile: he wasn't lying. Everything he'd offered was real. Somewhere behind those pleasant gray eyes, my mother's voice was sitting in the dark with a million other voices, alive, preserved, *kept* — the way a miser keeps coins he never spends. He hadn't eaten Ashkar. He'd hoarded it. And now he stood there offering to open the vault, and the terrible thing, the shameful thing, was that my feet wanted to say yes.

Three years. Three years of other people's happiness sold by the gram, of waking up not knowing why my hands scarred the way they did, of loving Wren with a tenderness I couldn't source and didn't dare examine. And here was a man offering to hand me the missing years like a merchant handing back a stolen purse — *no charge, madam, apologies for the inconvenience* — and all it would cost was everything else.

Because that was the shape of it, wasn't it. Walk away. Leave the Door shut. Let the fen stay a smudge on the map forever, let Wren stay a husk, let a whole country stay dead so that one girl could have her mama's voice back. My mother. Not everyone's mother. Just mine.

Vane watched me weigh it. He was good at waiting, I'd give him that. He had the patience of a man who had already rewritten the ending and was just letting the actors catch up.

"You hesitate," he said kindly. "That's wisdom. Most people rush toward their own past like children toward a well. Take a moment. Count what it's worth."

I counted. That was the joke of it — counting was my trade. I knew to the copper what a memory fetched in the night market. A soldier's happiest hour: four silver. A bride's first morning: six. And my whole childhood, entire, the only complete set in existence, held by the man who had murdered the country it belonged to?

Priceless. Literally. There was no price. That was how I knew it was a trap — not because the offer was false, but because anything truly priceless can't be traded, only given, and Vane doesn't give. He lends. At interest. I'd seen his kind of generosity before, in Maro's ledger books, in the fine print of every fence in Vess: *what is given freely can be taken back.*

Wren's hand tightened in mine. Small, muddy, certain.

"He's afraid," Wren said. Not in Vessian. In Ashkaran — and I understood it the way you understand your own heartbeat, without translation, without effort, the words landing somewhere older than learning. "Can't you hear it? Under the voice. He's been hearing knocking for three days and he came early because he's afraid of doors."

Vane's smile flickered. Just at the corner. Just for a breath.

"The child talks too much," he said, "for someone with no tongue's worth of soul left in him."

"He talks enough," I said. And I found, to my own surprise, that my feet had stopped wanting anything at all. The wanting had drained out of me like water through fen-mud, leaving something harder underneath. "Here's my counter-offer, usurper. You give me back nothing. I take nothing from you. I'm going to open that Door, and everything you swallowed for twenty years is going to walk out of you singing, and you're going to be left standing here holding an empty vault and wondering who you were before you started stealing other people's yesterdays to fill the hole where yours should be."

Something moved behind his eyes then — and it wasn't anger. Anger I could have handled. It was recognition, brief and appalled, the look of a man hearing his own diagnosis read aloud by a stranger in the street.

Behind him, far off on the ridge, his army had begun to descend. Banners I half-recognized from histories that had been rewritten twice. Drums like a slow heartbeat.

Ahead of us, the air over the fen shivered, parted, and showed — just for a moment, just to those who knew how to look — the grain of an enormous door, waiting.

Vane straightened his coat. Composed himself the way only a man who has practiced composure for twenty years can.

"So be it," he said, and his voice had gone quiet, almost gentle, which frightened me far more than the shouting would have. "Then we do this the old way. The way I did everything else."

He rolled up his sleeves, and I saw, crawling up his forearms like ink rising through water, the first shadows of everything he had ever drunk — a harvest song, a grandmother's grudge, a child's name — rising to the surface, ready to be spent.

"Stand behind me," I told Wren, "and whatever happens, don't let go."
