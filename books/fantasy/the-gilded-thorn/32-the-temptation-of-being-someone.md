## 32. The Temptation of Being Someone

Aurel Vane did not arrive with trumpets. I want you to understand that. Every story I'd ever lifted from a dying soldier had the tyrant arriving with drums and banners and a horizon of spears, and I had always thought, *how exhausting for him*, all that theater just to walk into a room.

Vane walked into the Fen the way a man walks into his own kitchen.

He came out of the curtain of nothing where Wren had parted the air, and the air let him, because the air didn't know yet what he was—or maybe because it knew too well. He was smaller than the coins bore his face. Smaller than the fear of him. A broad man going soft at the jaw, dressed like a merchant who had done well in grain, with the kind of face you forget while you're looking at it. That, I would learn later, was not an accident. You cannot drink the memory of a nation without learning a few tricks about faces.

Ilsa made a sound beside me that wasn't quite a word. Her hand went to her satchel—the unfinished map, the ink that faded as she wrote it—and stopped, as if she'd remembered there was nothing left in Vess's whole arsenal worth reaching for.

Wren stepped in front of me.

Small shoulders. Mud-colored coat. The child who had knocked at my door at the wrong hour with ash on their boots and a dead language in their mouth, and now stood between me and the most dangerous man alive like a fence post against a flood.

"Elodie Oyelaran," Vane said.

My name. My actual name, spoken aloud by someone other than me or Maro, which in three years of living in Vess had happened approximately never. I kept a list of people who knew it. The list had one entry, and Maro had sold me to the Hollow Men, so the list was functionally a eulogy.

"You have me at a disadvantage," I said. "You know my name and I only know your reputation."

"My reputation." He smiled, and the smile had a craftsman's pride in it. "Yes. They gave me teeth in the telling, didn't they? Good. Fear is cheaper than armies."

"Then you'll be relieved to hear it isn't working."

It was working. My hands were doing something embarrassing inside my sleeves, and Ilsa's breathing had gone thin, and Wren—Wren was humming. Low. In Ashkaran. The song from the orchard memory, the festival one, the one I'd drunk back in fragments outside the burnt marches: *lanterns on the water, a woman's voice singing the verses wrong on purpose because the right way was sad.* The Fen itself seemed to lean toward the sound.

Vane heard it too. Something moved behind his eyes—not rage. Hunger. The specific, patient hunger of a man who has tasted something once and has spent twenty years arranging the world to taste it again.

"You've been feeding it," he said softly. "Memories. True ones. Ashkaran ones." He looked at Wren the way Maro looked at an unopened strongbox. "Do you know what this child is?"

"I know what they are," I said.

"Do you?" He tilted his head, genuinely curious, and that was somehow worse than menace. "Because I don't think you do. I don't think you know what *you* are. Which is why I've come ahead of my army, Elodie. Alone." He spread his empty hands. "To make you an offer no army could enforce."

* * *

Here is what you should know about me before I tell you what happened next, because I intend to be honest even though honesty is a terrible habit for a thief.

Three years ago I arrived in Vess with nothing. Not poverty—poverty is a thing you can point at. Nothing. No mother's face. No first word. No reason I flinched at the smell of smoke or woke some nights with salt on my tongue and grief in my chest shaped exactly like a country. Maro found me on the docks holding a stolen purse I had no memory of stealing, and he looked at me the way you look at a locked door with no keyhole, and he said, "Girl, you're either the luckiest thief in Vess or the emptiest, and the two pay the same."

I built a life out of other people's leftovers. A soldier's wedding night. A baker's daughter learning to whistle. An old woman's last summer, sold to her grandson so he'd have something of her after she went. I wore their joys like borrowed coats, and every night I took them off and hung them up and stood there naked in my own skull, in the one room in Vess that had never been furnished, and I told myself that was freedom.

It was not freedom. It was a vacancy advertised as one.

So when Aurel Vane said *offer*, I already knew, in the marrow of me, what he had. And knowing it did not save me. That's the part I need you to understand. Knowing it did not save me at all.

* * *

"The Door wants true memory freely given," Vane said. We had drifted—I couldn't say how—into walking, the four of us across the white nothing of the Fen, and the nothing permitted us the way a stage permits actors. "That's its cruelty and its elegance. Stolen goods won't turn the lock. It knows the difference; everything here knows the difference." He glanced at Wren with something almost like respect. "But yours aren't stolen anymore, are they? You bought some back. You drank them down in the marches. Freely taken, freely held."

"How do you know that?"

"Because I can feel it." He touched his own sternum, casual as a man noting indigestion. "Every time you open one of those vials, girl, I feel it in my teeth. I drank Ashkar, do you understand? All of it. Every song, every harvest, every grudge and christening and lie told behind the temple. Twenty years ago I stood where you're standing and I swallowed a country whole, and I have been *full* ever since. Untouchable. Armies march at me and my generals remember losing battles we won. Assassins reach my bedchamber and forget why they came by morning." His voice dropped, and beneath the merchant's warmth I heard it—the vastness underneath, the ocean under the floorboards. "But the Door stirring wakes the dregs. Things rise. And I have begun, lately, to feel *hungry* again."

Wren's humming stopped.

"And so," Vane said, "the offer."

He reached into his coat and produced—of course—a vial. Of course. What else does a monster carry, but inventory? It was small, dark glass, stoppered with wax the color of dried blood, and it caught the lightless light of the Fen and threw back nothing.

"This is one hour," he said. "One hour of a burning orchard, the night Ashkar died. You've seen pieces of it. But this—" he turned it slowly, admiringly, "—this is the hour with your mother's face in it. Clear. Whole. Unbought and unbartered, given to me freely by a man who wanted his guilt erased more than he wanted anything else in the world. Maro drove the cart, Elodie. Maro watched them load you onto it. Maro sold me your childhood by the crate."

The Fen went very quiet, the way snow makes fields quiet.

"I know about Maro," I said. My voice came out level, which I counted as the professional triumph of my life.

"You know he sold your location to my inquisitors. You don't know the half. But that's another vial, and I'm not selling today." He held the orchard hour out to me, arm extended, patient as weather. "Here is my offer. Walk away. Turn around, take the child—no, keep the child, I don't even want the child if the Door stays shut—and come to Cael Vane, my city, and I will give you all of it. Every vial. Every year. Your mother's voice. Your own first steps. The name you had before Oyelaran, the street you played in, the festival where you got lost and found, the promise that was made to you at a door—you've seen that fragment, haven't you? You've been dreaming it."

I had. For three nights running since the marches. A door painted blue. Someone kneeling to look me in the eye. A voice saying words I couldn't hold on waking, gone like water through fingers, leaving only the shape of the hands.

"Every memory," Vane said, "that was ever taken from you. Restored. Whole. You will wake up tomorrow and know exactly who you are, and where you came from, and whose daughter you were, and you will *belong* somewhere, finally, completely, for the rest of your life. Three years of being nobody, ended in an afternoon." He smiled again, gently. "All it costs is a door staying shut."

* * *

I want to tell you I laughed in his face. I want to tell you I thought of Wren, of Ilsa's fading map, of Sef falling at the threshold with Hollow Men's blades in him, buying me minutes with his hesitation, his purchased past turned weapon against the men who'd sold it to him. I want to tell you the refusal was instant.

It was not instant.

Here is what happened instead: I stood on the white nothing where a country used to be, and I did arithmetic.

Not moral arithmetic. Thief's arithmetic. Three years, I thought. Three years of sleeping alone above a shop that smelled of tallow and other people's happiness. Three years of wearing joy like a coat and taking it off every night. Three years of being the emptiest thief in Vess, the woman with no face of her mother, no grave to visit, no birthday, no home street, no first snow, nothing, nothing, nothing—and here was a man offering me the whole inventory, free, no theft required, no lifting, no risk, no dying soldier's last hour pried loose with my thumb against his temple. Just walk away. Just let the door stay shut. Let the country stay dead—it was already dead, it had been dead twenty years, what difference did one more silence make?

My mother's face. I had seen it in fragments, in the orchard vial, a smear of motion and firelight, and I had wept over a *smear*. And he had the whole of it. Clear. Whole.

"Elodie," Ilsa said. Warning in it. Fading-map warning, the voice of a woman who had charted places that stopped existing and knew exactly how it happens: not with a roar, but with a decision, one at a time, each reasonable.

"I'm listening," I said, and hated myself even as I said it, and kept standing there anyway.

"That's all any of us ever needed to do," Vane said warmly. "Listen. History is full of monsters, they say. History is mostly full of people who listened to a reasonable offer."

And God help me, it was reasonable. That's the trap no one warns you about. Evil in the songs arrives ugly and demands your soul outright, and you can spit at it with a clean conscience. But this evil arrived soft-spoken in grain-merchant's clothes and offered you *yourself*, and asked only that you abandon strangers. And the strangers were asking you to burn your own childhood as kindling. Who wouldn't hesitate? Who wouldn't stand on the corpse of a country and think: *they're dead either way, but I could live*?

I reached out.

I am not going to pretend otherwise. My hand came up. My fingers were six inches from the vial—six inches from my mother's whole face, from my own name, from three years of hunger answered—and I swear to you I could already feel it, the weight of being someone, the furniture arriving in the empty room, the coats that fit—

Small, mud-caked fingers closed around mine.

* * *

Wren didn't pull my hand back. That matters. A hero would have pulled it back, or struck the vial away, or said something wise. Wren just held my hand, the way they had on the rooftops of Vess when doors opened onto wrong rooms, the way children hold hands: not to stop you, but to be *with* you, wherever your hand was going.

And then they spoke. One word. Their second word ever, and both of them, I would later understand, had been aimed at me like arrows.

"*Neshari.*"

Home.

But this time I heard all the way down into it. Because the language lived in my stolen memories—that was the revelation on the road, the proof that my past was the map—and the word unfolded as it landed. *Neshari* didn't just mean home. It meant *the place that holds you even when you hold nothing*. It meant the orchard and the blue door and the lanterns on the water and the woman singing the verses wrong. It meant: *this*. This white nothing, this Fen, this curtain of absence where a kingdom used to be. Wren was standing in the wound where home had been and calling *this* home—not the restoration, not the promise, the absence itself. Loving it ruined. Refusing to let it be traded.

The vial in Vane's hand threw back no light.

My mother's face was in it. I knew that. I would know it until I died, that six inches of distance between me and my mother's whole face, and I knew something else too, then, standing in the cold of the Fen with a child's hand around my fingers:

If I took it—if I walked away with my arms full of myself—every memory he gave me would be a purchase. Bought with a country's death. And I had spent three years learning what bought things were worth: nothing. Less than nothing. A charm grows cold. A purchased belonging would grow cold too, and I would wake one morning in Cael Vane with a mother's face in my head and no way to look her in the eye, because I would know—I would always, always know—what I'd let stay dead to get her.

Belonging bought with a country's death isn't belonging. It's just a nicer cell.

My hand came down. Empty.

"No," I said.

Just that. No speech. No curse. Vane deserved better oratory, probably; tyrants live on drama. But all I had was a thief's flat little syllable, and it turned out that was enough, because his face changed—the warmth draining out of it like water out of a cracked bowl, and underneath, for just a moment, I saw what twenty years of fullness had left: something enormous and starving, looking out through a merchant's eyes at the one thing it could not digest.

"You would rather be empty," he said, and there was real bewilderment in it. Real offense. "Than be someone."

"I'd rather be *me*," I said. "Even this version. Even the draft." I squeezed Wren's hand. "Besides. You should know something about thieves, my lord. We recognize our own kind. And you—" I nodded at the vial, at his coat, at the whole soft-spoken architecture of him, "—you're the biggest fence in history. You stole a country and you've been trying to sell it ever since. To yourself."

For one heartbeat, I thought he might laugh.

Then the Fen shuddered, and the curtain of air far behind him rippled black, and I felt—everyone felt—the ground-bass hum of the Last Door waking, hungry, waiting, wanting what only I could give it. Every recovered memory. Forever.

Vane's hand closed around the orchard vial, and his voice, when it came, was no longer a merchant's at all.

"Then we do this the old way," he said. "Child. Step aside."

Behind him, out of the black ripple, the Hollow Men began to arrive.
