## 35. The Country Remembers Itself

*—Mira Vosk, baker, waking mid-sentence*

I was saying something to my husband when the world came back.

That's not how I'd have put it at first, of course. At first I thought I was having a stroke, because there is no other polite explanation for standing in your own kitchen with flour on your hands and a sentence in your mouth — *Tomas, don't you dare feed that dog from the table, he'll never learn his manners and neither will you* — and realizing, with the particular vertigo of a woman whose feet have been kicked out from under her by history itself, that the dog has been dead for twenty years, and so has Tomas, and so, apparently, has my entire country.

The bread was in the oven. I knew that the way I knew my own name, which was suddenly, alarmingly, thoroughly: Mira Vosk, born under the pear tree on Coriander Lane, married at seventeen, widowed at thirty-one when the soldiers came over the hills with their grey banners and their grey faces and their grey, hungry nothing where their hearts should have been.

Except no one had come over the hills. That's the thing. That's the thing I still can't say out loud without my voice going strange. No one ever came. There were never any soldiers. There was only one morning when Ashkar wasn't, when every road led somewhere else and every song lost its middle verse and we all went on living — living! — inside the shape of an absence, baking bread for neighbors who had never existed, mourning children we could not name, and calling it Tuesday.

Twenty years of Tuesdays.

And then a girl opened a door that wasn't a door, and here I am, mid-sentence, holding a loaf that smells like grief and cardamom, sobbing into dough while a dead man's dog-shaped hole in my heart fills up so fast it cracks me down the middle like a bad kiln.

They tell me there was a stranger at the Door. A young woman, hollow-eyed, hands empty. They say she stood aside as we all came through, and said nothing, and looked at us the way you look at a house you once lived in.

I hope someone fed her. In Ashkar we fed everyone. It's the first thing I remembered and it's the first thing I intend to do about this whole impossible situation.

* * *

*—Davor Ilm, ferryman, waking mid-song*

Here's what nobody warns you about being unwritten: you keep singing.

Or — I kept singing. The same eight bars, over and over, twenty years, a boat-song with no boat and a river with no river, humming it into my ale and my work and my wife's hair until she asked me — kindly, the way you ask a madman if he wants soup — what tune that was supposed to be.

"I don't know," I told her, and that was true. "It doesn't end," I told her, and that was truer.

It ends. Oh, friends, it ends. It ends with a high note that cracked in my throat the instant it returned to me, because the last line is a woman's name — Liss — and the song is about drowning, and Liss drowned, or rather Liss was *drowned from existence*, which turns out to be a different thing entirely, and when the Door opened and the river came back, so did she, standing on the far bank with her sleeves rolled up, looking annoyed, the way she always looked when I was late.

I have been late twenty years. She hasn't aged a day. I have questions for whatever gods arranged this, but mostly I have the rest of the song, and I sang it across the water with my voice breaking on every third word, and she sang back, and the river between us ran the exact green-brown of home.

There was a stranger at the Door, they say. A girl who gave something up. I don't know her story yet — the stories are still arriving, tumbling through the pass like pilgrims, each one more impossible than the last — but I know this: whoever she is, whatever she paid, there should be a song about her too. And I'm the man to write it, since apparently I'm also the man who forgot an entire country and hummed its ghost for two decades without noticing anything wrong.

My wife-from-the-absence-years is taking the news of Liss remarkably well. She says she always knew I was humming at somebody.

Women. Honestly.

* * *

*—Sister Anthe of the Lantern Order, waking mid-grief*

Grief is a strange country to wake up in, and stranger still to wake up *from*.

For twenty years I tended a grave with no name on it. This did not strike anyone in Vess as odd — half the graves in the paupers' field had no names — but I tended it anyway, every seventh day, with oil and salt, because my hands insisted even when my mind had nothing to offer them. My hands are older than my mind. My hands remembered everything.

His name was Pell. My son. He was six when the world folded, and he was six when it unfolded, standing exactly where the folding left him, in the courtyard of our house on the Fen-road, mid-throw, a wooden bird still in his hand, frozen in the amber of a moment that lasted two decades.

He threw the bird. It landed in the grass. Then he saw me and dropped everything else.

I am sixty-one years old. He is still six. The theologians among us — and we have suddenly acquired a great many theologians, most of them previously carpenters — are going to be arguing about the metaphysics of this for a century. I don't care. I held him. He fit exactly where my arms remembered him fitting, because my arms, unlike my mind, had never agreed to forget.

They say the girl at the Door had empty hands. They say she stepped aside before any of us came through, as though she didn't belong, as though she hadn't just done the single largest act of love in recorded history.

I lit a lantern for her. We light them for the unnamed dead, but I think in her case it's for the unnamed living, which is worse and braver. If she comes to the temple, I will feed her. If she doesn't come to the temple, I will find her. The Lantern Order does not abandon the bereaved, and whatever she is grieving — and mark me, she is grieving; I know the posture; I've worn it twenty years — she should not grieve alone in a country full of people who owe her everything and don't even know her name.

We will learn her name. Someone here must remember her face. Someone must.

* * *

*—Old Haskel, who remembers more than is convenient*

I'll tell you what they won't tell you, because everyone else is busy weeping and singing and discovering their long-lost relatives, which is all very fine but somebody has to notice the awkward parts.

I was near the Door when it opened. Old Haskel, keeper of the eastern watch-post, which for twenty years was a watch-post over nothing, which suited me fine, since watching nothing requires no early mornings.

The air parted like a curtain — I'll grant the rumors that much — and through it came a nation. Not an army, not a parade. A *nation*: farmers blinking at their own fields, priests falling to their knees mid-prayer, a woman chasing a goose that had opinions about resurrection. And at the threshold, stepping back, standing aside, there she was. The stranger. Young, dark-haired, scar on her palm, eyes like a woman who has recently read her own ending.

Now. Here's the awkward part, the part I noticed because noticing things is my entire job and I'm very good at it.

She looked at us the way a beggar looks through a bakery window.

Not with envy. With *hunger*, yes, but with the settled kind, the kind that has already concluded the bread isn't for sale. She watched a mother find her son. She watched the ferryman sing to his dead-alive wife. She watched Sister Anthe hold that six-year-old boy, and I swear on my post and the nothing it guarded, the girl smiled, and it was the worst smile I have ever seen on a human face, because it was completely genuine and completely finished.

Then she turned to go, and a child caught her hand.

A small child, mud-colored, silent — I'd seen it earlier, or thought I had, hovering at the edge of the crowd like a draft through a door. When it touched her, the whole horizon shivered, just slightly, like a struck bell deciding whether to ring.

I've seen a lot of strange things from my watch-post. Mostly nothing, which is stranger than people admit. But I know an omen when I stand next to one.

Mark it well: the country remembers itself, but the country doesn't remember *her*. Every face in that crowd is a face someone here knows. Hers, nobody knows. And yet the child — the child clings to her like she's the only solid thing in a newly rebuilt world.

Either that girl belongs here more than any of us, or she traded her belonging away to buy ours, and either way, somebody had better go after her before she walks off the edge of her own miracle.

I would go myself, but I'm eighty-three and my knees remember twenty years of nothing all too well.

Someone younger. Someone quick. Go.

* * *

*—Liss, ferryman's wife, briefly, because someone has to have the last word*

Davor is writing the song already. Of course he is. Twenty years gone and the first thing he does is compose.

But I was on the bank when the stranger passed us on the road east, the child on her shoulders, both of them looking like the last two leaves of autumn refusing to fall. And I called out — because in Ashkar we call out, we have never once in our history let a person walk past unremarked — and I shouted the old blessing, the one for travelers and returning souls:

"May the road remember you!"

And do you know what she did?

She stopped. Just for a breath. And she said, in perfect Ashkaran, in an accent from the orchard country, the accent of my own mother —

"Thank you."

Two words. That's all I got. But I'll tell you this for nothing, ferryman, write it in your song: nobody learns a dead language in two words. She didn't learn it. She *remembered* it, the way my hands remembered my son's weight, the way the old man remembers everything inconvenient.

She's one of ours. Whoever she thinks she is now, however empty her hands — she's one of ours, walking out of her own homecoming.

So the song needs a second verse, husband. Make it a good one.

And someone — *someone* — had better bring her back before the door of her own story closes behind her.
