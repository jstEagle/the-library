## 37. Wren Chooses a Shape

Here is what nobody tells you about being a country: it is very heavy, and there are no pockets.

I know this because I was one. For twenty years I was Ashkar — or the last crumb of Ashkar, the bit that didn't get swallowed, the drop of soup left in the pot after the giant finished drinking. A soul doesn't die when its country is unwritten. It just gets very small and very patient, and it learns to wait inside shapes no one would think to look in.

A child, for instance. Nobody hunts a child. Children are mud and noise and hunger; they are beneath notice, which is the safest place in any kingdom. So I folded myself into a child's shape and walked out of the nothing where my country used to be, and I walked until my feet found a door in a city called Vess, which smelled of fish and fear and other people's memories.

The door belonged to Elodie Oyelaran.

I want to be honest about something, because honesty is the only currency I have left now that I'm mostly a girl again: I did not choose her door by accident. Souls don't have accidents. I had been circling her for years the way a moth circles a lamp it can't remember wanting. She carried pieces of me inside her — stolen, bottled, sold, but mine — and every time she lifted a memory from some dying soldier or grieving widow, she also brushed against the great wound underneath all wounds, the place where Ashkar had been cut out of the world. She couldn't feel it. But I could. From across the city, across the years, I felt her hands moving through the dark like someone feeling along a wall for a door they'd forgotten was there.

So I knocked at the wrong hour. And she opened it, because she was lonely in the specific way that people who have sold their own pasts are lonely — not missing anyone, just missing *something*, an itch with no location.

She took me in. That is the whole miracle, if you want it in one sentence. Everything else — the Hollow Men, the running, the rooftops of Vess bending around me like wet paper, the Salt Fen, the Door, the burning of everything she had clawed back for herself — all of that grew out of one tired thief looking down at a mud-caked child who wouldn't leave, and deciding, against every rule she had ever made, to let him stay.

She thought she was saving me.

Souls don't need saving. Souls need *witnesses*. And she witnessed me before she knew what I was — fed me, scolded me, slept curled around me like I was something worth guarding. By the time she learned I was a kingdom wearing a boy, it was too late for her to be sensible about it. Love had already gotten into her the way water gets into stone: slowly, invisibly, and then all at once, cracking her open.

* * *

Now let me tell you what happened on the other side of the Door, because Elodie can't. She gave too much of herself to open it; the woman who walked through remembers Vess only as a story someone told her once, a story about a clever, sad thief. She doesn't remember me as the child at her door. She remembers me the way you remember weather from a dream.

But I remember. I remember everything now, because I am everything again.

When the Door opened, I didn't walk through it. I *came apart* through it — gently, the way a knot comes undone when you finally find the right thread. Twenty years of being small ended all at once. I poured backward into the land like rain finding a dry riverbed. I felt orchards unburn. I felt rivers remember their names. I felt four hundred thousand people wake mid-breath, mid-song, mid-argument, mid-grief, each of them carrying a hole in themselves shaped exactly like home, and I poured myself into every hole at once, and the holes closed, and they wept without knowing why, and then they knew why, and they wept harder.

That part was good. That part was the best thing that has ever happened to anything.

But here is the trouble with being a whole country again: a country is not a person. A country is a chorus, and a chorus cannot hold your hand when you're frightened. For one long moment after the restoration — a moment that lasted, by the clocks of Ashkar, about nine days — I was everywhere. I was in the wheat and the wells and the wedding songs. I was in the mouths of grandmothers saying words they hadn't said since before the world forgot them. I was vast and complete and utterly alone, because completeness is its own kind of solitude. Nothing can touch you when you're everything.

And in the middle of all that vastness, one small point kept glowing. One thread of me had never gone back into the weave. One piece had stayed a child — stayed *Wren* — because that piece had spent three years learning what it was to be held, and fed, and scolded, and loved by a specific tired woman with a scar on her palm shaped like a key.

The rest of me was home. That piece wanted a mother.

* * *

The elders found her first, of course. They always do; it's their function. Old Mother Sefa — who woke mid-prayer with the name of every god back in her mouth — came down from the high terraces of New Ashkar (which is old Ashkar, which is the only Ashkar, though the maps argue) and stood at the edge of the Door-meadow where the stranger was standing, empty-handed, blinking at a country she didn't recognize and that didn't quite recognize her either.

"She has her face," Sefa said to the others gathered behind her. "Look at her face. That's an Oyelaran face if I've ever seen one. There were Oyelaran orcharders in the Vale of Nine Springs since before the walls."

"But she doesn't know us," said a younger man, a ferryman named Doss whose hands still remembered rope even though his mind had been reassembled nine days ago. "She looks at us the way you look at a word you almost remember."

"Then we will teach her," Sefa said, in the tone of a woman who had buried a husband and two sons in the Unwriting and had decided, somewhere in the dark, that grief would not get the last word. "We lost twenty years. We are not losing one more person to pride."

They went to her the way you approach a wild bird. Slowly. With bread.

I watched all of this from everywhere, the way you do when you're a country. I watched them offer her a house — the old Oyelaran house in the Vale, actually, which had stood empty and unfallen all this time because I had kept it standing, the way you keep a room ready for someone who might come back. I watched her walk its floors and touch its doorframe and say, in careful, newly-learned Ashkaran, "Was this mine?" And I watched Sefa answer, "It was waiting for someone. Perhaps it was waiting for you."

Elodie — no, she wasn't Elodie anymore, she'd left that name in Vess like a coat she'd outgrown; the elders called her *the Key-bearer*, and she flinched at it every time — Elodie-the-Key-bearer lived in the house and planted things and learned the language one word at a time, and every night she stood at her window looking east toward nothing in particular, homesick for a home she couldn't name.

Homesick. In her own homeland. Because the memories she'd traded to open the Door had taken her childhood with them, and belonging without memory is just geography.

I know. I was the geography. It stung.

* * *

On the ninth day, the chorus of me held a debate.

You should understand that countries don't debate the way people do. There are no chambers, no raised hands. When I deliberated with myself, the wheat leaned one way and the rivers leaned another, and the old stones under the temple hummed arguments in frequencies only dogs could hear, and the dead — oh yes, the dead came back with the land, all the ones the Unwriting had swallowed, returned to their graves or their places at tables as if no time had passed — the dead weighed in too, softly, the way the dead do.

The question was simple: what should become of the piece of me that remained a child?

One faction — the rivers, mostly, and the mountains, who have always been traditionalists — argued that the piece should return to the whole. Completeness. Wholeness. A country should not go about with a loose thread hanging off it. What use is a fragment? A fragment is a wound that hasn't finished happening.

Another faction — the orchards, the kitchens, the small warm rooms of the world — argued otherwise. They pointed out, with the smugness available only to orchards, that the fragment had done what the whole could not: it had been loved individually. The whole of Ashkar had been mourned by millions, but the child had been *fed soup* by one woman. There are things a chorus cannot learn.

And the dead — the dead remembered something the living had forgotten, because the dead have the best view of how stories end. They remembered that the Key-bearer had given up every memory of belonging so that the rest of us could belong. She had paid for a country with her childhood. And the child — the child was made of exactly what she'd paid for.

"You owe her a debt," the dead said, in the voice dead things use, which is the sound of wind through a doorway. "And debts to the generous must be paid generously, or not at all."

The wheat changed its mind. The rivers grumbled but conceded. Even the mountains, who concede nothing, developed a very slight westward tilt that everyone understood to mean *fine*.

Which left only the child to ask. Because a soul, even a fragment of one, even one shaped like a boy of perhaps seven years, does not get chosen for. Choosing is the one thing that can't be done to you.

They brought me — brought the child-shape of me, sleeping in a bed of wheat at the edge of the Door-meadow — they brought me to the foot of the hill below the Oyelaran house, and they set me down in the evening light, and the whole country held its breath, which is a strange sensation when you are also the breath.

I looked up the hill at the small figure silhouetted in the doorway.

Let me tell you what I saw, because I saw it with every sense a country has and a few that haven't been invented yet. I saw a woman with empty hands. I saw the ghost of every memory she'd burned to free us, hanging around her like heat shimmer. I saw that she was humming — badly, tunelessly — a lullaby from the Vale of Nine Springs that she couldn't possibly know, except that her body knew it, some deep muscle-memory of melody surviving the theft of everything else. Her body remembered being rocked. Her body remembered home even though her mind had sold it.

Her body had been waiting for me longer than I had been waiting for her.

I got up. I was wearing the shape of a boy, mud-caked, thin, seven years old forever if I wanted to be. I climbed the hill. My legs ached, because I had made them real legs with real aches, because some things you should feel honestly.

She heard me on the path and turned. And here is the detail I will keep when everything else wears away, the detail I will carry into whatever I am next: she did not ask who I was. She looked at this strange child climbing her hill at dusk, a child she had never seen in any life she could remember, and her face did the thing faces do when the body knows ahead of the mind — a crumpling, a softening, a reaching.

"Oh," she said. Not a word so much as an exhale with edges. "Oh, it's *you*."

"I don't have anywhere else to be," I said. Which was true, and also the largest understatement ever uttered in the restored Kingdom of Ashkar, and also — because I had spent three years as a child and children learn to say the smallest true thing — exactly enough.

She knelt down in the dirt of her own doorstep and held out her arms, and I walked into them, and the whole country exhaled.

Somewhere below us, the wheat lay flat in a wind that had no business existing, and the river changed course by a hand's width to run closer to the Vale, and every grandmother in every kitchen within fifty miles felt an inexplicable urge to start cooking, and none of them questioned it, because grandmothers know better than to question the obvious.

* * *

People ask me sometimes — well, people ask the elders, who ask the wind, which tells me, which is how questions reach me these days — whether I regret it. Whether being small again, being *one* again, feels like a demotion. Whether a soul that was briefly everything can bear to be somebody.

Here is my answer, and I give it freely:

I have been a kingdom. Kingdoms are magnificent. Kingdoms are also unable to be picked up when they fall down, unable to be kissed on the forehead, unable to fall asleep against someone's shoulder while she pretends not to cry because crying would wake you. When I was everything, no one could hold me. Now there is exactly one pair of arms in the world that fits me, and I have them, and they have me, and I would trade every wheat field in Ashkar — and I *am* every wheat field in Ashkar, so I speak with authority — I would trade them all for this.

Besides, I haven't stopped being the country. I've just stopped being *only* the country. On quiet nights I can feel the orchards dreaming. When Elodie — Mama, I call her now, a word that arrived in my mouth fully formed, the way the most important words do — when Mama plants something, it grows a little faster than it should, and neither of us mentions it. When she sleeps, I keep the bad dreams off her the way she once kept the Hollow Men off me. We look after each other. That's the arrangement. That's the whole of the law.

There is one more thing, though. One thing I haven't told her yet, because telling it will change things, and I wanted a few seasons of ordinary first — of burnt bread and scraped knees and learning letters together, her patience fraying over the same alphabet I was born knowing.

The thing is this: the Door didn't close all the way.

It stands in the meadow where it always stood, and it is shut, and it is quiet — but a door, once opened, remembers opening. And lately, on nights when the moon is thin, I can feel something on the other side of it. Faint. Patient. A pressure, like a palm laid flat against wood from the far side.

Something else was unwritten besides us. Something smaller and stranger, something that didn't get swept back in when I poured home. And it has noticed that the Door opens for those willing to pay.

Mama is in the garden now, planting apple trees where the burning orchard stood — she doesn't know it was the burning orchard, but her hands chose the spot anyway, hands being wiser than heads. I watch her from the fence, a boy of seven with a country in his pocket and a secret in his teeth, and I decide, the way souls decide, that tomorrow I will tell her.

Tonight, I will let her have the apples.

Tomorrow, the knocking starts.
