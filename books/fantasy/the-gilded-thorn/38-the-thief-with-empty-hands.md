## 38. The Thief With Empty Hands

The first thing you should know about having empty hands is that they are heavier than full ones.

I learned this in the spring after the Door, when I tried to steal a memory for the first time since Ashkar came back into the world, and found — not that I couldn't, but that I wouldn't. There is a difference, and the difference has teeth.

* * *

It happened at a wedding. That's usually where it happens, if it's going to happen at all; joy is the easiest thing to lift, because joy leaks. Grief people guard like coin. Joy they set down on tables while they dance.

The bride was a cooper's daughter from the river quarter of New Vess — we still call it that, though Vess stopped being new about six months after it stopped being the only city anyone could remember — and she was laughing so hard at her uncle's dancing that her whole face had come undone. I was standing near the cider barrels with a cup I wasn't drinking, doing what I had done for years without thinking: reading the light off people. Old habit. You learn to see memories the way a fisherman sees weather, and you don't unlearn it just because the sky has changed.

Her happiness was right there. Golden, loose, half-spilled already. One touch of my palm and I'd have had it bottled by midnight. A charm like that would sell for more than I made in a month back when I made money at all. And there are still buyers in Vess, even now — especially now, with a whole restored kingdom next door full of people who lost twenty years and want to feel something whole again.

I put my hand in my pocket instead and walked out into the yard.

Wren was sitting on the fence rail, because Wren is always sitting somewhere slightly too high for comfort, watching everything with that expression that isn't quite solemnity and isn't quite amusement. The soul of an entire nation, currently wearing a child about nine years old, swinging one boot.

"You didn't take it," Wren said.

"No."

"Why?"

I looked back at the lanterns, the dancing, the bride with her undone face. "Because it was hers," I said. And then, because lying to Wren has never once worked and never will: "And because I wanted it."

Wren nodded slowly, the way children nod when an adult finally says something true enough to be interesting. "That's the whole trade, isn't it," they said. "You used to be paid in other people's wanting. Now you have your own."

I didn't answer, mostly because a nine-year-old who used to be a country should not be allowed to land blows like that before supper.

But they were right, and here is the part nobody warns you about: I can still do it. My gift didn't burn with the orchard. It's all still there, the light-reading, the lifting, the little craft of bottling joy in glass and wax. What burned was the permission. For three years in old Vess I told myself I stole because I had nothing of my own, and that was true, and it was also the most comfortable lie I ever owned. The truth is I stole because taking was the only way I knew to hold anything, and holding things — even borrowed ones, even secondhand joys in stoppered bottles — was the only proof I existed.

Now I exist whether I hold things or not. It turns out that's terrifying. It turns out empty hands have to learn an entirely different trade.

* * *

*

The orchard ground lies two days' walk east of the Fen, in what the maps now — real maps, Ilsa's maps, the first ones she drew with ink that stayed — label the Ashkaran Marches. Before the Door it had been called nothing at all, or rather it had been called the Burnt Marches by people fleeing a homesickness they couldn't name, which is its own kind of map.

The burning orchard stood there. My orchard. The one I keep in the recovered vial, the one memory I refused to feed to the Door, because a person has to keep one thing or the giving means nothing — no. That's not honest either. I kept it because I couldn't give it. Because it was my mother's voice saying my name in it, and the Door asked for everything, and I am not a saint, I am a thief with one theft left in her, and it is mine.

So I went back to where that orchard had stood and burned, twenty-two years ago, when Aurel Vane came over the border with gold in one hand and hollow men in the other, and I planted trees in the ash.

Everyone said it wouldn't take. The soil was sour, Ilsa said, reading her own fading notes. Twenty years of ash and erasure, Sef's colleague Tam said, when he passed through on his slow, sad circuit between cities. Even the elder Maren, who remembers more of old Ashkar than anyone, shook her head and said fruit needs more than memory to grow.

But here is what none of them knew, and what I only understood standing in that grey field with a sack of seedlings on my back: the ground remembered being an orchard. Not in any way you could bottle or sell. In the way that ground remembers anything — the deep root-channels under the ash, the shape of the rows, the particular lean of soil where ten thousand fallen apples had fed it year after year. Erasure takes names. It takes maps and archives and mothers. It does not get the roots.

The first spring, seven of the forty seedlings lived. I cried over seven living sticks in a dead field, and Wren sat on the fence — there is always a fence, Wren finds fences the way water finds low ground — and said nothing, which from Wren is a benediction.

The third spring, which is this one, all forty are alive, and eleven of them flowered.

* * *

*

I want to tell you about the flowers, but first I have to tell you about my hands, because they're the reason I'm writing this down at all instead of just living it like a normal person.

After the Door, when my stolen childhood went up as kindling for a country's return, I assumed the thiefcraft would go with it. It didn't. The gift was never part of what Vane took or what I gave. It's just mine — the one thing I brought out of old Vess with nothing else. And so for a while I did what any sensible person does with a dangerous tool: I hid it. Kept my palms closed. Stopped going to markets and festivals and sickbeds, all the places where memory sits loose and liftable.

Then the petitions started.

They come to me now, people do. Word travels strange in a country that woke mid-sentence; everyone knows someone who knows the woman who opened the Door, and half of them have decided I can fix what hurts. A widow whose grief got erased along with everything else, so she mourns a husband she cannot remember mourning, which is a wound shaped like nothing. A man who fought for Vane, conscripted, and wants to forget what he did — wants it taken out and bottled and buried, the old trade exactly reversed. Mothers of children born during the empty years, asking me to give their babies the lullabies they themselves never learned.

I turn most of them away. I tell them the truth: I don't give memories back, I only ever took them, and what I took is gone. Some of them don't believe me. Some of them leave coins anyway, on my doorstep, in my boots, once inside a flowerpot, which felt pointed.

But last month a girl came walking up the orchard rows while I was pruning. Sixteen, maybe. She had the look of the generation born in Vess during the empty years — fluent in a language of shrugs, homesick for nowhere. She held out her hand and said, "They say you can take a memory out of a person and keep it safe. I want you to take mine. All of them. I'll grow them back. I just need them out of me long enough to breathe."

And every instinct I ever trained lit up like a market lantern. Here was a customer, here was a trade, here was the old competence sliding back over me like a coat that still fit.

I looked at my hands. Open, empty, smelling of tree-wax and spring dirt.

"I could," I said. "Once, I'd have done it before you finished asking. But listen — what I'd take wouldn't be safe. It'd be mine. That's how the craft works. The moment I lift it, I start spending it. Every charm I ever sold was somebody's best hour, and somebody spent it, and that hour is gone from the world forever." I turned my palms up so she could see them, ordinary and scarred, the key-shaped scar across the right one. "I won't take yours. But I'll sit with you while you remember them out loud, and I'll write them down, and you can keep the pages. It's slower. It costs nothing. It stays yours."

She thought I was refusing her. She got angry, actually, and told me I was a fraud, and stormed off down the rows.

She came back four days later. We've been at it every evening since, her talking and me writing, and I will tell you something I never expected to learn at my age: telling a memory is better than stealing one. When you steal, the memory dies in the bottle. When someone tells you theirs, freely, wanting to be heard — it grows. It gets bigger in the telling. Hers are getting bigger every night, and some nights she laughs, and last week she cried about her grandmother, whom she'd forgotten she remembered, and afterward she said, "Is this what you used to do? Take these?" and I said yes, and she looked at me the way you look at someone who confesses to a crime from a foreign country.

"Empty hands suit you better," she said.

Cheeky thing. I'm keeping her on.

* * *

*

The eleventh flowering tree is the one that matters.

It stands in the exact center of the old orchard, where the vial-memory places my mother's favorite plum, and when it bloomed — white, ridiculous, absurd against all that grey recovery — Wren climbed it immediately, obviously, because Wren climbs everything that holds still, and sat in the blossom-cloud looking like a small furious angel.

"It's the same tree," Wren said.

"It's a seedling from a nursery in Vess. It's maybe four years old."

"It's the same tree," Wren repeated, with the patience of an entire nation explaining something simple to one stubborn woman. And then they held out their hand, palm up, and said a word in Ashkaran — *sévala*, which means both *plum* and *the thing that returns* — and the blossoms stirred though there was no wind, and for half a heartbeat I smelled woodsmoke and festival sugar and my mother's hair-oil, all at once, from nowhere, from everywhere, from the ground itself.

I sat down hard in the dirt.

Here is what I have pieced together since, and Ilsa's theory, for once, matches the elders': when the Door opened and I poured my stolen childhood through it, the memories didn't vanish. Nothing given to the Door vanishes. It goes home. Somewhere in the restoration, in the great flooding-back of a country's soul, my small hoard — a mother, a festival, a promise made at a door — became part of Ashkar's remembering. Which means the orchard I burned in was never entirely gone. Which means the tree growing in its place is, in some way I cannot prove and do not need to, the same tree.

My mother may have eaten a plum from it last autumn. I haven't worked up the courage to find her yet. That's another chapter, and my hands are shaking too much to write it tonight.

* * *

*

Tonight, then. Dusk, the rows going blue, Wren asleep in the loft of the drying shed they refuse to call home because, they inform me, home is a country and countries don't have lofts, fine, whatever, sleep in the loft of the shed.

I walked the rows the way I do every evening now, counting trees instead of charms, and I thought about the girl with her written-down memories, and the widow I couldn't help, and the coins people keep leaving, and I thought: I spent twenty years believing I was empty because I had no past. Then I got the past back and gave it away, and discovered I'd been wrong twice. I was never empty. I was a locked room. Everything I needed was in me the whole time — the language I could understand without learning, the face people recognized before they knew my name, the gift itself, twisted from taking into telling.

A thief with empty hands. That's what I am. But it turns out empty is just what a hand looks like before it plants something.

At the gate, when I came back in, there was a letter wedged in the fence rail. No seal. Vess paper, river-quarter cheap, the kind wedding invitations come on. I almost threw it unread onto the compost, because I am done with Vess's ghosts, done with Maro's silence, done with everything that city owes me and I owe it.

But the handwriting stopped me. It was careful, old-fashioned, the letters formed like someone learned penmanship for a purpose and never found one — and underneath the strangeness of it, underneath twenty-two years and a war and a Door, I knew it. You know your own teachers.

It was Maro's hand. And it said only this:

*Elodie. I have something of yours I never sold. Come alone. Bring the child.*

I read it three times. The dusk got darker. Somewhere down the valley a nightbird started up, and the eleven flowering trees stood pale as doors in the dark, and Wren slept on in a shed that is not a home, in a country that remembers itself, while the man who raised me and betrayed me and drowned his own guilt in gold asked me to come back to the city that taught me to steal.

Bring the child.

He doesn't know what the child is. Or — worse — he does.

I folded the letter into my pocket, next to the key-shaped scar I carry under the skin, and went to wake Wren.

We ride at dawn.
