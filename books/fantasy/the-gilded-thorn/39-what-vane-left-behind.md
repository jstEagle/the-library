## 39. What Vane Left Behind

My name is Corvess Dune. I mention this because for eleven years I was not permitted to have one.

The Hollow Men do not carry names the way other people do — as something soft you keep in a pocket and take out when someone calls. We had designations. Mine was Ninth of the Vigil. Sef was Seventh. When Sef fell at the Salt Fen, holding the line against his own order so that a thief, a child, and a mapmaker could finish what we had spent our lives preventing, I was standing forty paces behind him with my hand raised to strike him down myself. I want that recorded somewhere. Not because it excuses anything. Because accuracy is the only penance available to men like me.

When the Door opened, I did not fall. I wish I could tell you I was struck blind by glory or flung across the fen by the force of a nation remembering itself. The truth is less flattering. The truth is that when Ashkar came back into the world, every Hollow Man within a league of it felt his purchased memories tear loose like stitches from old cloth, and most of us simply sat down in the mud and stayed there. I sat down. I sat down for what I am told was two days, though it felt like a season, and when I stood up again I was nobody in particular, holding a knife I no longer remembered how to use with any artistry, staring at a horizon where an entire country had grown back overnight like grass after rain.

I walked to Vess. It took six weeks. I will tell you about the walk later, if there is a later, because parts of it were funny in the way that only complete ruin can be funny. But this chapter of the story belongs to Vane, and I should give him what he gave no one else: an honest accounting.

* * *

The first thing you must understand about Aurel Vane is that he was never, strictly speaking, a liar.

This surprised me. I had served his Inquisition for a decade, and I had assumed — the way you assume water is wet and taxes are theft — that a man who rewrote the memory of an entire kingdom would be a man comfortable with falsehood. But in the three audiences I had with him over the years, I never once caught him in a lie. He lied constantly, of course. But he believed every word as it left his mouth, because the memories he used to build each lie were real ones. He drank them from living minds and wore them like coats, and a coat fits until it doesn't.

He drank Ashkar. That much the restored country has confirmed: twenty years ago, on the night they call the Unwriting, Aurel Vane stood at the border of a kingdom that trusted him and drank its collective memory — its songs, its laws, its grievances, its recipes, its dead — until there was nothing left of Ashkar but geography, and even the geography he papered over with salt fen and rumor. He became untouchable because to oppose him you would first have to remember what you were opposing, and no one could.

It was, I will grant him this, the most efficient tyranny ever devised. You cannot topple a statue you cannot see.

But here is what none of us understood, we Hollow Men who fed ourselves to his machine piece by piece: a man who eats memory does not digest it. He only holds it. And holding is not the same as being.

* * *

I saw him four days after I reached Vess, and I want to be precise about the moment, because precision is all I have left.

There is a soup kitchen near the Tanners' Canal, run by a widow named Brakka who asks no questions and ladles generously and has never once, in her long career of charity, looked anyone in the eye longer than courtesy requires. This is why half of ruined Vess eats there. The war had passed the city by — the free cities having concluded, on excellent archival evidence, that no such war existed — but the roads still funneled in the displaced: soldiers whose regiments had dissolved mid-march, farmers whose villages had been unmade in the great unwriting, and now, apparently, emperors.

He was third in the queue. I recognized him before I recognized anything else about him, which is a sentence that will require unpacking.

You must understand: I had seen Aurel Vane perhaps a dozen times, always at distance, always robed in the grey-and-gold of the Inquisition's founding, always attended. But there are men whose posture you memorize the way you memorize a blade's balance, and Vane stood like a man who owned the ground he stood on. Even in a beggar's coat, even with his hair gone grey at the temples and his hands cracked from road dust, he stood that way. So I knew him. And then I looked at his face, and I did not know him at all.

Because the face was wrong. Not altered — emptied. A face is mostly memory; the way a mouth holds a smile it learned young, the way eyes narrow at a name they've heard ten thousand times. His face had none of that. It was handsome in the way a rented room is clean, and it kept doing small, terrible things: reaching for an expression and finding nothing there. When the woman ahead of him in the queue dropped her bowl, he flinched and then, visibly, wondered why he had flinched.

I watched him take his bowl of barley soup from Brakka without a word, and then I watched him sit alone at the end of a bench, hold the spoon, and stare at it.

He stared at the spoon for a long time. Long enough that people began to notice. Long enough that a child across the table started giggling. And then — I swear this on whatever I have left to swear on — he turned the spoon over, examined the bowl of it, and said, quietly, to no one:

"I know this. I know that I know this."

And then he ate his soup like a man defusing a trap.

* * *

I followed him for three days. Call it professional habit; the Vigil trained nothing into us deeper than the following of persons of interest. Call it guilt, if you prefer the kinder word. Both are true, and I have stopped pretending the two are distinguishable.

Here is what I learned, catalogued with the dispassion of my former trade:

Day one: he did not know his own name. I tested this. I approached him at the canal steps — I had a cover story prepared about being a fellow traveler, which was even true in the way that mattered — and I asked him what he called himself. He thought about it. Actually thought about it, the way you think about a question in a language you studied years ago.

"I've been trying several," he said. "Aldous. That one feels like a coat. Merrin feels like a hat." He looked out at the water. "Do names usually feel like clothes?"

"Sometimes," I said, and hated myself for how gently I said it.

Day two: he remembered skills but not sources. I watched him repair a fishmonger's cart axle with the economy of a man who had done it a hundred times — except he hadn't; those were someone else's hundred times, drunk from some cartwright in a kingdom that no longer existed until last month. He fixed the wheel, stepped back, and stared at his own hands with an expression I recognized from mirrors: the look of a man whose house has been rearranged in the night.

Day three: he began asking questions in markets. Not the sharp interrogative probing of an inquisitor — I would know; I invented half those techniques — but the lost, hungry questions of a man trying to find himself in other people's answers. *Have we met? Did you serve under a general named—? No. Was there ever a king here called—? No. Do you know the song about the orchards? The burning orchard song?*

No one knew the song about the orchards. Except, of course, that now everyone did, because Ashkar was back, and its orchards with it, and its griefs, and somewhere across the Salt Fen a whole nation was waking up mid-sentence. But Vess is far from the fen, and news travels slowly, and besides — the restored Ashkarans did not sing their songs in Vess markets. They sang them home, where they belonged.

So Vane wandered the city asking after pieces of himself, and the city, kindly or indifferently, told him there was no such piece, no such man, no such song. Every answer confirmed the erasure he had engineered. He had built a prison with no doors and then, through the Door, been locked inside it himself. I have tried to feel sorry for him about this. Some days I succeed.

Most days I remember the neighbor they took from the house across from mine, back when I still lived in a house and took neighbors for granted, and I fail.

* * *

On the fourth day, I made a mistake. Or perhaps I made the only correct decision of my life; the accounting is not yet finished.

He was sitting on the canal steps at dusk, feeding bread to ducks with the concentration of a scholar attempting a difficult text. I sat beside him. I said, "You asked about the song. The orchard song."

His head turned very fast. For one moment — one flickering, vertiginous moment — I saw something move behind his eyes, something vast and crowded, a whole country's worth of borrowed light pressing against the inside of his skull like prisoners at a window. Then it settled, and he was just a tired man on steps.

"You know it?"

"I know of it," I said. Which was true. Sef had hummed it once, near the end, after he'd tasted that charm in the night market — the one with Ashkaran residue. He'd said, *Where have I heard this before?* and I'd reported the lapse to my superiors, because that was my job, and three weeks later Sef was reassigned to the Salt Fen detail, which was where they sent men they suspected. I helped send him there. I have decided to spend the rest of my life not looking directly at that fact, and I commend the technique to you; it works almost as well as drinking the memory away entirely, and it costs considerably less.

"There's a country it comes from," I said. "It came back recently. Across the fens. There are people there now who sing it."

Something happened to his face then that I do not have the craft to describe. Grief is too simple. Longing is too gentle. It was the expression of a man hearing that a feast had been held in his absence, in a house that had burned down while he watched it burn — and knowing, with absolute certainty, that no invitation was coming.

"They won't want me there," he said.

"No," I agreed. "They won't."

He nodded slowly, as if I had confirmed a suspicion rather than delivered a verdict. Then he said the thing I have turned over every night since, the thing I am writing all of this down to try to understand:

"I keep remembering a door," he said. "Not the big one. A small one. Green paint, peeling at the bottom. Someone promised me something in front of it. I don't know who. I don't know what. But it's the only memory I have left that hurts, and I think—" he laughed, a short, broken sound, "—I think it might be the only one I actually own."

I knew the door. Of course I knew the door. Everyone in the restored territories knew the door by now; it was in half the recovered memories that had come flooding back with the country, the little green door at the edge of an orchard, where a boy named Aurel — a real boy, an Ashkaran boy, before he ever drank a kingdom — had once been promised something by someone who loved him.

He had eaten everything, and the one thing that survived the eating, the single undigested scrap, was a promise. And he didn't even know what the promise was.

"Go east," I said. "Past the fens. Ask for the orchards. If anyone remembers you kindly, they'll tell you what was promised. If they don't—" I shrugged. "Then you'll finally know something true."

* * *

They say he went. I say *they say* because I did not follow him past the walls; my following days were done, and besides, some doors a man must knock on alone.

The tinker who saw him on the east road says he walked like a pilgrim, slow, stopping at milestones to read them twice. The ferryman at the fen's edge says he paid with a coin that shouldn't exist — an Ashkaran crown, twenty years out of mint — and stared at it in his palm the whole crossing, saying over and over, *I know this. I know that I know this.* The orchard-keepers outside the restored capital say a grey man came walking up the rows in late autumn, and that they knew his face — it is in their returned memories, the boy who left before the burning — and that they did not give him the promise, because the promise was not theirs to give. It belonged to a woman dead these twenty years, and she had written it down once, and the writing had survived the Unwriting in a place Vane never thought to drink: a child's copybook, kept in a crate, in a cellar, in the dark.

What was promised, the orchard-keepers will tell you, if you ask nicely and pick apples for your supper. I will not write it here. Some things should travel by mouth, from a person to a person, the old way — the way memory moved before men like me learned to bottle it, and men like Vane learned to drink it, and everyone learned that a thing possessed can be taken, but a thing told is carried safe in the one vault no usurper can breach.

I will tell you only this much: whatever was in that copybook, the grey man read it standing in the orchard where the burning one had stood, and then he sat down among the roots, and the people who found him there said he was weeping — not the tears of a tyrant undone, which would have satisfied something in me, I admit it, but the ordinary tears of an ordinary man meeting, at the end of a very long road, the person he had been before he decided that being someone meant devouring everyone.

Whether he stays in Ashkar, whether they let him, whether an ordinary man can live in a country he erased — I don't know. I am not the author of that story. I'm barely a character in it. I'm the colleague. The one who hesitated too late and followed too long and is telling you all this from a room above a chandlery in Vess, where I have taken up honest work, if you can call candle-dipping honest when your hands remember eleven other trades you never learned.

* * *

One more thing. The last thing, and then I'll let you get to the wedding, or the orchard-planting, or whatever the ending of this story turns out to be — I hear there's a girl with empty hands and a scar shaped like a key, and that the story ends gently, which is more than stories like this usually manage.

Last week, a child knocked at my door. Mud-caked, quiet, with the particular patience of someone who has waited outside many doors and expects nothing. I nearly didn't open it. Old habits. We Hollow Men were taught that knocking children are either bait or omens.

But I opened it, because Sef opened his, once, at the cost of everything, and I owe him at least a hinge's worth of courage.

The child looked at me for a long moment. Then she — I think it was a she; the soul of a country wears shapes loosely — pointed at my chest, where the Vigil's brand still shows under my shirt if you know where to look, and said one word in a language that made the candles on my shelf lean toward her like flowers toward sun.

I don't speak Ashkaran. Nobody in Vess does, officially. But I understood her anyway, the way you understand a thing you never learned, which is the whole lesson of this story if you ask me, and I suspect it's the whole lesson of hers too.

She said: *"Still."*

As in: still here. Still standing. Still possible.

Then she turned around and walked off into the evening, and I closed my door, and I lit a candle, and I sat down to finish writing this, so that somewhere, someday, someone would know what the tyrant left behind when everything he stole was taken back:

An ordinary man. A green door. An unpaid promise.

And, if the orchard-keepers are to be believed — and lately I believe everything the orchard-keepers say — a single apple tree, planted by his own hands at the edge of the rows, that no one has the heart to pull up.

It blooms early, they say. Before all the others. As if it's trying to make up for lost time.

As if it remembers spring.
