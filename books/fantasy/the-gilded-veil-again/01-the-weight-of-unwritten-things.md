## 1. The Weight of Unwritten Things

The counting house smelled of ash and old ink, and Lena Marchetti had long since stopped being able to say which scent she preferred.

She sat alone at the great oak table where twelve scribes had once worked shoulder to shoulder, their quills scratching in chorus like rain on a slate roof. Now her pen moved through silence so complete she could hear the tallow candle deciding whether to gutter. Three members remained of the Gilded Veil: Master Corvane, who had not descended from his study in nine days; Oskar Brandt, who kept the vaults and spoke mostly to the locks; and herself, keeper of ledgers, invisible by design—the guild's oldest insurance against its own extinction. No one could rob what no one could remember seeing.

Lena balanced the impossible inventory by candlelight, as she did every night, because the numbers only held after dark.

*Bottled grief, 412 vials.* She checked the seals on the ledger's facing page; the ink there still wept faintly when the moon was full, and she had learned to blot it with salt before it stained the entries beneath. *Borrowed years, unsorted: eleven crates.* A decade ago the crates had been counted individually—year by year, each one tagged with the name of the person who'd sold it and the name of the person who'd bought it wearing that person's smile. Now they arrived in bulk from desperate houses, unlabeled, because labeling cost money and the Veil was dying of exactly that.

*Names not yet spoken:* the entry had been frozen at three thousand for six years. Somewhere in the deep vaults, three thousand names waited in their lead-lined drawers for children who would never be born to claim them, or for parents too frightened to speak them aloud lest something hear.

Lena wrote the night's figures in her small, exact hand. The ledger drank the ink. It always did—a good sign, the old texts said. A ledger that refused ink was a ledger preparing to lie.

Upstairs, through the floorboards, she heard Master Corvane feed another letter to his fire.

She had begun counting those too. Nine nights, forty-one letters burned. She knew the rhythm of his burning now: the soft scrape of the grate, the pause while he read whatever he'd decided the world must never see, then the small wet sound of paper taking flame. He thought the chimney carried the smoke out over the rooftops where it would scatter among a hundred other chimneys' smoke, anonymous. But the counting house stood in the oldest quarter of the city, where every flue shared a wall with every other, and the smoke drifted down into the cellar instead, settling against the wards like a fine grey snow. The wards had grown dingy with it. They were meant to repel intruders, thieves, inquisitors, kings. No one had ever written a ward against sorrow, and Corvane's correspondence was soaked in it.

*Debt,* the letters said, in the voices of creditors across seven cities. *Default. Recall of charter. Final notice.*

The Gilded Veil had traded in impossible things for four hundred years, and it appeared that the impossible had finally called in its own accounts.

Lena finished the page. She blotted it, closed the ledger, and rested her palm flat upon the cover the way one rests a hand on the chest of a sleeping animal—one keeps it calm, and one keeps it honest. The leather was warm. It was always warm. There were nights, in the worst of the last two years, when that small animal warmth had been the only living thing in the building besides herself, and she was ashamed of how much comfort she took from it, and she took the comfort anyway.

Outside, the frost had come early. She heard it ticking against the shutters, patient as an accountant.

* * *

Midnight came, and with it the hour when Lena walked the floors.

It was not sentimentality. Well—it was not only sentimentality. A counting house full of impossible inventory required walking; the things on the shelves needed to be *regarded*, nightly, or they grew restless. Grief fermented. Borrowed years went stale and sour, and a sour year could curdle a whole life if someone careless uncorked it. The unspeakable names whispered if left too long in the dark, and whispering names was how hauntings began.

So Lena took her lamp and made her rounds, past the grief-vials racked in rows like wine bottles, blue-green glass catching the lamplight, some of them faintly luminous with what they contained. Past the crates of years, stenciled with the guild's sigil—a veil stitched in gold thread over a closed eye. Past the name-drawers, silent tonight, mercifully.

Past Oskar's domain, where the man himself sat dozing in a chair beside the inner vault door, his keys spread across his knees like a metal apron. He snored gently. In sleep his face lost its habitual suspicion and looked, briefly, like a younger man's—the man he might have been if he'd joined a baker's guild instead, if bread were the impossible thing and hunger merely ordinary.

"Goodnight, Oskar," Lena whispered, though he couldn't hear her. Nobody ever heard her. That was the design. When she'd signed her indenture at nineteen, the guildmaster then—not Corvane, the one before, whose name was already going soft in her memory—had pressed her thumb to the ledger and spoken the clause: *Let her go unseen, unheard, unremarked, until such time as the Veil has need of her voice.* Twenty-two years of walking through rooms like weather. Merchants who dealt with her weekly greeted her fresh each time with polite blankness. Once, a man she'd appraised a bottled memory for had proposed marriage to her the following afternoon, having entirely forgotten the proposal he'd made that morning.

She had said yes both times. It seemed rude not to.

Lena climbed the back stair to check on Tam—no. Not yet. There was no Tam yet. She climbed the back stair because the route passed Corvane's study, and habit is a kind of inventory too.

Through the keyhole: firelight, and the master of the Gilded Veil sitting with his head in his hands, a letter open on his desk, its seal broken and gleaming like a wound. Even without reading it she knew the color of the wax. Royal blue. The Crown did not write twice.

Corvane fed the letter to the fire. Forty-two.

* * *

She was descending again, lamp low, when the knocking began.

Three knocks. A pause. Then not knocking at all but a sound like knuckles on the inside of a drumhead—soft, resonant, wrong—and coming, she realized with a cold slide of understanding, not from the street door above but from the sealed door at the bottom of the cellar stairs.

There was no sealed door at the bottom of the cellar stairs. That was the point of it. The guild had bricked it over a century and a half ago, and the masonry had been warded since before anyone currently alive drew breath, and the wards were fed annually with a drop of master's blood and a line of ledger-ink, and in all her twenty-two years Lena had never once heard so much as a draft sigh through them.

The knocking came again. Patient. Small.

Lena set down her lamp. Every sensible instinct she owned—which was a considerable collection, curated over decades of invisibility—informed her that the correct procedure was to wake Oskar, wake Corvane, bar herself in the scriptorium, and let the masters of the guild handle whatever knocked on doors that did not exist.

She picked the lamp back up and went down instead.

The cellar air was cold enough to show her breath. The wards hung over the bricked doorway like curtains of heat-haze, greyed with the drifting ash of Corvane's burnt correspondence, and as she approached she saw that the ash was moving—swirling inward, toward the door, drawn the way moths are drawn, the way iron is drawn to lodestone. And through the haze she saw, impossibly, the outline of a door where there was only brick: a tall narrow door, older than the wall around it, older than the style of the hinges that shimmered faintly at its edges, wood gone silver with age.

Someone stood on the other side. The shape was small.

"Who's there?" Lena asked, and hated how thin her voice sounded in all that stone.

A child's voice answered—but the words were nothing. Syllables that fell like water over stones, rounded and ancient, a language that predated the gutters of this quarter, that predated perhaps the quarter itself. She understood none of it. And yet her body understood it the way the body understands a lullaby in a foreign tongue: something in the cadence said *safe* and something else said *come*, and underneath both ran a third note she recognized with a start because she heard it every midnight in her own head.

Loneliness. Spoken fluently, in a grammar older than kingdoms.

The wards answered him.

Lena watched it happen and forgot, for a long moment, to breathe. The grey curtain of ash parted. Along the shimmering seam of the impossible door, characters surfaced—guild-marks, ward-script, the old accounting signs that meant *entered*, *received*, *accepted*—lighting one by one like lamps down a corridor. The wards were not resisting him. The wards were *greeting* him. Four hundred years of protective craft, blood-fed and ink-fed, and they behaved toward this small unknown voice the way the ledger behaved toward true figures.

The door did not open. But the brick behind which it hid turned translucent as sea-glass, and through it she saw him clearly: a boy, perhaps eight years old, barefoot on frost-rimed stone, in a smock too thin for the season. Dark hair. A face ordinary and dear and exhausted, the face of a child who has walked a very long way on feet that hurt and has stopped mentioning it.

He pressed one palm flat against the inside of the barrier. Where his fingers touched, the brick warmed from grey to gold.

And he spoke again—that same flowing, water-over-stones language—and this time, though the meaning stayed sealed to her, one word recurred, three times, like a refrain, like a name:

*"Veyl. Veyl. Veyl."*

Not the guild's word. Older than the guild's word. Or—the thought arrived whole, unbidden, chilling her more than the cellar cold—the word the guild had borrowed, all those centuries ago, without ever asking what it originally meant, or whom.

The wards finished lighting. The last character to surface was one Lena knew intimately, because she wrote it at the top of every page of every book she kept: the sign for *balance*. An account opened. A debt acknowledged.

The Gilded Veil, dying of unpaid debts in a cold cellar of a city that had forgotten it, had just accepted a delivery.

Lena stood in the ash-grey light with her lamp trembling in her hand, and felt the thing she had armored herself against for twenty-two years crack straight down the middle—not fear, not duty, but wonder, raw and unledgered, the first unwritten thing to enter the counting house in a generation.

She put her palm against the warm brick, opposite the boy's hand, separated from him by a door that did not exist and a language that did not live.

"Hold on," she said, uselessly, wonderfully, in plain trade-tongue he could not possibly know. "I'll find someone who can open this."

Behind her, up the stairs and through the floorboards, she heard the fifty-second letter catch fire.
