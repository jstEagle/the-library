## 2. A Language Older Than Kingdoms

The boy had not slept in the counting house. He had *settled*, the way dust settles, the way a debt settles into bone. Lena found him at dawn curled beneath the appraisal table in the front room, bare feet tucked under the hem of a coat someone—Oskar, she suspected—had draped over him without comment. The candles they'd left burning for the wards had gone out hours ago. All but one.

That one was burning again now. She was certain she had watched it die.

Lena stood very still in the doorway with her ledger pressed to her chest and did the arithmetic of the impossible, because arithmetic was what she had instead of courage. One candle. Extinguished at the third bell by her own hand. Burning now, its flame standing straight as a pin in air that did not move. The wax had not melted further. If anything, there was *more* of it than there had been, a pale bead of it welling at the wick like something healing.

"Good morning," she said, in Guild Cant first, then in Low Speech, then, feeling foolish, in the courtly tongue her mother had beaten into her before the fever took her mother and the accent both.

The boy opened his eyes. They were gray, the gray of old pewter, of coins worn smooth by too many hands, and they looked through her the way light looks through water. Then he said a word.

It was not a word she knew. It was three sounds, low and rounded, like stones turning over in a riverbed, and when he finished saying them the candle flame *bowed*—leaned toward him, held, and straightened again, burning brighter than it had any right to burn without consuming anything at all.

Lena's pen-hand ached to write it down. She noticed that about herself the way you notice a bruise: only after it has already happened.

* * *

By midmorning the whole counting house knew, which meant Oskar knew, which meant Corvane would know by evening, so Lena made the only sensible decision available to an invisible woman: she got there first.

She found Master Corvane in his study feeding correspondence to the stove. He did this every morning now, though there was less and less to feed it—the Veil's creditors had stopped writing letters and started sending men. The firelight made his face look carved from tallow, soft in the wrong places.

"The child," she said.

"Is an asset." Corvane did not look up from the letter he was reading, deciding, condemning. "An unregistered asset, which is worse than no asset at all, but we will forgive the ledger that oversight if the ledger forgives us. Have you assessed him?"

"He speaks a language that isn't in any registry I keep."

"Then it is worth more than any registry you keep." He said it mildly, the way he said everything, and that mildness had always frightened her more than shouting ever could. "Lock him in the east storeroom. Feed him. Do not let him near the inventory."

"Master, the wards answered him. The door unsealed itself. I watched the iron *think* about it—"

"Iron thinks whatever I pay it to think," Corvane said, and fed another letter to the flames. "The east storeroom, Marchetti. And Lena—" now he looked up, and his eyes were the color of the ash he made every morning, "—whatever he says, do not write it down."

She had been with the Gilded Veil eleven years and had never once been told not to record something. That was the whole of the guild's covenant, older than its charter, older than its name: *what happens here is written here.* To leave a thing unwritten was to leave it unowned, unpriced, unbound—and Corvane knew that better than anyone alive.

"Yes, Master," said Lena Marchetti, who had already written it down.

* * *

The east storeroom smelled of cedar shavings and old regret. It was where the guild kept the things it could not sell: grief bottled by apprentices who hadn't yet learned to decant it cleanly, years borrowed against futures that never came due, a jar of names not yet spoken that hummed faintly if you put your ear to the glass. Tam sat on a crate in the middle of it all, small and grave, watching the inventory the way other children watched weather.

Oskar stood guard at the door, arms crossed, looking like a wall that had learned to disapprove.

"He ate," Oskar reported. "Half the bread. All the honey. He asked for neither—I just set them down and they were gone." He lowered his voice. "And Lena. The rust."

"What rust?"

"The hinge." He nodded at the storeroom's ancient door, its iron fittings furred orange with forty years of neglect. "Yesterday it screamed like a dying cat. This morning I came in and—" he touched it, almost reverently, "—it swings silent. Look at the metal. It's not oiled. It's *young*. There's rust on the floor beneath it like shed skin."

Lena looked. She was beginning to understand that looking was going to be her whole occupation where this boy was concerned.

Inside, Tam was speaking to the jar of unsaid names. Not loudly. Just talking, the way you talk to something lonely, in that riverbed cadence, those three-and-four-sound phrases that seemed to have no grammar she could find—or rather, whose grammar was *everything else*: the pause, the breath, the tilt of the head. As he spoke, the humming in the jar changed pitch. Warmed. The names inside, waiting all these decades for mouths that would never come, seemed to settle, like birds rearranging themselves on a branch.

Then he saw her, and stopped, and the silence he left behind had a shape, like a chair someone had just risen from.

"You understand them," Lena said. It wasn't a question; questions required shared words. She crouched to his level, ledger balanced on her knee. "The names. You were telling them they're heard."

Tam tilted his head, mirroring her exactly, a few degrees off true, like a reflection in dark glass. Then he spoke—a longer phrase this time, five sounds, six—and pointed one small finger at the dead hearth in the corner.

The kindling, untouched since winter, caught.

No flint. No spark she could see. The wood simply remembered being on fire, the way the candle had remembered burning, the way the hinge had remembered being new. Fire climbed the cedar shavings politely, contained, obedient, casting the boy's shadow huge and wavering across the jars.

Oskar swore in three languages and reached past her to smother it, and Tam watched them both with that pewter patience, and Lena understood two things at once, the way you understand lightning and thunder as a single event: the boy was not commanding objects. He was *reminding* them. Speaking to things in the voice of the time before things learned to be only one thing.

And she knew the cadence. Not the words—but the shape of them. She had spent eleven years copying forbidden texts into guild registries, pricing the unpriceable, and somewhere in the deepest vault, in a ledger bound in skin no beast would claim, she had seen phonetic transcriptions of a tongue the catalog called only *Pre-Dynastic. Provenance unknown. Do not read aloud.*

"Do not read aloud," she whispered.

"Lena?" Oskar said sharply. "You've gone white as a valuation."

"Get me the Vellum Codex. Vault four. Don't tell Corvane why."

* * *

He brought it up at dusk, wrapped in oilcloth, walking like a man carrying a sleeping snake. They spread it open on the appraisal table between them while Tam slept under his borrowed coat, and by candlelight—two candles, lit by ordinary means, checked twice—Lena compared the codex's cracked transliterations against the phrases she had memorized from the boy's mouth.

They matched. Not loosely. Exactly. Sound for sound, stress for stress, down to the little catch in the throat before the third syllable that no living scholar should have been able to reproduce.

"Read me the marginal note," Oskar said. His voice had gone quiet in the way it went quiet around dangerous inventory.

Lena traced the faded script. "'This tongue appears in no dynastic record. It predates the founding of Aldric's line by an age the chroniclers decline to number. It is the speech of the *before*—of whatever ruled or merely was, before crowns.'" She swallowed. "There's more. In a different hand, later, someone's added: 'Aldric the First forbade its transcription on pain of unmaking. Every copy we make, we make against his law.'"

"Aldric the First," Oskar repeated. "The founder of the dynasty. The king whose blood sits on the throne right now, if thrones can be said to sit." He rubbed his jaw. "So the boy speaks the language the first king was afraid of. A language older than kingdoms."

"And the objects obey him."

"The objects *remember* him," Oskar corrected softly. "There's a difference, Lena. Command is power. Memory is—" he searched for it, "—ownership. Things belong to whoever named them first."

Outside, the city bells rang the evening hour, and beneath their bronze voices Lena thought she heard—though she would never swear to it—the well in the courtyard singing back, one clear note, like something answering a call it had waited centuries to hear.

* * *

Corvane came at the ninth bell, alone, which was how she knew it was serious. He never wasted witnesses on decisions he'd already made.

"The Crown has been asking after strange children," he said, standing over the sleeping boy with his hands folded, a creditor appraising collateral. "Strange children, strange tongues, strange fires. There are men in gray coats at the district registries. So the boy stays locked until he can be properly appraised. Quietly. Off the books entirely—he does not exist until I decide what he is worth and to whom."

"Off the books," Lena said. "Master, the covenant—"

"The covenant," Corvane said, "is a roof, and roofs are for keeping rain off valuable things. The boy is currently rain." He turned to go, then paused at the door, and did not look back. "Marchetti. I know how you love your ink. Whatever you've already written—burn it."

She said yes, Master. She was getting good at saying it without meaning it, and hated how good.

* * *

Past midnight, in the counting room with one candle for company, Lena opened her personal ledger—the plain black one, the one with no guild seal, the one where she recorded debts of a different denomination: favors owed her, kindnesses owed others, the private accounting of a woman nobody counted.

She turned to a fresh page. At the top, in her smallest, most careful hand, she wrote the date, and beneath it:

*Item acquired: one boy, found at sealed door. Words: seven, transcribed below. Effect: restoration. Candles remember burning. Iron remembers being young. Fire remembers itself.*

*Provenance: predates kingdoms. Forbidden by Aldric the First.*

*Status: ordered destroyed (the record, not the boy). Status instead: preserved.*

Then, slowly, syllable by syllable, she copied out the seven fragments of Tam's speech as she had heard them, using the codex's notation, checking each against memory twice. Her pen shook on the fourth one—not from fear of Corvane, though there was that, cold in the stomach like swallowed coin. From awe. The letters seemed to sit differently on the page than other letters, heavier, as though the paper were having to hold them up.

When she finished the last one, the candle flame leaned toward the page. Held. Straightened.

Lena sat back and looked at what she had done. Eleven years of perfect entries, of never falsifying, never omitting, never once letting the guild's magic catch her in error—and tonight she had committed her first crime against Corvane, and it was not theft or forgery but its opposite: she had *recorded truly* what she'd been ordered to erase.

The guild's old magic moved through the counting house like a draft. She felt it pass over the ledger, pause, consider—and accept. The entry stood. Somewhere in the deep architecture of the Gilded Veil, a covenant older than its master quietly took her side.

From the east storeroom, faint through two doors, came the sound of the boy talking in his sleep—that riverbed cadence, gentle, unhurried. And beneath it, impossibly, the well in the courtyard answering, note for note, like a mother answering a child across a crowded room.

Lena blew out the candle, held the ledger to her chest, and went to stand outside his door until morning.

Because in the margin of the Vellum Codex, beneath the warning she had not yet told Oskar about, in that later hand, there had been one more line—one she had read and reread until the words stopped looking like words:

*'The last speakers of the before-tongue were not people. They were pieces.'*
