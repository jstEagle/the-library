## 3. What the Crown Remembers

*The words below are Lena Marchetti's. She sat by the boy's pallet for six nights, copying as he spoke in his sleep, translating what could be translated, and marking with a small black asterisk every phrase that refused to become anything but itself. What follows is her ledger. I have changed nothing but the order of the lines.*

— from the recovered papers of the Gilded Veil

Tam dreamed of drowning in a dry room.

That was the first thing he tried to tell her, on the third night, when she came with her candle and her book and sat where the moonlight wouldn't touch the pages. He had learned a few of her words by then—*yes*, and *no*, and *stay*, which was the one he used most—and she had learned to listen to him the way you listen to a well: patiently, leaning over the dark, waiting for the water to show you its own face.

"Drowning," he said in her tongue, because he had found it in her mouth two nights before, when she read aloud from an old inventory of bottled rivers. "But no water."

Lena's pen stopped.

"No water," she repeated. "Then how do you drown?"

He didn't have her word for it yet. He reached instead into the language that lived under his tongue like a stone under moss—the old one, the one that made candles stand up straighter when he spoke—and what came out of him was not a sentence but a sensation, a shape of meaning that Lena would later render in her ledger as:

*I am in a room full of people who love me, and I cannot breathe, and they are all smiling.*

She wrote it down. Her hand shook a little. He watched her write, because watching her write was the closest thing he had to knowing what he was: something that happened to other people, and got recorded.

* * *

The dream always began the same way, though he did not know yet that it was a dream, or that the man in it was called Aldric, or that Aldric was king of everything Tam could see from the counting house window if he stood on the third stair.

In the dream there was a crown being lowered onto his head, and it weighed nothing, and that was the horror of it. A thing should weigh. A thing that does not weigh is not a thing; it is a decision someone else has made about your skull. The crown settled onto him like a thought settling into a mind, and the cathedral roared, and beneath the roar Tam heard—or Aldric heard—a second sound, patient as sediment:

*At last. At last. At last.*

And then the sliding.

This was the part Lena could never get him to describe without his voice going strange, going layered, going *old*. Something slid beneath the skin of the man in the dream the way an eel slides beneath the skin of a river—not violently, not cruelly, but with the terrible gentleness of a thing that has waited so long for entry that it has learned manners. It did not push him aside. That was the worst discovery, the one that woke Tam sweating in the narrow bed above the empty counting house, staring at the ceiling beams. It did not push him aside at all. It poured itself into the spaces he wasn't using.

There were more of those spaces than Tam wanted to think about.

"Is it you?" Lena asked him once, pen poised. "In the dream. Are you the man?"

Tam considered this with the gravity of a child considering a question adults think is simple. He knew the answer was no. He also knew the answer was not *no*, and the distance between those two answers was the whole width of his fear, and he had no bridge across it in either language.

"I am," he said finally, "the part of the drowning that remembers being the sea."

Lena wrote that down too. Underneath it, in smaller script, she added a note he couldn't read: *God help me, I think the fragments aren't pieces of the parasite. I think some of them are pieces of Aldric. The parts it swallowed first.*

* * *

On the fourth night the dreams widened, and Tam learned that he was not alone in the city.

He dreamed of a well in the Tanner's Ward that had begun to sing—an old woman drawing water at dawn, and the bucket coming up ringing like a struck bell, and the notes spelling out a word in the language under his tongue, the same language, *his* language, and the word was *soon*. He dreamed of a priestess in the temple of the Ninefold Flame who had torn off her vestments and stood naked before the altar laughing, laughing, laughing, telling everyone who would listen that the king had a guest and the guest was hungry and the meal was nearly finished. They had taken her away. In the dream Tam could still hear her laughter through the walls of whatever room they'd put her in, and it was not madness. That was the thing no one else seemed to understand, the thing he grabbed Lena's sleeve to make her write down, shaking her arm harder than he'd ever touched another person:

"She is not broken. She is *listening*. We are all listening now. We can't stop."

And he dreamed of a coin.

A copper coin, ordinary, worn smooth by ten thousand thumbs, that had been passed hand to hand through the Fishmarket until a fishmonger's daughter bit it—for luck, for habit, children do such things—and found that it bled. Warm and red against her teeth, real blood, and when she screamed and dropped it, the coin lay in the gutter beating. Not spinning. *Beating.* Like a heart the size of a promise, keeping time with something far away, something crowned, something counting down.

Lena set down her pen after that one and did not pick it up for a long moment. The candle flame leaned toward Tam as if straining to hear better, and he noticed, and filed it away with all the other notices: how locks grew drowsy near him, how rust unwound itself from hinges when he walked past, how the wards on the sealed door had answered him before any human being did. Things liked him here. Or things *recognized* him here. He was beginning to suspect those were the same thing, and the suspicion tasted like the copper coin.

"The fragments," Lena said quietly. "They're waking up. All of them. And they're turning toward—" She stopped herself. She had a habit of stopping herself just before the end of sentences that frightened her, and Tam had learned to finish them in the silence.

Toward the king. Toward the crown. Toward home.

"Home," he said aloud, testing it, and hated the way his mouth shaped it. Because that was the question underneath everything, the one that swam beneath his sleep like something vast turning over in shallow water: if the singing well and the bleeding coin and the laughing priestess were all pieces of the thing in the dream—if *he* was a piece of the thing in the dream—then home was a monster. Home was the sliding eel-gentleness beneath a drowning man's skin. Home was the reason a king smiled wrong.

But home was also this room. This candle. This woman with ink on her fingers who stayed.

"Am I a boy?" he asked her, in her language, carefully, the way you carry something breakable across ice. "Or am I a piece?"

Lena looked at him for a long time. Outside, the city muttered through its evening noises, carts and dogs and the far-off bells of a kingdom that did not know it was wearing a parasite the way a hand wears a ring. Inside, the candle burned straight and tall, attentive.

"You're Tam," she said at last, and wrote the word in her ledger as she said it, as if committing it to record made it truer—as if naming were a kind of binding. "Whatever else you are, that comes second."

It was a kind lie, or a true hope, or possibly both. Tam took it anyway. Children take what warmth is offered; they are built for it, the way roots are built for rain. But that night, sleeping above the empty counting house, he dreamed the coronation dream again, and this time the dream knew he was dreaming it, and the ancient patience beneath the skin of the dying-king-that-was-him turned its attention—slowly, gently, with terrible manners—toward the small bright fragment that had learned to write its name in someone else's book.

*Soon,* sang the well. *Soon,* beat the coin. And beneath the city, in a palace Tam had never seen, a king smiled at his reflection, and the reflection smiled back a half-second late, and somewhere between the two smiles a door that had been closed for twenty years quietly began to open.

Tam woke before dawn with the taste of copper in his mouth and Lena's name—her true name, the one written in the guild's oldest books—already rising in him in the old language, unasked, unwanted, the way water rises in a well that something below is pulling on.

He did not know yet what it meant that he knew her name.

He only knew that the dream had taken it, and that taking was what the dream did, and that morning, when Lena climbed the stairs with bread and ink, the wards on the counting house door hummed a single low note as she entered—recognition, or warning, or welcome—and Tam could not tell which, and neither, he suspected, could they.
