## 4. The Appraisal

The inquisitor arrived on a morning that smelled of iron and coming frost, and Lena knew it before the knock came, because the wards along the counting house door—the ones that had answered for Tam—went quiet all at once, like birds hushing before a hawk crosses the field.

She was at her desk in the counting room, third ledger open, when she heard the carriage. Not the rattle of a tradesman's cart or the soft creak of a debt-collector's gig, but the deep, deliberate roll of Crown wheels, each spoke oiled, each horse shod in muffled iron. The Gilded Veil had not received a royal visitor in forty years. The last one, according to the marginalia in the oldest ledger, had come to *apologize*.

"Master Corvane," Lena called through the connecting door. "There's a coach outside. It has the sun-and-scales on the panel."

The silence from Corvane's study lasted three heartbeats too long. When he answered, his voice had the particular smoothness he wore like a second coat on bad days. "The back room, Miss Marchetti. Take the boy. Now."

Tam was already awake. He slept in the narrow cot behind the ink stores these days, ever since Oskar had grumbled that the boy's dreams made the candle flames lean toward him in the night—and this morning he was sitting up with his knees drawn to his chest, eyes wide and dark as spilled ink.

"He's here," Tam said. Not in the old language. In plain trade-tongue, which he'd been learning word by word from Lena's patient lessons, and which somehow made it worse. "The man who smells of chains."

"No one is going to chain you." Lena took his cold hands in hers. "We're going to play a game. Can you play a game? You're very good at being invisible. Better than me, even. I've had years of practice."

"What do I have to be?"

"Nothing," Lena said. "That's the trick of it. You have to be nothing at all."

She settled him into the gap between the shelved ledgers and the wall—a space barely wider than a coffin, though she did not let herself think that thought—and stacked two volumes of defunct shipping contracts in front of him. Then she pulled the hanging ledger-cloth across, the one embroidered with the Veil's sigil, scales balanced over an open book, and stood back.

From the shelves, faintly, she heard Tam breathing. Slow. Even. Practiced nothingness.

In the front hall, the knocker fell three times, precise as a headsman counting strokes.

* * *

Dame Serah Voss was not what Lena expected, which was its own kind of terror. She had imagined a brute in royal livery, all scarlet and threat. Instead the woman who stepped into the counting house was small and gray and neat as a filed document, perhaps fifty, with a face like a closed account and eyes that moved over the room the way Lena's own eyes moved over a ledger—reading everything, trusting nothing, totaling silently.

"Dame Serah Voss," she said, and did not offer her hand. "Office of Registries. His Majesty's crown has undertaken a survey of the chartered guilds. There are concerns regarding... solvency."

"Master Corvane will receive you in the reading room," Lena said, and her voice held, which felt like a miracle performed by someone else.

Corvane swept in a moment later, silver-haired and gracious, every inch the dying guild's dignified master, and Lena retreated to her desk with her quills and her ledgers and made herself into furniture. This, at least, she knew how to do. She had spent eleven years being furniture. She had spent eleven years learning that the safest place in any room was the place no one looked.

But Voss looked. That was the trouble with the woman. Her gaze snagged on things—the water stain on the ceiling where the roof leaked since the wards' maintenance fund ran dry; the dust-free rectangle on the mantel where a charter once hung; the door to the back rooms, which Corvane had locked, and which Voss regarded for exactly one second too long.

"The registries," Voss said, spreading gloved hands. "All inventories. All transactions for the past decade. The Crown wishes to confirm what remains of the guilds' holdings before the charters are formally reviewed."

"Reviewed," Corvane repeated pleasantly. "A generous word."

"Some words are." Voss smiled without warmth. "Others are *audited*."

Lena fetched the ledgers. All of them, because refusing anything would be worse than giving everything, and she laid them out on the reading table in chronological order while Voss removed her gloves finger by finger, unhurried, a woman who had never once needed to hurry in her life.

For an hour the only sounds were turning pages and the tick of the case clock. Lena worked at her desk with her back to the room and heard every page turn anyway, the way you hear your own heartbeat. Voss read the way Tam spoke, Lena thought wildly—in fragments that made things obey. Once, the inquisitor paused so long on a page that Lena's quill stopped moving entirely, and she had to dip it twice before the ink would take.

It was the third ledger that ended them. The current one. The one with Tam's words copied into the margins in Lena's own hand, hidden beneath a false column of candle inventory—or so she had believed. She heard Voss stop. Heard the small sound of a fingertip pressed flat against paper.

"Master Corvane." Voss's voice did not rise. It didn't need to. "Your candle inventory lists forty-one tallow dips purchased in the spring. Yet I count entries for *forty-one candles relit*, dated nightly, in a hand that is not your registrar's."

"A figure of speech," Corvane said smoothly. "Guild shorthand. We record services rendered."

"Shorthand." A page turned. Another pause, longer. "And this entry, dated nine days ago. *Subject acquired. Language: pre-dynastic. Appraisal pending.*"

The clock ticked. Somewhere in the walls, Lena thought she heard the wards holding their breath.

"That entry," Corvane said, and here his voice changed—dropped its silk and found something harder underneath, the voice of a man stepping off a ledge and discovering he could fly—"is precisely why I'm glad you've come, Dame Voss. Because what we have in this house is worth far more than a registry line. And the Crown, I suspect, would prefer to acquire it *privately*. Off the books, as it were."

Lena's quill tore through the page.

*Off the books.* Three words that in the Gilded Veil were not merely criminal but obscene. Every transaction, every debt, every impossible thing the guild had ever brokered lived in those ledgers because the ledgers themselves were the magic—the accounting *was* the binding, the record *was* the reality. To sell something off the books was to sell it out of the world's memory. Unrecorded. Ungrieved. Unavenged.

He was selling Tam the way you'd sell a stolen ring. No provenance. No witnesses. No boy.

"And the price?" Voss asked.

"My survival. Full immunity. The charter reviewed favorably—or quietly buried. And the matter of my predecessor's debts resolved in my favor." Corvane laughed softly. "You think I don't know what this survey truly is, Dame Voss. Dying guilds get audited. Dead guilds get dissolved. I am offering you the difference between a scandal and a solution."

"You are offering me a child."

"I am offering the Crown a *fragment*." A chair creaked; Corvane had risen. "You know what walks in the palace. Don't insult us both. Whatever wears the king's face has been shedding pieces for years, and my registrar has caught one in a jar. Name your own figure. The Crown has deep pockets and deeper secrets, and I am a modest man with modest needs."

Silence. Long enough that Lena dared turn her head, just slightly, just enough.

Voss sat with both hands flat on the open ledger, head bowed, as if in prayer. When she looked up, her face was perfectly composed and her eyes were doing arithmetic Lena couldn't read.

"I'll need to inspect the asset," Voss said. "Before terms are discussed."

"Of course. This way—"

"*Alone,* Master Corvane. Office procedure. You'll wait here."

* * *

Lena did not remember deciding to move. She was simply standing, quill still in hand, placing herself between Voss and the back-room door with her heart slamming against her ribs like something trying to escape a locked ledger.

"The back rooms are guild property," she said. "Sealed under charter law. No inspection without a writ countersigned by the sitting master *and* entered into the registry. It's in the founding statutes. Article nine."

Voss studied her. Up close, her eyes were pale gray, almost colorless, and utterly patient. "You are?"

"Lena Marchetti. Registrar."

"Eleven years?" Voss glanced at the desk, at the ledgers, at the torn page. "Your figures are beautiful, Miss Marchetti. I've audited four hundred registries in six years, and yours are the first I've read twice for pleasure." She stepped closer, voice dropping to something meant for no one else. "Which makes it a great pity that they're about to contain a lie."

"I don't—"

"Article nine is real. So is the writ requirement. And so is this." From inside her coat, Voss drew a folded paper—not a writ, Lena saw, but a list, written in a clerk's careful hand. Names. Dates. *"The singing well, sealed by order. The priestess of Saint Verene's, committed. The coin recovered from the drowned quarter."* "I have been collecting your scattered pieces for two years, child, and I am *tired*. So here is what happens now. You will open that door, and I will see the boy, and then you and I are going to discuss what gets written in that ledger of yours. Because whatever Corvane believes he is selling—" her mouth thinned, "—the Crown does not buy children. But someone must record what actually happened here, and records, Miss Marchetti, are your profession."

Every instinct Lena owned screamed at her to stay furniture. To be nothing. Eleven years of practice, and it all came down to this: a locked door, a trembling hand, a woman in gray waiting.

Behind the door, faintly, Tam began to hum—an old, old melody in an older tongue, the one that made rust unwind and dead wicks bloom. As if he knew. As if he had always known how stories like this went, and had decided, in his fragmentary, borrowed way, to trust her.

Lena unlocked the door.

* * *

What followed, she would remember all her life in ledger-terms, because it was the only language adequate to it.

They stood together in the back room—Voss, composed and watchful; Tam, blinking in the lamplight, small and barefoot and unafraid in a way that broke Lena's heart sideways—and Lena opened the third ledger to the damning page. *Subject acquired. Appraisal pending.*

"Strike it," Voss said quietly. "Write instead: *No such entry was ever made. No such subject exists within these walls. The Gilded Veil holds nothing the Crown seeks.* Sign it. Date it. Make it true."

"It isn't true."

"No," Voss agreed. "It isn't. That is rather the point. The guild's magic accepts what is *recorded*, does it not? Your own statutes. The record is the reality. So make the record say the boy was never here—and watch whether the old magic loves its books enough to believe you."

Her hand shook so badly the first attempt blotted. Lena set the quill down, pressed her palms flat against the desk, and thought of Tam humming in the dark between the shelves. Thought of Corvane's voice, smooth as oil, pricing a child like smuggled brandy. Thought of eleven years of balancing other people's sins in beautiful columns while calling it honesty.

Then she wrote:

*No such entry was ever made. No such subject exists within these walls.*

The moment the ink dried, the counting house *shivered*. Not the walls—the wards, the deep old guild-magic woven into the charter itself, waking for the first time in decades. Lena felt it move through the floor, through the desk, through the very ledger under her hand: a long, considering pause, like an ancient clerk reviewing a submission. Testing the figure. Checking the sum.

And accepting it.

The entry about Tam faded from the page—not crossed out but *unwritten*, the ink lifting away like breath from glass until the paper showed clean. In the margin, where his words had been copied, the old language dimmed to nothing. Somewhere in the house, Lena knew, the same erasure was moving through every record, every index, every memory the guild kept in ink. The Gilded Veil had decided, by its own oldest law, that Tam Marchetti—for she had written the surname without thinking, and the magic had kept it—did not exist.

And therefore could not be sold.

"There," Voss said softly. "Now you're a forger, Miss Marchetti. Congratulations. It's the most honest thing you've ever done." She was already moving to the door, gloves back on, voice rising to its official register for Corvane's benefit. "Master! Your registries are in order. Nothing of interest to the Crown. You may expect the standard certificate within the fortnight."

Through the connecting door, Lena heard Corvane's relief begin—and falter, because men like Corvane could smell a deal slipping away like blood in water. She heard him start to speak of private arrangements, of assets held elsewhere, and Voss cut him off with pleasant, impenetrable courtesy, gathering her papers, taking her time.

At the threshold, she paused beside Lena and murmured, without looking at her: "The well. The priestess. The coin. Someone is gathering them faster than I can. When they come for the last piece—and they will come—you'll want a friend inside the Crown. Keep your books clean, registrar. Keep your boy invisible. And keep your door unlocked at night."

Then she was gone, and the carriage rolled away, and the house was silent except for the clock and the small sound of Tam's bare feet on the floorboards as he came to stand beside her.

"You lied," he said, in trade-tongue, carefully, tasting each word. "For me."

"I falsified a record," Lena said. Her hands had finally stopped shaking, and what remained in their place was something new and steady and slightly terrifying—a purpose, entered in full, signed and dated. "The first lie I ever told. And the guild believed it." She looked down at the clean page, at the blank space where a boy had been erased and a family had been written. "Do you know what that means, Tam? It means the Veil chose. And it chose you."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, came the sound of Corvane locking his study door—and beneath it, softer, stranger, the whisper of parchment being fed into a fire.

Lena's blood went cold. Masters burned correspondence when they had something to hide.

Masters burned *charters* when they had something to sell.
