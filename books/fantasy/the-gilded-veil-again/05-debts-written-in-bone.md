## 5. Debts Written in Bone

Oskar Vane had been keeping the Gilded Veil's secrets for thirty-one years, and he had learned long ago that a guild which traded in impossible things kept its truest inventory in the dark.

He came down to the vault before dawn, as he always did on the last day of the month, when the candles were still guttering from the night watch and the air below the counting house tasted of cold stone and old ink. The vault was the oldest room in the building—older than the guild, if you believed the floor, which was laid in slabs of grey granite that no mason in living memory could name. The walls held niches, and the niches held ledgers. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Each one bound in leather gone soft as skin, spines cracked like knuckles, and each one holding accounts no honest merchant would recognize: bottled grief weighed against borrowed years; names not yet spoken, sold to mothers who wanted their children to be kind; a winter purchased whole and delivered to a dying woman so she might see one more snow.

Oskar had spent his life among these books and had never once been permitted to read the oldest of them.

That was the rule, and Oskar was a man of rules. It was why Corvane kept him. Lena was the talent—the girl could balance a ledger of dreams without waking—but Oskar was the lock. He was broad and slow and utterly reliable, a man shaped like a door, and Master Corvane trusted him with everything except the truth.

Tonight, the truth had come looking for him anyway.

* * *

It began, as most ruinous things did, with an audit.

Corvane had summoned him at midnight, his study thick with the smoke of burned correspondence. Three days now the master had been feeding papers to the fire, and the ash had gotten into everything—into the carpet, into Oskar's beard when he leaned close, into the wine Corvane drank with a shaking hand while insisting, yet again, that the guild was merely *weathering a difficult season*.

"The Crown's inquisitor comes within the week," Corvane said. "Voss. She'll want the registries. She'll want everything." He turned a charred scrap over with his penknife, as if the letter might reassemble itself under inspection. "Everything must be in order, Oskar. Everything must be *boring*. Do you understand? A dying guild is interesting. An insolvent one is a scandal. But a small, sad, honest ledger—" he smiled, and the smile didn't reach anywhere near his eyes, "—a boring book gets filed and forgotten."

"And the boy?" Oskar asked carefully.

The knife stopped moving.

"What boy?"

"The one in the east room, Master. Barefoot. Speaks the old tongue."

"Ah." Corvane set down the knife. "Yes. Him. He's not on any registry, Oskar. He doesn't exist. Which means when Dame Voss asks what's behind that door—and she will ask—you will tell her it's a storeroom. Damp. Mice. Nothing worth the key."

Oskar nodded, because nodding was what he did, and went up to bed, and lay awake listening to the house breathe.

But it was not the lie that kept him awake. Lies were currency here; he'd spent them all his working life. What kept him awake was the boy himself, whom Oskar had seen only twice—once being led through the corridor by two hired men who flinched whenever the child spoke, and once through the gap of the east room door, sitting cross-legged on the floor while a candle stub beside him burned *downward*, its flame pooling like honey toward the wax, and the boy watching it with an expression Oskar recognized from his own mirror on certain nights.

Homesickness. That was the expression. The boy was homesick for somewhere that wasn't a place.

Oskar got up. He dressed. And then, because sleep would not have him, because the house felt wrong tonight, creaking in rhythms that didn't match the wind, he took the ring of keys from its hook and went down—not to the east room, but deeper. To the vault. To the niche at the very back that every guildmaster sealed upon taking office and that no member, in three hundred years, had opened.

The Charter niche.

He told himself it was duty. Corvane needed the founding documents ready for Voss; that was reasonable; that was *boring*, even. He told himself this all the way down the stairs, past the ward-lamps that woke as he passed, humming like bees, until he stood before the iron door with the seven seals, and his hands—steady hands, door-hands, hands that never trembled—trembled.

The seals broke easier than they should have. That should have warned him. Three centuries of guildmasters' oaths, and the wax crumbled like bread.

Inside was a single ledger, and beneath it, wrapped in oilcloth, something heavy and round that clinked when he moved it. He unwrapped the ledger first. The leather was black—not dyed, he understood immediately, but stained, soaked through once with something that had dried dark—and the title page bore no title. Only a date. The year of Aldric the First's coronation.

And a line of accounting, written in a hand so fine it looked embroidered:

*Received: one crown, one kingdom, one line unending.*
*Paid: one charter, sealed and self-renewing.*
*Brokered by the Gilded Veil, who ask no questions and keep no copies.*

Oskar read it four times. His lips moved. The vault hummed around him, patient as a confessor.

There was more. Page after page, in different hands across different generations, all of it maintenance—all of it *bookkeeping*. Annual entries. Tithes. A column headed simply *Host*, and in it, generation after generation, a name: Aldric. Aldric. Aldric. Each entry noting adjustments made, payments rendered, silences purchased. And each entry, near the bottom, in smaller script, as though the writer could not help themselves:

*It grows. It eats. We do not speak of what it eats.*

*The king dreamed last night of drowning. We increased the tithe. He smiled at court today and did not know why.*

*The host is thinning. The thing inside him has begun to shed.*

Oskar sat down on the cold floor, the black ledger open across his knees, and understood, in the slow way that large men understand large things, that he had spent his life guarding a wound and calling it a wall. The Gilded Veil did not trade in impossible things.

It had *made* one. It had brokered the bargain that slid the parasite beneath King Aldric's skin—it or some ancestor of it, some earlier hunger wearing the same crown—and in exchange, the guild received its charter: sealed, self-renewing, above audit, above law, above memory. Every impossible thing the Veil had ever sold had been bought with a kingdom's stolen bloodline. Every bottle of grief on the shelf upstairs had been priced against a dynasty's hollowing.

We ask no questions. We keep no copies.

Except someone had. Someone in every generation had copied the truth into this black book and hidden it where no one dared look, because the guilt had to go somewhere, and paper was cheaper than confession.

Under the ledger, the oilcloth bundle. Oskar unwrapped it with fingers that no longer pretended to be steady.

A coin. Old, blackened, larger than a fist. And bleeding—there was no other word for it—a slow bead of red welling up from the embossed face and running down Oskar's wrist, warm as breath.

Tam's dream, Lena had said, translating fragments in her careful voice: *the singing well, the mad priestess, the coin that bleeds.* The fragments were waking. They were scattered through the city like seeds, and they were turning toward each other, and toward the crown, and the black book in Oskar's lap said, in a dead scrivener's neat hand:

*When the pieces remember, the contract comes due.*

Oskar wrapped the coin again, quickly, though wrapping it felt like closing a coffin lid on something still breathing. He put the ledger back. He resealed the door with fresh wax and pressed his thumb into it—the oldest mark of Veil custody, *mine, I saw, I hold*—and stood there a long moment in the humming dark, a man-shaped door that had finally seen what it was guarding.

Then he climbed the stairs to put the whole thing out of his mind, because that was his function, that had always been his function, and because the alternative was to run screaming into the street.

* * *

He found the forgery two hours later, and it saved him, in a way, from thinking about the coin.

Lena's desk in the counting room was immaculate—she was the only person in the guild whose workspace he'd never had to tidy, whose pens always lay parallel, whose ink pots were capped—and that was how he noticed. One register sat slightly askew. The October registry, the one that would go to Dame Voss, and Oskar knew every entry in it by heart because he had drafted half of them himself.

There was a new one. Third page, bottom margin, in ink that matched perfectly, in a hand that imitated his own blocky capitals almost flawlessly.

*Item 114: one storeroom, east wing. Contents: damp goods, rodent damage. Assessed value: nil.*

Almost flawless. Almost. The *n* in *nil* had Lena's little forward lean, the tilt she gave every letter like a listener leaning toward a speaker. Thirty years of checking ledgers had taught Oskar to read hands the way other men read faces.

She had forged an entry. In his hand. To hide the boy.

Oskar stood over the desk for a long time, the vault's cold still in his bones, the black ledger's words still swimming behind his eyes. *We do not speak of what it eats.*

Corvane would have her flogged. Worse—Corvane would sell her, the way he was selling everything else, the way the guild had sold a kingdom three hundred years ago and called it brokerage. Forgery against the registry was the one crime the Veil's law punished with erasure: your name struck from every record, every memory of you taxed out of existence. Oskar had seen it done once, to a smuggler named Pell, and afterward no one could say what they'd lost, only that a chair stood empty and grieved.

All he had to do was carry the register to Corvane's study. All he had to do was be the lock, the door, the reliable man. Thirty-one years of belonging pressed on his shoulders like the vault's stone ceiling—belonging bought with silence, built on buried sin, a home erected over a grave and swept daily.

*Belonging cannot be appraised,* Lena had said once, laughing, when Oskar complained about the price of candlewax. She'd been young then. They both had.

Oskar picked up the register, squared it on the desk, and turned instead to the shelf where the clean ink waited. He uncapped a pot, took a fresh sheet, and wrote—not a report, not a betrayal, but a note, in his own plain hand, which she would know instantly was his:

*Your n leans. Change it. — O.*

He left it beneath the register where only she would look, banked the counting room lamps, and went to stand his morning watch at the front doors as he had for thirty-one years.

Outside, the city was waking, and somewhere in it a well was singing, and a priestess was raving, and a coin was bleeding in a locked vault below his feet, and a king rode nowhere, smiling at nothing.

Oskar straightened his collar, arranged his broad face into its usual blankness, and prepared, one final time, to be a boring man guarding an interesting door.

He did not see Dame Serah Voss until she was already standing at the gate, silver-trimmed and patient, watching the counting house with the expression of someone reading a ledger only she could see.

"You're awake early," she said pleasantly. "Good. I hate arriving to sleeping houses. There's so much people hide when they think no one's counting."

She stepped past him into the hall of the Gilded Veil, and Oskar, loyal to his master, faithful to his guild, guilty beyond all appraisal, closed the door behind her.
