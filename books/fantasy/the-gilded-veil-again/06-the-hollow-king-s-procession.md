## 6. The Hollow King's Procession

The bells woke the city before the king did.

Lena heard them from the counting house, where she had not slept—had not tried to sleep—since the night Tam's words first made the candleflames stand up like soldiers at attention. Six bells, then a seventh that did not belong to any tower she knew. It rang low and wrong, a note felt in the teeth rather than the ear, and every ledger on her desk shivered in its binding.

She was at the window before she understood why.

Down the processional way, banners unfurled in the grey light—gold and black, the crowned stag of House Aldric—and beneath them came the king.

Aldric had not ridden publicly in six years. Everyone knew it the way everyone knows weather: the Hollow King, they called him in the taverns, in whispers behind cupped hands, because he appeared at windows like a man pressed against glass, because his decrees arrived written in a hand that changed month to month, because once—only once—a chambermaid had spoken aloud about the way his smile sat on his face without moving anything else. She'd been dismissed within the hour. The story remained.

Now he rode a white destrier down the avenue, and the crowd along the barriers was the largest Lena had seen since her childhood, when the guild still mattered enough to march in processions of its own.

He was smiling.

That was the first thing. Not the crown, though the crown looked heavier than crowns should—not merely gold but dense, the way a stone is dense at the bottom of a river. Not the guards, though there were too many of them, and their eyes kept flicking toward their own king as if he were the danger in the street. The smile. It was wide and warm and perfect, and it did not change. It had been painted on, Lena thought, by someone who had studied smiles from a great distance and never been asked to wear one.

And the city turned toward him.

Not the people—the people cheered, or stood silent, or held up children to see. But other things turned. Lena felt it before she saw it, a pulling sensation behind her sternum, like the moment before a door swings open on its hinges. Down in the counting house, the inventory stirred. Bottled griefs knocked softly against their shelf glass. The jar of borrowed years—three decades of them, seized from a debtor who never paid—began to glow through its lead lining like a lantern lit inside a skull.

Tam screamed.

She found him in the storeroom where Oskar had locked him—*until he can be appraised*, Corvane had said, and appraised was a word Lena had come to hate with her whole body—and the boy was pressed into the corner beneath the shelf of unlabeled griefs, hands over his ears, and he was *fading*. Not dying. Fading. His edges had gone soft the way ink goes soft in rain, and where his left hand gripped his knee she could see the wood grain of the floorboards through his fingers.

"No," Lena said, kneeling. "No, no—Tam. Look at me."

His eyes found hers and they were wrong—not brown anymore but shot through with gold filaments, threads of light moving under the surface like something swimming. When he spoke, it was in that older-than-kingdoms tongue, and for the first time Lena did not need Oskar's forbidden texts to feel the shape of it. The words pulled at her. Everything pulled at everything now. The whole city had become one great ledger, and somewhere down the avenue a hand was drawing a line beneath all the columns, calling every figure home to be summed.

"He's coming," Tam whispered, in trade-tongue this time, in *her* language, which frightened her more than the other. "I can hear him thinking. I didn't know it was thinking. I thought it was only sleeping in him." He looked down at his dissolving hand. "I'm going home, Lena. He wants his pieces back."

"You're not pieces," she said, and heard how thin it sounded. "You're a boy. You eat bread. You cry at night when you think no one hears. Kings don't do that."

"Neither does it," Tam said. "That's what's wrong with it."

She got him up. There was no plan—that was the truth she would not let herself speak even internally, because plans were ledger-things and what she was doing was off the books entirely—but there was a direction, and the direction was away. Out through the coal yard, past the ward-stones that knew her step and parted grudgingly, into the streets where the procession's noise rolled like surf.

The city was mad with it. Shopkeepers had abandoned their counters. A woman wept into an apron without knowing why. And everywhere, small wrong things: a fountain singing in a voice no fountain should have; a priestess of the Lantern Order standing in her doorway, eyes rolled white, mouthing words in Tam's language; a coin spinning on a cobbler's step, refusing to fall, bleeding a thin line of red into the gutter. The fragments. They were waking, all of them, and all of them leaning toward the avenue like grass bending under wind.

Lena walked against the current of the crowd with Tam's hand in hers, and the boy weighed less with every block. By the time they reached the tannery district his grip was a suggestion rather than a pressure, and she could see the cobbles through his wrist, and she was running now, really running, because there was a name—one name, half-remembered from a text Oskar had burned—an anchor-rite, something about holding a fragment to itself—

"Lady Marchetti."

The voice came from the alley mouth ahead, and Lena stopped so hard Tam stumbled into her legs.

Dame Serah Voss stood between a cooper's shop and a wall stained with tannin-black water. No armor. Plain travel clothes, a sword worn like an afterthought, and those grey eyes that took in everything and priced nothing—or priced everything, Lena couldn't decide which was worse. Two of Voss's people fanned out behind her with the unhurried ease of people who have done this many times.

"Inquisitor," Lena managed.

"You have the boy." Not a question. "Master Corvane reported a discrepancy in the registries. An unappraised asset, off the books." Her mouth curved, without warmth. "He sold you very cheaply, by the way. Your name was the second thing he offered me."

Tam's fingers tightened—or tried to—in hers. Lena stepped in front of him, an absurd gesture, a bookkeeper's body between a child and the Crown, and heard herself say: "Then take me and leave him. He's just a child. Whatever Corvane promised you—"

"Corvane," Voss said, "promised me nothing worth having. Which is why I'm here instead of him." She moved closer, and her people let her, and Lena saw now how tired she was—not soft, never soft, but tired in the deep way of someone carrying a weight they've stopped mentioning. Voss looked past Lena at Tam. At his fading hand. Something crossed her face that was not triumph and was not pity. Recognition, maybe. Grief's poor cousin.

"Do you know what I serve, Lady Marchetti?" she asked quietly.

"The Crown."

"The crown," Voss corrected, "is a hat. I serve what the crown is supposed to mean. There's a difference, and lately the difference has grown teeth." She drew a folded paper from her coat—unsealed, unofficial, covered in her own cramped handwriting. "Six years ago King Aldric stopped sleeping. Then he stopped eating in public. Then he stopped being Aldric. My predecessor investigated. My predecessor is buried under a monument to his loyal service. I have spent six years reading coronation records, blood-oath ledgers, and"—her eyes flicked to Lena, sharp as a scale finding balance—"guild charters. Yours especially. The Gilded Veil brokered something once. Something old. And whatever it put on that throne has begun calling itself home."

Behind her, across the city, the false seventh bell rang again. Tam made a small sound and sagged, and Lena caught him—caught most of him; her arms passed through his shoulder before the weight resolved—and Voss watched this happen with the stillness of a woman confirming a suspicion she had prayed was wrong.

"It's taking him back," Lena said. "Piece by piece. He's—" Her voice cracked and she hated it and let it crack anyway. "He eats bread. He cries at night. He's *a boy*, and it wants to unmake him into arithmetic."

For a long moment the alley held only the sound of the distant crowd roaring for their smiling king, and the fountain singing two streets over, and Tam breathing in a rhythm that was becoming less certain by the hour.

"I should arrest you both," Voss said finally. "It's what the registry requires. It's what keeps my head attached and my investigation breathing." She folded the paper away. "But the registry serves the charter, and the charter serves the thing on the throne, and I find I've stopped taking dictation from parasites." She met Lena's eyes. "There's a crypt beneath the coronation chapel. Old foundations, older oaths. If your boy is drawn home anywhere, it's there—and if we wait for him to fade entirely, we lose the only piece of that creature that ever wanted to be something else."

Lena stared at her. "You want to use him."

"I want to *save* him," Voss said, "and destroy what made him, and I suspect those are the same operation whether either of us likes it or not." She extended her arm, formal, soldierly—an offer of escort, or alliance, hard to say. "I can't give you guarantees, Lady Marchetti. I can give you my word, and my sword, and the fact that I could have taken you both ten minutes ago and chose conversation instead."

Trust, Lena thought, was a currency the Veil had taught her never to accept at face value. Every coin could be clipped. Every face could be worn by something else—she had seen the king's smile twenty minutes ago and knew it for proof.

But Tam lifted his fading head against her hip and looked at the inquisitor, and said, in that language older than kingdoms, one word. Lena didn't know it. She felt it anyway, the way you feel the meaning of a bell: *yes*.

She took Voss's arm.

"To the crypt, then," Lena said. "And Inquisitor—if we're doing this, you'll want to see what I've copied into my ledger. Every word the boy speaks. I think—I think they might be instructions."

Voss went very still.

"Instructions for whom?"

Lena looked down at Tam, at the gold threads swimming beneath his skin, already turning toward the chapel like iron filings toward a lodestone.

"That's what frightens me," she said. "I don't think we've been translating him. I think he's been teaching us."
