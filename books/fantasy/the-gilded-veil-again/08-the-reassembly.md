## 8. The Reassembly

The city was pulling itself apart, and Lena could feel it in her teeth.

They ran through streets that had gone strange with moonlight, Voss's armored boots striking sparks from the cobbles, Tam a pale weight against Lena's chest where she half-carried him, half-dragged him. He was lighter than he should have been. That was the horror of it—every day since the procession he had weighed less, as though the world were slowly remembering that he was only borrowed.

"Tell me again," Voss said without breaking stride. "Where."

"The coronation crypt. Under the Basilica of the Unconquered Sun." Lena's breath tore at her throat. "It's where they crowned Aldric. Where the bargain was sealed. The fragments are going home to the place they were made."

"And you're certain it's tonight?"

Lena looked down at the boy in her arms. His eyes were open but unfocused, fixed on something very far away or very deep inside. A thread of light—faint, gold, wrong—leaked from the corner of his mouth when he breathed.

"I'm certain," she said.

* * *

She had felt the first fragment wake three nights ago, standing over her ledgers in the counting house. It had been the well—the singing well on Tanner's Row, the one whose water had begun to hum an old coronation hymn in a voice like a drowned choir. The entry for it had been in the Veil's inventory for two hundred years: *one well-spirit, dormant, leased to the city under terms forgotten.* She had gone to look and found the water rising against its own gravity, reaching toward the palace, and every drop of it glowing faintly gold.

After the well came the mad priestess of Saint Ferren's, who had torn off her vestments and walked barefoot through the market screaming in the same language Tam spoke—not words, Lena understood now, but *coordinates*. And after the priestess, the bleeding coin, which Oskar had kept hidden in his coat pocket for years without knowing what it was, and which had burned through his shirt and left a golden scar shaped like a crown.

The pieces were waking because their center was calling them. The thing wearing King Aldric had finally grown hungry enough to stop pretending to be a man.

* * *

The Basilica doors stood open. No guards. Of course no guards—the king's own men would not be permitted to know what their sovereign did beneath the consecrated floor.

Voss went first, sword drawn, her face set in the expression Lena had come to think of as her armor: nothing behind it, nothing given away. But when they reached the crypt stairs and the cold air rose up around them smelling of stone and old incense and something else, something sweeter and fouler, like honey poured over a wound, Voss paused.

"Lena." Her voice was low. "Whatever happens down there. Whatever it offers you. It will offer. Understand that first."

"It already has," Lena said. "In my dreams. It offered me the guild restored. Full ledgers. Corvane's debts erased."

"What did you say?"

"I told it I'd need to see the collateral."

Something that was almost a smile crossed Voss's face, there and gone. Then they descended.

* * *

The crypt was a long vaulted chamber, older than the basilica above it, older than the kingdom perhaps. Sarcophagi lined the walls—dead kings in stone, their effigies worn faceless by centuries—and at the far end, beneath a dome painted with a sun whose gilt had flaked away until it looked like a dying star, stood King Aldric.

He was alone. He had dismissed everything human about himself, it seemed; he wore no crown, only plain clothes, as if he were coming home after a long journey and wanted to be comfortable. When he turned to them his smile was the one from the procession—that terrible, practiced curve that sat on his face like something nailed there.

"You brought him," the king said. His voice was gentle. It was the gentlest voice Lena had ever heard, and she understood immediately that this was a predator's gentleness, the softness of a thing that had never once needed force. "Thank you. I've been looking everywhere for him."

Tam stirred in Lena's arms. The gold light under his skin was brighter now, pulsing in time with something Lena could feel through the soles of her boots—a heartbeat in the floor, in the walls, in the dead kings' stones.

"No," Tam said, and it was the most himself he had sounded in days, small and fierce and terrified. "No, I don't want—"

"Hush," said the king kindly. "You're tired. We're all so tired. Come home."

And Tam looked up at Lena, and his eyes were wet, and he said the words that broke her heart along a seam she hadn't known was there:

"Let me go."

* * *

She held him tighter. "No."

"He's mine," Aldric said, and now the gentleness thinned, showing wire beneath. "Do you understand what he is, ledger-keeper? He is the part of me that learned your name. The part that learned to want supper and stories and someone to scold him about muddy feet. He is a sentiment I contracted out, and the contract has lapsed, and I am here to collect."

"I know what he is," Lena said.

Her voice surprised her. It didn't shake. Somewhere between the forged ledgers and Oskar's body holding the counting house door and Corvane's charter burning to ash in traitor's hands, her voice had stopped shaking.

"He is the part of you that wanted to belong," she said. "That's what he is. And you don't want him back—you want to *unmake* that wanting, because it's the one thing you ever made that you couldn't control. You've spent thirty years hollowing out a king and wearing him like a coat, and the whole time, the piece of you that fell in love with being *small*, being *someone's*, being *fed and scolded and sung to*—" her arms tightened around Tam, who was crying now, silently, the way children cry when they have learned not to make noise about it— "that piece has been living in a barefoot boy who says please and thank you. You're not collecting property. You're amputating your own conscience."

The crypt went very quiet.

Then the king laughed, and the laugh was not a laugh; it was the sound the well had made, the sound the priestess had made, the sound of the language older than kingdoms, rendered into a human throat. The dead kings' sarcophagi wept gold light from their seams. The dome's dying star began to brighten.

"You mistake me for something that can be shamed," it said. "Come here, little fragment. Come home, or I will take the woman apart joint by joint and make you watch, and then you will come home anyway, and you will carry the memory of it inside yourself forever, because that is what you are—a memory-keeper. My memory-keeper."

Tam turned his face into Lena's shoulder. And then, so quietly she almost missed it, he spoke—not in the old language, but in plain trade-tongue, the words he had learned from her:

"You said... everything owed gets written down."

* * *

Yes. Yes, it did.

She set him down. She opened her satchel and took out the oldest ledger—the guild's founding book, rescued from Corvane's burning pile by Oskar's hands before Oskar's hands went cold, its cover cracked leather, its pages soft as cloth. The original contract was written somewhere in its depths, in ink that had never dried, the bargain that had bound a parasite to a royal line in exchange for a charter. Every contract could be amended. Every account could be balanced. That was the guild's first law and its last.

But she needed more than the old book. She needed a page clean enough to hold a name, and names required witnesses, and witnesses required—

"Voss." She didn't look up. "I need your oath. On your crown service, on whatever crown you actually serve. Witness me."

Dame Serah Voss knelt beside her in the dust of dead kings, laid her sword flat on the stones, and put her hand upon it. "Witnessed," she said. "Before the Sun, before the law, before whatever's left that's listening."

The gold light in the room surged. The parasite understood, then. She saw it understand—in Aldric's widening eyes, in the sudden shriek of pressure against her skull, in the way every fragment in the city screamed at once, a chorus of wells and coins and bones all crying *no* in unison.

It threw everything it had. Thirty years of stolen decades crashed against her like a wave—she saw the guild restored, marble halls, full coffers, Corvane kneeling in apology, Oskar alive and laughing, every debt forgiven, every door open, everything she had ever been denied, offered, *promised*, *guaranteed*—

Lena dipped her pen in the inkwell of her own blood where she had cut her palm against the satchel clasp running through the dark, and she wrote.

*Received, one parasite, name unknown, style of the ancient tongue, herein entered under the only name it ever chose for itself:*

She looked up. Tam was watching her, gold light pouring from his eyes, dissolving at the edges like sugar in water.

"Your name," she said gently. "The one you used when you knocked on my door. Say it."

And the boy—the fragment, the conscience, the piece of a monster that had learned to want supper and stories—opened his mouth and gave her the word, the true word, the word that was also a debt and an address and a surrender.

She wrote it down.

The ledger drank the ink. The pages began, softly, hungrily, to turn themselves.

* * *

The last line she wrote was the closing figure, and the closing figure was this: *paid in full—one childhood, honestly lived.*

The dome's false star guttered. The well on Tanner's Row, miles above them, went silent mid-hymn. And King Aldric folded up like a puppet whose strings had been cut, collapsing onto the stones of the crypt where he had been crowned, breathing, alive, empty-eyed, and—for the first time in thirty years—entirely, pitifully, only himself.

Tam lay in Lena's arms, solid, warm, heavy the way a boy ought to be. Whole. Hers, insofar as anyone belonged to anyone.

Above them, far above, the bells of the Basilica began to ring—not the coronation peal, but something older, wilder, a rhythm no bell-ringer commanded. And through the crypt's high slit of a window, Lena saw the sky over the capital begin to glow, not gold, but white, like a page being erased.

"Voss," she said quietly. "What's happening up there?"

The knight was staring at the window, and her face, for the first time since Lena had known her, showed everything.

"The Veil," Voss whispered. "Every veil. They're lifting—all of them, all at once. The binding took the guild's secrecy as payment. Everything the Gilded Veil ever hid—"

The bells rang louder. The white light spread.

"—is about to come out."
