## 1. The Courier's Last Mile

The storm came in from the west an hour before dusk, and Kestrel felt it the way a man feels an old scar ache before rain — not as weather, but as memory.

She stood at the head of the column on a shelf of black stone above the Greymarrow pass, one hand resting on the wind-scoured rock, and let the pressure of it roll through her. Distant thunder, still over the far ridges, too far to matter tonight. The air tasted of iron and pine and the particular cold that Thornfell kept even in high summer, hoarded in its shadowed ravines like a miser's coin.

Behind her, eleven smugglers and their mules picked their way up the switchbacks, quiet as a funeral procession. That was her doing. She'd trained them to be quiet — or rather, she'd refused to lead anyone who wouldn't learn.

"Storm's turning south," said Brannoc, coming up beside her. He was the closest thing the company had to a second: broad as a door, gray-bearded, with hands so knuckled by frostbite they looked like roots. He squinted at the western sky with the professional disinterest of a man who had spent forty years being rained on. "We'll get the edge of it, maybe. Nothing worse."

"Nothing worse," Kestrel agreed.

It was a lie between friends, the comfortable kind. They both knew there was no such thing as nothing worse in the Thornfells. There was only what you could carry and what you couldn't, and the mountain decided which was which.

"Distance to the waystation?" she asked.

Brannoc spat thoughtfully over the drop. "Four hours if the mules behave. Five if Tamlin's beast throws another fit about bridges."

"It's not a fit," Tamlin called up from below, wounded. "That bridge is *wrong*, Kestrel. It hums."

"Everything in these mountains hums if you listen long enough," Kestrel said. "Move."

The column moved. She fell into place at its head, because that was where she always walked — first through the bad ground, last to eat, the one whose word ended arguments. Not because anyone had voted her into it. Because three years ago she'd come down out of the high passes half-starved with frostbite eating her left hand, and the smugglers of Thornfell had taken her in without asking a single question about where she'd come from or why her eyes went flat and strange when thunder rolled. In a land of outlaws and exiles, that kind of silence was worth more than gold, and Kestrel Eriksen paid for it the only way she knew how: by keeping people alive.

The pass narrowed as they climbed. The walls of it rose sheer on either side, granite streaked white with old snowmelt, and the trail itself was barely wide enough for the mules — a shelf of broken rock stitched together with rope handlines that some forgotten generation had driven into the cliff face. Below, a river ran milky with glacier silt, loud enough that conversation died and the world shrank to footsteps and breath and the creak of harness leather.

Kestrel liked this part best. The part where words were impossible and only the work remained. Her left hand ached inside its glove — it always ached now, the two fingers that hadn't quite healed right, curled like a bird's foot — and she flexed it against the pain the way she did a hundred times a day, without thinking, without letting herself think.

*You killed him,* the mountain wind seemed to say, the way it always did when the pressure dropped. *You meant to kill the other one, and you killed him instead, and now here you are.*

"Not tonight," she murmured to the wind.

The wind, being the wind, didn't answer.

* * *

They made the waystation as full dark settled, and found the door standing open.

Kestrel stopped ten paces out, one fist raised, and the whole column froze behind her with the discipline of people who had learned that her raised fist was the difference between growing old and not. The waystation was a squat stone shelter built into the mountainside, one of a dozen the old mining companies had raised along the passes a century back — a single room, a hearth, bunks, a roof that mostly kept the weather honest. Its door should have been shut. Everyone who used these trails shut doors; it was the first law of the mountains, older than courtesy.

Wind moved through the gap, carrying the smell of woodsmoke and blood.

"Brannoc," she breathed. "Circle wide. Torches lit but hooded. Nobody comes through that door until I say."

He nodded once and melted back into the dark, and Kestrel drew her knife and went forward alone, low against the rock, the way she'd learned to move in another life she tried very hard not to remember.

Inside, the hearth was burning low. A kettle hung over it, untouched, gone cold hours ago. And slumped against the wall beside the door — as though he'd crawled the last stretch on his belly and dragged himself upright just to reach it — was a man in the mud-stained livery of a royal courier.

He was dying. Kestrel had seen enough death to know it at a glance, the way you know a storm's direction: the gray lips, the sweat-slick skin in freezing air, the terrible economy of his breathing, each breath bought at cost. Three bolts stood in him. Crossbow, close range, fletched in city style. One in the shoulder, one low in the belly, and one — the killing one — high in the chest, dark blood seeping slow around the shaft with every heartbeat.

His eyes found her in the doorway. They were clear, which was worse somehow. Dying men with clear eyes had something to say.

"Shut the door," he rasped.

Kestrel weighed it for exactly one breath. Then she shut the door, because whatever had shot this man might still be on the trail behind him, and an open door was an invitation.

"Who shot you?"

"Doesn't—" A cough took him, wet and deep. "Doesn't matter. They'll be along. Or they won't." His hand scrabbled weakly at the courier's satchel strapped across his body, fingers slipping on the buckle. "Help me. Please. I've been carrying it since Vhalen's Ford and I can't— I was told to find someone. Anyone. Going into the deep passes."

"You found smugglers," Kestrel said flatly. "We don't carry letters. We carry brandy and stolen wool, and we don't get involved in crown business, and crown business is what gets a man filled with crossbow bolts on a mountain trail."

"Not a letter." His mouth twisted — pain, or something adjacent to laughter. "Listen to me. I'm past lying and you're past running. Take it or leave it, but take it *fast*."

Against every instinct she owned, Kestrel crossed the room and knelt beside him. Up close he was younger than she'd thought — twenty-five, maybe thirty, with a scholar's soft hands under the road grime. A courier who'd been something else once. His blood had soaked through to the stone floor and was already cooling tacky beneath her knee.

The buckle fought her; his hands shook too badly, hers too stiff. Between them they got the satchel open. Inside: a wax-sealed dispatch tube, cracked and empty. A water skin, punctured. And wrapped in oilcloth, bound with a leather cord, a book.

Not a ledger. Not a dispatch book. It was small, thick, bound in leather gone dark and supple with age, its corners worn round as river stones. Even before she unwrapped it, even before her thumb found the tooled design on the cover — a mountain range beneath a circle, the circle struck through with a single vertical line — Kestrel felt the wrongness of it. Like holding a door that opened onto winter.

"What is this?" she asked.

"A hundred years old. Older." His breath was failing; she could hear it, the rattle rising. "Royal archive. Sealed collection. I was sent to burn it — orders came down from the palace, burn it unread, no copies. But I read it." His eyes fixed on hers with fierce, desperate clarity. "Gods forgive me, I read it."

"That was stupid."

"Yes." The ghost of a smile. "It's a prophecy book. Not the fortune-teller filth — real ones, from before the kingdom, from the ash kings' time. Most of it's rotted or mad. But there are pages that aren't. Pages that..." He coughed again, longer this time, and when it passed there was blood on his lips and less of him behind his eyes. "There's a page for tomorrow. For tonight. I checked the calendar four times on the road. Four times."

"I don't want it."

"Nobody wants it. That's the whole—" breath, ragged, "—the whole point of prophecy, isn't it? Nobody ever wants it." His hand closed over her wrist, grip shockingly strong for a dying man, the strength that comes up out of the bottom of a person when everything else is spent. "My name is Ellery Vance. I was a courier of the realm for nine years. If anyone asks, tell them I died on my route. Tell them I didn't drop anything." His voice cracked. "Tell them I didn't drop anything."

"Who do I tell? Who's asking?"

"They'll ask." His gaze slid toward the windowless wall, toward the capital somewhere beyond the stone, and something in his face curdled from desperation into fear so pure it looked like awe. "They'll ask. The king will ask. The king is asking already, that's why the book burns, that's why I run, that's why—" He hauled in air. "Take it east. No. No, take it wherever you're going, it doesn't matter, it knows. The pages know. When the date comes, the ink—"

He stopped. All the strength went out of his hand at once, and Kestrel caught the book as it sagged, and for a moment the only sounds were the fire settling and the wind worrying the shutter and the thin whistle of the man's ruined breathing.

"The date," he whispered. "Kestrel. The date is tonight."

Cold walked up her spine on small feet.

"I never told you my name."

Something flickered in his fading eyes — apology, maybe, or pity. "It's in the book," he said. "Your name is in the book."

Then the light in him guttered, and he slid sideways against the wall, and Kestrel knelt on a stranger's cooling blood holding a century-old book while the fire burned down to coals, and outside the wind changed direction and began, very softly, to sing.

* * *

Brannoc found her there twenty minutes later, still kneeling, the book closed in her lap like something she'd been caught praying over.

"He's dead," she said, before Brannoc could speak. "Couriers. Three of them, probably — he said *they'll be along*. Armed, mounted, city-fletched bolts. We break camp now, we're over the saddle before they reach the waystation."

Brannoc looked at the dead man. At the blood. At the book. He'd known her three years, and in three years he'd learned that Kestrel Eriksen gave orders in complete sentences and never, ever looked shaken — and so the fact that she was sitting motionless in a dead man's blood told him more than any confession would have.

"Kess," he said gently. "What did he give you?"

"Trouble." She stood. Her knees cracked; her left hand throbbed in time with her pulse. "A dead man's trouble. We bury him properly — cairn, not a trench, he was crown service and it costs us nothing to be decent. Then we move."

"And the trouble?"

She looked down at the book. Dark leather, worn soft, the struck-through circle staring up at her like a blind eye. Every instinct she had honed in three years of survival said the same thing: throw it in the ravine. Let the river take it. Dead couriers and sealed archives and kings who burn books were problems for people with walls around them, and she had no walls, only a pass and eleven lives that trusted her to keep them boring and alive.

But the man had said her name. Had said it like a key fitting a lock, and no dying stranger guessed names like *Eriksen* out of thin mountain air.

"Into my pack," she said. "And Brannoc — nobody hears about this. Not Tamlin, not the twins, nobody. As far as the company knows, we buried a courier and moved on."

"You're a terrible liar on your good days."

"Then I'll have a good day."

He held her gaze a moment longer, then shrugged his enormous shoulders and went to organize the burial, because that was the arrangement between them: she led, and he decided which of her orders to pretend not to see through.

* * *

They climbed through the night.

The storm's edge caught them an hour past midnight, as Brannoc had promised — nothing worse, just a cold drizzle that turned to sleet on the high shoulder of the saddle, needling sideways, erasing the trail behind them. Good. Sleet erased tracks. Kestrel set a hard pace and didn't look back at the waystation's smoke-smudge on the dark, and told herself the tightness in her chest was only the altitude.

Twice she caught herself with her hand inside her pack, fingers resting on the oilcloth wrapping, and twice she pulled them away.

At the first rest halt, huddled under an overhang while the mules steamed and stamped, she finally gave in. She told herself it was tactics — know what's chasing you — and knew she was lying, and did it anyway.

The book opened easily, its spine cracked in familiar places by a century of other hands. The paper was strange stuff, dense and faintly yellowed, and the ink had gone brown with age except where it hadn't. She turned past fragments — genealogies of dead kings, a diagram of the heavens drawn by someone who clearly believed the stars were nails pinning the sky down, a prayer to gods whose names had been chiseled off monuments before her grandmother was born. Mad things. Beautiful, mad things.

Then a page near the middle, and the sleet drumming on the stone above her, and her own heartbeat suddenly very loud.

The passage was short. The handwriting was different from the rest of the book — newer, sharper, as if some archivist a lifetime ago had copied it fresh from something older. It began mid-thought, the way real prophecies always did, indifferent to whoever came stumbling upon them:

*...and in the hour when the veil grows gilded and the crown forgets the head that bears it, there shall come up out of the west a walker of high places, a caller of storms, marked upon the left hand, whose name is written in exile. On the eve of the deepening she shall stand at the gate of the ashen kings, and the book shall know her, and she shall know the book.*

Below it, in that same later hand, someone had added a date.

Kestrel stared at it for a long time. The numbers swam and steadied. Swam and steadied.

Tonight.

Her left hand, unbidden, slipped free of its glove. In the meager light of the hooded lantern, the old burn scars stood pale across her knuckles, and the two crooked fingers cast little shadows like broken branches. *Marked upon the left hand.* Any fool with a fire and bad luck was marked on the left hand. Half the miners in Thornfell—

*A caller of storms.*

Thunder muttered somewhere far to the north, and the sound of it went through her like a hand closing around her heart.

She read the date again. Checked it against the tally she kept in her head, the one every careful person kept, the one that never failed her. Checked it a third time, the way the dead courier had checked it four times on the road, and understood with a sick, sliding certainty why he'd needed four tries.

The page was dated tonight.

"Kess!" Brannoc's voice, low, from the edge of the overhang. "Light on the back trail. Two of them. Maybe three. Moving fast."

Kestrel closed the book. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she'd have expected them to shake. Inside her chest, something had begun to tremble and could not seem to stop — not fear exactly, and not quite dread, but the feeling of standing at the lip of a drop in the dark, knowing the ground was somewhere below and refusing to say how far.

She wrapped the book in its oilcloth. Stowed it. Pulled her glove back over her scarred hand.

"How fast?" she asked.

"Fast," Brannoc said. "And Kess — they're not following our trail. They're following *his*. Which means they know he reached the waystation." His eyes went to her pack, brief and deliberate, and away again. "Which means they know what he was carrying. And who he gave it to."

The sleet ticked against the stones. Far off, thunder rolled again, closer now, patient as a debt.

Kestrel looked east, toward the high saddle and the country beyond it — toward a ruin she'd never visited but whose name now sat in her mind like a splinter, unasked-for, unshakeable. *The gate of the ashen kings.* She didn't believe in prophecy. She believed in weather, in rope, in the loyalty of people who fed you when you were starving. She believed in keeping her head down and her name small.

But a dead man had known her name, and armed riders were climbing through the night to collect what he'd carried, and the book in her pack was warm against her back like a living thing.

"Break camp," she said. "We move now."

"Where to?"

She hesitated one breath too long, and Brannoc saw it, and said nothing, which was its own answer.

"East," Kestrel said. "Toward the old fortress. And Brannoc — pray I'm wrong about why."
