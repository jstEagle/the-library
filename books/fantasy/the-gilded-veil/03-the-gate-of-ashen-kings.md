## 3. The Gate of Ashen Kings

Kestrel told herself she was going the wrong way on purpose.

That was the trick of it — if you were lost by design, no one could say you'd been led. She'd spent three days since the waystation walking a line through the Thornfells that no honest map would draw: up the scree fields where the smugglers' paths braided apart, past the frozen waterfall the locals called the White Ladder, along ridgelines that smelled of iron and old snow. Every mile took her further from the lowland passes where her cargo waited and closer to something the burned-and-returned page had described in ink that knew her handwriting better than she did.

*She will come by the high road that is not a road, beneath the mountain with the broken crown.*

There was only one mountain in the Thornfells that anyone called crowned. The locals had another name for it — *Ashen Kings*, because its summit was ringed with the blackened stumps of watchtowers from some war so old even Ondrey's kind of people had stopped writing about it. From the valley it did look like a head wearing a circlet of char.

She should have turned back at the White Ladder. She'd stood there for the better part of an hour, watching the ice groan, telling herself the same things she'd been telling herself since a dying man bled out against her knee: *You are not a story. You are a guide with bad luck and a scarred hand. Prophecies are what priests sell to people who can't afford hope.*

But the courier had died holding her wrist. And the page had come back from the fire with her name in it, spelled the way her mother used to spell it, before Eriksen was a name anyone said above a whisper.

So she went up.

The last stretch was a goat track hugging the mountain's northern face, narrow enough that she walked with one shoulder to the stone and let the drop have the rest of her attention. Wind came off the peaks in long white breaths. Below and behind her, the smuggling road wound down toward the tree line like a gray thread someone had dropped and never picked up. Her cargo was two days' march east with Doss and the twins, who thought she'd gone ahead to scout a warden patrol. A lie. She was getting good at those, or maybe she'd always been good at them and just hadn't needed so many.

The fortress announced itself first as a smell — cold ash and pigeon droppings — then as a shape against the sky: a gatehouse split down the middle like a cracked tooth, walls running out from it in broken lines, half-swallowed by scree. The gate itself hung open on one hinge, oak gone silver with age, carved with figures she didn't stop to study. Kings, probably. Ashen ones.

*At the gate of the ashen kings, the veil-breaker arrives as the hunt begins.*

She stood in the shadow of the wall and listened.

Wind. Ice ticking somewhere inside the ruins as it expanded in the afternoon cold. And then — faint, wrong, human — a shout, and after it the flat crack of a crossbow being spanned.

"Damn it," Kestrel said quietly, to no one, to herself, to whatever had written that page.

She could still walk away. That was the thing about cracks in the world; they only swallowed you if you stepped into them. She had a rule, and the rule had kept her alive for six years: *other people's trouble is a door you close.* She'd watched men bleed out in ditches rather than break it, once, when breaking it would have cost her nothing but time.

The shout came again, closer now, and this time she heard the words in it.

"—help me! Please—"

A woman's voice. Young. Cracking on the second word, which meant she'd been running longer than her body wanted to allow.

Kestrel closed her eyes. Opened them.

"Damn it," she said again, with feeling, and stepped through the gate.

* * *

Inside, the fortress was a drowned thing — courtyards full of drifted snow, colonnades collapsed into their own bones, a chapel whose roof had fallen inward so that the altar stood open to the sky like a mouth waiting to be fed. Kestrel moved along the inner wall with her hood up and her hand on her knife, reading the ground the way she read any trail.

Boot prints. A dozen men, military spacing, fresh — hours old at most. And over them, lighter, faster, desperate: a single set of prints in soft-soled boots, cutting across the courtyard toward the far tower, where the wall had crumbled enough to climb.

They'd cornered her against the ruin. Of course they had. Ruins were traps for runners; there was nowhere to go but up, and up ran out.

Kestrel found them in the second courtyard, below the broken tower.

Six soldiers in royal blue and steel, snow-dusted, spread in a loose crescent. Crossbows — two of them, leveled at the base of the tower where a figure clung ten feet up the rubble slope, frozen between climbing and jumping. The woman was young, mid-twenties maybe, dark-haired, dressed in riding leathers that had once been fine and were now torn at the shoulder. Even at a distance, even filthy, there was something in how she held herself that didn't match the clothes — chin up, shoulders square, like she was the one doing the judging.

"Hold there," the sergeant was saying, in the bored voice of a man saying it for the tenth time. "Come down and it goes easier."

"It doesn't, though," the woman said. "Does it? You have orders."

The sergeant didn't answer, which was its own answer.

Kestrel counted without meaning to. Six men, two bows, sightlines. The courtyard's north colonnade gave her cover to within thirty feet. There was a wellhead near the center, waist-high, granite — good hard cover if she needed it, and she would. Snow deep enough in the corners to muffle steps.

*Walk away,* said six years of survival, very reasonably. *This is a royal matter. Royal matters eat people like you first, and slowly.*

But the courier had died pressing a book into her hands, and the book had put her name in a place names don't go, and every hour since she'd felt like a piece on a board someone else was moving. Well. If she was going to be moved, she'd at least choose the direction.

Besides — and this was the part she'd examine later, alone, with a drink — the woman had said *please*. Not to her. To the sky, to the soldiers, to nobody. But Kestrel had spent six years listening to people beg in the mountains, and she'd learned the difference between begging for mercy and begging for someone, anyone, to simply *see*. This was the second kind. Kestrel knew the second kind from the inside.

She drew her knife, considered it, and put it away. Knives made noise when they failed. Instead she pulled the coil of waxed cord from her belt — smuggler's cord, thin as sinew, strong as regret — and weighed the iron hook on its end.

The nearest soldier stood at the colonnade's edge, watching the tower, his back to the shadowed arch behind him.

Kestrel had done worse things to better people.

* * *

Afterward, she would remember it in pieces, the way she remembered most violence: not as a story but as a series of sounds.

The soft *whump* of the sap-wrapped pommel against the sentry's skull. The two heartbeats of silence after. Then the shouting, all at once, and the world going fast and bright.

She came off the archway as the first crossbowman turned, and she was inside his reach before he finished turning, driving him back into his partner so both bows tangled uselessly. Someone swung a sword at her; she caught the arm on her forearm guard and felt the bone shiver pleasantly all the way to her shoulder. The wellhead saved her twice — she put it between herself and the second crossbow, and the bolt buried itself in the granite with a sound like a struck bell.

The woman on the tower didn't freeze. Kestrel gave her that much, later, grudgingly. The moment the fight started, the woman came down the rubble in a controlled slide, snatched a dropped sword, and put herself at the sergeant's flank like she'd been drilled in exactly this — which, Kestrel realized, watching her move, she had. Whoever she was, she'd been taught by professionals.

Between them it lasted less than a minute. Soldiers were brave in groups and hesitant alone, and Kestrel fought like the mountains did — sudden, unfair, and finished before you understood what had happened. When the last man broke and ran for the gate, she let him. Running men brought friends, but dead men brought worse, and besides, her arms were already shaking with the cold adrenaline crash.

Silence rushed back in, ringing.

The woman stood three paces away, breathing hard, sword still up, watching Kestrel with eyes that were red-rimmed and ferocious and, underneath everything, terrified.

"You're not one of mine," the woman said. "Who are you?"

"Nobody." Kestrel retrieved her hook, coiled her cord, checked the sentry she'd dropped — alive, breathing, would wake with a headache and a story no one would believe. "And you're welcome."

"I didn't—" The woman stopped. Started again, more carefully, the way people speak when they've spent weeks choosing every word. "Thank you. I think. Are you a bandit?"

"Do I look like I'm collecting tolls?"

"You look like someone who hits people from behind archways."

"That's called surviving." Kestrel jerked her chin at the bodies, the scattered bolts, the whole tableau. "Now. Six royal soldiers hunting one woman through the Ashen Kings, and she fights like a palace guard. So before I decide whether I've just made the worst mistake of my life — and I want you to know the competition is fierce — you're going to tell me who you are."

Something crossed the woman's face — a flinch, quickly mastered, but Kestrel had built a career on catching flinches.

"My name is Sera," the woman said. And then, quieter, with the particular bitterness of someone tearing off a bandage: "Sera Aldane. My brother is the king."

The wind moved through the ruined colonnade, stirring the ash that gave the place its name. Somewhere above them, ice ticked and settled in the broken tower.

Kestrel laughed. It came out shorter and harsher than she intended.

"Of course you are," she said. "Why wouldn't you be."

"You know me?" Sera's sword came up a fraction.

"No. But three days ago a dying man gave me a book that thinks it knows me, and it's been right about everything since, and I am starting to hate the world's sense of humor." She rubbed her scarred left hand absently, the old burn aching in the cold the way it always did. "What are you running from, sister to the king? Because if it's just politics, I'm leaving, and you can keep the sword."

Sera looked at her for a long moment. Weighing her, Kestrel realized — the same way Kestrel had weighed her. Deciding whether the stranger with the knife and the smuggler's cord was safe, or merely useful, or neither.

"The king," Sera said finally, "is not my brother anymore. Whatever wears his crown — whatever gives his orders, signs his decrees, executed my father's council — it is not Aldric. And it has hunters in every pass in these mountains looking for me, because I'm the last person alive who knew him before." Her voice didn't break. It got quieter instead, which was worse. "I'm running because dead martyrs can't help anyone. I need to reach people who might actually be able to do something. And now I'm standing in a ruin with a bandit who talks to books, so my week is going wonderfully."

"I'm not a bandit."

"What are you, then?"

It was a fair question, and Kestrel found she didn't have a clean answer. Guide. Smuggler. Exile. Storm-caller, once, in another country, in another life that had ended the day her storm killed a man she'd never meant to touch. She was none of those things here, and all of them, depending on who was asking and what they planned to do with the information.

"Lost," she said at last. "I'm lost, and I'm going the wrong way on purpose, and I'd like to keep pretending that's a choice."

Sera almost smiled. It was a tired, cracked thing, but it was real. "I know the feeling."

Above them, on the ridge beyond the shattered gatehouse, a horn sounded — one long note, then two short. Answering horns echoed from the valley below, fainter, and then fainter still, until Kestrel couldn't tell how many there were or how far.

Sera went pale. "The patrol. They'll have found the others by now."

"How many is 'the others'?"

"For me? Dozens. For you?" Sera looked at her — really looked, at the cord, the hook, the burn-scarred hand, the way she stood with her weight already shifting toward the mountain's back side. "I don't know. Who are you, that the king's men would want?"

Kestrel stared up at the broken crown of the mountain, at the blackened towers against the white sky, and felt the prophecy book like a weight against her ribs where it rode in her coat. *The hunt begins at the gate of the ashen kings.* It hadn't said whose hunt. It hadn't needed to.

"Nobody important," she said, and discovered she was lying less than she'd hoped. "Can you climb?"

"I grew up in a palace. I can learn."

"Then stay close, don't talk above a whisper, and if I tell you to jump — jump." Kestrel turned toward the scree field on the mountain's blind side, where the tracks of goats and smugglers and no one else wound upward into the gathering dusk. Behind her, Sera fell into step without argument, which Kestrel noted with distant surprise: the king's sister, trusting a stranger with a knife, because the alternative was the horns.

The first snowflakes came down as they climbed, fat and slow, erasing the path behind them.

And in Kestrel's coat, against her heartbeat, the book felt warm.
