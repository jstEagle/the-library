## 7. Smugglers' Parliament

The Hollow Lantern wasn't a tavern so much as a throat of stone that had learned to swallow people.

Kestrel had found it seven years ago, half-dead of cold and carrying a stolen bolt of silk she'd been paid to lose. The entrance was a crack in the mountainside wide enough for a goat and no wider, and the passage beyond bent twice before opening into a cavern the size of a chapel, its ceiling hung with stalactites that wept mineral water into three natural basins. Someone — no one remembered who — had wedged iron brackets into the rock and burned tallow candles in them until the walls went black with soot. Now there were tables made of split planks, benches worn smooth by generations of backsides that all had warrants out on them, and a bar that was just an old ship's door laid across two barrels.

By midnight it was full.

She counted heads from the shadow of the entry bend, where the smoke thinned enough to see. Forty-one. More than she'd dared hope. The Thornfell runners were all there — Dagna Marsh with her cropped grey hair and her ledger-memory of every toll road in the range; the Vetch brothers looking like two crows who'd dressed up as men; Tam Ossler, who was seventeen and already owed blood-debts in three valleys. From the low country: Ferro Brant, who moved grain past checkpoints in coffins; the Widow Sarelle, whose "wool carts" had carried everything but wool; a knot of river smugglers Kestrel didn't know by name, sent by someone upstream who owed someone downstream who owed her.

They'd come because she'd sent word. Not an invitation — invitations could be refused. She'd sent *obligations*, itemized, each one tied to a name and a night. *You, because I carried your brother's fever down the mountain on my own back in Year of the Grey Frost. You, because when the customs men took your cargo I burned their warehouse so you wouldn't hang. You, because you asked me never to mention what happened at Marrow Ford, and I haven't, and I won't.*

It felt like extortion. It was also the only currency she had, and she had spent all of it getting these people into one room.

Sera stood beside her in the dark of the bend, hood back now that they were inside Thornfell's lawless courtesy, where nobody asked names and everybody watched hands. The princess looked at the crowd the way a person looks at deep water.

"These are the people who will get us into a royal palace," Sera said. It wasn't quite a question.

"These are the people who will get us *near* a royal palace," Kestrel said. "Getting in is a different conversation." She pushed off the wall. "Stay behind me. Don't tell anyone who you are unless I say."

"And if someone recognizes me?"

"Then we find out fast who in this room can be bought." She gave Sera a thin smile. "That's what parliaments are for."

* * *

The Hollow Lantern had one rule, older than anyone present and carved into the lintel above the bar in letters that might have been cut with a knife or a claw: **NO CROWNS PAST THIS STONE.**

Kestrel felt it like a hand on her shoulder as she walked under it, and heard Sera's breath catch beside her. Whatever the princess had expected from an outlaw den, it hadn't been scripture against her whole existence.

Dagna Marsh claimed the head table the way she claimed everything, by being there first and daring argument. Kestrel didn't argue. She took the stool across from the old woman and let the room settle around her the way water settles around a dropped stone.

"You look tired, Eriksen," Dagna said.

"I've been walking."

"You've been walking toward the capital with a king's sister at your elbow and a dead courier's book in your pack. Word travels faster than feet in these mountains." Dagna turned her tankard a quarter-turn, a habit Kestrel knew meant calculation. "Half the room thinks you're here to sell them something. The other half thinks you're here to get them killed."

"And what do you think?"

"I think both things are true, and I came anyway, which tells you something about how much I owe you or how old I've gotten." She drained the tankard and set it down with a knock that carried. "Say your piece, girl. The candles are expensive."

So Kestrel stood, and told them.

She kept it lean — the courier dying in the snow with his blood freezing black, the book with tonight circled years before tonight existed, the king wearing gold eyes and issuing winter edicts while his people burned their furniture to stay alive, the archivist's warning that the solstice would finish whatever binding had begun. She did not mention the prophecy calling her the veil-breaker. Some debts you pay in public; some you keep in your pocket where they can't be spent against you.

When she finished, the silence had a texture to it, like frost forming.

Then Ferro Brant laughed. He was a big man gone soft in the middle, with a voice built for auction houses, and he used all of it. "The king's gone mad, the sky's falling, and the mountain mouse wants us to march on the palace. What's next — you'll ask us to pay for the wine?"

"The wine's yours already," Kestrel said. "I paid this morning."

That got the laugh she wanted — small, grudging, breaking the ice so the real talk could come through. Because Ferro wasn't wrong to laugh. That was the trap of the thing. What she was asking sounded insane, and everyone in the room was smart enough to hear it, and brave enough — or desperate enough — to listen anyway.

"The question isn't whether the king's mad," said the Widow Sarelle from near the fire, her voice dry as chaff. "We've all seen the roads. Checkpoints doubled. Every cart weighed, every seal inspected, every driver questioned twice. You don't build that kind of order in two years without something driving it." Her eyes were shrewd and unkind. "The question is why we should care. Kings rot. New kings grow. Smugglers do business under any weather. Why should forty good criminals risk the rope for a throne we've spent our lives robbing?"

"Because in six weeks there won't be anything left to rob." Kestrel let that sit. "Ondrey — Master Ondrey, formerly First Archivist of the realm, and I'll vouch for him if my word counts for anything in this company — has spent thirty years studying what's inside Aldric. It isn't a man anymore. It's a thing that eats obedience. Every decree makes it stronger. Every bowed head feeds it. And when it finishes feeding at midwinter—" she spread her hands— "there's no more smuggling, because there's no more choosing. No more roads to run, because everyone walks in step. You've seen the villages near the capital. You've seen the ones that stopped trading because nobody there wanted anything anymore. That's not poverty. That's harvest."

"Stories," someone said, from the dark near the back. "Archivist stories."

"They are," Kestrel agreed. "And so is everything else worth knowing. The question is whether you trust the storyteller." She looked around the room, letting them look back. "You know what I am. You know I don't sell hope — I've never had any inventory of it. I'm selling a job. Hard, dangerous, well-planned, and paid in the only coin I have: I'm telling you that after midwinter, there is no Thornfell. There's just one long road with the king standing on it, forever. I'd rather die on the short road. Who's with me?"

Nobody spoke. Which was, in its way, better than laughter — laughter would have ended it.

It was Tam Ossler, seventeen and stupid and loyal, who broke first. "I'm in." He said it too loud, the way young men do, and his ears went red when the room turned to look at him. But nobody laughed at him either, because Tam had buried his father last spring and everyone knew it.

Then Dagna Marsh raised her hand, and the room went quiet in earnest, because Dagna's hand going up was like a drawbridge lowering.

"I'm in," she said, "but I'm in for the *why*, not the *what*. A palace job needs three things: a way in, a way out, and a reason for the guards not to be looking. I can maybe give you the third — there's a tithe convoy coming down from the northern holds in ten days, fat and slow and full of bribable drivers. Create noise there, and the garrison looks north." She fixed Kestrel with a stare like an awl. "But the way in. The palace sits on the river side, walls sixty feet, gates that haven't opened for strangers since the coronation. Your archivist says tunnels — fine, every old fortress has tunnels, and every tunnel under that particular fortress was bricked and warded by the founder-king himself, may he rest loud. So. How do we get through ground that doesn't want us?"

Kestrel had known this question was coming since the day she'd read Ondrey's maps. She'd rehearsed eleven answers and hated all of them.

"There's a pass," she said. "Under the Ashen Gate. Old, unmapped, and open — I've seen the mouth of it myself. It comes up inside the palace undercity."

Murmurs. Ferro Brant again, but slower this time, thoughtful: "If it's open and unmapped, why hasn't anyone used it? Nothing under that mountain stays open by accident."

"Because it's warded," Kestrel said. "Old wards. Founder's work. They don't stop bodies — they stop *power*. Anything that carries the old strength gets turned around in the dark until it wanders out or starves. Ordinary folk could walk it blindfolded." She paused, and felt the truth arrive ahead of her words like weather. "Which is the problem. The route only shows itself to someone who can feel the wards coming. Someone who knows what power tastes like from the inside."

The silence changed quality. It got heavier.

"No," said Dagna quietly. Not a refusal. A reading.

"You're asking," said the Widow Sarelle, "for a guide who can sense wards. And you're asking it in a room full of people who know exactly one kind of person who can."

Forty faces turned to her. Kestrel stood in the heat of it and did not flinch, though every scar she owned seemed to wake up at once.

* * *

They argued for two hours.

Not about the plan — the plan held. They argued about her.

It came out in pieces, the way these things always did, each speaker laying their stone on the pile. Ferro Brant: "Storm-callers are worth more delivered than employed. The border lords on the east side pay a hundred crowns gold for one, alive. You want to travel with that kind of price tag?" The Widow Sarelle: "I lost a cousin to the Purging of the Valleys. Whole village drowned when the weather-witch there lost control of her calling. You bring that kind of power near my people, you'd best be sure of her, Eriksen, and I'm not sure even she's sure of herself." A river man Kestrel didn't know: "Why her? If she's what she says, why hasn't she done this alone?"

To that last one, Sera stepped forward before Kestrel could answer, and Kestrel saw the moment the princess decided to spend her own secret like a knife.

"Because I've seen what wears the king," Sera said, and pulled back her hood.

The room went very still. Outlaws didn't gasp — gasping was for people with safe lives — but she felt the recognition move through them like current through wire. Half of them had seen her face on proclamations. The other half had helped burn those proclamations.

"She's telling the truth," Sera said into the quiet. "I'm Sera Aldric, and my brother is dying inside something older than this kingdom, and this woman—" she put her hand on Kestrel's shoulder, and Kestrel let her— "is the only person who's ever agreed to help without asking what she'd get. I've offered her titles. She refused. I've offered her pardon. She said she didn't need one. So ask yourselves what kind of fool refuses a crown's gratitude, and then ask whether that fool is likely to betray you for a hundred gold."

"It's not the gold I worry over," Dagna said, and her old voice was gentle in a way that frightened Kestrel more than anger would have. "Girl. We all know why you came to Thornfell. We've never asked, because that's the law of the stone — no crowns, no questions. But if you call a storm in that pass, every tracker from your homeland will smell it inside a day. They'll come over the border hunting you, and they won't care whose blood they spill crossing mine. You're not risking your neck. You're risking ours."

"I know."

"Do you? Say it plain, then. Say what you are."

Kestrel looked at the fire. At the soot-black rafters. At forty faces that had fed her, hidden her, bled beside her, and never once asked the question that had ended her life in the green country. Seven years of not being asked. It was the richest thing she owned, richer than the favors, richer than the network, and she was about to spend it.

"I'm a storm-caller," she said. "Born to it, taught to it, bound to it. Three years ago I called lightning into a courtyard and it killed a man I did not mean to kill, and my homeland named me kinslayer and put my face on every gate from the coast to the passes. Everything you've heard is true. I can't unsing it. I can't promise the power will always land where I aim. All I can promise is that I'd rather walk into that pass and take my chances than sit in the warm while the world freezes around the people who took me in when I had nothing but a stolen bolt of silk and a bad attitude."

Silence.

Then Tam Ossler started clapping, because he was seventeen, and Ferro Brant cuffed him, and then — slowly, like ice going out of a river — Ferro started clapping too, and then it wasn't clapping anymore, it was the sound of forty outlaws banging tankards on planks, which in the Hollow Lantern was a standing ovation and possibly had been since before the kingdom existed.

Dagna didn't clap. She leaned across the table and gripped Kestrel's wrist, hard, with fingers like rootwood.

"Fool girl," she said, almost fondly. "You know what you've just done."

"Bought the pass."

"You've bought the war." But her eyes were bright, and she poured Kestrel a drink from her own bottle, which among these people was a sacrament. "Ten days. Tithe convoy for the distraction. We move the night after."

* * *

Later, when the parliament had broken into knots of planners and drinkers and the candle-smoke hung thick as wool, Sera found Kestrel at the mouth of the exit passage, where the cold air came down the stone throat like a reminder.

"They followed you," Sera said. "All of them. After everything you told them. After learning what you are."

"They didn't follow me. They followed the debt." Kestrel rubbed her wrist where Dagna had gripped it. "Trust isn't a gift in Thornfell. It's a ledger. And tonight I emptied it."

"Was it worth it?"

Kestrel thought about the sound of forty tankards hammering wood. About Tam's red ears. About Dagna's bottle, shared like bread.

"Ask me in ten days," she said. "After the pass. After the trackers come."

"Trackers?" Sera went rigid. "What trackers—"

Kestrel nodded toward the entrance crack, where the wind had shifted, bringing with it the faintest thread of scent that did not belong in a mountain tavern: woodsmoke and horse and oiled leather. And beneath it, so faint she might have imagined it, the prickling pressure she'd hoped never to feel again — the static hum of another caller's ward-sense sweeping the slopes outside, patient as a snare, methodical as winter.

"They're already here," Kestrel said.
