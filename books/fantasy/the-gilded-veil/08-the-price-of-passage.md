## 8. The Price of Passage

The cavern tavern called the Hollow Crown smelled of tallow smoke, wet stone, and forty years of spilled ale that no one had ever bothered to scrub from the floor. Kestrel stood at its center with her arms crossed and her patience thinning, watching the smugglers of Thornfell argue about her like she was a shipment of contraband brandy.

"Three weeks," she said again, louder this time. "The solstice is in three weeks. We don't have time for a parliament."

"We have time for what we have time for." Maren Ostvik lifted her boots off the table and set them down with deliberate slowness, the way she did when she wanted everyone to know she was about to say something expensive. She was sixty if she was a day, gray-braided, built like a doorframe, and she had run the Thornfell roads since before Kestrel had been old enough to steal bread. "You're asking us to walk into the capital of a kingdom gone mad, past wards that cook flesh, into a palace full of soldiers who answer to something that isn't human. That's not a job, girl. That's a funeral with extra steps."

"It's payment," Kestrel said. "Triple rates. Every debt I'm owed gets called in, every favor—"

"I know what you're offering." Maren's eyes were pale as river ice. "I'm telling you what it costs."

Around the long table, the faces turned toward her: Doss the ferryman, who'd once carried her across the Sorrowmere with arrows in his back; the twins Ferro and Liss, who spoke so rarely that people forgot which one did the talking; young Tam with his burned hand and his hero worship he thought nobody noticed; old Wren, who had buried two husbands and one king and trusted none of them. Forty souls, give or take, the whole loose and quarrelsome family of the Thornfell roads.

Kestrel had spent seven years earning them. Seven years of guiding loads through passes that killed hired guides, of splitting fees down to the copper, of carrying wounded men on her own back when the weather turned. Trust, Ondrey had told her in his archive beneath the capital, was the only currency the Veil couldn't counterfeit. It fed on obedience. It had never learned to buy love.

She was counting on that now.

"The pass is warded," said Ferro — or possibly Liss; the voice came from somewhere between them. "The King's Road gates. Since midwinter last, they've hung cold-iron bells on every arch, and there are priests of the Order of Stillness walking the walls. You know what their bells do."

"They still the air," Kestrel said. "Kill wind. Kill breath, if you stand too close too long."

"And you're proposing to go *over* the Serpent's Tooth instead. In winter. Through the high pass that hasn't been crossed since—" Liss paused, and both twins looked at her with identical expressions of terrible patience.

"Since the year the storms ate the rope bridges," Ferro finished. "Which was the year you left home, wasn't it, Kestrel? The year of your storm."

The tavern went quiet the way a forest goes quiet when something large moves through it.

Kestrel kept her face still. She'd had seven years of practice keeping her face still. "The Tooth can be crossed. I've scouted it twice this season."

"In summer?"

"In autumn."

"With thirty people and enough powder and steel to arm a rebellion?" Maren shook her head slowly. "No. No, we don't cross the Tooth by skill or by luck. There's one way to move a company that size over that pass inside three weeks, and you know it, and you've known it since you walked in here, and you've been waiting for one of us to say it out loud because you don't have the stomach to say it yourself."

Kestrel's jaw tightened. "Say what, then."

"You'd call a storm."

There it was. Four words, and the whole room flinched from them like from an open flame.

* * *

She made herself look around the table while the silence stretched. She owed them that much — to be seen taking the blow.

They all knew. That was the thing she'd never said aloud and never needed to: they knew what she was. You couldn't guide caravans through Thornfell for seven years without the weather noticing you, without clear skies opening along your route like a path through a crowd, without the occasional thunderhead that formed precisely where you needed shade and dissipated precisely when you needed stars. The smugglers had never asked. Asking would have made it real, and real things could be sold, and the code of the roads was that nothing got sold.

But knowing and saying were different animals, and Ferro-and-Liss had just dragged the second animal into the light.

"A storm-caller," said Wren, into the quiet. Her voice was dry as old paper. "In the flesh. Sitting at our table drinking our beer. All these years."

"Not calling anything," Kestrel said. "Not for seven years."

"Why not?" Tam asked. He was twenty-two and hadn't yet learned that some questions stay folded. "If you can do that, why—"

"Because the last time I called one, it killed a man." The words came out flat and final, a door closing. "A man I didn't mean to kill. My homeland doesn't exile storm-callers, Tam. They execute them. I got exile because my grandmother bought the magistrate, and I got exile because the man I killed was the wrong man — a customs officer instead of the magistrate who'd taken his bribe. Somewhere north of here there are families with my name written on a bounty notice, and hunters who collect on those notices, and they have long memories and longer knives. If I call a storm — if I *show* what I am, out loud, under open sky where witnesses can see —"

"Bounty hunters," Maren said quietly. "From Vharsk. Coming into Thornfell after you."

"After me. And anyone standing near me has a bad day in their future."

"So that's the price." Wren leaned back, arms folding across her chest like a drawbridge rising. "You want us to march you over the Serpent's Tooth, and to do it fast enough to matter, you have to unchain the thing you've been hiding for seven years. And the moment you do, every hunter from the northern kingdoms starts sharpening knives, and every one of us becomes collateral."

"That's the price," Kestrel said.

"And you're asking us to pay it with you."

"No." She heard her own voice harden, and let it. "I'm telling you what it costs, and then I'm going to pay it whether you come or not. I'll cross the Tooth alone if I have to. But alone, I arrive too late, and Aldric dies, and whatever wears him finishes its binding, and the long winter comes down on *all* of us — Thornfell included. The Veil doesn't stop at borders, Maren. It stops at nothing. Obedience is its food, and it means to eat the world."

Maren studied her for a long moment. Then the old woman laughed — short, harsh, without much humor in it. "Look at her. Standing there talking about eating the world like she's reading a supply manifest." She pushed back her chair and stood, and the room stood with her, because forty years of roads buys that kind of obedience, the good kind, the kind that walks behind you into fire. "All right. Here's how it is, storm-caller. We'll take the job. Triple rates, debts called, the works. But we set our own condition, and it isn't negotiable."

Kestrel braced. "Name it."

"You lead from the front. Not from some ridge with a spyglass — the front. When you call your storm, you call it *with us*, over *us*, and you hold it steady over *us* the whole way across. You've spent seven years letting us believe you were just the best guide on the mountain. Fine. Now you show us what else you are, up close, where we can judge for ourselves whether we're following a woman or a weapon." Maren's ice-pale eyes didn't blink. "And when the hunters come — and they'll come, girl, you know they'll come — you don't slip away in the night to spare us. You stand. Because if you run, everything you've built here dies anyway, and we'll all know it was never real."

The silence rang.

Kestrel found that her hands had gone cold. Not fear, exactly. Something older and stranger — the feeling of a door she'd nailed shut seven years ago beginning, splinter by splinter, to give way.

"You understand what you're asking," she said. "Once I call, I can't uncalled it. Every road I've ever walked gets a new shadow on it. Every caravan I ever guided becomes evidence. You'd be tying yourselves to a fugitive the northern kingdoms want dead."

"We've been tied to you for seven years," said Doss the ferryman, speaking for the first time all night, his voice like gravel sliding. "We just didn't have the paperwork."

Laughter moved through the room — nervous, but real, and it broke something open. Kestrel felt the corner of her own mouth twitch despite everything.

"Maren's condition stands," Wren said. "Front of the line. And one more thing, from an old woman who's watched you a long time." She fixed Kestrel with eyes gone milky at the edges but sharp at the center. "When you call it — the first time, up on that mountain — don't fight it. Whatever it feels like. Don't fight it. I buried a hedge-witch once who spent her whole life strangling her gift half to death, and when she finally needed it, it came out crooked and took her with it. Power you starve turns on you. Feed yours, and maybe it'll remember whose hands it lives in."

Kestrel looked down at her left hand — the scarred one, the one the prophecy book had named, the one with the burn from the lightning that had jumped sideways on a day of rain and rage and a dead man in the mud. The scar ran from wrist to knuckle like a river on a map, and maps were exactly what she'd made of her life since: routes around the truth, detours around herself.

Seven years of survival. Ondrey had said it once, gently, in the dark of his archive: *Survival is not the same as living, child.* She'd thrown a book at him for it. She'd been wanting to throw something ever since, mostly because he was right.

"All right," she said.

Every head turned.

"All right," she said again, louder, and heard her voice carry into the stone vaults of the Hollow Crown and come back changed. "Front of the line. Storm held over the company, the whole crossing. No slipping away, no running, no shadows. You get all of it — the calling, the consequences, the hunters, everything. That's my price paid alongside yours." She pulled the prophecy book from her coat and set it on the table, and the candlelight seemed to bend away from its cover, the way candlelight does around things that are very old or very cold. "Because this says I'm the veil-breaker, and I am done pretending I get a vote."

Maren looked at the book, then at her, and something in the old woman's granite face shifted — softened, barely, the way permafrost softens in the first hour of thaw.

"Then we ride at first light," Maren said. "Company muster at the Grey Stair, dawn bell. Thirty picked, the best climbers, the coldest nerves." She stuck out her hand, gnarled as a root. "Triple rates. Debts called. And Kestrel Eriksen — may whatever gods listen keep the sky listening too."

Kestrel took the hand. It was warm, and rough, and real, and holding it felt like signing something.

* * *

She didn't sleep.

The waystation above the Grey Stair had one window, and through it the Serpent's Tooth showed itself against the stars — a black sawblade of rock and ice, nine thousand feet of the worst ground in Thornfell, wearing its winter clouds like a hood. Tomorrow, or the day after, she would stand on its spine and open her throat to the sky for the first time in seven years, and everything she had buried would climb out of the grave with the thunder.

Sera found her there near midnight. The king's sister moved quietly for someone raised in a palace; she'd learned fast on the road. She set two cups of something hot on the sill and sat without asking, which Kestrel was beginning to understand was simply how Sera did things.

"My brother used to stand at windows like this," Sera said, after a while. "Before. Watching the city. He'd say a king should know what the weather is doing to his people." She wrapped her hands around her cup. "Now the weather *is* him. Isn't that a foul joke."

"Sera."

"You're afraid." It wasn't a question. "Tomorrow. Or the day after. You're afraid of the storm."

Kestrel considered lying, decided she was too tired, and drank her cup instead. "I'm afraid it'll feel good."

Sera blinked. "Good?"

"When I called, the first time — the time that ruined me — everyone talks about the destruction. The man in the mud. The village lights going out. Nobody asks what it felt like *before* that part." Kestrel stared at the black teeth of the mountain. "It felt like being handed back to myself. Like every wall I'd ever built fell down at once and there was all this — space. Sky-space. Room. Seven years I've told myself I exiled myself to survive. Some nights I think I exiled myself because surviving was easier than wanting that feeling again and not trusting myself to have it."

Sera was quiet for a moment. Then: "My father told me once that the difference between a coward and a brave person is about four seconds. The coward feels the fear and turns. The brave one feels the same fear and counts to four and walks anyway." A ghost of a smile. "He was drunk at the time and quoting somebody smarter, but it's held up."

"Your father sounds like he deserved better than what he got."

"He deserved better than both his children got." Sera's voice went tight, and Kestrel saw her swallow it, grief swallowed so often it must have worn a groove. "Kestrel. Whatever happens up there — whatever the storm costs you — I need you to know that the thing sitting on my brother's throne counted on everyone being afraid. That's the whole engine of it. Fear of the winter, fear of the decrees, fear of the gold eyes. Every person who keeps their head down feeds it. So tomorrow, when you climb that mountain and do the terrifying thing—" she turned, and in the starlight her eyes were fierce and wet at once, "—you won't just be carrying us over a pass. You'll be doing the one thing it cannot digest. Someone refusing to be afraid."

Kestrel looked at her — this woman who had fled her own family to save her own soul and hated herself for the fleeing, this mirror with different scars — and felt the strange, frightening warmth that she was learning to recognize as belonging.

"Go to sleep, Sera."

"You first."

"I will." It was even mostly true. "Dawn bell."

Sera squeezed her shoulder once, hard, and left. Kestrel stayed at the window until the stars wheeled and the east went the color of a healing bruise, and below her, torches began to gather at the foot of the Grey Stair — thirty points of light climbing through the dark, coming to follow her into the sky.

She flexed her scarred hand. Somewhere far north, she knew, men were already sharpening knives with her name beside them. Somewhere south, a gilded thing on a stolen throne was weaving its long winter tighter. And here, between them, a storm-caller who had spent seven years being weather's widow picked up her pack and started down toward the torchlight.

The mountain waited. The sky waited. For the first time since the mud and the lightning and the dead man, so did she.
