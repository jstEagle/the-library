## 9. Storm Over Thornfell

The pass had a name on no map: the Hollow Throat. The smugglers called it that because the mountain swallowed sound — footsteps, voices, even the ring of a dropped blade all vanished a few paces from your ears, as though the stone drank noise the way the desert drank rain. Kestrel had led forty runs through it and never once heard her own echo.

Tonight, for the first time, she was going to give it something to swallow.

She stood at the mouth of the pass with the wind worrying at her collar and counted what she had left of herself. Two days since the smugglers' parliament in the Cavernlight. One night since she'd stood before Bramm Halloway and his crew and told them the truth she'd buried for six years: *I call storms.* The word had come out of her like a tooth pulled, leaving a hollow spot behind. They hadn't flinched, which somehow made it worse. Bramm had just nodded and said, "Figured you were more than a pretty voice on the ridge lines," and that was that. Her secret was currency now, spent, and there was no taking it back.

Behind her, thirty-one people huddled against the rock face — smugglers, Sera in borrowed leathers with her hair bound back like any other courier, old Ondrey wheezing under a pack twice his size because he refused to let anyone else carry his books. Somewhere behind *them*, three hours back down the valley, bounty hunters from Velmark moved through the dark with her name on their warrants and silver in their purses. She could almost feel them the way you felt a storm building past the horizon: not seen, but *known*.

"You don't have to do this," Sera said quietly, coming up beside her. "We can go around. Add four days."

"Four days is midwinter's throat," Kestrel said. "Ondrey says the binding completes at solstice. We have weeks, not seasons. We go through."

"And the storm?"

Kestrel looked up. Above the pass, between two black teeth of granite, the sky was clear — cruelly, perfectly clear, the kind of cold night where every star burned like a nailhead. Perfect weather. The worst possible weather for what she was about to do, because it meant she would have to drag every drop of it out of the air herself, by force, by will, by whatever was still coiled in her blood after six years of strangling it.

"The storm," she said, "is me."

* * *

They gathered close to hear the plan, which was short, because plans this desperate didn't deserve long speeches.

"I'll raise wind and snow off the western peaks," Kestrel said. "Not much. Enough to blind anyone watching the pass from above and cover our tracks behind us. You move in single file, follow the cairns, touch nothing metal to the walls." She looked at Bramm. "You know the drill. I can't hold it and count heads at once."

"How long can you hold it?" Bramm asked. He asked it plainly, the way he'd ask how long a fuse was.

"Honest answer? I haven't done this since—" Her jaw tightened. "Six years. Small storms, once or twice, when the alternative was dying. Never anything held. Never anything *shaped*." She saw the doubt move through their faces like weather of its own and hated that she'd put it there. "It'll be enough."

"It doesn't have to be enough," Sera said softly. "It just has to be true."

Kestrel almost laughed. Almost said something cutting, because kindness in the face of terror made her itch. Instead she turned to face the pass, and the joke died somewhere in her chest, and what was left was only the cold and the waiting dark and the thing inside her that had been waiting longer than either.

"Bramm," she said. "If I lose it — if it goes big — get them into the deep cut at the third cairn. Stone over a hundred feet thick. It'll hold."

"And you?"

She didn't answer that. She stepped forward instead, alone, to the flat shelf of rock where the pass narrowed to its throat, and she did the thing she had sworn never to do again.

She reached for the sky.

* * *

Calling a storm was not like people imagined. There was no shouting at clouds, no dramatic gesture. It was closer to opening a door you'd nailed shut from the inside — feeling along the frame in the dark, finding the first loose nail, pulling.

The first nail hurt.

Power came into her like cold water into a drowned lung — not filling but *flooding*, an ache that started under her sternum and spread outward until her fingertips buzzed and her teeth hummed. Six years of holding the door shut, and now it was open, and everything on the other side wanted in at once. She tasted copper. She tasted lightning that hadn't been born yet.

Above the western peaks, the stars began to go out.

Not clouds rolling in — clouds would have taken minutes. This was faster, uglier: pressure gathering, air stacking itself into towers of gray-black, the temperature dropping so sharply that frost bloomed across the rock around her boots in feathered white lace. The Hollow Throat, which drank all sound, suddenly had something to drink. Wind moaned down from the heights like something waking.

*Kestrel.*

She knew the voice wasn't real. It was memory wearing memory's voice — her grandmother, standing on the sea cliffs of Velmark, salt wind whipping her gray braid: *The storm isn't yours, child. You're just the door it chooses. Your job is to decide what comes through.*

Her grandmother had decided wrong, in the end. Or Kestrel had. The histories blurred; the dead man's name didn't. Lord Aldous Vane, struck down on the parade ground by a bolt that had been aimed — she'd sworn it was aimed — at the slaver captain beside him. A storm-caller who killed the wrong man. Exiled before the body cooled.

*Just the door,* the memory whispered. *Decide what comes through.*

"Come through, then," Kestrel breathed, and pulled.

The sky tore.

* * *

Snow came sideways, a white wall roaring down the pass, thick enough that the stars vanished entirely and the world shrank to the circle of her own straining breath. She held it shaped — that was the agony of it, the part no one understood. Raising a storm was easy, like knocking over a full cup. *Holding* one was balancing the cup on a knife's edge while walking a rope over a gorge. Every second she kept the blizzard wrapped tight around the pass and away from her own people was a second of pure, grinding concentration, sweat freezing on her spine while her blood burned.

"Move!" Bramm's voice, muffled to nothing by the wind, carried anyway because he was screaming it. Figures lurched past her position in single file — she saw them as flickers, shapes, the pale oval of Ondrey's terrified face, the set shoulders of Sera bringing up the rear with a crossbow cradled like a child. Thirty-one souls walking through her storm.

And it felt —

Gods, it felt —

Like coming home.

That was the horror she'd never told anyone. Not that the power hurt, though it did; not that it exhausted, though it drained her to the dregs. But that it felt *right*. Like a key turning in a lock she'd forgotten existed. The wind answered her the way a well-trained horse answers its rider, leaning into her intentions, and beneath the strain was a joy so fierce it scared her worse than the bounty hunters did. Because the last time she had felt this alive, she had killed a man. Because the exile hadn't been punishment enough if exile meant giving up the only thing that had ever made her feel whole.

The thunder rolled down the pass, deep and vast, and the mountain swallowed it, and Kestrel stood in the eye of her own making with tears freezing on her cheeks, laughing or sobbing, she genuinely couldn't tell which.

Then the wind brought her a scent that wasn't snow.

Smoke. Torch smoke, on the eastern ridge.

* * *

"They're on the high trail!" Dara's voice, one of Bramm's scouts, materializing out of the whiteout at Kestrel's elbow, hair plastered with ice. "Eight, maybe ten, with dogs. They've got crossbows and they're moving fast — the storm's slowing them but it's slowing us too—"

"How far from the rearguard?"

"Two hundred yards. Maybe less."

Two hundred yards. In this visibility, a crossbow's effective range was forty. But the hunters had dogs, and dogs hunted by nose, and the storm couldn't erase scent.

Kestrel's grip on the blizzard wavered — one heartbeat of slack, the wind stuttering, the wall of snow thinning — and she slammed it back with a grunt that bent her double. *Hold. Hold.* But now she was doing two things at once, and she could feel the cost arriving like a bill collector: her vision sparking at the edges, her knees going soft, some deep reservoir in her draining faster than it should.

"Get to the third cairn," she gasped. "Deep cut. Everyone in. Now."

Dara ran. Kestrel turned east, toward the torch-smell, and did the thing she'd promised herself she wouldn't do.

She reached further.

* * *

There was lightning in her storm — of course there was; you couldn't pull this much charge through cold air without it — but she'd kept it leashed, kept it high, kept it as distant grumble and internal flash. Now she went looking for it deliberately, the way you'd go looking for a knife you'd hidden from yourself.

She found it immediately. It had been waiting. It had *always* been waiting.

*Decide what comes through,* her grandmother said, and this time the memory's voice sounded less like wisdom and more like a dare.

"No killing," Kestrel snarled at the sky, at herself, at six years of guilt. "You hear me? No killing. Scare them. Blind them. Break nothing you can't mend."

The storm, being hers, obeyed her exactly as well as she obeyed herself.

She threw the bolt wide.

It hit the ridge crest fifty yards left of the hunters — she felt it strike through the storm-sense, a bloom of heat and shattered pine — and the thunderclap that followed rolled down both slopes like the mountain clearing its throat. Through the whirling snow she heard dogs shriek and men shout, heard something heavy tumble, heard the unmistakable panic of people realizing the weather had *aimed at them*.

One bolt. Wide. No killing.

But the storm had tasted release, and it wanted more, and for one vertiginous moment Kestrel felt the difference between guiding the power and riding it grow dangerously thin. The next bolt built itself without her permission — charge gathering in the clouds above the pass, hungry, eager, *her* hunger wearing *her* shape — and she wrestled it down with a cry that ripped her throat raw, forcing it apart, bleeding it off into harmless sheet-lightning that strobed the whiteout like a dying lantern.

*This,* she thought, gasping, bent nearly double, *this is why they exile us. Not for what we do. For what we almost do.*

The last of the company stumbled past her into the deep cut. She counted shapes — twenty-eight, twenty-nine, thirty — and Sera was last, dragging Ondrey by the arm, both of them iced gray, and then Sera stopped. Turned back. Stood in the screaming snow looking straight at where Kestrel stood, though no one could possibly have seen her.

"Kestrel!" Sera screamed into the wind. "*Come on!*"

One moment more. She held the storm one moment more, sweeping it back up the pass to bury the trail and the torches and the dog-scent under six feet of fresh drift, and then — hands shaking, vision tunneling, the door in her chest groaning on its hinges — she let it go.

The silence of the Hollow Throat closed over her like water.

She got three steps toward the cut before her legs quit, and then the snow was against her cheek and someone's arms were under hers, and the last thing she registered was Sera's voice saying her name again, softer now, and the strange, shameful warmth in her chest that had nothing to do with fire and everything to do with the fact that she had called a storm and it had *come home to her* — and that somewhere in the white dark behind them, the bounty hunters' torches had gone out.

* * *

She woke to the smell of stew and the taste of iron.

The deep cut was a slot canyon the smugglers had roofed with hide and heated with a single banked fire, and by its light she saw Bramm sitting across from her, sharpening a knife that didn't need it, which was how Bramm thought. Sera sat closer, awake, watchful, a blanket around her shoulders and another one draped over Kestrel.

"You slept nine hours," Sera said. "Ondrey says that's normal. He also says, quote, 'the fact that she woke up at all is frankly encouraging,' which I've decided not to take personally on your behalf."

"Did we lose anyone?"

"Not one." Sera's mouth curved. "Thirty-one in, thirty-one out. And eight Velmark hunters digging their dogs out of a snowdrift somewhere on the high trail." She hesitated, then: "I saw you. At the end. Holding it back from them when you could have—" She stopped. "Wide. You threw it wide."

"They were just men doing a job."

"So was the executioner who took our father's councilors' heads." Sera's voice didn't harden; it went quieter, which was worse. "Mercy is a choice, Kestrel. I watched my brother stop making it. Don't ever apologize to me for making it."

Kestrel looked away, into the fire, and found she had no answer — only the fading echo of that terrible, joyful rightness in her blood, already whispering that she'd need it again. Soon. That the palace ahead of them had wards older than the kingdom and guards who didn't sleep, and that the woman who'd sworn never to call a storm had spent her last coin of denial in the Hollow Throat.

Bramm cleared his throat. "There's a problem," he said, in the tone he used for problems, which was the same tone he used for weather. "Scout came in an hour ago. Down-valley traffic — royal soldiers, lots of them, moving toward the capital with wagons. Heavy wagons. And they're not resupplying."

Kestrel sat up slowly. "What's in the wagons?"

"That's the thing," Bramm said. "Whatever it is, they're carrying it under guard and keeping it *cold*. Ice in midsummer wagons, packed around crates the size of coffins." He met her eyes. "Heading for the palace."

From her nest of blankets, Ondrey spoke without lifting his head from his book, his voice thin with a dread he was clearly trying to keep academic. "Frost-iron," he murmured. "Or something very like it. The Veil is *building* something, children. Something it means to finish by solstice."

Kestrel stared into the fire, and in its depths, for half a heartbeat, she saw gold.

"We'd better move," she said.
