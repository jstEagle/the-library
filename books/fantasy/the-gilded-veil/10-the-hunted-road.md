## 10. The Hunted Road

The ambush came at the hour when even the mountains seem to sleep.

Sera had learned to read the smugglers' silences in the week since they'd left Ondrey's archive behind — how Vess's whistle changed pitch when she was tired, how Dorn went quiet not with fear but with hunger, how Kestrel stopped talking entirely when the land began to worry her. It was that silence Sera noticed now, falling over the column like a dropped cloth. Nine people picking their way along a ledge above a frozen river, and suddenly no one was breathing loudly enough.

Kestrel raised one scarred hand. The whole line stopped.

*There,* Sera thought, and hated herself for thinking it like a soldier. Two years of running had taught her to think in ambushes. Her father would have wept to hear it; her father, who had taught her falconry and statecraft and never once how to survive the kingdom he'd handed to her brother.

"What is it?" she whispered.

Kestrel's answer was a tilt of her chin toward the pines across the river. At first Sera saw nothing — snow, shadow, the blue-black density of Thornfell forest at dusk. Then the nothing moved. A shape that had been a fallen log became a crouched man. A knot of shadow resolved into a drawn bow.

"Homeland trackers," Kestrel breathed. "Three on the far bank, at least. There'll be more above us." Her voice did something Sera hadn't heard before. It went flat. Not calm — *emptied*, the way a room looks after the furniture's been carried out. "They've been on us since the pass."

"You knew?"

"I hoped I was wrong." Kestrel's eyes stayed on the treeline. "I called weather in that pass, girl. Storms carry. Word travels faster than weather. Someone sold my name, or someone recognized the work." A muscle moved in her jaw. "The bounty on me came home to roost."

"How much?"

"Enough," Kestrel said, "that your brother's soldiers won't be the only ones hunting us now."

The first arrow took Dorn through the shoulder.

It happened between one heartbeat and the next — the bowstring's snap from across the river, the wet punch of impact, Dorn spinning against the rockface with a sound Sera would hear in her sleep for years afterward. Then the mountain came alive. Arrows from above, three and four at once, hissing down through the pines. Shouts on the far bank in a border accent Sera knew from her father's war councils. Vess screaming Dorn's name and dragging him behind a boulder while bolts sprouted in the snow around them like iron flowers.

"Down! *Down!*" Kestrel had Sera by the collar, hauling her off the ledge path into the shallow hollow beneath an overhang. Stone closed overhead like a shell. Through the gap, Sera watched the battle break open around them — smugglers who had seemed like vagabonds an hour ago moving with terrible economy, returning fire, cutting switchbacks, one of them already scaling the slope toward the high shooters with two knives and no hesitation at all.

"They're good," Sera said stupidly.

"They're alive because they're good." Kestrel was watching the far bank, counting. Sera could see her counting — lips moving without sound. "Six visible. Probably eight. Border trackers work in eights." She pulled a knife, then put it back, which made no sense until Sera understood: Kestrel's hands were trembling. Not with fear.

With want.

"You're thinking about the storm," Sera said.

"I'm always thinking about the storm." Kestrel's laugh had no humor in it anywhere. "That's the disease of it. There's a sky up there full of everything I need, and every time I reach for it, someone dies who didn't have to. Last time it was a man named Halloran. Before him—" She stopped. Pressed her palm flat against the stone as if checking whether the mountain itself might object. "Before him there were others. You don't get a count that stops."

"Then don't call it," Sera said. "We can fight."

"We can fight eight trackers with bows on ground they chose." Kestrel looked at her then, and Sera saw the arithmetic happening behind her eyes and hated being a number in it. "Some of us will die either way. The storm just decides *which* some."

Above them, Vess screamed again — not Dorn's name this time but *Bren's*, and Sera's stomach dropped through the mountain.

* * *

They lost Bren on the switchbacks and Dorn an hour later, in a freezing waystation that smelled of pine pitch and old smoke, with his hand gripped in Vess's and his eyes fixed on the ceiling as if reading something written there.

He was a quiet man. That was what Sera kept circling back to, sitting against the wall with blood drying under her fingernails from where she'd held pressure she couldn't hold long enough. In nine days of travel he had spoken perhaps forty words to her, most of them directions. He had once silently corrected her grip on a rope without commentary, without judgment, the way you'd fix a child's coat collar. He had a wife in Lowmark, Vess said, and a daughter who thought he drove ore carts. He had died asking Vess to tell them exactly that — that the carts were going well.

"He shouldn't have been here," Vess said afterward, in the doorway, in a voice scraped down to the grain. "Dorn. He swore off runs after the Marrow job. Three years clean. I asked him myself. I *begged* him, because I trust him more than anyone breathing, and he came because I asked." She stared out at the dark. "You understand what I'm telling you, my lady? He's dead because I'm persuasive."

"Don't," Sera started.

"Don't call you 'my lady'?" Vess turned, and her face was wrecked and furious and grieving all at once, and Sera realized with a jolt that the anger wasn't for her. Or wasn't only for her. "No. I'll call you what I like. But you'll stand there and listen, because you wanted to come north, and Kestrel wanted to keep you breathing, and Dorn wanted to keep *Kestrel* breathing, and that's the whole chain that put a bolt through him. Nobody in it is innocent. That's what I'm telling you. Nobody. Including me. Especially me." She walked out into the snow, and no one followed, because everyone in the waystation understood that grief needs a corridor and you clear it when asked.

Sera stood there a long time, listening to the wind worry the eaves, understanding that she had just been given something rare. Not comfort. *Truth*. Her family had spent her whole childhood upholstering every hard edge with silk. The court had told her father what he wished to hear until the day the Veil wore his son's crown and ordered the council's heads off, and by then there was no one left in the palace capable of saying an honest sentence. And here was a smuggler with blood on her sleeves handing Sera the plainest sentence she'd heard in two years: *nobody in it is innocent.*

She found herself absurdly grateful. She found herself wanting to sit with that sentence like it was a coal in winter.

* * *

Kestrel was outside, at the tree line, doing something strange.

Sera watched from the doorway before approaching. The storm-caller had dug — with her hands, bare despite the cold — a shallow grave in the frozen ground beside the ones the others had managed for Bren. She was working at the earth with the patience of someone punishing herself, breaking the frost crust inch by inch, and when the others had finished and gone inside, Kestrel had kept digging. Now she laid Dorn's cloak-wrapped body into the ground herself, alone, and knelt there with her head bowed, and her lips moved.

Prayer, Sera thought. Then, closer, she caught the words and understood it was no prayer any god would recognize.

"Halloran. Marta. The twins from Skarrow — I never learned which twin. Old Cobb. The ferryman whose name I still don't know. Bren." Each name set down like a stone on a cairn. "Dorn. Who came back for me." A pause so long Sera thought she'd finished. Then, quieter: "And whoever comes next, because there's always a next, and I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm—"

"Dorn's wife thinks he drives ore carts."

Kestrel flinched as if struck. She didn't turn around. "You heard that."

"Vess told me. She's telling the others." Sera came closer, boots crunching, making no effort to be silent — stealth felt obscene out here, beside the graves. "Vess says you carry them. Every one. Like stones."

"Vess talks too much."

"Vess loves you too much to lie to you, and you know the difference." Sera sat down in the snow beside her, uninvited, because she had learned this week that Kestrel respected trespass more than courtesy. Courtesy, to Kestrel, was a wall. Trespass was a door. "How many names are in that list? Truly."

"I don't finish the list anymore. It got too long to finish."

"And you say each one anyway. Out loud. Alone. In the dark." Sera looked at the mounded graves, two of them now, side by side, strangers in life and neighbors in death. "My father used to say grief was a private thing. An *elevated* thing. We mourned in sealed rooms with the right clergy and the right incense, and the servants buried the actual bodies, and I never saw a single one go into the ground until—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Until the council. Seven coffins, and I wasn't permitted at the burial because my brother — because *it* — declared public mourning indecorous."

Kestrel finally turned her head. Gold hadn't touched her eyes; they were just gray and tired and human, and somehow that was worse, because it meant the exhaustion was real and hers and earned.

"You're comparing," Kestrel said. "Don't. Your dead were murdered by a monster wearing your brother. Mine were killed by *me*. There's no arithmetic that makes those equal, and I've done the sums every night for eleven years."

"No," Sera agreed. "There isn't. But you're wrong about the carrying." She reached down and took a handful of frozen earth, and held it out over Dorn's grave, and let it fall — a small gesture, clumsy, half a rite and half an apology. "You think the names are penance. I watched you just now. That wasn't penance. Penance doesn't learn the names. Penance doesn't dig with its hands. What you're doing is *keeping* them. There's a difference, and it matters, because one of those things will grind you down to nothing and the other one is why we follow you into mountains."

"I never asked anyone to follow me."

"No," Sera said. "That's rather the point."

The wind moved through the pines, and somewhere far off a wolf spoke and was answered, and Kestrel Eriksen — storm-caller, exile, killer of the wrong man — sat in the snow beside a smuggler's grave and cried without sound, the way people cry who learned young that noise brings attention. Sera did not touch her. She understood instinctively that touch would shatter whatever this was. She simply stayed, and after a while Kestrel leaned, very slightly, until her shoulder rested against Sera's, and they stayed like that while the dark deepened and the stars came out hard and cold over Thornfell.

"My mother tried to poison me when I was nine," Sera said, later, when the silence had gone soft enough to hold weight. "Not fatally. Enough to sicken. My father's rivals had bought her family's debts, you see, and I was the leverage that could move the king. I found out when I was fourteen. Everyone knew. Everyone had always known. They'd all decided it was better for me not to know." She laughed, and the laugh came out broken. "My whole childhood was a room full of people protecting me from the truth of them. So when Aldric changed — when the gold came into his eyes and the decrees went cold — I ran, and I told myself it was strategy. Survival. But part of it, if I'm honest in the dark, is that I couldn't bear to watch another family fail me. Better to leave before they do it properly."

"And mine threw me out before I could leave," Kestrel said. "Storm-calling's a crime in Haldenmark when it kills a lord's son. Even when the lord's son was mid-drowning and the storm was the only thing that could've saved the village he was flooding. They exiled me for the saving, because the saving was ugly." She flexed her scarred left hand in the starlight. "Eleven years guiding cutthroats through snow so I'd never owe another family anything. Families take. You know that. You were born knowing it."

"Yes," Sera said simply.

"But this—" Kestrel gestured vaguely at the waystation, at the smoke rising from its chimney, at the battered survivors inside who had buried two of their own and were, right now, Sera could hear, arguing about whether to press on or hole up, arguing the way families argue, with heat and history and no exits. "This isn't a family. Don't mistake it. It's worse and better. It's people who choose each other daily, with full knowledge, and can walk away any morning and mostly don't."

"That sounds like the definition of family to me. The only honest one."

Kestrel was quiet a moment. Then: "When this is done. When your brother is free or dead and the Veil is ash — what happens to you? Back to the palace? The crown they'll wave at you?"

"I don't know." And Sera found she meant it in a way that frightened her. "I used to know. I had a whole life mapped before the coronation illness. Now the map's burned and I'm walking with smugglers and archivists and a woman who talks to storms, and the strange thing is—" she searched for it, the true shape of it, "—the strange thing is I've never been less lost."

Kestrel huffed — almost a laugh. "Wait until you see the cost. There's always a cost."

"I know," Sera said. "I've started keeping count too."

Inside, the argument had resolved itself by the time they returned — not to agreement, but to the particular grim consensus of people who have decided that retreat costs more than advance. Vess stood at the map spread on the table, her eyes red-rimmed and dry now, her finger planted on the river road south.

"They'll report our position tonight," she said flatly, to all of them, though her gaze found Kestrel. "By dawn there won't be eight. There'll be thirty, and riders ahead of us closing the low passes. We go through the Gullet."

Murmurs — protest, dread. One of the younger smugglers said what they were all thinking: "The Gullet's warded. Old kingdom wards. Nobody's run it in twenty years, and nobody's run it *with* whatever she does." A jerk of the head toward Kestrel. Not hostile. Worse — hopeful.

Kestrel looked at the map for a long time. Sera watched her look, and understood that she was seeing more than terrain — she was weighing lives against secrets, exposure against graves, the bounty on her head against the bodies it might buy passage past.

"The wards are keyed to royal blood," Sera said quietly, and every head turned to her. "I used to read the old charters with Ondrey, before. The Gullet's ward-stones were set by Aldric the Third. They answer to the bloodline." She swallowed. "They'll answer to *me*. I can open it — probably. But opening it means the crown will know exactly where we are the moment it opens. It means announcing ourselves."

Silence, except for the wind.

"So we choose," Kestrel said at last, straightening, and her voice had that emptied-out steadiness again, but her eyes, when they met Sera's, held something new — not trust exactly, not yet, but the raw material of it. "Two days to the Gullet. We bury our dead, we march at first light, and we decide on the road who pays what." She rolled the map closed with finality. "Get your hours of sleep. All of you. The storm's coming — the real one, the one with teeth — and I intend to be the one holding its leash."

Outside, far to the north, thunder muttered under a cloudless sky, like something vast turning over in its sleep.

Sera did not need to be a storm-caller to know it was answering her.
