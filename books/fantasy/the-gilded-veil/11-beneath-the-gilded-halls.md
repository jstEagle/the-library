## 11. Beneath the Gilded Halls

The tunnel mouth opened behind a collapsed wine cellar in the Tanners' Quarter, breathing out air that smelled of stone dust and old secrets and, beneath it all, something faintly sweet — like honey left too long in winter.

Kestrel crouched at the threshold with her lantern shuttered to a knife-slit of light and studied the dark. Behind her, the company waited in the ruin's shadow: Sera with her hood up and her jaw set like a woman walking into her own funeral; Ondrey clutching his satchel of salvaged notes as though it were a child; six smugglers Kestrel had known for years, each one silent in the way people only got silent when they were afraid and refusing to say so.

"Two hundred paces down," Sera murmured, "the passage forks. Left goes to the old cisterns under the servants' wing. Right goes down into the deep vaults, and nobody who went right ever came back." She said it flatly, without drama, which somehow made it worse. "I used to play in these tunnels as a girl. Before."

Before. The word had become a country of its own since they'd left Thornfell. Before Aldric changed. Before the executions. Before the winter that would not end.

"Then we go left," Kestrel said.

"Obviously." But Sera didn't move yet. In the sliver of lantern light Kestrel saw her looking at the dark the way you look at a house where someone you loved died. "It's strange. I know every stone of this palace above. And I've never once felt what I feel right now standing outside it."

"That's your sense telling you the truth," Ondrey said quietly. "Listen to it. Then ignore it, because we're going in anyway."

Kestrel almost smiled. Almost. She unshuttered the lantern a finger's width more and stepped into the dark.

* * *

The tunnels were older than the palace above them — that much was clear within the first hundred paces. The walls here were rough-hewn granite, pre-imperial work, marked at intervals with symbols worn nearly smooth: spirals, branching lines, small carved figures with their arms raised. Old gods' marks, Ondrey called them, from the faith the crown had suppressed three centuries ago. Kestrel ran her scarred hand along one spiral as she passed and felt nothing, but the touch steadied her anyway. Whatever else this place was, it had been built by human hands for human purposes. She held on to that.

They moved in single file, boots wrapped in wool, speaking only in hand-signs. Kestrel took point because she always did, reading the tunnel the way she read mountain passes — by feel, by airflow, by the small animal instincts that had kept her alive through seven years of smuggling runs. The air was moving, barely. That was good. Moving air meant exits.

At the fork, they went left, down a sloping passage that grew colder with every step. Not winter-cold, not yet. Cellar-cold. But Kestrel noticed that her breath had begun to fog, and she noticed something else: the frost on the walls was not natural frost. It grew in patterns. Feathery whorls that repeated with geometric precision, spiraling inward toward some center she couldn't see, like ferns drawn by a draftsman with infinite patience and no soul at all.

She touched one whorl with her bare fingers before she could stop herself.

It was warm.

"Frost shouldn't be warm," she whispered back to Ondrey.

Ondrey leaned past her, squinting through his spectacles, and his face did something complicated. "No," he agreed. "It shouldn't." He didn't elaborate, and Kestrel decided she was grateful for that.

They came up through a rusted grate into the undercity proper — the warren of service corridors, storage vaults, and forgotten chapels that sprawled beneath the palace like the roots beneath a tree. And here, for the first time, Kestrel understood why Sera had needed to sit down against a wall when they emerged, pressing her palm over her mouth.

Because there were people.

Servants, dozens of them, moving through the great storage halls in long processions. They carried linens, candles, trays of bread, bundles of firewood — ordinary things, done in extraordinary silence. Not the silence of workers minding their own business. The silence of a hive. They moved in perfect synchrony, each procession spaced exactly evenly, each footfall landing in time with some rhythm Kestrel couldn't hear but could feel in her teeth, a slow pulse like a second heartbeat underneath the world.

And none of them were smiling.

That was the detail that lodged in Kestrel's chest like a fishhook. She'd seen misery before — conscripted laborers, debt-bonded miners, whole villages ground down by tax collectors. Miserable people found ways to suffer expressively. They dragged their feet. They muttered. Their eyes darted toward doors.

These servants' eyes never moved at all. They looked straight ahead, faces smooth as poured wax, and they worked with a grace that was worse than any shuffling despair — the grace of things being *used well*. A chambermaid set down a stack of linens, turned, walked twelve steps, took up a candlestick, turned again. Every motion identical to the maid beside her. Identical to the maid behind her. Identical, Kestrel realized with a slow crawl of horror up her spine, to the motion the same maid had probably made yesterday, and the day before, and every day since the Veil had settled into its harvest.

"They're not prisoners," Sera breathed. "Look at them. They're not even unhappy. There's nothing left in them to be unhappy *with*."

"Harvested," Ondrey said. He'd gone gray around the mouth. "This is what I tried to tell the council, twenty years ago. The Veil doesn't enslave, girl. Enslavement requires resistance. It *consumes*. It drinks the will out of a person the way weevils drink the heart out of grain, and what's left keeps working because working is all it knows how to do." He pressed his back against a pillar as a procession passed within arm's reach of their hiding place. None of the servants glanced over. "I watched my own colleagues begin to move like this, near the end. Perfect secretaries. Flawless copyists. And empty, empty, empty."

One of the smugglers — Brannic, a big quiet man who'd run passes with Kestrel for four years — made a small sound. When Kestrel looked at him, he was staring at a scullery boy in the nearest procession. The boy couldn't have been more than ten.

"We can't leave him," Brannic said. His voice cracked on the last word. "Kestrel. We can't just—"

"Brannic." She put her hand on his arm. "Can you get him out?"

"Yes."

"Can you get him out *without* every servant in this hall turning to look at us? Without whatever runs them noticing a piece of its harvest missing?" She held his gaze until he looked away. "Then no. Not tonight. Tonight we learn how to kill the thing that did this to him. That's how we save him. That's the only way."

Brannic's jaw worked. He nodded once, hard, and wiped his face with the back of his glove, and Kestrel pretended not to see, because that was a mercy he'd earned.

But she marked the boy's face. Brown hair, chapped lips, a smudge of flour on one cheek. If there was anything left of him anywhere inside that smooth wax calm, she intended to come back for it.

* * *

They moved deeper.

Sera led now, navigating by memories of childhood games, and her knowledge proved uncanny — a loose panel behind a rack of salted hams, a stair that let them bypass two checkpoints entirely, a chapel to some forgotten saint where the frost had grown so thick on the windows that the stained glass glowed like ice lit from within. Through it all, the pulse in Kestrel's teeth grew stronger. Slower, too. Deliberate. Like something enormous turning over in its sleep, counting the days.

Three weeks. Ondrey's voice in her memory: *Midwinter solstice. The binding completes, and then it won't matter what we do, because there will be nothing left of him to free.*

They reached the boundary of the royal wing an hour before dawn, and there Kestrel called the halt, in a narrow gallery hung with portraits of dead kings. Frost covered everything here — thick, deliberate, sculptural frost, climbing the gilded frames and creeping across the painted faces until each king wore a mask of white lace. Except the most recent portrait. The one of Aldric.

His frame was frosted like all the others, but his face had been left clear. Painted eyes looking out over the frozen gallery, young and tired and trapped inside his own likeness, and Kestrel stood in front of it for a long moment feeling something turn over in her chest that she refused to name.

"He looks like our father," Sera said softly, beside her. "In that one. He hated sitting for it. Fidgeted for hours. The painter finally threatened to tie him to the chair." Her voice caught. "That was two months before the coronation. Two months."

"Sera."

"I'm fine."

"You're not."

"No," Sera agreed, still looking at the painting. "But I will be. After."

Kestrel let it go. Some griefs you carried by setting them down for later, and she was the last person alive entitled to lecture anyone about that.

It was Ondrey who noticed the thing that changed everything.

He had drifted to the far end of the gallery, where a door stood half-open onto a service stair, and he made a small strangled noise. When they joined him, he was pointing — not at anything in the stairwell, but at the frost on the doorframe itself.

The patterns had changed.

Everywhere else in the undercity, the frost had grown in those feathery spirals, beautiful and dead. Here, on this doorframe, the whorls resolved into something else. Letters. Or almost-letters. Characters from no alphabet Kestrel knew, dense and interlocking, ring after ring of them circling the door like a collar.

"It's writing," Ondrey whispered. He had his spectacles off and was cleaning them with shaking hands, which he only did when his mind was running faster than his courage. "Or — no. No, it's accounting. Look at the repetition. Look at the intervals. This isn't decoration, this is a *ledger*." He looked up at them, and his eyes behind the smeared lenses were terrible. "We've been thinking about the Veil wrong. We've been thinking about it as a parasite, a disease, a madness. But parasites hide. Diseases spread blindly. This—" he gestured at the door, at the perfect spirals beyond, at the hollow-eyed servants below "—this is *bookkeeping*. Everything it takes, it records. Everyone it empties, it counts. It's not feeding the way an animal feeds. It's harvesting the way a farmer harvests. With intent. With patience. With a plan for next season."

The silence in the gallery was very long.

"So it's not mad," Kestrel said at last. "Aldric. The decrees, the executions, the endless winter. It's not a king going mad. It's a king being *used*, methodically, to strip his kingdom down to bare obedience before the binding completes."

"And every decree makes it stronger," Sera said. Her voice had gone flat and cold, the voice Kestrel had heard only twice before, both times just before violence. "Every law, every execution, every person who kneels and means it. We're not fighting a tyrant. We're fighting a *harvest*, and the whole kingdom is the field, and midwinter is the reaping."

Above them, muffled by floors and stone, a bell began to toll. Not the dawn bell — too early, too slow, each stroke falling precisely in time with the pulse Kestrel felt in her teeth. One. Two. Three. Nine strokes, and then silence, and in the silence every servant in the palace below would be turning, in perfect synchrony, toward the sound.

Toward the throne room.

Sera went white. "That's the summons bell. It hasn't rung since—" She stopped. Swallowed. "It only rings when the king commands the full court. Everyone. Everyone is being called."

"Why?" Brannic whispered. "What happens at midwinter court?"

Ondrey was already digging in his satchel, pulling out notes, his hands flying. "Nothing good. Nothing in any record I—" He stopped. Went still. Slowly, he looked up at the frost-covered door, at the ledger-rings circling it, and Kestrel watched a man realize something he desperately didn't want to know.

"The count," he said. "The ledger. It's been counting everyone in this palace. Every harvested servant, every obedient official, every bowed head. And a summoning of the full court—" his voice dropped to almost nothing "—would bring every unharvested soul in the building into one room. Into the field. All at once. Where the harvest is strongest."

Kestrel felt the bottom drop out of the night.

"It's not calling them to court," she said. "It's calling them to the *table*."

Above them, the pulse in her teeth quickened — and somewhere far overhead, through floors of marble and gold, something ancient and patient began, with great satisfaction, to open its books.
