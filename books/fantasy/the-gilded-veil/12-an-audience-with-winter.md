## 12. An Audience With Winter

They came for her at the third bell after midnight, and she let them.

That was the part Kestrel would turn over in her mind later, in the hours when sleep refused her — how easy it had been. She had walked the palace undercity for two nights with Sera's maps inked on the inside of her wrist, cataloguing frost-bloomed corridors and servants who moved like clockwork, and when the Veil's guards finally closed around her in a corridor that smelled of copper and cold iron, she did not run. She could have. There was a service tunnel six paces behind her; she'd marked it herself.

Instead she stood still and let six men in silver-and-gold livery bind her wrists with wire thin enough to bite.

*Deliberate,* Ondrey would have said, if he were there to say it. *Nothing in that palace happens by accident.* But Ondrey was not there. Ondrey was three weeks gone into the capital's undercity with his archives burning behind him, and every plan they'd built since had been built on guesses and Sera's ten-year-old memories of a brother who no longer existed.

So Kestrel had made a guess of her own: the Veil had let her walk out of its throne room once. It had looked at her — through Aldric's borrowed eyes, with a gaze like sunlight through ice — and it had let her go. Things did not spare what they feared. They studied it. They set snares for it.

She intended to be caught in the snare rather than wander the forest blind.

The guards did not speak. That was the first wrongness. They marched her up through the palace's frozen spine — stairwells where the banisters wore rime like fur, galleries where portraits of dead kings hung behind glass furred white at the corners — and not one of them breathed loud enough to hear. Their boots made sound. Their armor made sound. But their breathing, their swallowing, the small wet noises a body makes simply by being alive: nothing. As though the cold had taken those from them and left only the shapes of men.

Kestrel counted doors. Counted turns. Filed away every junction against the day she'd need to run this route in reverse, or drag someone else's body down it.

At the top of the final stair, a pair of doors tall as ship masts swung open without being touched, and warm light spilled out — actual warmth, golden and flickering, the first she'd felt since entering the palace — and she understood the cruelty of it immediately. The Veil kept one room warm so that everyone who entered it would know, viscerally, exactly what had been taken from everywhere else.

* * *

The king's study had been Aldric's once. She could see the ghost of him in it: a young man's clutter of maps and half-read books, a chessboard mid-game beside the window, a lute leaning in the corner with dust on its strings. The dust was the tell. Two years of dust, undisturbed, while the room's occupant sat at the desk beneath a candelabrum of forty lit tapers and did not appear to have moved since they entered.

King Aldric of Vaelmoor was beautiful the way winter is beautiful, which is to say from a distance, and briefly.

He was twenty-four years old and looked carved from candle-wax — smooth-cheeked, fine-boned, wearing a high-collared coat of white brocade shot through with gold thread that caught the light and seemed to move when he didn't. And threaded through his hair, along his temples, down the side of his neck like gilded ivy: filigree. Fine metal tracery grown into living skin, delicate as lacework, gleaming softly in the candlelight.

His eyes opened. They were gold. Not amber, not hazel — gold, molten and patient, the eyes of something that had watched empires freeze.

"Kestrel Eriksen," the thing wearing her king said. Its voice was Aldric's voice, low and pleasant, but it used the words the way a musician uses an instrument — precisely, and without any feeling you could name. "Storm-caller. Exile of the Skarnmark. Killer of Torvald Eriksen, your father's brother, on the night of your nineteenth year."

Her stomach dropped through the floor. "You've read my file."

"I have read *everything*." He rose from the desk, unhurried, and the candles leaned toward him like flowers toward sun. "Every ledger in this kingdom's memory. Every confession in its cells. You are more legible than most, Kestrel Eriksen. Guilt writes very clearly."

"I've heard the archivists talk like that." She kept her hands loose at her sides, the wire still biting her wrists. "Usually right before someone makes them disappear."

"I am not going to make you disappear." Aldric crossed the room, footsteps silent on the rug, and stopped just close enough that she could see the filigree moving beneath the skin of his throat — actually moving, a slow peristalsis, as though something beneath his collarbone were breathing separately from him. "I am going to make you an offer."

"There it is."

"You expected it."

"The last time I met something old and cold, it also wanted to talk before it ate me."

Something happened to his mouth. It took her a moment to recognize it as a smile, because it arrived without any of the muscles around the eyes participating — a smile painted onto a mask worn over a face. "Your storm killed the wrong man," he said. "One degree of wind astray. One heartbeat of misjudgment. And a whole country decided that what you were was too dangerous to keep." He tilted his head. "They exiled you for a mistake. I am offering you a correction."

Kestrel said nothing. Her heart was hammering and she hated that it was hammering, because some treacherous part of her had already understood what was coming and wanted it anyway.

"A pardon," the Veil said. "Formal. Sealed. Your name restored to the rolls of the Skarnmark. Your passage home paid. Your father's house — it still stands, does it not? On the cliff above Hjelsund? I have seen it in the shipping manifests. Salt-weathered. Empty eleven years. Yours again, if you want it." The gold eyes held hers. "And all I ask in return is that you take the smugglers' road south, and keep walking, and do not look back at my winter."

The candles crackled. Somewhere far below, frost crept another inch across another windowsill, and somewhere above them the stars turned, indifferent, and Kestrel stood in a warm room for the first time in three weeks and felt colder than she had in the mountain passes.

Because here was the terrible arithmetic of it: her name. Eleven years of it. *Kestrel the kinslayer*, whispered in every port from Thornfell to the sea. Eleven years of guiding other people's contraband through other people's mountains because no homeland would hold her and no honest work would touch her. A door back. A house. A father who had not spoken to her since the funeral, but who might — might — open the door.

"That's it," she said quietly. "Walk away. That's all it costs you."

"It costs me nothing. You are four people and a burned archive. My winter has stood for two years against armies." The Veil smiled its painted smile again. "But you are a variable, Kestrel Eriksen. Storms are variables. And I do not abide variables inside my walls when I can purchase their departure for the price of a signature."

*It's lying,* she told herself. *Or worse — it isn't.*

"And if I refuse?"

Aldric's head cocked, birdlike, and for a moment the filigree along his jaw brightened, gold pulsing faintly like veins of ore catching firelight. "Then we arrive at the alternative conversation, which is longer, and which I promise you will enjoy less."

* * *

She meant to refuse. She wanted that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger the old gods kept, in whatever book the Veil itself consulted — she meant to refuse. The words were already assembled behind her teeth: *Keep your pardon. Keep your winter. I've slept in snow before.*

What came out instead was a question, because Kestrel had spent eleven years learning that questions bought more than refusals.

"Why him?"

The Veil went still. Perfectly, utterly still — stiller than a man could be, stiller than a statue, a stillness that made the candle flames seem violent by comparison.

"Aldric," she said. "He was twenty-two. He had a sister who loved him and a council that trusted him and, judging by this room, a life he actually liked. Why does your kind need a crown? You had the mountains. You had the deep winters. You were doing fine freezing shepherds and closing passes. Why wear a person?"

For a long moment the gold eyes regarded her, and she had the sense — horrible and total — of being weighed on scales old enough to have weighed the first glaciers.

"Order," the Veil said at last, "is not cruelty. Cruelty is chaos with intent. What I bring is simpler than either. The river floods until it is governed. The mob burns until it is governed. The young king wept at his coronation because he understood, even then, how heavy the crown was — and heaviness is a door, and I am a patient tenant." It drifted closer, and now she could smell it off him: cold iron and candle smoke and underneath both, faintly, the smell of high-altitude snow, the smell of her childhood. "He fought me for eight months. Do you know what that is worth? Eight months. Most hosts last eight days. There is a stubbornness in this bloodline that I confess I find... instructive."

"He's still in there."

"He is *present*," the Veil corrected, with something almost like irritation. "Present is not the same as remaining."

And then — whether it was fatigue in the great machine of the thing, or the candles guttering in some draft she couldn't feel, or simply that the question had struck along a seam where the gold ran thin — Kestrel saw it happen.

Aldric blinked.

It was a human blink, slow and clumsy, nothing like the reptilian precision of everything else the body had done. And when the eyelids lifted, the eyes behind them were still gold, but the gold was *shaking*, like lamplight in wind, like something drowning under ice looking up through it — and the face, the wax-carved beautiful mask, cracked. His mouth trembled. His hand, rising off the desk, shook with effort, fingers dragging upward like a man hauling himself hand-over-hand out of deep water, and they stopped an inch from hers, trembling, curling, and his lips moved.

No sound came out. His throat belonged to something else. But she could read lips well enough — eleven years among smugglers taught you to read mouths through murk and muzzle-shadow — and the shape of the words was unmistakable, repeated twice, three times, tears cutting clean lines down cheeks that should not have been able to produce them:

*Don't listen. Don't listen. Don't—*

The gold flared.

It happened fast, a shutter slamming, and Aldric's spine straightened and the tears were simply gone, reabsorbed, and the hand dropped back to the desk as though it had never moved. But Kestrel had seen. She had seen the way a man looks at the one person in the world who might still be coming for him, and she had seen him spend what little strength he had not begging for rescue but warning her — *don't listen* — because he knew what the thing wearing him would offer and he knew, better than anyone alive, what accepting it would cost.

Her refusal arrived late and ragged. "No," she said. "Whatever you're selling. No."

"Pity," the Veil said, and the word was genuinely regretful, which was the worst part. "I thought you might prove sensible. Very well, Kestrel Eriksen. The alternative conversation."

She braced for the guards, for the cells, for the long interrogatory dark.

Instead, the king extended one ringed hand, palm up, and gestured toward the doors.

"You are free to go."

Kestrel stared at him. "That's not — you don't get to just—"

"The wire," the Veil said mildly, and the binding at her wrists fell away, coil by coil, unwinding itself like a snake deciding to leave. "The guards will escort you to the Fountain Gate. You will find your friends untouched, your tunnels unmapped, and your archivist's little duplicate codex exactly where you believe it is hidden." The gold eyes glinted. "Yes. We know about the codex. We have known since the archive burned. Burned archives are such honest things — they tell you precisely what their owners loved enough to copy."

Cold walked up Kestrel's spine on small feet.

"Consider tonight a courtesy," the Veil continued, settling back into the chair, arranging its stolen hands with terrible elegance. "And consider it also a message. I know your number. I know your names — the sister, the archivist, the knife-man with the debts, the twins. I know where you sleep. I permitted tonight's capture because I wished to see the storm-caller at close range, and having seen her, I find her smaller than her reputation." A pause. The smile again, painted on. "Go home, Kestrel. Whatever home is, for a woman like you. You have three weeks until solstice. Spend them running. It is what you are best at."

The doors opened. The guards waited.

Kestrel walked. She kept her back straight and her breathing even and her eyes forward the entire way down the frozen spine of the palace, past the rimed portraits of dead kings, past the silent soldiers whose chests made no sound of breath, out through the Fountain Gate into the black and freezing street — and only when the palace was a glittering mausoleum behind her did she stop in an alley and put her hand against the wall and shake.

Not from fear. Or not only.

From the image she couldn't put down: a drowned man behind ice, spending his last strength not to save himself, but to warn her.

* * *

The safehouse was a cooper's cellar near the tanneries, reachable through three false doors and one sewer junction, and by the time she reached it the sky over the capital was going grey with the hour before dawn. Sera was awake — Sera was always awake now, sleeping in fragments like a soldier — and she took one look at Kestrel's face and reached for the lamp.

"They let me go," Kestrel said.

Sera went very still. "Let you."

"Walking out the front gate. Guards at my elbow the whole way." She peeled off her soaked gloves and flexed her fingers, trying to warm them, trying to stop seeing the trembling hand. "It knows everything, Sera. Our names. Our numbers. The codex. It knew why I came before I came."

"Then why—" Sera's voice cracked and rebuilt itself. "Why release you? If it knows all of that, why not kill you? Why not take you?"

"Because killing me proves I'm dangerous. Taking me proves I'm necessary." Kestrel sank onto a flour barrel, bone-tired, and heard her own voice come out flat and cold. "Letting me go proves neither. It wants us to sit in this cellar asking exactly that question — *why did it let her go* — turning it over and over until we trust nothing, move nowhere, suspect every door we walk through. Fear doesn't need chains. It just needs a reason."

Behind them, at the top of the stairs, a floorboard creaked.

Ondrey descended slowly, one hand on the rail, his burned-hands wrapped in fresh linen, his eyes red-rimmed and ferocious above the grey stubble of three bad weeks. He had heard. Of course he had heard; the man slept like a cat sleeps, with one ear working.

"She's right," he said. "And she's also wrong, because there is a second answer sitting underneath the first one, and it's worse." He lowered himself onto a crate with the care of a much older man. "The Veil doesn't study threats, girl. Threats it crushes. What it studies is *hope*. Hope is the one harvest it cannot grow on its own — obedience it can farm, fear it can farm, but hope has to walk in through the front door carrying its own lantern." His eyes found hers, and they were gentle and terrible. "It showed you Aldric. Didn't it. For a moment. Long enough."

Kestrel said nothing. Which was answer enough.

"Then understand what you were shown," Ondrey said quietly. "Understand that the thing in that chair *chose* to loosen its grip for exactly the length of time required to break your heart. It knows the codex says the crown must be freely surrendered. It knows you'll now believe surrender is possible — that the boy inside can be reached, that mercy is hiding in there waiting for you. And it will use that belief to lead you, step by step, to exactly where it wants you standing when the solstice comes." He leaned forward, linen-bound hands clasped. "Hope is the hook, Kestrel. Tonight it baited the hook. And the cruelest trick of all—"

"—is that it might be true," Kestrel finished.

Silence filled the cellar, thick as tallow.

Above them, the city bells rang the dawn hour, and beneath the ringing, faint but unmistakable, came a sound none of them had heard in the capital before — distant, crystalline, wrong. The sound of the harbor freezing. All of it. At once.

Ondrey closed his eyes. "Three weeks," he whispered. "And it's accelerating."

Kestrel looked down at her scarred left hand, at the palm that had called one storm and buried a man, and made herself say the thing she'd carried out of the palace like a splinter under the skin.

"It begged me, Ondrey. Not for rescue. It told me *don't listen*. It's in there, and it's drowning, and it used its one free breath to try to keep me safe." Her fist closed. "Bait or not — trap or not — I'm going back in."
