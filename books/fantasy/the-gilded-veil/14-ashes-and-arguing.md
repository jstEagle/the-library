## 14. Ashes and Arguing

The sewers of Vhalen's Reach smelled like the death of a city that had once been proud.

Sera had grown up above them, in the sunlit corridors of the palace, learning statecraft from her father and swordwork from masters who smelled of cedar oil. She had never once wondered where the water went, or what lived beneath the marble. Now she knew: the answer was everything the city wanted to forget, and it dripped from the ceiling in long, slow beads that caught the light of their single lantern like falling amber.

They had found a junction chamber — a vaulted knot of brick where three tunnels met and the water ran shallow over a floor of ancient cobbles. Ondrey would have known its name. Ondrey would have known its history, its builder, the year the mortar was laid. The thought arrived unbidden and landed like a knife between her ribs.

*They took him at dusk,* she thought. *And we ran.*

She sat on a dry ledge above the waterline with her back against the brick, her knees drawn up, and watched the others arrange themselves around the chamber the way people do when they have stopped trusting one another. Kestrel stood apart, near the tunnel mouth they'd come through, her scarred left hand pressed flat against the wall as if she were listening for pursuit through the stone. The smugglers — what remained of them — clustered around a guttering brazier Doss had conjured from scrap fat and rags. There were nine of them now. Two days ago there had been eleven.

Maren and Cott. Sera made herself hold the names in her mind, because no one else seemed willing to. Maren, who had laughed at her own jokes before anyone else did. Cott, who had carried a carved wooden bird in his pocket and claimed his daughter had made it, though everyone suspected he'd whittled it himself and was too embarrassed to say so. Both of them dead in the archive fire's aftermath, cut down by Veil-touched guardsmen in the streets while the rest of them scattered like startled pigeons.

The smoke from the burning archive had been visible across half the city. Sera had watched it rise from an alley mouth — a black column standing straight up in the windless air, like a finger pointed at heaven, accusing it.

"They'll be questioning him now," said Vetch, one of the older smugglers, a grey-bearded man who'd lost two fingers to frostbite years before Thornfell ever got cold enough to explain it. He spoke to the brazier rather than to anyone in particular. "Ondrey. They'll have him in some cellar with the gold-eyed ones asking questions, and he'll tell them everything. He's a scholar. Scholars talk."

"He won't talk," Kestrel said, without turning from the wall.

"You know that how?"

"Because paranoid men build failsafes, and failsafes are promises you make yourself." She still hadn't turned. "He told me once: *the first rule of hiding a thing is to be worth more silent than broken.*"

"And what if he isn't?" Vetch pressed. "What if they don't need him to talk? What if they already know everything he knew, because they've been watching him for thirty years and letting him think he was hidden?" He spat into the water. "We put our whole plan in the hands of an old man who talked to books, and now the books are ash and the old man is in chains. I say we're done."

Sera felt the words land in her chest like stones dropped into still water. "Done," she repeated. "Done meaning what?"

"Meaning we cut our losses." Vetch finally looked up, and his eyes in the lantern light were flat and tired and utterly without shame. "Meaning some of us have families in Thornfell who'd like to see us again. Meaning the king's men know our faces now, all of them, and every hour we stay in this city is an hour closer to a headsman's block. Meaning the smart move — the only move — is north, tonight, through the tunnels Kestrel knows, and never come back."

"Never come back," Sera said again. Her voice came out strange to her own ears, very quiet and very cold, the voice her father had used when dismissing ambassadors whose petitions displeased him. "You want to run."

"I want to *live*, girl. There's a difference, and dead people can't tell you what it is."

"I am not—" She caught herself. Breathed. "I am not a girl. And my brother is being eaten alive by something older than your grandmother's grandmother, and every day we delay is a day closer to midwinter, when it finishes whatever it started, and then there will be nothing left to run *to*. You've seen the villages. You've seen what the winter edicts do. How long do you think Thornfell stays free? How long do you think anywhere does?"

"Longer than us, if we're not corpses in a ditch outside the palace walls."

"It wouldn't be a ditch," said another voice — Tam, young Tam, who hadn't spoken since the ambush. He was sitting with his arms wrapped around his knees, staring at the water, and when he looked up his face was the face of someone reading his own death warrant and finding it fair. "It'd be the throne room. Have you seen it? Have any of you actually *seen* what's up there now?" His laugh came out cracked. "I have. Through a grate, two nights ago. The whole court standing in rows, perfect as soldiers, and the king on his throne with frost growing out of the armrests like it was *growing out of him*, and nobody — *nobody* — moving except to breathe. We're talking about fighting that? With nine people and a smuggler's knives?"

"Yes," Sera said.

"With *what*?"

"With whatever we have. Because it's what we have."

"That's not a plan, that's a eulogy—"

"ENOUGH."

Kestrel's voice cracked across the chamber like the first thunderclap of a summer storm, and every head turned. She had finally moved away from the wall, and she stood in the center of the junction now, lantern light sliding across the burn-scarred skin of her left hand, and Sera saw that she was shaking — not with fear, Sera realized, but with the effort of holding something enormous inside a small frame.

"We are not assaulting anything," Kestrel said. "And we are not running. And if either of you says another word about either, I will personally throw you in the water and let the current decide."

* * *

The silence afterward had weight, like snowfall.

Sera stared at her. "You would argue for *patience*. Now. With three weeks left."

"I'd argue for not being idiots," Kestrel said. "Which is different, and harder. Sit down, Sera."

"I will not—"

"*Sit down.*" It wasn't shouted this time. It was worse than shouting — quiet, level, final, the voice of someone who had led frightened people through deadly places for years and had learned exactly how much force a human voice could carry. Sera sat. She hated herself for sitting, and sat anyway.

Kestrel crouched by the brazier, close enough that the firelight reached her face, and Sera saw the exhaustion there properly for the first time — the hollows under her eyes, the rawness at the corners of her mouth. She had been carrying them all, Sera understood suddenly. Since the mountain gate. Since the courier. Carrying them the way she carried everything: silently, and with a fury she mistook for discipline.

"Here's what patience looks like," Kestrel said. "Vetch is right about one thing: Ondrey taken means the Veil knows our shape. Our faces, our numbers, our tunnels — maybe. So we assume the worst. We assume every route we planned is burned. We assume the palace undercity is a trap with our names on it." She held up her scarred hand, ticking points off against her thumb. "That leaves us one asset it doesn't know about, because it can't. Something Ondrey hid so well even the fire didn't find it."

"The codex," Sera breathed. "The duplicate. You think it's real."

"I think Ondrey was the most frightened man I've ever met, and frightened men like him don't keep single copies of anything. I think it's under the spine of the book that started all this — because that's the one place in the world he knew I'd never destroy, no matter how badly I wanted to." Kestrel's mouth twisted. "I've been carrying his life's work in my pack for a month and cursing its weight every mile."

Tam stirred. "Even if it exists. Even if it tells us something. Three weeks, and we're nine, and half the city wants us dead."

"Then we'd better read fast."

"And if it doesn't exist?" Vetch asked. "If the old man's paranoia was just paranoia?"

Kestrel was quiet for a moment. When she answered, she didn't look at Vetch. She looked at Sera, and her eyes were the grey of storm-light over open water.

"Then we're nine people in a sewer with a dead plan," she said. "But we'll still be nine people who didn't run. And I've learned — recently, and expensively — that what you carry out of a losing fight matters more than whether you win it."

* * *

They almost ended, later. Over nothing, which is how the worst endings happen.

It began because Sera went looking for the prophecy book. Kestrel kept it wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of her pack, and Sera told herself she only wanted to look at it — to hold the thing that had set all of this in motion, to feel its weight and know it was real, because everything else had come apart and she needed one solid object in her hands.

Kestrel found her unwrapping it.

"Give it back."

"I'm not stealing it. I'm reading it."

"It's mine."

"It fell into your hands by accident!"

"And everything that matters falls by accident!" Kestrel crossed the chamber in four strides and took the book, and Sera let go of it too late and too hard, and the oilcloth tore, and for one horrible heartbeat they stood chest to chest with the prophecy between them like a child fought over in a divorce, and Sera saw — clearly, terribly — that Kestrel's eyes were wet.

Not with rage. Sera knew rage; she'd been raised in a court full of it. This was something rawer. Grief wearing rage's clothing.

"You think I don't want to go up there right now," Kestrel said, low. "You think I sleep well down here while he's in chains and you're chewing yourself hollow? Every hour we wait, I hear it. The same as you." Her grip on the book tightened until the leather creaked. "But I have buried people because I moved too fast. A whole valley of them, once. I called a storm to save a village and it killed the wrong man, and I have spent six years learning the difference between *now* and *too soon*, and I am telling you — I am *begging* you — not to make me learn it again with your brother's life."

"My brother is *dying*—"

"My brother died six years ago!" Kestrel's voice broke open, and the whole chamber flinched. "And I have walked past his grave every day since! Do you understand? Every step I take toward that throne room, I pass it! So don't stand there and tell me about dying, Sera Aldric's-sister, because I am the world's leading expert on what it costs to be too late, and I am telling you that rushing up those stairs with nine knives and a prayer gets him killed *certainly* instead of *maybe*, and I will not — I will *not* — bury you both!"

The echo came back off the wet brick three times, and then there was nothing but the drip of water and the pop of the scrap-fat fire and nine people discovering simultaneously that they had been holding their breath.

Sera became aware that she was crying. Not elegantly. Not the way she'd been taught, with a handkerchief and a lowered gaze. Ugly crying, in a sewer, in front of smugglers, with snot and everything, and she found she could not summon the smallest shred of shame about it.

"He used to sneak me honeycakes," she whispered. "Before coronations. Before councils. He'd climb through my window with his boots in his hands so the guards wouldn't hear, and we'd sit on the roof and eat stolen honeycakes and he'd tell me how he was going to be a good king. He *practiced* being good, Kestrel. Who practices being good?" Her breath hitched. "And now he's up there behind those gold eyes, and I keep thinking — is he in there? Is he watching? Does he know I ran? Does he know I left him alone with *it*?"

Kestrel let go of the book. She crossed the last distance between them and did something Sera had not expected, had not permitted herself to expect from anyone in six years of exile and flight: she pulled her in, awkward and fierce, one hand fisted in the back of Sera's coat, and held on.

"He knows you came back," Kestrel said into her hair. "Whatever else is true — however deep it's buried — he knows that."

* * *

Later, when the others had settled into uneasy watch rotations along the three tunnel mouths, Sera sat beside Kestrel on the ledge and watched the water slide past their boots, black and patient, going somewhere they couldn't follow.

"Vetch and Tam will leave," Sera said quietly. "Maybe tomorrow. Maybe tonight."

"Some of them might."

"Will you stop them?"

"No." Kestrel turned the prophecy book over in her scarred hands, not opening it. "People get to choose what they survive. That's the whole point of surviving."

"And if there are only five of us left when the codex tells us what we have to do?"

Kestrel was silent long enough that Sera thought she wouldn't answer. Then she lifted the book, and with her thumbnail — deliberately, without ceremony — split the binding along the spine, where thirty years of Ondrey's careful re-sewing had left a seam thin as a hair.

Something slid from within the leather. A second codex, slim and dense and wrapped in waxed linen, sealed with a blob of black wax stamped with the archivist's personal sigil: a key, biting its own teeth.

Kestrel held it in the lantern light without breaking the seal, and Sera saw her swallow.

"Well," Kestrel said softly. "The paranoid old bastard."

Above them, far away, muffled by forty feet of brick and earth and the sleeping, harvested city, the great bells of Vhalen's Reach began to toll midnight — and Sera counted the strokes, and counted again, and felt the blood leave her face.

Fourteen days to solstice.

One fewer than any of them had believed.
