## 16. The Sister's Gambit

The sewers beneath the capital smelled of rot and old rain, and Sera had stopped noticing three days ago. That was its own kind of grief — the daughter of a king, raised in marble halls where the floors were washed with rosewater twice a day, now sitting on a wet stone ledge with filth to her ankles, and feeling nothing.

She watched Kestrel across the flooded channel. The storm-caller sat apart from the others, as she always did, turning the prophecy book over in her scarred hands. Her left hand. The one the book had written about before any of them knew her name. Around her, what remained of their company huddled in small knots: Vess sharpening a knife that was already sharp, the twins Dara and Pell whispering over a scrap of tunnel map, old Mott asleep sitting up, which he claimed was a skill learned in prison and Sera had long since stopped questioning.

They had lost Ondrey four days ago. They had lost the archive, the codex, the research — twenty years of a mad old man's careful heresy, gone up in smoke that still smelled faintly of it when the wind shifted through the tunnels. And they had lost something harder to name: the belief that they were hunting the Veil rather than being hunted by it.

Sera stood, and every eye came up. She had been a princess for twenty-six years; she knew how rooms went quiet.

"We need to talk about what happens next," she said. "All of us."

Kestrel didn't look up. "We've talked. We run, we hide, we wait for spring."

"Spring won't come." Sera stepped over the channel, boots splashing, and planted herself in front of the other woman. "You heard Ondrey. Midwinter solstice. Three weeks. After that, the binding completes, and Aldric stops being a man wearing a crown and becomes a crown wearing a man. There is no waiting out a thing like that. There is no spring after it. There's just winter, forever, with everyone smiling."

"So we're agreed," Kestrel said flatly. "Running is pointless. Which means we're down to hiding or dying, and I've gotten very good at hiding."

"You're down to hiding." Sera let the words sit. "I'm not."

Vess looked up from her knife. Even Mott cracked an eye.

Kestrel finally raised her gaze, and Sera met it. The storm-caller's eyes were gray as slate weather, and just as hard to read, but Sera had spent two years learning to read her brother's eyes while something else moved behind them. She was not afraid of difficult eyes anymore.

"What are you proposing?" Kestrel asked, and her voice had gone quiet in the way it did when she already suspected the answer and hated it.

"I'm going to walk up to the palace gates," Sera said, "in daylight, in front of witnesses, and claim the throne."

* * *

The silence afterward lasted long enough that somewhere in the dark, water dripped eleven times. Sera counted. It was a habit from childhood — Aldric counting drips during storms, telling her that if you could count the spaces between frightening things, the frightening things got smaller. She wondered if he was counting anything now, inside whatever gilded cell the Veil had built around his mind.

"You're insane," Vess said at last, not unkindly. "No offense meant, Highness."

"None taken. Insanity is often just clarity spoken too loudly."

"It's suicide," Pell said. He was the younger twin, the optimist, and even he sounded hollowed out. "The moment you say those words in public, the Veil knows exactly where you are. It doesn't need to hunt you. You'll be delivering yourself."

"Yes," Sera said. "That's rather the point."

She turned and paced the length of the ledge, because she had rehearsed this argument alone for two nights and her body needed the movement. "Think about what the Veil is. Ondrey told us — it feeds on obedience. Every decree strengthens it. Every bowed head, every tax paid without complaint, every courtier who smiles when they want to scream. It grows fat on consent, even frightened consent. It has spent two years teaching an entire kingdom to kneel."

"And?" Kestrel said.

"And nothing threatens it more than a rival. Not a blade — blades it can catch, we've seen that. A *claim*. An unbroken line of succession standing in front of the whole court saying: this crown does not belong to you. Because here is what the Veil cannot do, friends. It cannot make people obey someone they believe is false. Oh, it can frighten them. It can freeze them. But obedience freely given is its food, and if half the kingdom looks at my brother on the throne and sees a usurper — sees *me*, the true heir, dragged away in chains — then every act of cruelty it commits to silence me becomes a meal it chokes on. Doubt is poison to a thing made of order."

Mott had woken fully now. "It'll kill you," he said simply. Old soldiers wasted no words.

"Perhaps." She kept her voice level. "But consider how. The Veil is careful. It survived a hundred years between hosts by being patient, by never drawing attention, by ruling through a beloved king so gently that no one noticed the frost creeping in. If it executes me publicly, it creates a martyr with royal blood. If it hides me away, it confirms every rumor. Either way, the court starts whispering, and whispers become doubt, and doubt starves it. Its best move is to make me disappear quietly — and even that requires chaos, cover, distraction." She spread her hands. "Chaos and cover and distraction happen to be exactly what a certain group of smugglers specializes in."

"That's why you really want this," Kestrel said slowly. "Not the martyrdom. The noise."

"The noise," Sera agreed. "While every eye in the palace turns to the estranged princess making her ridiculous claim, someone walks through the undercity to my brother's bedchamber. Someone who can get past wards that answer to royal blood." She looked at Kestrel directly. "Someone who has already seen him once and walked out alive."

"He let me go," Kestrel said. "Ondrey said it was a trap."

"It was a trap. And we're going to spring it anyway, but on our terms instead of theirs." Sera felt the old courtroom fire kindling in her chest, the one she'd thought had died with her father. "The Veil thinks it knows all our moves because it took our map. So we make new moves. Moves it can't predict because they cost more than any sane person would pay."

* * *

They argued for hours. Sera had expected that, and welcomed it — an argument was at least motion, and motion was better than the stagnant despair of the last four days.

Vess objected first and longest: the plan put their only link to the throne's inner workings at risk, and Sera couldn't argue with the logic, only answer it with the truth. "There is no version of this where I stay safe in a hole while my brother drowns. I ran once. I told myself it was strategy, survival, living to fight another day. Two years I've told myself that. And you know what I learned? Running isn't a strategy. It's just dying slowly, somewhere else."

Dara wanted to know who would smuggle her to the gates, and that turned into a genuine tactical debate — which entrances, which guards could be bought, which had families the Veil might hold hostage. Pell sketched the plaza before the Gate of Ashen Kings from memory, marking sightlines. Mott, of all people, proved to have opinions about crowd dynamics gleaned from thirty years of riots, and delivered them with the dry authority of a man describing weather.

Through all of it, Kestrel said almost nothing. She sat with the prophecy book in her lap and watched Sera with that slate-gray stare, and Sera found she minded it less than she should have. There was no pity in that stare. Pity would have been unbearable. There was only assessment — the same look Kestrel gave a mountain pass before deciding whether it could be crossed.

When the arguments finally wound down into exhausted silence, Kestrel spoke.

"Why you?"

Sera blinked. "What?"

"There are other ways to make noise. Burn a granary. Assassinate a minister. Any of a dozen things that don't require putting the last person in the world who loves Aldric Eriksen on a stage in front of the thing that eats kings." Kestrel's voice wasn't cruel. That was the terrible part. It was genuinely curious, the way she'd ask about a route through badlands. "Why does it have to be you?"

And there it was. The question underneath everything, the one Sera had been circling for two days, the one she'd hoped nobody sharp enough would ask.

She sat down on the ledge across from Kestrel, close enough that the channel's black water lay between their feet like a border.

"Because I know what crowns cost," she said. "Do you know why I ran? Everyone assumes it was the executions — Father's council, dragged out and killed in a single night. And yes. That broke something in me that hasn't healed. But that's not why I ran." She looked down at her hands. Courtiers' hands, soft once, now calloused from two years of rough roads. "I ran because when the order was given, I looked at my brother — at the thing wearing my brother — and I *knew*. Before anyone else. Before the gold showed in his eyes, before the edicts, before any of it. I saw him sign that death warrant and I saw that his hand hesitated, just for a heartbeat, and I understood that some part of Aldric was still in there, screaming, watching himself do it. And I did nothing. I packed a bag in the night and I climbed out a window and I told myself there was nothing I could do."

The words came easier than she expected, as though they'd been waiting years behind her teeth.

"Aldric used to tell me, when we were children, that a crown was a debt. Everyone thinks it's a treasure — power, wealth, adoration. He said no, it's a debt you owe to everyone under it, and most kings spend their lives pretending the debt doesn't exist. Our father pretended beautifully. Aldric—" Her voice caught, and she let it. "Aldric actually believed it. That's why the Veil chose him, maybe. Not weakness. Conscience. A parasite of obedience found the one king who took the debt seriously, and it's been feeding on his conscience ever since. Every decree he signs that he hates, it grows stronger. Every atrocity committed in his name that he's forced to watch — that's the harvest, Kestrel. It's not eating the kingdom. It's eating *him*, one broken promise at a time."

She lifted her head. "So when I say I know what crowns cost, I mean I have watched the price paid in installments for two years, from a safe distance, doing nothing. And I am done paying in regret. Regret is the most expensive currency there is, and I am bankrupt with it. If claiming the throne buys him one chance — one hour, one door opened, one moment where the thing in the gold has to look away from him to deal with me — then the crown will finally have cost me something useful instead of something shameful."

The tunnel was very quiet. Even the water seemed to move more softly.

"You understand it'll use you against him," Kestrel said at last. "If it can't kill you cleanly, it'll make him watch. It'll hurt you where he can see."

"Yes."

"And you're going anyway."

"I'm going anyway."

Kestrel held her gaze for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, the smallest motion, and Sera understood it was the highest honor the woman offered — no comfort, no false reassurance, just recognition. One survivor acknowledging another's arithmetic.

"Then we do it properly," Kestrel said, and turned to the others, and the tiredness fell off her voice like a shed skin. "Dara, Pell — I need the undercity maps again, every gate within a quarter mile of the great hall. Vess, I need your cousin at the Saltmarket, the one who owes you the unforgivable favor. Mott—" 

"I know," Mott rumbled. "Crowds. Riots. Exits."

"Crowds, riots, exits." Kestrel looked back at Sera, and for just a moment something flickered behind the slate — not warmth exactly, but its distant cousin. "If we're going to hand the Veil a spectacle, we'll give it one it can't look away from. And while it's staring at you, Princess—"

"Sera," she said. "If I'm to die for this family, call me Sera."

Something almost like a smile touched the storm-caller's mouth. "While it's staring at you, Sera, I'm going to go get your brother back."

* * *

They spent the next day preparing, and Sera discovered that preparation was its own strange mercy — fear given tasks became merely fuel.

Vess's cousin arrived at dusk, a wiry woman named Brannoc with a dyer's cart and a talent for looking forgettable, and laid out the route: the Saltmarket at midmorning, when it was fullest, the plaza before the Gate of Ashen Kings by the eleventh bell. Word would travel ahead of her — the smugglers' network was a web of gossip older than the kingdom itself, and by tomorrow half the city would know that the lost princess had returned to make her claim. Half the city, and therefore, inevitably, the palace.

"They'll come for you before you finish speaking," Brannoc predicted cheerfully, chewing a strip of dried fish. "Guards, probably. Something worse, if the stories are true."

"Let them come," Sera said. "An arrest in front of a thousand witnesses is worth ten speeches."

That night she cleaned and mended the one fine garment she owned — a gown of deep blue, her house colors, hidden at the bottom of her pack for two years like a wound she couldn't stop touching. She brushed the road dust from it stitch by stitch. When it was done she held it up and saw, superimposed on the fabric, her father wearing it at her presentation court, and Aldric beside him, fifteen years old and solemn, whispering to her that the whole ceremony was absurd and wouldn't it be funny if they both just walked out right now.

She had laughed then. She found she could almost laugh now, remembering. Grief, she was learning, was not the opposite of courage. It was the raw material of it.

Near midnight, Kestrel appeared at the mouth of the side passage where Sera sat with the gown across her knees. The storm-caller carried two tin cups and the smell of terrible tea.

"Couldn't sleep either?" Sera asked.

"I sleep fine. I just don't do it much." Kestrel handed her a cup and leaned against the stone. For a while neither spoke. The tea was awful. Sera drank it anyway.

"When I was exiled," Kestrel said abruptly, "I told myself I'd never stand in front of a crowd again. Last time I did, they were throwing stones." She turned the cup in her scarred hand. "Tomorrow you're going to stand in front of thousands, knowing what's coming, and say the words anyway. I keep trying to decide if that's bravery or if there's a word for something worse."

"Is there?"

"No," Kestrel said. "I think brave is the only word, and it's too small. But it's the one we've got." She drained her tea and pushed off the wall. "Get some sleep, Sera. Tomorrow the Veil learns what it costs to underestimate a family that fights."

She vanished down the passage, footsteps silent as falling snow, and Sera sat alone with the blue gown and the awful tea and the memory of her brother's voice — *wouldn't it be funny if we both just walked out* — and she slept, at last, without dreams.

At dawn, she dressed in her house colors, pinned her hair the way her mother had taught her, and walked toward the light.

By the eleventh bell, the plaza before the Gate of Ashen Kings would be full. By the twelfth, everything would change — though none of them yet knew how much, or what the Veil had already set in motion on its side of the game.
