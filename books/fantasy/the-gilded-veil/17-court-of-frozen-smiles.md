## 17. Court of Frozen Smiles

The throne room of Vael Corin had been built to make a person feel small, and it still did its work admirably — even now, even hollowed out, even with its king reduced to a gilded puppet on a chair of black oak and frost.

Kestrel watched from the shadow of a buttress forty feet above the floor, pressed into stone so cold it burned through her gloves. Below, the court assembled itself with the terrible smoothness of water finding its level. Lords and ladies in winter silks, their breath pluming in unison, their faces arranged in smiles that never quite reached the eyes. No one shivered. That was the thing that made her skin crawl — the cold had settled into the hall like a permanent tenant, frost feathering the stained glass, icicles hanging from the ribs of the vaulted ceiling like the teeth of some enormous jaw — and no one shivered. They simply stood in it, warm-faced and smiling, as if the cold were a courtesy they had all agreed to extend.

*Harvest,* Ondrey had called it. She understood the word now. These people weren't subjects. They were fields.

Below them all, on the dais beneath the great window of the First King, sat Aldric.

She had expected a monster. What she saw was worse: a young man, perhaps twenty-four, handsome in the way that portraits are handsome, wearing a crown of gold that seemed to have grown into him rather than been placed upon him. Its points disappeared into his hair. Where metal met skin there was no line at all — just a gradual brightening, flesh becoming filigree, as though someone had begun pouring molten gold over his head very slowly and he had simply let it happen. His hands rested on the arms of the throne, and those too were changing: gold veins tracing up from his fingertips like ivy, delicate and precise, beautiful in the way that things are beautiful when they are killing you.

His eyes, when he lifted them to survey his court, gleamed like coins at the bottom of a well.

"Two years," Sera had said, three nights ago, in the smugglers' safehouse with her voice shaking only once. "He caught a fever after the coronation. Everyone said it was the mountain air. He woke up and he was *tidy*. That's how I'd describe it. Tidy. Like a room someone had cleaned while you slept, and everything was where it should be, and nothing was where you left it."

Kestrel had known tidy men before. None of them had ordered their father's council executed in a single afternoon.

Now she crouched above the court and waited for the signal.

Sera stood at the base of the dais steps, dressed in mourning white, her mother's signet ring on her hand — the ring she had sworn she'd never wear again, because wearing it meant claiming everything that came with it. Her hair was bound up in the formal style of the royal house. From this distance she looked less like the woman who had wept over a burned map than like a portrait stepped down off a wall: Princess Sera Aldraine, returned from exile to claim what was hers.

The herald's voice rang out, flat and clear as a struck bell:

"The Lady Sera of House Aldraine, daughter of King Maren the Second, comes before the Winter Court to press her blood-claim."

A ripple went through the assembly — not gasps, not murmurs, but something more unsettling: every head turning at exactly the same speed, every smile widening by exactly the same degree. Kestrel's stomach turned. It was like watching a field of flowers track the sun.

On the throne, the thing wearing Aldric tilted its head.

"Sister," it said. The voice was Aldric's voice, and it wasn't — there was a resonance underneath it, a harmonic like ice cracking on a lake, something vast speaking through a small instrument. "You have been far away."

"I have." Sera's voice carried without effort; she had been raised for this room, whatever else had been raised out of her. "Far enough to hear what my brother's kingdom has become. Far enough to count the empty chairs at the council table. Far enough to remember that our father ruled *with* men, not instead of them."

"You speak of tidiness," the king said, and smiled, and the smile was the worst thing yet because it was warm, genuinely warm, a young man's crooked grin surfacing through the gold like a drowning swimmer's hand breaking water. "I have made everything tidy, Sera. Would you like to see?"

Kestrel didn't wait to see what happened next. The plan required chaos, and chaos required time, and every second Sera held the court's attention was a second bought with her own body as collateral. Kestrel moved along the buttress, quick and low, toward the narrow servants' stair that wound up behind the great window — the one Sera had described, the one that hadn't existed on any map Ondrey possessed, because it had been bricked over during Maren's reign and reopened by masons who answered to the Veil alone.

The stair smelled of old mortar and newer frost. She climbed in the dark by touch, counting turns the way Sera had taught her — *fourteen to the landing, then left, then the door with the sun carved on it, because Father thought it was funny, hiding the way to his own bedchamber behind a joke.* At the fourteenth turn she stopped, breath fogging, and listened.

Above her, faintly, came music. A single instrument, plucked slow and sweet. A lute, or something like it.

Her skin prickled. There shouldn't be anyone up here. The plan accounted for guards, wards, the Veil's attention fixed on Sera below — but not for music.

She drew her knife anyway and climbed the last turns.

* * *

The corridor at the top was lined with the old kings.

Not statues — paintings, dozens of them, Maren the Second and his fathers back into legend, each face rendered with the loving precision of artists who knew their heads depended on the likeness. Frost had crept across all of them equally, silvering each crown, each beard, each set of painted eyes, until the whole gallery looked like one long funeral procession frozen mid-march.

And at the end of the corridor, sitting on a stool outside the bedchamber doors, an old woman played a lute.

Kestrel froze, knife low.

The woman didn't look up. Her fingers moved over the strings with the absent competence of someone who had played the same song ten thousand times, and her lips moved around the melody without sound. She wore the plain grey of a palace servant, and frost had grown across her shoulders like epaulettes, and she did not shiver either.

"Wrong door," the woman said mildly, between phrases of the melody. "Everyone always wants the wrong door."

Kestrel kept her voice to a whisper. "Which is the right one?"

"For you?" The old woman's hands stilled. She looked up then, and her eyes were ordinary — brown, clouded, human — which was somehow more frightening than gold would have been. "The right one's behind you. Always has been. But nobody ever goes back." She resumed playing. "Go on, then. He's waiting. He's been waiting since the courier died on the mountain. Did you know that? He felt it. Felt the book change hands. It's the only thing he feels anymore that isn't cold."

Kestrel's mouth went dry. "What are you?"

"Choir," the woman said, as if that explained anything. "We sing the long winter in. We've been singing it for a hundred years, dear. You're the first audience the song's ever had." Her fingers stumbled, found the phrase again. "Go. The princess below can't hold them much longer. He's getting hungry, and she smells like a claim."

Kestrel went. There was no time to wonder about choirs, or songs a hundred years long, or why an old woman with human eyes was playing lullabies outside a monster's bedroom. She crossed the corridor, put her shoulder to the carved sun, and slipped inside.

* * *

The royal bedchamber of Vael Corin was a tomb that someone had tried to keep looking lived-in.

A fire burned in the hearth and gave no heat; the flames themselves were rimmed with frost, burning cold, casting light without warmth. The great bed was made with military precision. Every surface was dusted. And everywhere — everywhere — the gold.

It climbed the bedposts like vines. It traced the ceiling beams in branching patterns, filigree spreading across plaster the way frost spreads across glass, except frost melts and this did not. It webbed the windows. It had consumed an entire writing desk, leaving only the shape of the desk visible beneath a shell of shining metal, and on the desk's golden surface lay a half-finished letter, the ink frozen mid-word, the quill standing upright in a small puddle of gilded ice.

And in the center of the room, seated on the floor with his back against the cold fire's hearthstone, was the king.

Up close, the transformation was worse. So much worse. The filigree wasn't decoration laid over him — it was *him*, growing out of him, his own body translated into ornament. Gold ran up his throat in delicate branching veins. One ear had gone entirely to bright metal, shaped like a leaf. His left hand was a sculpture of itself, fingers perfect and immobile, resting in his lap like something belonging to a saint's reliquary. Only his face remained mostly flesh, and his eyes —

His eyes were closed.

But they snapped open when the door clicked shut, and they were not gold.

They were grey. Human grey, tired past exhaustion, and they found Kestrel in the doorway with an expression she had seen only once before in her life — on her own face, in a river's reflection, the night after the storm that killed the wrong man. The look of someone drowning who has stopped being surprised by the water.

"You're real," Aldric said. His voice was a ruin — hoarse, cracked, the voice of a man who hadn't used it for himself in a very long time. "You're — I can see you. Not through glass. Not through *him*." He tried to rise and couldn't; the gold had rooted him to the hearthstone, tendrils running from his spine into the stone itself. He laughed, and it broke into coughing. "Forgive me. I'd stand. I'd — gods, I'd bow. You're the storm-caller."

"How do you know that?"

"He knows it. Which means I know it. Everything he knows, I—" Aldric pressed his lips together, steadying himself. "I'm in here. All of me. Every hour, every decree, every execution — I watch. I feel my hands do it. Do you understand what I'm telling you? I signed my father's councilors' death warrants and I *felt the pen move*, and I screamed, and no sound came out, because he doesn't waste anything, not even screaming." His grey eyes were wet. "So yes. I know your name, Kestrel Eriksen. I know your scarred hand. I know the book reached you because he felt it reach you, and he laughed, and I felt that too."

Kestrel's knife hand trembled. She sheathed the blade — it was suddenly obscene — and crossed the room slowly, boots silent on frost-slicked stone, and knelt in front of him.

Up close she could see the rhythm of it: the gold pulsing faintly, breathing, in time with something slower than a heartbeat. Feeding.

"Why haven't you told anyone?" she asked. "Sera. Your sister. She thinks you're gone. She thinks she's avenging you, not saving you."

"She came to me. Eight months ago. Before she fled." Something moved in his face — grief with nowhere to go, dammed up behind two years of silence. "I couldn't warn her. When I try, he — tightens. You can't imagine. It's not pain. Pain would be a mercy. It's *cold*, it's being buried in cold from the inside, and he holds you there until you agree to stop trying. I lasted three days the first time." He swallowed. "I lasted six hours the second. I stopped trying. That's the harvest, storm-caller. That's what he grows. Not obedience in the courts — obedience in the *king*. Every will he breaks makes the winter longer. Every surrender feeds the binding. And I have surrendered so many times that I don't—" His voice failed. He looked at his gilded hand. "I don't know how much of me is left to give."

The cold fire crackled silently beside them. Somewhere far below, muffled by floors of stone, Kestrel could just hear the throne room — a swell of voices, Sera's rising above them, holding, holding.

"There's a way," Kestrel said. "Ondrey found it. The rite needs a crown freely surrendered. Not taken. Given. Witnessed."

Aldric stared at her. And then he laughed — really laughed, helpless and cracked, tears freezing on his cheeks before they fell. "Freely," he whispered. "Oh, that's the joke, isn't it. That's the whole joke. He's spent two years making sure I have nothing left to give freely. That's what he *is*. He takes until giving is impossible, and then the rite fails, and the winter goes on forever." He looked up at her, and his eyes were fierce now, blazing under all that ruin. "Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless the surrender doesn't come from what's left of me." He strained against the gold, and the veins in his throat pulsed brighter, and he grimaced through it. "There's a shrine. Behind the eastern panel. My father's — the old gods, the ones before the Veil's church, the ones the mountain people kept. He can't watch it. He *hates* it. It's the one place in this room I can still think clearly." His breath came ragged. "Help me to it. Please. Help me to it and I'll show you what I've been keeping alive in here for two years. One coal. He hasn't found it. He doesn't know it exists. I hid it the way you hide a spark under ash — and a spark is all a free choice needs."

Kestrel got her arms under his and pulled.

The gold fought her. The moment her hands touched him, the filigree *moved* — fine metallic tendrils lifting off the walls, reaching for her wrists like curious snakes, and where they brushed her skin they left lines of cold that sank straight to the bone. She gritted her teeth and hauled. Aldric weighed what a drowning man weighs, anchored, fighting his own anchor, and together they scraped and dragged across the floor, his gilded hand carving a bright furrow in the frost, until they reached the eastern wall and he collapsed against it, gasping.

"The seam," he managed. "Left of the third panel. Push where the sun's worn away."

She found it — a depression in the carved wood, rubbed smooth by a single finger used over and over, hundreds of times, in secret, in the dark. She pushed.

The panel swung inward on oiled hinges.

Behind it: a shrine no bigger than a bread oven. Three rough carvings on a shelf of mountain stone — a woman with lightning for hair, a man with a hammer, a small figure whose face had been worn blank by touch. Before them, in a dish of black iron, sat a single ember, red as a wound, alive against all reason in a room where fire itself had frozen. Someone had fed it. Someone had coaxed it along for two years, a pinch of charcoal at a time, in stolen seconds, in the spaces between decrees and executions, keeping one small heat alive inside the coldest room in the world.

Kestrel knelt before it and felt her eyes sting. She knew what it was to keep a coal hidden. She had done it herself, in exile, for seven years — the stubborn banked thing at the center of herself that refused, that *refused*, no matter what the snow said.

"It's yours too, if you want it," Aldric said quietly behind her. "That's what my father believed. The old gods don't belong to kings. They belong to whoever's cold."

She set the iron dish between them, on the floor, in the open space before the little carvings. Then she took Aldric's remaining flesh-and-blood hand in her scarred one.

"Say it," she said. "Whatever the rite needs. Say it like you mean it, because I'll know if you don't."

He bowed his head over their joined hands, over the ember, before the worn faces of the old gods, and his voice came out steady — the first steady thing about him.

"I am Aldric, son of Maren, and I was crowned in this house. Whatever sits upon my head, I renounce. Whatever rules through my hands, I refuse. I surrender the crown of Vael Corin — freely, wholly, unwitnessed by no one—" his grey eyes flicked up to hers, and the ghost of the crooked grin surfaced one last time, "—witnessed by the storm-caller and the old gods and one stubborn coal. Take it from him. Give it to no one. Let the winter end."

The ember flared.

For a heartbeat the whole shrine blazed with light that had warmth in it, real warmth, and Kestrel felt the words land somewhere deeper than her ears — felt them the way she felt weather coming, pressure behind the eyes, a taste of copper on the tongue. The surrender *took*. She couldn't have explained how she knew, but she knew, the way the book had known her name. Something had changed hands in the world's ledger. Something had been given that could never be taken back.

And then the temperature of the room dropped so fast that the frost on the walls shrieked.

The gold filigree went dark — not dead, *alert* — every tendril in the chamber flexing at once, and from everywhere and nowhere came a sound like a thousand coins being counted by a patient hand.

Aldric sagged against her, drained, smiling, free for the length of a held breath. "Run," he whispered. "It knows. Storm-caller — it knows, and it's going to offer you *everything*—"

The cold fire in the hearth went out.

Every candle in the room guttered. And in the sudden dark, gold light began to gather — not in the filigree, not in the walls, but in the air itself, drawing inward, taking shape, growing tall.

Kestrel's hand found her knife out of pure instinct, and she almost laughed at the absurdity of it, kneeling in a hidden shrine with a dying king's hand in hers and an ember for an army, as the thing she had come to kill finished putting itself on.

Below her, faint through the bones of the palace, Sera's voice stopped mid-sentence.

Then the doors of the bedchamber opened without being touched, and the Gilded Veil came home.
