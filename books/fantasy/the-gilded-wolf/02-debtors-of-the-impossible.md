## 2. Debtors of the Impossible

The snow had stopped pretending to be weather and become a verdict.

Juniper pulled her coat tighter and counted the collectors on the far side of the frozen canal: three of them, gray-cloaked, their breath hanging in the air like signatures awaiting ink. The Guild's Collectors of Arrears did not run. They never needed to. Debt was patient, and they were its patient hands.

She had been running for two days anyway.

The prophecy ledger rode against her ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, warm as a stolen loaf. Every few streets she pressed her palm to it just to feel it there — that impossible weight, heavier than paper had any right to be. *Juniper Thackeray,* it had whispered in a dead woman's voice. *The winter door.* And beneath the date — tonight's date — a line she could not stop reading: *she who unlocks must also be unlocked from.*

"Thackeray."

The voice came from behind the fishmonger's shuttered stall, low and dry as kindling. Juniper spun, hand going for the little paring knife she'd taken to carrying. A woman stepped out of the shadow between crates — tall, angular, hair the color of storm-water, eyes like the moment before lightning. Frost bloomed and died across the cobbles where she walked, an uncontrolled shiver of weather clinging to her like a bad reputation.

"You're Sable Wren," Juniper said. "They struck you off the rolls."

"They struck me off because I made it rain on the Guild's roof for eleven straight days until the accounts ran." Sable's mouth curved, not quite a smile. "And you're the Ledger-keeper who's gone missing with a sealed book. The collectors are offering a bounty. I came to collect."

Juniper didn't move. "Then collect."

Sable studied her, and the air between them tightened, pressure before a storm. Then, slowly, she laughed — a rough, surprised sound, like ice cracking on a river in spring. "No. No, I don't think I will. Because you've got the look of someone the Guild has decided to own, and I spent nine years learning what that feels like." She jerked her chin toward Juniper's coat. "Show me the book."

"I don't—"

"You've been touching your ribs every forty steps like it might bolt. Show me."

* * *

*They always show me eventually,* said the ghost who drifted at Juniper's shoulder, unseen by either woman. Odile had followed the ledger since the guildhouse, drawn along its binding like a fish on a line. *I showed them my hands first. Bone-carvers always do. Look, look what I can make of nothing. That was how it began — showing, and then owing.*

Sable turned the wrapped book over twice, frowning at the weight of it. "This isn't paper debt."

"No," Juniper said. "It's older. There's a name in it. Mine. And a date — tonight."

"And whose handwriting?"

"That's the trouble. I don't know. But it whispers."

Something moved through Sable then — not fear exactly, but recognition, the flinch of a woman hearing an old song from a country she'd fled. "There are only three people in this city the Guild still holds paper on that matter like that," she said quietly. "Me. You now. And Bram."

"Bram the mute? The giant who carries the vault doors?"

"He carried them once. Then he set one down in the wrong place, and the Guild decided that was a debt too. They charge him interest in silence." Sable's jaw tightened. "He sleeps in the tannery yard. He'll come if you ask him right."

"How do you ask a man who can't speak?"

"You don't ask. You offer."

* * *

The tannery yard smelled of lime and old hides, and Bram sat against the wall like something the mountain had misplaced — seven feet of quiet, mending a net with fingers delicate as a surgeon's. He saw them coming and did not rise. He had learned, apparently, that rising for strangers was a habit with costs.

Juniper unwrapped the ledger and held it out to him, open to the page with her name.

Bram read it. She watched his enormous hands go still, the net forgotten. His thumb traced the date once, twice, and then he looked up at her with an expression she would spend years trying to name: grief and relief arriving together, twins.

He tapped his chest. Then he pointed at the word *winter*, and then — slowly, deliberately — he drew a door in the frost on the wall beside him, and closed his fist over it.

"He says he's seen it," Sable translated softly. "In dreams, since he was a boy. A door under the mountain, and cold coming through the seams. The Guild used to ask him about those dreams when he was small. They wrote everything down." Her voice went thin. "Everything anyone ever told them became a debt somehow. Even a child's nightmares."

Bram stood. It was like watching a cliff decide to walk. He picked up his net, folded it with terrible gentleness, and set it down — the last thing, perhaps, he intended to leave behind.

*Oh, you foolish, brave children,* Odile breathed, circling them all. *You have no idea what door that is. I do. I named it. I sold the key.*

* * *

They gathered in the ruined counting-house by the river, where the Guild's reach thinned and the rats had long ago eaten through the important documents. Sable lit no fire; instead she coaxed the wind through the broken windows until it hummed a low, steady note, and the room felt almost guarded.

Juniper laid the ledger open on the table. "It whispers. It's whispered since the first night. Tonight it finally gave me a name."

And as though summoned, the cold in the room gathered itself, and the ghost came clear — a woman of smoke and bone-white light, standing over the page of her own handwriting, her face a ruin of sorrow and fury.

"*Odile,*" Sable whispered. "Odile Vane. They teach your carvings in the apprentice classes. They say you died mad."

"I died *indebted*," the ghost said, and her voice filled the counting-house like water fills a hull. "A hundred years ago I carved a prophecy for a court beneath the mountain, and the price of the carving was my soul. I thought I was writing a warning. I was writing a *contract*. Every word a clause. And now the court has found its counterparty — a ledger-keeper with my dates inked under her name." She turned her burning gaze on Juniper. "You were chosen the moment you learned to read, girl. The Guild has been raising a payment without knowing it."

"The winter door," Juniper said. "What is it?"

"A door under the mountain that holds back an endless winter. And here is the arithmetic the court loves so well:" Odile's smoke-hands clenched. "The door does not lock from outside. To shut it, a soul must stand within and hold it — forever. They took mine to make the prophecy, and they mean to spend yours to close it. My soul hangs in their masque-hall as a chandelier. Yours is the wick they intend to trim."

Silence, except for Sable's wind, which had stopped humming and started growling.

"So we don't close it," Juniper said.

"We don't *pay* it," Sable corrected, sparks crawling along her knuckles. "There's a difference. Debts aren't weather, Thackeray. They're not natural law. Someone writes them down. Which means—" her eyes went to the ledger, glowing faintly on the table like a heart in a box, "—someone can unwrite them."

Bram made a sound then, the first Juniper had ever heard from him — not a word, but a low resonant note in his chest, agreement or oath, hard to say. He placed one vast hand flat upon the ledger, careful as a man laying flowers on a grave.

Odile looked around at the four of them — witch, giant, ghost, and keeper — and something in her smoke-face softened into wonder. "A hundred years I've haunted my own handwriting," she said quietly, "waiting to see if anyone would read it as a warning instead of a sentence. Four debtors. That's all it took."

"Then it's settled," Juniper said, and heard in her own voice none of the clerk left, only the thing underneath. "Tonight the court expects its payment to come walking down the mountain path with a ledger in her arms." She wrapped the book again, tucked it away, and met each of their eyes in turn. "So let's not disappoint them. Let's deliver something else entirely."

Outside, the snow had begun again — falling upward now, toward the mountain.

Sable watched it climb and went pale. "That's not weather," she said. "That's an invitation. The court knows we're coming."

*It has known since the day she was born,* Odile thought, and said nothing, and followed them down into the dark.
