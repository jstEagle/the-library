## 3. The Court Beneath the Mountain

I had forgotten what it was to be cold.

That is the first cruelty of dying, the one no one warns you of: you lose the weather. For a hundred years I had drifted through the guildhall like a draft under a door — felt nothing, weighed nothing, remembered warmth only as a scholar remembers a language she once spoke. But as we descended the stair that spiraled beneath the mountain, cold found me again, or I found it, and I nearly wept at the ache of it in my phantom knuckles.

The stair was carved from bone. Not stone shaped like bone — actual bone, femurs of something vast laid end to end and fused with frost, each step groaning faintly under Bram's weight like a ship's timbers. Sable walked with her hood up and her hands jammed in her sleeves, muttering weather to herself the way other people pray. Juniper came behind me with the Gilded Ledger strapped across her chest like a shield, and every few steps her fingers rose to touch its cover, checking, confirming, the way a mother checks a sleeping child.

*You should not have come down here,* I wanted to tell her. *You especially.*

But I had spent my life selling truths I did not want to keep, and so I said nothing, and the mountain swallowed us one turn at a time.

***

The door at the bottom was not a door.

It was winter itself, standing upright — a wall of wind and white so dense it seemed solid, humming with a note too low for living ears. I heard it fine. It sang my name.

"Odile," it said, in the voice I'd had before the carving, before the bargain. "Odile, Odile, you left your soul on the table."

"I know," I whispered.

Juniper stepped up beside me. She couldn't hear it — I watched her tilt her head, catching only the hum — but she could read me; she'd spent three years translating my whispers from the ledger's margins, and ghosts are not so different from margins. "What does it say?"

"That I'm expected."

"Then we're all expected." She squared her shoulders. "Good. I hate arriving unannounced."

Sable made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't frozen somewhere in her years ago. Bram simply reached out and pushed his palm flat against the wall of winter, and where his skin touched it, the wind parted like a curtain — not because he was strong, though he was, but because even the cold seemed reluctant to be rude to him.

We stepped through into the court.

***

I had imagined the court beneath the mountain a hundred times in the century since I sold myself to it. In my imaginings it was always terrible: rivers of black ice, thrones of teeth, the kind of place a bone-carver deserves to end.

It was worse than terrible. It was beautiful.

The cavern opened above us like the inside of a geode, veined with blue crystal that burned with its own slow light. Snow fell upward. A masque was already underway — hundreds of figures gliding across a floor of polished mirror-ice, masked in porcelain faces painted with seasons. Winter masks, mostly, white and silver and austere. But here and there a spring mask, a summer mask, worn by courtiers who moved stiffly, who did not sweat, whose feet left no marks. I understood what they were before Juniper did. They were paid-for souls, wearing their old selves like costumes.

And above them all, hanging from the vaulted dark on chains of woven hair—

Me.

My soul hung caged in a lantern of ribs, glowing the soft gold of candlelight through honey. It turned slowly, shedding light over the dancers, and I felt the pull of it in my chest like a hook behind my breastbone. A hundred years of being nothing, and there I was, everything I had ever been, suspended as furniture.

"They use it as a chandelier," Juniper breathed. Her voice cracked on the last syllable.

"They use everything here as something else," I said. "That is the whole of their economy."

A herald approached — tall, robed in ermine the color of drowned things, wearing a mask of bare branches. He did not walk so much as arrive, relocating himself across the ice without the indignity of steps.

"The ledger-bearer," he announced, in a voice like snow sliding off a roof. "The prophecy walks. The court rejoices."

Around the ballroom, two hundred porcelain faces turned toward us in perfect unison. Sable's hand found Bram's sleeve. Bram's fists found themselves.

"We're here for the soul," Juniper said, lifting her chin. "Not for dancing."

"Everyone dances here," the herald said gently. "Some merely call it by other names."

***

They took our coats. That was how it began — a small courtesy, attendants of frost collecting our cloaks, and when they lifted the Ledger's strap Juniper nearly took the attendant's fingers off. "This stays on my body."

"As you wish," the attendant murmured. "Everything here stays on your body. That is rather the point."

So we danced. There was no refusing it; the music started whether one consented or not, a waltz played on instruments of ice that sounded like grief learning arithmetic. The courtiers closed around us, polite and ravenous, asking questions between turns. What was the price of bread, this year, above? Was the sun still yellow? Had anyone missed them?

Juniper danced with a girl in a summer mask who asked, twice, whether her mother still kept bees. Juniper lied beautifully and I loved her for it.

I could not dance — I passed through partners like a cold spot in a warm room — so I drifted instead toward the thing I could not stop looking at. My cage. Up close, the light inside it moved like breath. And carved into the lantern's base, in my own handwriting, in my own blood-ink, was the clause I had written a hundred years ago and never understood until now:

*The door shall shut when a soul remains to hold it.*

Below the cage, set into the floor like a drain, was the door itself: a circle of absolute black, rimmed in frost, breathing out a cold that made the masque's chill seem like a summer breeze. The endless winter they meant to give the world was coiled behind it, patient as debt.

"You see it now."

The voice came from beside me, and I turned to find a figure unmasked among the masked — an old woman carved entirely of pale wood-grain and patience, her eyes the gray of the far side of sleep. The Bone-Court's regent. Or perhaps only its oldest clerk.

"The prophecy was never a prediction," she said. "It was an invoice. You wrote the terms yourself, carver. A door that shuts must have a hand upon it. A hand must have a soul behind it. One stays. Forever is the fee."

"And if no one pays?"

"Then the door stands open." She smiled with grain and shadow. "And winter walks out on its own legs."

***

I found Juniper at the edge of the dance floor, pale, the Ledger clutched against her sternum.

"It's a trap," I told her, and my whisper came out ragged, which I hadn't known a whisper could be. "It was always a trap. The door doesn't close on its own. Someone has to stay — a soul, holding it shut from the inside, forever. That's the price of the prophecy. That's what I signed."

Juniper looked at the door. Then at my caged soul, turning slowly overhead, gilding the dancers below. Then at Sable and Bram, standing together at the ballroom's edge like a lighthouse and its rock. I watched her do the arithmetic — I could see it moving behind her eyes, that ledger-keeper's mind, balancing columns of lives.

"No," she said quietly.

"Juniper—"

"No." Louder now, and the nearest dancers paused mid-turn, porcelain faces tilting. "I know what you're going to say. I know what you already decided, a hundred years ago, and tonight, probably in the last thirty seconds. I've read your handwriting, Odile. I know how you sign away things."

She untied the strap. She held the Gilded Ledger against the light of my caged soul, and the gold of it and the gold of it answered one another like two bells.

"But this time," she said, "nobody signs anything."

Above us, the chandelier of my soul flared — and every mask in the court, porcelain and branch alike, turned toward the sound of a debt about to come due.
