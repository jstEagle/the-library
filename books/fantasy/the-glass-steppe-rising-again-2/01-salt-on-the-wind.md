## 1. Salt on the Wind

The wind came off the high pass carrying salt that had never seen the sea—mineral salt, mountain salt, the ghost of old storms ground fine against granite—and Juniper Crane stood in it with their eyes closed, reading the morning the way other people read letters.

Cold front moving down from the Teeth. Two days of clear weather at most, then sleet in the saddles between peaks. Somewhere downslope, a woodsmoke smell that didn't belong to any of the three farms whose chimneys Juniper had memorized years ago. And underneath all of it, faint as a whisper through a wall, the particular silence of a road with no one on it.

Good, Juniper thought. Empty roads were honest roads.

They shifted the pack higher on their shoulders and started down the switchback trail toward Hollowmere, where a tanner named Osk would be waiting with seven bolts of dyed leather he'd rather not show to the king's assessors, and where Juniper would be waiting with a smile they'd practiced until it looked like it cost them nothing.

It was good work, smuggling. That was the thing nobody understood who hadn't done it. People thought it was about greed, or danger, or some romance of the outlaw life. It wasn't. It was about being *needed* by exactly one person at a time, for exactly as long as the job lasted, and then walking away into the wind again before anyone could ask what your favorite meal was or whether you slept badly in springtime like your mother had. Juniper's mother had died when they were nine, so the comparison was safe enough now, mostly.

Below them, Thornfell opened up in folds of pine and grey stone, the smuggler's roads threading through it like veins through a wrist. Official maps showed nothing here but blank space and a warning about rockslides. Official maps were written by people who believed the mountains belonged to somebody. Juniper knew better. The mountains belonged to whoever knew them best, and nobody knew them better than a person who had spent eleven years learning every goat track, collapsed mine shaft, and conveniently loose fence post between Hollowmere and the far side of the pass.

Eleven years. Long enough that the number no longer stung when they counted it. That was new, actually, and Juniper decided not to think about why.

* * *

Osk was waiting behind his tannery with the nervous energy of a man who sweated for a living and resented doing it for free.

"You're late," he said.

"I'm not," said Juniper. "You're early. There's a difference, and the difference is that one of us knows the roads."

Osk grunted, which from him was practically a sonnet. He was a big man gone soft around the middle, with forearms like hams and the permanent expression of someone smelling something faintly disappointing. He jerked his chin toward the shed, and Juniper followed him in, past hanging hides that turned the light the color of weak tea.

"Seven bolts," he said, kicking a crate open. "Crimson. Good crimson, not that madder-root wash the southern mills pass off. The garrison quartermaster wants it, and the garrison quartermaster's wife wants it more, and neither of them wants to explain to the tax man why a border fort needs forty yards of ball-gown red."

"And I don't want to know why you can't just sell it to them openly."

"Because openly costs double." Osk grinned suddenly, and for a moment he looked like the boy he must have been, before tanning chemicals ate his eyebrows. "Same goods. Same road. Funny how paper makes things heavier."

Juniper crouched and ran a thumb along the leather. Supple. Deep color. They let the silence stretch just long enough to make Osk shift his weight.

"Twelve crowns," Osk said.

"Fifteen."

"It was twelve last month."

"Last month there wasn't an Order patrol camped at Greyford Bridge asking travelers for their papers." Juniper watched him carefully as they said it—not watching for guilt, but for confirmation. Osk's face did something complicated. "You knew that already," Juniper said. "That's why you're early."

"They came through three days ago. Ashen Order, grey cloaks, the whole somber procession." Osk wiped his hands on his apron, though they weren't dirty. "Asking after a courier. Some man they say went missing on the high pass. Description so vague it could've been me, or you, or the mayor's grandmother."

"A courier carrying what?"

"That's the thing." Osk leaned in, lowering his voice as though the hanging hides might repeat gossip. "They wouldn't say. Which means it's worth saying. You know how it goes—if it were taxes or letters, they'd have told everyone. Silence means treasure."

Silence means trouble, Juniper thought, but aloud they only said, "Fourteen, and I'll take the north route around the bridge."

"Done, you thief." Osk shook their hand, sealing it with a grip like a bear trap wrapped in flannel. Then, as Juniper loaded the bolts into their pack, he added, too casually: "You ever hear from her? Your aunt?"

The word landed in Juniper's chest like a stone dropped down a well. They kept their face still. Years of practice.

"No," they said. "And she's not my aunt."

"Hedge-witch up past Waymeet. Maren Crane. Everyone says—"

"Everyone says a lot of things." Juniper cinched the pack straps tight, tighter than the leather needed. "She taught me letters and herbs and which mushrooms lie about being edible. Then she tried to sell me to people in grey cloaks, and I left. That's the whole story. It's shorter than most stories and it doesn't improve with telling."

Osk held up both hands. "Just asking. She came through town last autumn, is all. Bought salt. Looked old."

"They do that." Juniper slung the pack over their shoulder. "Salt, was it?"

"Half a sack."

Something cold slid down Juniper's spine and settled in their stomach, familiar as an old scar aching before rain. Maren bought salt twice a year, always at midwinter and midsummer, for wards and preserving both. Autumn was wrong. Off-season purchases meant a working in progress, and Maren never started a working without a reason, and every reason she'd ever had had eventually involved either money or the Order.

Mostly both.

"Well," Juniper said brightly, "enjoy your crimson. If anyone asks, I was never here."

"Nobody ever asks," Osk said. "That's the arrangement."

* * *

The north route added half a day and took Juniper along a shelf of trail above the Greyford valley, narrow enough in places that they walked with one hand trailing the rock face like a lover too shy to commit. Below, the river ran loud and white with snowmelt. Above, ravens argued about something in the crags.

This was the part of the work Juniper loved, if they were honest—which they generally weren't, even to themselves, because honesty invited follow-up questions. The part where the world narrowed to boot placement and breath rhythm and the wind's opinion. No names, no debts, no history. A body moving through empty country is the closest thing to freedom the world sells, and it sells it cheap, if you're willing to pay in loneliness.

Loneliness, Juniper had long ago concluded, was a fair price. Loneliness never lied to you. Loneliness never smiled at you across a kitchen table while writing your name in a ledger belonging to men in grey cloaks. Loneliness asked nothing except everything, constantly, forever, and Juniper respected that kind of straightforward dealing.

So why, they wondered, hopping a gap in the trail, did the quiet feel different this year?

They'd noticed it first at midwinter, alone in a waystation with a fire and a stolen chicken and absolutely nothing to complain about, when they'd caught themselves talking aloud just to hear a voice. Not talking *to* anyone. Just... narrating. *Chicken's done. Wind's picking up. Should mend that sock.* And then catching themselves doing it and feeling, briefly, like a door had blown open onto a room in their chest they hadn't known was furnished.

Since then the feeling had been growing, patient as lichen. The comfortable silence kept developing seams. On the trail, when a hawk cried, Juniper caught themselves wanting to point it out to someone. Pointing out hawks. Eleven years of flawless solitude, undermined by a bird.

*Getting soft,* they told themselves, and mended the sock that night anyway, because a voice in their head pointed out that the heel was going, and arguing with yourself was nearly as good as company. Nearly.

* * *

They made camp that night in a hollow beneath an overhang, out of the wind, with a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand. The leather bolts made a decent bedroll frame, if you didn't mind smelling like victory and commerce. Juniper ate hard bread and dried apples and watched the stars come out over the Teeth, sharp and cold and utterly indifferent, which was one of their better qualities as far as Juniper was concerned.

Somewhere south, Maren was buying salt out of season.

Somewhere east, grey-cloaked soldiers were asking vague questions about a courier who'd gone missing on the high pass.

Juniper lay back and performed the ancient smuggler's ritual of deciding these facts had nothing to do with each other and less to do with them. The mountains were full of couriers. Couriers went missing the way rain went upward—all the time, if you watched closely enough. Whatever the Order hunted, it would stay east, and Juniper would stay west, and the two facts would pass each other in the night without so much as a nod.

They were very good at this ritual. They'd had practice.

Sleep came slow and thin. In the grey hour before dawn, Juniper dreamed—not of anything, precisely, but of the kitchen in Maren's cottage, the one with the crooked chimney and the kettle that sang flat. In the dream the kettle was singing and someone was laughing in the next room and Juniper stood in the doorway knowing, with dream-certainty, that if they stepped through it they could not ever step back out.

They woke with their heart going hard and the fire dead and frost furring the edges of their cloak.

"Ridiculous," they told the hollow, and packed up in the dark, and got moving, because movement was the cure for everything except blisters, and blisters at least kept you honest.

* * *

The third day brought them up toward the high pass itself, the saddle between Thornfell's two greatest shoulders, where the wind lived permanently and the snow never quite finished melting. The plan was simple: cross before noon, drop down into the western valleys, deliver the leather to a milliner in Lowmarsh who paid promptly and talked never, and be home—home being a word Juniper used the way misers used coins, sparingly and only among trusted parties—by week's end.

The crossing went perfectly for two hours.

Then the wind changed.

Juniper stopped mid-stride, one boot on a slab of wet granite, and lifted their face into it. The salt-smell was there again, the mineral bite of the high stone—but under it, something else. Woodsmoke, yes. Blood, faintly, the way blood smells when it's been in the air a while and given up on being noticed.

And something else besides. Something Juniper had smelled only once before, eleven years ago, in a cottage with a crooked chimney, the night they'd run: a sweetness like burnt sugar and lightning, the smell of magic spending itself recklessly, a purse emptied all at once instead of a coin at a time.

Juniper stood very still. The ravens had gone quiet. Ahead, the trail curved around a shoulder of rock toward the pass's high notch, and beyond the notch the road descended in long grey sweeps toward country Juniper couldn't see from here.

Every instinct they owned—the instincts that had kept them alive through eleven years of borders and bribes—said the same thing, clearly and calmly: *Turn around. Take the south route. Whatever is around that bend has already cost someone everything, and the toll booth is still open.*

Juniper looked at the curve in the trail for a long moment.

"Oh, come *on*," they said aloud, to the wind, to the ravens, to whatever furnished that room in their chest and apparently kept leaving doors open. "I was having such a nice walk."

Their feet, traitors to the entire philosophy of a lifetime, carried them forward.

Around the shoulder of rock, at the edge of the high notch where the world fell away on both sides, something dark lay sprawled across the trail—and against the white patches of old snow, Juniper could see that it was bleeding.

It was, they realized, walking slowly closer with their heart climbing into their throat, a man. A courier's riding cloak, travel-worn. One hand pressed to his side. And clutched in the other, gripped so hard his knuckles had gone the same grey as the stone beneath him, a rolled length of parchment stained dark with something that was definitely not ink.

His eyes found Juniper's. He smiled, which was the worst part, and his lips moved around words the wind tore apart before they arrived.

Juniper knelt in the salt-smelling wind beside a dying stranger on an empty road, and understood, with the clarity that only comes too late, that the comfortable loneliness was over.

"The Steppe remembers," the courier whispered, and pressed the map into Juniper's hands.
