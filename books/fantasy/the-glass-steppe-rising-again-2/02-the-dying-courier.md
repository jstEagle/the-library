## 2. The Dying Courier

The trouble with the high pass in late autumn, Juniper Crane had learned, was that it gave you exactly enough warning to be wrong about everything.

The wind had been coming out of the west all morning—clean, cold, smelling of pine and stone and nothing else. That was a good wind. A nothing wind. Juniper had crossed the saddle above Ravenmere with their hood up and their thoughts comfortably empty, leading two mules whose opinions of the weather were considerably louder than their own, and they had thought: *quiet day.*

That was the first mistake. The pass always punished the word *quiet*.

Juniper heard the ravens first. Not the lazy, conversational croaking of birds arguing over a carcass—that was ordinary mountain noise, as common as scree. This was different. This was the sharp, agitated kind, the kind that meant something was dying that hadn't been dead long enough to suit them. Ravens had excellent taste in timing and terrible manners about sharing it.

Juniper stopped the mules, listened, and felt the small cold hand of instinct close around the back of their neck.

Three years on the smuggler's roads had taught them a simple catechism. *If the birds are wrong, turn back. If the snow is wrong, dig in. If a stranger is wrong—* well. That one had variations.

But turning back meant losing the light, and losing the light on the Thornfell saddle meant a night among the rockslides with mules who held grudges, and Juniper had a delivery to make in Coldharbor by week's end if they wanted to keep eating through winter. So instead of doing the sensible thing, they did what they usually did: they tied off the mules, told them firmly not to do anything stupid, and climbed toward the ravens with their heart tapping out a nervous little rhythm against their ribs.

They found him in the lee of a fallen pillar of granite, half in shadow, half in the flat white glare of noon.

He had been a big man once. Even curled the way he was—even with the blood—he looked like he'd been built for carrying things. Saddlebags, timber, other people's burdens. Now he was carrying nothing at all except his own breath, and losing ground in the argument.

There was a wound under his left arm, low, where a blade had gone in and someone with more sense than kindness had twisted it on the way out. The snow around him was no longer white. It had gone the color of old rose petals, then the color of rust, and it was still spreading, patient as anything in this world.

"Hello," Juniper said, because the alternative was running, and some stubborn animal part of them refused to run before knowing what it was running from.

His eyes opened. They were grey eyes, the color of weather, and they found Juniper's face with an effort that seemed to cost him something physical, like hauling a rope hand over hand.

"You're not them," he said. His voice was gravel rolled in wool.

"That depends," Juniper said, crouching at the edge of arm's reach—the distance you kept from strangers on the pass, the distance that let you stand up and be three steps gone before anything bad happened. "Who's *them?*"

"Grey." He tried to laugh and it turned into a wet cough. "Riders in grey. You're not wearing grey."

"I don't wear grey. Grey's for people who want to be seen being grim." Juniper was already moving without deciding to move, kneeling in the bloody snow, pulling off a glove, pressing fingers to the side of his throat. His pulse was there, but it had the quality of a stream in a dry month—present, technically, and going nowhere. "You've been cut deep, friend. This is past what I can fix."

"I know." He said it simply, the way you'd say *it's Tuesday.* "I know what I am. Listen."

"I'm listening."

"No." His hand came up—it took a long time—and caught Juniper's wrist with a grip that had surprise left in it. His skin was freezing. "Listen. They can't get it. Do you understand me? Whatever happens. However it ends. They can't get it."

"They?" said Juniper, who already knew, and hated knowing. "Get what?"

The man smiled. It was a terrible smile, blood-flecked and crooked, but underneath it there was something almost apologetic, like a man handing you a bill he knew you couldn't pay.

"You have an honest face," he said.

"People keep telling me that. People keep being wrong."

"It doesn't matter." He was fumbling at his chest now, at the lacings of a leather doublet gone dark and stiff. "It doesn't matter if you're honest or a thief or a murderer. It only matters that you're here, and they're not, and I have maybe a minute before my heart remembers it's supposed to stop." He laughed again, that awful bubbling sound. "Gods. Forty years carrying other men's secrets and this is how I spend the last of it. On a child on a mountain."

"I'm twenty-two."

"You're a child," he said with great certainty. "Everyone under fifty is a child. Everyone over fifty is a fool. I've been both."

Whatever he drew out from inside his doublet was wrapped in oilcloth, and the oilcloth was stained, and when he pushed it into Juniper's hands it was heavier than its size had any right to make it. Not physically heavy. Some other kind of heavy. The kind of weight you feel in your back teeth.

Inside the oilcloth, when Juniper peeled back one corner with numb fingers, there was a map. Folded small, creased soft with handling, and soaked through along one entire edge so that the paper had gone translucent and brown.

"Don't look at it here," the man said sharply, and the sharpness in him startled Juniper more than the blood had. "Not in the open. Never in the open. Walls first. Stone walls. And never—" his grip tightened, dragging Juniper closer, close enough to see the burst vessels in his eyes, "—never let anyone see that you have it. Not soldiers. Not friends. Not priests. Especially not priests."

"You have the wrong person," Juniper said. Their voice came out smaller than they intended. "I'm a courier. Well—a smuggler, honestly, if we're being exact. I carry brandy and forged writs and once an entire beehive, which is a longer story than we have time for. I don't carry wars."

"Everyone carries wars." He sagged back against the granite. The grip on Juniper's wrist loosened, but didn't let go. "Mine started exactly like this. Someone bleeding, someone young, someone saying *you have the wrong person.*"

The wind came down the saddle and flattened the grass and made the ravens lift off in a black scatter, and in the sudden silence Juniper became very aware of the mules, tied forty paces below, and of the road behind the dead man—his horse was gone, or taken, and there were hoofprints churned in the frozen mud of the track, many hoofprints, going both directions.

"How many rode after you?"

"Six." He coughed. "I dropped two at the ford. Four is too many for one man and not quite enough to catch me, which was my whole strategy, right up until it wasn't." His eyes drifted shut. Juniper shook his wrist, gently, and they opened again. "Sorry. Dying is dull work. Where was I."

"You were telling me why I should take a bloodstained map from a stranger instead of walking away like any sensible person would."

"Were you going to walk away?"

Juniper opened their mouth. Closed it. The honest answer sat in their throat like a stone: *yes, probably, yes, I have walked away from worse than this, I once walked away from a burning barn with my own mentor inside it—*

"No," they said finally.

"There it is." He sounded pleased, in a faint, faraway way. "Honest face. Told you."

"Tell me your name, at least. If I'm to be saddled with your dying wish, I'd like to know whose it is."

"Hale," he said. "Tam Hale. Courier, third rank, Order of the Grey Road—which you've never heard of, because we were good." He managed another of those crooked smiles. "We carried things kings weren't supposed to know existed. And now I'm the last one carrying this, and I'm going to be the last one not carrying it in about thirty seconds, so. Walls first. Trust no grey. And whatever you do—"

He stopped. His eyes went wide and fixed on something over Juniper's shoulder—not the road, not the sky, just middle distance, the place where the dying look.

"What," Juniper said. "What do I do?"

"The Steppe remembers," Tam Hale whispered.

And then, with the last strength in his big ruined body, he folded Juniper's fingers closed around the map, the way you'd fold a child's hand around something precious—or something cursed—and he let go of everything else at once.

The wind moved. The ravens came back down, complaining.

Juniper stayed kneeling in the red snow for a long moment, holding a dead man's hand because letting go of it alone seemed rude, and then they closed his eyes with two fingers and stood up too fast and had to stand very still until the world stopped tilting.

"All right," they said aloud, to nobody, in the voice they used for frightened mules. "All right."

* * *

The sensible thing—the truly sensible thing, the thing Juniper's three years of careful solitary living argued for with great eloquence—was to leave him. Cover him with stones, take nothing, mark nothing, walk to Coldharbor, deliver the brandy, forget.

Juniper got as far as standing over him with the first flat rock in their hands.

Then they looked at his face. At the forty-odd years written on it, and the last hour written over those, and the crooked apologetic smile still ghosting around his mouth, and they put the rock down.

"No," they muttered. "Absolutely not. I'm not digging a grave on a mountain pass for a man whose name I knew for four minutes. That's how you end up in songs, and the songs are always wrong."

They dug anyway.

It took most of an hour, with the shortening knife and then with a slab of shale used as a shovel, and the ground fought them the whole way—frost-hardened, stony, full of the mountain's ancient objections. Juniper swore at it fluently in three languages and invented a fourth. By the end their palms were blistered through their gloves and their shoulders were shaking, and the grave was barely deep enough to be called a grave, more a suggestion of burial, a polite request that the earth consider taking him.

But it was something. It was walls around him, of a kind. Stone walls, he'd said. Juniper figured that counted.

They laid him in with his hands folded on his chest, because that seemed right, and stacked the shale above him in a rough cairn, and then stood back with their breath steaming and realized they were talking to him.

"—and if your Order comes looking, I never saw you, understand? As far as I'm concerned you were a boulder that talked. Which happens on these passes more often than people admit."

The cairn said nothing. Cairns were good listeners.

Juniper turned to go, and stopped.

The mules. Below. One of them—Bramble, the opinionated one—was making a sound she only made when something was wrong in a way her small angry brain couldn't categorize. Not the wolf-alarm whinny. Something lower. Warier.

Juniper went down the slope fast and quiet, boots finding the silent places between the loose stones, and reached the mules in time to find Bramble with her ears pinned and the other mule, a placid gelding named Turnip who had never had a suspicious thought in his life, standing perfectly still and staring at Juniper with the fixed attention of an animal that has noticed you are carrying something new.

Something that hums.

Because it did hum. That was the thing Juniper had been refusing to notice since the pass, the way you refuse to notice a toothache. The oilcloth bundle, tucked inside their coat, against their chest, was humming. Not a sound, exactly. More a feeling that insisted on pretending to be a sound—like the memory of a bell struck years ago, still traveling outward through whatever it traveled through.

"Stop that," Juniper told it, through the coat.

It did not stop. It also, notably, did not get louder near the mules or softer away from them, which ruled out every natural explanation Juniper possessed, and they possessed several; Maren Crane had made sure her apprentice could list the ways a thing might seem magical before teaching them the ways it might actually be so.

*Maren.* Juniper put the thought down like something hot.

They untied the mules and stood a moment in the failing light, looking east where the road fell toward the valley and the waystation at Miller's Rest—an ugly little stone box of a building, abandoned half the year, with a roof that leaked philosophically rather than practically and walls thick enough that Tam Hale's advice about stone walls could be honored without irony.

Walls first, he'd said. Trust no grey.

The light was going. Four riders might be anywhere behind them on the track, and the map—if that's what it was—was sitting against Juniper's ribs like a second heartbeat that belonged to somebody else.

"This isn't my problem," Juniper informed the mountains generally.

The mountains, being mountains, declined to comment. But somewhere downslope a raven called twice, sharp and specific, and Juniper thought of the man's grey eyes opening one last time, and the weight of his frozen hand folding their fingers shut around a stranger's whole life's work, and heard their own voice say *no* when every clever self-preserving part of them had voted *yes.*

They led the mules toward Miller's Rest as the dark came up out of the valleys like water filling a cup, and the humming against their chest kept time with their footsteps all the way, patient as the spreading blood in the snow, and Juniper caught themselves—halfway there, too committed to turn back—wondering what a map could possibly show that six riders in grey would kill for.

They'd find out within the hour. They only wished, looking at the waystation's dark windows, that they wouldn't.
