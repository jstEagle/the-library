## 3. What the Map Shows

The waystation at Miller's Rest had not been restful since before the war, and possibly not even then.

Juniper knew this because they'd slept in it four times over three years, and each time something new had rotted, collapsed, or been claimed by squirrels with ambitious architectural visions. The roof leaked in two places—one over the hearth, one directly over the sleeping loft, as if the building itself had opinions about where a person should lie down. A family of martens had moved into the chimney and expressed their territorial claims with a musk that could strip varnish.

It was perfect. Nobody came to Miller's Rest unless they were lost, desperate, or smuggling, and Juniper was only one of those things by trade.

They bolted the door anyway. Then the shutters. Then, because habits kept people alive, they dragged the broken table against the wall beneath the window where a person might stand to look out, so that anyone doing so would have to make noise first.

The courier's pack sat in the middle of the floor like a guest nobody had invited.

Juniper made tea instead of looking at it. There was still a tin of blackleaf in their kit, half-crushed but serviceable, and the hearth drew well enough once Juniper remembered which flue was blocked by what was almost certainly a marten nest and adjusted accordingly. Water, leaves, the chipped clay cup they'd left here last autumn tucked behind a loose stone exactly where loose stones were supposed to keep things. Some small part of the world behaving as expected was a comfort.

The tea did not help. The pack sat there being dead-shaped.

"You're being ridiculous," Juniper told themselves, and drank the tea anyway, because being ridiculous went down easier warm.

* * *

The problem—and Juniper laid it out with the methodical honesty of someone who had learned the hard way that lying to themselves was a luxury for people with fewer enemies—was that they had taken things from dying men before. Not often. But once is enough to learn the shape of it: the weight of a stranger's hand going slack around yours, the way you promise things in those moments because refusing feels like murder, and the way promises to the dying turn out to be heavier than any cargo on the mountain.

This time they hadn't even promised anything aloud. That was the trap of it. He'd pressed the map into their hands and said *the Steppe remembers*, and died, and now the silence where his voice had been was doing all the demanding.

Juniper set down the cup. Crossed the room. Crouched by the pack.

It was good leather, oiled and cared-for, the kind of gear issued rather than bought—stamped on the flap with a crest Juniper didn't recognize: a circle bisected by a line, worn nearly smooth. Inside: a spare shirt, dried meat wrapped in waxcloth, a water skin, a folding razor, and a small wooden case no bigger than a prayer book, which rattled when shaken and therefore held either the map or Juniper's doom, depending on how one counted.

They opened the case at arm's length, the way you'd open a box that might contain a snake.

A map. Vellum, folded into eighths, creased soft from years of handling. Blood along one edge, already browned. Juniper carried it to the table by the window—the noisy table, the safe table—and unfolded it flat, weighing down the corners with the cup, a horseshoe, a lump of pitch, and their own elbow.

It showed the Thornfells. That much made sense; he'd died in the Thornfells. The cartography was fine work, better than fine—the ridge lines rendered with little hatched shadows like comb strokes, the passes named in a small precise hand. Juniper found the smuggler roads marked too, the ones that officially did not exist, drawn in a different ink, fainter. Someone who'd made this map had known the mountains the way Juniper knew them: from underneath.

And there, in the lower right corner, past the mountains' southern skirt where the paper should have shown empty grassland, two words in that same careful script:

*The Glass Steppe.*

"Huh," said Juniper, who had heard of the Glass Steppe the way everyone had heard of it—in the tone people used for plagues and old kings. A place where a battle had been fought so hard and hot that the earth itself had melted. Where nothing grew. Where the last war had ended, supposedly, because there was finally nothing left worth fighting over.

The ink around those words looked darker than the rest. Fresher. As though—

As though it were still drying.

Juniper leaned close. Held the lamp nearer. The letters gleamed, wet-black.

"That's not possible," they informed the map, in the reasonable tone they used on marten nests and other problems that refused to listen.

Because here was the thing about ink: Juniper had spent six years apprenticed to a hedge-witch whose entire livelihood was making ink do things it shouldn't. Maren Crane could write words that crawled off the page and bit. She sold love-charms with handwriting that whispered your name at midnight, and warding scripts that walked their borders like patrolmen, and once—for reasons Juniper had never gotten a straight answer about—a letter that screamed. Juniper knew living ink. Knew its smell, its shimmer, the faint warmth it gave off like a sleeping animal.

This ink wasn't alive. It was *moving.*

Slowly. So slowly Juniper might have called it imagination, except that Juniper had a smuggler's eyes and a liar's memory, and both agreed: the fine hatchwork of the mountains was creeping southward. Millimeter by millimeter, the whole western half of the map was crawling toward the Glass Steppe like iron filings toward a lodestone, ridges sliding, rivers stretching, the blank grassland shrinking as the land itself flowed toward those two darkening words.

Juniper stood up very fast, knocking the cup off the table. It hit the floor and rolled, unbroken, into the corner, where it came to rest with an air of having seen this sort of thing before.

"Right," said Juniper. "Right. Okay."

There was a procedure for this. Maren had drilled it into them until it lived in the spine: *when magic does something it shouldn't, you do not touch it, you do not name it aloud, and you do not let it know you're frightened.* Magic was a marten too, in that respect.

Step one: don't touch it. Juniper's hands were already behind their back. Good start.

Step two: observe without naming. Juniper observed. The map continued its slow migration, patient as erosion. The pass where the courier had died had slid perhaps a finger's width closer to the Steppe since Juniper first looked. Which meant it had been moving the whole ride here. The whole time it had been folded against Juniper's ribs, inside their coat, riding their heartbeat like a tick.

Something cold walked down Juniper's back wearing spiked boots.

Step three—

Step three had never come up, actually, because in six years of apprenticeship Juniper had never once encountered magic that moved toward something instead of just *being*. Maren's charms were static. Wards held. Curses sat where you put them, sulking. Everything Juniper understood about the craft said that magic was a tool, and tools waited.

This was not waiting. This was *wanting.*

* * *

Juniper did the sensible thing. They put the map back in its case, put the case in the pack, put the pack outside in the lean-to against the far wall, and went back inside to drink cold tea and pretend to sleep.

This lasted approximately forty minutes, most of which was spent staring at the ceiling listening to the wind and composing arguments.

*It's not your problem,* argued the sensible voice, the one that had kept them alive since they'd walked out of Maren Crane's cottage at nineteen with a bindle and a grudge. *You found a body. You took his pack so scavengers wouldn't get it. Very civic. Bury him in the morning, say a few words, walk south, forget him. You're good at forgetting. It's practically your trade.*

*He chose you,* argued the other voice, quieter, the one Juniper trusted least precisely because it sounded most like them. *Out of every soul on that mountain, he crawled toward you.*

"He was dying," Juniper muttered at the ceiling. "Dying people aren't choosy."

*He said the Steppe remembers. He wanted it remembered. Somebody has to do the remembering.*

"Somebody" was a word Juniper had spent three years avoiding. Somebody got involved. Somebody got asked questions by people with grey uniforms and greyer eyes. Somebody stopped being invisible, and invisibility—this was the creed Juniper had built their whole life on, plank by careful plank—was the only safety there was. Be small. Be unremarkable. Be a face the innkeeper forgets by morning. The mountains didn't care about you, and that was the kindest thing anyone could say about the mountains.

But the map was out there in the lean-to, and Juniper could feel it—not literally, probably, almost certainly not, they were a hedge-apprentice dropout and not a seer—but the way you can feel a letter unopened on the table. The way a house feels different with a stranger in it.

At some point past midnight, Juniper gave up, dressed, retrieved the pack, and brought the map back to the table, because apparently they were the kind of person who did this now. Good to know. Three years of self-cultivation and it turned out the finished product was *curious to a fault.*

They spread it flat again under the lamp.

It had kept moving. The southern passes were noticeably further along—the whole western range had gathered itself up like a hem being taken in, drawing toward the Steppe. And now, in the space where the blank grassland used to be, faint lines were surfacing. Roads. Or the ghosts of roads, emerging the way a face emerges in fog, drawn in ink so old-looking it seemed to be rising out of the vellum rather than onto it. They converged, all of them, on the two darkening words.

"It's a route," Juniper breathed. "You're—someone wrote you a route, and it's still writing."

The courier hadn't been carrying a map of the Steppe. He'd been carrying a road *to* it, one that assembled itself as it traveled, as though the map knew where it was going and was simply, patiently, walking there in ink.

Juniper thought about the man on the pass. Grey-clad under the blood, they realized now—remembered, the mind supplying details after the fact the way it always cruelly did. Grey coat, grey scarf, boots of Order issue. And the crest on his pack: a circle bisected by a line. Juniper had seen that mark before, years ago, branded on a crate Maren had been paid to ward and never asked about, and Maren had gone quiet for a week afterward, and Juniper had learned not to ask.

The Ashen Order. The ones who burned books and blessed ashtrays and believed, according to tavern talk, that the last war ended rightly and everything since had been a long mistake waiting to resume. People who did not send lone couriers through smuggler country unless the cargo mattered more than the courier.

Which meant whoever killed him had known. And whoever killed him would want what he carried. And what he carried was currently crawling across a table in front of Juniper Crane, who had spent twenty-two years on this earth learning exactly one skill thoroughly:

How not to be found.

The lamp guttered. Juniper told themselves it was the draft from the bad flue. The lamp guttered again, and this time the flame bent *toward* the map, leaning like a listener, and the wet-dark words at the corner caught the light and threw it back in a color firelight doesn't usually make—a deep, glassy amber, like sun through a bottle.

And beneath the crackle of the hearth and the wind and the martens' offended scratching, Juniper heard—or felt, or imagined so vividly that the distinction stopped mattering—a hum. Faint. Rhythmic. Patient as the tide coming in.

Coming from the map.

Or coming from under the map. From under the vellum, under the table, under the mountain itself, from somewhere south and east where a plain of glass lay remembering whatever it was the dead man had said it remembered.

Juniper blew out the lamp.

Darkness. Silence. The hum persisted for three heartbeats and faded, reluctantly, like a guest who knows he's been asked to leave but wants you to know he'll be back.

Juniper stood in the dark of Miller's Rest, hands flat on the table on either side of the map, breathing carefully, and performed the final step of Maren's procedure, the one she'd never taught because she'd never needed to:

Decide, quickly, whether you are holding a treasure or a lit fuse.

The trouble was that Juniper had been around hedge-magic long enough to know the answer, nine times out of ten, was *both*, and the remaining tenth was *worse*.

They refolded the map—it flinched, or seemed to, at being folded against the direction of its travel—and stowed it inside their shirt this time, against skin, because if the thing insisted on having a heartbeat then it could at least beat where Juniper could feel it. Then they repacked the courier's effects with more care than they'd given anything in years, ate the man's dried meat because wasting food was a separate sin, and sat by the dead hearth working out distances.

South through the low pass: three days to the river towns, anonymity, tea that didn't taste like guilt. East-southeast, following the map's crawling ink: unknown days to a place where wars went to die, hunted by whoever had hunted him.

"I'm a smuggler," Juniper reminded the empty room. "I move cargo. I don't ask what it dreams."

The map, warm against their chest, hummed once—softly, almost fondly, the way a cat acknowledges being discussed.

Outside, far down the valley, a light moved where lights shouldn't be. Lanterns—three, maybe four—traveling the switchback road at an hour and a pace that belonged to soldiers, or to people pretending very hard not to be.

Juniper pinched out the coals, took up their pack, and slipped out the back way into the dark, moving fast and quiet along paths that existed on no honest map at all.

Behind them, through the gap in the shutters, the waystation's cold hearth held the last reflection of lantern-light growing steadily nearer—and somewhere in the dark of Juniper's shirt, faint as a second pulse, the map began to hum a little louder, as if it too had heard the riders coming, and knew them by name.
