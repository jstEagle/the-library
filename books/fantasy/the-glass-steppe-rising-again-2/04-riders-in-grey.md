## 4. Riders in Grey

The trouble with hiding in the Thornfell mountains, Juniper had learned over six years of practice, was that eventually you had to come down for supplies.

Salt, specifically. Salt and lamp oil and, if she was feeling flush, a twist of dried apricots from the peddler who worked the lower valley road. A person could live on smoked goat and pine tea for only so long before they started dreaming about things that grew in dirt instead of on trees, and Juniper had reached that point somewhere around the third week of eating nothing that had ever seen sunlight.

So it was that on a grey morning thick enough to chew, she came down out of the high smuggler's paths—the ones Maren had taught her, back when teaching her had still been an act of love rather than commerce—and made for the market at Hollowmere, where nobody asked questions because everybody was selling something they shouldn't.

She had the map with her. Of course she had the map. She'd thought about leaving it buried under the hearthstone of the waystation, wrapped in oilcloth, weighted down with the good iron pot. She'd thought about it for most of a sleepless night, actually, lying there listening to the wind worry the shutters while the map sat on her table like a guest who wouldn't leave.

But every time she imagined walking away from it, something in her chest pulled tight, like a stitch catching. Not magic—she'd checked, pressed her palm flat against the parchment until her hand went numb, felt nothing but old blood and older paper. Just ordinary guilt, then. The kind that comes with carrying a dead man's last words.

*The Steppe remembers.*

"Well," she muttered to the mule track ahead of her, "it can remember on its own time."

The mule track did not answer, which was one of the reasons she preferred it to people.

* * *

Hollowmere smelled the way it always did: woodsmoke, wet wool, someone's ambitious attempt at frying onions, and underneath it all the particular sourness of a town that knew exactly how much of its prosperity depended on looking the other way. Juniper liked it enormously. It was the sort of place where a person could be nobody with real distinction.

She'd gotten as far as the chandler's stall—had the salt in one pocket and the oil jug swinging from her elbow and was haggling over the price of tallow candles with the enthusiasm of a woman who intended to win—when the bells started.

Not the market bells. Those rang bright and quick, three notes, come-and-buy. This was the deep bell, the one bolted to the watch-house roof, and it rang slow and heavy, over and over, like a heart that had forgotten how to stop being afraid.

Juniper's hands went still on the candle she'd been turning over.

Around her, the market didn't panic. That was the first strange thing. Hollowmere's vendors simply began, with the smooth unhurried motion of long practice, putting away their more interesting merchandise. The chandler slid a tray of candles beneath his counter that were, Juniper happened to know, not candles at all. The woman selling "mountain honey" covered her jars. Somewhere behind her, a flap of canvas dropped over a table that had been displaying knives a moment ago.

The whole market closed like a sea anemone poked by a finger.

"Riders in grey," the chandler said quietly. He wasn't looking at her. He was very carefully not looking at anything. "Best move along, miss. Back way, if you're not from here."

"I'm not from here," Juniper said, which was true in several directions at once.

"Then you especially want the back way." He pushed her change across the counter without counting it, which in Juniper's experience meant either extraordinary kindness or extraordinary fear, and the man's knuckles were white as new tallow.

She took her candles and went.

* * *

The back way out of Hollowmere ran along the millrace, past the tannery—which improved no one's day—and up through a fold in the hills where the sheep grazed on slopes so steep they looked permanently surprised to be standing on them. Juniper took it at a walk, because running drew the eye, and kept to the hedgerows, and told herself firmly that she was not afraid.

She was, of course, afraid. But there was a difference between being afraid and letting the fear drive, the way there was a difference between rain falling on you and drowning. She'd spent six years learning that difference, and she could feel it holding now, a thin strong membrane between her and the bell's slow tolling.

From the ridge above the millrace she stopped—crouched in the gorse, because gorse forgives everything—and looked back down into the valley.

They came up the king's road in a column of eight, riding horses the color of bad weather, and they wore grey. Not the grey of soldiers' cloaks gone threadbare, not the grey of honest work. This was a uniform grey, dyed deliberate, the grey of ash after a fire has finished with a thing. Their helms were plain. Their shields were plain. Even their banners were plain—a field of grey with nothing on it at all, which Juniper found somehow worse than any sigil. An order that had once believed in something enough to paint it had decided, apparently, that belief itself was too identifiable.

The Ashen Order. She'd heard the name the way you hear the name of a disease that hasn't reached your village yet. Stories, mostly. Storytellers' furniture. *And then the Ashen Order came, and—* whatever terrible thing the plot required.

Except here they were, eight riders sweeping her valley, and at their head rode a woman whose bearing suggested that stories were beneath her.

Juniper watched them work. It was, she had to admit, well done. They didn't thunder through Hollowmere shouting demands. They dismounted at the well, unhurried, and the officer spoke to the headman quietly, and the headman nodded a great deal, and then the riders spread out through the lanes two by two, knocking on doors, asking questions with the patient courtesy of tax collectors.

Asking, she would learn shortly, after a courier.

Her hand drifted to the satchel at her hip, where the map lay rolled against her ribs like a second heartbeat. She made herself take the hand away. Made herself breathe. Gorse pricked through her trousers at both knees, and she focused on that, the small honest pain of it, because pain was simple and the alternative was imagining those grey riders peeling open her satchel with courteous hands.

*You're a smuggler,* she reminded herself. *Smugglers get asked questions. Smugglers have answers.*

The problem, of course, was that for once she actually had something to hide, and she was discovering that innocence had been load-bearing in her personality in ways she hadn't appreciated until now.

* * *

She should have gone straight up-mountain. Every instinct she owned, every hard-won scrap of craft Maren had beaten into her—and some lessons really had been near-beatings, though Maren would have called them *instruction*—said: leave now, climb fast, be a rumor by dusk.

Instead she stayed on the ridge and watched, because there is a particular stupidity that lives in all of us, and Juniper Crane's had always been curiosity.

Two riders broke off from the others and started up the sheep tracks. Toward her side of the valley. Not toward her, precisely—toward the shepherd huts and the drover's path—but *her side*, and that was close enough to relocate her legs without further discussion.

She moved. Low, quiet, along the spine of the ridge where the wind covered sound, cutting across toward the old quarry road and the network of smuggler cuts beyond it. She knew this terrain the way other people knew their own kitchens: every hollow, every blind crest, every stretch of gorse deep enough to swallow a kneeling woman whole.

Which is how she came to be flattened in a drainage ditch beneath a hawthorn hedge, smelling of mud and her own poor decisions, when the two riders passed within ten feet of her.

"—sweeping the high passes by tomorrow," one was saying. His voice carried the flat boredom of a man who had asked the same question in forty villages. "The captain wants the river crossings watched tonight."

"And what do we say it is we're watching for?" the second rider asked. Younger. Newer. There was an eagerness in his voice that Juniper distrusted on principle.

"A courier. Dead or alive, though dead is preferred, per the writ." A pause; leather creaked; a horse snorted. "Carrying documents pertaining to a matter of historical significance."

"Historical significance," the young one repeated, tasting the phrase. "That's what they're calling it?"

"That's what I'm instructed to call it. In my experience, when the Order reaches for long words, it's because the short ones frighten even them." The older rider sighed. "There's a word going around the senior ranks. I heard it from a quartermaster who heard it from a chaplain, so weigh it accordingly."

Juniper, pressed into the cold mud of the ditch with a hawthorn scratching her scalp, weighed it with every nerve she possessed.

"Heartstone," the rider said.

The word landed in the world and changed its weight. Juniper felt it go through her like a stone through a pond—not the sound itself, but what rippled out behind it. Because she *knew* the word. Everyone knew the word, the way everyone knew the word for the sun. The heartstone of the Glass Steppe, the thing that had ended the last war by ending, some said, the possibility of another one. Shattered. Gone. A story mothers used to end arguments: *that's how wars start, love, and look what happened last time.*

A story. Furniture for storytellers.

Except a dying courier had bled out against her shoulder whispering about the Steppe, and now eight riders in ash-grey were pulling her valley apart looking for his papers, and a quartermaster's chaplain was using the word *heartstone* in the plural company of quartermasters.

"The heartstone's destroyed," the young rider said. "Everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows what they were taught. Yes." The older rider's horse shifted, and Juniper held her breath as a hoof came down inches from her hidden hand, close enough that she could smell the animal, warm and hay-sweet and utterly indifferent to her survival. "All I know is that the Archive has opened the sealed stacks for the first time in twenty years, and men who don't pray have started praying, and we are sweeping a mountain valley for a dead courier's satchel. Draw your own conclusions. I intend to draw mine and keep them to myself, which is why I'm still alive."

They rode on. Juniper lay in the ditch a long while after the hoofbeats faded, staring up through the hawthorn at a sky the color of dishwater, doing arithmetic she didn't like.

One plus one plus one. A courier who died saying *the Steppe remembers*. A map whose ink crawled like something alive. Riders in grey asking polite questions in forty villages. And underneath it all, humming like a struck bell that wouldn't quite fall silent: *heartstone.*

The hunt had begun. Not for her—she was nobody, a smudge, a woman who bought salt. For the map. But the map lived in her satchel now, and satchels were carried by bodies, and bodies could be searched, and the older rider had said *dead is preferred*, and he hadn't sounded like a man who expected anyone to argue.

"Right," Juniper said to the hawthorn. Her voice came out steadier than she felt, which she chose to count as a professional skill. "Right."

* * *

She climbed the rest of the afternoon, taking the smuggler's cuts, the paths that existed on no chart and in no memory except hers and a few dead friends'. By the time the light went copper and then grey, she'd gained the high shelf below the crags where the waystation crouched against the weather like a stubborn old animal.

She built a small fire anyway. That was the thing about fear—it wanted you cold and dark and moving, and the surest way to beat it was to sit down, light a flame, and eat something hot out of pure spite. She fried onions in the blackened pan. She made pine tea. She unrolled the map on the table between the salt and the candles and looked at it while she ate, the way you'd watch a sleeping snake.

The ink had moved again. She was nearly sure of it now. The little crawling lines had shifted a finger's width since morning, all of them leaning the same direction, like grass bending before wind—leaning south and east, toward the blank space at the map's edge where a cartographer had written, in faded capitals, GLASS STEPPE, and then, smaller, as if apologizing: *here be the war's ending.*

"You and me both," Juniper told it.

She thought about the young rider's voice. *Everyone knows the heartstone's destroyed.* And the older one's answer, dry as old paper: *everyone knows what they were taught.*

She thought about Maren.

It arrived in her chest the way bad news does—not as a thought but as a weight, sudden and specific. Because if there was one person in these mountains who would know whether the heartstone was story or stone, one person who had spent fifty years hoarding secrets the way other women hoarded jam, it was Maren Crane, hedge-witch, liar, teacher, the woman who had raised her and betrayed her in roughly equal measure, and whom Juniper had not spoken to in six years.

Going to Maren meant walking back into the worst argument of her life. It meant admitting, by the act of arrival, that she needed something. Maren would make her say it out loud, too—that was the cruelty of the woman, she never let you need things silently—and then she'd charge for the help, because Maren Crane believed with religious fervor that nothing given free was ever valued, and possibly she was right, and Juniper hated that possibly she was right.

But the alternative was sitting alone in a waystation with a living map and a word like *heartstone* ringing in her ears, waiting for grey riders to work their way up the valleys until they reached hers.

Outside, the wind turned. It came down off the crags carrying the first bite of true cold, and with it—faint, so faint she might have invented it—a smell that didn't belong on a mountaintop at all.

Smoke. Woodsmoke, but wrong for the season, wrong for the distance, threaded with something sharper underneath. Something chemical. Something that burned green things and left ash.

Juniper stood very still, the tea cooling in her hands, and listened to the dark.

Down-valley, where Hollowmere's lights should have been twinkling safe and small, the horizon glowed.
