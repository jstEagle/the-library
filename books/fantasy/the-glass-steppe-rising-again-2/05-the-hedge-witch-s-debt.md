## 5. The Hedge-Witch's Debt

The map did not sleep.

Juniper had tried everything a sensible person could try: wrapping it in oilcloth, burying it beneath the waystation's floorboards, setting a kettle on top of it as though it were nothing more than a stubborn loaf refusing to rise. Through all of it, she could feel it—the faint tug at the edge of her awareness, like a fisherman's line hooked somewhere behind her ribs, reeling gently, patiently, toward a place called the Glass Steppe.

She'd named it *the itch*. Naming things made them smaller. Maren had taught her that.

Maren.

Juniper sat in the grey half-light of dawn, elbows on her knees, and let the name sit in her chest like a swallowed stone. Three years since she'd climbed out of Maren Crane's window with a satchel and a grudge and not so much as a goodbye scratched into the frost of the glass. Three years of smuggler roads and borrowed names, and now here she was, map crawling against her spine like a second heartbeat, thinking: *I need her.*

She hated needing anyone. It was a skill she'd practiced diligently, like scales on a fiddle, until she was very nearly good at it.

But the Ashen Order was sweeping the valley, grey riders asking after a courier who no longer breathed, and Juniper Crane—runaway apprentice, occasional smuggler, current custodian of what was apparently the most dangerous piece of parchment in the Thornfells—could not outrun an entire order of inquisitors alone. Not with the map. Not with whatever *the Steppe remembers* meant.

And there was exactly one person in these mountains who knew more about hiding things from the Order than Juniper did.

"One conversation," she told the kettle. "In and out before noon. I don't even have to knock."

The kettle, being a kettle, offered no opinion. But the map tugged once, sharply, as if to say *liar*, and Juniper pulled on her boots.

* * *

Maren Crane lived where the mountain forgot to be cruel—a fold of valley above the old resin-tappers' road, where a crooked cottage leaned against a boulder like an old drunk leaning on a friend. Smoke curled from the chimney. Herb-baskets hung from the eaves in their usual chaotic abundance, some labeled, most not, one labeled *DO NOT* in Maren's spiky hand, which had never once stopped Juniper from stealing from it as a girl.

Juniper crouched in the bracken for a long moment, watching smoke rise, feeling absurdly like a fox deciding whether the henhouse was worth the dogs.

"You can come in or you can freeze out there," said a voice from the doorway, dry as autumn thistle. "But either way, stop crushing my monkshood. It's sulky enough already."

Juniper's heart performed something complicated. She stood up slowly.

Maren Crane had gone greyer at the temples and no softer anywhere else. She stood in her doorway with flour on her knuckles and her sleeves rolled to the elbow, arms crossed, one eyebrow doing the work of an entire tribunal. She looked at Juniper the way she'd always looked at Juniper—as if measuring her for a poultice and finding several symptoms already.

"Hello, Maren," Juniper said.

"Three years." Maren turned and walked inside, which in Maren's language was an invitation. "Three years, and you show up at dawn smelling of waystation dust and bad decisions. Sit. There's porridge. Don't touch the jar on the third shelf; it's not for eating, it's for threatening people with."

Juniper sat. The cottage smelled exactly as it always had—dried yarrow, woodsmoke, bread, and underneath it all that faint mineral tang Juniper had never been able to name and had missed more than she'd ever admit. Her body remembered this room. Her knees remembered where the loose floorboard was. Her hands remembered the chipped blue mug that was hers, that was still hers, sitting on its hook by the fire as if three years were a long weekend.

She hated how much that undid her.

"Porridge is fine," she managed.

"Porridge is what there is." Maren ladled two bowls without asking whether Juniper wanted any, because Maren had never once asked whether Juniper wanted anything; she simply decided, correctly, and moved on. She set the bowl down, then paused, ladle dripping, nostrils flaring slightly.

Her eyes went to Juniper's pack. To the shape inside it.

"Ah," Maren said quietly.

"Maren—"

"Sit. Eat." The ladle went back in the pot with a clank. "We'll talk after. Nothing true gets said on an empty stomach, and I suspect we're going to need every true word we can find."

They ate in silence. It should have been uncomfortable. Instead it was the first silence in four days that hadn't felt like being hunted, and Juniper despised herself for the relief of it, spoonful by spoonful.

When the bowls were empty, Maren took them, washed them, dried them, put them away—all of it with the deliberate slowness of someone buying time—and then sat down across the table and folded her hands.

"Now," she said. "Show me."

"How did you know I have something?"

"Because you came back." Maren's voice didn't sharpen; it went flat, which was worse. "You ran from me like the house was on fire, girl, and you swore on your own blood you'd never cross my threshold again. People only break oaths like that when they're carrying something heavier than pride. So." She extended one flour-dusted hand across the table, palm up. An old gesture. *Give it here. Let me see.*

Juniper's fingers found the strap of her pack and held on.

"I found a courier on the pass," she said. "Dying. Order arrows in him, or something like Order arrows—I've never seen fletching like it. He gave me a map. It—" She stopped. Started again. "It moves, Maren. The ink crawls. And the riders in grey are asking after him in every valley south of the ridge, and they used a word I've only ever heard in your stories. *Heartstone.*"

Something crossed Maren's face. Juniper had spent eleven years learning to read that face—the twitch at the mouth that meant *you're lying*, the slow blink that meant *you're an idiot but a survivable one*—and what crossed it now was none of those things. What crossed it now was fear, arriving late and trying to look like resignation.

"So you came to me," Maren said.

"You know how to hide things from them. You taught me half of what I know about—"

"I know." Maren rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand. "Yes. I know what I taught you. And I'm going to tell you something now, Juniper, and you're going to want to throw the porridge bowl at me, and I'd ask you not to, because it's my good bowl."

Juniper waited.

"I served the Order," Maren said. "Eleven years. Hedge-division—they called us root-and-rune, the ones they sent into villages to sniff out talent before it grew teeth. I mapped gifted children for them, Juniper. Names, hamlets, what they could do, who'd miss them. I was good at it. They gave me a commendation and a pension and a silence-oath sealed in ash-ink, and the day I broke that oath was the day I ran up into these mountains and became the harmless old hedge-witch who threatens people with jars."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a jay screamed once and thought better of it.

Juniper heard her own voice come out very small. "You *mapped* children."

"Yes."

"For them."

"Yes."

"And me?" The question tore out of her before she could dress it up in caution. "Eleven years old, orphaned, too much talent and nowhere to put it. Was I a name in one of your little books? Is that why you took me in? Was I a *commission*, Maren?"

Maren didn't flinch. That was the terrible thing—she didn't flinch, didn't look away, just sat there and took it like weather.

"No," she said. "You were the reason I burned the books."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one I have." Maren leaned forward. "There was a girl in Lowmarsh, before you. Six years old. Could make plants grow by singing to them, badly, off-key, wonderful. I wrote her name down because that was the job. The Order came in spring. I told myself they only wanted to teach her. I told myself a great many things, right up until the wagon left and I understood that nobody teaches a child behind a locked door." Her jaw tightened. "So when a ragged little thief with hedge-sense like a lit lantern fell through my herb fence stealing comfrey, I looked at her, and I looked at my ledger, and I lied. First lie I ever told them. *Nothing here,* I wrote. *Old woman, no students.* And then I taught you everything anyway, off the page, under the table, because somebody had to, and because—" her voice caught, just barely, "—because I owed a debt I couldn't pay to a girl whose name I still remember."

Juniper's hands were shaking. She pressed them flat against the table.

"You should have told me."

"When? At eleven? *Good morning, child, your teacher sold children to the grey riders for a decade, pass the salt?*" Maren shook her head. "I told myself you'd understand, someday, when you were older. Then you got older and angrier faster than I planned, and then you were gone, and telling you became a thing I'd missed the window for. That's how it goes with debts, Juniper. You keep meaning to pay them, and meanwhile the interest compounds."

"The Order," Juniper said. "Your oath. If they knew you were harboring me—if they knew you lied in your ledgers—"

"They'd burn this cottage with me in it, yes, and enjoy the paperwork." Maren said it lightly, which was how Juniper knew it was true. "Which brings us to the interesting part of the morning, doesn't it. Because you didn't climb my hill to trade grievances. You came because you need a hiding place for that map, and you need one fast, and you need someone who knows exactly how the Order sniffs. And I am the best and worst possible person for the job, and we both know it."

Juniper stared at her. "Then help me anyway."

"Oh, I intend to." Maren stood, went to the dresser, and began pulling out supplies with brisk, angry efficiency—wax, salt, a bundle of dried sage, a knife Juniper recognized as the one Maren used for cutting bindings. "That's the trouble with debts. They don't care whose side you're on. I owe the Order my silence, and I owe that dead girl in Lowmarsh my penance, and I owe you—" she paused, knife in hand, and for one unguarded moment looked every year of her age, "—more than I can carry. So we'll call this payment on the last one. But hear me, Juniper Crane, and hear me clearly."

She set the knife down between them, point toward herself.

"If the Order comes here—and they will, because you led them to my door the moment you walked up my path, you great galloping disaster—then my silence-oath and my penance are going to collide, and I don't yet know which one wins. I would like to believe I'm the woman who burned the books. But I was also the woman who wrote them, and both of those women live in this house, and only one of them has practice at surviving."

Juniper looked at the knife. At the woman across the table who had raised her, lied to her, saved her, and failed her, often in the same afternoon.

"The courier said something," Juniper said quietly. "Before he died. He said *the Steppe remembers.*"

Maren went very still. Stillness, on Maren Crane, had always been louder than shouting.

"Then you'd best tell me everything," she said, "from the beginning. And Juniper—when you're done, we're going to talk about the fact that the Order doesn't send riders into the Thornfells for maps. Maps are paper. Whatever that courier was carrying, the paper is only the leash." Her eyes dropped to Juniper's pack, and this time there was no mistaking the fear in them, or whose throat it was for. "The thing at the end of it is what they want. And leashes, girl, have two ends."

Outside, far down the valley, a bell began to ring—not the chapel bell, which rang in threes, but the resin-tappers' warning bell, which rang without stopping.

Maren closed her eyes. "Grey riders," she said. "At my gate, or I'm a hedge-witch's hat. How fast can you run?"

Juniper was already reaching for her pack.
