## 6. A Knight Without a Banner

Ser Aldric Vane had been waiting on the mountain road for three days, and in that time he had learned two things: the smuggler's path through Thornfell was exactly as treacherous as the old maps promised, and his left boot had developed a squeak that could wake the dead.

He crouched behind a shelf of granite above the pass, watching the narrow track wind down toward the valley, and tried to remember when he had last slept under something other than sky. Four months? Five? The Order's fires had taken the chapterhouse in autumn, and winter had taken everything else—his brothers, his banner, his name in any register that mattered. What remained was a man in a knight's coat too big for him now, because grief, he'd discovered, made you thinner.

The squeak again. He shifted his weight off his left foot and glared at it as though it were a traitor. In a way, it was. Everything about him was traitorous now, depending on who was doing the counting. To the Ashen Order, Ser Aldric Vane of the Order of the Mirrored Shield was a heretic who had read things he shouldn't have and asked questions no one wanted answered. To himself, he was simply a man who had seen a ledger he couldn't unsee.

*Twelve shipments of ash-glass to the Chapterhouse at Corren. Twelve. And a requisition for iron bands large enough to bind a man.*

He had asked what the ash-glass was for. Three days later, the chapterhouse burned, and Aldric had stopped asking questions out loud.

Movement below. He went still, breath shallow, hand finding the hilt of a sword that had seen better decades.

A figure came around the switchback—small, hooded, moving with the particular economy of someone who knew every loose stone on this road by heart. Not a soldier. A courier's stride, quick and low, shoulders angled like they expected trouble from above. They paused at the bend, tilted their head back, and scanned the ridgeline with an expression Aldric could only describe as *professionally annoyed*, as if the mountains themselves were acquaintances who owed them money.

Then the figure looked directly at his hiding place.

Not near it. *At* it.

"Whatever you're selling," the figure called up, voice young and dry as gravel, "I don't want any. And whatever you're hunting, I'm not it."

Aldric considered, briefly, the dignity of remaining silent. Then his left boot squeaked as he rose, ruining everything, and he decided honesty was the better armor.

"I'm not hunting anyone," he said, stepping into view with his hands open and away from his sword. "I'm waiting for you."

"Well," said the stranger, "that's worse."

* * *

They stood ten paces apart on the switchback, the valley falling away behind them in folds of pine and grey stone. Up close, the courier was younger than Aldric had guessed—young enough that the world hadn't finished deciding what shape they'd be. Sharp-eyed. Wiry. A travel cloak patched with three different cloaks, and boots that had walked a very long way from wherever they'd started.

"You're the one carrying the map," Aldric said.

The change was immediate and total. The courier's whole body went quiet—not afraid, exactly, but *braced*, the way a door braces against wind. Their hand drifted, casually, terribly, toward a knife at their belt.

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"A courier died on the high pass four days ago. Ashen arrow, through the lung. He crawled half a mile before the cold took him." Aldric kept his voice level, gentle even, though gentleness felt like handling glass. "You found him. You've got blood on your sleeve you haven't managed to wash out, and you've been walking downhill ever since instead of over the pass, which is wrong for wherever you were going, which means the map is pulling you, or you're running, and either way—"

"Stop." The word cracked like a whip. The knife was out now, low and ready, though the courier's eyes were wide. "Who *are* you?"

Aldric exhaled slowly. He had rehearsed this part on three cold nights, and every version sounded foolish in daylight. But there was nothing for it now except the truth, laid out plain, like a beggar showing empty hands.

"My name is Ser Aldric Vane. I was a knight of the Order of the Mirrored Shield." He pulled back his hood so she could see his face—the scar through his brow, the grey coming in early at his temples. "Our order guarded the old roads and the old records. We kept histories nobody else was allowed to keep. Eleven days ago, the Ashen Order burned our chapterhouse to its foundations and put every brother and sister they could find to the sword. I survived because I was in the archives cellar when the roof came down, reading a ledger I wasn't supposed to see."

The courier didn't lower the knife. But they didn't run, either, and Aldric counted that as a small victory.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you have something they burned my home to find. Because I've spent eleven days following rumors of a dying courier and a stolen map, and because—" he hesitated, then committed, "—because I would rather stand between you and them than let them take another thing I care about."

"That's very noble," the courier said. "Nobility gets people killed on this road."

"Yes," Aldric agreed. "I've noticed. It's why I'm the last one left."

Something flickered across the courier's face—not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, quickly strangled. "Juniper," they said at last. "My name's Juniper Crane. And if you say that word again—*map*—I'll assume you're Order and treat you accordingly."

"Fair terms."

"It's not a term, it's a threat."

"I grew up with knights, Juniper Crane. Threats and terms look identical from the inside."

That did it. The ghost-smile escaped, lived for half a second, and died. Juniper sheathed the knife with exaggerated slowness, keeping their eyes on him the whole time, the way you'd watch a dog you didn't trust not to bite.

"You said your order kept records," they said. "What kind of records?"

"The kind that get people killed." Aldric sat down on a boulder, deliberately, making himself smaller, less threatening—he'd learned that trick from an old sergeant who claimed the best way to win a fight was to convince the other fellow there wouldn't be one. "Records about the Glass Steppe. About what actually happened there, forty years ago, and why the histories everyone learns are lies of omission."

Juniper had gone very still. "Everyone knows what happened at the Steppe. The heartstone shattered. The war ended. Everyone went home."

"Everyone knows *that* the heartstone shattered. Nobody asks how a stone shatters itself in the middle of a battle, or why both armies stopped fighting the moment it did, or why the Ashen Order—which didn't exist before that day—was founded within the year and given authority over every archive in the realm." He looked up at them. "Somebody broke that stone on purpose, Juniper. And somebody has spent forty years making sure no one finds the pieces."

The wind moved through the pines below, a long sighing sound, like the mountain thinking it over.

"And you think my map leads there," Juniper said quietly. It wasn't a question anymore.

"I think the Order thinks so. That's enough for me."

* * *

They walked together down the switchbacks, ten paces apart, which Aldric noted was a diplomatic distance: close enough to talk, far enough that a drawn blade would be fair warning. Juniper moved like water finding its level, choosing footholds without looking, and twice redirected them around stretches of trail that looked perfectly fine to Aldric and were apparently, per Juniper's terse commentary, "ambush-shaped."

"You've used these roads long?" he asked.

"Long enough."

"Alone?"

"Is this an interrogation?"

"It's a conversation. I'm told they look similar."

Juniper snorted. For a while, the only sounds were boots on scree and, mortifyingly, the squeak of Aldric's left boot, which Juniper commented on with the third step and did not stop commenting on for a quarter mile.

"Do you want to know what I think?" Juniper said finally, as the trail widened into an old logging cut. "I think you're either exactly what you say—a broken knight with a grudge—or you're Order, and very good at looking broken. Both stories end with you wanting my map. So here's my problem, Sir Aldric: I can't tell the difference, and I've stayed alive this long by never betting on a story I can't check."

"What would let you check it?"

They stopped walking. Turned. And for the first time, Aldric saw past the wariness to something rawer underneath—a young person carrying weight well beyond their years, holding it the way mules hold loads: without complaint, without rest, and without any expectation of putting it down.

"Show me your arm," Juniper said.

Aldric frowned. "My arm?"

"Every sworn member of the Ashen Order carries the brand. Inner forearm, a grey circle. If you're theirs, you'll have it. If you're not—" they shrugged. "Well. Then you're just a madman in a dead man's coat, and honestly, that I can work with."

Without a word, Aldric stripped off his glove, pushed back his sleeve, and held out his forearm to the pale afternoon light. Unmarked skin, weathered and scarred at the knuckles, but bare where the grey circle should be.

Juniper studied it for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, they laughed—a short, startled sound, as if the laugh had escaped custody.

"What?"

"Nothing. It's just—" they shook their head. "You really are it, aren't you. The disgraced knight. Off the old ballads. Next you'll tell me you've got a tragic backstory and a sword with a name."

"His name is Oathkeeper, and he's embarrassed to be mentioned."

"*Oathkeeper.*" Juniper pressed a hand over their face. "Of course it is. Of course. Why not just hang a sign around your neck—*plot-relevant, approach with caution*."

"He was named by my swordmaster, who had no imagination and less shame. I've suggested 'Gregory' several times. I've been overruled."

And there it was again—that strangled smile, fighting free. Aldric found himself absurdly pleased by it, the way a man is pleased by sun after a week of rain, and told himself firmly that this meant nothing except that he'd been alone too long.

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the logging cut, a fire small enough to hide in a cupped hand. Juniper produced tea leaves from somewhere in their cloak with the smugness of a magician, and for a little while neither of them spoke, and the silence was almost comfortable.

Almost. Aldric had spent eleven days being hunted, and comfort was a language he'd forgotten how to speak.

"The courier," Juniper said at last, staring into the flames. "Did you know him?"

"No. But I knew of him. Rellin Marsh, senior courier of the Mirrored Shield. He carried our most sensitive correspondence." Aldric turned his hands toward the heat. "When the chapterhouse burned, we assumed the Order had killed him too. Apparently he ran further than we gave him credit for."

"He was already hurt when I found him. Badly. There was an arrow—" Juniper's jaw tightened. "He made me swear. He said, *the Steppe remembers*, and then he made me swear again, and then he died, and I've been trying to decide ever since whether swearing an oath to a dying stranger makes me brave or stupid."

"In my experience," Aldric said, "it usually makes you both, and the best people are."

Juniper glanced at him sidelong. "You talk like a book."

"I was raised by librarians with swords. It leaves marks."

The fire popped. Somewhere upslope, a bird complained about the evening. Aldric watched Juniper's hands, wrapped around a tin cup, and saw the tremor in them that pride was holding mostly still.

"May I tell you what I read in that ledger?" he said quietly. "You should know what's chasing you, even if you never show me what you carry."

Juniper was silent a long moment. Then they nodded, once.

"The Order isn't hunting the heartstone to destroy it. That's the story they tell. But the requisitions—the ash-glass, the iron bands, the vault construction beneath their new chapterhouse at Corren—they're building a *container*. Something sized to hold a shard the length of a man's arm." He met Juniper's eyes across the fire. "They don't want the heartstone gone, Juniper. They want it *kept*. Held. Guarded by them, wielded by them, quietly, forever. Whoever holds the heartstone holds the thing that ended the last war—and the thing that could start the next one on their terms."

The color had drained from Juniper's face. "And the map shows where the pieces are."

"I believe it does. And I believe that's why Rellin Marsh ran toward Thornfell instead of away from it—because these roads are the one place in the world the Order's eyes don't reach." Aldric leaned forward. "You're not carrying a map, Juniper Crane. You're carrying the reason for forty years of burnings. And they will follow you to the ends of the earth to take it."

For a long time, Juniper said nothing. The firelight moved on their face, and Aldric watched a dozen calculations chase each other behind those sharp eyes—flight routes, betrayals, odds, all the arithmetic of a life spent alone.

Then Juniper set down the cup, reached into their coat, and drew out a leather tube sealed with wax—and stopped, holding it, not offering it yet.

"If I show you," they said, "you don't touch it. You don't take it. You don't *suggest* taking it. You advise, I decide. Those are the terms."

"Those are good terms."

"And if you turn out to be Order after all—if there's a grey circle somewhere I didn't check—I will drop you off the next bridge and tell myself a very satisfying story about it."

"Also good terms." Aldric smiled tiredly. "You'd make an excellent knight."

"I'd make a terrible knight. Knights die for causes." Juniper worked a thumbnail under the wax seal. "I intend to live for mine."

The tube opened. Inside, rolled tight, was a sheet of vellum that even in the firelight seemed to move—shadows swimming across its surface like fish under ice—and as Juniper unrolled it, Aldric felt the hair rise on his arms, because he knew that shimmer. He'd seen it once before, in a locked case in the deepest archive, in a fragment no bigger than a coin that three of his brothers swore hummed when you weren't listening.

Ash-glass. The map wasn't drawn *on* ordinary vellum.

It was drawn on a piece of the Steppe itself.

"Oh," Aldric breathed. "Oh, that's why they want it so badly."

"Yeah," Juniper said grimly, watching ink crawl across the surface like living things. "I figured that out around the same time I figured out I can't put it down. Look—it's *moving*. It's been moving since the pass. Toward something."

Aldric leaned closer, careful not to touch, and followed the slow migration of lines converging on a blank space at the map's heart where letters were surfacing one by one, rising through the glass like drowned men.

G-L-A-S-S—

He never saw the word finished. Down the valley, faint but unmistakable, a horn blew—one long note, then two short ones. A signal he knew from the other side of a burning wall.

Juniper's head snapped up. "Tell me that's a hunter."

Aldric was already on his feet, snuffing the fire with his boot, his heart going cold and clear as well-water. "It's a sweep pattern," he said. "Three riders, closing from the north. They found the courier's body." He looked at Juniper, and Juniper looked at him, and whatever either of them had been planning to do alone suddenly wasn't an option anymore.

"So," Juniper said, rolling the map with shaking hands. "About that alliance."

"I know a tunnel," they both said at once—and then stared at each other, and despite everything, despite the horns and the dark and the forty years of buried war closing in around them, Aldric laughed.

"Lead on, Juniper Crane," he said. "I'll try to keep the boot quiet."
