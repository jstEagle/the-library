## 7. Terms of Passage

The knight made tea the way he did everything else: with a formality that suggested somewhere, at some point, there had been rules about it.

Juniper watched from her corner of the ruined shepherd's hut as Ser Aldric Vane filled a dented tin kettle from his waterskin, set it over a fire no larger than a cupped hand, and waited for it to boil with the patience of a man who had never once in his life been late for anything. He'd produced two cups from his pack. Actual cups. Ceramic, chipped, but cups. Who carried cups across the Thornfell mountains? Who carried *two*?

"You're staring," he said, without looking up.

"I'm assessing."

"Ah." He poured water over a twist of dried blackleaf—good blackleaf, too, not the sawdust smugglers drank—and handed one cup toward her handle-first, like a squire presenting a sword. "And what does your assessment conclude?"

"That you're either the most dangerous man on this mountain or the saddest."

Aldric considered this. He was a big man gone slightly soft at the edges, the way old armor goes soft, with grey coming into his beard and a scar that split one eyebrow into two tufts that never quite agreed with each other. When he smiled—which was rare, and seemed to surprise him more than anyone—it rearranged his whole face into something almost kind.

"Possibly both," he said.

Juniper took the cup because refusing tea in the Thornfells in autumn was how people died stupidly, and she had sworn years ago to die of nothing stupider than pride. The tea was good. She hated that it was good.

* * *

They had come to their arrangement the way rivers come to the sea: slowly, with a great deal of grinding against stone.

It had started an hour after she'd caught him following her—or rather, an hour after he'd stopped pretending he wasn't following her, which was somehow worse. She'd put three ridges between them using tricks Maren had taught her before everything went wrong, doubling back through a scree field where her boots left prints only a tracker would read, and when she finally turned to confront him, he was already sitting on a rock eating bread, waiting for her to finish.

"You knew I'd circle back," she'd said.

"You're a smuggler's runner. You know these roads better than any road knows itself. But you also can't resist finding out if someone's still behind you." He'd offered her the bread. "Curiosity. It'll get you killed someday."

"It's gotten me out of worse than you."

"I doubt that," he'd said mildly, "but I admire the confidence," and that, more than anything, was what had kept her from running. Not the sword at his hip—he wore it like furniture, unremarkable—but the fact that he didn't reach for it, didn't posture, didn't do any of the thousand things men with swords usually did. He just sat there, eating bread, looking at her like she was a problem he intended to solve politely.

Then he'd said, "Show me the map," and everything had gotten complicated again.

* * *

Now, in the hut, with the tea steaming between them, they circled the same ground for the fourth time.

"You want me to hand it over," Juniper said. "To you. A man I met yesterday. A man whose entire order—" she gestured vaguely with her cup, "—whatever a *Vigil* is, exactly—was destroyed for knowing things. Which means everyone who ever knew those things is dead, which means the smart move, Aldric, the *obvious* move, is to stay very far away from you."

"And yet here you are, drinking my tea."

"Your tea is excellent and I'm a coward about cold mornings. That proves nothing."

He huffed something that might have been a laugh. Outside, the wind worried at the hut's missing door, and somewhere up-slope a rock came loose and clattered down the mountainside, and both of them flinched—not at the sound, but at what the sound might be covering. They were both listening for riders. They would be listening for riders for a long time.

"The map stays with me," Juniper said. "That's not negotiable. It's mine. I carried it off a dying man; that makes it mine by every law that matters out here."

"Salvage law," Aldric agreed. "I know it. I also know that the Ashen Order doesn't care whose map it is. They'll burn the body and take the paper and ask questions of whoever's holding it last." He set his cup down with a small, precise click. "You've seen them work. In the valley."

She had. Two days ago, from a hollow beneath a fallen pine, she'd watched grey-cloaked riders move through the low country like ink through water, stopping at every farmstead, every waystation, asking their quiet questions. She'd heard the word pass between two of them—*heartstone*—spoken the way people speak of plagues or gods. And she'd understood, with a cold settling into her bones that hadn't left since, that the courier's blood on her hands had signed her name to something much larger than a smuggling run.

"They asked after a courier," she said carefully. "Not after me."

"They will. The courier's trail ends where you found him. From there it's arithmetic." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the firelight caught his face and she saw past the tired ex-knight to whatever had been underneath—the man who'd once believed in something strongly enough to wear its colors. "Juniper. I'm not asking you to trust me. Trust is for people with time. I'm asking you to make a cold calculation."

"Fine. Calculate with me, then. Why should I keep *you* instead of losing you in the next valley?"

"Because you can't lose me in the next valley."

"I'm very good at losing people."

"I don't doubt it. But I spent eleven years hunting couriers and runners and smugglers for the Crown before my order fell. I know how you think, because I used to think it. Every trick you know, I've either used or caught someone using." He said it without boast, which somehow made it worse. "If we're enemies, you'll spend so much attention watching your back trail that you'll miss the Order coming down the front of it. If we're allies—" he spread his hands, "—you spend nothing. One pair of eyes, pointed the right way."

"And what do *you* get? Besides my sparkling company."

"The map reaches the Glass Steppe. I need it to reach the Glass Steppe. Whatever happens after—" His jaw worked. "What happens after is why my brothers are dead. I'd like to see it finished. Even if all I get to do is stand there at the end."

There it was again, that thing under his voice. Grief with a job to do. Juniper recognized it because she kept her own locked in a box in the back of her skull, and she knew the sound of someone else's rattling.

* * *

They negotiated through the afternoon, and it went roughly as negotiations between two proud, frightened people go: badly, then worse, then suddenly, surprisingly, well.

His first offer was insulting. He would guard the map, carry the map, and consult her on route-finding "as needed." She laughed until she coughed.

Her counter-offer was equally insulting. He could walk behind her at a distance of no less than twenty paces and speak only when spoken to. He accepted it so readily she immediately suspected a trap and withdrew it.

By the third round they'd worn each other down into something honest.

"Here's what I have," Juniper said, pacing the width of the hut—four strides, turn, four strides back. Old habit. Maren used to say she thought with her feet. "I have every smuggler's road through the Thornfells and half the ones through the Lowmarch. I have ways through mountains that don't exist on any map the Order owns, because the maps live in heads, and the heads mostly belong to people who owe me or owe Maren Crane, and—" she stumbled slightly on the name, recovered, "—and that's worth more than steel. Steel they have. Roads they don't."

"Agreed. And here's what I have." Aldric counted on thick fingers. "I can fight six men and reasonably expect to walk away. I can read High Ashen script, which the map uses in three places, and which you cannot. I know what the heartstone is—not the stories, the *actual* thing—and knowing may keep us alive at least twice before the Steppe. And I have no one left to betray you to."

That last one landed strangely. It wasn't self-pity, exactly. It was offered flat, like a coin placed on a table.

"No one?" she said, before she could stop herself.

"My order held nine houses across the Marches. There are four of us now that I know of. One's gone mad in the Saltmarshes. One I'd trust as far as I could throw his horse. And one—" the pause was small, but she felt it, "—one I haven't been able to find. So yes. No one."

Juniper stopped pacing. She looked at this large, careful, ruined man sitting in a shepherd's hut drinking tea out of ceramic cups he'd carried across a mountain range, and she thought: *he's as alone as I am, he's just worse at pretending otherwise.*

The thought annoyed her intensely.

"Terms," she said briskly, before the annoyance could turn into anything softer. "One. The map stays on my body. Wrapped, hidden, always. You guard *me*, not the paper. If I die, it's yours, and may whatever gods you abandoned have mercy on you."

"Accepted. Two: I fight first. You run second. If it comes to blades, your job is the map and the road, not standing beside me getting heroic."

"I'm not heroic."

"Thank the gods. Accepted?"

"Accepted. Three: no lying. Not 'no lies that matter'—no lies. If you don't want to answer something, say 'I won't answer,' and I'll hate it, but I'll take it. But if I catch you telling me a comfortable falsehood, the pact's done and I vanish, and you can explain to the Order how you lost the map."

Something flickered across his face at that—quick, there and gone. Guilt, maybe. Or the memory of guilt. Everyone she'd ever met had some.

"Accepted," he said. "Same rule binds you."

"Obviously. Four." She hesitated. This one she'd been turning over since morning, and she still wasn't sure why she wanted it, only that she did. "We eat together. Both meals. Nobody eats walking watch alone. It's—" she waved a hand, embarrassed, "—it's practical. Morale. Smugglers' wisdom."

Aldric looked at her for a long moment, and she braced herself for mockery, for that knightly amusement that treated small kindnesses like curiosities in a cabinet.

Instead he nodded, slow and serious, as if she'd proposed a battle formation. "Shared meals build shared habits. Shared habits build trust faster than oaths do. My order's founder wrote that." A pause. "He also died in a ditch, so weigh it accordingly."

"He sounds like he'd have gotten along with my mentor."

"Maren Crane." He said the name gently, watching her. "You mentioned her yesterday. Then went white and changed the subject."

"I did no such thing."

"You did exactly that. I won't answer whether I know the name, per our terms—I'll simply note that I noticed." He refilled her cup before she could refuse, which was its own small act of war. "When you want to talk about it, I'm told I listen adequately."

"I don't want to talk about it."

"Noted."

* * *

They sealed it without ceremony, because neither of them trusted ceremonies. No oaths, no clasped forearms, none of the theater Aldric clearly knew by heart and Juniper distrusted on principle. Instead he tore the last of his bread in half and gave her the bigger piece, and she ate hers, and he ate his, and that was apparently that.

"We should shake on it," she said, mostly to be contrary.

"We ate on it. Better." He gathered the cups, washed them in a splash of snowmelt with the same maddening thoroughness he brought to everything, and packed them away. "Though if it comforts you, the old Vigil form for a pact of passage was to exchange weapons. Each carries the other's blade until the journey's done. So that betrayal requires disarming yourself first."

"Huh." Juniper considered her knife, worn smooth at the grip from eight years of daily use. "That's actually clever."

"It's why they destroyed us. Cleverness frightens institutions." He stood, rolled his shoulders until something cracked, and glanced at the darkening doorway. "We move at first light. There's a valley south of here called Hollowmere—an old mining cut, mostly worked out. The Order swept it two seasons ago and hasn't returned, which means it's due. We cross it before they think to come back."

"A mined cut. Wonderful. Nothing bad has ever happened in an abandoned mine."

"Sarcasm noted."

"Recorded and archived."

For a moment—one strange, suspended moment—they were almost easy with each other, two tired people trading dry remarks in a firelit room while the world outside sharpened its knives. Juniper caught herself almost smiling and turned it into a scowl on principle.

But as she settled onto her bedroll near the door—her spot, nearest the exit, always—she found her thoughts circling back to the map wrapped against her ribs, and to the way the ink had moved when she'd unwrapped it alone in the waystation two nights ago. Crawling. Reaching. Like a compass needle made of words, straining toward a place called the Glass Steppe.

*Aldric says he knows what the heartstone really is,* she thought. *Tomorrow I make him prove it.*

And beneath that thought, quieter, one she didn't examine too closely: *he listened when I said I don't want to talk about Maren. He just... noted it. Nobody's ever just noted it.*

Grudging respect, she decided, was a lot like a splinter. Easy to ignore right up until it wasn't.

* * *

She woke once in the black hours to the sound of Aldric moving—not loudly, never loudly, but enough. She cracked one eye and saw him standing at the broken doorway, silhouetted against a sky full of hard stars, one hand resting on his sword hilt, watching the southern slopes.

"Anything?" she murmured.

"Wind," he said. "Only wind." A pause. "Sleep, Juniper. I have the watch."

And the strange thing—the thing she'd chew on for miles afterward—was that she believed him, and believing him, she slept. Deeply. For the first time since a dying stranger had pressed a bloodstained map into her hands and whispered *the Steppe remembers.*

* * *

First light came grey and thin. They struck camp with the wordless efficiency of two people who had already learned each other's rhythms without meaning to: she scattered the fire-ash, he buried the tea leaves, and neither commented on how naturally the division of labor had arrived.

Hollowmere opened before them an hour later—a long scar of a valley where the mountain had been gutted for copper a century ago and left to rot. Collapsed adits gaped along the walls like missing teeth. Rusting cart rails ran into darkness. The wind moving through the cut made a low, moaning note, the mountain breathing through its wounds.

"Cheerful place," Juniper said.

"Mines are honest," Aldric replied. "They show you exactly what was taken and by whom."

They were halfway across the valley floor, picking their way along a shelf above a flooded pit, when Juniper heard it.

At first she took it for wind again—that same moaning note, rising and falling. But wind didn't hiccup. Wind didn't break, mid-wail, into something smaller. Something thinner.

Aldric had stopped too. His hand was on his sword now, but his head was tilted, listening, and she watched the soldier fall away from his face and leave something else behind.

"That's not wind," he said quietly.

"No," Juniper agreed. Her heart had begun doing something complicated in her chest. "That's a child."

The sound drifted from the mouth of a collapsed shaft on the valley's far side—a black opening choked with fallen timber, its entrance half-hidden behind a curtain of dead ivy. And as they drew closer, the crying faded, replaced by another sound that stopped Juniper's breath entirely:

Humming. A small voice, singing to itself in the dark, a tuneless little melody that rose and fell—and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, coming from deep inside the mine, a soft golden light pulsed.

Once. Twice. Keeping time with the song.

Aldric and Juniper exchanged a look over the dark mouth of the shaft—their first truly shared look, pact or no pact—and the light in the depths of Hollowmere pulsed again, patient as a heartbeat, as if something down there had been waiting a very long time to be found.
