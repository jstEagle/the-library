## 8. The Child in the Hollow

Ser Aldric Vane had learned, over eleven years of disgrace, to read weather in the behavior of birds. The swallows had gone quiet an hour past, and now they flew low and hard for the treeline, which meant either rain or something worse walking up the valley behind them.

He chose to believe it was rain. A man could only be hunted by so many things before he started seeing hunters in every shadow, and Aldric had promised himself—somewhere around the third year of exile, drunk on cheap plum wine in a river town whose name he'd never bothered to learn—that he would stop flinching at ghosts.

The map made that promise difficult.

It rode against his chest now, wrapped in oilcloth inside his breastplate where Juniper couldn't see him touch it. She thought she was being clever, letting him carry it. He suspected she thought she was testing him. Both of them were right, probably. That was the trouble with alliances built out of mutual suspicion: everyone got to feel clever and no one got to sleep well.

"Mine," Juniper said, without looking up from the map's ordinary cousin—the honest, non-crawling road map she kept folded in her satchel like a sensible person. "There's a mine. Old silver workings, maybe two miles east of here. If those riders are sweeping the valley, we'll want stone over our heads."

"You know a collapsed mine," Aldric said, "in mountains you claim you've never walked."

"I said I've never walked *these* mountains." She tucked a strand of dark hair behind her ear and gave him the look she reserved for his slower moments, the one that managed to be patient and contemptuous at once, which he hadn't known was possible before meeting her. "Collapsed mines collapse the same everywhere. Smugglers' tunnels run under half of them. Somebody dug in, somebody dug out, somebody left the middle to fall. You just have to know which somebody."

"And you're which somebody?"

"The one who's still alive." She shouldered her pack. "Come on, knight. Rain's coming, and whatever else is coming with it."

---

The mine mouth was exactly as ugly as she'd promised—a black wound in the hillside, half-swallowed by scree and bramble, with a rotted timber frame leaning over it like a drunkard against a doorpost. Someone had chalked a smuggler's mark on the left jamb: three lines and a circle, old and faded but legible if you knew the hand.

Aldric didn't know the hand. But Juniper touched it with two fingers the way another woman might touch a grave marker, and something in her face went soft and complicated, and he decided not to ask. They'd been traveling together six days. There was a whole economy of not-asking between them now, carefully balanced, mutually beneficial.

"Air's moving," she said, crouching at the entrance. "Feel that? Cold coming out means cold going in somewhere. There's a back way."

"Or it means something large is breathing in there."

"Everything's breathing if you're dramatic about it." But she paused, head cocked, listening the way she listened—with her whole body, like a cat deciding whether the rustle was mouse or owl. Then, quieter: "There's water. And—" She frowned. "Something else."

"Bandits?"

"Bandits don't worry me." She said it lightly, but her hand had gone to the knife at her hip. "It's more like... humming. Faint. You hear anything?"

He listened. Wind in the bramble. Distant thunder, or the Order's drums, indistinguishable at this range. And beneath it—no. Nothing. "No."

"Huh." She stared into the dark a moment longer than comfort allowed. "Well. In we go."

*In we go,* Aldric thought, following her into the black. Six days ago he'd been a man with nothing left to lose, which is a kind of freedom, if a bitter one. Now he had a map that wanted to be found, a companion who lied beautifully about small things, and enemies on both sides of a war he'd already lost once. He had things to lose again. It was remarkable how quickly a man could relearn fear.

---

The tunnel narrowed, then opened into a chamber where the ceiling had come down in some long-ago year—great slabs of rock tumbled like dropped crockery, dust lying thick and undisturbed except for one set of small footprints leading deeper.

Juniper saw them at the same moment he did.

They both stopped. Neither spoke. The footprints were bare feet, child-sized, pressed sharp in the fine grey silt. Fresh. Hours old, perhaps less.

"Could be a miner's kid," Aldric said, because someone had to say something stupid and hopeful.

"Miners left these workings twenty years ago. My mark-book says so." Juniper knelt, didn't touch the prints, just hovered her hand above them. "Small. Light stride. Walking, not running—but careful. Look how she steps around the loose stones. This is a child who knows how to be quiet."

"Then why leave prints at all?"

"Because even quiet children get tired," Juniper said, and there was something in her voice he hadn't heard before—not softness exactly, but the place where softness had been boarded over. "Because eventually you stop caring who finds you."

She stood. "We follow."

"We don't know what's down there."

"No," she agreed. "But we know what's up top, and it wears grey." She was already moving. "Besides. Nobody leaves a child in the dark. Not even us."

*Not even us.* Aldric turned the phrase over as he followed her down through the fallen dark, and found, to his mild irritation, that it had lodged somewhere under his ribs and refused to dislodge.

---

The humming grew as they descended.

He still couldn't have sworn to hearing it. It lived below sound, in the register of teeth and bone—a faint pressure, rhythmic, patient. But the map felt it too. Through the oilcloth, through his breastplate, through the linen shirt he'd worn for six days because neither of them had planned for a long campaign, the map warmed against his chest like a sleeping animal waking. By the time they reached the second chamber, it was almost hot enough to notice, and Juniper had stopped pretending she wasn't staring at him.

"It's doing that thing again," she whispered.

"What thing?"

"The crawling thing. The *wanting* thing." Her eyes were wide in the gloom. "Aldric. It's pulling toward something down here."

"Maps can't want."

"This one can. I've watched it try for four days." She swallowed. "I was hoping you'd tell me I imagined it."

"I was hoping you'd tell me *you* imagined it," he said. "Between us we've now confirmed nothing, and we're standing in a haunted mine arguing about it."

That earned him a ghost of a laugh—her first real one since the bridge, three days back—and he found himself absurdly pleased by it, the way a man is pleased by sunlight after a week of rain, and equally annoyed at himself for the pleasure.

Then the light came.

It seeped around the edge of a fallen slab at the chamber's far end: pale gold, gentle, pulsing slow as breath. Not torchlight. Not any fire Aldric had ever seen. It moved like something alive, and the map against his chest surged so hot he gasped and clapped a hand to his breastplate.

Juniper spun. "What? What is it?"

"It's the map. It's—" He fumbled the oilcloth bundle out, and even wrapped, even double-wrapped, the glow bled through the seams, ink-gold, urgent. "It knows something in there."

"Or something in there knows *it*," Juniper breathed.

They looked at each other across six days of mutual suspicion, and for once neither of them had a clever remark. Then Juniper drew her knife, nodded at the slab, and said, "Together?"

"Together."

---

The child sat in a hollow beneath the slab, in a nest of sacking and old rope, and she was glowing.

Not blazing. Not burning. Glowing the way a coal glows hours after the fire's died—faint gold light rising off her skin in slow waves, brightest at her hands and throat, dimming toward her bare, filthy feet. She was perhaps seven years old, thin as a winter branch, with a mop of brown hair gone to knots and eyes that were open and fixed on them with a terrible, exhausted calm. The calm of someone who has cried herself empty and gone somewhere past crying.

Around her, scratched into the stone floor with a pebble or a nail, were drawings. Hundreds of them. Spirals, mostly. Spirals within spirals, radiating outward from where she sat, covering every inch of reachable stone, as though she'd spent days—or weeks—drawing the same shape over and over because something inside her demanded it and she didn't know how to refuse.

"Oh," Juniper said softly. "Oh, sweetheart."

The girl didn't flinch. Didn't speak. Just watched them, glowing, while the map in Aldric's hands pulled so hard toward her it nearly leapt from his grip.

He knelt. Slowly, the way you kneel near a spooked horse, palms open, armor clinking despite his best efforts. Up close he could see the details the glow had hidden: cracked lips, the fine tremor in her small hands, the way her ribs showed through a dress that might once have been blue. How long since she'd eaten? How long since anyone had—

"Hello," he said. His voice came out rougher than intended. He tried again. "Hello. We're not going to hurt you. My name is Aldric. This is Juniper. We heard the humming and we came to see."

Nothing. The glow pulsed. Somewhere in the walls, water dripped its slow count.

"She's heartstone-touched," Juniper whispered. She'd gone very pale. "Aldric, look at her. Look at the drawings. That's the Steppe. That's the pattern on the map—I've seen it in the margins, the spirals, the—she's been drawing it in her sleep, she doesn't even know what it is—"

"How could a child—"

"I don't know. Born near a shard, maybe. Found one. Fell into it, I don't *know*." Juniper pressed the heels of her hands to her eyes for a moment, and when she lowered them she was all business again, but her voice shook underneath. "The Order will kill her. You understand that. Not capture. Kill. A living piece of the stone walking around—that's worse than the map, that's the map with a heartbeat—"

"Juniper." He said her name quietly, and to his surprise she stopped. "You're frightening her, and she can't even hear you yet. One thing at a time."

For a moment she looked at him with an expression he couldn't read—something between gratitude and resentment, as if tenderness from him were an accusation. Then she nodded, crouched down beside him, and did the thing he would think about for years afterward, though he didn't know it yet.

She started humming.

Low, tuneless, matching the rhythm of the girl's glow. Pulse for pulse. Not a song, exactly—just a sound, steady and warm, the sound a body makes when it isn't afraid. The girl's eyes flickered. The glow stuttered, brightened, steadied.

"There you are," Juniper murmured. "There you are. That's it. We're just noise, see? Just two more noises in the dark. Nothing special about us."

The girl's lips moved. No sound came out. Then, barely, cracked and rusty as an unused hinge:

"...warm."

"Yes," Juniper said. "Warm. Are you warm? Are you hungry?"

The girl considered this with the gravity of a judge. "Hungry," she ruled.

"Well," said Juniper, and her voice did something complicated, "we can fix hungry. Hungry is fixable." She was already digging in her pack—hardtack, dried apples, the honey-cakes she'd bought in the last village and pretended she hadn't bought for herself. "What's your name, love?"

Silence. The girl took the honey-cake with both hands, the way small children take sacred things, and ate it in tiny, ferocious bites. The glow dimmed slightly as she ate, Aldric noticed—as if feeding the body let the other thing rest.

"Wren," she said at last. Around the cake. "Wren."

"Wren." Juniper tested it. Nodded, as if confirming a price. "Good name. Bird names are lucky."

"They're small," Wren said.

"They're survivors," Juniper said. "Small things survive everything. Ask any wren."

And Wren—glowing, starving, orphaned in a hole in a mountain, surrounded by spirals she'd scratched into stone with a nail—smiled. It transformed her face entirely, the way a lamp transforms a room, and Aldric felt something crack in his chest that had been sealed for eleven years.

He looked away, blinked hard at the tunnel wall, and told himself it was dust.

---

They made camp in the hollow, because night had fallen outside and the Order's fires were visible through the mine mouth, orange stars strung along the valley floor. Too many to slip past. Here, at least, the stone was thick, and the humming—Wren's humming, they realized; she hummed constantly, unconsciously, the stone's pulse given a child's breath—seemed to confuse pursuit rather than invite it. Juniper theorized the resonance masked them. Aldric theorized they were dead either way and preferred not to examine it.

Wren slept between them, which nobody had planned. She had simply, after eating, leaned sideways until she tipped against Juniper's shoulder, and Juniper had frozen like a man holding a live grenade, and then—slowly, with the care of someone defusing it—put her arm around the child and stayed exactly, precisely, motionlessly still for two hours.

Now Wren lay curled against Juniper's side, glowing faintly in her sleep, one small fist closed around a fold of Juniper's sleeve. Juniper sat rigid, staring at the far wall, clearly terrified that breathing wrong would wake her.

"You could lie down," Aldric offered.

"If I move, she wakes."

"If you don't move, your arm dies."

"Then my arm dies." A pause. "It's a good arm. It's had a full life."

Aldric laughed—actually laughed, a short, rusty sound that surprised them both. Wren stirred; they froze; she settled; they exhaled.

"She'll slow us down," Juniper said quietly, after a while. Not cruelly. Testing the thought aloud, weighing it honestly, which was more than most people would do. "A child. On smuggler roads, with the Order behind us and gods know what ahead. She can't march twenty miles a day. She can't climb the Weeping Pass. She'll cry when she's frightened, and she'll be frightened often, and her crying will carry, and—"

"You've thought this through."

"I've thought everything through. It's what I do instead of sleeping." She looked down at the sleeping weight against her side. "So tell me why I'm not leaving at first light."

Aldric considered the question properly, because she deserved a proper answer and because he needed to hear it himself.

"Eleven years ago," he said, "my order found a village burning. Plague quarantine, the crown called it. It wasn't plague. We carried out forty-one people. Forty-one, and the order was destroyed for it—every knight attainted, every record burned, our grandmaster hanged from his own gate. I was the youngest. I ran." He watched the glow rise and fall on Wren's cheek. "For eleven years I told myself I'd learned something. Caution. Discretion. The wisdom of keeping your head down. And then a dying courier handed you a map, and I looked at it, and do you know what I felt?"

"Fear."

"Greed," he said. "Terrible, shameful greed. Because I thought—if I'm the one who carries it, I matter again. If I return it, or sell it, or destroy it, I get to be Ser Aldric Vane again, with a banner and a name." He shook his head slowly. "And then we found a glowing child in a hole in the ground, and she smiled at you, and I understood that I have been carrying the wrong thing for eleven years. It was never the map. It was the idea that I could put myself back together by grabbing hold of something big enough." He looked at Juniper. "You can't. I know that now. You put yourself back together by picking up what's small and broken and in front of you, and carrying *that*, and letting it change you."

Juniper was silent a long moment. The drip of water counted the dark.

"That's the most words you've said since the bridge," she said finally.

"Don't get used to it."

"Too late." But she said it softly, and her hand—carefully, so carefully, so as not to wake the child—came to rest briefly on his forearm. Knight's forearm, scarred, disgraced, suddenly worth something. "We keep her," she said. It wasn't a question anymore.

"We keep her."

"And when the Order comes—"

"Then they find out what a knight without a banner is still willing to do."

Above them, through a crack in the mountain, the first rain arrived, drumming soft on the stone. Wren sighed in her sleep and burrowed closer, and the glow around her flared once—bright, brief, joyful, like a held breath released—and in that flare, Aldric saw the map's ink shift through the oilcloth, spiraling, reaching, *recognizing*.

And far off, down the valley, a hunting horn sounded. Once. Twice.

Then silence, which was worse.
