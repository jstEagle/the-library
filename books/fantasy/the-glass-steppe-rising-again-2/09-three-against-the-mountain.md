## 9. Three Against the Mountain

The trouble with saving people, Juniper had learned in her twenty-three years of mostly failing to do it, was that they kept following you around afterward.

Case in point: Ser Aldric Vane, disgraced knight, currently crouched at the mouth of a collapsed mine shaft arguing with a seven-year-old about whether glowworms counted as pets.

"They're not *pets*, Wren. They're wild creatures that happen to emit light."

"So's Mama."

Juniper nearly dropped the rope she was coiling. "I am not—"

"She means you glow when you're near the map," Aldric said, with the maddening calm of a man who had never once been embarrassed by anything, including his own hair, which was doing something heroic and stupid in the mountain wind.

"I don't *glow*."

"You did. Last night. Around the edges." Wren held up her own hands, which did faintly shimmer, like moonlight caught in milk. "It's very pretty. You shouldn't be ashamed."

"I'm not ashamed, I'm—" Juniper gave up on the rope and tied it off with more force than necessary. "I'm a smuggler. Smugglers don't glow. Glowing is how you get caught."

"Then it's lucky," Aldric observed, "that you know so many ways not to get caught."

It was meant as a compliment. It landed like one, which was worse, because Juniper had spent six days building walls against exactly this kind of thing—the small approvals, the shared meals, the way Wren had started sleeping curled against Juniper's pack instead of Aldric's cloak, as though a decision had been made somewhere above all their heads without consulting any of them.

They were three days out of Maren's burned waystation. Three days since Juniper had watched smoke rise from the only home she'd ever run away from, carrying the woman she'd run away *to*. She hadn't cried yet. She was saving it for a convenient moment, perhaps never.

"We should move," she said. "We've sat too long. The mine's a landmark—if anyone's tracking us, this is where they'll look first."

"The Order doesn't know these mountains," Aldric said.

"The Order has money, and money buys guides." Juniper shouldered her pack. "And we have a child who lights up like a festival lantern whenever the map gets excited. We are not subtle, Vane. We are a traveling bonfire."

Wren looked delighted by this. Aldric had the grace to wince.

They moved north along the old mine road, single file, Juniper leading. The Thornfells rose around them in grey shoulders, pine-dark and indifferent. Somewhere behind them, past two ridges and a frozen waterfall, the Ashen Order was sweeping the valley for a courier who no longer needed finding—which meant they were sweeping it for whoever had what the courier carried. Which meant, with the particular arithmetic of misfortune that governed Juniper's life, they were sweeping it for her.

She'd known it would come to this eventually. Maps that crawled were maps that mattered, and things that mattered drew men in grey like blood drew crows.

What she hadn't planned for was the company.

* * *

The patrol found them at midday, in the worst possible place: a bare saddle between two peaks, no cover taller than a boulder, sky wide open above them like an accusation.

Juniper saw them first—a flicker of grey among the grey scree, half a mile back and closing. Four riders, maybe five, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who had never once been lost.

"Down," she breathed, and pulled Wren behind the largest boulder before the girl could ask why. Aldric folded down beside them, hand already on his sword hilt, and Juniper put her own hand over his.

"Not yet. Look at their spacing. They're not charging—they're herding. They think we're going somewhere specific." Her mind was already running the terrain the way she ran it in dreams, every gully and goat-track catalogued across years of moving other people's contraband through these exact hills. "Which means if we bolt for the obvious pass, we confirm it, and they ride us down in the open."

"Then what do we suggest?" Aldric murmured.

"We suggest nothing. We go where they can't follow." She pointed—not at the pass, but at a scar in the mountainside below them, a dark seam in the rock that most eyes would read as shadow or old burn. "There's a mine entrance under that overhang. The Kettlevein. It runs clean through the shoulder of the mountain and comes out in the birch hollow on the far side. Half a mile of walking, if you don't mind the dark."

Aldric studied the seam. "That's not on any map I've seen."

"No map worth having is." She was already uncoiling the rope. "It's also not on any Order survey, because the Kettlevein collapsed officially eleven years ago. Officially. Unofficially, my mentor paid three miners to collapse it *loudly* while leaving the main gallery intact. Best investment she ever made. Saved my life twice before I turned nineteen."

"You're telling me there's a secret tunnel through the mountain that you've known about this whole time."

"I'm telling you there are *eleven* such tunnels, and you've known about one of them for six days, so honestly, Vane, keep up."

Something happened to Aldric's face then—something that on a less dignified man would have been called a grin. "Lead on, then."

Getting to the entrance was the ugly part. They had to descend forty feet of loose scree while staying out of the riders' sightline, which meant moving when the wind moved, freezing when it stilled. Juniper went first, then lifted Wren down hand to hand; the girl came light as a bird and silent as one, which was its own small miracle. Aldric came last, heavy and controlled, kicking loose exactly one stone, which clattered down the slope with the sound of a dropped dinner service.

All four riders' heads turned.

Nobody breathed.

The wind picked up again, rattling scree across the whole face of the mountain, generous and indiscriminate, and the heads turned back toward the pass. Juniper let out a breath she'd been holding since the waystation burned.

"That," Aldric whispered, "was the loudest rock in the world."

"It does that. I've hated it personally for years." She reached the overhang and ducked beneath it into cool dark air that smelled of stone and old timber and, faintly, of Maren's pipe tobacco, because everything smelled of Maren now and probably would forever. The entrance was exactly as she remembered: a crawl of rubble narrowing to a gap you had to turn sideways to pass, braced with beams that had no business still standing.

"After me. Hands on the person ahead. Wren, love, your glow—we need it low. Can you do that?"

Wren frowned in concentration, and the light in her skin dimmed to something like a banked coal. "Like hiding under blankets," she said.

"Exactly like that."

They went in.

* * *

The Kettlevein was not, strictly speaking, pleasant.

For the first hundred yards it was hands-and-knees work through rubble, and Juniper heard Aldric's armor scrape stone more than once, each scrape followed by a muttered word that would have gotten him excommunicated twice over. Then the passage opened into the main gallery, tall enough to walk upright, and Wren's glow rose shyly around them like dawn underwater.

Old support beams marched away into the dark. Rusted cart rails ran along the floor. And everywhere, in the beam-light, the walls glittered faintly with quartz and something else—something that caught Wren's light and threw it back in slow colors, blue and green and a deep bruised violet.

"Oh," Wren said softly. "Oh, it's *pretty* in here."

"Mm. Pretty and full of deadfalls. Stay between me and Vane, and step where I step." Juniper moved to the front, one hand trailing the left wall, counting side-shafts by feel. Third branch: flooded, avoid. Fifth: collapsed in '09, avoid harder. Seventh—

Seventh was where she stopped.

Because seventh was breathing.

Not literally. But there was air movement where there shouldn't be, a faint cold current against her fingers, and cold currents in the Kettlevein meant connections, and connections meant the Order's sappers might have reopened more than the official record said. She stood very still, listening, and beneath the drip of water and the tick of settling timber she heard it: voices. Far off, echoing up some distant shaft. Boots on stone.

"Vane." Her voice came out flat. "How fast can you fight in the dark?"

He was beside her instantly, sword half-drawn. "How dark?"

"Total. There's a patrol in the lower galleries—they must have come up through the flood-shaft. We've got maybe ten minutes before they reach the junction behind us, and the only way out is forward through the long gallery, which is pitch black and rigged with rotten beams." She was already pulling strips of cloth from her pack, tearing them, working quickly. "So here's what happens. We go dark. Completely dark. No glow, no light, nothing. I lead by touch and memory. You keep one hand on Wren and one on your sword, and you do *not* draw unless I say the word, because steel catches even a spark of light and a spark is all they'd need."

"And Wren?"

Juniper crouched to the girl's level. In the dimness, Wren's eyes were enormous and steady, and it occurred to Juniper—with the sudden, sickening clarity of a trap springing—that she was about to ask a seven-year-old to be brave in the dark for her sake, and that she would have burned the whole mountain down rather than do it, except there was no other way.

"Wren. Sweetheart. I need you to hold your light all the way inside. Tighter than hide-and-seek. Can you do that for me? Even if you're scared? *Especially* if you're scared."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a much older person. "If I get scared, will you hold my hand?"

"I will not let go of your hand from this moment until we see sky. That's a promise."

"Okay." And the light simply—went. Not dimmed. *Vanished*, drawn inward like a snuffed candle, until Juniper was holding the hand of a child she couldn't see, only feel: small, warm, gripping back hard.

"Saints," Aldric whispered. "She's gone."

"She's *smart* is what she is. Now quiet. Touch only."

They went into the black.

Juniper had walked the Kettlevein maybe thirty times in her life, always with a lantern, always with Maren's voice behind her—*left at the third brace, mind the knee-cracker, trust the wall, the wall never lies.* Now Maren's voice lived only in her head, and it guided her the same as ever, patient and dry and unbearably dear. Left at the third brace. Mind the knee-cracker—she felt Aldric crack his knee anyway and heard him swallow the sound whole, which she decided to respect forever. Trust the wall.

Behind them, growing louder, the boots. A lantern-glow bloomed faintly around a bend, orange and hostile.

Juniper pressed them into a side alcove—an ore pocket, waist-deep, barely room for three—and pulled Wren against her chest and Aldric's arm across them both, and they stood in absolute darkness while the light grew, and grew, and stopped.

Voices. Close enough to count words.

"—says the courier came through Thornfell, so the cargo's still in these hills."

"And the ones carrying it?"

"Burn the waystation witch, didn't they. So: anyone traveling. Anyone at all." A pause, the wet sound of someone drinking. "General's orders say bring the map intact. Says nothing about the carriers."

Juniper's heart was a fist in her chest. Beside her, Aldric had gone very still in the way of a man holding a sword-sheath closed by will alone. Against her collarbone, Wren's breath came quick and small—and then, Juniper felt it, the glow trying to rise, fear pushing it up through the girl's skin like sap in spring, a faint silvering in the darkness that would bloom any second into a beacon—

She bent her head and did the only thing she could think of. She hummed.

It was the smuggling song, the silly one, the one Maren used to sing off-key on purpose to make her laugh—*oh the road goes over the mountain, and the mountain doesn't care*—hummed soft as a heartbeat, barely breath. Wren stilled. The glow sank. In the alcove's black, Juniper felt the girl's grip change, relax by degrees, settle into trust.

The lantern swung past. The boots moved on, deeper into the mountain, hunting a ghost in the wrong direction.

They waited a full hundred count after the light faded. Then Juniper took Wren's hand, found Aldric's sleeve, and led them up out of the dark.

* * *

The birch hollow opened around them like an answer: white trunks, late gold leaves, actual honest sky. Wren's glow came flooding back the moment they cleared the entrance, rising off her skin in joyful waves, and she spun once in the leaf-litter with her arms out, lighting the whole grove like a lamp being born.

"We did it! We did it, we *did* it—"

"We did," Juniper agreed, and then her knees informed her that adrenaline was a loan, not a gift, and she sat down hard on a fallen trunk.

Aldric lowered himself beside her, wincing at his knee. For a while neither spoke. The birches ticked and whispered. Somewhere a woodpecker hammered away at its own private war.

"You sang," he said finally.

"I hummed."

"You *sang*. In the dark. With an Order patrol ten feet away." He shook his head slowly. "Six days ago I pulled steel on you on the high road, and today I watched you hum a lullaby at a heartstone-touched child in a haunted mine, and I find I no longer remember which of those people I was supposed to be arresting."

"I wasn't arresting anyone. You were going to arrest me and then got distracted by my excellent personality."

"That too."

From across the hollow, Wren came pelting back through the leaves and climbed onto the log between them with the entitlement of a cat, and then, without warning or negotiation, burrowed herself under Juniper's arm and announced, to the general vicinity:

"I wasn't scared. Mostly. Because Mama was there."

The word went through Juniper like a splinter finding its depth.

She looked at Aldric. Aldric looked at the woodpecker with tremendous scholarly interest. Traitor.

"Wren," Juniper managed, "I'm not—you know I'm not your—"

"I know." Wren said it comfortably, the way one says *the sky is up*, and leaned her head against Juniper's ribs. "Maren said families are just people who keep choosing each other. She said you chose me the night you wouldn't leave the mine without checking every tunnel. So." A shrug, small and complete. "Mama."

Juniper opened her mouth to explain—about running, about how everyone she stood close to ended up burned or buried or betrayed, about how the safest thing she could give this child was distance—and found that every word had quietly died of embarrassment.

"*Oh,* the road goes over the mountain," she heard herself sing instead, low and rough and entirely off-key, "and the mountain doesn't care—"

Wren beamed and joined in on the second line, gloriously flat. Aldric lasted four bars before surrendering and adding a bass rumble that was, objectively, worse than either of them.

They were three verses in, laughing in a birch hollow on the wrong side of a hunted mountain, when Juniper saw it: far off across the valley, on the flank of Mount Ceren itself, a line of torches moving in perfect formation. Dozens of them. Then dozens more.

Not a patrol. A column.

Aldric stopped singing. "That's not a sweep," he said quietly. "That's an army."

And down in the valley, faint on the wind, came the sound of drums—Ashen-grey, deliberate, marching toward the one pass that led to the Glass Steppe.

Juniper gathered Wren closer and looked at the torches, and understood that whatever time they'd bought in the dark, they'd just started spending it.

"We need to move," she said. "Now. Before that column reaches the pass—because the only other road to the Steppe runs through territory I've been avoiding for six years, and the person who controls it is someone I *really* hoped never to see again."

"Who?" Aldric asked.

Juniper stood, pulling Wren up with her, and felt the map shift warm against her ribs like a living thing that had heard its name.

"General Yara Sable," she said. "And pray we find her in a recruiting mood."
