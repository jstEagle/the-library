## 10. General Sable's Offer

Yara Sable had learned long ago that the best ambushes began with tea.

Not the threat of it. Not the promise of it. Actual tea, poured steaming into tin cups, set out on a flat stone at the roadside as though she were a merchant's wife waiting on a delivery of wool rather than the most wanted woman in three provinces. People approached a cup of tea differently than they approached a drawn blade. A blade made them calculate. Tea made them sit down.

And a person sitting down was already halfway to listening.

She'd chosen the spot herself, two days ago, walking the old drover's road where it bent around the shoulder of Mount Carness before dropping toward the river flats. Good sightlines in three directions, one narrow approach from the fourth, and a stand of birches that would muffle hoofbeats and give her archers something to lean against while they got bored. Bored archers were sloppy archers. She'd given them permission to nap.

"They're coming up the switchback," said Brannic, her quartermaster, materializing out of the birches the way he always did—suddenly, apologetically, and holding a ledger he had no business carrying on a field operation. "Two adults and—" He squinted. "A child? You said two adults."

"I said a courier's map, a disgraced knight, and complications." Yara adjusted the fold of her grey-green coat so that the general's braid at the shoulder was visible but not *displayed*. There was an art to this. Too much rank and people braced for orders; too little and they wondered why they should bother talking at all. "The child is the complication. Nobody told me about her because nobody who saw her lived long enough to file a report."

"That's grim."

"That's war, Brannic." She smiled at him, because it was important to smile at the people who kept your ledgers, and because smiling was, like the tea, a kind of weapon she'd never been issued but had learned to forge anyway. "Pour when I nod."

Through the birches she watched them come: a tall man in armor gone shabby at the joints, walking like a man who still believed his body was a fortress even though the garrison had long since deserted it. Ser Aldric Vane. She knew the name the way she knew all the names of the Order's discarded—the knight who'd asked one question too many about Kellin's Ford and been stripped of banner and name for the asking. She'd drunk to his disgrace once, in a tavern in Lowmarsh, along with forty other people who'd lost family at the Ford. It occurred to her now, watching him walk, that she'd never once wondered what *he* had lost.

Beside him, smaller and quicker, moved a figure in a smuggler's patched leathers—hood back, dark hair cropped short, eyes doing what smuggler's eyes always did: counting exits, weighing purses, pricing everyone in sight. Juniper Crane. The courier's map was almost certainly under that coat, and the way those eyes flicked to the birches—once, twice, cataloguing her archers without appearing to—said the runner knew exactly what kind of trap this was.

Good. Yara distrusted people who walked into traps believing them to be picnics.

And between them, holding a hand of each, a girl of perhaps seven with a smudge of dirt on her chin and light—actual light, faint as moonrise on snow—under the skin of her forearms.

Yara felt something turn over in her chest, slow and heavy, like a millstone starting to grind.

*There you are,* she thought, and did not let it reach her face.

* * *

"General Sable." The knight stopped ten paces out, which was correct protocol for a parley and also, she noted, exactly the distance from which he could reach either of his companions if things went wrong. Old habits. "You have us outnumbered and outflanked. I assume there's a reason we're being offered tea instead of arrows."

"There's always a reason for tea, Ser Vane. The reason is that tea is delicious, and that I've been drinking pine-needle broth for eleven days and would stab a man for chamomile." She gestured at the flat stone. "Sit. All of you. The little one gets honey, if we haven't eaten it all, which Brannic may have."

"I have *not*," said Brannic, wounded, producing the honey jar from somewhere inside his ledger.

The smuggler hadn't sat. Juniper Crane stood with weight on both feet evenly, hands loose, gaze moving from Yara's face to her braid to the archers in the trees and back again—a circuit, Yara realized, that the runner was running every few heartbeats, updating the count.

"You've been following us," Crane said. "Since Glassford."

"Since before Glassford. Since the pass, if I'm honest, though my scouts lost you in the tunnels and I had to buy the trail back from a man who smelled strongly of goat." Yara poured the first cup herself and held it out. "I'm not going to insult you by pretending otherwise. You're too clever to believe it and I'm too tired to perform it."

Something flickered across the runner's face—surprise, maybe, or the particular exhaustion of someone who'd been lied to so often that honesty felt like a new kind of trap. Yara recognized it. She wore it herself most mornings.

Crane took the cup. Didn't drink it. Held it like a small hot shield.

"And the child?" Aldric asked quietly. His hand had found Wren's shoulder. "Is she part of why we're having tea?"

Yara looked at the girl. The girl looked back at her with the frank, unblinking appraisal only children and cats can manage, and then said, "Your cup has a chip in it," which was true, and which broke whatever tension the adults had been carefully constructing, because Brannic laughed and even the knight's mouth twitched.

"The child," Yara said gently, "is the reason I asked for tea instead of building a pyre. Sit down, Ser Vane. Please. I promise no one here gives an order until the pot is empty, and I promise the second thing more firmly: everything I say next, you are free to refuse."

* * *

They sat. The girl drank her honeyed tea with both hands wrapped around the cup, and the light under her skin pulsed faintly with each swallow, like a lantern breathing. Yara made herself look away from it. Made herself look at the road, the birches, the chipped cup. There would be time to look at the girl. Looking too soon, too hungrily—that was how men became monsters, she suspected. Not through cruelty. Through looking.

"My father farmed the valley below Thornfell," she began, because she had learned that stories worked best when they started in dirt. "Barley, mostly, and apples that were famous for fifty miles and worth nothing, because famous and profitable are different animals. When the Empire came through the second time, they burned the orchards—not for strategy. For punishment. Someone in our village had fed rebel scouts, which was true, because I was one of the scouts, and I was hungry, and their apples were right there."

Wren had stopped drinking. Listening.

"The Empire called it pacification. Forty-one dead, my father among them, and the survivors resettled to a labor camp in the east where the barley grows thin and the guards grow rich." Yara turned her cup slowly on her knee. "I tell you this not to make you sad. Sadness is cheap. I tell you because everything I've built since—every camp, every column, every scarred-up farm boy and mill girl carrying a pike—I built it on the belief that what happened to my father's orchard should never happen to anyone's again. And I have found, in nine years of fighting, exactly one thing in this world powerful enough to guarantee that."

"The heartstone," Aldric said. His voice was flat as a frozen pond.

"The heartstone." She met his eyes. "Yes."

"It shattered a country once already."

"It *ended* a war," Yara corrected, and heard the warmth come into her own voice, unbidden, the way it always did when she spoke of this—the one sermon she had left in her. "It ended a war that had burned for thirty years. Do you know what the histories call the years after the Shattering, Ser Vane? They call them the Quiet. No empire marched. no order purged. For a generation, the strongest artifact in the world lay buried in glass, and the world lay down beside it like a dog by a fire. That's not corruption. That's *peace*. Real peace. The kind that doesn't depend on the goodwill of whoever holds the biggest army that season."

"The Quiet ended," Crane said softly. "That's the whole point of this story, isn't it? Everything ends."

"Everything ends," Yara agreed, "which is precisely why it must be guarded while it lasts. And here is my offer, freely given and freely refused." She set down her cup and spread her hands, empty, palms up—an old soldier's gesture, showing no steel. "The heartstone belongs to the people the war broke. Not to the Ashen Order, who would burn half the world to keep it buried. Not to some king or council who'd lock it in a vault and charge admission to righteousness. To the broken. The orphaned. The resettled. The ones whose names aren't in any archive because archives are written by winners." Her voice caught, just slightly, on *orphaned*, and she let it catch, because it was true, and truth that doesn't cost the speaker anything isn't worth hearing. "Give me the map. March with me to the Steppe. And when we raise it from the glass, it will be raised by farmers and smiths and smugglers' children—not by grey cloaks, and not by me alone. I swear that on my father's orchard."

Silence. Wind in the birches. Somewhere behind her, an archer shifted, and Brannic cleared his throat in the specific key that meant *the honey is nearly gone, General.*

Wren tugged Crane's sleeve. "Mama," she whispered—not quietly enough, children never whisper quietly enough—"is she telling the truth?"

And Yara watched the smuggler's face do something complicated and painful: watch it want to say *yes*.

* * *

Later, much later, Yara would replay that moment the way she replayed every battle—looking for the hinge, the instant where the day turned. In the moment itself, she simply waited, because waiting was the other half of tea.

Crane crouched to Wren's level. Whatever was said passed between them too low to hear, but Yara saw the girl's small glowing hand press flat against the smuggler's cheek, and saw Crane close her eyes, and understood that the argument was already over before the negotiation began. Some refusals are decided by the people we love, not by us.

"You mean all of it," Crane said, standing. "That's the worst part. You actually believe it."

"Belief is the only supply line that never runs out," Yara said.

"But you'd carry it yourself, wouldn't you? If it came to it. If the column wavered, if the Order came, if carrying it was the only way to keep it safe—you'd take it up." Crane's eyes were steady and terribly kind, which was somehow worse than hatred. "You'd be the guardian. Just until things were stable. Just until the world deserved it."

Yara opened her mouth to deny it and found, to her genuine surprise, that she could not—not cleanly, not with the runner watching. The honest answer rose in her like water in a well: *yes. Of course yes. Who else? Who else has lost what I've lost and learned what I've learned?*

"I would carry it," she said finally, "if carrying it meant no one else had to bleed for it."

"There it is," Crane murmured. Almost gently. "Everyone says that. The stone only ever hears that."

Aldric rose then, slow as a drawbridge, and put himself—she noted it, filed it, admired it—squarely between Yara and the child. "The answer is no, General. With respect. And with thanks for the tea."

"No," echoed Crane, and the word came out quiet and final as a door closing. "But—" and here the smuggler glanced at Wren, at the archers, at the narrow road south, doing arithmetic with lives, "—we'll walk out of this camp alive, and you'll let us. Because you need us to believe you're better than the Order more than you need the map today. Don't you."

It wasn't a question. Yara held the runner's gaze for a long moment, feeling the strange vertigo of being *read*—thoroughly, accurately, by a person half her age with mud on her boots—and discovered that she was smiling, and that the smile hurt.

"Aye," she said. "Don't I."

She nodded once to Brannic. The archers stood down. The road south lay open, birch-shadowed and empty, and Yara Sable watched the knight, the smuggler, and the glowing child walk away from the future she was offering them, and she let them go, because she had promised, and because promises were the last currency she had that couldn't be counterfeited.

But as they passed, Wren stopped. Turned. Looked up at Yara with those grave, lamplit eyes, and said, "You shouldn't hold it. It doesn't do what you think. It just makes the loudest thing in you louder."

Then the child took Crane's hand and was gone down the road, and Yara stood alone among the cooling teacups with the wind moving through the birches, thinking: *and what is the loudest thing in me?*

She found she didn't want to answer.

"General?" Brannic, hesitant, ledger clutched to his chest. "Do we follow?"

Yara looked south a long moment. Then she rolled up her sleeves, gathered the cups, and said, in a voice that made even the napping archers wake:

"Yes. But send for Sister Ilvane first. And Brannic—" she pressed the honey jar into his hands, and her smile was warm, and her eyes were not, "—tell her to bring her blessing-chalk. We're going to need a miracle of diplomacy before this road ends."

What she did not say—what she would not say aloud for another hundred miles, not even to Brannic, not even to herself in the dark of her tent—was that she had already begun composing the blessing in her head. Something small. Something kind. Something a child would accept from a stranger's hand.

After all, she thought, folding the mapless map of the future into her breast pocket, the best ambushes begin with tea.

The next ones begin with blessings.
