## 13. Wren's Dreams

Wren knew the song was wrong before she was fully awake, the way you know porridge is burning by the smell even before your eyes open.

She was singing it herself. That was the first strange thing. The second strange thing was that she didn't know the words — not really — and yet her mouth made them anyway, small syllables like pebbles dropping into deep water, and each one landed somewhere behind her ribs and rang.

She sat up in the dark of the ruined chapel where they'd sheltered from the rain. Aldric slept sitting against a fallen pillar, sword across his knees even in sleep, which Juniper said was either very brave or very sad and possibly both. Juniper herself was curled on the far side of the cold hearth, one arm thrown over her face, breathing the careful shallow way of someone who only ever half-slept. Wren had watched her do it for weeks now. Juniper slept like a rabbit in a field. Even her dreams looked like they were checking over their shoulders.

The singing stopped when Wren stopped it. Her throat felt odd, as if someone else had been borrowing it.

*Down where the mirror drinks the sky,* something whispered at the edge of her hearing, sweet and patient as water under ice. *Down where the broken pieces lie.*

Wren pulled her blanket up to her chin and did not sing anymore.

---

In the morning she didn't tell them.

It wasn't that she meant to keep secrets. It was just that every time she opened her mouth to say *I dreamed in a voice that wasn't mine*, the words turned to porridge on her tongue, and also there were more important things happening, like Juniper burning the oats.

"You've burned them," Aldric observed from his post by the door.

"I have not burned them. They're *toasted*. There's an art to this."

"The art is smoke."

"The art is *character*." Juniper scraped the pot and divided its contents into three bowls with the fierce precision of someone who had grown up hungry and never quite believed the hunger had ended. She gave Wren the least burnt portions without appearing to, which was its own kind of magic, and Wren ate and said nothing about dreams or voices or the way her fingertips had glowed faintly green in the night, like foxfire, like the map's ink.

The map itself lay rolled in Juniper's pack, and Wren could feel it from across the room. Not hear it exactly. More like standing near a beehive: a low warm hum through the soles of her feet, up through her shins, settling somewhere in her chest where it hummed along in harmony with whatever the voice was singing.

That was the worst part, Wren thought, chasing a scrap of oat around her bowl. It wasn't scary music. It was *pretty*. If it had been horrible, growling, full of teeth, she could have hated it properly. Instead it sounded like a lullaby someone had written for a place instead of a person, and it wanted her to come home to it.

"Your hands," Juniper said suddenly.

Wren looked down. Her fingertips were glowing again, pale green light leaking between them like water through cupped palms. She shoved them under her legs.

"It does that," she mumbled.

"When did it start doing that?"

"...Sometimes."

"*Wren.*"

"That's my name," Wren agreed, because stalling was easier than answering, and she'd learned stalling from watching Juniper dodge questions about her past, which Juniper thought nobody noticed but everybody noticed.

Juniper set down her bowl. She crossed the chapel and crouched in front of Wren, and her face did the thing it always did when she was frightened and trying to look like she wasn't — went very smooth and still, like a pond pretending to be a stone. "Sweetheart. When your hands glow. Does anything else happen?"

Wren considered lying. She was honestly quite good at lying; Aldric said she had a natural talent for it and then looked sad, like he wished she hadn't. But Juniper's eyes were the grey-green of the mountains in early spring, and they were looking right at her, and lying to them felt like kicking a small animal.

"There's a song," Wren admitted. "I don't know it. But I know it." She frowned, trying to make the inside of her head make sense on the outside. "It's like — you know how you can remember a smell? Not what it is, just that you know it? It's like that but with a place. And last night I was singing it. Out loud. In my sleep." Her chin started to wobble and she hated that, she was seven-and-a-half and too old to cry about songs, but her eyes went hot anyway. "I couldn't stop."

The silence stretched. Behind them, Aldric had gone very quiet in the doorway, the particular quiet of a soldier listening for danger.

Juniper gathered Wren up into her lap, oat-bowl be hanged, and held her close. Juniper smelled like woodsmoke and pine sap and the road, which was, in Wren's professional opinion as a seven-and-a-half-year-old who had lived in three caves and two cellars, the best smell in the world.

"You're safe," Juniper said into her hair, which was what she always said, and which Wren had noticed was not actually an answer to anything, but was somehow still comforting. "Whatever it is, we'll figure it out. Together. That's the whole point of together."

"Even if I'm the problem?"

"Especially if you're the problem." Juniper's mouth did its crooked almost-smile. "Problems are my specialty. Ask anyone. Ask the entire Ashen Order, actually, they'll confirm it."

---

They walked that day, up out of the valley and along a ridge where the wind came clean off the snowfields, and Wren walked between the two of them and felt like a kettle slowly coming to boil.

By midday the humming in her chest had words again. By late afternoon the words had a direction. She kept catching her feet turning — left when the path went right, uphill when everyone else went down — as if some part of her already knew the way to the place in the song and saw no reason to pretend otherwise.

*West,* sang the hive-voice in her bones. *Past the weeping stone. Past where the river forgets its name.*

"Stop that," she muttered at her own feet.

"Talking to me?" Aldric asked. He'd taken to walking beside her more often lately. He walked like a wall that had decided to be kind — big and slow and deliberate, always positioning himself on the side nearest the drop.

"No. Talking to my feet. They're being traitors."

"Feet usually are." He considered. "My feet once marched me three days in the wrong direction because I was too proud to ask where the ford was. Feet hold grudges."

"That's not — " Wren kicked a stone. "It's the song. It knows where it wants to go and it's using me to steer." Her voice cracked on *steer*, and she scowled furiously at the horizon to hide it. "And I don't want it to use me. I'm not a cart."

"No," Aldric agreed gravely. "Carts are much less trouble."

But he'd gone watchful again, the way he did, and Wren saw him trade a look with Juniper over her head, one of those long silent adult looks that contained approximately nine arguments, none of which either of them won.

---

The dream came back that night, and this time it didn't wait politely for sleep.

She dreamed she was standing on a plain made of glass — not sharp glass, not broken-window glass, but glass like the bottom of the lake in summer, smooth and green-gold and stretching away forever under a sky with too many stars. And beneath her feet, deep, deep down, something enormous and bright was breathing.

Not alive exactly. Alive the way a fire is alive. Alive the way a promise is alive.

*You are ours,* breathed the bright thing, gently, the way you'd tell a child the tide would come back. *You were made of us. Come down where the mirror drinks the sky. Come down where the broken pieces lie. We have been waiting so long to be whole.*

"I'm not yours," Wren said in the dream, and her voice was very small on all that glass. "I'm mine. Juniper says. She says I got to pick my own name and everything."

*We know,* said the bright thing, and it sounded pleased, horribly pleased, like a spider admiring a web. *That is why you will carry us so well.*

Wren woke with her whole body glowing — not her fingertips this time but her arms, her collarbones, the thin skin over her heart, veins of green light showing through like leaves backlit by the sun. She was floating. Not high. Just an inch or two off her bedroll, her hair drifting around her head like she was underwater.

She screamed.

---

Afterward there was a lot of noise. Aldric bursting in with his sword drawn at nothing. Juniper catching her out of the air — Wren hadn't even known she was floating until Juniper's arms closed around her and pulled her down, down, back into the world where people weighed things. The fire being rebuilt. Sweet tea forced into her hands, too hot, drunk anyway.

"Tell me everything," Juniper said, kneeling in front of her, holding both her hands — her ordinary, non-glowing, thank-the-stars hands. "Everything. Don't leave bits out because you think they'll frighten us. We're already frightened. You can't make it worse."

So Wren told them. The glass plain. The stars. The breathing brightness underneath, and the words it used, and the awful pleased sound of it. *That is why you will carry us so well.*

When she finished, the silence was the bad kind, the kind with plans forming inside it.

"A compass," Aldric said quietly. "It's turning her into a compass."

"It's turning her into a *child*," Juniper snapped. "Who happens to be pointing somewhere. There's a difference."

"Not to the Order. Not to Sable. To anyone hunting the stone, she's worth more than the map now. Maps can be stolen. Maps can't walk into a vault and open it." His jaw worked. "We have to move. Somewhere it can't reach her. High ground, old ground — Maren's country has wards older than—"

"Maren," Juniper said, in a voice like a door closing, "is not an option."

"Maren is the only hedge-witch within a hundred miles who might know how to quiet a resonance bond—"

"We are *not* going to Maren."

Wren looked between them. She knew the name. Maren Crane. The mentor Juniper never talked about except in her sleep, sometimes, in a voice that made Wren's chest ache. She'd once asked Aldric about it and he'd said, *Some roads have thorns on both ends, little bird, and your Juniper has walked this one barefoot.* Which was a knightly way of saying nothing at all.

"She can help me?" Wren asked, small.

Both adults stopped arguing instantly, which showed how fast they could move when it mattered.

"It doesn't matter—" Juniper began.

"If she can help, then we should go," Wren said, and heard her own voice go stubborn, the Sable-stubborn, the voice that had gotten her extra bread twice and grounded once. "Because last night I floated, Juniper. Floating is *bad*. What happens when it's not an inch? What happens when it's the roof? What if I float away in the middle of the night and you wake up and I'm just — gone, over the trees, singing?" Her eyes filled again and this time she let them. "I don't want to be a compass. Compasses don't get to pick."

Juniper knelt there stricken, and Wren saw the exact moment the argument lost — saw it happen behind her eyes like weather changing.

"Compasses," Juniper said roughly, pulling Wren in again, "get carried. That's the whole job. Nobody carries you anywhere you don't agree to go. Do you understand me? Whatever's in that song, whatever it thinks it's promised — it doesn't get a vote. You get a vote. Me and Aldric, we get votes. That's the entire committee."

"Committees are terrible," Aldric offered from the doorway.

"This committee is armed," Juniper said, "so it's a good one."

But later, when Wren was wrapped in blankets pretending to sleep, she heard them talking in low voices by the dead fire.

"—the dreams are getting stronger. Three nights running. It found her while she was awake today, Juniper, her *feet* were navigating—"

"I heard."

"And you know what's west of here. You know what's on the western road."

A long pause. The fire crackled back to life as Juniper fed it, spitefully, one stick at a time.

"Yes," Juniper said at last. "Maren's cottage is on the western road."

Another silence, longer and colder than the mountain night.

"Then we'd better hope," Juniper said finally, "that the old woman remembers she owes me. Because if she asks for a price this time — if she asks for the map, or for *her*—" and Wren knew without looking that *her* meant Wren, "—then I will burn that debt down to the stones, Aldric, and Maren Crane can stand in the ashes of it and explain to the Ashen Order why her favorite runaway came home after all."

Wren lay in the dark, glowing faintly at the edges like a dying coal, and hummed — carefully, silently, only inside — the fragment of the song she couldn't forget.

*Down where the mirror drinks the sky.*

West. Everything they needed was west, and everything Juniper feared was west, and Wren's own bones were pulling west like a fish on a line.

She closed her eyes, and in the dark behind them the glass plain waited, patient and bright, counting the nights until she came home.
