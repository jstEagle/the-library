## 14. Maren's Price

The storm found them before Maren did.

It came down out of the western peaks like something owed money—thunder first, then sleet that fell sideways and stung like a swarm of tiny angry bees. Juniper had been through a hundred storms in the Thornfells and knew their moods the way she knew the smuggler's roads: which passes silted shut, which overhangs held, which caves were dry and which merely claimed to be.

This one was a liar of a storm. It promised an hour of fury and delivered four.

They sheltered in a hollow beneath a shelf of granite that Aldric insisted on calling "defensible" and Juniper insisted on calling "a rock," because it was a rock, and knights would knight. Wren slept between them in a nest of cloaks, her faint glow dimmed to a coal-ember shimmer, dreaming whatever heartstone dreams a heartstone-touched child dreamed. Every so often her lips moved, shaping syllables that weren't in any language Juniper knew, and every so often the map—in its oilcloth wrapping against Juniper's ribs—grew warm in answer, like a dog hearing its name called from another room.

*Stop listening to her,* Juniper thought at it. *And you stop singing to her.* Neither obeyed. Maps and children rarely did.

It was near dawn, when the sleet had thinned to a miserable drizzle, that Wren sat bolt upright and said, quite clearly, "Someone's coming."

Aldric was on his feet before his eyes opened, sword half-drawn, which was a trick Juniper had never quite gotten used to—the way he woke like a hinge snapping shut. "How many?"

Wren frowned, tilting her head as though listening to a conversation happening somewhere just behind the rain. "One. She smells like rosemary and burnt bread."

Juniper went cold all over, in a way that had nothing to do with the weather.

Only one person in the world smelled like rosemary and burnt bread. Only one person had ever burned bread for her, morning after morning, in a crooked stone house above a village called Latchford, insisting each time that the charred crust was *good for the constitution,* and only one person had ever, in the same breath, tried to sell Juniper's talent to the Ashen Order like a calf at market.

"Maren," she said.

"You know her." Aldric's voice had gone flat and careful, the voice he used when he was doing arithmetic with lives.

"She raised me." Juniper pulled her hood up. "And then she nearly un-raised me, if you take my meaning."

* * *

Maren Crane came up the trail without hurry, leaning on a blackthorn staff taller than herself, a travel cloak beaded with drizzle and a basket hooked over one arm as if this were a Sunday stroll rather than a climb into the teeth of a hunted mountain. She was smaller than Juniper remembered—or perhaps Juniper had simply grown—and her hair had gone from iron-grey to full white, but her eyes were the same: sharp, brown, and capable of looking straight through a person to the lie underneath.

She stopped ten paces off and looked at the three of them lined up against the rock. Her gaze snagged on Aldric's sword, then Wren's glow, then settled on Juniper with an expression caught somewhere between fury and relief.

"Well," Maren said. "You look terrible. Have you been eating?"

"Hello to you too," Juniper said.

"I taught you manners, or I thought I did. Apparently I taught you to vanish for six years instead, which is a different curriculum entirely." Maren set down the basket with great deliberation. It clinked. It smelled, unmistakably, of fresh bread—not burnt, for once—and dried plums and something honeyed. Juniper hated how much her stomach noticed. "I've been walking for two days. Do you know what these mountains do to old knees? Do you know what the *rain* does? And you stand there under your rock making faces at me."

"You sold me," Juniper said quietly. The words came out steadier than she felt. "Or you tried to. To the Order. So forgive me if the welcome's a little frosty. We seem to have weather for it."

Something flickered across Maren's face—hurt, quickly folded away and hidden under the briskness, the way she'd always hidden everything. "Yes. Well. There's a longer story there than the one you've been telling yourself, girl, but I don't expect you'll want it standing in the wet." She looked past Juniper, at Wren, who had crept forward despite Aldric's warning hand, drawn like a moth to the smell of bread and the older, stranger pull of something else. "Hello, little one. What's your name?"

"Wren," said Wren. Then, with the terrifying honesty of children: "Are you Juniper's mama?"

The silence that followed had texture. Juniper could have crawled inside the rock.

"No," Maren said at last, and her voice did something complicated. "I'm her—well. I'm the woman who found her stealing apples off my sill when she was nine years old and too skinny to climb back down. I took her in. Fed her. Taught her hedge-magic and road-craft and how to gut a fish without gagging." A pause. "The fish-gutting she learned faster than the gratitude."

"I was twelve," Juniper muttered.

"And I'm still waiting for the rest of it." Maren picked up her basket again. "There's a place, half a day south—a shepherd's bothy, abandoned since the plague winter. Dry walls, good hearth, no neighbors worth mentioning. You're being hunted by men in grey; I can ward the door so thoroughly they'd walk past it twice and wave. I'm offering shelter. Food. A roof that doesn't argue."

Aldric stepped forward. "In exchange for?"

Maren looked at him with the weary patience of a woman who had dealt with soldiers before and expected little. "Straight to terms. Fine. In exchange for the map, knight. That's my price. The map comes with me, and the three of you come with it."

* * *

The bothy was exactly as advertised: low, dry, smelling of old smoke and sheep, with a hearth that drew well once Maren coaxed it. She fed them—bread that was miraculously unburnt, stew from a packet of dried things that should not have tasted as good as it did, tea brewed from pine needles and honey—and while they ate, she talked, filling the room with small talk the way she'd always filled silence: complaints about the roads, gossip about Latchford, a long digression about a neighbor's goat that Juniper suspected was mostly fiction.

But Juniper watched her hands. Hedge-witch hands, knotted and quick, and Juniper had spent nine formative years learning what those hands did when they lied. They didn't. They *tidied.* Maren's fingers found work whenever truth got uncomfortable—straightening the fire, folding cloth, smoothing the table as though it had wrinkles.

Right now they were smoothing the table a great deal.

"So," Juniper said finally, when Wren had gone glassy-eyed and heavy-headed by the fire, and Aldric had positioned himself by the door with the studied casualness of a man pretending not to guard it. "The debt."

Maren's hands stopped.

"How did you—" She sighed. "Of course. You watched me like a hawk even as a child. Worst habit I ever failed to break you of." She lowered herself onto the bench across the table, and for the first time since finding them, she looked genuinely old. "Yes. The debt. You deserve the whole of it, and you'll hate me after, and I'd rather you heard it from me than pieced it together wrong."

"I already know the shape of it. You tried to sell me."

"I *did* sell you," Maren said. "That's the part you don't know. Not to the Order—to a broker who served them. Sixteen years ago, when you were fifteen and your talent was blooming like a weed in a cellar. He paid me forty crowns and a promise of protection, and I took both, and I told myself I was saving you from a harder fate." Her jaw tightened. "Then I walked home, and you were gone, and it took me a full season to understand what I'd actually done."

Juniper's tea had gone cold in her hands. She drank it anyway, because her body wanted something to do. "You told me once you didn't remember why I left."

"I lied," Maren said. "I'm good at it. It's why the Order kept me alive as long as they did." She turned her cup slowly, around and around. "Here is the rest, then. Before you—I wasn't just a hedge-witch. I was the Order's warden for this whole stretch of Thornfell. Twenty years. I found talented children and brought them in. I told myself it was rescue—they'd have starved or been conscripted otherwise—but it was collection, girl. I was a collector. And when I finally couldn't stomach it anymore and walked away, they let me go on one condition: that I owe them. A debt of silence, called in whenever they chose. For sixteen years they never chose. And now—" she spread her hands, helpless, hateful— "now they've chosen. Because of you. Because of that map in your shirt and the glowing child at my fire."

"They know about Wren?" Aldric asked sharply.

"They know someone heartstone-touched crossed the pass. They know a courier died carrying something important. They do *not* know where you are—which is the only coin I have left, and I mean to spend it buying your safety." Maren leaned forward. "Give me the map. I carry it to the Order's chapter-house at Grey Ford. I tell them I found it on the courier's body, that the girl was never involved, that the child is dead of fever in some ditch. They believe me, because I've lied to them faithfully for twenty years and betrayed them only once. You three walk north over the border and disappear, and I pay my debt with their prize instead of with your bones."

"That's madness," Aldric said. "They'll read the map. It leads to the Steppe. To the heartstone."

"It leads nowhere," Juniper said slowly, and felt the idea take hold of her the way a current takes a swimmer. "Not really. It moves. It crawls. It shows a different route depending on—who knows what. Blood, maybe. Intent. If Maren carries it alone, it might show her nothing at all. Or it might show her exactly what it wants her to see." She looked at the old woman across the table—at the white hair, the blackthorn staff propped against the wall, the hands that had taught her to write her own name. "You'd be carrying a live coal into their house and calling it safe."

"I've carried worse," Maren said. "I carried *you* for six years, and look how that ended."

The words landed harder than any blow Juniper had taken on the mountain. Because here was the thing—the ugly, tangled thing—that six years of running had never managed to burn out of her: she loved this woman. Fiercely, stupidly, the way you love a hand that fed you even after you learned the same hand had reached for the lock on your cage. Every night on the road, when Juniper made tea, she made it the way Maren had taught her. When she set wards, she hummed Maren's tuneless humming. When she cursed under her breath at bad luck, it was Maren's curses, Maren's cadence. You could run from a person. You could not run from everything they'd built inside you.

And Maren was offering to walk into the Order's chapter-house carrying the most dangerous object in the world, to buy three strangers'—no, not strangers—one stranger's life. Hers. And a child's. And a disgraced knight's.

"It's a good offer," Juniper heard herself say.

"Juniper." Aldric's voice was very quiet. "If the Order believes the map is theirs, they stop hunting you. But they also stop hunting you *here.* They march for the Steppe. Everything we've learned—the vaults, the shards, what the stone does to whoever holds it—"

"Then we find another way to warn people," Juniper snapped. "We're three people and a child against two armies. I'm tired of being clever, Aldric. I'm tired of every road being a trap. Here's a door that opens, and it costs us a scrap of goat-skin, and you want to stand in front of it lecturing about consequences."

"The goat-skin," Maren said mildly, "is currently crawling toward the Steppe and whispering to the baby. I've seen it do it twice since I walked in. Whatever else that map is, it isn't a scrap of anything." She reached across the table and, for the first time in six years, covered Juniper's hand with hers. Her palm was rough and warm and smelled faintly of rosemary. "I know what you think of me. You have every right. But hear an old traitor out: I have betrayed everyone I ever served, and the only ones I never betrayed are sitting at this table. Let me finish the pattern right for once."

By the fire, Wren stirred. She wasn't asleep after all—children rarely were when adults assumed it—and she padded over on bare feet and climbed onto the bench beside Juniper, pressing close, her glow brightening until it gilded the whole smoky room.

"You shouldn't give it to her," Wren said.

"Why not, little one?" Maren asked gently.

Wren considered this with the grave attention she gave all important questions. "Because the map doesn't go where you carry it. The map goes where *it's* going. And it likes her." She pointed one small finger—not at Maren, but at Juniper. "It picked her. On the pass. Out of everybody in the world, it waited for her."

The hearth popped. Outside, the drizzle had thickened again, ticking against the roof slates.

"That," Aldric said, "is the least comforting thing anyone has said in my presence, including the time a dying archivist told me the vaults sing."

Juniper looked down at Wren's finger, then at Maren's hand still resting over hers, then at the oilcloth bundle against her ribs, which had gone distinctly, defiantly warm—as though it understood it was being discussed and wished to register a complaint.

"Nobody's deciding anything tonight," she said at last. "You walked two days through sleet, old woman. Sleep. If I'm going to hand my worst enemy my oldest wound, I want to do it rested."

Maren snorted—an honest, ugly, familiar sound. "Spite. Good. Spite keeps better than bread." She rose, joints crackling, and paused at the doorway to the back room. "For what it's worth," she said, not turning around, "the bread isn't burnt anymore. I fixed that. Some things you get a second chance at."

She disappeared inside. Juniper sat staring at the fire for a long time, Wren drowsing against her shoulder, Aldric sharpening a knife he did not need sharpened.

It was near midnight when the dogs started—not real dogs, but the deep, wrong baying that meant hounds of the Order's breeding, far off yet, but coming. And it was Wren who lifted her head, eyes shining like coins, and said:

"There's more of them. And they're not following the map." Her small face crumpled with fear. "They're following *me.*"

The sigil Sable had pressed into her arm as a blessing had begun, very softly, to glow through the bandage.
