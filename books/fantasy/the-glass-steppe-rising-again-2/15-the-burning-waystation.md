## 15. The Burning Waystation

The waystation smelled of rosemary and burnt honey, which was how Juniper knew Maren had been expecting them.

She'd known it before they'd even crested the ridge—known it in the way the chimney smoke sat fat and unhurried against the grey sky, in the way the herb boxes on the windowsill had been turned to face the road instead of the sun. Maren Crane had spent forty years reading the mountains, and she'd read this one correctly: three travelers, two of them limping, one of them small and glowing faintly at the edges like a coal that hadn't decided whether to catch.

"You're favoring that leg," Juniper said to Aldric as they came down through the birches.

"I'm *respecting* that leg," Aldric said. "There's a difference."

"There is not."

"It's a subtle difference. It's mostly about dignity."

Wren giggled from between them, her hand tucked into Juniper's, and for one absurd moment Juniper thought: *this is what it would be like.* A road with people on it. Someone to argue with about legs. She crushed the thought the way you crush a spark in dry grass—quickly, and with a little more force than necessary, because you know exactly how much damage it could do if it caught.

Maren was waiting in the doorway with her arms crossed, silver hair braided tight, her face arranged into an expression of profound inconvenience.

"Three mouths," she said. "In winter's edge. On my doorstep."

"Maren—" Juniper began.

"I said three *mouths*, girl, not three burdens. Get inside before something sees you. The stew's been on since noon because I knew you'd come hungry and I refuse to be accused of hospitality." She stepped aside, then paused, her eyes finding Wren's. Something moved behind them—old and complicated and quickly buried. "And who's this?"

"Wren," Wren said, with the gravity of a duchess. "I like your chickens."

"The feeling is not mutual. They're vicious. Don't turn your back on them."

* * *

Dinner was stew and dark bread and a silence that kept almost becoming conversation and then thinking better of it. Maren ladled seconds without being asked. Aldric told a story about a mule he'd once served alongside that had achieved higher rank than two lieutenants, and even Maren's mouth twitched at the corner, which Juniper recognized as the equivalent of another woman weeping with laughter.

It was later—Wren asleep by the hearth, Aldric checking the shutters out of some knightly habit he probably couldn't have stopped if he'd tried—that Maren cornered Juniper by the woodpile out back.

"You have it," Maren said. Not a question. "The courier's map."

Juniper's hand went to her coat before she could stop it. "How—"

"Because I know what the Ashen Order does when they lose something, and I've watched you walk like a woman carrying a lit lantern through a hayfield since you crossed my bridge." Maren's voice was low. "Give it here. I'll ward it. There are old bindings I still remember, ones that'll keep the ink quiet and the hunters blind."

"Why?" The word came out harder than Juniper intended. "Why help me now? You wouldn't help me leave. You tried to sell me to them, Maren. I was fifteen."

The old woman flinched. It was small—a tightening around the eyes—but Juniper saw it, and hated how much satisfaction she took from seeing it, and hated the satisfaction too.

"Yes," Maren said quietly. "I did. And I have had eleven years to consider what I am." She held out her hand. "Let me do one thing right, Juniper. That's all I'm asking. One thing, at the end, done properly."

Juniper didn't give her the map. But she didn't say no either, and Maren nodded as though she'd expected exactly that, and went inside to bank the fire, and Juniper stood in the cold telling herself that the ache in her chest was just the wind off the pass.

* * *

They came at moonset.

Juniper woke to a wrongness she couldn't name—the particular silence of birds that have somewhere else to be—and was out the window with Wren under one arm before her mind finished the sentence her body had started. Below the slope, torchlight moved through the birches in disciplined lines. Grey cloaks. White ash-marks on the hoods.

"Aldric," she breathed through the window. "Aldric, wake up, they're—"

The front door exploded inward.

Firebrands—Order zealots, the ones who believed burning was a sacrament—came through in a wave of smoke and shouting, and the first thing Juniper saw clearly was Maren standing in the middle of her own kitchen with her arms raised and her feet planted like a woman defending a harvest, and the second thing she saw was the hedge-magic rising off the old woman in visible waves, rosemary-scented and furious.

"*Run,*" Maren said, without turning around. "Out the back. The cellar passage goes to the creek bed. Go *now*, girl, or I swear by every herb in that garden I will haunt you so thoroughly you'll beg for ghosts of a lesser commitment."

"Maren, no—"

"That wasn't a request." The old woman glanced back, just once, and her eyes were wet and absolutely steady. "Eleven years I owed you a door held open. This is me paying it. Now *go*."

Aldric had Wren. He was already moving, shield arm wrapped around the child, and his hand found Juniper's shoulder—not pulling, just there, solid—and Juniper understood with terrible clarity that if she stayed, Maren's sacrifice became a corpse instead of a gift.

She ran.

At the cellar mouth she looked back one last time. Maren stood alone in the doorway of the kitchen, arms wide, filling it entirely, an old woman against a tide of fire, and her voice carried over the roar—cracking, defiant, unbowed:

"This house has sheltered thieves and cowards and one genuinely awful apprentice, and it will not be emptied by *ash and arrogance*—"

Then the flames took the doorway, and took the voice, and Juniper went down into the dark with the sound of everything she'd lost and never claimed burning above her.

* * *

The creek bed was freezing and black and perfect. They followed it a mile downstream while the waystation's glow painted the clouds orange behind them, and none of them spoke, because there was nothing to say that wouldn't break something.

Wren cried first—silent, shaking sobs against Aldric's chest, the kind of crying children do when they understand more than their years should allow. Aldric held her and walked and held her, and his jaw was set so hard Juniper could see the muscle jumping even in the dark.

Juniper walked ahead and felt nothing, which was a lie, and knew it was a lie, and kept walking anyway because the alternative was stopping and stopping meant drowning.

It was dawn before she finally spoke, her voice scraped raw.

"She held the door."

"She held the door," Aldric agreed quietly.

"She could have come with us. She was fast enough. She *chose*—" Juniper's breath hitched. "Eleven years I hated her. Eleven years I built my whole life out of running from her, and she pays it back by *burning*—"

She couldn't finish. She stood in the grey morning light with frost on her lashes and grief and fury tangled in her chest so tightly she couldn't tell which was which, and then Wren wriggled free of Aldric's arms and crossed the frozen creek bed and put her small glowing hands on Juniper's cheeks.

"Mama," Wren said softly. "You're allowed to be angry *and* sad. Grandma Maren said so. She told me last night, when you were outside. She said, 'That girl will try to carry it all alone, and you mustn't let her.'"

Juniper made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh and wasn't quite anything else, and knelt down in the freezing water, and let the child hold her face together while the waystation burned to ash behind them.

When she finally stood, wiping her eyes with a sleeve already soaked through, she felt it settle in her—not peace, nothing like peace, but a direction. A heat. The map pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and somewhere beyond the ridgeline, the Order was regrouping, and somewhere beyond *that*, Sable's rebels were watching, and somewhere beyond *everything*, the Glass Steppe waited with its buried heart.

"They wanted a fire," Juniper said, and her voice came out low and level and stranger to her than any stranger's. "Fine. Let's give them one they'll regret."

Aldric studied her for a long moment. "That sounds less like running and more like marching."

"It is." She pulled the map from her coat, and in the dawn light the ink was moving faster than she'd ever seen it, crawling toward the Steppe like it knew its keeper was dead and its bearer was ready. "We stop hiding. We go to the Steppe—and we get there before anyone who wants to *hold* it does."

Behind them, the smoke rose straight and thin into the pale sky, like a line drawn from the ashes toward the horizon.

Like a road.

Like a promise.
