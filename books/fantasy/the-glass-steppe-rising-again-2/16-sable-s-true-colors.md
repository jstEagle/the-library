## 16. Sable's True Colors

The prisoner had been singing.

Yara Sable heard it before she reached the tent—a thin, reedy tune drifting through the canvas walls, some old Ashen marching hymn slowed down until it sounded almost like a lullaby. She paused outside the flap and listened, arms crossed against the mountain cold, and felt something in her chest do a small, complicated thing that she chose to ignore.

"You're supposed to be interrogating him," she said, ducking inside, "not holding choir practice."

Corporal Ives looked up from his stool, entirely unbothered. He was a broad, patient man with the face of someone who had once been handsome and had decided, somewhere along the way, that being comfortable was better. "He wouldn't talk, General. So I made tea instead." He gestured at the cup steaming in the prisoner's bound hands. "Turns out he talks fine after tea."

"I could have you flogged for coddling the enemy."

"You could," Ives agreed. "But then who'd make your tea?"

The prisoner laughed—a dry, cracked sound—and Yara got her first proper look at him. An Ashen Order officer, grey robes scorched at one shoulder where the ambush had caught him, middle-aged, with the kind of tired eyes that came from decades of believing something very hard. His wrists were tied to the chair, but he held his teacup with the dignity of a man holding a scepter.

"General Sable," he said. "The rebel who feeds prisoners."

"The rebel who *interrogates* prisoners," Yara corrected. "The feeding is incidental."

"Everything is incidental with you people, I imagine." He took a sip. "Except the heartstone. That, I suspect, is rather central."

Yara kept her face still. Around them, the camp murmured—six hundred rebels in a hollow below the Thornfell passes, cooking fires smoking, sentries stamping their feet. Her army. Her ragged, half-starved, magnificent army of people the war had chewed up and spat out. Farmers whose farms were ash. Soldiers whose banners no longer existed. A whole generation of the discarded, following her because she had promised them the one thing nobody else would: that what was broken could be unbroken.

"Sit down, Ives," she said quietly.

Ives sat. He knew that voice. Everyone in the camp knew that voice. It was the voice she used right before she did something the histories would either praise or condemn, depending on who wrote them.

"Name," she said to the prisoner.

"Officer Dain Ferrow, Third Circle, Order of Ash." He said it like a man reciting his own eulogy. "And before you ask—no, I won't tell you where the Order's columns are marching. Yes, I will tell you anything else, because frankly, General, I've been wanting to have this conversation with you for eleven years."

That was not how interrogations usually began. Yara pulled over a crate, sat, and waited. Patience was the first weapon she'd ever learned to use; her mother had taught her that waiting out a stubborn goat was not so different from waiting out a stubborn man, except goats eventually respected you.

"Eleven years," she prompted.

"Since the armistice. Since the Steppe went glass and everyone agreed to pretend that burying the problem was the same as solving it." Ferrow set down his teacup with great care. "You know what the Order teaches its novices about the heartstone?"

"That it's an abomination," Yara said. "That it shattered itself in pride, and its fragments must never be gathered, and any who seek them are heretics."

"Mm. And what do you teach *your* people?"

"That it belongs to us," Yara said. "To the ones the war broke. The stone was made from the land and the blood of everyone on it—it isn't the Order's relic or the crown's treasure. It's ours. And when we find it, we'll use it to mend what was taken. The burned villages rebuilt. The dead—" she stopped herself, as she always did there, "—the wounds closed. Justice, finally, for people who've only ever been given graves."

Ferrow watched her for a long moment. Then he did something worse than argue.

He nodded.

"Yes," he said softly. "That's exactly what I thought you'd say. Word for word, near enough. You've been saying it for years—the speeches, the pamphlets, the little sermons by the fire. *The stone belongs to the broken.* It's good rhetoric, General. I mean that sincerely. Half my novices could recite it back at me, which is why the Order wants you dead so badly. You're not dangerous because you're wrong. You're dangerous because you're *nearly* right."

"And you're going to tell me how I'm wrong," Yara said, "in exchange for your tea."

"I'm going to tell you how you're being *used*, which is different, and considerably more insulting." He leaned forward as far as his bonds allowed. "Tell me about the courier."

Something cold slid down Yara's spine. "What courier?"

"The one on the pass. The one your scouts reported dying with a map in his hands. The one your riders have been chasing through these mountains for two weeks now, along with a disgraced knight and—" Ferrow's eyes flicked to her, sharp as a splinter, "—a child who glows."

"We don't discuss operational matters with prisoners."

"No. But you discuss them with your officers, and your officers discuss them with each other, and soldiers gossip worse than washerwomen. I've been in your camp four days, General. I know your supply situation, your casualty count, and that Corporal Ives here cheats at cards." 

"I absolutely do," Ives confirmed.

"What you don't seem to know," Ferrow went on, "is why the Order has committed three full circles to these mountains. Not to stop *you*—you're an irritant, a brushfire, we've smothered bigger. Three circles, General, for a runaway apprentice and a knight with no banner. Ask yourself why."

Yara didn't answer. She was already asking herself, and she didn't like any of the answers forming.

"Because the map is real," Ferrow said. "Because the courier was real—he was one of ours, actually, Third Circle, before he decided the Order's plan for the stone was worse than letting it be found. Because the heartstone's shards are still down there under the glass, still humming, still *waiting*. And because the moment anyone gathers them—" he spread his bound hands, an odd, apologetic gesture, "—the war doesn't reignite, General. It *completes*."

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning the stone doesn't share. It never has. Whoever holds it doesn't wield power—they become it. One will, poured into every living thing within reach. The last war wasn't fought over the heartstone. It was fought by it. Two kings, both certain they were righteous, both wearing the stone's favor like a second skin, until the whole plain burned and someone finally had the sense to shatter the thing." He picked up his tea again, hands steady. "The Order's official position is that the shards must be destroyed. Our actual position, known to perhaps forty people alive, is that they cannot be destroyed. Only carried. And every carrier becomes its prisoner."

Yara stood. Walked to the tent's edge. Stood there looking out at her camp—at the cookfires, at the mended banners, at old Marta teaching children letters in the firelight because their school had burned with everything else.

She had built all of this on a single sentence: *the stone belongs to the broken.*

It had never occurred to her—not once, not in eleven years—to ask whether the stone might belong to no one. Whether *belonging* was the trap itself.

"You're lying," she said, without conviction.

"I might be," Ferrow agreed pleasantly. "Check my story. The Order's archives, the sealed annals, the testimony of the First Guardian's line—all of it corroborates. Or better: watch yourself, General. Watch what you're willing to do, starting tonight, for a thing you've never seen. If a man can lie to you that well, imagine what a god-shard can do."

* * *

She didn't sleep. She walked the camp instead, which was what she did on bad nights, and the camp pretended not to notice, which was what it always did.

Past the farriers' row, where the smell of hot iron cut through the pine cold. Past the infirmary tent, where she stopped longer than she meant to, watching a boy of maybe sixteen sleep with his leg freshly amputated at the knee, lost to a rockslide two days ago on a road that led nowhere important. He'd joined her because the Order had burned his village looking for smugglers. He'd told her, the day he signed on, that he wanted to be there when things were made right.

*When we find the stone,* she'd told him, *everything changes.*

She had believed it the way you believe sunrise. And now a grey-robed prisoner with a teacup had put a hairline crack across the whole sky of it, and she couldn't stop staring at the crack.

"You look," said a voice behind her, "like a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't like."

Vess, her quartermaster and oldest friend, fell into step beside her. Vess was short, round, sixty-two, and had once carried Yara three miles on her back through a flooded river, a fact she mentioned whenever Yara seemed in danger of forgetting who was actually in charge.

"How much do we have left?" Yara asked. "Honestly."

"Food for nine days. Arrows for six. Hope for—" Vess shrugged. "Hope renews itself. It's the only supply line the Order can't cut." She squinted at Yara. "But that's not what's eating you. What's eating you is the prisoner."

"He says the stone can't be shared. That whoever takes it takes *everything*—that it pours one person's will into the world and calls it peace." Yara's breath fogged. "He says the last war was fought by the stone, not over it."

Vess was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice was gentler than Yara had ever heard it. "And does it matter? Truly? Say he's right. Say the stone makes a tyrant of whoever holds it. You'd hold it, Yara. Not Ives. Not me. You. And I've watched you for twenty years give away your own rations, your own blanket, your own—"

"My own judgment?" Yara said bitterly.

"Your own *self*. There's nothing left over to corrupt, girl. You gave it all away years ago." Vess patted her arm. "That's either the best argument for your plan or the worst one. I genuinely can't tell, and I've been telling you so since the river."

Yara laughed despite herself—a short, cracked sound, not unlike the prisoner's. "You're a terrible counselor."

"I'm an excellent quartermaster and a mediocre counselor, and you keep me because you need one honest number in your life." Vess squeezed her arm and left her to the dark.

But the arithmetic didn't stop. That was the trouble with cracks: once you saw one, you saw them everywhere. In the boy missing his leg. In Marta's firelight lessons. In her own speeches, played back now in Ferrow's dry voice—*good rhetoric, General*—and sounding, for the first time, slightly too polished. Slightly rehearsed. Slightly like something she'd needed to believe so badly that she'd stopped checking whether it was true.

*You're dangerous because you're nearly right.*

Nearly. The word sat in her chest like a swallowed coal.

* * *

Near dawn, she went back to the prison tent. Ferrow was awake—or had never slept—and greeted her with the weary courtesy of a man who had expected this visit around midnight.

"Well?" he said. "Have you decided I'm lying?"

"I've decided I don't know. Which is worse." She crouched to his eye level. "So here's what happens now. You'll write to your Order. You'll tell them I have the courier's trail, the knight, the child—that I'm days from the Steppe. Every word true."

Ferrow's eyebrows rose. "You're going to feed me to your enemy."

"I'm going to feed them a *reason* to come to me on ground I choose, instead of hunting me across mountains I don't control. And while they march to your coordinates, I take the pass they've left thin." She smiled, and knew even as she did it that it was her campaign smile, the one from the pamphlets, and hated herself a little for reaching for it. "You see, Officer Ferrow, whatever the truth about the stone—I still need to reach it before they do. Because if it truly can't be destroyed, then it can't be left for the Order to carry, either. Their forty people who know the secret? I've met men like them. They'd carry it straight to a throne and call it mercy."

"And you'd carry it to the people," Ferrow said. "And it would pour *your* certainty into the world, and the world would call it justice, and it would be the same throne with better songs." He tilted his head. "Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless you're not the one who carries it." His voice dropped, and the weariness in it turned to something almost like pity. "There's a child in these mountains, General. Heartstone-touched. Do you understand what that means? The stone has already begun choosing its vessel, and it hasn't chosen a king or a general or a grey-robed officer. It chose a *child*—because a child is the only shape of person it hasn't ruined yet." He leaned close. "You marked her, I hear. A blessing, your people called it. How long before you admit to yourself what it actually was?"

Yara rose slowly. The cold in the tent had nothing to do with the weather.

"That mark," she said, "was to find her. To protect her."

"Every chain ever forged was to protect something," Ferrow said. "Ask the smith."

She left him there with his empty teacup and his terrible, patient truths, and stood outside in the blue-grey light watching the sun come up over Thornfell, and made herself say the sentence she'd been avoiding all night:

*I marked a child to keep her.*

Not to save her. To *keep* her. To keep the compass that pointed to the stone, the key that fit the lock, the vessel-in-waiting. She had dressed it in blessing-words and warm oil and told herself it was guardianship, and Ferrow had peeled the dressing off in four sentences over tea.

"General?" Ives appeared at her elbow, tea in hand, offering her a cup. "You've got the face again. The one from before the bridge at Corren's Ford."

"I know where the girl is," Yara said. "Or close enough. The sigil sings if I listen hard." She took the tea and drank it scalding, needing the pain of it. "Three days northeast, through the Weeping Pass. The Order thinks the pass is haunted. The girl's protectors think it's impassable."

"So naturally we're going through it."

"Naturally."

Ives nodded slowly. "And when we catch up to them? A runaway, an old knight, and a glowing child who trusts us about as far as she can glow?" He looked at her sideways. "What's the plan, General? Genuinely asking. Because I've followed you into fires before, but I've always known what we were burning *for*."

Yara watched the sun clear the ridgeline and gild the frost on the tents, six hundred cookfires waking below her, an entire nation of the discarded stirring toward another day of her promises.

"The plan," she said, "is that I'm going to look that child in the eyes and tell her the truth. All of it. Even the parts that damn me." She drained the cup. "And if she runs anyway—if she looks at me the way that prisoner did and sees the throne underneath—then I'll know which side of the war I've actually been fighting on. Won't I."

Ives said nothing. He just refilled her cup, which was, Yara reflected, the most honest counsel anyone had given her in weeks.

Far to the northeast, past the ridgelines, the Weeping Pass waited in the clouds—stone arches that moaned in the wind, old roads that the maps refused to agree on, and somewhere inside it, a knight, a runaway, and a child who glowed like the last ember of a fire the world had sworn was out.

"Break camp," Yara said. "We march within the hour."

And beneath her feet, though she could not yet feel it, the glass of the world's oldest scar lay quiet in its buried vaults—humming, patient, and already listening for her name.
