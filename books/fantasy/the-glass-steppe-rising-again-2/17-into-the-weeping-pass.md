## 17. Into the Weeping Pass

Aldric Vane had fought in eleven battles, been burned twice, stabbed once, and thrown from a horse with such enthusiasm that he'd spent a week learning to write with his left hand. He considered himself reasonably difficult to frighten.

The Weeping Pass frightened him.

It began at the mouth of it—a cleft in the mountain like a wound that had never quite decided to heal. Water seeped from the rock face in long silver threads, gathering in beads along the stone, and each bead caught the light and held it wrong. Not reflecting. Remembering.

"The smugglers call it the Weeping Pass," Juniper said, standing at its threshold with her arms crossed and the particular set to her jaw that meant she was daring someone—him, usually—to object. "Because of the water."

"I had guessed," Aldric said.

"And because sometimes it sounds like crying." She said this lightly, the way she said most terrible things, as if she were handing you bad news wrapped in brown paper and expected you not to make a fuss about it. "The Order doesn't guard it. Nobody does. The roads around it are watched by both armies, but this—" she gestured at the cleft "—this everyone agrees is worse than soldiers."

"Everyone," Wren said, "except us."

"Except us," Juniper agreed, and something in Aldric's chest did an uncomfortable thing at how easily the girl had said *us*, as though it had always been true, as though three people who'd known each other barely two seasons were simply a fact of the world now, like weather.

He adjusted his sword belt—the old habit, the one that meant he was steadying himself rather than preparing—and looked up at the walls of the pass. The water beaded there, dozens of drops, and he could have sworn one of them turned, slowly, to watch him go by.

"Well," he said. "After you."

* * *

The first mile was merely unpleasant. The second was strange.

The map had grown restless since Glassford Bridge. It had always moved—that crawling ink, that patient migration toward the Steppe—but now it seemed to *pull*. Juniper carried it against her chest in an oilcloth wrap, and every hour or so Aldric would catch her pressing her palm flat against it, murmuring, the way one soothes a spooked animal. Or argues with one.

"It's louder here," she said, when they stopped to eat hard bread and harder cheese beneath an overhang of dripping stone. "In the pass. Like it knows where it's going and it's annoyed we're slow."

"Maps are famously impatient," Aldric said.

"This isn't funny."

"No," he admitted. "But I've found that if I don't say something ridiculous soon after saying nothing at all, my thoughts start arranging themselves into sermons, and nobody wants that." He tore the cheese into three pieces and gave Wren the largest, which was becoming another habit, and which he would deny under torture.

Wren accepted it solemnly. She had been quiet all day—not frightened-quiet, which was small and tucked-in, but listening-quiet. Her head kept tilting, birdlike, toward the walls of the pass. Toward the water.

"There are voices in the drips," she said at last, matter-of-fact, the way children announce things that should stop adults' hearts. "Not real ones. Echo ones. But they're saying words."

"What words?" Juniper asked.

Wren chewed, considering. "Mostly names. And 'turn back.' But one of them said 'the cheese is poisoned,' so I don't think they know everything."

Aldric paused with his own piece halfway to his mouth.

Juniper took the cheese from his hand, sniffed it, bit it, chewed, swallowed, and handed it back. "There. If I die, avenge me. If you die, I'll be very annoyed, because I've only just gotten used to you."

"That may be the kindest thing anyone has ever said to me," Aldric said, and meant it more than was comfortable.

* * *

The pass narrowed as evening came on, until the three of them walked in single file through a corridor of wet stone, and the water fell thicker, threading down the walls in curtains, pooling ankle-deep on the path. The sound built and built—hundreds of small falls, thousands of drops—and somewhere inside the sound, if you let your mind loosen even slightly, you could hear what Wren heard.

Weeping. Not one voice. Many. Layered, overlapping, some high and some low, and none of them quite human, though all of them wanted to be.

Aldric kept his hand on his sword hilt even though he knew, in the way he knew things now without being told, that a blade would do no good here. You cannot cut grief. He had tried, in other years, in other forms.

"We should find high ground before dark," he said. "If there is any."

"There's a smuggler's cache," Juniper said ahead of him. "Halfway through. A ledge above the flood line, dry-ish, big enough for a fire if the smoke finds the crack in the roof. My mother used it."

"You've never mentioned your mother."

"You've never asked."

"She raised you alone in these mountains, ran contraband, and died—" he stopped. "I'm sorry. Maren. Your aunt. I conflate them."

"They blur," Juniper said, and her voice had gone careful and flat, the tone she used when a subject had teeth. "That's rather the point of family."

The ledge appeared when she said it would, because Juniper's knowledge of these mountains had the unnerving quality of prophecy delivered casually: *there'll be a handhold on the left, don't trust the second one.* They climbed. The cache was exactly as promised—dry-ish, with an iron ring for tethering and a fire-black circle worn into the stone by generations of smugglers who had also needed, briefly, to stop being afraid.

Aldric built the fire while Juniper checked the map. He heard her breath change.

"Aldric."

He turned. She had unwrapped the oilcloth, and the map lay open on her knees, and the ink was moving faster than he'd ever seen it—not crawling now but flowing, rivers of it, all streaming toward the mark at the center that stood for the Steppe. And where the lines converged, the paper had begun to glow. Faintly. Warmly. The color of the light through ale, of banked coals, of—

"Of heartstone," he said quietly. "It's resonating. The shards below the Steppe. We're close enough now that the map can hear them, and they can hear it."

"Can it hear *us*?"

"I don't know."

"Wonderful. I'm traveling through a haunted mountain with a haunted map, and the honest answer is *I don't know*." She rewrapped it, roughly, and then—Aldric saw her do it—pressed her hand flat over it once, gently, before tying the cord. An apology, maybe. Or a promise.

Wren was asleep already, curled against Juniper's side, one hand fisted in her sleeve. In the firelight her skin held that faint luminescence it had carried since the hollow—barely visible, like moonlight remembered rather than seen.

"Sleep," Juniper told Aldric. "I'll take first watch."

"You slept last night."

"And you didn't, because you were pretending to sharpen that sword for four hours, and I could hear you thinking the whole time. It was very loud. Something about siege formations and regret."

"That is my favorite combination," Aldric said, but he lay down, because she was right, and because arguing with Juniper Crane was a battle he lost on principle so that she would keep winning things. It was, he suspected, good for her.

He slept. That was the mistake.

* * *

The dream came the way dreams come to men with his history: dressed as memory, armed as memory, wearing the faces of the dead.

He stood in the courtyard of the Order's first hall—not the Ashen Order, but his own, the Knights of the Broken Circle, before their name became a cautionary tale. Rain. Banners soaked black. Ser Osric dying against the wall with his hand out, mouthing Aldric's name, except in the dream the mouth moved and the voice that came out was the water-voice of the pass, many-layered, weeping:

*You left.*

*I didn't,* dream-Aldric said. *They burned it. There was nothing—*

*You left,* said Ser Osric, and then it wasn't Osric, it was Maren Crane, which made no sense, dreams don't obey geography or biography, and she was holding out the map and saying *you left, you always leave, ask him why he really lost his order*—

He woke with his hand on his sword and the fire nearly dead and Juniper shaking his shoulder, her face pale in the embers.

"You were calling out," she said. "Names. A lot of names. And then—" she hesitated. "You asked someone to forgive you. Over and over. I couldn't tell who."

"The pass," Aldric said hoarsely. His throat felt scoured. "It pulls things up. Old things. Wren said it speaks in echoes."

"It's not just echoes anymore." Juniper's jaw tightened. "Look at the walls."

He looked. The water on the stone had stopped falling. Every thread of it hung motionless, suspended, and each drop glowed faintly gold, and each drop, as he watched, slid—against gravity, against everything—a few inches closer to where the map lay wrapped against Juniper's pack.

"Oh," Aldric said. "That's not good."

* * *

They broke camp in the grey before dawn, moving fast, and the pass decided, around mid-morning, to stop being subtle.

It began with the water. The suspended drops released all at once—not falling but *flying*, streaming inward off the walls in golden threads that wove together as they came, forming shapes in the air ahead of them. Shapes with too much intention in them. One looked like a hand. One looked like a door. One, horribly, looked like a knight kneeling, armor rendered entirely in running water, head bowed, waiting for a blow or an absolution.

"It wants the map," Juniper said. "Or it wants us to want to give it the map. I genuinely can't tell, and I hate it."

"Walk," Aldric said. "Don't run. Running tells it we're afraid, and I suspect fear is the food here." This was, he realized as he said it, completely invented. He had no idea what fed the pass. But Juniper nodded as though he'd cited a text, and Wren took his hand, and they walked.

The water-knight unknelt and stood in their path.

Up close it had no face, just a smooth sheet of falling-that-wasn't, and from inside it came the layered voice, and this time the words were clear enough that Aldric felt them land in his chest like cold coins:

*"Bearer. Bearer. The stone remembers its carriers. Come home. Come home. Come home—"*

"It's not talking to me," Juniper breathed. "It's talking to—" and she looked at Wren.

And Wren stepped forward.

Later, trying to reconstruct it, Aldric could never get the order of events straight. He knew that he shouted. He knew that he moved—some part of him noting with distant academic interest that he had never in his life moved that fast outside of actual battle. He knew that the water-shape lashed out, not at Wren, but at Juniper, at the wrapped map, a whip of golden threads cracking through the air where Juniper's head had been.

He knew that he put himself in its path, because that was the whole of him, distilled: put yourself in the path. It was the only prayer he'd ever learned.

The whip of water caught him across the side and shoulder, and it did not feel like water. It felt like being struck with every goodbye he had ever failed to say properly. Cold went through him, deeper than flesh, some old frozen place cracking open, and he heard himself make a sound he didn't recognize as coming from a man.

Then Wren screamed—not in fear; in *command*—and the pass listened.

Every drop of suspended water in that corridor of stone shuddered, hung, and fell. All at once. Straight down. The shapes collapsed into ordinary rain, ordinary runoff, ordinary mountain water minding its own business, and the silence afterward was enormous.

Aldric sat down in a puddle. This was not strategic.

"Aldric!" Juniper was on her knees beside him, hands hovering, afraid to touch. "Where—how deep—"

"Flesh wound," he managed.

"You're bleeding through your shirt."

"A *dramatic* flesh wound."

"Don't joke, you're grey as ash—" Her hands found the gash below his shoulder blade and pressed, competent and gentle at once, and he discovered that he could bear the pain perfectly well as long as he concentrated on the fact of her hands. This was information he filed away and resolved never, ever to examine.

Wren crouched at his other side. Her glow had brightened—visibly, undeniably now, a soft gold radiating off her skin like heat shimmer—and she laid one small hand flat on his forearm.

"It's sorry," she reported. "The pass. It thought you were taking me away from it. It's lonely." A pause. "Also it says your wound will fester if we don't clean it, because the water in here is old and mean."

"The pass," Aldric said weakly, "has opinions about field medicine."

"Mama says all water in Thornfell has opinions."

Juniper went still for half a heartbeat at *Mama*—she did every time, a small flinch of wonder she clearly believed she was hiding—and then got to work tearing his shirt hem for bandages, scolding him the entire time, her voice rougher than her hands.

"You absolute idiot. You great armored idiot. There was no need—I had it—I was dodging—"

"You were not dodging."

"I was *about* to dodge."

"Juniper." He caught her wrist, carefully, mindful of the blood. "Let me have this. Let me have done one thing right this year. I've been keeping count, and the tally is embarrassing."

She stared at him. Something moved behind her eyes—something she'd been carrying a long time, heavier than any map—and for a moment he thought she might actually answer it, whatever it was.

Instead she tied the bandage off with a vicious knot and said, "You're walking. Slowly. And when we're out of this pass, you're letting me look at that properly, and if it festers I'm putting maggots in it, and I won't feel bad."

"Understood."

"And Aldric?" She glanced back at him as she rose, Wren's hand tucked in hers, and the morning light came down the pass behind her, catching the last of the golden water as it drained away into ordinary stone. "Thank you. Don't make me say it again. I have a reputation."

"Notorious," he agreed. "The Terror of the Smuggler's Roads. Terrifying. Very feared."

"Shut up and walk, Vane."

* * *

They reached the far mouth of the pass at dusk, and the world beyond it was almost aggressively normal—a green valley, honest wind, stars arriving one by one like guests who hadn't heard the news.

They made camp in the lee of a boulder, and Juniper cleaned the wound properly, and it was as clean a gash as one could hope for, but by midnight Aldric's teeth were chattering and the edges of the bandage had gone an ugly angry red, and Juniper's face, when she held a flame close to inspect it, went grim in a way that frightened him more than the pass had.

"The water got inside somehow," she said. "Old water. Mean water." She laughed, but it came out cracked. "You're going to burn tonight, Aldric. Maybe tomorrow too. And I need you to do one thing for me."

"Anything," he said, and was too fevered to guard the word.

"When I talk to you—and I will, all night, because that's what fevers are for—you have to remember which parts are the pass and which parts are me." She pulled the blanket up over him, and her hand lingered at his shoulder a moment longer than medicine required. "Because I have some things to say that I've been not-saying for a very long time. And I'd rather you remember them."

Above them the stars multiplied. Below them, in the wrap against her chest, the map pulsed once, softly, like a second heartbeat.

Aldric sank toward the fever feeling, oddly, less like drowning and more like being carried—and the last thing he heard, before the old names came rising up out of the dark to meet him, was Juniper settling herself at his side with a sigh, and saying quietly, to him, to the fire, to whoever was listening:

"All right. Where shall we start? Let's start with why I really ran."
