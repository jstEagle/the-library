## 18. What Juniper Ran From

The Weeping Pass had a way of wringing a person out and forgetting to put them back together.

They'd made camp in the lee of an old watchman's hut—or what remained of one. Three walls, half a roof, a hearth that some long-dead border warden had built to last, because everything else here fell apart the moment you stopped holding it up. The stone still held heat like a grudge. Juniper fed it what little dry scrub she'd gathered before dark and told herself the smoke wouldn't be visible from the heights.

It probably would be. But Aldric was burning from the inside, shivering under two blankets and his own soaked-through cloak, and Juniper had decided—somewhere around the third hour of listening to him breathe like a man hauling water uphill—that visibility could go hang.

Wren slept curled against his side, one small hand fisted in his tunic, as if even unconscious she was keeping him tethered. Her glow was faint tonight, a candle behind skin. It rose and fell when he did. Juniper had noticed that three nights ago and hadn't said anything, because there were things you noticed on this road that you kept in your pocket like river stones, smooth and heavy, for later.

"Aldric." She touched his forehead. Too hot. "Drink."

He surfaced from wherever he was—some fever-country with its own geography—and blinked at her with eyes that took too long to focus. "Juniper Crane," he said gravely, "you have the face of a woman who has been arguing with me for some time."

"I have. You keep winning, which is insulting, since you've been asleep."

"Good tactics." He drank when she held the waterskin to his lips, coughed, drank again. "The pass. Did we—"

"We're through the worst of it. You don't remember?"

"I remember the stones singing." He frowned at the fire. "Or screaming. I may have been unhelpful."

"You tried to fight a rockslide, Ser Vane. A *rockslide*. You drew your sword at it."

"It seemed hostile."

Juniper laughed despite herself—a short, cracked sound, the first laugh in days—and something in Aldric's fever-bright face softened at it, pleased, like a man who'd been trying to make her laugh on purpose and had finally succeeded by accident, through delirium.

She got more blanket on him. Checked Wren. Sat back against the wall where she could see both the door-gap and the fire, because old habits died hard or not at all.

For a while there was only the wind doing its work outside, pressing at the hut like a beggar at a door, and the wet tick of snowmelt somewhere in the walls—the pass weeping, as advertised. Juniper turned her knife over in her hands. Not sharpening it. Just turning it.

"Say something," Aldric murmured.

"About what?"

"Anything. Your voice keeps the dark honest."

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's ever said to me, and I once had a mule driver propose."

"Mule drivers are sincere men." His eyes were drifting closed again. "Talk, Crane. Or I'll start talking, and fever-talk is worse than anything you could say. I'll confess to crimes. I'll recite the entire Vane family lineage, going back eleven generations, all of them terrible."

"Gods preserve me." She thought about it. Thought about how the silence had been sitting on her chest all day, heavier than the pack, heavier than the map sewn into her coat lining. How confession worked on a body the way lancing worked on a wound—you didn't want to, but the pressure was going to find its own way out eventually, and better through a small deliberate hole than a big ragged one.

Besides. He'd earned a piece of it. He'd taken a wound on the pass for a girl who wasn't his and a woman who barely tolerated him.

"Fine," she said. "You asked for it. Here's what I ran from."

* * *

"Maren Crane raised me from the time I was eight," Juniper began. "Not my mother—no relation at all, though we shared a surname by her arrangement, because 'Crane' travels better than whatever my mother left me, which was nothing except a talent and no name worth keeping. Maren found me in Saltmarsh. I was doing hedge-tricks for coppers—finding lost buttons, telling fishwives whether their husbands were lying, small stuff, harmless stuff. And she watched me do it and bought me a meat pie, and I followed her home like every fool orphan follows every kind stranger, and I never looked back."

"Were you happy?" Aldric asked. His voice was slurred at the edges but present.

"For years. Genuinely. She taught me everything—how to read a person's grief like weather, how to ground myself after touching someone's pain so it doesn't come home with you, how to brew forty teas and lie about all of them." Juniper smiled at the fire. "She called me her hedge-apprentice. Said the Order wanted talent born in towers and polished by masters, and talent like mine grew wild, and wild things did work the towers couldn't. I believed her. Why wouldn't I? She fed me, taught me, scolded me, loved me—I'm nearly sure she loved me—in her way, which was a dry, rationed way, like everything else about her."

Aldric shifted. Wren stirred, resettled. "What changed?"

"She started having visitors." The words came easier now, which surprised Juniper; she'd expected them to cost more. "Grey cloaks. They'd come at night, twice a year, then four times, then more. I wasn't meant to see them. But I saw everything on that mountain—you learn that or you die of curiosity. And I noticed things. That after the visitors left, Maren would be quiet for days. That she'd take out her ledger and write in it with a shaking hand. That we got new roof-beams the winter after the first visit, and a second goat, and a copper kettle I'd admired in the market in Lowbridge, sixty miles away."

"You think she was selling information."

"I know she was." Flat now. "I just didn't know what kind, or how much, until the night I came home early and heard her talking to a grey-cloaked man in the kitchen. About me."

The fire popped. Outside, the wind changed its mind about something and went quiet, as if it, too, were listening.

"He said—" Juniper turned the knife over again. "I can still hear his voice. He said, 'The girl's aptitude is unusual. Untrained talent of this grade appears perhaps once in a decade.' Like I was ore in a hillside. And Maren said, 'She's not ready. Give me another year.' And he said, 'We've been patient, Mistress Crane. The debt stands. The girl, or the arrears.'"

"What debt?" Aldric asked quietly.

"That's the part that took me years to understand, and I only understood it last month, standing in her kitchen with your sword between us and the Order hunting us both." She laughed without any humor in it. "Maren served them. Before me. I don't know exactly what she did—she never confessed it, even at the end, even holding that door—but I know she owed them. Money, or service, or silence, or all three. And I know that when the debt came due and she couldn't pay it in coin, she looked at the wild-talented orphan she'd collected off the Saltmarsh docks and thought: *there's my payment.*"

Aldric said nothing for a moment. When he spoke, his voice had gone careful, the way voices do around open wounds. "How old were you?"

"Fifteen."

"And what did you do?"

"The reasonable thing. I packed a bag in the dark and I ran." She shrugged, though her shoulders didn't feel casual about it. "Climbed out the loft window, down the alder tree, gone before dawn. Took nothing but my knife and the tea-blends in my head. I told myself for years afterward that she deserved it—that I'd caught her mid-sale and saved myself, end of story. Very tidy. Very satisfying." She paused. "But here's what I couldn't fit in the tidy version, Ser Vane, and it's been rattling around in me ever since: she said *give me another year.*"

Aldric's brows drew together.

"If she was selling me—if I was just arrears walking around in boots—why bargain for time? Why not hand me over that night and be done with it?" Juniper stared into the fire until it hurt. "I've turned it over ten thousand ways. Maybe she was stalling to find another payment. Maybe she was greedy and wanted to raise my price. Or maybe—" and here her voice did something traitorous, went rough— "maybe she loved me in her rationed, dried-up way, and was trying to buy one more year of having me before she lost me either to the Order or to running. And I never stayed to find out which. I never asked. Fifteen years old, and I decided the whole story in one night, and I've been carrying that decision around ever since like a stone in my boot."

The wind returned. Somewhere above them, ice cracked and slid down the pass wall with a long sighing sound.

"She held the door," Aldric said.

"Yes."

"For you. For Wren. Against the Order that owned her."

"Yes." Juniper wiped her face quickly, efficiently, the way she did everything. "So you can see my problem. Either I misjudged her fifteen years ago, or she spent fifteen years becoming someone worth the misjudging, and either way she's dead now, and I buried her teacup on a glass plain instead of telling her—anything. Any of it. That I understood. That I didn't. That I'd have come back if she'd ever once asked me to instead of trading on me in the dark." She exhaled. "Grief's a bad accountant. It only totals up what you didn't do."

Aldric was quiet a long moment. Then, slowly, painfully, he pushed himself up on one elbow. Fever-flushed, grey-lipped, trembling with the effort, but his eyes were clear.

"My turn," he said.

"You should be asleep."

"I will be. After. Fair trade, Crane—you gave, now I give." He settled back against the wall, breathing carefully, and began. "You know I was Ashen once. Everyone who knows my name knows that much—it's the interesting part of the scandal. What they don't know is why the order fell."

"Tell me anyway. I enjoy scandal."

"The Order of the Ashen Hand wasn't founded to hunt heartstone-magic. That came later, after the Steppe, when everyone suddenly needed someone to blame. It was founded to *guard* it. To keep the knowledge, hold the relics, watch the places where the shards hummed. We were librarians with swords. Deeply boring men. My grandfather served forty years and fought one battle, and complained about it for the other thirty-nine." A ghost of a smile crossed his burned face. "Then came the war, and the shattering, and the Order reinvented itself overnight as cleansers. Purifiers. Because an institution that exists to guard a thing cannot admit the thing is gone, and an institution that exists to be right cannot admit it was wrong. So we burned. Books first. Then people."

"Including your own."

"Including my own." He said it steadily, but his hand found Wren's shoulder in his sleep and rested there. "My master, Serah Ondile, found records—true ones, pre-war ones—showing the shattering was deliberate. Chosen. She brought them to the Grand Prior expecting horror and reform. Instead they declared *her* corrupted, purged her circle, and put the archive to the torch with the brothers who sided with her still inside." His jaw worked. "I was twenty-two. I carried out the books I could save and walked away, and they stripped my name and called it mercy, and I spent twelve years being a knight without a banner, drinking in towns whose names I didn't bother learning, telling myself I'd escaped."

"But you didn't believe you'd escaped."

"No one escapes an institution. You just carry a smaller copy of it around inside yourself." He looked at her across the fire, and the fever-light made his eyes look young. "Here is my confession, Juniper Crane, since you've been brave enough to make yours: when I caught you on the road with that map, I didn't ask to see it to protect you. I asked because I wanted it. Twelve years of exile, and the chance to finish what Serah Ondile died for walks past me wearing a stolen coat—and I thought, *mine.* I dressed the wanting up as duty within the hour, wanting always does, but I want you to know the raw thing underneath. I've been using you and the child to purchase back my honor. And the longer we travel, the less that purchase matters to me, and the more the two of you do, and I don't entirely know what to do with that arithmetic."

Juniper sat very still.

There it was, laid out in the firelight: his ledger, matching hers. Two people who'd told themselves clean stories and were only now, sick and tired and trapped in a weeping pass, admitting the stories had holes in them.

"Well," she said at last, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "That makes us even, then. You wanted the map. I wanted the sword. First week we traveled, I lay awake planning how I'd slip away if you turned on me—three routes out of every camp, I had them memorized."

"And now?"

"Now I mostly plan how to get breakfast out of you before Wren wakes up, because you cook like a soldier and eat like a horse and leave nothing." She threw a twist of scrub on the fire. "And I sleep through the night. Mostly. Which is either trust or exhaustion, and I've decided not to examine it too closely."

"Prudent," Aldric murmured. His eyes were closing. "Examination... ruins more than it fixes..."

"Go to sleep, Ser Vane."

"Juniper." His voice was nearly gone, sliding under. "Your year. Whatever Maren was buying with it—she bought you a year. Take that from the ledger. It counts."

She didn't answer. He was already gone, breath easing into something deeper, the fever still banked in him but bankable, survivable, if the wound stayed clean and the cold stayed out.

Juniper sat with the knife across her knees and let the silence be different than it had been. Lighter. Confession, it turned out, was like splitting firewood—you dreaded the swing, and then the log fell open, and there was all that pale clean wood inside, and you wondered why you'd waited.

Wren sighed in her sleep and glowed a little brighter, and Juniper reached over and tucked the blanket higher, and thought: *a year. She bought me a year. I'll carry that.*

She was reaching for the waterskin when she saw it.

Wren's arm, bare where the sleeve had ridden up, showed the faint mark below her elbow—the blessing Sable had pressed there, dismissed weeks ago as vanity, a general's flourish. Except it wasn't faint anymore. In the firelight it had a fine silver shimmer, like frost forming in a pattern, and as Juniper watched, frozen, the lines of it moved—crawled, precise as ink, pointing south.

Toward the Steppe.

Toward whoever had drawn it.

"Oh no," Juniper whispered. "Oh, you lying, smiling—"

Behind her, out in the dark beyond the broken wall, something answered: a single note, far off, high and clear. The hunting-horn of the Ashen Order.

And beneath it, fainter, wronger, a sound like glass singing.
