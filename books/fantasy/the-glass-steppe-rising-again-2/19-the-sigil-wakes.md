## 19. The Sigil Wakes

Wren had learned to count the camp's sounds the way other children counted sheep. Aldric's armor came off piece by piece, each buckle a small click, and then his breathing went long and even, the breathing of a man who had trained himself to sleep anywhere and wake at anything. Juniper made a different music: the soft scrape of a whetstone she never seemed satisfied with, the rustle of her pack being rearranged for the fourth time, and once—only once, when she thought no one was listening—a low humming of a song with no words that Wren had decided was hers alone.

Tonight there was a new sound. A faint, wet sizzle, like rain falling on a stove.

It was coming from her own arm.

Wren sat up in the dark beneath the leaning slab of stone they'd sheltered under since the Weeping Pass had spat them out, bruised and short one knight's dignity. The mark Sable had pressed into her skin—the blessing, the woman had called it, all warm hands and warmer words—had begun to glow. Not the friendly glow Wren knew from her own chest, the one that rose near the map like candlelight through honey. This was different. This was thin and sharp and wrong, a thread of light pulled taut beneath her skin, running from the crook of her elbow up toward her shoulder, and it hummed.

Not sang. Hummed. Like a fly trapped in a jar.

She looked at it the way you look at a spider on your blanket: very still, very politely, while planning to scream.

The pull started gently. That was the trick of it, she'd understand later. It didn't yank. It *invited*. Somewhere far to the south and east, past the pass and the bridge and all the places they'd bled to get away from, General Yara Sable was holding a scrap of paper or a bowl of water or maybe just her own clever hand, and the mark was telling Wren: *come back, little compass, come home.*

"No," Wren whispered, because she was six-going-on-seven and had firm opinions about being borrowed without asking.

The hum deepened. The thread of light thickened. And then it wasn't an invitation anymore.

* * *

Pain is difficult to describe when you are small and have never had a word for it beyond *ow* and its louder cousins. What Wren felt was less like fire and more like being *read*—like someone had opened her the way Juniper opened her maps, smoothing her flat against a table, tracing lines across her with a fingertip that never asked permission. The sigil drank. She could feel it drinking, feel her own light—that soft, secret shimmer she'd had as long as she could remember, the one that made the mine rats leave her alone and made Juniper go quiet and careful whenever it showed—being siphoned down her arm and out, southward, eastward, into the dark where a general waited with open hands.

Wren did the sensible thing. She tried to run.

Her legs didn't agree. The mark had rooted itself deeper than skin; it had found whatever part of her knew directions, the part that had sung her out of the collapsed mine and toward the map before she'd ever met these two stubborn adults, and it was turning her like a weathervane. Her body listed south. She caught herself against the stone slab, breath coming in small panicked hitches, and dug her bare toes into the cold dirt as if dirt were something you could hold on to.

*Come,* said the mark, in a voice that wasn't a voice but felt like one, felt like General Sable smiling down at her with that look adults got when they'd already decided what you were for. *You belong to the cause now, little one. You're going to help so many people.*

"I don't want to," Wren said out loud, which was brave, and "help me," which was not, but nobody has ever died of doing both at once.

Aldric woke the way he always woke: all at once, silently, with a knife already in his hand and his eyes finding her by the light of her own traitorous arm. For a heartbeat he simply stared. Then he was beside her, big hands hovering an inch from her skin, not touching, the way you hover around something burning.

"Juniper." His voice was gravel and calm. "Juniper, wake up. Now."

* * *

Juniper Crane woke angry. This was, Wren would learn over time, simply how Juniper woke; sleep was a country she visited reluctantly and left resentful, and being summoned out of it by a glowing child and a shouting knight did nothing to improve her diplomatic relations with the morning.

But she took one look—at Wren shuddering against the stone, at the light crawling up her arm like ivy made of lightning—and every trace of grumpiness fell off her face like a dropped cloak.

"Sable's mark." Juniper was already moving, pulling her pack open, dumping half its contents onto the ground without ceremony. Bottles rolled. A dried frog, which Wren had been assured was decorative, bounced twice. "I told her. I *told* her I didn't trust a blessing that came with eye contact."

"You told her a great many things," Aldric said. "Most of them impolite."

"And I stand by every one." Juniper knelt, took Wren's face in both hands, and looked at her—not at the arm, at her *face*—and something in Wren's chest unclenched just slightly, because this was the look Juniper gave maps she intended to keep. "Hey. Sprout. Look at me. Can you hear me?"

"It's pulling," Wren said, and hated how small her voice came out. "It's pulling me south and I don't want to go and my legs won't listen."

"Legs are notorious gossips." Juniper's thumb brushed Wren's cheekbone, quick and rough and kind. "We'll give them something better to talk about. Aldric—hold her still. Gently. She's going to want to fight me in about a minute and I need her to lose."

"What are you going to do?" Aldric asked, and there it was in his voice, the question underneath the question: *how much will it cost her?*

"Hedge-magic." Juniper was crushing something pungent between her palms—rosemary and salt and something darker, resinous, that stung the nose. "The kind they don't teach in towers. The kind Maren called 'kitchen work' right up until the day it saved her life." Something flickered across her face at the name, grief moving through like weather, and then it was gone. "It's going to hurt, Wren. Worse than the pull. I'm not going to lie to you about that, because lying to children is how you end up with generals."

"That's a strange lesson," Aldric said.

"It's the only one I've got." Juniper took Wren's arm in a grip that was iron wrapped in flannel. "Last chance to say no, sprout. It's your arm. Your choice. Nobody else's. That's the whole point of tonight."

And that—more than the pain, more than the fear—was what made Wren nod. Because General Sable had never once asked. She had smiled and blessed and decided, and here was this sharp-tongued smuggler woman kneeling in the dirt asking permission to save her life.

"Do it," Wren said. "Before my legs win."

* * *

Juniper worked fast and muttered constantly, which Wren later understood was less spellcraft than self-management, the way some people swear. Salt in a ring. Rosemary crushed into oil and painted along the sigil's crawling path. And then Juniper's thumb, pressed hard at the mark's center, beginning to *burn*—

Not metaphorically. Hedge-magic, Wren learned, was honest about what it was. There was no incantation, no thunderclap. There was only Juniper's ordinary human heat, drawn up and concentrated and pushed, the way you might push water through a cloth, except the water was fire and the cloth was a seven-year-old's arm.

Wren screamed. She was not ashamed of it later, though she pretended to be. She screamed and fought and Aldric held her the way he held everything—carefully, like it mattered, like breaking it was unthinkable—and through the white roar of it she heard Juniper talking, low and relentless, a rope thrown into dark water:

"I've got you. I've got you, sprout, I'm right here, you come back to *me*, do you hear? Not to her. Not to the south. To me. You're mine to worry about now, that's the arrangement, nobody signed anything but it's binding anyway—"

The sigil fought. That was the horrible thing. It *fought*, coiling in her flesh like a live wire, and for one endless moment Wren felt two wills pulling her in opposite directions like a wishbone: Sable's distant, patient hunger, and Juniper's furious, salt-stinking stubbornness.

Then Juniper said a word Wren was pretty sure wasn't magic at all, just a very heartfelt curse, and *pushed*.

The mark broke.

It didn't fade—it *shattered*, the thread of light snapping with a sound like ice on a pond, and the backlash ran up Juniper's arm and knocked her backward into the dirt. For a moment neither of them moved. Then Wren, gasping, looked down at her arm.

Where the blessing had been, there was now a scar: a pale, branching thing, like frost ferns or river deltas seen from very high up, seared permanently into the skin from elbow to wrist. It didn't glow. It didn't hum. It was just hers—proof of the night she'd been fought over and kept.

Juniper lay flat on her back, breathing like a bellows, her own hand blistered red.

"Well," she managed. "That went *fine*."

* * *

They didn't sleep again. Aldric built a small fire, mostly for the light, and Juniper bandaged her own hand with the brisk incompetence of someone who considered her body a coworker rather than a treasure. Wren sat between them, wrapped in Aldric's cloak, holding a cup of tea that was mostly honey because Juniper believed trauma should be sweetened wherever possible.

"Does it still pull?" Aldric asked quietly.

Wren listened inward, the way she listened for the map's song. Nothing. Just the ordinary hum of herself, and the fainter, stranger warmth of the two people beside her—which had been getting easier to feel all week, though she hadn't told them. Some things you kept like stones in your pocket.

"No," she said. "It's gone. But—" She hesitated, working at the edge of the thought. "She knows where we were. When it was pulling. She was *looking* through it. I could feel her looking."

Juniper went very still. "Then she knows we're past the Weeping Pass. Which means she knows exactly which routes are left to us." She stared into the fire, and Wren watched her do sums behind her eyes, ugly ones. "There's one road from here to the Steppe that Sable won't have guessed. One. And it goes through Glassford Bridge."

"The bridge is contested," Aldric said. "Order garrison on the north bank. Sable's scouts on the south."

"Yes," Juniper said. "Isn't that convenient. Everyone we're running from, gathered in one place, standing between us and the only door out." She laughed, short and humorless, then caught Wren watching and softened it into something more survivable. "Don't worry, sprout. I've smuggled through worse. I once moved a piano through a wedding."

"Why was the piano hiding?" Wren asked.

"It owed money," Juniper said gravely, and Aldric made a sound that in a lesser man would have been a snort.

But after Wren curled up—closer than usual, closer than she'd admit, with her scarred arm tucked against Juniper's side like a secret—she heard the laughter drain out of the adults' voices, replaced by the low urgent music of plans. And she lay awake a while longer, feeling the new scar cool on her skin, feeling the shape of what had happened settle over her like the cloak.

General Sable had marked her to be used. The Order wanted her dead for what she was. And Juniper—Juniper who smelled of salt and rosemary, who lied badly and loved worse at hiding it, whose hand was burned because she hadn't hesitated for even half a heartbeat—had chosen to be scalded rather than let go.

*Mine to worry about,* she'd said. *That's the arrangement.*

Wren decided, with the full solemnity available to a seven-year-old, that arrangements cut both ways. If anyone came for Juniper Crane or the broken knight, they would have to go through a heartstone-touched girl first, and the girl was done being a compass.

Somewhere south, a general was reaching for her again and finding only ash.

Wren smiled into the dark, and let herself sleep.

Outside the ring of firelight, far off along the southern ridgeline, a single signal flare rose against the stars—white, then red, then white again—and began to move north, toward Glassford Bridge, faster than any army had a right to march.
