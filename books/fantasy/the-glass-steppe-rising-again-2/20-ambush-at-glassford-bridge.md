## 20. Ambush at Glassford Bridge

The bridge was called Glassford because, three hundred years ago, a glassmaker had built it to prove he could. It spanned the River Sennet in a single graceful arch of green-tinted panes fused into something harder than steel, and for three centuries merchants had crossed it, children had dared each other to lick it in winter, and exactly one person had ever fallen through—which everyone agreed proved the glassmaker's point.

Juniper Crane had crossed it perhaps forty times in her life as a smuggler, and every one of those crossings had been boring.

She would have given anything, crouched now in the reeds on the south bank with Wren's small fingers laced through hers and Aldric bleeding quietly two paces behind her, for one more boring crossing.

"They're coming," Aldric said. He said it the way he said everything lately—through his teeth, conserving breath, because the wound in his side from the Weeping Pass had gone from angry to sullen and refused to heal properly no matter how much hedge-salve Juniper smeared into it. "Both columns. Listen."

Juniper listened. Under the river's chatter she caught it: boots. Many boots. From the north, the measured, unhurried tread of the Ashen Order—grey riders who walked like they had already won and were simply arriving on schedule. From the south, along the ford road, the looser rhythm of Sable's rebels, who marched like people arguing about whether they'd already won.

"Two armies," Wren whispered. Her glow was banked low under the cloak Juniper had wrapped her in, but even so, faint light leaked between the folds like a lantern with a bad shutter. "One bridge."

"One bridge," Juniper confirmed. "And us in the middle of it, which is the traditional place to be when everything goes wrong."

"You're joking," Wren said. It wasn't quite a question. Wren had learned, over the last weeks, that Juniper joked hardest precisely when things were worst, and had developed the habit of flagging each instance with that flat little note, like a scholar cataloguing a strange bird.

"A little," Juniper admitted. "Mostly I'm terrified. The jokes are load-bearing."

Aldric made a sound that might have been a laugh if it hadn't had to squeeze past his ribs first.

They had meant to cross at dawn, before either force arrived, and be deep in the marsh tracks beyond the Sennet by midday. But the Order had moved faster than the map promised, and Sable's column had moved faster than sense allowed, and now the sun was climbing toward noon and both roads fed onto the same narrow arch of ancient glass, and there was nowhere left to be except here: hip-deep in reeds that smelled of mud and rotting sweetgrass, with a knight who could barely stand, a child who glowed, and a smuggler's knowledge of terrain that had, for the first time in Juniper's life, run completely out of useful answers.

Because there were no tunnels here. No smuggler's cuts, no forgotten mine-shafts, no clever back ways through the mountain's bones. The Sennet valley was open country. You crossed the bridge or you swam, and swimming meant leaving Aldric behind, and Juniper found—to her considerable irritation—that this was no longer an option she could even pretend to consider.

"We split up," Aldric said again. It was the fourth time he'd said it. "I'll draw them. Grey cloaks remember me. I'm worth a hundred of you to them, dead or alive, and I move slow enough now that catching me will flatter them."

"No," said Juniper, for the fourth time.

"Juniper—"

"You can't even draw a sword without your side opening up like a wet sack. You're not drawing anyone anywhere except maybe a physician." She kept her voice down, but not all the way down; some things deserved volume. "We don't split up. That's the whole point of us. We are a *we* now. I decided it while you were unconscious, and I didn't take a vote, and I'm not taking one now."

Wren squeezed her hand. Said nothing. Squeezed again.

From the north, a horn. Long, grey, patient—the sound of a door closing. From the south, an answering horn, shorter, angrier, the sound of a door being kicked.

"Oh," said Juniper, "they're going to do it right on top of us."

* * *

What happened next happened fast, the way disasters always did—fast enough that afterward Juniper could never assemble the pieces in the right order, only hold them all in her cupped hands like river stones and turn them over and over.

The Order reached the bridge first. They came out of the birch stands in perfect silence, which was somehow worse than shouting—a hundred and fifty grey figures in a line as straight as a ruled page, ash-grey cloaks moving in wind that didn't touch anything else. Their commander rode forward alone, a tall figure whose face was wrapped in the pale bandages the Order gave its firebrands, and called across the water in a voice like dry paper:

*"Yara Sable. By authority of the Ash, surrender the vessel and the map-bearer, and your people walk away."*

From the south, Sable's voice came back over the reeds, carrying impossibly far, warm as a hearth and hard as a hoof-strike: *"The heartstone belongs to the people the war broke! Not to ash-monks, not to kings, not to anyone who'd hoard it! Come take it from them if you dare!"*

*"Vessel,"* Wren repeated softly. She had gone very still against Juniper's side. "That's me. They mean me."

"I know," Juniper said.

"I don't want to be a vessel."

"I know." Juniper shifted so that she stood squarely between Wren and the bridge, which was absurd—reeds were not armor—but felt necessary anyway, the way prayer felt necessary to people who didn't believe in gods. "You're a girl. You like frogs. You cheat at cards. That's what you are. Everything else is just weather."

Then both horns sounded together, and the world came apart.

The Order charged the bridge in ranks, shields locked, and the old glass sang under their boots—a high, clear note, three hundred years of merchant carts and daring children suddenly remembering its one true voice. Sable's rebels met them at the crown of the arch, and the sound of the collision rolled down the river like thunder learning to speak.

Juniper pulled Wren flat into the reeds and watched the thing she had spent her whole life avoiding: a battle, close up, without the mercy of distance.

It was uglier than stories. Stories gave battles choreography. This was mostly shoving and screaming and men falling off the bridge's edge into shallow water and not getting up, and the glass itself turning treacherous with blood so that soldiers skated and went down flailing, and above it all the Order's firebrands beginning to chant, and the air taking on that dry, burnt-paper smell that Juniper had last smelled coming off the burning waystation, and—

Maren. The smell brought Maren back whole: her hands on the door, her voice through the smoke saying *go*, her debt paid in the only coin she had left. Juniper's vision went white at the edges. She breathed. She breathed again.

"Juniper." Aldric's hand on her shoulder, heavy, steadying. "Stay with us."

"I'm here."

"Then tell me what you see."

She looked. Smuggler's habit—when you couldn't fight, you mapped. And the mapping showed her something the commanders on both sides plainly hadn't noticed yet, because they were looking at each other and not at the ground.

"The eastern stair," she said. "Under the bridge. The glassmaker built maintenance stairs—access to the under-arch. There's a landing halfway along, big enough for forty men, where they used to lower repair cradles. It's blind from the deck. Nobody fighting up top can see down there."

Aldric understood before she finished. "Wounded fall or get thrown off. They'll land on the stair."

"Some of them will." Juniper's jaw tightened. "Most will go in the water. The current's fast in the channel. But some."

"And you want to—" Aldric stopped. Looked at her. Looked at the bridge, where Sable's rebels were being pressed back step by step, where the Order's line advanced with that horrible patient inevitability, where wounded rebels were already stumbling toward the edges with grey blades behind them. "Juniper. Those are Sable's soldiers. Two days ago they were hunting us alongside everyone else. If we pull rebels off that field, the Order marks us. And if Sable learns we saved her people and then hears we've been lying to her about the route—" he shook his head slowly, "—there is no version of this where we come out clean. You understand that? There is no side of this we can stand on."

"Yes," Juniper said. "I know."

She watched a rebel soldier—a boy, sixteen at most, wearing a quilted jacket two sizes too big—take a blade across the thigh and go over the railing's broken stump. He screamed all the way down and landed on the hidden stair with a crack she heard through the battle-noise, and then he lay there, moving, alive, crawling toward the under-arch shadow with nobody to help him, because everybody above was busy killing and everybody below was busy hiding.

Her. Hiding. Juniper Crane, who had spent eleven years perfecting the art of being elsewhere, looked at that crawling boy and understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, that she was done.

Not brave. She wanted to be clear about that, even inside her own head. Not brave, not noble, not making some grand statement about the war or the stone or the shape of history. Just done. Done with running past people. Maren had died holding a door. The courier had died pressing a map into a stranger's hands. Everybody she had ever respected had, at the end, stopped moving away from trouble and moved toward it, and she was tired—tired down in the marrow—of admiring them from a distance.

"Aldric," she said. "Get Wren to the willow stand downstream. The big one, with the hollow trunk. If I'm not back in an hour—"

"Stop." His hand tightened. "If you're doing this, I'm doing this. My sword arm works fine. It's everything else that's decorative." He managed half a grin, grey-faced. "Besides. Someone has to carry them up the stairs. You weigh less than my rations."

"Mama." Wren's voice, small and fierce, from inside the cloak. "Come back. Both of you. That's an order. I'm the important one, the grey people said so, so my orders count extra."

Juniper laughed—cracked, wet, real—and kissed the top of the hood. "Yes, captain."

* * *

The under-arch of Glassford Bridge was cool and dim and full of echoes, and it smelled of river stone and old bird nests and, increasingly, of blood.

Juniper had been right about the stair. Within minutes of the fighting reaching its peak, the wounded began arriving—some crawling down on their own, some dragged by comrades, one thrown bodily from the deck who hit the landing hard and lay groaning with a broken arm and a rebel banner clutched in his fist like a talisman.

By the time the light began to fail, there were eleven of them on the landing and the shelf of rock beside it. Rebels, all of them—Sable's people, since the Order's discipline held even at the edges and grey soldiers fell backward into their own lines, not off the bridge. Eleven rebels, bleeding and burned and broken, lying in the dark under a battle they'd lost, waiting either for rescue or for the grey cloaks to come down the stairs and finish the arithmetic.

Juniper worked.

She worked the way she'd once worked smuggling routes—fast, methodical, unsentimental about the order of operations. Pressure first, then tourniquets, then the salve, then water. Aldric carried the worst of them up to the old repair-cradle platform where they could be moved out along the riverbank path, one trip after another after another, until his wound reopened and Juniper bound it herself with strips cut from her own cloak, snarling at him the entire time, and he sat still and took it, which told her more about his pain than any groan would have.

The boy with the ruined thigh was the sixth one she reached. He'd been crying without noise for an hour, she realized—crying the way soldiers learned to, silently, so as not to spend attention they might need.

"Hush," she told him, tying off the tourniquet. "You'll keep the leg. You'll limp at dances and blame the war, and girls will find it romantic, and honestly it will be insufferable within a year."

He stared at her. "Who—who are you?"

"Nobody," said Juniper Crane, who was currently the most wanted woman on either side of the Sennet, and passed him the water skin.

That was when the boots came down the stairs.

Three of them. Grey. A firebrand officer and two swords, descending into the under-arch with torchlight throwing their shadows huge against the glass, and the wounded rebels on the landing went silent as graves, and Juniper—kneeling in blood, salve on her hands, no weapon but a knife good for cutting rope and bread—stood up.

The officer saw her. Recognition moved across the bandaged face like a fish under ice. *"The map-bearer,"* it said, in that dry paper voice. *"Surrender, and the Order will weigh your cooperation favorably. The child also. Where is the child?"*

Juniper discovered that she was not afraid. This surprised her. She catalogued the surprise, set it aside, and heard herself say, in a perfectly level voice:

"There are eleven wounded men on this landing. Your Order's own creed says the Ash spares the broken—it's painted on your waystations. 'What is broken is finished; what is finished is at peace.' These men aren't finished. So either your creed means something, and you let us carry them out, or it doesn't, and every one of your soldiers chanting it upstairs should ask themselves what they're actually kneeling for."

The officer was quiet for a moment. Torch flame popped.

*"The creed governs the worthy,"* it said finally. *"These are traitors."*

"Then your creed governs nothing, and it never did, and you've all been kneeling to an empty word for a hundred years." Juniper's hands were shaking now. She let them. Some truths needed shaking. "I'm going to keep binding wounds. If you want to kill a woman with salve on her hands in front of eleven helpless men, go ahead. But write it down somewhere first. Write down exactly what the Ash does, so that in a hundred years, when someone digs up this bridge, they'll know what your word was worth."

Silence. The longest silence of Juniper's life, stretched over the sound of the river and the muffled war overhead.

Then the officer turned, and climbed the stairs, and was gone.

The rebels on the landing exhaled all at once, a long ragged sigh, and someone started laughing, and someone else started crying, and the boy with the thigh wound grabbed Juniper's sleeve and said, "Lady—they'll report you—you've made enemies of the whole Order—"

"I had those already," Juniper said. "I collect them. It's a hobby."

* * *

They got nine of the eleven out before Sable's rear-guard found the stair an hour later. The other two died quietly in the dark, holding hands, and Juniper closed their eyes and marked the spot with a strip of grey cloth torn from a dead man's cloak, because somebody should come back for them someday, and she intended to be that somebody.

General Yara Sable found Juniper at the foot of the stairs, bloodied to the elbows, supporting a limping boy on one side while Aldric took the weight of a man twice his size on the other.

For a moment the general just looked. Torchlight moved on her face, and Juniper watched her do the arithmetic—watched her arrive at what it meant that the woman she'd threatened, coerced, and marked with a false blessing had spent the battle hauling her soldiers out of the dark.

"You saved my people," Sable said.

"Your people saved themselves," Juniper said. "I carried some of them."

"That's the same thing."

"It really isn't." Juniper handed the limping boy over to a rebel sergeant and wiped her hands uselessly on her ruined cloak. "But you're welcome anyway."

Sable stepped closer. Her voice dropped, warm, urgent, that hearth-and-hooves tone that had nearly worked on Juniper once and knew it. "This is what I'm talking about. This. What you did today—that's the heartstone's work done by human hands. That's what power is *for*, Juniper, when it's held by someone with the will to use it rightly. Give me the chance to hold it rightly. Help me reach the Steppe, and there will never need to be another bridge like this one—"

"General." Juniper held up her hands, and saw Sable register the blood on them, and press on regardless.

"—and I know you doubt me. I'd doubt me too, in your place. But look at your knight. Look at your girl. Ask yourself who protects them better: a woman with an army, or a woman with a knife and good intentions—"

"General," Juniper said again, louder, and something in her voice must have changed, because Sable actually stopped.

"You misunderstand what happened on that stair today," Juniper said. "I didn't save your soldiers because they're yours. I saved them because they were hurt and I was there. That's the entire philosophy. That's the whole of it. There's no army version of it. There's no stone version of it. The moment you need a heartstone to do what I did tonight, you've already lost whatever it was you were trying to win."

Sable looked at her for a long moment. Something flickered behind the general's eyes—hurt, maybe, or the ghost of an argument she'd once had with a younger version of herself.

"You're a fool," Sable said at last, gently. "A brave one. The worst kind."

"Probably," Juniper agreed. "Good night, General. Tell your sentries we're camping downstream and we'd rather not be stabbed in our sleep; we've had a long day."

* * *

The willow stand was silvered with moonlight when they got back to it, and Wren burst out of the hollow trunk before they were even in earshot and hit Juniper at knee height hard enough to stagger her.

"You're late," Wren announced into her cloak. "You said an hour. It was four hours. I counted. I counted *everything*. Aldric, tell her she's late."

"He can't talk," Juniper said. "He's using all his strength staying upright."

"It's true," Aldric managed, and sat down against the willow with the grace of a felled tree, and laughed, and winced, and laughed again.

Later, when Wren had finally gone to sleep curled against Aldric's good side, her glow dimmed to the soft ember-light of a sleeping coal, Juniper sat apart with her knees drawn up and took honest stock.

Enemies on both sides now. The Order had her face and her name and a fresh grievance; Sable had her refusal and, soon enough, would discover the altered route and add betrayal to the ledger. Ahead lay the Steppe, and beneath the Steppe the humming shards, and around all of it two armies converging like weather fronts. Every road forward cost more than the last.

And yet.

Nine men breathing tonight who wouldn't otherwise be. A boy who'd limp at dances. Wren asleep, safe, counting hours. Aldric laughing through cracked ribs because laughter was load-bearing too—he'd learned it from her, she realized, and the thought warmed her more than the fire did.

She had made enemies on both sides. Fine. Let them line up. She knew exactly who she was now, and it wasn't anybody's map-bearer, vessel, or fugitive.

It was the woman with the salve on her hands.

She was still holding onto that thought, warm as a stone from the fire, when Aldric spoke quietly from across the embers:

"Juniper. The scouts Sable posted downstream just lit a signal lamp. Three flashes, then five." He paused. "That's not a patrol signal. That's a *summons*. Someone's coming up the river road tonight—someone Sable answers to."

Juniper looked at him. "Sable answers to someone?"

"Everyone answers to someone," Aldric said grimly. "Question is who, and why they need an army's worth of manners." Far off down the dark river, a second lamp answered the first—and its light, even at that distance, burned a color neither of them liked at all.

Ash-grey.
