## 21. The General's Bargain

Yara Sable had learned long ago that the most important battles were fought before anyone drew a weapon. She fought this one across a campfire, with tea.

The tea was terrible. That was deliberate. Good tea made people feel like guests; bad tea reminded them they were prisoners who happened to be warm. She watched the girl—Wren—blow on her cup with the solemn concentration of a scholar, and she watched the smuggler watch the girl, and she noted that Juniper Crane had not touched her own cup at all.

Smart. Cautious. Exhausted under it, though. Yara could see the seams now that she was looking: the way the woman sat angled so both exits stayed in view, the bruised shadows beneath her eyes, the left hand that kept drifting toward her belt knife and then away, like a dog told to stay.

And the knight. Aldric Vane stood at the edge of the firelight like a man attending his own funeral and hoping the eulogy would be short. His arm was bound from the Weeping Pass, his color was wrong, and he was standing anyway. Yara filed that away too. Men who stood when they should lie down were either devoted or stubborn, and in her experience the two made equally useful tools.

"More tea?" she asked pleasantly.

"I'd rather you got to the point," Juniper said.

"There *is* no point." Yara spread her hands. "That's what I keep trying to tell everyone. There's only direction."

Wren giggled into her cup. Yara let the sound sit there a moment. Children laughing in a war camp did more for morale than any speech she'd ever given, though she'd have flayed anyone who repeated that.

* * *

She had prepared for this conversation the way she prepared for everything: by learning everything there was to learn about the people across from her.

Juniper Crane, runaway apprentice of Maren Crane, hedge-witch of Thornfell. Smuggler of record on eleven routes through the mountains, wanted by three different jurisdictions for crimes ranging from tax evasion to "improper familiarity with locked doors." No family. No allegiances. A talent for finding ways where roads didn't go.

Ser Aldric Vane, last knight of an order the Ashen Order had burned out of its own history books. Excommunicated, disgraced, presumed dead twice—once officially, once by rumor, which had been more thorough. Carried a sword older than the kingdom and used it, according to the stories, exactly as often as necessary.

And Wren. Wren was harder. The records said nothing because there were no records. But Yara had eyes, and the girl glowed faintly near the map like a lamp near a window, and children who glowed were worth more than whole armies. Or they were worth nothing, if you broke them first. The trick—the entire art of command, as far as Yara was concerned—was knowing which things you must never break.

She refilled her own cup and began.

"You want to know why I haven't killed you," she said. "Or worse, handed you to the Order, which is much the same thing but slower and involves more paperwork. So let me save us all the suspense." She leaned forward. "Because you're going to take me to the Glass Steppe."

Juniper's face did something very small and very fast. Most commanders would have missed it. Yara had built a career on not missing it.

"I don't know what you mean," Juniper said.

"You know *exactly* what I mean, and you're wondering whether the sigil on your friend's arm is still working, and whether I've told my officers where you are, and how many arrows are trained on this campfire right now." Yara smiled. "The answers are yes, no, and none. I don't need arrows. Arrows are for people who won't listen."

"And if we won't listen?"

"Then I'll be sad, and you'll be dead, and the heartstone will end up in the hands of men who burn libraries." She sipped her tea. It really was dreadful. She'd have to speak to whoever was in charge of the tea, which was also her. "But I don't think you'll refuse. Not because I'm threatening the child. Because of what happened at Glassford Bridge."

Juniper went still.

"Yes," Yara said softly. "I saw you. Half my officers wanted to shoot you—you were standing between two lines of soldiers like an idiot with a death wish—but I gave the order to hold. Do you know why?" She didn't wait. "Because you ran *toward* my wounded while the Order advanced on your position. You dragged boys you'd never met out of arrow range while their own commander was deciding whether they were worth the cost of retreat. I've been a soldier for twenty years, Crane. I know what it looks like when someone can't stop caring. It looks like leverage, and it looks like hope, and usually those are the same thing."

"That's a horrible thing to say," Wren observed.

Yara blinked. Then laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her—and found, to her mild alarm, that it felt good. "It is," she agreed. "War teaches you horrible sentences. You get fluent."

"My mama says you're trying to trick us."

"Mama does say that," Juniper muttered.

"Your mama," Yara said, keeping her voice light even as something in her chest tightened at the word, the way it always did, "is right to say it. I *am* trying to convince you. Tricking implies I want something you don't want. But here's my wager, child: I think you want the same thing I do. You just haven't admitted it yet."

* * *

Here was the thing about Yara Sable that her enemies never understood and her soldiers understood completely: she had not always been a general. She had been a farm girl from the Vellish lowlands, and the last war had eaten her village the way a fire eats a barn—completely, indiscriminately, and then moved on without apology.

Her father had died carrying grain to soldiers who burned it rather than let the enemy eat. Her mother had died in the winter after, quietly, of cold and arithmetic—there wasn't enough food, and love doesn't fill stomachs. Yara, eleven years old, had learned the lesson that shaped everything after: *someone* decides who eats and who burns, and if decent people refuse the deciding, indecent ones will volunteer.

So she had volunteered. Twenty years of it. She'd fought for lords and against them, for rebels and beside them, and everywhere she went she found the same disease: people who wanted power in order to use it, and couldn't understand why the using always looked like the burning.

The heartstone, though. The heartstone was different. Everyone said so. The Order said it was too dangerous to exist. The old histories said its shattering ended the war. And Yara—who read everything, who had looted more archives than castles—had noticed what the histories skipped past: the war ended because the stone took *choice* away. Both armies stopped fighting because they stopped being able to choose anything else.

Which meant, if you thought about it honestly, that the stone was the most honest thing ever made. It didn't corrupt. It *concentrated*. Give it to a butcher, get slaughter. Give it to a builder—

Give it to someone who had spent twenty years watching decent people starve while indecent ones decided, and what did you get?

She intended to find out.

She just needed a smuggler to show her the door.

* * *

"The Order will destroy it," she said now, pacing the edge of the firelight, gesturing the way she did before battle speeches, though she softened it—these weren't soldiers, and speeches would spook them. "You've seen their archive-burnings. They call it purification. If they reach the Steppe first, the stone dies, and with it every chance of—" She caught herself. Recalibrated. "Every chance of using it properly."

"Properly," Aldric said. His voice was gravel and disapproval. "There's a word that's killed more people than plague."

"Careful, Ser Vane. Your order knew the truth and hid it. That makes you complicit in twenty years of ignorance."

"My order is dead."

"Then you of all people know what the Order does to people who know things." Yara crouched, bringing herself level with the fire, with the child, with the smuggler's wary eyes. "Listen to me. Both of you. I don't want a weapon. Weapons end wars by killing everyone capable of starting the next one, and then you wait a generation, and it starts again. I want something better. I want to *decide*, once, correctly, with the full weight of what that stone can do—and build a world where nobody's mother dies of arithmetic."

Silence. Wind off the pass. Somewhere behind her, a soldier's laugh and the clink of mess tins.

"You believe that," Juniper said slowly. It wasn't a question; it was an autopsy. "That's the awful part. You actually believe it."

"Belief is the only thing I've never counterfeited."

"And if the stone shows you something else? If it takes what you believe and—" Juniper stopped herself, jaw tightening. Whatever she'd seen or guessed, she wasn't sharing it with the enemy.

"If it amplifies me," Yara finished for her, "then the world gets twenty years of a woman who feeds villages first and asks questions later. I can live with that verdict." She smiled again, and this time let a little of the real thing through—not the commander's smile, the other one, the one she kept in a box with her mother's name. "Can you?"

* * *

They talked until the moon crossed the ridge. Or rather, Yara talked and Juniper parried, and Aldric occasionally contributed sentences like tombstones, and Wren fell asleep against the smuggler's shoulder halfway through the second hour, glowing very faintly whenever the map's presence came up, which Yara pretended not to notice and noted with great care.

The terms, when they finally took shape, were simple enough to say aloud:

Guide me to the Steppe. In exchange, the girl goes untouched—not conscripted, not studied, not so much as frowned at by any soldier under Yara's command. The knight keeps his sword and his dignity, such as remained. And when the stone was found, it would not be handed to any distant council or hidden king. It would be used, openly, in sight of everyone, for the people the war broke.

"And if I say no?" Juniper asked, one last time, because she was the sort who asked last questions.

"Then tomorrow morning my scouts find the trail the Order has already found, and I ride ahead of them instead of with you, and we all race to the Steppe like fools in a ballad." Yara shrugged. "I'd rather walk together. Fools in company travel farther."

Juniper looked at the sleeping child. At the knight swaying slightly on his feet. At the fire, the dark, the mountains full of ash-grey soldiers. Yara watched her weigh it—watched her actually *weigh* it, honestly, which was more than most people managed—and saw the exact moment the decision arrived, settling over the smuggler's face like frost forming on glass.

"All right," Juniper said. "We guide you."

Yara felt the victory settle into her bones, warm as brandy. She stood, offered her hand, and Juniper shook it—briefly, coolly, with the grip of someone closing a door rather than opening one.

"Welcome to the column," Yara said warmly. "We march at dawn."

* * *

Later, alone in her tent, Yara sat with her maps and her ledgers and allowed herself the small luxury of honesty.

She liked them. That was the inconvenient part. The child with her terrible blunt questions; the smuggler whose suspicion had the decency to be intelligent; even the broken knight with his tombstone sentences. In another life—in the life she'd have chosen, if the last war had left such lives available—she might have sat around fires like this one and traded stories and meant nothing by it.

But wanting things was how you lost them. Wanting things was how villages burned, because somebody, somewhere, had wanted more than his share. So she folded the liking up small and put it away, and looked instead at what was true: three exhausted travelers with nowhere else to go, marching willingly into her column, carrying her future across the plain in a moving map and a glowing child.

She checked the reports from her outriders. The Order's vanguard was four days back and gaining. She marked positions. She wrote orders. She thought about the stone, and what it would amplify in her, and did not let herself flinch from the answer, because flinching was for people who hadn't buried a mother in a famine winter.

At the tent flap she paused, listening. Across the camp, faintly, she could hear the smuggler's voice, low and steady, telling the child some story or other. The knight's deeper rumble underneath it. A little thread of warmth running through her army like a coal through ash.

Yara smiled at it. Then she called her quartermaster in and ordered double rations for three specific bedrolls—generosity costs nothing, loyalty is cheap at that price—and gave quiet instructions to Captain Ferro to watch the knight's wound, and gave quieter instructions to Lieutenant Ashe to watch the smuggler's hands at night.

Not because she distrusted them.

Because trust, like everything else worth having, was best guarded.

Dawn would come grey and cold, and the column would move east, toward the mirror plain at the world's edge—and Yara Sable would march at its head wearing warmth like armor, and no one in the world, least of all the three walking beside her, would guess that she had already counted the steps from alliance to ownership, and found the distance pleasingly short.
