## 22. Halfway Lies

Aldric Vane had marched in columns before. He had marched in triumphal processions down the marble spine of Corveth, in funeral lines behind the banners of his dead order, in retreat through the Saltmarsh with mud to his knees and arrows singing overhead. He had thought, somewhere in the years since, that he had learned everything a column could teach a man.

He had not learned what it was to march in one while lying to it.

The rebel column moved east along the old quarry road at a pace set by supply carts and optimism—mostly optimism, because Sable had promised her people they would reach the Steppe in nine days, and nine days was a number that looked well painted on a banner and fell apart entirely when confronted with an axle. They were on day four. The men and women around Aldric were farmers mostly, and weavers, and one extremely talkative farrier named Doss who had adopted Aldric sometime on the second morning and showed no sign of releasing him.

"You were a knight," Doss said, for perhaps the eleventh time. It wasn't a question anymore. It had stopped being a question yesterday.

"I was."

"And you left."

"The order ended."

"Ended how?"

"Dramatically," Aldric said, and adjusted the strap of his pack, and hoped that would be enough. It never was.

Doss leaned closer, lowering his voice with the delicacy of a man sharing state secrets over a fence. "Between us—I think the general likes you. She doesn't talk to half the column. She talks to *you*."

*She talks to me because she doesn't trust me,* Aldric thought, *and she wants me to know she knows.* What he said was: "I have an honest face."

Doss looked at him for a long moment. "Right," he said, in the tone of a man filing something away for later consideration, possibly under *liar*, possibly under *mad*. He clapped Aldric on the shoulder hard enough to rearrange his spine and dropped back toward the cart where his bellows rode.

Ahead, two ranks up and one across, Juniper Crane walked with their hood up and the map case slung against their chest like a second heart. Which, Aldric reflected, it had more or less become. They hadn't spoken since dawn. That was by design. Every conversation between them now had to be conducted in the language of glances and small gestures, because Sable's scouts drifted along the column like crows along a fence line, and crows notice when two travelers put their heads together.

The plan, such as it was, lived in those glances.

It had been born three nights ago, in the dark of the tent Sable assigned them—"assigned" being a generous word for a space between two supply wagons with one oilcloth stretched overhead—and it had been born out of necessity dressed up as opportunity. Sable believed Juniper was guiding her army to the Glass Steppe. Juniper intended to do exactly that, eventually, in roughly the right direction, but along a route of their own choosing: one that bought time, avoided the Order's known pickets, and above all did not deliver four hundred rebels to the vaults before Wren could be gotten clear. So each night, when the camp settled, Juniper sat by the fire with the map open on their knees and made small, patient changes to its inked roads—a bridge nudged west, a ford invented where none existed, a mountain pass drawn a day longer than it truly was. And each morning, Sable read the map and believed it, because Sable had no reason not to, and because the map was magic and magic was presumed honest.

Which was, Aldric thought, the single most dangerous assumption anyone in this army had made.

He caught Juniper's eye as the road bent around a rockfall. Juniper gave him a look he had come to know: *anything?* He answered with a fractional shake of his head: *nothing yet.* Then, because even conspiracies need levity or they rot from the inside, he added the smallest possible gesture toward Doss behind them and rolled his eyes.

Juniper's mouth twitched. It was almost nothing. It was also, he suspected, the first genuinely unguarded expression they'd worn in four days, and he found he'd walk the whole cursed quarry road twice to earn another one.

* * *

They halted at midday in the shadow of a burned granary—the war's fingerprints were everywhere in these hills, ten years faded and still fresh if you knew where to press—and Aldric found himself summoned to the command wagon.

Sable received him sitting on the wagon's step rather than inside it, which was a calculated thing; Yara Sable understood theater the way some people understood breathing. Her officers stood in a respectful crescent. Her map lay across her knees—the map, the true one, the one Juniper had touched last night by firelight while pretending to study weather.

"Vane." She tapped the parchment. "Tell me about this pass here. You served in the Thornfell garrisons before your fall. You'll know it."

*Aldric plays loyal escort.* That was the phrase Juniper had used, half-apologetic, the night the plan took shape, and he had accepted the role without flinching because it fit him like an old boot: comfortable, slightly humiliating, and better than walking barefoot. But there was a difference between playing loyal and standing in front of four hundred people whose general was asking direct questions.

"The Hollow Stair?" he said. "I passed through it once. Narrow. Defensible from the high side, death from the low. If the Order holds its head, you don't want it."

"If the Order holds its head," Sable said pleasantly, "we've already lost. They don't. They burn things." She looked up at him, and her eyes were warm, and that warmth was the most carefully maintained thing about her. "You've been useful, Vane. More than I expected. I want you to understand that when we reach the Steppe, there will be a place for you. Not as a hired sword. As a founder."

Around the crescent, officers shifted. One or two exchanged looks that Aldric catalogued without appearing to: surprise, resentment, calculation.

"That's generous," he said.

"It's practical." She rolled the map closed with a snap. "I'm rebuilding something, Vane. Not an army—an *order* of my own. A just one. And just orders need men who know what unjust ones look like from the inside." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "Men who've been betrayed by institutions and learned from it."

There it was. Not quite an accusation. Never quite. Sable didn't deal in accusations; she dealt in mirrors, held up at angles designed to make you see yourself the way she wanted you to.

"I'll consider it," Aldric said.

"Do." She smiled and dismissed him, and he walked back down the line of wagons with his pulse loud in his ears and his face arranged into something appropriately thoughtful, and only when he reached the rear of the column did he let himself exhale.

Juniper fell into step beside him as though by accident. They walked thirty paces in silence, past Doss (who winked, gods help him) and past the water cart and past a pair of soldiers arguing about whether glass could grow, which was a debate Aldric privately intended to settle for them once the world was less on fire.

"She offered you a founding place," Juniper said quietly.

"You heard."

"Hood carries sound. Old smuggler trick." They kicked a stone off the road, watching it. "What did you say?"

"I said I'd consider it."

"Did you mean it?"

Aldric considered his answer with the care the question deserved. "I meant that saying no outright would get us watched. Possibly separated from Wren." He glanced sideways. "Why? Would it trouble you if I meant it?"

Juniper was quiet long enough that a pheasant burst from the verge and startled them both. "No," they said at last. "That's the awful part. I keep waiting to feel betrayed, and instead I feel—practical. Like we're all just doing what keeps us alive, and I can't tell anymore whether that's wisdom or rot."

"That," said Aldric, "is called adulthood. I'm sorry. Nobody warns you."

And there it was again—that twitch at the corner of their mouth, quickly suppressed, quickly glanced away from, but real. He filed it away next to the eye-roll from the morning, in the small growing collection of moments that made the lying bearable.

* * *

The camp that night was pitched in a bowl of dead orchard, trees silvered and leafless, rows still obedient after a decade of neglect. Fires bloomed among the trunks like slow orange flowers. Somewhere a fiddler had survived the war and was proving it at volume.

Aldric took first watch over the tent where Wren slept—which was to say, he sat on a root forty feet away looking like a man resting, while Wren slept in fact against Juniper's shoulder inside, and the glow in the girl's skin had gone quiet as banked coals. Since the sigil had been burned out of her arm, the glow came and went with her moods. Tonight it had been calm all evening, a faint pearl light along her knuckles as she'd eaten her supper, and she'd fallen asleep mid-sentence, mid-complaint about the fiddle, mid-everything, the way children do when they finally trust the ground under them.

He heard Juniper come out of the tent a few minutes later. They didn't approach him. They walked the perimeter of the little camp instead, unhurried, a sentry inspecting nothing, until they arrived naturally beside his root with a tin cup of something hot.

"Cider," they said. "Stolen. Well—traded. Traded aggressively."

"From whom?"

"The quartermaster. I may have implied Doss vouched for me."

"Doss will be insufferable."

"He's already insufferable. I merely redirected the vector." Juniper sat down on the next root, close but not touching, and blew steam off the cup. For a while they drank in silence, listening to the fiddle butcher a reel.

Then, softly: "Tonight. While everyone's drinking."

Aldric nodded slowly. He'd known it was coming—the glances had been stacking up all afternoon, Juniper counting fires, counting patrols, timing the quartermaster's rounds. "How long?"

"An hour's work, maybe less. The route bends north after the river crossing. I'm going to make it bend harder. There's an old salt road through the Grey Fells—bad going, cart-hostile, exactly the kind of ground that makes a general send her army around and her scouts ahead." They turned the cup in their fingers. "Her scouts I can handle. Her army, three days behind us in the wrong valley, I can live with."

"And if she notices the ink moving?"

"Then I tell her the map does that sometimes." Juniper's voice was dry as chalk. "Magic maps are famously temperamental. Ask any hedge-witch. It's practically our founding lie."

It should have been funny. It was, a little. But underneath the joke Aldric heard the thing that had been growing in Juniper's voice for days now: the specific strain of a person carrying deception the way mules carry loads—not complaining, just bearing it, and bearing it, and the straps cutting deeper every mile.

"You hate this," he said.

Juniper didn't deny it. They looked out at the fires, at the soldiers laughing around them, at Doss teaching a boy of maybe fifteen how to hold a hammer properly with the patience of a man who'd forgotten he was talking to a child. "They're not monsters," Juniper said. "That's what nobody tells you about armies. You spend so long thinking of them as a wall coming at you, and then you're inside the wall and it's just—people. Tired people who miss their kitchens." A pause. "I altered the map last night too. And the night before. And every time, I think, one of these people is going to die in a ditch because I drew a road crooked, and then I think, and if I draw it straight, what happens to Wren? And then I drink stolen cider and do it anyway."

"Yes," Aldric said. "That's the job."

"What job?"

"The one where you choose, knowing both choices cost, and pay anyway." He looked at the tent where the girl slept. "I watched my whole order agonize itself to death over that exact arithmetic. Dozens of meetings. Years of it. In the end the ones who acted badly at least *acted*." He drained his cup. "Be bad at it if you must. But be done by midnight. Moon sets early tonight, and you'll want the dark."

Juniper studied him sidelong. "When did you become the encouraging one?"

"I'm told it happens to all knights eventually. Like grey hair."

* * *

The camp went down by degrees. The fiddler lost his audience to exhaustion around the second watch. Sable's command fire burned longest, her silhouette sharp against it as she spoke with her captains, and Aldric tracked it from his root with the patience of a man who had spent a decade learning to wait. When it finally collapsed to embers and the last captain stumbled away, Juniper moved.

They did it beautifully. That was the thing he kept forgetting to expect—how good they were. Juniper crossed the dark camp like a rumor, unhurried, unremarkable, a figure fetching water or checking a horse, and arrived at the cold firepit nearest the command wagon where the map had been left rolled in its case beneath Sable's folded cloak. Unrolled it by touch alone. Began to work.

Aldric kept his post at the tent and watched the shadows, and listened, and did the other thing he had volunteered for, the thing that made his stomach tight and his hands steady: he played loyal escort. Twice during that hour a scout drifted near the firepit, and twice Aldric rose, stretched audibly, and ambled over to share a word about the watch rotation—loudly, comfortably, a trusted man making conversation—while behind him, in the pit's shadow, Juniper's fingers coaxed rivers into new beds.

The second scout lingered longer than the first. Young, nervous, new to the night shift. "You're the knight, aren't you," he said. "The one the general favors."

"Favor's a strong word."

"She says you'll help build what comes after." The boy's voice dropped, reverent. "Is it true? About the Steppe? That there's something buried there that can fix everything? The taxes, the burned farms, my brother's leg—all of it?"

Somewhere behind him, ink crawled. Somewhere behind him, Juniper Crane held their breath.

"There's something buried there," Aldric said carefully. "Whether it fixes anything depends on whose hands hold it. That's always been true of powerful things."

"But the general—"

"The general believes it will." He put a hand on the boy's shoulder, gentle as he could manage. "Belief is a fine thing to march on. Just keep your eyes open on the march, eh? Roads lie. Maps especially."

The boy laughed, saluted clumsily, and moved on. Aldric waited until the footsteps faded, then returned to his root and gave the smallest nod toward the firepit. A minute later Juniper rose, re-rolled the map, replaced it precisely, and ghosted back through the sleeping orchard to sit beside him, breathing a little fast, cheeks flushed with the particular exhilaration of the successfully wicked.

"Well?" he asked.

"Done. The Grey Fells road is now the only sensible route, and it's a lie." They rubbed their ink-stained fingers on their cloak. "Three days' detour, minimum. Maybe four, if the river's high."

"Then we've bought ourselves time."

"We've bought ourselves time," Juniper agreed. And then, quieter, staring into the embers: "Do you hear that?"

He listened. Wind in the dead branches. The fiddler snoring. Ordinary camp sounds, ordinary night—

And underneath it, thin and rising from the tent like smoke through floorboards, a child's voice. Singing.

Wren's voice. But not a song Aldric knew, and not a tune a child should know either—it moved in intervals that made his teeth ache, ancient and glassy, and the glow was bleeding through the canvas walls now, pearl-white, brightening, brightening, and across the camp heads were beginning to lift, soldiers stirring at their fires, and Juniper was already running for the tent, already calling her name—

The singing stopped.

The glow died.

And in the sudden black, from the direction of the command wagon, came the unmistakable sound of General Yara Sable opening her map case.
