## 23. Wren Runs

Wren had learned a long time ago that grown-ups told you things were true by saying them in a certain voice. Low and steady, like water poured from a jug. *You're safe here. Nobody's going to hurt you. We're not like the others.*

Juniper used that voice. So did Ser Aldric, though his came out more like gravel being polite.

And tonight, around the fire in the hollow beneath the leaning stone, General Sable's gift-mark on Wren's arm had used it too.

She hadn't told them. That was the first thing she decided, sitting with her knees pulled up, watching Juniper portion out dried plums with the careful arithmetic of someone who'd been hungry before. She hadn't told them because if she told them, they would look at her arm, and then at each other, and then at her again, and something would change in their faces. Something small. The kind of small that was worse than big.

The sigil itched under her sleeve where Juniper had burned it out days ago. Burned it out — that's what Juniper had said, with her jaw tight and her hands shaking afterward, holding Wren so hard her ribs had creaked. But Wren knew what a scar was. And she knew what lived in this one, because it talked to her when she slept.

*Come south,* it said. Not in words exactly. More like warmth moving through her, like sun through a window, like the feeling of someone waiting for you somewhere with your favorite thing. It was a kind voice. That was the worst part. Sable's voice had been kind too, the day she'd pressed her thumb to Wren's forehead and called it a blessing, and Aldric had watched it happen and said nothing, and Juniper had been busy with the horses.

*Come south, little lantern. Come home.*

"I'm not a lantern," Wren whispered to the dark past the firelight.

Juniper glanced over. "What was that?"

"Nothing." Wren took her plum. It tasted like nothing at all.

* * *

Here is what Wren knew about being useful, and she knew quite a lot:

Useful children got kept. Useless children got left. She had worked this out at six years old in the mining town whose name she didn't say anymore, back when her skin still only glowed a little, like candlelight through fingers, back when people called it *charming*. Then the map had come into their lives, and the glow had grown, and *charming* had become *valuable*, and valuable was just another word for wanted-for-a-reason.

The Order wanted her dead or dissected; she wasn't sure which order those came in, and she suspected the soldiers weren't either. Sable wanted her as a compass. A compass! Like she was brass and glass, like you could tap her and point her north. And Juniper and Aldric —

Well. They were kind. She believed that the way you believe in rain: completely, but knowing it would stop sometimes.

But kindness didn't change the shape of the thing. Everyone wanted to use her glow. Even Juniper followed it down into the vaults under the Steppe. Even Aldric, who held her hand crossing bad ground, had looked at her shining in the dark with an expression Wren couldn't name but recognized anyway — the expression of a man seeing a door where he'd thought there was a wall.

And now the sigil in her scarred arm was pulling, pulling, pulling south toward Sable's camp, and every hour it pulled harder, and Wren could feel herself becoming a rope in a tug-of-war between armies, and ropes don't get asked. Ropes get pulled until they fray.

So she did the math, which Juniper had also taught her, though probably not for this purpose.

If she stayed, Sable's mark might drag her out of her own sleep someday and walk her south like a puppet. If she stayed, the Order would keep hunting all three of them, and hunting got easier when the hunted traveled together in a nice visible line. But if she went alone, into the mountains, away from everyone —

Then nobody could use her. Not the Order, not the general, not even the people she loved. Especially not the people she loved, because using-by-accident was still using, and it would hurt worse.

Wren waited until Juniper's breathing went long and even, until Aldric's snores settled into their familiar broken rhythm — he snored like a cart on cobblestones, and she would miss that, which made her eyes hot, so she stopped thinking about it.

She packed three things: the plum she'd saved, the river-stone shaped like a sleeping cat that Aldric had given her at Glassford Bridge, and one sock containing four buttons, because buttons were treasure and everyone knew it.

Then she wrote a note, because Maren — Maren who was dead now, who had died holding a door — had once told her that leaving without a word was how you broke people. Wren didn't want to break anyone. She just wanted to not be a weapon.

Her letters were crooked. Juniper had been teaching her, patient as spring. She wrote:

*I am sorry. I am not mad at you. I am going so nobuddy gets hert becuz of me. Do not follow. Plese. I mean it. — Wren*

She read it three times. She almost added *I love you*, and then she didn't, because if she put it down on paper it became real and then leaving would be impossible, and it had to be possible, it had to be, the way jumping off a dock has to be possible or you'd stand there forever.

She put the note under Juniper's boot, where it couldn't blow away.

And she walked into the night.

* * *

The mountain at night was a different animal than the mountain by day.

By day it was work — climbing, ducking, Juniper's voice ahead of her naming things (*scree there, mind the scree, step where I step*) — and work felt safe. At night it was all sound and breath and the enormous black shapes of pines, and Wren's own heartbeat loud in her ears like someone following her.

Which was silly, because nobody was following her. That was the whole point.

She cried a little for the first mile and then made herself stop, because crying made noise, and noise made her easy to find, and the entire plan was *not being found*. She wiped her face on her sleeve and pretended it was just cold wind.

The moon helped. It hung fat and silver over the eastern ridge, laying a pale road across the rocks, and Wren followed it the way you'd follow any sensible road. Below her, far behind, the valley where they'd camped sank into shadow. Somewhere in it, a fire had burned down to coals. Somewhere in it, two people she loved were asleep, and by morning they'd be awake and angry and frightened, and the frightened part was what she couldn't think about, so she thought about other things instead.

She thought about the sleeping-cat rock in her pocket. She thought about whether plums grew on trees or bushes (she'd forgotten to ask, and now she'd never know, unless — no, she'd see them again, obviously, someday, when everything was fine). She thought about the heartstone singing in her dreams, that vast slow voice under the glass, and how lately its song had started including her name.

That was the thing she hadn't told anyone, not even Juniper, especially not Juniper. The stone knew her. Called to her. *Wren, Wren,* it sang, like a mother bird, and she was terrified — genuinely, bone-deep terrified — that some morning she'd wake up already walking toward it, and her legs wouldn't be hers anymore.

Everyone assumed the danger was outside her. The Order coming, Sable's mark, the armies. But what if the danger was her? What if she was the trap, walking around in boots?

Better to be a lost child in the mountains than a loaded weapon among friends.

That was the sentence she carried with her up the trail, turning it over like the cat-rock. By the third mile she'd polished it smooth enough to believe.

* * *

Dawn caught her in a fold of the mountain, a green hollow where a stream ran shallow over stones, and Wren realized two things nearly at once.

First: she was very tired, her feet were blistered, and being a runaway was much less romantic than stories claimed. Stories never mentioned that you got hungry within four hours, or that your socks could develop opinions.

Second: she was being followed.

Not by soldiers. Not by the mark, which had gone strangely quiet since midnight, like it disapproved of the whole enterprise. No — by sound. A soft irregular scrape, stopping whenever she stopped, starting when she moved. She stood very still in the stream's cold chatter and listened the way Juniper had taught her. *Don't listen for footsteps. Listen for the gaps in the stream's noise. Anything alive makes gaps.*

There. Left and above. A gap, then another.

Wren's stomach dropped. Then it dropped further, because she recognized the rhythm of the movement — heavy, favoring one side, a limp-and-scrape she'd know anywhere, because she'd listened to it cross every bad stretch of trail from the Weeping Pass to here.

"Ser Aldric," she said loudly, "you're terrible at sneaking."

Silence. Then, from the bracken, with tremendous dignity, the scrape came closer, and Aldric Vane emerged looking like a man who had lost an argument with a hillside — twigs in his hair, mud to the knee, one hand pressed to his bandaged side where the pass had bitten him.

"You," he said, breathing hard, "were supposed to be asleep."

"So were you."

"Yes, well." He lowered himself onto a boulder with the groan of old wood. "One of us is seven, and it isn't me, so let's not pretend we have equal responsibilities here."

Wren wrapped her arms around herself. The stream ran on. "Juniper's going to find my note."

"She found it hours ago." He said it gently, but it landed like a dropped stone. "She's been tracking you since before moonset. I told her I'd go ahead, talk sense, spare you both the shouting part."

"There doesn't have to be a shouting part. There doesn't have to be anything part. You could let me go." Her voice cracked on *go*, which ruined it, so she said the rest fast. "Nobody asked me. Did you notice? The Order decides, the general decides, you two decide where we walk and when and what I'm for — and I'm sorry about your side, and your order, and everything, but I am not a *map*, Ser Aldric. I'm a person who happens to shine. And everybody keeps forgetting the second part."

Aldric was quiet a long moment. He picked up a flat stone and turned it over in his scarred fingers.

"When my order fell," he said, "they didn't kill us all at once. First they gave us a choice. Serve, or be scattered. And do you know what most men chose?" He didn't wait. "They chose to serve. Because serving feels like belonging, and belonging feels like safety, and men will swallow a great deal of poison if it comes in a cup labeled *safety*." He set the stone down. "You looked at that cup and knocked it out of everyone's hands and ran into a freezing mountain alone at seven years old. That's not nothing, Wren. That may be the bravest thing anyone's done on this whole wretched road, and I once watched a woman hold a burning doorway with her bare hands."

Wren's throat went tight. "Then you'll let me go?"

"No," said Aldric.

"Why not!" It came out as a shout, startling birds from the pines.

"Because letting a child vanish into Thornfell alone isn't respect, it's abandonment wearing respect's coat. I've seen that coat before. It fit me badly for years." He levered himself up off the boulder, wincing. "But I'll make you a bargain, and I'll keep it, because my word is the last thing I own that still works. Walk back with me — not because you're dragged, but because you choose it — and I give you my oath that from this day forward, nothing happens to you, near you, or because of you without your yes. Not Juniper's plans. Not mine. Certainly not any general's. Your yes or your no, and we live by it. That's the bargain."

Wren stared at him. The stream filled the silence.

"That's it?" she said suspiciously. "Just... ask me?"

"Asking is harder than deciding for people. Anyone who tells you otherwise has never tried it." His mouth twitched. "Also, Juniper is forty minutes behind me and gaining, and she looks, and I quote the last thing she shouted at me, 'like weather.' So whatever you decide, decide quickly, because arguing with weather is a losing trade."

Wren looked south, where the mountains folded away toward the Steppe and the singing stone and everything that wanted a piece of her. Then she looked north, back along her own crooked tracks, toward anger and worry and burnt-out sigils and someone who'd once held her so hard her ribs creaked.

Neither direction was safe. She understood that now, standing in the cold stream-light with her blisters and her four buttons. Safe had been the poisoned cup all along. There was only chosen and unchosen.

"If I come back," she said slowly, "the deal starts right now. First test. I say where we camp tonight."

"Agreed."

"And I get the biggest plum."

"I'll defend that clause with my life."

"And — " her voice wobbled, and she let it, because Aldric had said asking was hard, and maybe telling the truth was hard the same way, and she was done doing only the hard parts in secret — "and you tell Juniper I'm sorry. Not the note. Me. Out loud. Because notes can't cry properly."

Something in Aldric's face went soft, the armor sliding just slightly aside. "She knows," he said. "But I'll say it anyway. Words out loud are worth ten written. Maren taught me that too, actually. Strange how much of her is still walking around."

He held out his hand — not grabbing, just offering, palm open, the way you offer a perch to a bird you're not sure trusts you yet.

Wren looked at it. Then, upstream, she heard it: the quick crunch of boots on stone, closing fast, and a voice she'd have known blindfolded calling her name — furious and relieved and cracked right down the middle, weather indeed, a whole storm arriving at once.

Wren took Aldric's hand, turned to face the storm, and lifted her chin.

"We need to talk," Juniper Crane announced, skidding down the bank, wild-eyed and mud-splattered and holding Wren's crooked note crushed in her fist. "All three of us. Right now."

And Wren, seven years old, exhausted, brave, and — for the first time in longer than she could remember — *asked*, opened her mouth to begin.
