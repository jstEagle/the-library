## 24. Found in the Dark

The camp woke to an empty bedroll and a hole in the hedge.

Not a cut hedge—a *torn* one, branches snapped outward in a hurry, the way a rabbit bolts when the fox is already committed. Wren had gone through it sometime between the second watch and dawn, small enough to fit where the sentries couldn't follow without announcing themselves to half of Sable's pickets.

Juniper stood over the broken branches for a long moment before anyone spoke to her.

"She can't have gone far," Aldric said. His arm was still bound from the Weeping Pass, held against his chest like something borrowed. "A child alone, on Sable's roads, with the Order sweeping the valley—"

"I know how far she can go." Juniper crouched and touched the snapped wood. Still green-sapped. An hour, maybe two. "I know exactly how far she can go. I did it myself when I was nine."

"And you were found?"

"No." She stood, brushing bark from her palms. "That's rather the point."

Sable's people wanted to organize a sweep—riders fanning out along the river, dogs if they could be spared, a cordon at the ford. General Sable herself appeared at the edge of the campfire circle with her cloak still fastened for sleep, which meant she'd dressed quickly, which meant she understood what she was losing. A heartstone-touched child was not a soldier who deserted. She was a weapon walking off the field.

"We'll find her," Sable said. It wasn't quite a promise. Promises bend toward the person they're made to. This bent toward the Steppe. "Every road out of this valley is watched by noon."

"Then don't watch the roads," Juniper said.

Sable's eyes narrowed. "You have a better idea?"

"Yes. Everyone stay here."

* * *

They argued, of course. Aldric argued loudest, which for Aldric meant he said three sentences in a voice barely above conversation while everyone else shouted around him. Sable offered trackers. Juniper refused them. Sable offered herself. Juniper refused that harder, and was surprised to find she meant it—not because she distrusted the general, though she did, thoroughly and with receipts, but because whatever was about to happen in the dark hills belonged to her and Wren alone.

"You're going to walk into the mountains after a frightened child with no plan," Sable said.

"I'm going to walk into the mountains after a frightened child with no *map*," Juniper corrected. "There's a difference. Maps tell you where things are. They don't tell you why things left."

She took nothing but her coat, a heel of bread, and the flint she'd carried since she was eleven years old. At the treeline she paused and looked back once—at the fires, at Aldric standing with his good hand pressed flat over his heart as if holding something in, at Sable already turning away to speak to her captains—and then she stepped off the edge of the firelight and let the mountain close behind her like a door.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about searching: the instinct is to move. To cover ground, to check hollows, to call a name into every shadow until your voice goes hoarse and the name stops meaning anything. Juniper had been found that way once—or rather, *hunted* that way, the summer Maren Crane decided her apprentice's talent was worth more than her apprentice's trust.

She had been nine. She had run because she'd overheard Maren talking to a grey-cloaked visitor about "the girl's sensitivity" and "what the Order would pay for a tested subject," and she had done what children do, which is go small. She'd folded herself into the root hollow beneath the lightning-struck pine above the village, the one place she knew no adult body could reach, and she had stayed there while Maren searched.

Maren had called her name for two days. *Juniper. Juniper.* Louder each time, angrier each time, and each time the name came down the hill it pushed Juniper deeper into the hollow, deeper into silence, until the word stopped sounding like love and started sounding like a net being dragged through water. On the third day Maren stopped calling and started bargaining—shouted offers up the hillside, sweets, forgiveness, a pony—and Juniper had learned, curled in the dark with her knees against her chin, that everything offered loudly is offered to the searcher's own conscience, not to the lost.

Maren never found her. A trapper did, four days later, half-starved and feverish, and carried her down to the village where Maren wept over her so convincingly that Juniper almost believed the tears were for her and not for the near-loss of an investment.

So Juniper did not call Wren's name.

She walked instead. Slowly, deliberately slowly, the way you walk when you want the world to forget you're in it. The moon was a fingernail paring and gave her just enough light to keep from breaking her neck, and she used even that sparingly, keeping to the darker side of the trail where her eyes could drink the black. Around her the mountain went about its night business—an owl asking its one question over and over, wind combing the high pines, somewhere below the river worrying at its stones.

And she listened.

Not for footsteps. A frightened child who has grown up in a collapsed mine does not make footsteps unless she wants to. Not for breathing, either—that was a tracker's trick and trackers always assumed the quarry was stupider than they were. She listened for the shape of the silence. For the way a mountain sounds when it has a small tense heartbeat hidden inside it.

Because here was the other thing nobody tells you: hiding is loud. Fear is loud. A child alone in the dark spends the whole night *not* making noise, and all that not-making-noise piles up in one place like snow against a fence. You can't hear it. But you can feel where it isn't—the bird that won't settle on that particular branch, the rabbit path that goes suddenly unused, the stretch of hillside where the night insects hold their breath.

Juniper followed the hush.

It led her up past the old charcoal burners' terraces, over a scree fan that clattered treacherously whenever she forgot herself, along a ridge where the wind came through a notch in the rock and made a low mournful note like a bottle blown across. Twice she stopped and simply sat, cross-legged on cold stone, eating a little bread, doing nothing, being nobody. Patience, she had learned, was not the absence of urgency. It was urgency held very still, like a bird in cupped hands.

Near midnight she felt the silence change texture.

It was ahead and below—a fold in the hillside where a slab of granite had shrugged loose centuries ago and come to rest against the slope, leaving a wedge of darkness underneath. The kind of place Juniper herself would have chosen at nine. The kind of place, she realized with a small cold shock, she had *chosen*, more than once, in the early runaway years before Thornfell taught her better roofs.

She didn't approach. That was the whole trick of it. She sat down thirty feet away, put her back against a boulder, and spoke to the mountain in general.

"It's cold tonight," she said quietly. "I brought bread. There's no one else up here—I made sure. Sable's people are all asleep by their fire, and Aldric is sitting up worrying, which he's very good at, possibly his best skill after standing in doorways looking noble."

Silence. But a different silence. A listening silence.

"He's furious with himself, you know. He thinks it's his fault for arguing with you about the dreams. He told me—" she hesitated, then decided honesty was the only currency worth carrying tonight, "—he told me that when he was a boy, he ran away from his father's house twice, and both times what he wanted wasn't to be chased. It was to be *missed*. Correctly. By someone who understood what he was running from." She turned the flint over in her fingers. "I never had anyone miss me correctly. So I know how it feels to be looked for by people who are only looking for what they lost."

The wind moved through the notch. The bottle-note sounded and faded.

"You dreamed the stone singing again," Juniper said. "That's why you ran. Not because you're a coward—you've never been a coward, you faced down a general with your chin up—but because everyone keeps treating your dreams like directions. Like you're a compass that grew legs. And nobody asked whether the compass wanted to point anywhere at all."

Something shifted in the dark under the slab. Very small. The sound of a knee drawing up, maybe, or a breath caught and released.

"I'm not going to ask you to come back," Juniper said. "I'm going to sit here, and I'm going to eat this bread, and I'm going to be cold, because I deserve to be a little cold for not noticing sooner. And if you come out, we'll talk. And if you don't, I'll still be here when the sun comes up, and I'll still be here tomorrow, and I will sit on this mountain until one of us turns to stone, and I warn you, I'm very stubborn and I have excellent circulation."

A pause. Then, from the darkness, small and cracked and furious:

"You *altered the map*."

Juniper closed her eyes. Of course. Of course the child had heard—children hear everything, especially the things said in low voices beside banked fires. Halfway Lies. The route she and Aldric had secretly bent away from Sable's intended line.

"Yes," she said.

"You lied to her."

"Yes."

"To protect me." The voice shook. "Everybody lies to protect me. Maren lied. The Order lied. *You* lie. Nobody ever asks me what I want protected *from*."

Juniper sat with that. It deserved sitting with. She thought about defending herself—the map was forged, the route altered to buy time, the lie tactical and loving—and she heard, in advance, exactly how hollow it would sound, and she set it down.

"You're right," she said. "We should have asked you. We treated you like something precious instead of someone precious, and those aren't the same thing, and I knew that, and I did it anyway, because lying is easier than trusting a nine-year-old with the truth. Which is its own kind of insult, when you think about it. I insulted you by deciding you couldn't carry it."

Silence again. Longer. When the voice came back it had lost some of its fury and gained something worse—weariness.

"The stone sings louder every night," Wren said. "It knows where it is now. It knows we're close. And when I sleep it shows me the vaults, and the shards, and there's a door down there that opens for me like it's been waiting, and I don't *want* it to wait for me, Juniper. I don't want to be the key. Keys don't get to choose which locks they open."

"No," Juniper agreed softly. "But people get to choose whether they turn them. And I promise you—we will stand between you and that door if it comes to it. Not because you're fragile. Because you're *ours*."

Another long quiet. Then, wry, wet-edged, unmistakably Wren: "You're shivering. I can hear your teeth."

"Hedge-mages run cold. It's in the guild charter."

"That's not a real charter."

"It's extremely real. I wrote it."

And then—there, in the wedge of dark beneath the fallen stone—a small face, pale and tear-streaked and trying very hard to look unimpressed. Wren crawled out into the moonlight with pine needles in her hair and dirt on her chin and her arms wrapped around her knees, and she looked at Juniper sitting thirty feet away, not moving, not reaching, hands visible and empty, and something in her shoulders came down half an inch.

"You didn't call my name," she said.

"No."

"Maren would have called my name."

"Maren called mine for two days straight," Juniper said. "It's how I learned names can be nets. So I left mine at home."

Wren studied her for a long moment with those too-old eyes, the ones that glowed faintly at dusk now, the ones that had seen vaults in their sleep. Then she unfolded—one limb at a time, a hermit crab deciding the world might be survivable—and crossed the thirty feet of cold ground and sat down next to Juniper, not touching, but near enough that their coats brushed.

"I'm still angry," she announced.

"Good. Anger means you expect better from us. That's the healthiest thing anyone's felt in this entire army."

"I want to walk in front. Where I can see you."

"Done."

"And I want the truth about the map. All of it. Even the parts that scare me."

Juniper reached into her coat and produced the map itself, rolled and tied, and laid it on the stone between them like a treaty. "Then we start now. Watch the ink."

* * *

They came down off the mountain at first light, side by side, Wren in front as negotiated, moving at her pace. Juniper had given her the bread and most of her dignity, and in exchange Wren had shown her the dream-vaults in halting pieces—a stair of black glass, a humming like a struck bell that never finished ringing, a door with no handle that leaned toward her warmth. It was worse than Juniper had feared and clearer than she'd dared hope, and she filed every detail away with the cold diligence of a smuggler planning a route through country that wanted her dead.

The camp saw them coming and erupted—sentries shouting, runners sprinting, Aldric arriving at a pace just short of undignified with his bound arm forgotten and his face doing something complicated that he would later deny was relief. Sable emerged from her tent already armored, already composed, already calculating, and Juniper watched the general's gaze go to Wren first, weigh her, price her, and only then soften into something approximating warmth.

"You found her," Sable said.

"We found each other," Juniper said. "It's different. You'll have to take my word for it."

Aldric knelt—actually knelt, the disgraced knight, in the mud—in front of Wren. "I owe you an apology," he said. "For speaking about you as though you weren't in the room. It's a habit of my order I'm supposed to be ashamed of, and I clearly haven't finished being ashamed of it yet."

Wren considered him gravely. "You can carry my pack," she said. "As penance."

From Aldric Vane, former shield of a destroyed order, this was received with a solemn nod, as though he'd been granted a knighthood.

But it was Sable's reaction Juniper kept her eye on, because Sable's reaction was the weather report for everything coming. The general smiled at Wren—warmly, convincingly—and thanked the mountain and the stars and Juniper's cleverness, and ordered hot food and fresh horses, and every gesture of it was perfect.

And underneath it, brief as a fish turning in dark water, Juniper saw the recalculation. The child had run once. Would run again. Could not be trusted to walk willingly to the Steppe.

Which meant the pretense of partnership had a shelf life now, and Juniper could read its expiration date in the set of Sable's jaw.

That evening, as the column broke camp and the Glassford road opened southward, Wren slipped her small cold hand into Juniper's and said, quietly, so only she could hear:

"The song changed last night. After you found me." Her glow was faint under her sleeves, but Juniper could see it at her wrist, pulsing gently, like a second pulse. "It's not singing about the vaults anymore. It's singing about *who's coming*. And Juniper—" she swallowed, "—it says the Order is marching on the Steppe. All of them. Every grey cloak they have left. And they're not coming for the stone."

Juniper's hand tightened around hers. "What are they coming for?"

Wren looked up, and her eyes caught the morning light and held it wrong, glass-bright, terrified.

"For whoever touches it. They're coming to make sure there's no one left to carry it. And their vanguard reaches the plain in six days."
