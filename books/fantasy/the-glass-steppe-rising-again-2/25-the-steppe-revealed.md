## 25. The Steppe Revealed

Aldric Vane had believed himself past the age of wonder. He was forty-one years old, he had buried an order, a king's favor, and most of his illusions, and he had assumed that what remained of him was the sort of man who noticed weather and little else.

Then they crested the last ridge before dawn, and the Glass Steppe opened beneath them like a second sky laid wrong-side down.

He reined in without meaning to. Beside him, Juniper's mule stopped too, because Juniper had stopped, and Wren—half asleep in the saddle blanket slung across Juniper's back—lifted her head like a flower finding sun.

"It goes on," Juniper said flatly, as though accusing it.

It did go on. To every horizon, a plain of fused glass, pale green where the first light caught it, deep blue-green in the hollows, silver where the wind had polished it smooth over twenty years. No road crossed it. No tree broke it. It simply lay there, vast and silent and shining, the scar of the final battle of the Last War, where the heartstone had been shattered and everything living within thirty leagues had been burned into mirror.

Aldric found that his mouth had gone dry.

"I've read about it," he said, and heard how foolish that sounded, and said it anyway. "In the archive. Twelve accounts by survivors. Not one of them agreed on its color."

"They wouldn't," Juniper said. "It changes." They slid down from the mule and stood squinting against the growing light. "Maren told me once. She said the Steppe shows you whatever you're least prepared to look at."

"That seems unnecessarily dramatic."

"Maren was an unnecessarily dramatic woman who also tried to sell me to a death cult, so take her observations with salt."

Despite everything—the Weeping Pass behind them, the bandage on his side where the pass's angry magic had opened his ribs like a careless letter-knife, the three days of fever that had followed—Aldric laughed. It came out rusty. He was beginning to suspect that was simply how laughter sounded now, after everything: a hinge that needed oiling.

Wren wriggled free of the blanket and dropped to the ground, and then went very still.

Her skin had begun to glow.

Not brightly—not the alarming lantern-glow of the mine, or the desperate flare when Sable's sigil had nearly pulled her apart—but a soft, steady luminescence along her arms and throat, like moonlight trapped just under the skin. She was staring out at the glass with an expression Aldric had no name for. Reverence, perhaps. Or recognition.

"It's singing," she said.

* * *

They made camp at the edge rather than crossing at once, because Juniper insisted—and because, if Aldric were honest, none of them wanted to be the first to step onto it. There is a particular kind of fear that has nothing to do with danger. The Steppe would not hurt them. Probably. It was the other thing it might do that kept their boots at the boundary: it might *mean* something.

Juniper built a small fire anyway, which struck Aldric as either very brave or very stubborn, lighting flame atop the memory of a fire that had swallowed an army.

"Explain to me again," he said, settling carefully—healing ribs complained about saddles and bedrolls alike—"why we've come to the one place in the world every sane person avoids."

"Because the map brought us here."

"The map sank into your pack three days ago. It's been crawling toward this place since before you met me. That's not a reason, that's a current. One can be swept."

Juniper poked the fire. In the firelight their face was all sharp angles and reluctant honesty. "Because Wren hears it. Because the Order wants it destroyed and everyone near it. Because Sable wants it whole and everyone kneeling. And because I spent six days lying awake in the Weeping Pass thinking about what happens if we just stop, and turn around, and let someone else decide." A pause. "I didn't like any of the someones else."

"So it's spite."

"A great deal of good history is spite, Ser Aldric."

"Wren," Aldric said gravely, "your mother is teaching you cynicism again."

"She's not my mama," Wren said automatically, in the tone of a child maintaining a fiction everyone had privately abandoned. She was crouched at the very edge of the glass, one hand hovering above it without touching. "She's my Juniper."

"Ah. My error."

"And you're her Aldric."

"I am nobody's anything," Aldric said, "and I'd thank you not to spread slander."

But Wren wasn't listening anymore. Her glow had brightened, and she pressed both palms flat against the glass, and the whole plain—miles upon miles of it—shivered once, gently, like the surface of a pond accepting a stone.

The fire guttered. Juniper was on their feet instantly, knife drawn, scanning a horizon that offered nothing but light.

"Wren," Juniper said, in the careful voice they used for the girl now, the voice that had replaced the gruff deflection of the early weeks. "Sweetheart. What did you do?"

"Nothing. It said hello."

"It *said hello.*"

"Through my hands." Wren looked up, glowing faintly, utterly unbothered, and Aldric marveled—as he marveled daily—at how a child could carry the voice of a broken god-artifact in her blood and still complain about porridge. "It's been waiting a long time. It's lonely."

Juniper sat back down slowly. "Everything in this country is lonely," they muttered. "Mountains, artifacts, disgraced knights."

"I'm not lonely," Aldric said. "I'm selectively solitary. There's a difference, and it's mostly company."

That earned him half a smile from Juniper, quickly suppressed, and he counted it as a victory and banked it against the cold.

* * *

They crossed at midmorning, when the light was high enough to make the glass blaze, and Aldric learned why twelve survivors had described twelve different colors.

The Steppe changed underfoot. Where Juniper walked, it ran pale gold. Where Wren walked, it bloomed soft rose-white around her small boots, rings of it spreading and fading. Where Aldric walked—

Where Aldric walked, it went grey. A clean, honest grey, like slate, like ash, like the banners of his order before they burned.

He stopped walking and stared down at it for a long moment.

"Well," he said. "That's about right."

Juniper glanced back, saw the color, and for once had the grace not to joke. They simply waited until he caught up, and matched their pace to his, and said nothing at all—which from Juniper Crane was practically an embrace.

The silence of the Steppe was the strangest part. No birds. No insects. Wind moved across it and made no sound, as though sound itself had been one of the casualties. Their footsteps rang faintly, musical notes struck on a dead instrument, and each ring traveled outward in visible ripples of light that faded after ten feet, as if the plain remembered how to listen but not how to answer.

Halfway across the morning, Juniper unpacked the map.

Aldric had seen it do strange things—ink crawling like living things, coastlines rearranging themselves, a small red mark that had once been a waystation blinking patiently out of existence after Maren died. But here, unrolled on the glass, the map did something new.

It began to sink.

Not tear, not burn, not fly away. It simply grew translucent, edge to edge, the parchment becoming water, the ink becoming currents, until Juniper was holding a rectangle of clear liquid that sloshed gently and reflected the sky.

"No," Juniper said. "No, absolutely not, I carried you across a mountain range, you ungrateful—"

The liquid-map slipped through their fingers like a handful of creek water and poured itself onto the Steppe.

For a heartbeat it lay there, a perfect rectangle of brightness on the glass. Then it spread, thinning, thinning, threads of silver running outward through the fused surface in branching lines—roads, Aldric realized, or rivers, or veins—a last map drawn in light, pointing everywhere and nowhere, and then even that faded, and the Steppe was only the Steppe again, vast and silent and keeping its own counsel.

Juniper knelt where it had gone, hands hovering over the glass, and Aldric watched them grieve a piece of parchment. He understood it more than he expected to. The map had been the mission. The mission had been the shape of their days. Whatever came next had no edges yet.

"It's done its work," Aldric said quietly. "Maps are allowed to retire."

"It could have retired *after* telling us where the vault entrance is."

"Perhaps it did." He nodded ahead. "Look."

Far out across the plain—perhaps two miles, perhaps five; distance lied politely on the Steppe—there was a break in the mirror-smoothness. A dark seam, a jagged line where the glass had cracked and healed crooked, and around it the surface glowed with a faint pulse, slow and rhythmic.

Like breathing.

Wren tugged Juniper's sleeve. "That's where it lives," she said. "Underneath. It wants us to come down. It says—" her brow furrowed, listening to something the rest of them couldn't hear, "—it says it's sorry. About the war. It says it didn't mean to."

"Artifacts don't apologize," Aldric said.

"This one does." Wren's chin lifted with a stubbornness Aldric recognized, with a jolt, as Juniper's. "And it says the men who broke it are still down there. Some of them. Waiting."

The warmth went out of the morning all at once.

"Waiting," Juniper repeated.

"For someone to come down and choose." Wren shrugged, the universal gesture of children delivering apocalyptic news. "It doesn't say what kind of choosing."

* * *

They walked toward the seam because there was nowhere else to walk, and because retreating now would mean carrying the knowledge of it home through country full of people who wanted it, and Aldric had learned—his order had died teaching him—that knowledge you try to bury grows teeth.

His side ached. His order was ash. His king had called him traitor with a straight face, and the last friend from those days had died in the Weeping Pass with Aldric's name in his mouth. By every measure he knew, Ser Aldric Vane was a finished thing.

And yet.

He watched Juniper walk ahead with Wren's hand tucked in theirs, the smuggler who had once threatened him with a fish-gutting knife over an insult to their mule, the runaway apprentice who had chosen—again and again, badly, bravely—to keep walking toward the thing instead of away from it. He watched the glass bloom gold under their boots and rose-white under Wren's, and he thought: *finished things don't get to be afraid for someone else.*

He was afraid for them. Terribly, specifically, uselessly afraid. Which meant, by his new arithmetic, that he was not finished after all.

"You're limping," Juniper said, without turning around.

"I'm walking with dignity."

"You're limping with dignity. Sit when we reach the seam. That's not a request, knight."

"Yes, Mother."

"Don't make it weird."

Aldric smiled at the back of their head, and the glass under his feet stayed grey, and he found he didn't mind it so much. Grey was honest. Grey was what remained when the burning was done. And if the stories were true—if the heartstone amplified what a person already believed—then perhaps grey was simply the color of a man whose beliefs had been boiled down to a few hard, survivable things:

Guard the small ones. Keep your word. Walk toward the seam.

Ahead, the dark crack in the world drew closer, pulsing its slow light, breathing its long breath. Wren's grip tightened in Juniper's hand. Somewhere far below, patient as stone, something that apologized for wars began, very softly, to sing.

And the ground beneath the seam—Aldric saw it clearly as they came within fifty yards—was not empty.

There were shapes in the glass. Standing figures, fused mid-stride, twenty years old and perfectly preserved: soldiers of the last battle, caught forever in the moment the heartstone shattered. Dozens of them. Hundreds. All facing the same direction.

All facing the seam.

All facing *down.*

"Oh," said Wren, in a very small voice. "I think I know what kind of choosing."

The nearest figure turned its head.
