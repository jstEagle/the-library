## 26. Beneath the Mirror Plain

Wren had decided, somewhere around the third hour of walking across a plain made of broken sky, that she did not like it here.

It wasn't the cold. Cold was familiar; cold was just the mountain being itself. It wasn't even the glass, exactly, though the glass was strange — the whole Steppe was one smooth sheet of it, green-grey and faintly cloudy, like the ice on the millpond back in Thornfell, except that when you looked down you didn't see your boots. You saw light. Not reflection-light. *Other* light, deep down, moving slow as honey.

It was the humming.

Nobody else seemed to hear it. Juniper walked ahead with her hood up and her shoulders doing their careful-not-to-look-nervous thing, and Ser Aldric limped along beside her with his bandaged arm held close, and both of them kept saying things like *nearly there* and *just past that ridge* in the bright voices grown-ups used when they wanted you to stop asking questions. But Wren could feel it through the soles of her feet, up through her shins, settling into her teeth like a song someone was singing three rooms away.

*Come down,* the humming said. Or maybe: *come home.* She couldn't tell which, and that was the part she didn't like.

"Your hands are glowing again," Aldric said, without turning around. He had a way of noticing things sideways, out of the corner of his eye, like he didn't trust anything to behave if he looked at it straight on.

Wren stuffed her fists into her armpits. "Sorry."

"Don't apologize." Juniper glanced back. Her face was pale under the freckles, but she smiled the smile she saved for Wren specifically, the crooked one. "Just tell us if it gets louder."

"It's not loud," Wren said. "That's not how it works. It's more like—" She hunted for the word. Maren would have known the word. Maren had known all the words, before the fire, before the door, before the terrible smoke-smell that still lived in Wren's nose at night. "It's more like full. Like a cup filling up. And I'm the cup."

Juniper stopped walking.

Aldric stopped too, and for a moment the three of them just stood on the endless mirror with the wind combing over them from nowhere, and Wren watched Juniper do the thing she did — the quick flick of her eyes, left, right, behind, the smuggler's habit of checking every shadow even when there were no shadows, only light.

"We don't have to go down," Juniper said quietly. "You know that. We could turn around. We could walk until the map stops pulling, and then we could find a town with bad bread and good gossip, and we'd never speak of this place again."

"You'd hate that," Wren said.

"I'd *love* that," said Juniper. "Bad bread is my favorite food group."

But her hand found Wren's shoulder, and it was shaking, very slightly, and Wren knew — the way she knew things now, the way she'd been knowing things since the mine, since the glow started — that Juniper meant it and couldn't do it. The map was gone, sunk into the glass two days ago like a stone into a pond, but it hadn't really gone anywhere. It had gone *down*. And whatever pulled it pulled everything else too: Juniper's guilt, Aldric's honor, Wren's own strange bright blood.

"We're almost there," Wren said. "I can tell because my teeth are buzzing."

"That's either the heartstone or you need lunch," said Aldric. "Possibly both."

They walked on.

* * *

The way down opened in front of them the way secrets open — all at once, and only when you stopped looking for it.

One moment the glass stretched unbroken to the horizon, and the next the ground simply wasn't there anymore: a shaft, wide as a barn, dropping straight into the earth, its walls the same fused glass as the surface but darker, deeper blue, shot through with veins of gold that pulsed — Wren was sure they pulsed — in time with the humming.

Steps spiraled down the inside of the shaft. Old steps, worn smooth, each one carved — no, *grown*, she thought, they'd been grown — from the same glass, and each one lit from within by a soft amber glow that brightened as Wren came near and dimmed again behind her, so that descending felt like being carried by something patient.

"Oh," said Juniper. "Oh, I hate that."

"You hate everything," said Aldric.

"I hate *stairs that watch me*. That's a reasonable hatred. That's a well-earned hatred." But she went first anyway, because that was Juniper: she hated things and did them first so nobody else had to be scared alone. Wren had noticed that about her early on and had never told her, because telling her would make her embarrassed, and embarrassed Juniper got snippy, and snippy Juniper made worse tea.

Aldric went second, sword drawn, which Wren privately thought was silly — what was he going to stab, the architecture? — but she understood it too. He needed his hand on something real. Everyone needed their hand on something real down here. That was probably why the stairs glowed: so you wouldn't have to hold the dark.

Wren went last, and the stairs loved her best. By the tenth step the glow around her was so bright it threw her shadow up the walls, huge and wavering, a giant girl-shape walking on the ceiling of the world.

"I look tall," she reported.

"Frighteningly so," said Aldric.

"Like a grown-up."

"The most frightening thing yet," said Juniper, and Wren laughed, and the laugh fell down the shaft ahead of them and came back changed, softer, multiplied, until it sounded like a hundred people laughing together in a room below.

The humming got fuller.

* * *

The bottom was a city.

Not a city like Thornfell, all crooked lanes and leaning roofs. This was a city the way a jewel is a city — vaults and galleries opening off a central hall vast enough to swallow the whole waystation where they'd hidden from the Order, ceilings lost in golden gloom, columns of glass rising like frozen waterfalls. Everything was empty and everything was waiting. Dust lay on the floors in perfect undisturbed drifts, centuries deep, and Wren felt a pang of sympathy for it, the poor dust, sitting here keeping the shapes of everyone who'd ever walked away.

And everywhere — *everywhere* — the shards.

They grew from the walls and floors like crystals in a cave, some small as her thumb, some taller than Aldric, all of them faceted and clear and lit from inside with that deep-down light she'd seen beneath the surface of the Steppe. They hummed. Of course they hummed. A thousand of them, ten thousand, each one a piece of the same song, and when Wren stepped into the great hall the song *recognized* her.

She felt it happen. It was like walking into a room full of relatives she'd never met who all knew her name.

The nearest shard flared white. Then the next. Then the next, a ripple of light running outward through the vaults in every direction, and the humming rose into something almost like voices, almost like *singing*, and Wren stood in the middle of it with her arms out and her skin blazing gold and felt, for one dizzy moment, completely and perfectly known.

Then she felt the other thing underneath it. The thing she'd been trying not to feel since the pass.

The shards weren't singing *to* her. They were singing *through* her. Testing her voice. Trying it on. As if they were deciding whether she fit.

"Wren." Juniper's voice, sharp with fear. "Wren, come back, love, come *here*—"

Wren blinked. The flare died down to embers. Juniper was halfway across the hall toward her, having apparently abandoned dignity entirely, and Aldric was behind her with his sword up at nothing, and Wren realized her feet had carried her forward without asking her permission.

"I'm okay," she said. Her voice echoed strangely. "I'm okay. It just — it knows me."

"Yeah," Juniper said, reaching her, gripping her shoulders, looking her over like a hen inspecting a chick for hawks. "That's what worries me."

* * *

They made camp in an alcove off the great hall, because Aldric's arm had started bleeding again on the stairs and he refused to say so and Juniper refused not to notice, and the argument about it — conducted entirely in raised eyebrows while Wren pretended to explore — ended with him sitting on a glass bench looking martyred while Juniper unwound the bandage.

"It's clean," he said.

"It's *seeping*."

"It's seeping cleanly."

"You're the worst patient in forty kingdoms. I've smuggled brandy past customs officers with better attitudes than you."

"Customs officers don't have to listen to you talk."

Wren grinned and wandered the edge of the alcove while they bickered, because bickering meant normal, and normal meant safe, or near enough. The shards here were smaller, clustered like flowers. When she held her palm near one, it leaned — actually *leaned*, the way a plant leans toward a window — and warmth poured into her hand like sunlight through a jar.

*Hello,* it hummed. Or she imagined it hummed. *Hello, hello, hello.*

"Hello," she whispered back.

"What've you got?" Juniper called softly.

"They're friendly." Wren hesitated. "I think they're lonely. They've been down here a long time."

Something crossed Juniper's face — quick, complicated, gone. Wren had learned to read those crossings. That one was grief wearing a practical mask. Juniper was thinking about Maren. Wren was almost sure. Maren, who had known all the words, who had held the door, who was buried somewhere far above under ordinary dirt instead of beautiful glass, and who would have given *anything* to see this.

"She'd have liked it here," Wren said, before she could decide whether to say it.

Juniper's hands stilled on the bandage. For a long moment she didn't answer, and Aldric, wisely, studied his boots.

"She'd have tried to sell pieces of it," Juniper said finally, and her voice wobbled and steadied. "And then she'd have sat down right there and cried about how pretty it was. Both things. At the same time. That was Maren."

"Yeah," Wren said. "Both things at the same time." She touched the little shard again, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping cat. "I miss her loudly."

"I know," Juniper said. "Me too. Quietly. But me too."

* * *

Sleep wouldn't come.

Wren lay between them — Juniper on one side, Aldric propped against the wall on the other, both pretending not to arrange themselves protectively and failing utterly — and stared up at the vaulted dark, and listened.

The song was clearer now. Down here, away from wind and distance, she could almost pick out threads: not words, exactly, but *shapes* of meaning, the way you can understand a dog's bark without translating it. There was a thread like sorrow, old and worn smooth. A thread like pride. A thread like warning, sharp and repeating, over and over, the way a bell rings before a storm.

And underneath all of them, a thread like a door, slightly open.

*Come down further,* that thread said. *Further. Further. The heart of it. The first of it. Come and see.*

"I can hear it planning," Wren whispered into the dark.

There was a pause. Then Juniper's voice, sleepless and dry: "Planning what?"

"Where it wants me to go."

Another pause. She heard Juniper sit up. Heard Aldric's hand move to his sword out of pure habit, though there was nothing to fight down here except possibly the architecture, and even Aldric had conceded that stabbing architecture was a last resort.

"Does it want to hurt you?" Juniper asked. No cleverness in it now. Just the question, bare.

Wren considered honestly. That was another thing the grown-ups had taught her without meaning to: consider honestly, even when the honest answer is scary, especially then.

"No," she said. "It wants to *show* me. That's different." She rolled onto her side. In the shard-light her own hands glowed faintly against the floor, two small lanterns. "It's like — you know how you get when you find a locked room? Juniper, you go all quiet and your fingers do the twitchy thing. You don't want to break the lock. You want to *know*. It's like that. All the time. About everything. And I'm a locked room it's been waiting ages to open."

Juniper was quiet for a long moment.

"And do you want to be opened?" she asked, very carefully.

Wren thought about the mine, where she'd lived alone in the dark with glowing hands and no name anyone used kindly. She thought about the waystation burning. She thought about the general with the warm voice and the cold eyes, and the sigil that had burned her arm, and Juniper's face while she burned it out — furious and terrified and *hers*, entirely hers, nobody else's.

"I want to choose," Wren said. "That's all. Whatever it shows me. I want it to be because I said yes."

Above her, Juniper let out a breath that shook only a little. "Then we say yes together or not at all. Deal?"

"Deal," said Wren.

From the wall, Aldric rumbled, half-asleep: "I'll stand in front of whatever it is. On principle."

"You can't stand in front of a *song*, Ser Vane."

"Watch me."

Wren smiled into the dark, and the dark smiled back in ten thousand tiny lights, and eventually — impossibly, wonderfully — she slept.

* * *

She woke because the song changed.

Not louder. *Closer.* The thread like a door had become a thread like a staircase, and her body was already sitting up, already standing, already three steps out of the alcove before her mind caught up and grabbed its own collar.

*No,* she told herself firmly. *We knock first.*

She padded back and crouched by Juniper, whose eyes were already open — of course they were; Juniper slept like a cat in a house full of dogs — and put her mouth close to her ear.

"It wants to show me something. Now. Tonight. I'm going, and I'd rather you came, but I'm going either way, because I said yes and I meant it."

Juniper stared at her. Wren watched her wrestle it — the fear, the wanting-to-say-no, the promise from hours ago, *together or not at all* — and Wren loved her fiercely for wrestling it at all, for not just scooping her up and running, which was the old Juniper thing, the runaway thing, the thing Juniper was slowly teaching herself out of.

"Together," Juniper breathed. She reached over and kicked Aldric's boot. "Up. Knight business."

"There's no such hour as *now* o'clock," Aldric said, but he was already reaching for his sword.

They followed the glow — Wren's glow, spilling off her skin and answered by the shards as she passed, corridor after corridor lighting up like breath on cold glass — down through the vaults, past galleries full of shapes under dust sheets that Wren deliberately did not look at, past doors carved with soldiers and kings and a woman holding a star like a lantern, until the corridors ended at a chamber that was not carved at all.

It was *grown*. One single bubble of glass, round and seamless, and in its center, floating a hand's width above the floor, hung a shard bigger than any they'd seen — tall as two men, faceted like a cut gem, turning slowly, slowly, casting light that moved like water across the walls.

And standing in front of it, though the dust on the floor showed no footprints at all, was a figure.

It was shaped like a person. It was made of the same light as the shards. Its face was kind and ancient and terribly sad, and when it turned to look at them — at *Wren*, at the cup that had come to meet the flood — it smiled, and the smile was the loneliest thing Wren had ever seen.

"You came," it said, and its voice was the humming, the whole song, all at once. "Little vessel. Little lamp. We have waited so long for someone to carry the truth."

Behind her, Wren heard Juniper's breath catch, and Aldric's sword whisper free of its sheath.

The guardian raised one luminous hand.

"Before you take another step," it said, "you must hear what happened to the last ones who did."
