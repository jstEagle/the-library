## 27. The First Guardian's Warning

Aldric Vane had spent thirty years learning to read a room for danger—the angle of a door, the weight of silence, the way a host's smile didn't reach the eyes. He had never, in all those years, learned to read a room that was itself the danger.

The vault beneath the Glass Steppe was circular, or nearly so, its walls of fused glass worn smooth as sea stone. Their footsteps made no echo. Light came from nowhere and everywhere, the faint amber glow Wren carried in her skin bouncing off every surface until the whole chamber seemed to breathe. And in the center, on a pedestal of black basalt, stood a figure.

It had been a person once. That much was clear from the shape—a tall woman, robed, one hand raised as though mid-gesture, caught forever in the act of explaining something important. The preservation magic had done strange work over the centuries. Her robes had gone translucent at the edges, her face smoothed to porcelain blankness, and her hair—this was the detail Aldric found himself unable to look away from—her hair moved, gently, continuously, as if stirred by a wind that existed nowhere else in the sealed vault.

"She's dead," Juniper said flatly. "Right? Tell me she's dead."

"She is preserved," said the woman.

Juniper made a sound like a kettle deciding against boiling. "She's *talking.*"

"I am preserved," the guardian repeated, with what Aldric could only describe as patience worn down to its last thread, "which is not the same thing. Please do not touch the pedestal."

Wren stepped forward before either of them could stop her, her glow brightening until the shadows fled to the walls. "Hello," she said. "I'm Wren."

The guardian's porcelain face turned toward the child. Something shifted behind it—not expression exactly, but the memory of expression, like sunlight moving under ice.

"Ah," said the guardian softly. "You've been drinking from the well."

"We haven't been near any wells," Juniper said.

"The heartstone, child of Thornfell. She carries its light in her blood. I can taste it from here." A pause. "It tastes like the year the war ended."

---

They had come down through three levels of vaults following Wren's glow, past shelves of glass tablets that crumbled when touched, past doors carved with armies marching in both directions, past a corridor where the air still smelled faintly of smoke after four hundred years. Wren had walked ahead the entire time, unerring, humming fragments of a song none of them knew. Now they stood in this final chamber, and the guardian waited on her pedestal like a teacher who has been waiting four centuries for students who would not stop being late.

"You are here for the stone," the guardian said. It was not a question.

Aldric stepped forward, because this was the part he'd rehearsed in his head for two hundred miles. "We're here to understand it. There are people coming who want to use it, and people coming who want to destroy it, and we—" He faltered. In rehearsal it had sounded more noble. "We thought if we understood it, we might find a third thing."

"A third thing." The guardian's hair rippled in its private wind. "In four hundred years, forty-one parties have stood where you stand. They wanted to take it, break it, sell it, worship it, or marry it—that last one was an emperor, and we don't speak of him. You are the first to want a third thing." Another pause. "I am going to tell you why there isn't one. I apologize in advance. It is not a pleasant lesson, and I have had four hundred years to make it concise."

Behind him, Juniper muttered, "I already hate this lecture."

"You should," said the guardian. "Sit if you can. Standing listeners argue more."

---

There were no chairs. There was, however, a low ledge of glass along the eastern wall, worn smooth by what Aldric realized must have been generations of anxious visitors. He sat. Juniper sat beside him, arms crossed, radiating the particular defiance of someone who intends to be convinced by nothing. Wren did not sit. Wren sat down directly on the floor in front of the pedestal, cross-legged, chin in hands, glowing like a small lantern, and said, "Tell us about the war first. Nobody ever tells me anything in order."

The guardian looked at her a long moment.

"The war," she began, "was fought by everyone, against everyone, over everything. Land. Water. Gods whose names you would not recognize and whose existence I could not confirm. By its fourth decade, the kingdoms had ground each other down to hatred wearing armor. There were no lines left worth holding. Only the fighting remained, and the fighting fed itself."

Her voice was even and old, the voice of someone reciting something she had recited ten thousand times, and yet Aldric heard, underneath it, the tremor of a woman remembering.

"There was an order then too. Not yours, knight. Older. They were called the Wardens of Concord, and they believed that hatred was not a choice but a sickness—and that a sickness could be cut out. They built the heartstone. Every artifice of their age went into it: binding-craft, memory-work, things your scholars will call impossible because your scholars are correct. The heartstone did not make peace. Understand that. It did not soften anyone. What it did was simpler and far worse."

The wind in the guardian's hair stilled.

"It gathered. Every intention held within its radius—every ambition, every grievance, every dream of conquest—it drew into itself and held fast, the way amber holds a fly. Men woke on battlefields unable to remember why they'd marched. Kings sat in their halls unable to lift a pen to sign a war order. The stone drank the *wanting* out of the world, and without wanting, the war simply... stopped. Like a fire with its air cut off."

"That sounds like a good thing," Wren said.

"It ended the war," the guardian agreed. "And it ended everything else. Do you know what a person is, little lantern, once you remove all their wanting? They are a body that eats and sleeps. Nothing more. The Wardens discovered this within a season. Whole cities sat down in the streets and forgot to be hungry for supper. Mothers looked at children they loved and felt—nothing. Not hate. Nothing. Love is a kind of wanting too."

The silence in the vault was total. Even Juniper had stopped radiating defiance; she was leaning forward now, elbows on knees, and Aldric saw her knuckles white around her own wrists.

"So the Wardens broke it," Aldric said quietly. "The stone. That's the history my order kept—the heartstone shattered, the world woke, and the shards were buried so no one could reassemble them."

"The Wardens broke it," the guardian confirmed, "at the cost of every life in this room. Eleven of them stood around it while it drank, and let it drink, until there was nothing left in them to hold onto, and they fell like cut strings. The last one—my teacher—shattered the stone with her dying breath. The shards scattered. The Steppe above us is where the great shard struck the earth, and the glass is what became of the land when the stone's death passed through it." Her porcelain face turned slowly, taking in each of them. "That was four hundred years ago. Since then, the shards have hummed in the dark, and the world above has fought smaller wars, with ordinary hatred, which I am told is preferable."

"It is," Juniper said, with feeling. "Ordinary hatred you can at least argue with."

"And now," the guardian continued, "you have come asking whether it can be destroyed entirely. Whether the shards can be unmade, so that no army ever digs here again." She paused. "I have asked myself the same question for four hundred years. I will give you the answer I have earned. No."

---

"No?" Juniper's voice cracked upward. "No as in *difficult,* or no as in—"

"As in the stone cannot be destroyed. It can only be *carried.*" The guardian's hair resumed its motion. "Do you know what the heartstone is made of, at its center? Not crystal. Not craft. It is made of wanting. Every desire it has ever swallowed is still inside it, compressed past bearing. That is its substance. You cannot destroy wanting, child of Thornfell. Kill a man and his wanting passes to his sons. Break a kingdom and its wanting rises in the next one. The stone is only wanting given a body—and bodies can be broken, but wanting finds new ones."

"Then the Wardens failed," Aldric said. His mouth had gone dry. "Shattering it bought four hundred years and nothing else."

"They bought more than that, but less than they hoped. Listen carefully, because this is the trap, and I will say it plainly." The guardian leaned forward—or seemed to; the preservation magic bent her like reflection bends a face in water. "The shards can be gathered. They want to be gathered; that is their nature. And whoever gathers them—whoever *holds* them, even one shard, for long enough—becomes their vessel. The stone does not corrupt them. I need you to hear this, because forty-one parties before you have assumed corruption, and corruption implies a fight you might win. The stone does not corrupt. It *concentrates.* Whatever the carrier most deeply believes, the stone amplifies until it fills them to the edges and pours out into the world."

"Then a good person—" Wren started.

"A good person becomes terribly good," the guardian said. "A healer becomes a plague of mercy, healing whether the sick consent or not. A judge becomes a sentence without appeal. A liberator becomes a storm that frees people from their own choices. And a tyrant—" she spread her translucent hands— "a tyrant needs no stone at all. He does fine work alone. The stone takes whatever is truest in you and makes it the only truth left standing. And here is the prison, knight, since you asked for the trap defined: once the stone has concentrated you, you cannot put it down. You will not *want* to. Setting it down would feel like setting down your own spine. Carriers have starved rather than release it—not from greed, but because releasing it feels like dying. Some of them begged us to take it from their hands. We did. They thanked us with their last breaths and hated us with the breath before."

Aldric sat very still. Thirty years of soldiering had taught him to recognize the moment a plan dies—not with a crash but with a quiet click, like a lock turning. He heard it now.

"And the Order above us," he said. "The Ashen Order. They believe the stone must be destroyed, and everyone touched by it with it."

"They are half right," said the guardian. "Destruction is impossible. But burning away everyone who *carries* even a fragment of its light—" her gaze settled on Wren, and the temperature of the room seemed to drop— "that they can do. They have done it before. Why do you think the vaults above are empty? We were forty guardians once. We are one."

Juniper had gone very pale. "You're saying the Order purged the keepers."

"I am saying the Order's founders decided that a thing which cannot be destroyed must be starved instead—of carriers, of vessels, of anyone the shards might concentrate. It is a policy of extraordinary cruelty and impeccable logic, and I have had four hundred years to admire both." The guardian's tone did not change, but something in it did. "They will burn this child, knight. Not out of evil. Out of arithmetic. You should decide, before they arrive, what you intend to be: the argument, or the exception."

---

For a while nobody spoke. Wren reached out and patted the pedestal's base, the way one pats a frightened horse, and the guardian looked down at her with what Aldric could have sworn was envy.

"How do you stay?" Wren asked. "Down here. All alone. For four hundred years?"

"Because someone must warn the next ones," the guardian said. "And because I cannot leave. Preservation binds as surely as the stone does. We are sisters, she and I—she holds the wanting, and I hold the warning, and neither of us may set our burden down." A pause, and then, gentler: "You glow very brightly, little lantern. Brighter than the last ones. Do you dream of the shards?"

Wren nodded. "They sing. Mostly at night. Lately they've been singing louder, like—" she frowned, searching— "like when you're almost home and you can smell supper."

"Then hear my last instruction, and carry it better than you carry the song." The guardian straightened, and for one instant the preservation thinned and Aldric saw the woman she had been—young, exhausted, terrified, resolute—before the porcelain closed over her again. "The stone cannot be destroyed. It cannot be safely held. It cannot be left, because armies dig. Forty-one parties have failed to find a third thing." Her voice dropped. "But you said you wanted one. So I will tell you what my teacher whispered as she died, the words I have rolled over for four centuries like a stone in the shoe, wondering if they were wisdom or only wishful thinking:

*'A burden shared by many hands is not lighter—but it is harder to steal.'*

She meant—I have always believed she meant—that the flaw of the stone is concentration. One vessel, one will, one truth grown monstrous. But wanting dispersed... Wanting dispersed is just people. Living, arguing, hungry, ridiculous people, each carrying a grain of it, each keeping the others honest." The porcelain face tilted. "Whether that is possible, I do not know. Whether it is survivable, I doubt. But you wanted a third thing, and that is the only third thing four hundred years have shown me. Take it or leave it."

Above them—faint, distant, filtering down through four hundred feet of glass and stone—came a sound that did not belong to the vault.

Drums. Many drums, marching in step.

Juniper was on her feet instantly, hand finding Wren's shoulder. Aldric rose slower, his knee protesting, and looked up at the ceiling as though he could see through it to the grey ranks assembling on the plain.

"They're early," he said.

"They are punctual," the guardian corrected. "The Order has always been punctual. It is their single virtue, and they practice it obsessively." Her hair stirred in its windless wind. "Go, then. Upstairs. Choose your third thing quickly—the Ashen Order brings shovels and fire, and I suspect the general you've been running from brings worse. And knight—"

Aldric turned back.

"When you stand between them and the child," the guardian said, "and you will—remember that the stone concentrates what is truest in a person. Ask yourself what is truest in you. Then go be that, deliberately. It is the only armor that works down here."

The drums grew louder. Somewhere above, glass began to crack.
