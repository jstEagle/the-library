## 28. Sable Arrives

Yara Sable had marched through mud at Redfern Crossing, had slept three nights in a snowdrift with her fingers frozen to her sword hilt, had once eaten a boiled boot and pronounced it *adequate* before four hundred laughing soldiers. She was not a woman easily impressed.

The vaults beneath the Glass Steppe impressed her.

Her torchlight ran ahead of them along walls that were not walls at all but frozen cascades — glass poured downward in slow amber rivers, catching the flame and throwing it back doubled, tripled, until the corridor seemed lit from within by a hundred small suns. Her column of thirty picked fighters moved in silence, which for Sable's people was a small miracle; usually they moved in argument, song, or complaint about the rations.

"Hells," breathed Tam Vetch, her sergeant, a broad man who'd once carried a wounded ox two miles on his back because it "seemed rude to leave him."

"Mind your language," Yara said mildly. "We're probably standing in someone's church."

"Whose?"

"That," said Yara, "is an excellent question I very much intend to answer."

The map-bond had died two days ago — she'd felt it go out like a candle pinched between wet fingers, all across her marked scouts, a collective flinch that had made six of them sit down hard in the snow. But it didn't matter anymore. The sigil on the child had burned itself out — Juniper Crane's work, no doubt, clever hedge-rat — but not before Wren's dreams had sung loud enough, long enough, for Yara's dreamers to catch the shape of the way down. Children sang in their sleep whether they meant to or not. It was one of the many things about children Yara found both beautiful and tactically useful.

So here they were, descending into the ribs of the old world while above them the Ashen Order mustered on the plain like a grey bruise on white skin.

She could hear the shards before she saw them.

It began as a hum in her back teeth, then in the bones of her wrists, then everywhere — a low sweet note like a wet finger run round the rim of a glass, except the glass was the whole world and the finger was something patient and enormous. The men behind her shifted. Old Marta crossed herself in the fashion of the southern provinces, which was useless up here but comforting, and comfort mattered more than correctness after thirty years of war.

"Steady," Yara said, and her voice came out strange, harmonizing faintly with the hum, and she decided not to think about that yet.

The corridor opened.

The chamber beyond was vast enough to have its own weather — Yara would swear there was a breeze moving through it, warm and mineral-scented, though they were fifty feet underground. The floor was smooth glass, and beneath the glass, light: veins of gold-white radiating outward from the center of the chamber like the roots of some inverted tree. And at the center, cradled in a lattice of fused crystal that might once have been an altar, a pedestal, a cage —

— a shard the size of a sleeping cat, turning slowly in the air, humming its single endless note.

Yara stopped walking.

Not from fear. From courtesy. There are moments that demand you arrive properly or not at all, and this was one. She had imagined the heartstone a hundred times: a jewel, a coal, a lump of divine slag. She had not imagined it would look like this — like a piece of morning caught mid-fall, like the exact color of the sky over Thornfell in the hour before her mother woke her to run.

"Well," said Tam, reverently. "There it is. The thing we've been walking toward since Redfern."

"The thing everyone's been walking toward since before Redfern," Yara corrected. "We're just the ones who arrived."

She started forward alone. Behind her, boots stayed planted. Good. This part wasn't theirs.

The hum deepened as she approached, and Yara felt it begin, gently, to speak.

Not in words. That was the first thing she noticed, and the thing she would remember longest: it did not use words, because words were too small for what it had to say. What it offered instead was *recognition*. It looked at her — if a stone could be said to look — and it saw Redfern Crossing, the mud, the boot, the snowdrift, the four hundred laughing soldiers who were now two hundred and eleven graves scattered across three provinces. It saw her mother's hands. It saw the ledger she kept, the one no one knew about, where every name lost under her command was written in her own blood-ink so she could never pretend they'd been numbers.

And it said — without saying — *all of that mattered. All of that can matter more.*

Yara stood in front of the floating shard and laughed, once, a short startled sound that echoed off the glass vaults.

"You're not a weapon," she told it softly. "You're a promise. No wonder everyone wants you buried."

The shard turned. Waiting. Patient as geology.

She thought of Crane — prickly, stubborn Juniper Crane, who had looked at Yara across a campfire with those sharp smuggler's eyes and said, *the last person who held that thing started a war, General, and she probably also thought she had excellent reasons.* Yara had liked her immediately and trusted her not at all, which was, in Yara's experience, the correct posture toward most people including herself.

Crane thinks power corrupts, Yara thought. Crane thinks the moment you touch it, you stop being yourself.

But Yara had spent thirty years watching powerful people fail, and she'd noticed something they never admitted: corruption was too flattering a word. Corruption implied something had gotten *in*. Most of them hadn't changed at all when they took power — that was the horror of it. They simply became, finally, without limit, exactly what they'd always been. The coward became a greater coward. The cruel man became cruelty with an army.

And Yara? Yara had always believed the broken deserved mending, and had been willing to break anything necessary to do the mending.

The shard hummed, and the hum sounded, just slightly, like agreement.

"All right," she said aloud. "But I want it understood that I'm doing this for them. Not for me." She gestured vaguely upward, toward the surface, toward the world. "You can check. Can't you. Go ahead and check."

The light beneath the floor brightened, which she chose to take as assent rather than amusement.

She reached out.

* * *

The first thing was warmth. Not heat — warmth, the specific warmth of a hand held in a cold season, of soup arriving unasked, of a bed already warm because someone got there first. The shard poured into her palm and up her arm and Yara gasped, because she had braced for pain and instead received what could only be described as *welcome*.

Then everything else.

She saw the war entire — not as she'd lived it, in fragments and fog and rumor, but whole, laid out like a map she could walk across. Every battle, every border shift, every treaty signed and betrayed. And threaded through it all, the heartstone's own history: how it had been raised, how it had been loved, how it had been shattered — deliberately, by people who feared choice more than slaughter — and how its essence had scattered into earth and water and the marrow of living things, waiting, patient as geology, to be gathered again.

*I can finish it,* the shard said, still wordlessly, still unmistakably. *I can make it so no one ever chooses wrongly again. Give me your grief and I will give you a world where no one grieves.*

"No," Yara heard herself say, even as her fingers closed around it. "No — give me a world where no one *has to*. There's a difference. I want there to be a difference."

The warmth pulsed. Amused? Agreeing? With the stone, Yara suspected, the distinction would soon stop mattering, and some distant clear-eyed corner of her noted this the way a soldier notes the bridge going out behind her.

Power came next.

She had expected it to feel like strength. It felt instead like *clarity* — like cataracts falling away after forty years. Suddenly she could see the whole chamber perfectly in the dark. Suddenly she could see *through* the chamber, through fifty feet of stone, up through the glass plain, where the Ashen Order's vanguard was breaking camp in the pre-dawn grey, ten thousand strong, their pyres already smoking. She saw their commanders' faces. She saw the writ pinned to the Herald's chest: *CLEANSE AND CONSUME. NOTHING THAT HAS TOUCHED THE STONE MAY LIVE.*

Including, she noted, the child. Especially the child.

Rage rose in her, clean and enormous, and the shard drank it gladly and gave it back multiplied, and for one vertiginous moment Yara Sable stood in a room underground and simultaneously stood on a windswept plain ten miles wide, and both of her bodies were angry, and the anger felt less like a feeling than like a country annexing her.

"General?" Tam's voice, far away, worried. "General, your eyes—"

"I know," she said.

She could feel them. Heat behind her face, a brightness that cast shadows — she could see her own shadow on the glass wall, thrown forward by light coming *out of her skull*, and her shadow's eyes were two molten pools, gold running to white running to something past white. She should have been terrified. Some part of her filed a formal complaint about not being terrified enough.

Instead she flexed her hand — her ordinary scarred hand, the one missing half the little finger to a Redfern pike — and watched light run along the old scars like dawn along a river.

"Marta," she called, and her voice came out layered, harmonized, one voice singing quietly underneath another. She cleared her throat. Tried again. Better. Mostly. "Marta. Report from the surface. How long do we have?"

Marta squinted up the corridor, listening to whatever the relay runners were shouting down. "Order's moving at first light, General. Two hours, maybe three. They'll reach the Steppe by midmorning."

"Then we hold the vaults," Yara said. "All of them. Every stair, every tunnel. Nobody touches the heart-chamber."

"And Crane?" Tam asked carefully. "Crane and the knight and—" he paused, diplomatic, "—the little one?"

Yara turned. The motion felt different now — smoother, as if the air itself were assisting. Through the stone she could feel them: three small bright knots of life somewhere in the lower galleries, moving fast. The child blazed like a struck match. Crane burned low and steady, banked-coal stubborn. The knight limped.

"They're here," she said. "Good. Then we end this properly — all of us, together, in the place where it started." She smiled, and felt her new eyes do something that made Tam take half a step back, bless his sensible heart. "Tell the men: today the war ends. One way or another, it ends today, and no one leaves this mountain having merely survived it. We leave having *won*."

Tam hesitated. Twenty years of soldiering lived in that hesitation. "General," he said slowly, "with respect. You're glowing."

"I'm aware, Sergeant."

"It's coming off you like heat off a forge."

"Also aware."

"And you're giving orders like a woman who's already won a battle that hasn't started yet."

Yara considered him. Through the shard's clarity she could see him perfectly — see the fear in him wearing loyalty's uniform, see the memory of Redfern behind his eyes, see the exact moment twenty years ago when a young lieutenant named Yara had pulled him out of a burning supply wagon and he had decided, silently and permanently, to follow her anywhere.

Anywhere, apparently, included here.

"Yes," she said honestly, because lying to Tam Vetch would be like lying to a favorite uncle. "That is precisely what's happening. And here's the terrible part, Tam." She held up her burning hand, watched the light play across his weathered face. "I've never been more certain of anything in my life. Whatever's happening to me — it isn't lying to me. It agrees with me. Everything I've ever wanted, it wants too. Tell me, Sergeant — in thirty years of this rotten trade — has *anything else* ever agreed with me?"

Tam's mouth worked. "No, General. Nothing's ever agreed with you. Not even me, mostly."

"There you have it." She turned back to the heart-chamber, to the lattice where the central shard had hung, now empty, its siblings dimming in respectful — or perhaps wary — silence around the room. "Now seal the stairs. Wake the sappers. If the Order wants to come down here, let them learn what it costs to dig."

* * *

They worked fast after that, and Yara worked fastest, because working had always been how she kept honest and she needed honesty now more than ever.

She set charges at the great stair. She warded the gallery mouths with wards the shard taught her directly into her hands, knowledge arriving like memories of lessons she'd never taken. She sent runners to the surface to harry the Order's advance, buying hours. Between tasks she caught her reflection in the glass walls — a tall woman wrapped in lamplight that wasn't lamplight, eyes like twin crucibles — and each time she made herself stop, and look, and ask the question:

*Are you still in there, Yara Sable?*

Each time, the answer came back warm and immediate: *more than ever. More Yara than ever before. All your grief, remembered perfectly. All your dead, honored completely. All your cause, finally possible.*

And that was the seduction, she understood at last, standing in the humming dark with molten light pooling in her eye sockets. It wasn't temptation. Temptation would have been easy to refuse — she'd refused plenty, wine and vengeance and the comfortable lie. This was worse. This was *consensus*. Somewhere in thirty years of war she had built a fortress of certainty around her beliefs, and the heartstone had simply walked in, hung its coat up, and declared the architecture sound.

*You were right all along,* it murmured through her bones. *You only lacked the means. I am the means.*

"Juniper Crane would say that's exactly what a trap sounds like," Yara muttered to the empty chamber.

The shard-light within her flickered — not in guilt, she was almost sure, but in something adjacent to it.

From far above, muffled by fifty feet of stone, came a sound like the world clearing its throat: the Ashen Order's war-horns, ten thousand voices answering them, and beneath it all, faint but unmistakable, the thin high note of a child singing in the deep galleries — singing fragments of a melody the heartstone itself had taught her, the same melody now coiling contentedly through Yara's veins.

Three notes matched perfectly.

Yara closed her luminous eyes and allowed herself one breath of pure, uncomplicated joy, because whatever came next, the stone was *hers*, the cause was *right*, and the ending — whichever ending — would be magnificent.

"Come down, then, ash-men," she said to the ceiling, and the ceiling glowed faintly where her voice touched it, and far away on the surface ten thousand grey soldiers felt the mountain warm beneath their boots and did not know why.

The horns answered.

The descent had begun.
