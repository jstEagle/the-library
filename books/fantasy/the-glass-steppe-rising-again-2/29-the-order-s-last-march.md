## 29. The Order's Last March

Juniper felt the Order before she saw them.

Not through any hedge-witch trickery—she'd never had much talent for the far-sensing that Maren could do with a bowl of water and a muttered word—but in her teeth, in the soles of her feet, in the old smuggler's instinct that had kept her alive through ten years of Thornfell winters. The mountain itself seemed to hold its breath. Somewhere above and behind them, beyond the shattered mouth of the vault stair, something vast was coming down through the Weeping Pass with the patience of an avalanche.

"They're singing," Wren said.

The girl stood at the edge of the heart-chamber's threshold, one small hand pressed flat against the glass-smooth wall. The shards embedded in the vault around them hummed their low, endless chord—the sound Juniper had first taken for wind trapped underground—and beneath it now, faint but growing, came another sound. Voices. Hundreds of them, chanting in unison as they marched.

"The Litany of Ash," Aldric said from where he leaned against a broken pillar, one arm pressed to his ribs. He was grey-faced, and every breath cost him, but his voice was steady. "They sing it when they march to burn something they consider unredeemable. I sang it myself, once." His mouth twisted. "It has a lovely harmony part."

"Aldric."

"I'm allowed to joke. It's either that or sit down, and if I sit down I'm not entirely certain I'll get up again."

Juniper crossed to him and eased him into a more upright position against the pillar, ignoring his token protest. Up close she could see how bad it was: the wound in his side from the Weeping Pass had soaked through two bandages, and his skin had gone the color of tallow. But his eyes were clear, and they were fixed on her with the particular stubbornness of a man who had decided exactly how he intended to spend what remained of his strength.

"You should be lying down," she said.

"What I should be," he said, "is dead three times over by now. I've stopped taking advice from the ledger of things I should be." He nodded toward the chamber behind her, where the great central shard rose from its bed of fused glass like a spear of frozen dawn. "How is our guest?"

"Still humming," Juniper said. "Still waiting."

That was the thing about the heartstone—or the largest surviving piece of it—that no one had warned them about. It didn't menace. It didn't threaten. It simply waited, radiating warmth and welcome, patient as a grandmother's open arms, while two armies converged to fight over who got to embrace it. Juniper had spent the last day half-dreading and half-longing for the moment she'd have to walk back into that chamber. The stone knew it, too. She was almost sure it did.

* * *

The plan—if you could dignify it with the word—had been made in the desperate hours before dawn, huddled among the humming shards with Sable's rebels holding the upper galleries and the Order's vanguard already visible as a line of torches winding down through the pass like a lit fuse.

Sable herself had come to them changed. Juniper had seen her only briefly since the confrontation in the heart-chamber's anteroom, since the moment the general had seized the central shard's edge with both bare hands and begun her slow, terrible transformation. Her eyes were molten now, amber shot through with veins of light. She moved like someone walking slightly above the ground. When she spoke of justice, and she spoke of little else, the words came out with a resonance that made the shards sing along, and Juniper had watched hardened soldiers weep at the sound of it.

That was, Juniper thought, precisely the problem.

"She can't be reasoned with anymore," she said now, low, crouched beside Aldric at the gallery rail overlooking the stair. Below them, Sable's people were fortifying the landing with whatever rubble the vaults offered—broken pillars, shards of ancient glass, the bones of a civilization that had learned this lesson once already. "You saw her. She thinks she *is* the argument now."

"I saw her," Aldric agreed. "And I saw four hundred of her soldiers ready to die on that stair because her voice makes them believe it's worth doing." He winced as he shifted. "The tragedy of Yara Sable is that everything she believes is true. The war broke these people. They were owed a future. The stone *was* stolen from them by men who wanted power without consequence. Every single word out of her mouth is correct." He paused. "And she will grind the world into glass to make those true words real, because the stone doesn't corrupt, Juniper. We know that now. It concentrates. And what it's concentrating in her is certainty."

Below, the singing grew louder.

Juniper looked down the long spiral of the stair, down past the galleries and the rebel barricades, to the far entrance of the vault system—a black archway that had been sealed with glass for a hundred years until Sable's engineers cracked it open. Through it, torchlight flickered. Grey robes. White masks. The Ashen Order had come in force, and not just a patrol this time, not just firebrands or hunters. This was the whole weight of them: knights and chanters and the masked figures called Unmakers who walked at the center of every burning, carrying instruments Juniper didn't want to think too hard about.

"How many?" she asked.

Aldric was quiet a moment. "When I left them, we mustered six hundred swords. That was eight years ago, and they've had eight years of fear to recruit with." He exhaled slowly. "Call it a thousand. Perhaps twelve hundred."

"And Sable?"

"Three hundred. Maybe less, after last night's fighting."

"So we're outnumbered three to one by people who want to kill everyone in this room," Juniper said, "including—" She couldn't finish it. Her eyes found Wren instead, across the gallery, sitting cross-legged with Tamsin the cook-soldier, eating a heel of bread as though the world weren't ending. The glow under the girl's skin had brightened over the past day, pulsing gently in time with the shard-song, and every chanter in the Order below would be able to feel it the way a shark feels blood.

"Including a nine-year-old child whose only crime is being loved by the wrong artifact," Aldric finished quietly. "Yes."

"There has to be another way down. Another way out. There's always another way, that's the whole point of smuggler roads, there's *always*—"

"Juniper." He said her name gently. "Look at the map."

She didn't need to. The courier's map had sunk into the Steppe above days ago, but its memory lived in the vault walls themselves, ink-crawling lines that surfaced and vanished in the fused glass like fish under ice. Juniper had studied them for hours while Aldric slept. There was one other exit—an old ventilation shaft on the northern face, narrow enough that Wren might squeeze through but no one full-grown, and it climbed four hundred feet of loose scree directly into the path of the Order's second column.

There was nowhere to go. For the first time since she'd run away from Maren's cottage with nothing but a satchel and a head full of fury, Juniper Crane stood in a place with genuinely no way out.

And the strangest part—the part she kept turning over like a stone found in a shoe—was that she wasn't panicking. She was tired, and frightened for Wren, and grieving Aldric by inches every time she looked at his wound. But some frantic animal part of her that had been running for ten years had finally, quietly, sat down.

"No good options," she murmured.

"That's usually what the end of a war feels like from the inside," Aldric said. "All the histories skip it. They go straight from the brave last stand to the peace treaty. Nobody writes the hour where everyone stands around doing arithmetic with children's lives."

* * *

Wren found her an hour later, at the edge of the heart-chamber, where Juniper stood watching the central shard breathe its slow light.

"Mama says I shouldn't come in here alone," Wren said, coming in anyway, because she was Wren.

"Your mama," Juniper said, and then heard it, and felt her ears go hot. "I mean—I'm not—"

"You're my mama," Wren said with the devastating patience of a child explaining weather to an adult. "I decided. You don't get to decide it back, that's not how deciding works for mamas." She planted herself beside Juniper and looked up at the shard. Its light moved across her face, and where it touched, her skin answered—faint gold rising to meet it, call and response. "It talks different today."

"Different how?"

"Scared." Wren frowned up at it. "It's been alive a hundred years all alone down here, and now everybody's coming, and they're all so loud, and none of them are asking it anything. They're just arguing about who gets it." She kicked at the glass floor, a small furious gesture entirely at odds with the gravity of her words. "Nobody ever asks things what they want. Not mountains, not rivers, not stones. Papa used to say that's why everything breaks."

"Papa?"

"The miner. Before the mine fell." Wren shrugged, the shrug of a child who has done her grieving in private and does not intend to share it. "Anyway. If they break it again, will it hurt? The stone?"

Juniper thought about lying to her, and found she physically couldn't.

"Yes," she said. "I think it would hurt very much. And I think—" she crouched to Wren's level, "—that hurting it is exactly what the people downstairs want to do. All of them. Both sides, in their own way. Sable wants to own it, and the Order wants to unmake it, and nobody—" her voice caught, and she steadied it, "—nobody is standing where we're standing."

Wren considered this with her whole solemn face. Then she reached out and took Juniper's hand, and the glow beneath her skin spread, gently, up Juniper's wrist—not painful, just warm, like sunlight through a window—and for one moment Juniper felt what the stone felt. Loneliness, vast and old. Hope, absurd and undimmed. And underneath both, something that took her a moment to name:

Relief.

Someone had finally come down here who wasn't reaching to take.

"Wren," Juniper whispered, "when this is over—whatever happens—you remember that you showed me that. You hear me?"

"I hear you," Wren said. "But also Tamsin says supper's on, so."

Despite everything—despite the thousand grey-robed singers closing in and the transformed general upstairs and Aldric bleeding quietly against his pillar—Juniper laughed. It came out ragged, but it was real.

* * *

They came at dusk.

There was no herald, no parley, no offer of terms. The Order had passed beyond negotiation years ago; the Litany of Ash did not include verses for conversation. Juniper watched from the gallery as the first ranks filled the lower vault, white masks catching torchlight, and the chanting swelled until the shards themselves trembled with it—and then, horribly, began to harmonize. The Order's song and the stone's hum braided together into a single sound, and every person in the vaults flinched at it, because it sounded like grief given a melody.

"Hold the stair!" Sable's voice rang out, amplified to inhuman resonance, and her rebels roared back.

What followed was not a battle. Juniper had seen skirmishes, ambushes, the brief ugly violence of smuggling turned sour. This was something older and worse: two convictions colliding at the bottom of the world. The Order advanced in perfect silence now, the Litany finished, shields locked, and Sable's people broke against them like water on rock. Glass-shard spears splintered on ash-grey shields. Chanters at the rear lifted their voices and the barricades began, slowly, terribly, to crumble—not broken, *unmade*, their history sung backward out of them.

"They'll reach the heart-chamber within the hour," Aldric said. He had gotten himself upright, which Juniper knew had cost him enormously, and he was strapping on his battered breastplate with hands that shook only slightly. "Maybe less."

"Then we run for the shaft now, all three of us—"

"With me unable to climb and Wren unable to fit the last hundred feet?" He smiled at her, and it was the gentlest terrible smile she had ever seen. "No. Listen. The stair narrows at the third gallery. One man with a sword can hold that bottleneck against fifty—it's practically designed for it, some architect a thousand years ago understood exactly what vaults like this eventually need." He settled the strap, tested the give, met her eyes. "Let me be the architect's answer."

"No." The word came out flat and immediate. "No. Absolutely not. We find another way, we barricade, we—"

"Juniper." He took her hand, and his grip was still strong, still steady. "Eight years ago my order died because we knew too much and acted too late. Every night since, I've lain awake composing the speech I should have given, the stand I should have made. This—" he gestured at the stair, the singing dark below, the child glowing softly at the gallery's edge, "—this is the speech. This is the stand. You don't get to take it from me. It's the only thing I've ever truly earned."

Somewhere below, the first barricade gave way. Screaming. Then Sable's voice, resonant and radiant and utterly lost, rallying her people toward a victory that would damn them.

Juniper gripped his hand hard enough to hurt. "You fall, you crawl back to me. Do you understand? You don't get to be noble and dead. Noble and *alive*, Aldric, or I swear I'll haunt you, I'll learn ghost-magic specifically—"

"Noted," he said, and kissed her forehead, brief and fierce. "Guard her well, Juniper Crane."

Then he was gone, limping fast down toward the third gallery, sword drawn, and Juniper stood at the rail with Wren's small hand finding hers, watching the grey tide surge upward through the singing dark toward one battered knight standing alone on a narrow stair.

Above them, the heartstone pulsed. Below them, Aldric set his feet.

He raised his voice—one voice against a thousand, cracked and human and completely unafraid—and called out the old challenge of his destroyed order:

"*None shall pass who carry hate!*"

The tide paused. And came on.
