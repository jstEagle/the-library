## 30. Stand at the Shards

The vault beneath the Glass Steppe hummed like a struck bell that had forgotten how to stop.

Aldric Vane stood at the foot of the great stair and listened to it, because listening was easier than thinking about what came next. The sound rose through the glass floor, through his boots, through the old scar tissue along his ribs where a mace had found him at Redford Bridge eleven years ago. It was not an unpleasant sound. That was the trap of it. Everything about this place was designed to be not unpleasant — the soft blue light leaking up between the shards, the warmth in the air like a hearth-room in winter, the way his own tired thoughts seemed to smooth out and settle, like a horse calming under a good hand.

*It wants you comfortable,* he reminded himself. *Comfortable men do not run.*

Behind him, halfway up the stair, Juniper Crane had stopped moving.

She was doing the thing she did when she was frightened, which was to become very still and very sarcastic at the same time, as though stillness and sarcasm together might form some kind of armor. She had Wren by the hand. The girl's skin gave off its faint pearly glow, brighter here than anywhere they'd traveled, bright enough now that it threw their shadows up the stairwell wall — three long figures climbing toward something none of them could name.

"You're going to stand there," Juniper said. Not a question. She'd read his face the way she read maps: quickly, completely, and without any mercy for what she found.

"I'm going to hold the stair," Aldric said.

"With what? You have one sword, half a shield, and a shoulder that clicks when it rains."

"It's not raining."

"Aldric."

"Wren." He crouched, which his knees protested loudly, and looked the girl in the eye. She was nine years old and had been heartstone-touched since before anyone thought to ask her opinion on the matter, and she was looking at him now with the grave, ancient patience children sometimes wore like a borrowed coat. "You remember what we talked about. What I asked you."

"Keep Mama moving," Wren said. "Don't let her argue. Don't let her come back for you."

"That's my girl."

"She's going to come back for you anyway," Wren said. "She always comes back. She came back for me when the mine fell. She came back for you at Weeping Pass even though you told her not to. She's bad at not coming back."

"I know," Aldric said. "I'm counting on it taking her a while."

He heard Juniper exhale through her nose — the specific exhale of a woman assembling an argument — and he stood before she could deploy it. There was no version of this conversation that ended well. He had rehearsed it all the way down through the vaults, in every variation, and in none of them did Juniper Crane agree to walk away from a fight while he held the door. She would have to be already walking. It was a coward's strategy and a strategist's, and he had learned both trades from the same order, the one that no longer existed except in him and in a burned archive and in the habits of a man who still polished a sword nobody had asked him to carry.

"The Order is coming up the pass," he said instead, to the air between them. "Two hundred, maybe more. Sable's people will be right behind them, or ahead of them, or mixed among them by now — it doesn't matter. What matters is that this stair is narrow, and dark, and I know how to make two hundred men wish they'd brought more torches."

"And then what?" Juniper's voice cracked on the *what*, just slightly, and she covered it with speed. "Then you die heroically at the bottom of a hole in the ground and Wren and I live happily ever after knowing exactly what it cost? That's not a plan, that's a funeral with extra steps."

"Juniper." He kept his voice level. Gentle, even. He'd been told once, by a woman he'd loved badly and lost cleanly, that gentleness was the only weapon he'd ever mastered. "If we all go up together, we all arrive tired, and we arrive pursued, and whatever happens in that chamber happens with two hundred soldiers at our backs. If you go now — if you go *quietly* — you get there first. You get there whole. Whatever you're going to do in that room, you do it with time to think. Time is the only thing I can give you that's worth anything."

"You could give me your help."

"My help is a man standing on a stair with a sword," Aldric said. "I've seen what you do with hedge-magic and stubbornness. Trust me, you're getting the better end of this trade."

For a moment — one long moment, in which the humming of the shards seemed to lean in and listen — he thought she was going to refuse outright. Her jaw set. Her hand tightened around Wren's small fingers. And then Wren did the thing that undid them both, which was simply to tug twice on Juniper's hand and say, in the reasonable tone of a child who has heard adults negotiate for weeks:

"Mama. He practiced."

Juniper blinked. "*Practiced.*"

"He practiced holding doors," Wren said. "In the mines. When you were asleep. He said — " and here Wren pitched her voice down into a gravelly approximation of Aldric's low register that was, frankly, unflattering and accurate in equal measure — " 'a knight without a banner holds the last door, because it's the only post left that doesn't require anyone to believe in you.' "

Aldric closed his eyes. Eleven years of careful dignity, undone by a nine-year-old's impression of him.

"He also said," Wren continued, merciless, "'ow' a lot, because the stairs were cold."

"*Thank you,* Wren."

"You're welcome!"

Juniper made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. She stepped down two stairs so that she was nearly at his eye level, and she looked at him the way she had looked at him across a campfire in the Thornfells, back when they were strangers negotiating terms of passage — measuring him, weighing him, deciding whether he was worth the terrible risk of trusting.

"If you fall," she said, "I am going to be so angry with you."

"I know."

"Not grief-angry. *Angry*-angry. I'll dig you up and kill you again."

"That seems proportionate."

"And if you *don't* fall — if you somehow survive this stupid, noble, self-important — " her voice went, and she stopped, and pressed the heel of her hand briefly against her eye, and when she spoke again it was quiet enough that only he and Wren could hear it over the hum of the stone. "Come back up those stairs, Ser Aldric Vane. Knight without a banner. Come back, and I'll sew you a banner myself. Any colors you want. Ugly ones. I don't care."

He smiled. It hurt his face; he wasn't used to it anymore. "Green," he said. "And gold. And put a rabbit on it. Something humiliating."

"A rabbit."

"A fat one. Mid-leap. Looking surprised."

"Why a—"

"So that when people ask, I get to tell them the story of the woman who outran an empire because she refused to be caught, and the story starts with a rabbit looking surprised." He touched two fingers briefly to her shoulder — the old salute of his order, the one that meant *I see you, and I go where you cannot follow* — and turned away before she could see what his face was doing.

"Go," he said. "Both of you. Now, while the stair is ours."

He heard them climb. He heard Wren's small feet quick on the glass steps, and Juniper's longer stride behind, and then — at the top, where the stair bent out of sight toward the heart-chamber — he heard Juniper stop. One breath. Two.

"Rabbit," she said, half to herself, wondering. And then her footsteps resumed, faster, and were gone.

* * *

Alone on the stair, Aldric Vane did what he had done before every battle of his life, which was to check his equipment and lie to himself about the odds.

Sword: good steel, edge honed that morning against a shard of the Steppe itself, which had proven sharper than any whetstone and faintly warm to the touch, like a living thing pleased to be useful. Shield: half of one, technically, the rim scorched from the waystation fire, but a shield's job was to be in the way, and his was still entirely capable of being in the way. Armor: boiled leather over a mail shirt with three links missing at the left shoulder, because Maren Crane had cut them out years ago to treat a wound and he'd never gotten around to replacing them, and because some part of him had apparently known, all along, that he would die the way he lived — slightly repaired, mostly functional, held together by habit.

Odds: poor. He assessed them honestly, which was a discipline his order had drilled into him until it became a kind of prayer. Two hundred against one, on ground he could defend but not hold. No relief column. No second line. No banner, no order, no brother-knights at his shoulder singing the old marching hymn off-key the way Brannock used to, may the earth keep him gently.

Against that: a narrow stair. Darkness they didn't know and he did. And the simple, unfashionable truth that one determined man who has already decided to die is the most expensive object in any army's path.

He climbed down to the bottom of the stair and chose his ground.

The vault opened here into a wide antechamber — the guardians' hall, Wren had called it, where the preserved spirit had spoken to them in a voice like frost forming. Pillars of fused glass rose from floor to ceiling, each one shot through with veins of slow blue light. Between the pillars, the floor was mirror-smooth, and it showed every reflection with cruel honesty. Aldric looked down at himself and saw a broad, gray-bearded man in battered leather, standing alone in a cathedral of light, and he thought: *Well. There are worse mirrors.*

He positioned himself at the base of the stair, back to the first step, sword low, shield angled. Then he waited.

Waiting was its own craft. His order had taught him that too — the Vigilant, they'd called themselves, before the world decided vigilance was a crime. You did not pace. You did not count your heartbeats or rehearse your fears. You breathed, and you watched, and you let the silence become a country you knew how to live in. He had been good at waiting once. He had spent eleven years practicing nothing else.

The light in the pillars dimmed.

That was the first warning. The hum changed pitch — dropped, deepened, became something with weight to it, like a drum heard through a mountain. Somewhere far above, past the vaults and the buried roads and the scarred plain itself, the Ashen Order had reached the Steppe. He felt their arrival the way you feel a storm front: pressure, and a wrongness in the air, and the animal certainty that the sky intended violence.

Then torchlight, reflected up the stairwell from the passage beyond — dozens of torches, then hundreds, a crawling orange tide — and voices, and the ring of iron-shod boots on stone, and the Ashen Order came into the antechamber.

They were grey from helm to boot, faces masked, and they moved well. He granted them that much, professionally. They fanned out between the pillars in disciplined pairs, blades drawn, and their commander — tall, a white sash at the waist marking seniority — raised one gauntleted hand, and two hundred soldiers stopped as one.

The commander looked at Aldric. Looked at the stair behind him. Looked back at Aldric.

"Stand aside," the commander said. The voice was flat, amplified strangely by the glass, arriving at Aldric's ears twice — once directly, once as a reflection off the mirrored floor, so that the words seemed to come from everywhere. "The Order claims this place. The abomination above must be cleansed, and the stone destroyed. No harm needs to come to you, knight. Your order is dead. Lay down what remains of it."

Aldric considered several replies. Most of them were witty. He had found, in recent months, that he was becoming a witty man — Juniper's influence, undoubtedly, like catching a cold — and he weighed the pleasure of a good line against the reality that everything he said now was a delay, and delays were measured in seconds, and seconds were what he was buying with his life.

"Your order burned my order's archive," he said, "with forty-one of my brothers inside. So you'll forgive me if I don't take architectural advice from you."

"The archive harbored heresy."

"The archive harbored *books.*" He rolled his shoulders, felt the clicking one complain, and settled. "But let's not litigate history on such short notice. Here are my terms. Every step of this stair costs you ten men. I've counted; the arithmetic favors me for at least the first twenty steps. After that we'll renegotiate. Turn around, march back up the pass, and tell whoever sent you that the Glass Steppe belongs to no one — least of all to men in masks who've mistaken obedience for virtue."

The commander's head tilted. Behind the mask, Aldric imagined, someone was recalculating.

"You are one man."

"I am," Aldric agreed, "one man. On a stair. In the dark. With nothing left to lose and a great deal of spite in reserve." He lifted his sword, and the blue light of the pillars ran down the blade like water finding a channel. "Gentlemen. Ladies. Whoever's under the hats. Come and spend yourselves."

They came.

* * *

Later — much later, in the strange stretched time that fighting makes — he would remember it not as a battle but as a piece of music with a very limited score.

The first pair reached him in eight seconds. He gave the stair its toll: a parry, a step, a shield-boss driven into a masked face, and the first soldier of the Order went down the steps like a dropped doll. The second lasted longer, and was better, and died on Aldric's blade with a sound he would hear again in dreams.

Ten men for the first stretch of stairs. He'd promised the arithmetic, and the arithmetic held — barely, badly, with a gash opening along his forearm and a boot heel skidding once on glass-slick stone — but it held. They could not come at him more than two abreast, and he was wider than two men, and angrier than ten.

"Is this all of you?" he shouted, because shouting was free and breathing was not. "I've fought drunk smugglers with better formation! No offense, Juniper!"

Someone loosed a crossbow bolt from the back of the hall. It skipped off a pillar and shattered, and the commander's voice cracked out — *"Hold ranged fire, you fools, the resonance—"* — and Aldric filed that away with a surge of grim delight. They were afraid of the vaults. Afraid of what the shards did to magic, to noise, to careless power. Every rule they obeyed down here was a rule slowing them, and slowing them was the entire shape of his life now.

So he made them afraid of more things.

He broke a pillar's base with his shield — three blows, screaming shoulder, and the glass column groaned and shed a rain of splinters that turned the Order's neat pairs into a scrambling mob. He kicked a torch into a drift of glass dust that flared blue-white and blinded a full rank. He fought dirty and loud and, when the mood took him, he insulted their lineage, their armor, and their marching cadence, and somewhere around the fourth wave he realized he was laughing — actually laughing, a wild ugly sound bouncing off the vaulted dark — and understood that this, too, was a weapon. Men hesitate before a laughing enemy. Hesitation on a stair is death.

The cost arrived on schedule.

A spear found the gap at his left shoulder — the missing links, Maren's old surgery, the wound that had never quite finished being a memory — and went deep, and the world went white at the edges. He killed the spearman. He was fairly sure he killed the spearman. His hands were doing their work without much consultation from the rest of him now, and he let them, because the rest of him had begun to feel like a house with the lights going out one room at a time.

*Buy the night,* he told himself. *Not the morning. Just the night. One night is all they need.*

He thought of Juniper's face when she'd said *rabbit*. He thought of Wren asleep against his side on the road, drooling on his cloak, entirely unbothered by destiny. He thought of a green-and-gold banner with a fat astonished hare mid-leap, and he thought: *I would very much like to see that flag raised. I would like it very much indeed.*

So he did not fall yet. He planted himself on the ninth step, bleeding freely onto glass that drank the red and glowed a little brighter for it — the Steppe remembering him, maybe, adding him to its long ledger — and he raised his sword one more time.

"Next," he rasped, at the assembled grey ranks. "Form an orderly queue. My mother raised me polite."

The commander pushed forward at last, drawing a blade that drank the light rather than reflecting it, and Aldric smiled, and set his feet, and met the descent of that black steel with everything he had left —

— which, as it turned out, was almost enough.

* * *

He fell on the eleventh step.

He was aware, in the drifting way of the gravely wounded, of the fight going out of the stairwell — of the commander standing over him, chest heaving, black blade lowered; of soldiers hanging back, unwilling to be the next man up those stairs even against a dying foe; of the hum of the shards swelling, changing, rising toward something that sounded almost like alarm.

"Who waits above?" the commander demanded of him. "What does the Crane woman intend?"

Aldric looked up past the mask, past the torchlight, into the dark of the stairwell where his friends had gone, and he smiled with bloody teeth.

"She intends," he said, "to surprise you."

The commander struck him — a blow meant to finish, deflected at the last instant by a last flicker of shield, so that instead of death it gave him darkness — and Aldric Vane, knight without a banner, slid down the glass steps and lay still among the fallen, and bought the night.

Above him, far above, in a chamber at the top of the world's oldest wound, Juniper Crane stood before the heartstone — and the heartstone, patient as gravity, began to speak her name.
