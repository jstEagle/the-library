## 31. What Power Wants

The heart-chamber was smaller than Juniper had expected.

That felt like a joke the world had been building toward for thirty chapters of her life—caves and passes and burning waystations, a knight bleeding out on a stair behind her, an entire war converging on this one place—and at the center of it all, a room you could have fit inside Maren's old kitchen, if you didn't mind bumping your elbows.

The walls were glass. Not built glass—*grown* glass, she thought, or perhaps *remembered* glass, because the vaults beneath the Steppe had a quality of memory about them the way old houses had a quality of their dead. Light came from nowhere and everywhere, the way it did through fog, and it pooled in the middle of the floor where the heartstone sat on a plinth of fused sand.

It was not what the songs promised. The songs said *a star brought low*, said *a sun in chains*, said things that made drunk soldiers weep in taverns. What Juniper saw was a shard the size of a loaf of bread, faceted like a cut gem but irregular, like something that had been broken by hand rather than craft. It was the color of light through honey. It hummed—not a sound, exactly. More a feeling that had gotten lost on its way to becoming sound.

And it was waiting for her.

She knew that the way she knew weather coming. She'd spent eleven years reading storms off the backs of her hands, reading men's intentions off the set of their shoulders, reading danger in the half-second before it arrived. The heartstone's attention settled over her like a coat being placed around her shoulders by someone who loved her.

"Oh no," Juniper said aloud, to the empty room. "No, I know this trick."

The stone, naturally, did not answer in words. It answered in warmth.

---

Behind her, far above and back down the way they'd come, Aldric was holding the stair. She had left him there with his sword and his ruined shoulder and his terrible, gallant smile, and he had said—the last thing he'd said, and she was going to be angry about how perfectly he'd said it for the rest of her life—"Go on, then. I'll mind the door."

*Mind the door.* As though he were a shopkeeper. As though two armies weren't pouring into the vaults above him like water finding every crack.

And Wren—

Juniper turned. Wren stood at the chamber's entrance, and the glow of her was brighter than Juniper had ever seen it, bright enough to throw shadows, bright enough that Juniper could see the girl's heartbeat in the pulse of her own skin. Eight years old. Heartstone-touched since before anyone had found her in the collapsed mine, glowing faintly near the map like a candle near a draft. Now there was no map anymore—it had sunk into the Steppe above like a stone into a pond—but Wren glowed anyway, because Wren had never needed the map. Wren *was* the map. Had been becoming one the whole time, dream by singing fragment by sleepless night, and none of them had been able to stop it, and now here they were.

"Mama," Wren said. Her voice echoed strangely in the glass room. "It's talking to me too."

"I know, love." Juniper kept her voice steady the way she'd learned to keep her hands steady when stitching wounds: by pretending very hard that they belonged to someone calmer. "What's it saying?"

Wren's face did something complicated. Fear and wonder fighting for the same ground. "It says it can fix everything." A pause. "But I don't think everything's broken in the way it thinks it is."

Out of the mouths of babes, as Maren used to say, usually right before charging someone triple for a poultice.

"Wren." Juniper crouched, which put her eyes level with the plinth, which put the heartstone directly in her peripheral vision like a guest who wouldn't stop trying to catch your eye across a crowded room. "I need you to do something very hard. Harder than anything I've ever asked you."

"Harder than the pass?"

"Much harder."

"Harder than when you made me eat the gray kind of mushroom?"

"The gray mushrooms are *medicinal*, Wren—"

"They taste like a wet sock."

"*Wren.*" Juniper took both the girl's small hands. They were warm. They were always warm; that was part of what she was now. "I need you to go stand by the wall over there, and whatever happens next—whatever you see, whatever you hear, whatever *I* say—I need you to remember that I love you more than anything the stone could show me. Can you do that? Can you hold onto that?"

Wren looked at her for a long moment with those too-old eyes that children get when the world has been careless with them. Then she nodded once, businesslike, and went and stood by the wall, and folded her arms, and glowed.

It was, Juniper would think later, the most terrifying act of trust she had ever witnessed. An eight-year-old, watching an adult walk toward a thing that ate people whole, armed with nothing but the belief that she was loved.

Juniper turned to face the heartstone.

---

Here is what nobody tells you about temptation: it doesn't look like temptation. It looks like relief.

The songs got that wrong too. The songs always showed power as a serpent, a beautiful poison, a crown of thorns worn gladly. But standing there in the honeyed light, Juniper felt none of that. What she felt was the specific warmth of a bed when you've been walking for three days. What she felt was the smell of bread when you haven't eaten. What she felt was *home*, and she hated herself for how much of her leaned toward it before her mind caught up.

*You're tired,* the stone said—or didn't say; the words simply arrived in her the way her own thoughts arrived, which was the horror of it, that she couldn't tell the difference between her wanting and its offering. *You've been tired since you were nineteen years old.*

"I'm fine," Juniper said, to a rock. "I'm extremely well rested."

*You ran from Maren Crane with nothing but the clothes you stood up in and a talent you'd been taught to be ashamed of. You've slept in ditches. You've eaten things you won't name even to yourself. Every kindness you've ever accepted, you've paid back double so no one could own you. Eleven years, Juniper Crane. Eleven years of doors closing softly behind you so no one hears you leave.*

"Stop it," she whispered.

*I'm not doing anything,* said the stone, and the worst part—the truly unforgivable part—was that it was telling the truth. It wasn't pushing. It was just... opening doors. Showing her rooms. And every room had her name already carved over the lintel.

The first room was safety.

She saw it plainly, no metaphor, no vision-shimmer: a cottage. Stone walls, slate roof, a garden that actually grew things because she would finally live somewhere long enough to learn its soil. There was a woodstove with a kettle on it. There was a cat—she'd never allowed herself to want a cat, cats required staying, cats were a commitment to a future—and the cat was orange and enormous and asleep on the windowsill in a square of sun.

In the cottage, she was not afraid. That was the detail that undid her. Not rich, not powerful, not safe behind wards and walls. Just—not afraid. The particular quiet of a body that has stopped listening for hoofbeats. She hadn't felt it since she was a child, and she had forgotten it existed, the way you forget a language, and here it was again, spoken fluently, offered freely.

"You can't give me that," Juniper said. Her voice cracked on the second word. "Safety isn't a thing you *give*. It's a thing that—"

*Is given,* the stone finished, gently. *By someone strong enough to hold it. By someone who stays.*

The second room was belonging.

Oh, this one was crueler. This one knew her.

She saw the waystation—not burning, not yet, not ever—a table with four places set. Aldric arguing cheerfully about the correct way to season stew, wrong as always, his shoulder healed, his order's disgrace a story told over cups instead of a wound carried under armor. Wren with her feet not touching the floor, laughing at something, growing, *growing up*, and Juniper present for all of it, every scraped knee and every first frost and every year turning like pages. Maren's chair empty but not mournful. Just empty. Just a chair.

And everyone in that vision looked at her—looked *at* her, not past her, not through her, not with the careful arithmetic people used when deciding whether a smuggler was worth trusting—and nobody was calculating anything. They just wanted her there. That was all. That was the whole miracle.

"That's not real," Juniper managed. "That's just—you're showing me what I—"

*Yes,* the stone agreed, without shame. *I show you what you want. That is all I have ever done. Ask Yara Sable. Ask the Ashen Order. Ask the kings who broke me to end their war—they each saw only their own face, magnified. I am a mirror, little runner. I have never corrupted anyone in my existence. I only concentrate.*

The third room, then. She knew it was coming. She braced for it the way you brace for cold water.

Maren.

Not Maren as Juniper had last seen her—old and stubborn and holding a burning doorway with her whole failing body so that a child she'd betrayed and a knight she'd never met could live. Not even Maren as she'd been at the end, when she'd found them on the road with shelter in her voice and a debt in her eyes. But Maren from before. Maren the day Juniper had learned the truth: that her mentor, her savior, the woman who'd pulled her out of the gutter at fourteen and taught her every herb and hedge-charm and hard-won survival trick she knew—that woman had been negotiating to sell Juniper's talent to the Ashen Order. To the grey riders. To the people who burned what they couldn't catalog. *A fair price,* Maren had called it. *You'd be provided for.*

Provided for. Like livestock.

The vision the stone offered was simple. No violence. Nothing so crude. It simply showed her a world where Maren Crane lived long enough to understand what she'd thrown away—lived to see Juniper come back wearing power like a crown, lived to see the Order kneel, lived to hear Juniper say, calmly, in Maren's own teaching voice, *"This is what my talent was worth. You sold it cheap."*

Lived to feel, every day for the rest of her long life, the exact weight of that mistake.

Juniper's breath came out shaking.

"There she is," she said quietly. "There's the honest face of it. Safety, belonging—those are just the bait. This is the hook. This is what I actually want, isn't it? Somewhere under everything, some mean little cellar of my heart, I want her to *hurt* the way I hurt."

The stone said nothing. It didn't need to. The vision held, patient as honey, and Maren's imagined face held too—regretful, diminished, *sorry*—and it fit Juniper's anger like a key fits a lock, and that fit was the most frightening thing in the whole glass room.

Because here was the truth the songs never sang: the heartstone wasn't lying to her. That was the trap. Every word it offered was true. She *was* tired. She *did* want the cottage and the cat and the table with four places. She *did* carry Maren's betrayal like a splinter worked too deep to dig out. The stone concentrated what you already believed, Wren had dreamed, and what Juniper believed—in the cellar, in the dark, in the place she never lit—was that she had been owed a better life and everyone who might have given her one had failed her, and if she just took hold of something strong enough, she could finally collect.

Almost, she reached out.

Her hand rose. The honey-light bent toward her fingers like water finding its level. The hum became almost audible, almost a word, almost her own voice saying *yes*—

"Mama?"

Small. From the wall. Not afraid—Wren had promised to hold on, and Wren held on the way she did everything, with her whole stubborn entire self—but *checking*. Checking whether the woman at the plinth was still the woman who'd crouched down and asked for trust.

Juniper looked at her hand, hovering an inch from a god's broken heart.

And she thought about the gray mushrooms. About how Wren complained every single time and ate them anyway, because Mama said they'd help, and that was enough. About Aldric minding a door he probably couldn't hold, grinning while he did it, because she'd asked. About a courier dying on a frozen pass pressing a bloodstained map into a stranger's hands because a stranger was the only hand available, and sometimes that's all trust is—all any of it has ever been: the only hand available, choosing to hold on.

Nobody had ever given Juniper Crane safety. Nobody had ever handed her belonging like a gift. And the rage at Maren was real, and it was hers, and it deserved better than to be spent buying a lie shaped like justice.

"No," Juniper said.

The light dimmed—not angry, not wounded. Almost relieved, she'd swear later. Like a test concluded.

"No," she said again, stronger, pulling her hand back, and the words came up out of her like water out of a cracked dam, eleven years of them: "I'm tired. I'm so tired. And I want the cottage, and I want the stupid cat, and I want Maren alive just so I could be angry at her properly, face to face, and have her *hear* me. I want all of it. But not like this. Not bought with a chain around my neck, not even a comfortable chain, not even a chain shaped like a kitchen. You don't get to keep me. Nobody keeps me. That's the whole point of me."

The heartstone hummed. And in the hum, for the first time, she heard what it had been saying all along, underneath the offers, underneath the warmth:

*Then what will you do with me, little runner? Everyone who finds me takes. Everyone who takes me breaks. Everyone who breaks me starts the war again. You are the first in nine hundred years to stand there and say no. So. What. Will. You. Do?*

And Juniper Crane—who had spent her whole life running from questions with no good answers, who had navigated by avoiding, survived by refusing, lived by never letting anything matter enough to hold—stood in the honeyed light with an eight-year-old glowing at the wall behind her and a knight bleeding on the stairs above her and two armies thundering closer, and understood, with terrible clarity, that *no* was not going to be enough this time.

Refusing the stone would leave it here for Sable. Destroying it was impossible. Carrying it made prisoners.

Unless.

An idea began, slow and enormous, at the base of her skull—the kind of idea that is either salvation or suicide and refuses to specify which. It involved opening. It involved the one thing she had never, in eleven years, permitted herself to do with anyone, about anything, ever.

It involved letting go.

"Mama?" Wren said again, and stepped away from the wall, glowing like a small dawn, walking straight toward the plinth with the fearless certainty of a child who has decided something. "I think I know what it wants us to do. Can I show you?"

"Wren—" Juniper reached for her. "Wren, wait—"

But the girl was already reaching out her small, warm, heartstone-touched hand toward the humming light, and above them, faintly, through nine hundred feet of glass and stone, Juniper heard the sound of Aldric Vane's sword meeting something that was not a sword.

* * *

The vision hit her like the memory of sunlight.

Not Juniper's vision—Wren's. The girl's fingers had barely brushed the facet when the chamber filled with images that were not images, a knowing that poured itself into both of them at once, generous as spilled milk:

The stone did not corrupt. It had never corrupted anyone. It *concentrated*—took what one person believed and gave it weight, gave it reach, gave it teeth. Sable believed in justice, so the stone would make her justice vast and merciless and blind. The Order believed the world was safer without choice, so the stone would make their certainty a bonfire that burned everything flammable, starting with a glowing child. And Juniper—Juniper believed, in the cellar, in the dark, that she was safest alone.

Give the stone to Juniper Crane, and it would build her cottage walls so high and thick that no one would ever get in again. Not Sable. Not the Order. Not Aldric. Not Wren.

The vision showed her that future, gently, the way you show a child why they can't keep the wild bird: Juniper in the cottage, safe forever, alone forever, the orange cat the only living thing that visited, until even the cat grew old, until even the cat was gone, until the kettle on the stove whistled for no one in a house that no one would ever knock upon.

"No," Juniper breathed—and this time the word was different. This time it wasn't refusal.

This time it was grief.

Because she finally understood what the stone had been asking, all along, with its honeyed patience and its honest face. Not *take me*. Not even *refuse me*.

It was asking the question she'd been running from since she was nineteen years old, since a waystation door, since a woman she loved had priced her like goods:

*Will you let yourself be held?*

And the answer, Juniper realized—watching Wren turn to her with shining eyes, hearing the clash of battle grow louder on the stairs, feeling the idea at the base of her skull unfold its enormous wings—was going to require her to open every door she'd ever closed.

Starting with the one inside herself.

"Wren," she said, and her voice didn't shake, "tell me exactly what it showed you. All of it. Because I think I know how we end this—and I'm going to need you to hold my hand."
