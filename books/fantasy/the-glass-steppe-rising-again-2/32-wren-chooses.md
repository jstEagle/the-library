## 32. Wren Chooses

The heart-chamber was warm.

That was the first thing Wren noticed, and the thing she would remember longest—not the light, not the humming in her teeth, not the way the walls of the vault curved like the inside of a shell—but the warmth. It came up through the floor and settled into her boots like a cat deciding to stay.

She had been cold for a very long time. Since the mine, really. Since before the mine, if she was honest, back to the years she didn't talk about even inside her own head, the years that lived behind a door she kept her hand pressed flat against at night so nothing could come out of it. The world above ground was cold too, mostly. Juniper was warm, but Juniper was always moving, always half-turned toward the next exit, and you couldn't lean on someone who was already running. Aldric was warm when he let himself be, which was about as often as it snowed in summer.

But this room was warm all on its own, without asking anything of anybody.

The heartstone sat in the middle of the chamber on a pedestal of fused glass, and it did not look like much. That was the second thing Wren noticed. She had expected—she didn't know what she'd expected. Something enormous. Something with teeth. The stories Aldric told around the fire were full of artifacts that roared and burned and demanded blood, and General Sable talked about the heartstone the way hungry people talk about bread, and the Ashen Order wanted to destroy it so badly they were willing to burn down a whole mountain range to get at it.

It looked like a lamp. A big one, maybe as tall as Wren herself, shaped roughly like an egg that had been dropped once and glued back together with gold light in all the cracks. It hummed. It glowed. It sat there being warm, and Wren thought: *all this fuss over a nightlight.*

Then it noticed her, and she stopped thinking that immediately.

* * *

"Juniper," Wren said. Her voice came out small. "Juniper, it's looking at me."

Juniper was three steps behind her, which was where Juniper always was lately—close enough to grab, far enough to bolt. She had a knife out. Wren wasn't sure when she'd drawn it or what she planned to do with it against a rock, but it made Juniper feel better, and Wren had learned that letting Juniper feel better was sometimes more important than pointing out that things made no sense.

"It's a stone," Juniper said. "Stones don't look."

"This one does." Wren held up her hands, palms out, the way you did with horses. Her skin was glowing again—that soft amber light that leaked out of her whenever the map got excited, except there was no map anymore, Juniper had broken it, and still the glow came, brighter here than it had ever been, filling the chamber like she was a lantern with the shutter open. "It's looking at me the way Maren used to look at the good cheese."

"That's a deeply unsettling comparison," Juniper said.

"You asked."

"I didn't."

"Well, you were going to." Wren took another step. The warmth climbed higher, up past her knees now, and the hum found a note that matched something in her chest, and behind the door in her head—the locked one, the one with the bad years behind it—something stirred and then, gently, lay back down. Like the stone had put a hand on the door from the other side. Not pushing. Just... standing there. Keeping watch.

Behind them both, at the top of the stair, came the sounds Aldric had promised to make: steel ringing, men shouting, the deep thud-thud-thud of the Order's ram against the outer doors. He was holding the stair alone. He had kissed the top of Wren's head first, quick and rough, like he was mad at himself for doing it, and said, "Whatever happens down there, you come back up. Both of you. That's an order." And then he'd added, because he was Aldric, "I'm allowed to give orders. I'm technically still a knight. Nobody's taken the paperwork away."

Wren loved him so much it hurt in a place she didn't have words for.

"Juniper." She turned around. Juniper's face was doing the thing it did—jaw set, eyes flicking between Wren and the exits, counting enemies who weren't even in the room yet. "I want to touch it."

"No."

"You didn't even think about it."

"I thought about it the whole way down the stairs. The answer is no." Juniper crouched to her level, which she only did when things were serious; usually she just talked down at Wren like everyone else did, and Wren pretended to mind. "Listen to me. Everything that's happened—Sable, the Order, the general with her eyes turning into glass—all of it started with people touching this thing. I am not letting it happen to you."

"But that's the thing." Wren grabbed Juniper's knife-hand, both hands, the way you held onto someone in a crowd. "Nobody ever touched it *on purpose*. Sable grabbed it because she wanted to win. The old kings used it because they wanted to be right. Even the guardian downstairs said it—everybody who ever carried it got *carried*. But nobody ever just—" Wren searched for the word. "Nobody ever knocked."

Juniper blinked. "Knocked."

"Manners," Wren said. "You're always telling me about manners. You say smugglers live and die by them. You knock before you go in somebody's house. You ask before you take the last sausage." She nodded at the heartstone, glowing patiently in its cradle of glass. "Everybody who ever met this thing kicked the door down. Maybe it's been waiting its whole life for somebody to knock."

The silence stretched. Somewhere above, the ram struck again, and a shower of dust sifted down through the amber light.

"That is either the wisest thing anyone has ever said in this vault," Juniper said slowly, "or the most dangerous, and I genuinely cannot tell which."

"It's the wisest," Wren said. "I'm eight. I don't have time to also be dangerous."

And Juniper laughed—that short, surprised laugh she almost never let out, the one that escaped sideways like a bird through a window—and Wren knew, in the way she knew things, that she'd won. Not because Juniper was weak. Because Juniper had spent her whole life being pushed by people who kicked doors, and here was a small glowing child asking permission instead, and some knot in Juniper's chest had come loose at the sight of it.

"If anything goes wrong," Juniper said, gripping Wren's shoulders hard enough to leave marks, "you pull your hands back and you step away and you let me handle it. Swear it."

"I swear."

"And if it offers you anything—anything at all—you tell me first. Deals are how it gets you. The guardian said so."

"I swear," Wren said again, and meant it, and knew even as she said it that she was going to break it, because the stone was already offering, and what it offered wasn't treasure or power or any of the things grown-ups thought people wanted.

What it offered was to show her the truth. And Wren Crane had spent eight years living behind a locked door, and there was nothing in any world she wanted more.

* * *

She stepped forward. The warmth rose to meet her, wrapped her arms, her throat, the crown of her head, and the hum became a chord, and the chord became—almost—a voice. Not words. More like the feeling of being understood that comes right before words do.

Wren laid both palms flat against the glass.

The world went white, and then it went everything else.

* * *

She saw a king.

He stood in a hall of banners, and the heartstone floated above his cupped hands, and his belief poured off him like heat off a forge: *we are right, we must win, our cause is just.* And the stone drank it in and gave it back tenfold, and the king's soldiers suddenly could not miss their targets, could not lose their nerve, could not doubt—and neither could the king. He believed so completely that he stopped being able to see anything else. His own son stood in front of him pleading for peace, and the king looked straight through the boy as though he were a curtain, because the stone had concentrated his certainty until there was no room left in him for anything but the war.

He never doubted. That was the horror of it. He died certain, on a field of glass he had made himself, smiling.

* * *

She saw a woman in grey robes, kneeling before the same stone generations later—an Ashen matriarch, her belief pouring out just as hot: *this thing must never be wielded again.* And the stone gave that back tenfold too. The woman became incapable of compromise, incapable of mercy, incapable of seeing that the village she burned to keep a shard from falling into rebel hands was itself a kind of wielding. She had picked up the stone's opposite and been picked up by it in turn. Certainty, Wren understood, watching the fires, was the same shape whichever direction it pointed.

* * *

She saw Yara Sable.

Not the general with molten eyes—though she saw that too, saw it coming like weather—but the girl Sable had been, standing in the ashes of a border town, believing with her whole scorched heart: *the people the war broke deserve justice.* And the stone, when she finally seized it, had done exactly what it always did. It had taken that belief and made it enormous. Made it load-bearing. Made it the only wall left in her, so that every doubt, every question, every voice that said *maybe we're becoming what we fought* simply bounced off. Sable wasn't lying when she said she'd build justice. That was the terrible thing. She believed it absolutely, and the stone would move mountains to make sure she went on believing it, forever, while the towns burned and the lists of enemies grew and the justice got smaller and smaller and the certainty got bigger and bigger until there was no Sable left at all, only the belief, wearing Sable like a coat.

*It doesn't corrupt,* Wren realized, and the realization rang through her like a bell through water. *It concentrates. It finds the one thing you believe hardest and it makes that the whole world.*

* * *

And then—because the stone was generous, because she had knocked, because it liked her, she was sure of it, the way you're sure a dog likes you—it showed her the people she loved.

She saw Juniper. Saw the belief buried so deep Juniper probably didn't know it was there: *if I hold power, I become Maren. If I hold anything, it will be taken, so I will hold nothing.* And Wren saw, with a lurch, what the stone would do with that if Juniper ever took it up. It wouldn't make her cruel. It would make her *gone*—would concentrate her running until she ran clean out of herself, until there was nothing left but the empty road and the closed hand.

She saw Aldric. *I failed my order, so I must fail nothing ever again.* The stone would grind him down to a single duty, a man-shaped shield, until he forgot he'd ever laughed at his own jokes, forgot the paperwork line, forgot how to sit by a fire doing nothing, which was secretly his favorite thing.

And Wren saw herself—or the self she might become. *Keep them safe. Keep them together. Don't let anyone leave.* A small belief, a child's belief, and the stone would grow it into a cage with the whole world inside it.

Her eyes stung. In the vision-light, she felt the stone waiting, patient as winter, warm as a hearth.

*Do you see?* it seemed to say. Not in words. In the way a fire seems to say *come closer.* *This is all I have ever done. I am a mirror for wanting. Your kings brought their wars and I gave the wars back bigger. Your grey mother brought her fear. Your general brings her grief. I have never once made anyone anything. I have only ever made them more of what they already were.*

*Then why does everyone fight over you?* Wren asked, inside the light.

*Because,* said the stone, with something that was almost sadness, *everyone believes they alone can be trusted with themselves.*

* * *

Wren opened her eyes.

She was crying. She was also, she discovered, floating about four inches off the floor, glowing bright enough to throw shadows of herself onto the curved walls, and Juniper was halfway across the chamber with the knife raised and her face gray as ash.

"Wren." Juniper's voice cracked down the middle. "Let go. *Now.*"

"In a minute." Wren wiped her nose with her wrist, which was difficult while floating, and managed to descend by thinking heavy thoughts about porridge. Her feet touched the glass floor. The glow dimmed to its usual ember-soft leak. "I'm fine. Better than fine. Juniper, listen—I know how to beat her."

"Beat who?"

"Sable." Wren turned, and the knowledge was still in her, whole and shining, simple as arithmetic. "She's not evil. That's the whole problem. She's *certain*, and the stone keeps feeding her certainty, and you can't cut certainty with a knife, Aldric's tried knives on lots of things and they don't work on that." She grabbed Juniper's hands—both of them, folding the knife away between their palms. "But she wasn't always certain. There's a girl under there. A girl in a burned town. And the stone showed me—if somebody gets inside the certainty, if somebody makes her *doubt*, just once, just long enough—the concentration breaks. It all spills out. All the stuff she's been too full of to feel."

"Doubt her how?" Juniper said. "With an argument? Wren, that woman has an army."

"With the truth." Wren squeezed Juniper's hands. "You're good at truths. You've been running from yours for years, so you know exactly where everybody hides theirs."

Above them, the ram struck the outer doors again, and this time the sound was different—deeper, splintering, the sound of a thing giving up. Dust rained through the amber light. Somewhere on the stairs, Aldric shouted a word Wren wasn't allowed to use, followed by the ring of steel on a lot of steel.

Juniper looked at the heartstone. At Wren. At the stair. Wren watched her do the arithmetic she was always doing—exits, odds, costs—and then watched her arrive somewhere new.

"The guardian said the stone can't be destroyed," Juniper said quietly. "Only carried. And every carrier becomes its prisoner." Her jaw set. "But nobody ever asked what happens if you make the prisoner drop it. If you break the grip instead of the stone."

"Now you're getting it," Wren said. "Also we should probably help Aldric."

"We should probably—" Juniper was already moving, hauling Wren toward the stair, and for one moment—one golden, impossible moment—her hand in Wren's was steady, unafraid, pointed forward instead of away. "Stay behind me. If Sable's up there, you let me talk first."

"What are you going to say?"

"I haven't the faintest idea," Juniper said, and drew her knife, and grinned like a smuggler at a locked door. "But I'm going to knock."

The outer doors came down behind them like thunder.
