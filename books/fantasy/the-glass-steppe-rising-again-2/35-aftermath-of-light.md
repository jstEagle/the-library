## 35. Aftermath of Light

Aldric Vane had died twice in his life, or close enough that he felt qualified to speak on the matter.

The first time had been at Carrowmere Bridge, the day his order burned—when a hammer blow to his helm left him face-down in a stream with his brothers' bodies floating past him like autumn leaves. He had been twenty-six then, and death had felt like failure: cold water filling his mouth while the world went on being loud above him.

The second time was last night, on the vault stair, holding it alone against the Ashen Order's vanguard because two people he loved needed time and he had nothing left to give them but minutes.

He had expected this one to feel like triumph. It didn't. It felt like lying on broken glass with his sword arm useless and the sky doing something impossible overhead.

Because the sky *was* doing something impossible.

The light was rising out of the ground.

* * *

He remembered fragments, and the fragments came back wrong, out of order, like a shuffled deck. The stair. His shield splitting down the middle. A grey-cloaked knight whose face he almost recognized from before—the Order recruited heavily from ruined orders, hungry men who wanted their faith back—and the moment when the man hesitated, just one heartbeat, because Aldric had laughed at something, actually laughed, blood in his teeth, and said, "You're going to have to do better than that, friend, I've been hit by professionals."

Then the light had come up through the floor like dawn arriving from underneath the world, and every man on that stair had stopped fighting at once.

Not been stopped. *Stopped.* There was no force in it. That was the terrible, beautiful thing about it—it hadn't knocked anyone down or burned anything. It had simply risen through them, through their boots and greaves and skin, the way warmth rises through a stone bench in late summer, and for one long moment every soldier of the Ashen Order had stood in the dark holding a weapon they suddenly couldn't remember the reason for.

Aldric remembered thinking, with the peculiar clarity of the nearly dead: *So that's what she did.*

Juniper Crane, who apologized to doors when she bumped into them. Juniper, who once spent an entire evening teaching Wren how to pick pockets—"for emergencies only, and never from anyone poorer than us"—and who had just unmade the oldest war in the world by opening her own chest as a doorway.

Then he'd thought nothing at all for a while.

* * *

Waking was gray and soft and smelled of juniper berries, which would have been funny if he'd had the breath to laugh about it.

"You're not allowed," said a small voice, very close, "to die on the glass. It's rude. Juniper says."

His eyes wouldn't focus yet. Everything was white-gold haze. But he could hear Wren breathing beside him, could feel a small warm hand wrapped around two of his fingers with the fierce grip of a child who has decided, on her own authority, that letting go is not among her options.

"I'll take it under advisement," Aldric croaked.

"Good." A pause. "You're glowing."

"I beg your pardon?"

"You're all—" she made a frustrated sound, searching for the word. Her hand released his fingers and patted vaguely at his forearm, and where she touched him, he saw it: a faint luminance under his own skin, pale gold, like moonlight caught in milk. It moved when he breathed. It pulsed, gently, in time with his heart.

"Everyone's got it," Wren said. "Look."

She held up both arms. The glow ran through her strongest of all—of course it did, she'd been touched by the heartstone since before any of them found her in that collapsed mine—but now it wasn't only her. Down the slope of the shattered plain, figures were stirring in ones and twos among the glass, and every single one of them carried the same faint light. Rebels. Order knights. The wounded and the whole alike. From this distance they looked like a field of lanterns someone had forgotten to extinguish at dawn.

"The whole Steppe is full of it," Wren said. "It went everywhere. Into everybody." She said it without fear, which told Aldric more than any report could have. This child knew the heartstone's voice better than any living soul, and she sounded like a girl describing rain.

"Does it hurt?"

"No." She considered. "It feels like being *included.*"

Aldric lay back against whatever they'd propped behind him—a folded cloak, he discovered; Juniper's, still smelling faintly of road dust and the rosemary oil she used on her boots—and let himself look at the sky properly for the first time.

Dawn was breaking over the Glass Steppe, and it was breaking twice. Once above, the ordinary sun climbing red and gold over the eastern mountains. And once below, rising out of the plain itself, ten thousand points of buried light kindling in answer, so that the horizon glowed in both directions and the air between seemed to hold its breath.

The glass plain had been dead for three hundred years. Everyone knew that. It was the scar of the final battle, fused sand and fused bone, a monument to what the war had cost, walked around by sensible travelers and cursed by poets.

This morning it looked like a lake catching sunrise.

"It's beautiful," Wren said softly.

"Yes," said Aldric, and meant several things at once.

* * *

Finding Juniper took some effort, mostly because standing took some effort.

He made it upright on the second try, using a shard of fused glass the size of a door as a crutch—typical of this place, even its debris was beautiful—and leaned there a while, taking inventory. Left arm: functional, bruised to the color of storm clouds. Right arm: present, attached, and filing formal complaints. Ribs: negotiable. Pride: miraculously intact, considering.

He limped down toward the center of the plain, where the crowds were thickest, Wren trotting at his side with her hand hooked in his belt—not because she needed support, he suspected, but because she'd appointed herself his guardian until further notice and took the duty seriously.

They found Juniper sitting on the ground.

That was the first strange thing. Not kneeling, not collapsed—sitting, cross-legged, the way she sat at campfires when she finally forgot to be watchful, and there was such peace in her posture that Aldric stopped a dozen paces off rather than disturb it.

Around her, the world was reassembling itself.

The Ashen Order was withdrawing. He watched it happen and could scarcely believe his eyes: ranks of grey cloaks forming up in good order—but slowly, reluctantly, the way men leave a funeral they'd wanted to stay for. Their Grand Marshal stood at their head, and Aldric saw the exact moment the old woman looked down at her own glowing hands, turned them over, turned them back, and then—this was the part he would tell his grandchildren, if he lived to have any and dared lie to them—the Grand Marshal of the Ashen Order sheathed her sword, bowed her head briefly toward the plain itself, and gave the signal to march.

No argument. No decree. Whatever had risen through them on that stair had settled the question more thoroughly than any edict. You cannot preach the destruction of a thing you are currently carrying, glowing, in your own veins.

"They're leaving," Wren whispered. "Nobody's making them."

"No," Aldric agreed. "I don't think anyone could."

Sable's army was dissolving differently—not marching away but simply coming apart, soldiers drifting out of formation in twos and threes, standing together staring at their hands, at each other, at the sunrise coming up out of the ground. Some wept openly. Some laughed, the helpless laughter of men who had braced for death and received a miracle instead. A knot of them had built a rough stretcher and were carrying wounded comrades toward the eastern road, arguing amiably about the best route, rebels and defected Order men shoulder to shoulder without anyone having negotiated anything.

One young rebel broke away from the rest and came stumbling toward Juniper, fell to his knees in front of her, and Aldric tensed—but the man only pressed his forehead to the ground, glowing hands flat on the glass, and said something too low to hear. And Juniper reached out and touched his hair, awkwardly, the way she did everything tender, like a person handling something breakable with hands that had only ever been taught to steal.

"She looks tired," Wren observed.

"She looks like a woman who held a war in her arms all night," Aldric said, "and set it down gently."

They approached the last few steps together. Juniper heard them—she always heard them; years of smuggler's roads had given her ears like a fox's—and looked up, and Aldric saw her cataloguing his damage in one sweep, the way she did, and watched her decide not to scold him about it, also the way she did.

"You're alive," she said.

"Barely, loudly, and against medical advice."

"Good." Her voice cracked in the middle of the word, just slightly, and she covered it by standing, wincing, and gripping his good arm to steady herself—or possibly to steady him; with the two of them it was always ambiguous. Up close he could see what the night had cost her. She was gray-faced, hollow-eyed, her hands trembling with a fine persistent shiver, as if she'd carried something enormously heavy and her body hadn't yet been informed it was over.

But her eyes—

Her eyes were the eyes of someone who had stopped running. He'd known her a season now, long enough to know that Juniper Crane moved through the world like a woman perpetually listening for footsteps behind her. There were no footsteps anymore. There was just morning.

"Wren tells me everyone's glowing," he said.

"Mm." Juniper glanced down at her own forearms, where the light moved beneath the skin like slow golden fish. "Everyone who stood on the Steppe when it happened. Which is—thousands. Thousands of people carrying a piece of the heartstone home to every corner of the map." She huffed something that was almost a laugh. "I meant to disperse it. I didn't quite plan on it being *democratic.*"

"Is it dangerous?"

"I don't know." She said it plainly, without flinching, and he loved her a little for that—she'd never once lied to him about the size of what they faced. "It doesn't feel dangerous. Wren says it feels like being included. But I've been wrong before, and next spring somebody's going to glow in a way nobody expected, and we'd all better be ready to deal with it honestly instead of reaching for swords." She looked out across the plain—at the withdrawing Order, the dissolving army, the scattered knots of the stunned and shining living. "Which means someone has to stay. Someone has to *watch.* Learn what this is. Write it down before the stories get worse than the truth."

Aldric looked at her for a long moment.

"Juniper Crane," he said gravely, "are you volunteering for responsibility? Should I sit down? My ribs can't take the shock."

"Don't make me regret sparing your life on that stair."

"You spared nothing; I held that stair through sheer stubbornness and excellent commentary."

"Your shield is in two pieces."

"My shield died a hero and will be spoken of with respect."

Wren made the sound she made when the grown-ups started being ridiculous—a long-suffering exhale through the nose, learned entirely from Juniper—and tugged them both by the sleeves until they were walking, the three of them, slowly, across the glowing glass toward the edge of the plain where the campfires of the night before still smoldered.

* * *

They found Sable at the eastern edge, sitting alone on a ridge of glass that had buckled upward in some ancient blast, watching her army evaporate.

Yara Sable, General of the Broken Banners, terror of three provinces, was not glowing any brighter than anyone else. That was the first thing Aldric noticed, and he understood it immediately, and it hurt him in a place he hadn't known was exposed. The stone had poured itself into everyone equally. It had not chosen her. For a woman who had staked everything on the belief that power should belong to the people who suffered most—that it should belong, specifically and particularly, to *her*, as their instrument—the equality of it must have been its own particular wound.

She was weeping. Quietly, without drama, the tears simply running down a face too exhausted to manage them, and when she saw the three of them approach she didn't wipe her eyes or square her shoulders. She just looked at them the way a traveler looks at a country she'll never reach.

"You're alive," she said to Aldric. "Good. I owe you a death and I pay my debts."

"You owe me nothing. Sit down before you fall down, General."

"I'm not a general anymore." She gestured at the plain, at the scattering remnants of her host, already half-ghosts heading for the horizon. "You can't command people who are glowing. Have you noticed that? I tried to give the withdrawal order and half of them thanked me. *Thanked* me. Like I'd done something." A wet, ragged almost-laugh. "I spent nine years building an army out of other people's grief and it dissolved in one night because everyone woke up feeling—"

She stopped. Her jaw worked.

"Included," offered Wren helpfully.

Sable stared at the child for a long moment. Then she bowed her head, and her shoulders shook, and it might have been laughing and might have been sobbing and was probably both.

Juniper crouched in front of her. Slowly, giving her every chance to refuse, she took the general's glowing hands in her own trembling ones.

"What happens to you now?" Juniper asked.

"Trial, probably. The provincial councils have wanted my head for years; they'll finally agree on something." Sable lifted her chin, and there—buried deep, nearly drowned, but there—the old fire. "Let them. I'll confess everything. It'll be the first honest thing some of those councils have heard in a decade." Her grip tightened on Juniper's fingers. "But hear me, smuggler. Whatever they do to me—I want the accord. The one you're going to build, because I know you, you're already building it in that scheming head. When they sign it, I want it read aloud in my hearing, so I know the Steppe belongs to no one. Even if it has to be read to me through prison bars."

"It will be," Juniper said.

"And I want—" Sable's voice failed, recovered, dropped to almost nothing. "There's a boy. Tam. Fourteen. He carried stretchers last night. If anyone comes looking for reprisals against my people—"

"He'll have a place," Juniper said. "They all will. That was rather the point."

Sable closed her eyes. Nodded once. And Aldric, watching, understood that he was seeing the end of a war conducted properly—not with a surrender signed in ink and resented in secret, but with a woman setting down a weight she'd carried so long she'd forgotten it was killing her.

* * *

Evening came, and with it, exhaustion like a tide.

They made camp at the Steppe's eastern edge, where grass began again—real grass, dusty and ordinary, and Aldric had never been so moved by weeds in his life. The fires were small and many. Nobody sang. Everybody ate, which was its own kind of hymn.

Aldric sat propped against a wagon wheel with his ribs strapped, his right arm splinted, and a bowl of barley soup balanced dangerously on his knee, and discovered that he was too tired to lift the spoon and too proud to say so.

He didn't have to. Wren appeared at his elbow, took the spoon with the brisk authority of a field surgeon, and began feeding him in efficient, businesslike spoonfuls, narrating as she went: "Careful, it's hot. You bite like a horse, has anyone told you that? More?"

"I can feed myself."

"You held a stair against forty knights yesterday."

"Thirty-eight," Aldric corrected. "Two of them surrendered to me personally. It was very flattering."

"*More,*" said Wren, with her mother's inflection so exactly that Aldric laughed, and laughing cost him two ribs' worth of protest, and was worth it.

Across the fire, Juniper sat sharpening knives she no longer needed to sharpen, because her hands needed work the way other people's needed sleep. She'd been quiet since the ridge. Good quiet, mostly. Thinking quiet. But once, when she looked up and found him watching her, she said, without preamble:

"I keep waiting to feel different. Like it should have changed me more. I'm still—" she turned the whetstone, still turning it, "—still me. Still counting exits. I counted four on the way to the latrine trench this morning. Old habits."

"You opened your soul and ended a war," Aldric said. "Give yourself a fortnight before expecting personality changes."

That startled a real laugh out of her, brief and rusty. Then she looked at the fire.

"Aldric. When I was—while it was happening. The stone showed me things. Offered me things." The whetstone stopped. "And one of them was Maren. Alive. Apologizing. And I *almost*—" Her jaw tightened. "I need you to know I almost said yes. To all of it. Safety, revenge, the whole rotten banquet. There was maybe a heartbeat where the world was exactly the shape I wanted it to be, and I would have stayed there."

"I know," Aldric said.

"You don't—"

"Juniper." He set down the bowl, and Wren permitted it, sensing treaty negotiations underway. "I stood on that stair and fought thirty-eight of the finest killers in the world, and do you know what I was thinking about? Not glory. Not my order restored. I was thinking about a collapsed mine, and a little girl with glowing hands, and whether she'd eaten enough that week." He shrugged, and paid for it, and continued anyway. "The stone doesn't show us strangers. It shows us ourselves. You saw what you'd have sold the world for, and you refused it anyway. That's not weakness. That's the entire test, passed."

Juniper looked at him for a long time across the firelight, her eyes overbright.

"Hedge-witch," she managed finally—which was what she called him when knight felt too heavy a word and friend too frightening one—"you are insufferably wise for a man who lost a fight with a soup bowl."

"The soup bowl cheated."

Wren, sensing the emotional weather had passed into safe territory, reclaimed the spoon. "More," she commanded.

* * *

He slept, eventually—truly slept, dreamless, the first such night since Carrowmere Bridge—and woke near midnight to find Juniper sitting beside him, keeping watch, though there was nothing left in the world to watch for.

"Go to sleep," he murmured.

"In a minute." She was looking east, where the Steppe stretched away under the stars, glowing faintly, patient as a banked fire. "Aldric. People are going to come here. Pilgrims, scholars, the curious, the greedy—all of them, sooner or later, drawn to the light. Somebody has to be here when they arrive. To teach them what it means. To make sure it stays theirs—all of theirs." A pause. "I think I'm going to stay."

Aldric considered this. Considered her—the runaway apprentice, the smuggler of the Thornfell roads, the woman who had taught him that maps could be prayers and that family was a thing you built out of strangers and stubbornness.

"Well," he said at last. "Someone will have to guard the guardian. I'm told I'm between positions."

Juniper smiled in the dark—a real smile, unguarded, lit faintly gold from below by the plain that had once been a battlefield and was becoming, slowly, impossibly, something else.

Above them, far out on the glass, a night bird cried once and was answered by another, and the Steppe held its light like a promise, and the first true day of the new world waited a few hours off, gathering itself like dawn.

In the morning, Juniper would find the courier's name scratched inside the map's leather backing—*Tomas Ellery, for the Steppe remembers*—and begin, in her careful smuggler's hand, the first line of the Guardianship Accord.

But that was tomorrow. Tonight there was only the fire, and the child asleep against Aldric's uninjured side, and the woman beside him who had finally, at long last, stopped running.

Aldric closed his eyes, glowing gently in the dark like a coal wrapped in ash, and slept without dreams, and was not afraid.
