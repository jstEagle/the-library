## 36. Burials and Beginnings

Wren had never been to a funeral before. She'd heard about them the way you hear about the far side of the moon—something that happened to other people, in other lives, with other families who had things like funerals.

Now she was standing on the edge of the Glass Steppe with her boots in cracked glass-dirt, holding a bundle of Maren Crane's things wrapped in a blanket that smelled of smoke, and it turned out funerals were mostly just people standing around not knowing what to do with their hands.

Juniper's hands were worst of all. They kept starting toward the bundle and then stopping, like birds that kept changing their minds about landing.

"You don't have to carry it," Wren said. "I can."

"I know." Juniper's voice came out strange and flat. "I'm deciding where to put my hands."

"Your hands are fine," Aldric said from behind them. He was leaning on a spear he'd cut from a dead pine, because his leg still didn't trust him after the vault stair, and his voice was doing the thing it did when he was trying to be gentle, which was basically his normal voice but slower, like a man walking through a room full of sleeping cats. "They've been fine for thirty years. It's everything else that's been the problem."

"That's very comforting," Juniper said. "Thank you for your wisdom, Ser Vane."

"I have a great deal of it. Most of it cost me something."

* * *

The place they'd chosen was at the very edge of the Steppe, where the glass plain gave up and became ordinary ground again—brown dirt, gray stones, a few stubborn weeds that had pushed up through the cracks like they hadn't gotten the memo about the apocalypse. The glass itself here was thin, melted into the soil in ribbons, so that when the morning sun hit it the whole ground glittered like someone had spilled a jewelry box and apologized for it.

Wren liked it here. She couldn't have said why, exactly, except that the Steppe hummed under her skin—not the way it had in the vaults, where the humming had been a shout, a hand on her collar—but quiet now, like a big animal sleeping. Since Juniper had opened herself up and let the heartstone pour out into everything, the whole plain felt less like a wound and more like a scar that was learning how to be skin.

She could feel it in her fingertips when she pressed them to the ground. A faint warmth. A patience.

"It's waiting," she'd told Aldric last night, by the fire.

"For what?"

"I don't know. For spring, maybe." She'd thought about it more. "For somebody to plant something."

Aldric had looked at her for a long time with that face he made when she said things that were either very wise or very seven-years-old, and he'd never figured out which, and honestly neither had she.

* * *

The courier's name was Tam Vessil. They knew this because Juniper had gone through his map case one last time before burying it, and found a letter half-written in the back pocket, addressed to a sister in a town called Lowmark, three lines long and ending mid-word. *Tell her the roads were good to me this year. Tell her I finally saw the*

That was all.

"He was carrying the most dangerous thing in the world," Juniper had said, reading it twice, "and he was worried about telling his sister the roads were nice."

"People are like that," Aldric had said. "The important things and the small ones live in the same pockets."

So they dug two graves, or rather one grave and one hole, because Maren Crane's body had stayed behind in the burning waystation and all anyone had was what she'd left: a copper kettle dented on one side, a knife with a bone handle worn smooth by forty years of herbs, a ring of keys to doors that no longer existed, a jar of dried yarrow, and a wooden practice wand with teeth marks on the end, which Wren recognized immediately because those were *her* teeth marks, from when she'd been small enough to chew on things and Maren had let her, laughing, saying *that one's yours anyway, girl, I only ever used it to stir soup.*

The hole was for the grave goods. The grave was for the name.

"We can't bury her," Juniper had explained to Wren that morning, crouched down so they were eye to eye, which was something Juniper did more and more lately, as if she'd finally realized Wren wasn't going to get any shorter. "But we can bury the shape of her. The parts of her we carried out. People did that, sometimes. Before. When someone was lost too far away to bring home."

"Is that true? That people did that?"

"No," Juniper admitted. "I invented it just now. But inventing things is allowed when the old ways don't fit."

Wren had considered this with the seriousness it deserved. "Can I invent a part too?"

"What part?"

"You'll see."

* * *

The digging took most of the morning, because the ground at the Steppe's edge was half glass and half stubbornness. Aldric dug with the pine spear like a giant trowel, which was undignified and he knew it, and every few minutes he'd stop and say something like "In my order, gravedigging was considered an honor" and Juniper would say "You're doing wonderfully, Ser Vane" in a tone that meant *keep digging,* and he would sigh and keep digging.

Wren carried stones. Small ones, smooth ones, ones that fit in her pockets, which was her favorite kind. She lined them along the edge of the hole in a pattern that started as a circle and became, through no particular decision of hers, a spiral. When she noticed what her hands had done, she went cold all over for a moment—spirals were heartstone shapes, vault shapes, the whorls in the fused glass beneath the mirror plain—

But the stone under her fingers was just stone. Warm from the sun. Dumb and sweet and ordinary.

"It's all right," she whispered to it, or maybe to herself. "It's just a spiral. Spirals belong to everybody."

* * *

They buried Maren's things first.

Juniper laid them in the hole one at a time, and for each one she said something, and the somethings got worse as she went, in the sense that they got smaller and truer.

"The kettle. She made tea in this kettle the night I ran away, and she left it steaming on the stove for me, in case I came back. I didn't come back. She kept making tea in it anyway." A pause. "I used to think that was her being cruel. I don't anymore."

"The knife. She taught me to cut willow bark with this. She held my hands too tight and I hated her for it and my cuts were always clean."

"The keys. I don't know what these opened. I don't think she knew either, by the end. I think she kept them because throwing things away is a kind of admitting."

"The yarrow. For wounds." Juniper stopped there, and her jaw worked, and Wren reached over and took two of Juniper's fingers in her fist, the way she'd done since she was very small, and Juniper breathed and finished. "For wounds. She always grew too much of it. I understand why now. You can't grow just enough yarrow. Grief doesn't come in just-enough amounts."

Then the practice wand, last, and Juniper held it a long moment before setting it down.

"This one isn't mine to say anything about," she said. "This one's Wren's."

Everyone looked at Wren.

Wren knelt at the edge of the hole. The practice wand lay there with its tooth marks, its soup-stirring history, its whole small life of being chewed and waved and pointed at imaginary dragons. She picked it up. It was lighter than she remembered, which meant she was bigger than she remembered, which was a strange thing to learn at a funeral.

"Maren," she said. Her voice wobbled. She let it. Juniper had told her once that wobbling voices at funerals were traditional. "You found me in the snow and you fed me egg soup even though you said eggs were for paying customers. You called me *girl* and *pest* and once, when you thought I was asleep, *little bird.* You tried to sell Juniper to bad men and that was wrong and I'm still mad at you about it, and also you died holding a door so I could run, and both of those things are true and I'm putting them both in the ground together because I don't know what else to do with them."

She set the wand down on top of the kettle.

"There," she said. "That's my part."

Nobody spoke for a while. Then Aldric cleared his throat in the way that meant his eyes were doing something he'd deny later, and began shoveling dirt back into the hole, and the sound of it was soft and final, like snow sliding off a roof.

* * *

The courier's grave was smaller. Just the map case, cleaned of blood as best Juniper could manage, and inside it the half-letter, folded, and a strip of leather with his name burned into it, which Aldric had done himself with the tip of his knife heated in the fire, because apparently knights learned useful things besides falling off horses.

"TAM VESSIL," it read. "COURIER. THE ROADS WERE GOOD TO HIM."

"I added the last part," Aldric said. "Artistic license."

"It's perfect," Wren said, and meant it, and saw Juniper turn away quickly and pretend to be very interested in the horizon.

They filled that grave too, and Wren set one of her smooth stones on top—the best one, gray with a white stripe all the way around it like a belt—and pressed it down with both palms until it sat just right.

"Tam," she said quietly. "I didn't know you. But you gave Juniper the map instead of keeping it, and keeping it would've been easier, and I think that means you were brave in the boring way, which is the best way." She thought about it. "Also your handwriting was terrible. Juniper says so. She says it like it's a compliment."

From somewhere behind her, Juniper made a sound that was definitely a laugh trying to disguise itself as a cough.

* * *

Afterward there was the seed.

Wren had been carrying it for three days, in a twist of cloth in her inner pocket, right over her heart where the glow used to pool. She'd found it on the second morning after the Breaking, walking the Steppe's edge, when she'd seen something green-white pushing up through a crack in the glass—a shoot, an actual living shoot, the first thing anyone had seen growing on the plain since the war ended it. Around its base, half-buried, a single seed casing, empty, its work done.

She hadn't taken the shoot. She'd taken the empty casing and the three seeds still clinging near it in the crack, and she'd carried them ever since, checking on them at night like a dragon with a hoard of one.

Now she knelt at the edge of Maren's grave, where the disturbed soil was softest, and worked her fingers into the glass-cracked earth. It was strange stuff—dirt shot through with threads of glass, sharp and soft at once, the war and the world grown together. She made a hole with her thumb. She put in one seed. Then, after thinking about it, a second, because one might fail and Maren had never in her life planted just enough of anything.

"What are you doing?" Juniper asked, crouching beside her.

"Planting."

"I can see that. What?"

"A beginning." Wren patted the soil down. It occurred to her that she should probably explain better, because adults needed explanations the way wagons needed wheels. "Maren grew things. Yarrow and feverfew and that mint she wouldn't let anybody touch. And the Steppe is starting to grow again—I found the shoot, remember? So if I put a growing thing on top of Maren's shape, then every year when it comes back, it'll be like she's still doing her one good thing." She frowned. "Her one good thing that I know about. Maybe she had others. People are like that. Important things and small things in the same pockets."

She heard Aldric make the throat-clearing noise again.

Juniper didn't say anything for a moment. When Wren glanced over, Juniper was looking at the little patch of planted earth with an expression Wren didn't have a word for yet—it was sad and hopeful and a bit broken and a bit mended, all at once, like the ground they were sitting on.

"She'd have complained about the soil quality," Juniper said finally. "And the drainage. And the fact that you didn't ask her permission."

"I know," Wren said. "That's how I'll know it's working. When it grows anyway."

* * *

They stayed until the light went long and gold.

Aldric stood watch at the top of the rise, on his bad leg and his borrowed spear, because he said someone should, and because standing watch was how he prayed, though he'd deny that too. Juniper sat cross-legged by the graves and talked—to Maren, mostly, in a low voice Wren politely didn't listen to, except that Wren's hearing had always been too good and she caught fragments anyway. *You should have told me about the Order years ago. I'd have been angry and then I'd have understood, the way I understand now.* And: *The tea was terrible. You know it was. I'd give a lot for one more cup.*

And once, very quietly, in a voice Wren was absolutely not supposed to hear: *I stopped running. You'd have found some way to take credit.*

Wren lay back in the grass and put her palm flat on the earth and listened to the Steppe hum its slow sleeping hum, and felt the seeds under the soil doing their small patient work, and thought about how strange it was that grief and planting felt so similar from the inside. Both of them were just putting something down into the dark and trusting it to become something else.

The sky over the Steppe went pink, then violet, then the deep honest blue that comes just before stars. Somewhere out on the glass plain, the first star's reflection lit in the fused surface, and then another, and another, until the whole Steppe was a second sky lying on the ground, and Wren couldn't tell which stars were up and which were down, and decided it didn't matter.

"Wren." Juniper's hand on her shoulder. "Time to eat something. You're running on feelings and that's not sustainable."

"Feelings are very nutritious."

"They're not, actually. Come on."

* * *

They ate bread and hard cheese and the last of the honey, sitting around a small fire within sight of the two mounds, because leaving them alone on their first night seemed rude. Aldric told a story about the worst funeral he'd ever attended, involving a knight who'd specified in his will that his sword be buried with him and then, per a second document discovered later, that his squire be buried with the sword, and how the order had spent three days in legal crisis before someone pointed out the squire was still alive and extremely offended.

"And what did you do?" Wren asked.

"I was the squire."

Wren laughed so hard she fell off the log.

Later, when the fire had burned down to orange lace and Juniper had gone to check the picket line, Wren sat next to Aldric in the dark and asked him the question that had been sitting in her chest all day like a swallowed stone.

"Do you think she knew? Maren. At the door. Do you think she knew she was going to die there?"

Aldric was quiet for a while. Firelight moved on his face.

"Yes," he said at last. "I think she counted the men at the door and did the arithmetic."

"Then why?"

"Because you were behind her." He said it simply, the way he said true things, without decoration. "People do sums differently when the answer is someone else."

Wren nodded and looked at the graves, dark shapes against the faintly glowing ground. "I'm going to be like that," she announced. "When I grow up. I'm going to do the arithmetic wrong on purpose."

"That's not—" Aldric started, and then stopped, and she watched him decide not to argue with a seven-year-old at a funeral, which showed real growth on his part. "Just do it carefully," he said instead. "Wrong arithmetic costs. Make sure whatever you're buying is worth the price."

"I already know what I'd buy."

"Oh?"

"You two," Wren said, and then got embarrassed, and threw a twig at the fire, and pretended to be sleepy.

* * *

She woke once in the deep of night, the way she often did, dreams full of singing that was softer now, more distant, the heartstone's voice dispersed into ten thousand small voices until it was less a voice than weather. She crawled out of her blankets and walked to the edge of the firelight and looked at the mounds.

There was a light on Maren's grave.

Small. Green-white. Rising out of the planted earth like a held breath.

Wren's heart slammed. She almost called for Juniper—her mouth was already open—but then she looked closer, and the light resolved, and it wasn't fire or magic or anything to fear.

It was a sprout. Two leaves, translucent, glowing faintly with the same light that lived now in everyone who'd stood on the Steppe when the stone broke open. The seed had taken root in the glass-cracked soil in a single day, which was impossible, and was happening anyway, which was becoming the theme of Wren's entire life.

She stood there a long time, hugging her own arms, watching it breathe.

Behind her, quiet footsteps. Juniper, wrapped in her cloak, following the same pull Wren had followed. She stopped beside Wren and looked, and Wren felt her go still all over.

"It grew," Wren whispered.

"It grew," Juniper agreed. Her voice was rough.

"Is it the Steppe magic? Or is it just a plant being a plant?"

Juniper was silent for a moment. Out on the plain, the reflected stars shimmered.

"I think," she said slowly, "that from now on, nobody's going to be able to tell the difference. And I think that was the whole point."

They stood together until the sprout's glow dimmed with the coming dawn, and Wren memorized it—the color, the size, the exact tilt of its two small leaves—because she had a feeling she was going to need to describe it soon, to a lot of people, and she wanted to get it right.

Because she'd heard Juniper and Aldric talking the evening before, when they thought she was asleep. About the survivors camped at the Steppe's western rim. About the Order's commanders asking for terms. About someone needing to stand up tomorrow and say words that everyone could sign, and Juniper saying *why does it have to be me,* and Aldric saying *because you're the one who broke it open, and breakers owe the broken a plan.*

Tomorrow, Wren suspected, the grown-ups were going to start building something. Accords and guardianships and yearly renewals—all the heavy, careful machinery of peace.

But tonight, on Maren Crane's grave, something had simply come up through the cracks and glowed.

Wren went back to her blankets, and slept, and dreamed of green.
