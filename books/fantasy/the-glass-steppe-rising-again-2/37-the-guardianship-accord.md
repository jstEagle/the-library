## 37. The Guardianship Accord

The trouble with ending a war, Juniper discovered, was the paperwork.

Not the fighting—fighting had been simple in its way. You held a stair, or you didn't. You opened yourself to a power older than language and prayed it didn't hollow you out, or you did. The paperwork came after, when everyone who'd survived stood blinking on a glass plain at dawn, looking around at each other like strangers waking from the same dream, and someone had to decide what happened next.

That someone, apparently, was her.

"You drafted it," Aldric said, for perhaps the fourth time that morning. He sat propped against a shard of fused glass taller than he was, his broken body arranged with the careful dignity of a man who refused to be an invalid even when the universe insisted. His sword lay across his knees—not drawn, just present, the way old soldiers liked their oldest friends nearby. "You shattered the stone. You emptied a general. You made light come out of the ground. Drafting is nothing."

"Drafting," Juniper said, "is where wars go to be lost all over again. Every treaty ever written has been a trap wearing nice ink."

"And yet." He gestured with two fingers at the camp spread across the Steppe's edge—the Order's grey tents on one side, Sable's rebels on the other, and between them a growing no-man's-land of cookfires where soldiers from both armies had begun, cautiously, absurdly, to share bread. "Someone has to write down what we agreed on. Memory is a liar. I've known men to forget their own oaths by spring."

Juniper looked down at the scrap of parchment on her knee. She'd been staring at the same blank space for the better part of an hour. Around her, the Steppe hummed faintly—that new sound, the one that hadn't been there before the Breaking, a resonance like a struck bell heard through water. The glass plain caught the morning sun and threw it back in colors that had no business existing: rose and green and a deep violet that made Wren gasp every time she noticed it, which was approximately every eleven seconds.

"Mama," Wren said now, materializing at Juniper's elbow with the silent suddenness of a child who had spent formative years in a collapsed mine, "the grey soldiers are arguing about whose turn it is to fetch water, and one of them called the other one a 'shard-kisser,' and I don't think it's an insult anymore because now everybody wants to be kissed by shards."

"That's—" Juniper began.

"I told them if they can't share the well they can't have any of my glow," Wren continued, with the gravity of a magistrate. "So now they're taking turns. I fixed it."

Juniper closed her eyes briefly. "You fixed it."

"With fairness."

"With fairness," Juniper repeated, and then, because the thought arrived fully formed and would not leave: "Wren. What do people need to hear so they'll keep doing that?"

Wren considered this with the terrible seriousness of an eight-year-old handed a philosophical problem. "They need to know nobody gets to keep it. Not even them. Especially not them." She shrugged. "People are fine sharing things that aren't theirs anyway."

Juniper stared at her daughter—because that was what Wren was now; there had been no ceremony, no formal adoption, just a moment on a burning stair when Juniper had realized she would tear the world apart rather than let anything happen to this glowing child, and some realizations don't need paperwork—and then she picked up her pen.

*The Glass Steppe belongs to no one,* she wrote, *and therefore to everyone.*

It wasn't much. It was, in fact, almost exactly what Wren had just said, which Juniper supposed was fitting. Most of the truest things she'd ever written had been stolen from children, drunk uncles, and dying couriers.

She kept writing.

*No hand shall hold it. No crown shall claim it. No order, army, faith, or family shall call it theirs. Once each year, when the glass blooms, those who stood here shall return—to walk the plain, to renew the vow, to remember what power costs and why we chose to carry it together instead.*

She read it aloud, because treaties read silently were how tyrants slipped clauses past tired eyes, and the little crowd that had gathered—Aldric, Wren, a handful of Order soldiers and rebels still smelling of smoke and yesterday's terror—listened with the particular attention of people hearing their own survival described back to them.

"It needs a bit about the wells," Wren said.

"The wells?"

"Sharing. Water. If they're going to come back every year they'll get thirsty, and thirsty people argue." Wren nodded firmly. "Put the wells in."

Juniper put the wells in.

* * *

The signing took three days, and it was, Juniper reflected, the strangest thing she had ever been part of, including the time she'd smuggled a live wyvernling through Thornfell in a flour sack and only found out at customs.

The problem was not getting people to agree. That part was eerily easy. The Ashen Order's survivors—those who hadn't fled into the mountains when their Grand Inquisitor burned, those who had stood on the Steppe and felt the stone's essence pass through them like sunlight through water—had come to the same conclusion from the opposite direction. Their whole doctrine had been *destroy the stone, kill its carriers, end the danger.* But the Breaking had shown them a third path none of their scriptures contained: not destruction, not dominion, but dispersal. The danger ended not by killing anything but by making the power belong to everything.

Commander Elsen Vair, third-ranking officer left alive in the entire Ashen Order, had approached Juniper's tent on the second morning with the expression of a woman walking to her own execution.

"You're the one who broke it," Vair said.

"I'm the one who broke *my connection to it.* The breaking itself was more of a group effort."

Vair's jaw worked. "I have spent thirty years learning that artifacts like that must be destroyed and everyone touched by them must burn. And then yesterday I watched a rebel soldier give his bread ration to one of mine because the boy was crying." She said it like an accusation. "My doctrine doesn't explain him."

"No," Juniper agreed. "Mine either, mostly. People keep being better than their doctrines. It's very inconvenient."

Vair signed. So did forty-one of her officers. Some signed weeping. One signed, then asked if he could copy the text to bring to the families of the fallen, "so they know what their dead bought." Juniper wrote him a fair copy herself and did not trust her voice enough to say anything while she did it.

Sable's army was harder—or rather, Sable's absence made it hard. The general herself lay in a guarded tent at the camp's edge, emptied and diminished, weeping in fits and lucid in between, molten glass fading slowly from her eyes like a fire settling into ash. Her colonels had taken command in her name and did not know what to do with it. They had marched across half a continent behind a promise: *the heartstone belongs to the people the war broke.* Now the stone belonged to the grass and the wind and the soldiers themselves, which sounded like the same promise fulfilled and felt, Juniper suspected, like a rug pulled out from under a lifetime of righteous anger.

It was Colonel Brann—a broad, gray-templed woman with a voice like a landslide—who settled it. She came to the signing table on the second afternoon, read the accord twice, and laughed, a short harsh bark that startled the nearest Order officers into reaching for weapons.

"'Belongs to no one and everyone,'" Brann read. "Do you know what Yara promised us? Same words. Different meaning. She meant *everyone* as in *us, specifically, under her.*" She shook her head slowly. "This version's meaner. This version includes the people we hate." She picked up the pen. "Yara would've hated this. Which is how I know it's right." She signed with a flourish that tore the parchment slightly, then glared around the table. "Anyone laughs, I'll break their arm."

Nobody laughed. Several people signed immediately afterward, possibly out of fear, which Juniper decided counted.

By the third evening there were two hundred and six names on the accord, and the parchment had been reinforced with a backing of sailcloth because it could no longer support its own significance. They read it aloud at dusk, all together, hundreds of voices over the humming glass, and when they reached the line about returning yearly, a strange thing happened: nobody cheered. Instead there was a silence, long and full, the silence of people realizing they had just promised to see each other again next year—which meant surviving the winter, which meant going home, which meant all the ordinary terrifying business of living.

Then somebody started a cookfire song, an old marching tune both armies somehow knew, and the moment passed into memory the way such moments do: imperfectly, warmly, with someone singing off-key.

* * *

"You know they'll make you the guardian," Aldric said that night.

They sat outside Juniper's tent, watching the Steppe glow. It glowed most beautifully at night now—the whole plain lit from beneath, faint as foxfire, as though the world had swallowed a lantern and couldn't quite hide it. Wren had finally gone to sleep inside, mid-sentence, mid-explanation of why the accord needed a clause about cheese rations. (Juniper had added it. History would record that the Guardianship Accord of the Glass Steppe contained a provision regarding equitable cheese distribution, and history could cope.)

"They won't," Juniper said. "There's nothing to guard. That was the whole point."

"There's always something to guard. That's the other whole point." Aldric shifted his splinted leg with a grunt. "Someone keeps the text. Someone keeps the list of names. Someone reminds everyone, every year, why they're standing on a field of glass instead of digging under it. Someone the others look to when they start forgetting." He paused. "And Juniper—they will start forgetting. By the third winter, half these people will remember the war as glorious. By the fifth, someone's grandson will find a shard and think it's pretty and take it home. Guardianship isn't guarding a stone anymore. It's guarding a memory. It's worse. It never sleeps."

Juniper was quiet for a while.

"When Maren taught me letters," she said finally, "she used to say writing was a kind of hiding. You write something down, you seal it up, you bury it, and nobody can touch it. That's what I believed for years. Secrets as safety. Smuggler logic." She turned the pen over in her fingers. "But this—" she tapped the accord, rolled and tied beside her— "this is the opposite. This is writing something down so it *can't* be hidden. So every year people have to come stand in the open and say it out loud where anyone can hear."

"Structure instead of secrecy," Aldric said.

"Gods help me, yes. I've become a person who believes in *procedures.*" She made a face. "Maren would laugh until she cried."

Aldric was quiet a moment too long. When Juniper glanced over, his face had gone carefully neutral in the way that meant he was about to say something difficult.

"She'd also be proud," he said. "For whatever that's worth. From what you've told me—from what any of us saw at the end—she died holding a door for a child she'd wronged. People are allowed to be contradictions. You taught me that, actually. A disgraced knight and a runaway smuggler raising a glowing orphan on the road. We're all contradictions. It's the holding-together that matters."

Juniper looked out at the glowing plain so she wouldn't have to look at him. "When did you get wise?"

"About the time a piece of glass went through me and I had a lot of time to think on a stairway." He smiled faintly. "Dying concentrates the mind. Apparently so does being saved by it."

* * *

The election—if it could be called that—happened by accident, which Juniper maintained forever afterward was the only way she'd have accepted it.

On the final morning, Commander Vair stood before the assembled survivors and announced that the accord required a keeper, and that the keeper should be chosen before the armies dispersed, and that nominations were open.

What followed was less an election than a slow-motion ambush. Vair nominated Juniper. Brann nominated Juniper. A young Order lieutenant whom Juniper had never spoken to nominated Juniper, citing "the thing with the bridge," which several people apparently remembered as far more heroic than Juniper remembered experiencing. An old rebel sergeant stood up and said, "She carried the map through hell and never once tried to use it. I marched behind a general for six years and I can't say the same. She's got my vote." Somebody else mentioned the wells clause approvingly. Wren, who had been sitting on Aldric's shoulders, raised her hand and shouted "MAMA!" in a voice that carried across the entire Steppe, and after that Juniper suspected the vote was a formality.

She tried to refuse. She gave a speech about it—short, because her speeches tended to wobble—but a good one, about how she'd spent her whole life running and wasn't sure she could be trusted to stand still, about how guardianship sounded suspiciously like a cage with better curtains.

Brann listened politely and then said, "That's exactly why it should be you. Anyone who *wants* the job is lying about something."

The vote, such as it was, was unanimous. Juniper stood there in the bright morning with two hundred faces turned toward her and felt something she hadn't felt since childhood: not trapped, not hunted, not needed-for-what-she-could-carry, but simply—expected. Counted on. Part of a structure that would hold whether or not she was having a good day.

"All right," she said, when she could speak. "But I'm setting terms. First: I don't do this alone. There's a council—Vair for the Order, Brann for the rebels, and—" she looked up at Wren, perched on Aldric's shoulders, glowing faintly gold in the morning light like a small smug lantern— "and a voice for the touched. Wren's the only one who ever heard the stone clearly. She speaks for it, or through her proxy, until she's old enough to speak for herself."

"Second term," Wren said immediately, from above. "Cheese."

"The cheese clause is already in the accord."

"Second term: Mama gets to sleep late after renewals, because ceremonies are boring and she gets grumpy."

"The child is right," Brann said gravely. "Motion carries."

"Third term," Juniper said, before this could escalate further, "next year, we plant something. The Steppe's been dead glass for a generation. If the essence is in everything now—in the soil, in the water—then maybe things grow. Maybe that's the real renewal. Not just saying words. Coming back to see if the world got greener." She looked out at the vast shining plain, at the horizon where the glass met the mountains, and something in her chest tightened with a hope so large it was nearly fear. "We don't guard a grave. We tend a garden that hasn't sprouted yet."

Silence. Then Vair, quietly: "Seconded."

And that was how, at twenty-six years of age, after nine years of running, Juniper Crane became the first elected Guardian of the Glass Steppe—by unanimous vote, with a cheese provision, and a mandate to garden.

* * *

They dispersed over the following week, companies peeling away toward the lowlands in ones and twos, soldiers exchanging addresses carved on wooden tags, promising letters, promising returns. The Steppe grew quiet again, but a different quiet than before—not the dead silence of a scarred place, but the settled quiet of a room after guests have gone, still warm from them.

On the last evening, Juniper sat at the edge of the plain with the accord in its sailcloth case, adding the final touches to the keeper's copy: dates, names, the record of the vote. Wren was asleep against Aldric's side, and Aldric was telling her, in the low rambling voice he used for bedtime stories, about the knight who fought a duel with his own shadow and lost on purpose because the shadow had better reasons.

"Next spring," Aldric said, when the story wound down and the girl's breathing went slow and deep. "First renewal. You'll stand up there and read your own words back to everyone. How does it feel? Knowing you have to come back? Knowing there's a place now that expects you?"

Juniper thought about it honestly, the way she was trying to learn to think about most things.

"It feels like a debt," she said at last. "But the good kind. The kind you owe to people who'd forgive it if you couldn't pay." She rolled the parchment, tied it, and set it in her pack—right on top, where she could reach it easily, where it would travel with her the way the map once had, except this burden weighed nothing and grew lighter every year. "I spent so long believing the safest place for anything precious was buried and hidden. Turns out the safest place is in the open, with everyone's hands on it."

Aldric grunted agreement. Then, after a moment: "There's a rider coming up the pass road. Grey cloak. Alone. Been sitting at the tree line since sundown, not coming closer."

Juniper went still. Old instincts woke, sharp and cold—*runners, hunters, the hunt beginning again*—but she breathed through them, the way she'd learned to breathe through everything now, and looked.

A single figure sat a horse at the edge of the darkening plain, motionless, watching the glow. Not advancing. Not fleeing. Waiting, the way people wait when they've ridden a long way to say something and haven't yet found the courage.

"Well," Juniper said, standing, brushing off her knees, picking up her staff. "Guardianship starts now, I suppose."

She walked out onto the glass to meet whoever had come, and—for the first time in nine years—she did not check over her shoulder once.
