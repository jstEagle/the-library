## 38. Letters Not Sent

Aldric Vane had written many letters in his life. Reports to his commander. Condolences to widows whose husbands he had failed to bring home. Once, memorably, a formal complaint about the quality of the boots issued to the Order of the Silver Vigil, which had been answered by a letter informing him that his order no longer existed and neither did his boot allowance.

He had never before watched someone write letters they fully intended to burn.

Juniper Crane sat cross-legged on the floor of the waystation they'd commandeered—*borrowed*, Juniper insisted, though the coin left on the table suggested otherwise—with paper spread around them like the aftermath of an explosion at a stationer's shop. There were seven sheets visible. Possibly more under Wren, who had fallen asleep in the middle of the workspace with the absolute confidence of a child who knows she will not be moved until morning.

"You're staring," Juniper said without looking up.

"I'm observing," Aldric corrected. "There's a difference. Staring is rude. Observing is what knights do."

"Knights are mostly rude."

"That is a slander I am choosing not to dignify."

He settled onto the bench across from them, his bad leg stretched out—the Weeping Pass wound had healed crooked, and the healer in Glassford village had told him, with a cheerfulness Aldric found professionally offensive, that he was lucky to be walking at all. He was learning to live with the luck. It sat in his knee like a stone and reminded him of its presence every time the weather turned or he tried to kneel.

The waystation smelled of woodsmoke and the rosemary Maren's dried bundle gave off when it hung near the fire. They had carried her herbs three hundred miles. Nobody had discussed it; the bundle had simply appeared in Wren's pack one morning, and that had been that.

Juniper dipped the pen again. Their handwriting was small and slanted, like something written by a person perpetually braced to grab their belongings and run. Which, Aldric reflected, described Juniper for most of the year he'd known them.

"Who's that one?" he asked.

"The Order." Juniper's voice was flat as slate. "Grandmaster Corvane himself. I'm telling him exactly what I think of men who march an army against a child."

"And then you'll burn it."

"And then I'll burn it." A pause. The pen scratched. "Burning it is the point. If I sent it, it would just be words arriving at his door. Burned, it's words that lived in me long enough to stop being true."

Aldric considered this. "That's either very wise or very convoluted."

"With Juniper it's usually both," said a small voice from the floor.

Wren had opened one eye. She was supposed to be asleep. She was frequently supposed to be asleep. In the months since the Steppe, since the light had gone into everyone who stood on that glass plain and refused to leave anyone unchanged, Wren had developed the sleeping habits of a cat and the commentary of a much older person. The heartstone's essence ran quiet in her now—not a compass anymore, not a weapon, just a faint warmth behind her ribs that made her glow, very slightly, when she laughed.

She glowed a lot these days. Aldric had stopped pretending not to notice how much better that made everything.

"It's true, though," she said, propping herself on her elbows. "Mama burns things so she doesn't have to mean them forever. She taught me. You write the angry letter, you read it out loud, you burn it, and the anger goes into the fire instead of into somebody."

"Mama," Aldric repeated, savoring it. He still wasn't used to it. He suspected he never would be, and had decided that this was fine.

"She started it," Wren said, unrepentant.

"I started *nothing*," said Juniper, ears going pink. "You called me that at Glassford Bridge in front of forty wounded soldiers, and by the time I finished triaging all of them there was no tactful moment to raise the issue."

"There was never going to be a tactful moment. You'd have waited years." Wren rolled over and put her chin in her hands. "You wait about everything. That's why the letters are a good idea. You can't wait on a fire."

Aldric hid a smile in his cup of tea. The tea was terrible—Glassford's best offering, brewed from leaves that had seen things—but it was hot, and the fire was warm, and outside the winter wind worried at the shutters like a large polite animal asking to be let in.

Three months since the Steppe. Three months since Juniper had stood in the heart-chamber and done the thing none of them had thought to do: refused. Refused to take the stone, refused to destroy it, refused Sable's vision and the Order's ash-and-iron certainty alike. Instead they had opened themselves up like a door in a storm and let the heartstone pour through them into everything living on that plain—the soldiers, the rebels, the birds startled from the glass-cracked ground, the seed Wren would later plant. No single hand could hold it now. It was in everyone, which meant it belonged to no one, which meant—for the first time in recorded history—it was safe.

Sable had collapsed weeping, emptied of her molten-glass certainty, and spent the first weeks after in a cell of her own army's making, because her officers could not decide whether she was a criminal or a woman recovering from a fever. The Grandmaster of the Ashen Order had withdrawn his forces with a document full of language about "strategic reassessment" that Aldric found almost impressive in its cowardice.

And the Guardianship Accord had been signed on a folding table in the shadow of the glass plain, and Juniper Crane—who had spent ten years being nobody from nowhere—had drafted its first line in their small slanted hand: *This place belongs to everyone who tends it, and no one who wants to own it.*

Now the world expected things of them. Letters arrived. Requests. Invitations. The Order wanted a statement. The rebel remnant wanted guidance. Three separate villages wanted Juniper to arbitrate water disputes, on the theory that anyone who could end a war could surely handle an irrigation channel.

Hence the burning.

* * *

By the second hour, Juniper had produced five letters and a growing pile of candle stubs.

The first, to Grandmaster Corvane, had been four pages of controlled fury that ended with the sentence *You will send no one near her, ever, or I will show you what I showed your vanguard,* which even Aldric admitted was better burned than posted.

The second had been to the Glassford council, declining the irrigation arbitration with a courtesy so barbed it took Aldric a full minute to locate the insult. ("Tell me if I got it wrong," Juniper said, holding it over the flame. "I want it accurate." "It's accurate," Aldric assured them. "'I have every confidence in your council's ability to resolve the matter of the eastern ditch'—yes, that will sting for weeks.")

The third was harder to watch.

It was addressed to Maren Crane, who was dead, and who had died holding a door open so that a runaway apprentice and a heartstone-touched child could live. Betrayer and savior in the same body, in the same night. Juniper wrote it slowly, stopping often, and Aldric pretended to study a map of the southern roads he already knew by heart.

*I don't forgive you,* the letter said, in part. *I don't think forgiveness is mine to give for what you did to me, and I know you wouldn't want it anyway—you never could stand owing anyone. But you held that door. Whatever else you were, in the last hour you were the person you should have been my whole apprenticeship, and I was too angry, for too many years, to see you trying to become her sooner. I see it now. Too late and not late enough. Both.*

When Juniper fed that one to the fire, they kept their face perfectly still, and Aldric looked away to give them privacy in the only way they ever accepted it.

The fourth was to Yara Sable. That one was short. Aldric caught only fragments before it burned: questions, mostly. *Did you believe it, at the end? Did you believe you were building justice? Do you believe it now, in the quiet? Is the quiet worse?* And then, at the bottom, smaller: *I hope the quiet is worse. I also hope you sleep. I hate that I hope you sleep.*

"The cell they've got her in," Aldric said quietly, when the ashes settled. "In Vellmark. It's not cruel, as cells go. Window faces east."

"I know. I asked." Juniper fed another sheet to the flames. "I hate that I asked."

"You do a great deal of hating that you do things."

"It's my most consistent trait." But some of the tightness had gone out of their shoulders, and they reached over and tucked the blanket more securely around Wren, who was pretending to be asleep again and fooling no one.

The fifth letter was to the courier. The dying man on the pass whose name they had finally learned—Tomas Ellery, of the Rivermark relay—a name recovered from an Order manifest Aldric had pulled strings to see. They buried his name on the Steppe's edge in autumn, beside Maren's herbs, and Wren had said a word in a language none of them knew but all of them somehow understood.

That letter was a thank-you. That letter was also an apology, because Juniper had spent a year cursing the map and the man who pressed it on them, and only lately had come to see that Tomas hadn't handed them a burden. He'd handed them a road. Every road Juniper had ever taken had been an escape route. This was the first one that led somewhere.

They burned that one too, but slower, watching each word curl.

Then Juniper took out two fresh sheets, and Aldric understood that these were different, because Juniper did not hunch over them. Juniper wrote these sitting straight.

"What are those?" Wren asked, abandoning all pretense of sleep.

"None of your business."

"Is it the guardianship renewal?"

"How do you—" Juniper closed their eyes briefly. "You read over my shoulder."

"You write in front of the fire. Everything you do is in front of the fire. Our whole life is in front of the fire."

"She has a point," Aldric offered.

"No one asked the disgraced knight."

"No one ever does. I've made peace with it."

But Juniper didn't hide the two letters. They set them apart on the clean corner of the table, weighted with the river-stone Wren had brought from the Steppe, and looked at them the way a person looks at a bridge they've finally decided to cross.

"One goes to Serah Dunn," Juniper said. "My old cohort. The other apprentices Maren trained before me. She wrote to me in the autumn. First word in ten years. She's keeping bees in the Thornfell foothills and she heard—I don't know how anyone hears anything, the roads talk faster than riders—and she wrote to ask if I was well." Juniper's thumb moved along the edge of the page. "Ten years I assumed they all thought I was dead or worse. Turns out Serah kept setting a place for me at Midwinter every year. Just in case."

"What does the letter say?" Wren asked.

"'I'm coming in spring. Save the honey wine.'" Juniper's voice did something complicated on the last three words. "That's the whole thing. It seemed like enough."

"It's perfect," said Wren, with the authority of a nine-year-old.

The second letter stayed face-down. Juniper looked at Aldric across the fire, and something passed between them that needed no words—or rather, something that had been waiting for words for a long time and had grown patient enough to wait a little longer.

"Aldric," Juniper said. "Would you read one aloud?"

He blinked. "Read what aloud?"

"To us. Before I decide whether it gets sent." They slid the face-down letter across the table. "You've got the voice for it. All those years of reading orders to troops. Make it sound official."

"This is going to be a trap."

"Obviously. Read it anyway."

He turned it over. The handwriting was Juniper's, but steadier than usual, each letter placed rather than flung down. It was addressed to no one. Or possibly, he realized as he began, to everyone. Or possibly—to the dead, and the living, and the plain of glass that slept under snow a hundred miles east, glowing faintly green beneath the frost where the first shoots had broken through last summer.

He cleared his throat, because some habits survive the death of an order, and read:

*"To whoever finds this, or whoever needs it, which might only be me:*

*I spent ten years believing that safety was a place you could reach if you ran fast enough and trusted no one. I was taught that by someone who loved me badly, and I believed it completely, and it nearly got a child killed and a good man killed and me killed, in roughly that order of importance, though don't tell the good man I ranked him.*

*Here is what I learned instead, standing on ground made of everything we broke: power doesn't corrupt. It concentrates. It takes whatever you already believe and makes it the only thing. So the answer was never to find the right hands. There are no right hands. There is only the choice to hold nothing alone.*

*I am bad at this. I will probably always be bad at this. But I have a daughter who glows when she laughs, and a knight who complains about his knee like it's a personal enemy, and a friend keeping bees who never stopped saving me a seat, and a general in a cell facing east who I cannot hate properly no matter how hard I try, and a mountain full of smuggler roads I know better than anyone alive, and next spring I intend to walk every one of them not running from anything.*

*If you're reading this and you're tired, and hunted, and sure the safest thing is to be no one from nowhere: it isn't. I checked. I checked for a decade.*

*Be someone from somewhere. Even if the somewhere is people.*

*—J. Crane, guardian, first among equals, extremely reluctant, no takebacks."*

The fire popped. The wind leaned on the shutters and gave up. Wren was crying a little and pretending she wasn't, which she had learned from Juniper, who was also crying a little and pretending they weren't, which they had learned from absolutely no one, it being an entirely self-taught skill.

"Well," Aldric managed, when his voice agreed to work. "That's..."

"Don't say 'lovely.'"

"I was going to say 'seditious.' 'Be someone from somewhere,' honestly, the Order would've had you in irons for less."

"Good thing the Order is busy reassessing strategically."

He set the letter down carefully. "Where will you send it?"

"Nowhere. Everywhere. Serah's taking a copy when I visit—she says the beekeeping community has an information network the Order should envy. And the original..." Juniper looked at it for a long moment. "The original goes in the Accord archive at the Steppe. For whoever comes next. Some tired runner, some scared apprentice, a hundred years from now, wondering if it's safe to stop."

"And the others?"

Ashes, all of them, except the ones that mattered. Juniper swept the cold cinders into the fire to join their brothers, and the flames took them without ceremony, which Aldric felt was the correct tone.

* * *

Later, when Wren had been carried—protesting she was awake, then falling asleep mid-protest—to her bedroll, and the fire had been banked to embers, Aldric sat with Juniper on the waystation step and shared the last of the terrible tea.

"Your knee," Juniper said. "How is it?"

"Lying to me about the weather. It swears tomorrow will be clear and warm."

"Then it's improving. It used to swear constantly."

They sat in the kind of silence that had taken a war to learn how to hold. Above them the winter stars wheeled, sharp and indifferent and beautiful, and somewhere east under the snow the Glass Steppe dreamed its slow green dream.

"Spring," Aldric said eventually. "Serah's. Then the roads."

"The roads." Juniper turned their cup in their fingers. "Wren keeps asking about the Thornfell tunnels. The ones I used to run. She wants to see where I hid." A pause. "I keep catching myself planning the route. Marking safe camps. Water sources. Places with good bread." Their mouth curved. "I'm mapping it as a place to go, not a place to flee. Ten years of knowing every rock, and it's only now turning into a map of anywhere worth arriving."

"About time," Aldric said. "Though I warn you, if there's no good bread I'm lodging a formal complaint."

"To whom? Your order's gone."

"I'll start a new one. The Order of the Silver Vigil, Reformed Branch, Solely Concerned With Bread Standards."

"We'd need a banner."

"Wren would make us one. She'd put a bee on it."

"She would absolutely put a bee on it."

Inside, faintly, they heard Wren turn over in her sleep, and the soft glow of her dreaming seeped under the door like a lamp left burning for someone expected home. Juniper looked at that line of light for a while.

"First day of thaw," Juniper said. "We leave the first day of thaw. Serah's by the second week, and after that—" they gestured at the stars, at the dark road, at all of it, "—everywhere. Slowly. Teaching her the roads right."

Aldric raised his cup of terrible tea in salute. "To slowly."

"To slowly."

And far to the east, under snow and starlight, something green shifted in its sleep, and waited, patiently, for spring.
