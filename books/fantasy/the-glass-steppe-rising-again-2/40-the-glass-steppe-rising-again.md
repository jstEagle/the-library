## 40. The Glass Steppe Rising Again

The first green thing came up through the glass in the shape of a question.

Juniper found it on her morning walk of the perimeter—a habit she'd sworn she'd never adopt and now performed with the devotion of a woman who had once believed herself allergic to routine. It was a shoot no taller than her thumb, pale as new candlelight, pushing its way between two plates of fused sand that had been fused for a hundred years and had, until this moment, seemed perfectly content to stay that way.

She crouched. She did not touch it. Touching was Wren's department; touching was how you learned things, and learning things was how you got conscripted into wars, or so the last year had taught her in letters written across her ribs in scar tissue.

"Hello," she said to it anyway, because a year ago she would have said nothing at all, and growth deserved acknowledgment wherever it appeared—hers included.

The shoot leaned very slightly toward her voice. Or perhaps that was the wind off the eastern flats. She chose to believe it was the former. A woman who had spent most of her life believing the worst about everything was entitled to a few small generous beliefs now, like a pension.

* * *

"You're talking to plants again," Aldric observed when she returned to the watch-house. He sat on the porch step with his bad leg stretched out before him, mending a strap of Wren's pack with the patient, slightly grumpy competence of a man who had discovered, late in life, that his hands liked work better than his sword ever had. The leg still ached in cold weather—the Weeping Pass had taken its toll, and then some—but he'd stopped pretending it didn't, which Juniper considered the braver victory.

"I am conducting a census," Juniper said. "Of things growing where nothing should grow."

"Find anything?"

"One thing."

"That's not a census. That's an anecdote."

"It's a census with a promising margin for error." She dropped onto the step beside him, close enough that their shoulders touched, which neither of them remarked upon, because remarking upon things had never been their way. "Green, Aldric. Actual green. Coming up out of the glass."

His hands went still over the strap. For a long moment he looked east, toward the plain, where the dawn light was doing what it always did now—catching along the mirror-flat sheets of the Steppe and throwing it back broken and softened, so that the whole horizon shimmered like the inside of a pearl.

"A year," he said quietly.

"Give or take a week."

"I thought—" He stopped. Restarted. Aldric Vane had never been good at sentences that mattered; he built them the way he'd built fortifications, slowly and with visible effort. "I thought we'd bought the world one quiet winter. Maybe two. I didn't think we'd bought it spring."

"No," Juniper agreed. "Neither did I."

And that was the truth of it, the strange humbling truth they'd all carried through the thaw: nobody had expected the dispersal to *work*. Not like this. When Juniper had opened herself as a conduit beneath the Mirror Plain—when she had shattered the map-bond and let the heartstone's essence pour outward into every living soul standing on that scarred ground—she had done it as a desperate gambit, a third option scraped together from refusal and love and sheer bloody-mindedness. She'd expected exhaustion. Silence. Perhaps nothing.

Instead the light had gone into people. Into Sable, kneeling in the vaults with her molten eyes cooling back to brown and her face wet and astonished. Into the Ashen Order's soldiers, who had come to destroy and left having witnessed something their doctrine had no shelf for. Into Aldric, bleeding on the stair. Into Wren, glowing like a lantern with the wick finally trimmed right. And into the glass itself—not concentrated anymore, not hoarded, but thinned out like honey stirred through water, so faint and so everywhere that no hand could gather it, and no absence could starve it either.

The stone hadn't been destroyed. It had been *shared*, whether the world consented to the arrangement or not.

Mostly, it seemed, the world had consented.

* * *

Wren came running from the direction of the settlement with her arms full of seed packets and her boots full of, apparently, mud, which was itself a minor miracle given that there hadn't been proper mud within fifty miles of the Steppe in a century.

"Mama! Mama, Tam says the well-water tastes sweet again, and Marta pulled carrots, actual carrots, out by the south cairn, except they're purple, is that supposed to happen, can carrots be purple—"

"They can," Juniper said. "Everything can be purple if it tries hard enough."

"—and there's grass by the western edge, whole patches of it, and Ser Aldric, Tam says if the grass keeps coming the goats can come back, and if the goats come back we get *cheese*, and—" Wren skidded to a stop, nine years old and vibrating, her skin giving off that faint warm glow it carried now, the glow she'd been born half-into and finished becoming that day under the Steppe. She glowed brightest when she was happy. These days she glowed quite a lot.

"Cheese," Aldric repeated gravely, as though receiving a military briefing. "This changes the strategic situation entirely."

"I KNOW," Wren said.

"Wash your boots before you track that mud onto the floor I just swept," Juniper added, and Wren rolled her eyes in the specific way of children who know exactly how loved they are and find it occasionally inconvenient, and thundered inside, leaving muddy prints anyway, because autonomy.

Juniper watched her go and felt the old reflex stir—the reflex that had ruled her life since Maren Crane's betrayal, since the courier's blood-soaked map, since every road she'd ever taken at speed in the wrong direction. The reflex that said *this won't last, run now while it costs less.*

She let it say its piece. Then she set it down like a tool she no longer needed, and picked up the mending needle Aldric offered her without being asked, and settled in beside him on the step.

A year ago she couldn't have sat still this long. A year ago sitting still meant dying. Now it meant morning light, and a man's shoulder against hers, and the sound of a child arguing cheerfully with someone about goat logistics inside the house, and green coming up through the glass at the edges of the world.

Belonging, it turned out, felt almost exactly like nothing at all. That was the trick of it. The trap of it, even—you only noticed it was there when you tried to imagine leaving.

She tried to imagine leaving. She found she couldn't make the picture hold.

"Huh," she said aloud.

"What?"

"Nothing. Just—huh."

Aldric glanced at her sidelong. He had gotten better, this past year, at reading her silences instead of merely enduring them. "That was a large 'huh.'"

"It was a census-sized 'huh,'" Juniper said, and threaded the needle, and said no more about it. But she caught him smiling down at the strap in his lap, and she decided that was fine. Let him be smug. He'd earned it, holding that stair alone against half the Ashen Order while she and Wren ran below. They'd dug three arrows out of him and he'd complained about the stitching quality for a month, and then one gray morning he'd simply started helping Maren's old students rebuild the watch-house roof without anyone asking, and that had been that. Some men were made knights by ceremony. Aldric had been unmade by one, and then quietly remade by carpentry.

* * *

Midmorning brought the riders, as it did every season—the first of the returning parties, here for the Renewal.

They came from the west first, Sable's old soldiers, or what remained of the army that had dissolved like sugar in rain the day their general emptied herself on the vault floor. Yara Sable herself would not come until the equinox; she traveled from her cell in Corvane under honor-guard of the very Order she'd once fought, a sentence she had requested herself and refused every petition to shorten. *"I held the stone for eleven minutes,"* she'd written to Juniper in the only letter she'd sent. *"Eleven minutes of perfect certainty. Do you understand? I have never been more certain of anything in my life, and it was all borrowed. Someone should sit with what I nearly did for a while longer. It's the least interesting penance and therefore the correct one."*

Juniper wrote back twice a year. Short letters. Reports of weather and crops and Wren's height marked on the doorframe. Once, a pressed sprig of the first grass. She never mentioned forgiveness, because it wasn't hers alone to give, and she never mentioned hatred, because it had quietly packed its bags sometime around midwinter and she'd only noticed it gone weeks later.

The riders dismounted at the boundary stones, as the Accord required—no armed party past the cairns, no banner raised above knee height, everyone arriving as guests and leaving as guarantors. Their captain was a broad woman with an Order brand half-scrapped from her cheekbone; she'd defected during the Last March and never explained why beyond a shrug and the words *"saw a child glow, reconsidered my life."*

"Guardian Crane." She saluted, awkwardly, with the wrong hand. Everyone did. The Accord hadn't specified which hand and so both got used, which Juniper privately considered a masterpiece of diplomacy-by-confusion.

"Just Juniper is fine."

"Guardian Crane," the captain repeated, with the obstinacy of someone who'd decided titles were the whole point. "We've brought the tally-books from the western settlements. And a complaint."

"A complaint."

"The Kettleford contingent says our sheep grazed their side of the third marker. Our Kettleford-born sergeant says their fence is on *our* side of it. There were words. Possibly some shoving. One hat was damaged."

Juniper closed her eyes briefly. A year ago, these same people had been marching on this plain intending to kill a child. Now they feuded over fence lines and hats. It was, she supposed, the whole point. Peace wasn't the absence of conflict; peace was conflict that stayed small enough to gossip about.

"I'll hear both sides after the Renewal," she said. "Nobody touches the markers before then. If the dispute can't survive four days of waiting, it isn't worth having."

"Yes, Guardian." A pause. The captain hesitated, then, quieter: "There's talk in the lowlands, ma'am. Merchants asking why a plain of glass should belong to everyone when it could belong to whoever buys it. Someone's been offering money for survey rights."

Ah. There it was. Every season, a new shape of the old hunger—not the grand devouring kind, not Sable's righteous certainty or the Order's cleansing fire, but the small gray appetite of ledgers. Juniper had learned to respect it. Grand hungers announced themselves. Gray ones knocked politely.

"The Accord covers survey rights," she said. "Article Nine. Nothing may be taken from the Steppe by any single interest. If someone wants to study it, they study it in company, and whatever they learn belongs to everyone."

"And if they don't like Article Nine?"

"Then they can write me a letter," Juniper said pleasantly, "and I will write them back one that takes considerably longer to read."

The captain grinned, saluted with the wrong hand again, and went to see to her horses.

* * *

Evening came down gold. Juniper walked the boundary with Wren, as they did on the nights before a Renewal—an inspection, nominally, though really it was the hour when Wren asked her hardest questions, when the day's business had burned off and only the true things remained.

They stopped at the place where Juniper had found the morning's shoot. By evening it had been joined by two others, and Wren knelt over them with her hands cupped near but not touching, reading them the way she read everything now—by listening with her whole self.

"There's more underneath," Wren reported. "Not seeds. *Remembering.* The glass remembers being sand, and the sand remembers being a shore, and somewhere way down there's a river that remembers everything." She frowned, concentrating. "It wants to be a river again. That's what all this is. Everything growing—it's not fighting the glass. It's remembering through it."

Juniper crouched beside her. "Is that what the stone wanted, do you think? Back when it was a stone?"

Wren considered this with the terrible seriousness of nine years old. "I think the stone was lonely," she said at last. "That's why it grabbed people so hard. It only knew how to be *everything* to *one person* at a time." She looked up, and her eyes in the dusk were ordinary brown with a coal of light deep in each. "You taught it the other way. Sharing. You didn't even mean to."

"I meant to," Juniper said. "I want credit."

Wren laughed—that bright, unguarded laugh that had cost a war to make possible—and threw her arms around Juniper's neck, and glowed, softly, like a lamp turned low for the night.

They walked home in the dark, and the dark was full of fireflies that had no earthly reason to be there yet, and Wren declared them early, which Juniper suspected was the stone's dispersed memory misfiring gently through insect nervous systems, and decided that was also fine. The world could have a few mysteries. It had earned them.

* * *

On the eve of the Renewal, they buried the last of the letters.

It had become tradition in its second year, which was fast for a tradition but slow enough to feel chosen rather than imposed. Anyone could bring a letter—to the dead, to the absent, to versions of themselves they'd outgrown—and read it aloud or burn it or bury it at the eastern edge where the first green had come up, and no one would ask what it said.

Maren Crane's effects had been buried here a year ago: her hedge-knife, her false maps, the ledger page where she'd priced her own apprentice's talent in coin. Juniper had stood at that grave and felt—nothing dramatic. No absolution, no fury. Just the honest arithmetic of a woman who had been hurt badly by someone who had also, in other seasons, taught her to read stars and pick locks and trust her feet. Both things were true. Both things stayed true. She'd stopped waiting for one to cancel the other.

Tonight Aldric read his letter aloud, because he did every year and pretended to resent it every time. It was addressed to his old order, the Knights of the Broken Banner, destroyed for knowing too much.

*"You told me duty was a wall,"* he read, in his gravel-and-oak voice, while Wren leaned against his good side and Juniper sat cross-legged at his feet. *"I spent thirty years defending walls. Turns out the thing worth guarding was never behind one. Signed, your worst student, who finally learned something."*

He burned it. The ash went east.

Then Wren stood, cleared her throat with tremendous formality, and produced her letter, which she had clearly labored over and which she read at top volume:

*"Dear everybody who is here and also Mama and Ser Aldric who are already here so this is mostly for the record: thank you for finding me in the mine. Also I forgive you for the porridge. Also I am going to be a guardian someday and my first law will be about cheese."*

She buried it. The crowd—which was most of the settlement plus the visiting contingents, maybe sixty souls huddled against the evening chill—applauded with real feeling. Somewhere in the back, the Kettleford factions briefly united in applause before resuming their feud.

Juniper had not planned to read one. She never planned to. But she'd brought paper in her pocket, as she always did, just in case, and when the circle turned toward her with the gentle patience of people who knew her well, she sighed and unfolded it.

"This is short," she warned them.

It was addressed to no one living, and no one dead either. She'd figured that out only that afternoon, watching the green shoots.

*"For a long time I thought safety was a place you found by getting far enough away,"* she read. Her voice came out steadier than she expected. *"Then I thought it was a thing you could carry, if you ran fast enough and trusted no one. I was wrong both times. It turns out safety is a thing you build with other people, out loud, on purpose, and then defend with fence disputes and cheese legislation. To whoever needs to hear this: stop running. The road doesn't end anywhere better than here. Here is where the green comes up."*

She folded the letter. Then, deliberately, in front of everyone, she put it back in her pocket.

"Keeping that one," she said. "Some instructions you need twice."

* * *

Later—much later, when the fires had burned to coals and Wren slept in a heap with two dogs and half a goat-herding family's children—Juniper stood the last watch alone at the eastern edge, as guardians traditionally did on Renewal Eve. Tradition, population: her. She'd invented it precisely so that she'd have one hour of the year entirely to herself, and then, this year, had immediately ruined it by inviting everyone to join her if they wished.

Aldric wished. He limped out to stand beside her as the sky went from ink to iron to rose, and neither of them spoke, because they had long since passed beyond needing to.

Below them, in the strengthening light, the Glass Steppe did what it now did every dawn: it caught the sun and scattered it, not in the harsh blinding sheets of the war-years but soft and fractured, thrown upward in motes and glimmers, so that the air itself seemed full of drifting sparks—as if the light remembered being a stone, and remembered being shared, and had decided it preferred traveling.

And between the glass plates, in the cracks, in the places where the wind carried soil and patience: green. Hundreds of shoots now, where last spring there had been dozens. Grass in patches. A brave, stubborn scrub-willow by the south cairn that Marta swore would be a tree within five years. The plain was rising again—not as the weapon it had been, not as the treasure it had been coveted as, but as land. Ordinary, miraculous land, waking up a hundred years late and in no hurry whatsoever.

"First elected guardian," Aldric said at last. It wasn't quite a question.

"By a vote of forty-one to nineteen, with twelve abstentions and one ballot reading only 'CHEESE.'" Juniper shrugged. "Democracy is a rough trade."

"Nineteen against."

"Nineteen people who think I'm too fond of smugglers' roads and insufficiently solemn about paperwork." She smiled at the horizon. "They're right on both counts. That's rather the point of electing someone—to choose a person, not a portrait."

The sun cleared the rim of the world. Light poured across the plain and lit every shard and every blade of grass alike, without preference, without concentration, belonging everywhere and holding nowhere—the exact opposite, Juniper thought, of everything the heartstone had ever been.

Down the slope, a small figure detached itself from the sleeping heap by the fire and came running barefoot through the dew, glowing like a struck match, waving something overhead.

"MAMA! SER ALDRIC! The willow—Marta's willow—it's got *buds*, actual buds, come LOOK—"

Aldric laughed, low and helpless. Juniper took one last breath of the cold bright air, felt the old runner's itch rise and settle and dissolve like frost in sun, and turned to go meet her family halfway.

"Coming," she called. And—for the first time in a living memory she trusted—she meant it in every direction at once.
