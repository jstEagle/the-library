## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The thunder arrived on a Tuesday, in crates stamped with the seal of the Saltwind Cartel, and every bottle was lying about its weight.

Wren Brightwater knew this the way she knew her own heartbeat—by ledger. She stood at the guildhall's long counting table with the manifest in one hand and a bottled storm held up to the light in the other, watching the lightning inside it sulk and coil like a cat that had been put in a sack. The glass was good work, mouth-blown, waxed with black pitch against leakage. But the liquid weight was wrong. Every crate she opened told her the same quiet lie: *forty measures* when there were thirty-two, *storm-cured* when this thunder had been caught green off some summer sky and bottled too fast, still tasting of rain.

"Thirty-two," she said aloud, to no one, because saying numbers made them true. She dipped her pen and wrote it down, and somewhere in the world the Saltwind Cartel's margin grew thinner by eight measures of stolen sky.

Around her, the guildhall of the Glass Steppe went about the business of impossible things with the placid industry of a granary. Bottled storms racked along the east wall, humming faintly in their sleep. Borrowed years sat in stoppered vials on the high shelves, gold as honey and twice as dangerous—apprentices were forbidden to dust that shelf without wearing gloves lined with lead and regret. In the cold cellar below lay names not yet earned, each one wrapped in linen like a knife, waiting for someone desperate enough to claim one before they'd grown into it.

And Wren kept the ledgers. All of them. That was what a swordspeaker did now that there were no more swords to speak for—or so everyone insisted on telling her, gently, the way you tell a dog not to chase carts.

"You're counting wrong."

Master Iven's voice came from the doorway of his office, dry as old paper. He was a small man made smaller by age, with ink-stained fingers and eyes the color of a winter morning, and he had been arguing with her about the same thing for eleven years.

"I'm counting exactly right," Wren said, not looking up. "That's the problem."

He came over and looked into the crate, then at her ledger, then at her face—the order never varied. "You could round up. Everyone rounds up. The cartel would thank you; the guild would profit; and you might, for once in your life, sleep past dawn."

"The ledger is an oath," Wren said. It was the oldest sentence she owned. Her mother had said it, and her grandmother, back through a line of Brightwaters who had kept accounts for guilds since before the Glass Steppe had glass in it. "Every number is a promise that the thing written matches the thing that is. Round one number and—" she tapped the shelf of borrowed years, "—next thing you know we're selling ten years to a farmer and only giving him nine, and telling ourselves the tenth got lost in transit."

Iven sighed, which was how he conceded. "You swore that oath as a child, Wren. Children swear things beautifully and badly. You've carried it like a stone in your mouth for twenty years. There are easier oaths. There are kinder ones."

"There aren't truer ones." She set the bottled thunder back in its straw. Inside the glass, the lightning had gone still, listening. Even storms, it seemed, respected a well-kept account.

It was late afternoon by then, the light coming long and amber through the high windows, laying stripes across the floorboards like something patient waiting to be fed. The Steppe outside was doing what it always did at this hour—turning to hammered silver, heat shimmering off the salt crust until the horizon dissolved and the guildhall seemed to float alone in a sea of light. Caravans would be making for shelter. Nobody crossed the open flats after dark unless they were running from something or toward it.

Wren was balancing the third column of the manifest when the door banged open.

She heard it first as weather—a hot wind shouldering into the hall, carrying salt and the smell of scorched cloth—and turned with her pen still wet. A man stood in the doorway, or mostly stood: he was leaning on the frame the way a drowning man leans on driftwood, and the wood was the only reason he hadn't already fallen. His coat had been fine once, court-cut, grey silk gone greyer with road dust and worse. Across the front of it, from shoulder to hip, ran a wound.

Wren had grown up around swordspeakers. She had seen blade wounds, arrow wounds, the clean honest damage that steel makes. This was none of those. The cut in him was too smooth, its edges seared white, and where flesh should have bled it instead came apart like paper held near a flame—curling, blackening, unraveling into threads of smoke that rose and did not quite dissipate. He was dying of something that was eating him out of the record of the world.

"He's bleeding smoke," said Pell, the youngest apprentice, from behind the storm racks. "Wren, he's—that's not possible, is it?"

"Nothing here is possible," Wren said. "That's rather the point of us."

She was moving before she decided to move, which was how duty always arrived for her—not as a choice but as a fact she discovered already underway. She crossed the hall, caught him under the arms as his knees went, and lowered him to the boards. Up close he smelled of ozone and burnt hair and, beneath it all, faintly, of lilies. Court lilies. Palace lilies. She knew the scent from the imported candles the guild sometimes traded in.

His eyes found hers. They were pale and full of a terrible clarity, the way eyes get when everything unimportant has already burned away.

"Brightwater," he rasped. Not a question. "The guild ledgers. You're the one who keeps them true."

"I keep the ledgers," she said carefully.

"They sent me to the one person alive who can't look away." Something like a laugh moved through him and cost him dearly; the smoke bleeding from his wound thickened. His hand came up—shaking, fingers seamed with the same slow burning—and pressed something into hers, folding her fingers shut over it with a grip that had nothing left in it but will.

Paper. Warm paper, warm as if it had been held near a fire, and when she glanced down she saw why: it was a map, hand-drawn on heavy stock, its edges scorched brown, a route traced across the salt flats in fading ink toward a mark in the far mountains shaped like a crown.

"Listen." His voice dropped to almost nothing, and she bent close to catch it, and so she alone heard the warning that would unmake her life:

"The king is already gone. What sits on the throne wears him like a coat. It's fed by—" a shudder took him, "—fed by the last dragon's fire, under the palace, and it knows I'm coming, and now it knows you're coming too. Don't trust the law. The law is its handwriting." His fingers tightened on hers, brief, urgent. "Ledger it. Whatever happens—put it in the book. Someone has to keep the count true."

Then the light went out of him between one breath and the next, not the way people die but the way candles die, and what remained in her hands weighed less than a man should, as if part of him had been subtracted rather than lost.

For a long moment the guildhall was silent except for the hum of sleeping storms.

"Wren?" Pell's voice, small. "What do we do? Do we send for the wardens?"

Wren looked down at the map in her hand, at the crown-shaped mark, at the route across the salt flats she would have to walk alone, at night, pursued by the very law she had spent her life believing in. She thought of Iven's voice: *There are easier oaths.*

She thought: *Yes. And none truer.*

"No wardens," she said quietly. "Fetch me my traveling cloak, my ledger-satchel, and don't write anything down until I'm gone."

Outside, the sun touched the edge of the Steppe, and far to the north—too far to see, close enough, suddenly, to feel—something wearing a king's shape lifted its head and began, patiently, to remember her name.

* * *

She was three miles out onto the flats when she stopped, unfolded the scorched map by the last red light, and saw what she had missed in the guildhall: small writing along the border, cramped and hurried, in a hand that shook.

*If you are reading this, Brightwater, then the count was true after all. Forgive me. I have signed your name to something terrible.*

Below the signature was a date.

It was tomorrow.
