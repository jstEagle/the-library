## 3. Dragon-Fire, Guttering

The palace of Vhalen was built like a held breath.

Wren had always imagined it would be marble and gold, the way the ledgers described it—*one crown, one law, one flame beneath*. But the corridors she slipped through in the physician's stolen cloak were older than that, raw stone sweating cold, and every archway bore the same worn inscription: *THE KING IS THE LAND.* The masons had carved it when it was a blessing. Now it read like a threat, or an apology.

She moved by feel and by the map, which had begun to behave strangely since the salt flats. The scorched lines no longer pointed north or south but *inward*, curling toward her own sternum, as if the route to the king ran through something living. Behind her, three days back, she'd left the physician Marenna weeping over her disgraced charts, whispering what she'd helped do: *They didn't steal his mind all at once. They sold it. Piece by piece. A memory for a treaty, a name for a border. The crown-thing only wears what he agreed to give.*

Wren had sworn oaths once—child-oaths, guild-oaths, the swordspeaker's triple vow that lived in her blood like swallowed embers. She had believed the law was the shape justice took so that people could hold it without burning. She had not yet decided what to believe now.

The last stair went down farther than any palace should reach.

* * *

She smelled it before she saw it: sulfur and rain and something sweeter underneath, like bread baked in another country, like summer remembered rather than felt. The stair opened into a cavern vast enough to have its own weather, its own slow wind, and Wren stopped on the threshold because her body refused to take the next step until her mind caught up.

A dragon lay coiled at the base of the throne.

Not dead. Not sleeping. *Chained*—a dozen fetters of pale metal driven into the rock around it, each link inscribed with contract-script so dense it looked like frost. The creature was gray as ash and half the size the old bestiaries promised, its wings folded like a beggar's cloak, and from its throat came not fire but a thin, guttering thread of light, rising through channels cut into the stone, up through the foundations, up to the throne room above where a thing shaped like a king wore a crown and waited to be fed.

The dragon's eye found her. It was the color of deep water, and it was tired in a way Wren had no word for—the tiredness of something that had been useful too long.

"You're the last," it said. Its voice was small for its size, cracked as an old bell. "The oath-bound one. I can taste the vows on you. They're loud."

"I'm a ledger-keeper," Wren said. Her voice sounded absurd in that vast dark. "I keep accounts."

"So do I." The dragon exhaled, and the guttering thread of fire flickered. "Every treaty they bought with him, I burned the ink for. Every year of his mind they traded away, I lit the signing. Do you know how much peace costs? It costs everything, paid slowly, so no one notices the bleeding."

Wren descended the last steps. The heat was wrong—not warm but *hungry*, pulling at her, at her oaths, at the memories stitched behind them. And past the dragon, half-hidden in the coils of chain, there was a man.

He sat against the beast's flank as if leaning on the only friend he had left. He wore a plain shift; the crown was nowhere on him. He was perhaps fifty and looked eighty, and when he turned his face to her she understood what Marenna had meant about pieces. His eyes were kind and vacant by turns, like rooms with the furniture sold off. But some of him was still home, because he flinched from her drawn sword and said, gently:

"No. Please. Don't save me."

"Your majesty—" she began, and hated herself for the reflex.

"Don't." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, thin as the dragon-fire. "Do you know what's left? They took my mother's face first—I signed away the memory of it for the ceasefire at Corren Ford. Then my daughter's voice. Then my own name, most days. What walks above us is made of the parts they left in the dish. If you cut it off, I don't grow whole again. There's nothing to grow *with*." He pressed his palm flat against the dragon's scales. "But if the fire dies, it dies. That's the mercy. Let the fire go out."

"The fire doesn't have to die." The dragon's eye turned to Wren. "It has to be fed."

* * *

That was when the wardens came.

They poured down the stair with lantern-light and drawn steel, six of them, then nine, their white-and-gold livery immaculate even here in the dark, and Wren understood that the crown-thing had known she was coming for days—that it had let her come, perhaps, the way a spider lets a fly commit. Their captain was a woman Wren had trained beside in another life, Sera Voss, whose sword-hand she had bandaged after the riot at Millbrook, who had once shared Wren's bunk and her bread and her certainty that the law was good.

"Wren Brightwater." Voss's voice carried no hatred, which was worse. "By warrant of the crown, you are under arrest for treason, trespass, and the theft of court documents. Surrender the blade."

"The crown isn't the crown," Wren said. "You've seen it. You've stood near it. You know."

"I know my orders."

"Yes," Wren said softly. "So did I. Once."

And the oaths rose.

She hadn't called them. They rose the way water rises in a reed—unbidden, ancestral, the triple vow spoken into her blood when she was seven years old and too young to understand what she'd promised: *When law and justice part ways, the sword speaks for the severed thing.* Every dead swordspeaker's oath came with it, a chorus of voices under her skin, the guildhall ghosts she'd been haunted by since the stranger died at her door. Her sword was singing. Her hands were steady.

"Last chance," Voss said, and there was grief in it now, real grief. "Put it down, and I swear I'll see you treated gently."

"They'll feed me to it," Wren said. "Piece by piece. A memory for my silence. You know that too."

Voss closed her eyes. Opened them. "I'm sorry."

"I'm sorry too."

What happened next, Wren would later refuse to write down in any ledger, though the guild insisted on recording it anyway. Nine wardens came down those stairs, and nine wardens fell, and she cut them down outside any law—no warrant, no trial, no tribunal of peers—because the law itself was the thing wearing the crown, and the oaths knew it, and the oaths did not care that she cared. When it was over she knelt among them shaking, and Voss's blood crept toward the dragon's chains and steamed there, and Wren could not remember drawing the first stroke, only the sound the oaths had made, which was like bells, which was like weeping.

"I begged you," the hollowed king whispered. "I *begged* you."

"I know." Her throat was ash. "Forgive me. I couldn't stop being what they made me either."

* * *

She stood at last between the chained dragon and the dying man, her sword wet, her oaths still humming in her marrow like struck glass, and she understood the shape of the choice at last—the true shape, the one the map had been curling toward all along.

The fire needed feeding. Not fuel. Not victims dragged down these stairs in the dark. It needed *belonging*—the dragon said so, in its cracked-bell voice, explaining what the contracts had never stated plainly: that a crown-thing sustained by theft could be unmade only by a gift freely given, large enough to burn away everything it had stolen. Memories. The warm ones. The ones that made a self a home.

Wren thought of the guildhall on the Steppe. Of her mentor's grumbling voice and the smell of ink and thunder in bottles. Of the child she had been, swearing oaths she didn't understand. Of belonging—every scrap of it, every hand that had ever known her name.

She thought: *it will cost everything.*

She thought: *good.*

The dragon watched her with its deep-water eye, and something moved in that weariness that might have been hope. "Speak it, then, swordspeaker," it said. "Speak your final oath—and tell me, before you begin: are you certain?"

Above them, faint through the stone, the thing wearing the crown began to scream.

Below her feet, the fire guttered, and waited.

Wren Brightwater drew one breath—the last breath of the woman she had been—and opened her mouth to speak.
