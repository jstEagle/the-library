## 4. An Oath Spoken Once

The dragon-fire guttered like a candle in a sickroom, and Wren knelt before it with the hollowed king's hand still trembling against her wrist.

"Let me die," he had begged, and she had not answered him, because answering would have been a kindness and she had no kindness left to give—only oaths.

She understood now what she had refused to understand since childhood, since the day on the guildhall steps when Master Corvane had pressed a stylus into her small hands and asked if she meant it. Swordspeakers did not keep oaths the way merchants kept contracts. They kept them the way wicks kept flame: by burning. Every oath she had spoken had lived somewhere in her, banked and breathing, waiting for the moment it would be spent. This was that moment. There would be no other.

The thing wearing the crown was screaming above them—or laughing; at this depth of the palace, beneath the throne-room's marble heart, the two sounds had become indistinguishable. The wardens' boots rang on the stairs. Somewhere in the stone, chains older than kingdoms groaned as the last dragon shifted its ruined weight.

Wren laid her palm flat against the fire. It did not burn her. It *recognized* her—the way fire recognizes anything willing to be consumed—and something vast and exhausted turned its attention toward her from behind the flame.

"I am Wren Brightwater," she said, "last of the swordspeakers, oath-bound and unspent." Her voice sounded strange to her own ears, too loud, too certain. "I have one oath left. I spend it now."

*Speak,* said the fire, in a voice like wind over salt flats, like the Steppe itself remembering rain.

She closed her eyes and reached for the memories the way she had once reached for her sword hilt: by habit, by faith.

The guildhall first. Always the guildhall. Dawn light coming through the high windows in slanted amber bars, laying itself across the ledgers like gilt. The smell of ink and beeswax and the particular dust that gathered on impossible things—storms in glass, years in stoppered vials, names folded into paper until they learned their shape. Master Corvane's voice, dry as August gravel: *An entry is a promise, girl. Write nothing you don't mean to answer for.* The way he'd looked at her when she'd sworn her first oath at nine years old—not proud, exactly. Afraid for her, and honest enough not to hide it.

The taste of salt-plum tea shared on the loading dock with Sefa the cartwright while thunder-bottles hummed in their crates beside them like sleeping cats. Sefa's laugh, which started in his belly and took its time arriving anywhere else.

Her mother's hands. She had almost forgotten she remembered them—scarred from the kilns, always warm, always smelling faintly of clay. *You'll come back to us,* her mother had said at the door, the last morning. Not a question. A ledger-entry. A promise written down in the only book that mattered.

The fire drank each memory as she lifted it, and with every one, the light grew stronger—amber deepening to gold, gold to white—and with every one, something in Wren went quiet. Not gone. Spent. She could feel the difference, the way one feels a coin leave the hand: the weight remains in the memory of the palm.

The wardens burst into the chamber then, six of them, crowns stamped on their breastplates, blades already drawn. Their captain opened his mouth—to command her surrender, or the dragon's death, or both.

She stood up out of her kneeling like something rising out of deep water.

"You are outside any law," the captain said. "You understand that. There is no writ for what you've done."

"I know," Wren said, and found that she was smiling, though her cheeks were wet. "That's rather the point."

The oaths of the dead rose in her throat—all the swordspeakers who had ever spoken themselves into service, all their voices braided through hers like strands of one rope—and she cut them down where they stood. It was not a battle. Battles have two sides. It was an accounting, and the ledger balanced in eleven seconds flat, and when it was done the chamber was very quiet except for the fire, which was no longer guttering at all but roaring, roaring, white-gold and joyous, and the chains—

The chains broke.

Not snapped. *Unbound.* They fell away from the dragon's body link by link, rusting into red snow, and the creature that rose from the throne's foundations was smaller than legend insisted and more beautiful than legend dared: lean and dark as cooled iron, wings like torn banners, eyes the color of the dawn it had not seen in three hundred years.

The crown-thing came apart in the firelight above them. Wren felt it go—a long, thin shriek through the stone, the sound of a stolen shape losing its argument with truth—and when the dust settled, the man who remained on the throne-room floor was only a king. Old. Mortal. Small. Alive, and accountable, which was worse than death and more just than mercy, and Wren found she could live with both.

"Who are you?" the king whispered, looking up at her with the blank courtesy of a stranger.

And she opened her mouth to say *I am Wren Brightwater* and discovered that she did not know.

* * *

The flight back across the Glass Steppe took most of the night, and she spent it holding onto a spine of warm iron-scales with her face pressed against a neck that smelled of lightning and old libraries. The dragon did not speak. It hummed instead, low and continuous, a sound she felt in her teeth and ribs, and though she could no longer remember why, she found herself crying steadily the whole way home—grief without content, mourning something whose name had been poured into fire and burned.

Dawn caught them over the salt flats. The sky went from iron to rose to a pale unbearable gold, and as the sun crested the horizon the dragon began to come apart into light—gently, gratefully, scales loosening into motes that drifted upward like sparks returning to some enormous hearth. Wren slid from its shoulders and landed on her feet in the shallows of the guild road, and the last of the dragon dissolved around her, and the morning was ordinary, and she was alone.

She walked the rest of the way.

* * *

The guildhall smelled of ink and beeswax and impossible things, exactly as it should, exactly as—she supposed—it always had.

"Wren!" Sefa the cartwright waved to her from the loading dock, salt-plum tea steaming in his fist, thunder-bottles humming in their crates. "There you are. Where in the nine winds have you been? You missed the audit."

"Did I," she said.

"Corvane's been asking after you. Well—" he laughed, that slow belly-laugh, familiar as her own pulse, and yet it landed on her like weather from an unknown country, "—not after *you*, exactly. After whoever keeps the Brightwater ledgers. Says there's entries in the last volume in your hand that no one can account for. Bottled storms signed off at prices that make no sense. A map, logged and then struck through. And one line near the end that just says *paid in full*." He squinted at her, friendly and baffled. "Your handwriting. But nobody remembers writing it. Corvane says either you're a ghost or the ledgers are haunted, and honestly at this guild those aren't mutually exclusive."

"No," Wren said. "They never were."

He handed her the tea without thinking, the way you hand tea to someone who has always stood on this dock, and she held the warmth in both hands and tasted salt-plum and remembered nothing of why it mattered, only that it mattered, only that her chest ached around it like a wound that had healed clean and painless and wrong.

Inside, the ledgers waited on her desk, open to the final page. In her own handwriting—she verified it the way she verified everything, by habit, by faith—she found the closing entry:

*All accounts settled. The king owes his people a reckoning, and will pay it. The fire is free. So am I.*

Below it, unsigned. Below that, a blank line where a name belonged.

Wren sat down, dipped her pen, and considered the blank line for a long time. She belonged to no one now—not the crown, not the dead, not the guild, not even to her own past. It should have felt like falling. Instead it felt like standing in a high wind with nothing left to lose hold of.

At last she wrote a single word on the empty line, a name not yet earned, the kind of impossible thing this guild traded in—and set the pen down as dawn filled the windows, and outside, far across the Steppe, something new began to burn.
