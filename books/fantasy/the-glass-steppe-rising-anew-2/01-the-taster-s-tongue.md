## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The quail arrived at the king's table glazed in honey and rosemary, its skin lacquered to a shine that caught the candlelight like something jeweled rather than roasted. Quentin Underhill watched it come down the length of the high table on the shoulders of a nervous second-footman and thought, as he always thought when the food looked too beautiful: *there it is.*

Beauty was the first warning. In eleven years of service at the court of King Aldric, Quentin had learned that poison rarely traveled in ugly company. It preferred silk. It preferred saffron and candied peel and sauces the color of sunset. Nobody bothered to kill a king with turnip porridge.

He stood when the dish reached him — the taster's stool sat one step below the dais, close enough that his shadow fell across the king's boots — and lifted the bird from its silver platter with fingers that did not tremble. That was the first rule, though no one had ever written it down for him: *never let them see you flinch.* Not the courtiers craning from their benches, not the chamberlain with his ledger of dishes, not the king himself, who watched Quentin eat with the mild, patient interest of a man observing a well-trained dog perform an old trick.

Quentin tore a haunch free. The honey clung to his skin, warm and fragrant, and beneath it — faint as a whisper under a sermon — came the other smell.

Bitter almond.

It was so delicate that anyone else at that table would have called it imagination, or the ghost of a marzipan course served three days ago. But Quentin's tongue had been educated the way other men's hands were trained to swordwork, and it knew the difference between a memory and a presence. He let the meat rest on his tongue for a slow count of five. Honey first, sweet and honest. Rosemary, resinous. Then, arriving late like a guest who hoped not to be noticed, the almond-bitterness threading up through the fat, cold-tasting even in warm meat.

Cyanide, or something dressed as it. Enough, if he read the weight of it right, to stop a strong heart between the second course and the wine.

*Never refuse a plate.* That was the second rule. A taster who refused a dish announced poison more loudly than any scream, and half the time the refusal itself was the assassination — a king thrown into panic, a rival blamed, a court turned against itself. So Quentin chewed, and swallowed, and set the haunch down on his own plate with the unhurried calm of a man enjoying his supper.

Then he waited.

This was the part strangers never understood about his work. They imagined the danger lived in the swallowing. It didn't. The danger lived in the waiting — those ninety heartbeats between the bite and the verdict, when every eye at the table measured his face for the smallest betrayal. Quentin had made a study of stillness. He kept his breathing level, his jaw loose, his gaze fixed politely on the middle distance as though savoring. Inside, he counted. Sixty. Seventy. His pulse stayed where it belonged, because his pulse had been taught by years of small poisons to stay where it belonged.

At ninety he nodded to the chamberlain. "The quail is sound," he said, and heard the table exhale around him, and watched the king reach for the leg Quentin had not touched.

What Quentin did not say — what he would never say, not to the chamberlain, not to the captain of the guard, not to Aldric himself — was what he had tasted. That was the third rule, the one that lived deepest: *tell them only what keeps you useful.* If he named bitter almond, there would be an investigation, arrests, perhaps an execution, and then questions. How had the poison entered a kitchen guarded by forty men? Who had access? And why — this question arriving last and quietest, the way dangerous questions always did — why had the taster survived a dose meant to kill?

So he said nothing. He ate a second piece, because confidence was part of the performance, and he drank the honeyed wine that followed, and through all of it he kept the taste of almonds folded away behind his teeth like a coin held in a closed fist.

King Aldric licked honey from his thumb and smiled down at him. "Good evening's work, Underhill."

"Thank you, Majesty."

"You never worry," the king observed, in the tone of a man making conversation while deciding whether to be pleased. "I've watched you eat things that would fell a horse. You never so much as blink."

Quentin bowed his head. "Worry is bad for the digestion, Majesty."

Laughter moved down the table. Quentin joined it at the correct volume, and beneath the laughter he ran his tongue once more along his back teeth, where the bitterness lingered like frost.

He was not worried. Worry implied uncertainty. What he felt was closer to arithmetic: one dose, roughly a grain's weight, metabolized and already being filed away in the deep vaults of his blood alongside everything else he had swallowed these eleven years. Arsenic from the winter he was twenty-three. Hemlock in a fish stew. Something in a Corsican wine that had left him blind for two days and for which he had never learned a name. Each dose had gone into him and, instead of ending him, had taught him — his body learning poison the way a riverbed learns water, widening, deepening, becoming the thing that carries what should destroy it.

He was, by any honest measure, the most poisoned man in the kingdom. He tried not to think of what that made him. A fortress, his mother might have said. A graveyard, said the voice that spoke to him sometimes at night, in the dark before sleep, when the day's doses settled into his blood like sediment finding a riverbed.

* * *

The feast wound down the way royal feasts did, in stages of increasing drunkenness and decreasing sense. By the time the candles had burned down two full inches, the Duke of Marrow was weeping over a ballad, the queen had retired with a headache that everyone correctly understood to be contempt, and King Aldric was holding forth on the subject of loyalty to an audience too wise to disagree.

Quentin used the lull to make his rounds. This was the unglamorous half of his office, the part that never appeared in songs: moving along the tables, sampling from dishes whose tasters were new or sick or simply unlucky, tasting the sauces before they were served rather than after. He worked methodically, left to right, the way he'd been taught by old Master Corvin, dead now eight years, who had drilled him in the kitchen yard with cups of increasingly dishonest tea.

"Corvin used to say," Quentin told the young scullion trailing him — they assigned him one each season, a boy or girl who lasted until the boredom or the fear sent them back to the wash-halls — "that a taster's job isn't dying for the king. Anyone can die for the king. Dying's easy; the kingdom's full of volunteers." He touched the rim of a venison pie, sniffed, tasted the crust. Clean. "The job is *not* dying. Over and over, meal after meal, year after year. Dying takes a moment. Not dying takes a career."

The scullion, a girl called Bess with flour still in her eyebrows, looked at him with the expression he'd come to dread in the young ones: awe. "Is it true you've eaten poison a hundred times?"

"Ninety-one that I've counted. There may be others I didn't catch."

"And they don't hurt you?"

"They hurt," Quentin said. "They just don't finish."

She considered this with her whole face, which was the trouble with the young ones — they hadn't yet learned to keep their faces locked. "My gran says folk like you ought to live in the Steppe. She says that's where the safe ones go."

Quentin's hand paused, very slightly, over the bread trenchers. "Does she."

"That's what she says. The Glass Steppe. Where nothing unnatural can follow you." Bess lowered her voice, thrilled by her own boldness. "They say the valley eats magic. Like a—like a mouth. Anything gifted that crosses the stones just... stops being gifted. My gran saw it happen to a hedge-witch when she was a girl. Walked in with fire in her hands, walked out with nothing but chapped ones."

"Your gran tells good stories," Quentin said, and handed her the pie knife, and moved on down the table.

But the word stayed with him the way certain words did, ringing in the hollow places. *The Steppe.* Everyone at court knew he came from there — it was in his papers, his provenance, the reason he'd been chosen for this post above all other candidates. A man from the valley without magic could be trusted near the king's food, or so the reasoning went; the valley vouched for him the way a wall vouches for what stands behind it. No witch-blood. No gift. Nothing unnatural in him or his line.

His mother had spent her last ten years making sure that was what the papers said.

* * *

Midnight found him in the taster's quarters, a narrow stone room off the buttery that smelled of candlewax and dried herbs and, underneath, faintly and permanently, of the antidote cabinet — vinegar and charcoal and ground hartshorn, the taster's poor arsenal. Quentin sat at his desk with the door barred and performed the nightly rite: the journal.

It was a plain book, bound in undyed leather, and in it he recorded every dose. Date, dish, suspected agent, quantity estimated, symptoms noted, duration of effect. Master Corvin had made him start it in his first month, saying: *A taster who doesn't keep accounts is a gambler, and gamblers die broke.* Eleven years on, the journal had grown fat as a psalter, and Quentin knew its contents the way priests know scripture.

Tonight's entry was short. *Honeyed quail, royal table. Bitter almond beneath the glaze — cyanic compound, possibly distillation of peach kernels given the sweetness of the note. One grain, perhaps less. No symptoms. Filed.*

He sat looking at the entry for a long time after writing it.

Because here was the thing he had not written, the thing that would not fit into columns and dates: the dose had been *precise*. Not a cook's careless handful, not a desperate wife's whole vial. One grain — enough to kill a king of Aldric's age and appetite, and no more than that. Whoever had done it had known exactly how much death the dish required, had calibrated it like an apothecary, and had gotten it past forty kitchen guards and a chamberlain who counted peppercorns.

And they had not known — could not have known — that the man standing between the fork and the king's mouth was a man who drank poison the way other men drank buttermilk.

Unless.

Quentin closed the journal and pressed his palm flat against its cover, as though holding something shut inside it.

Unless they had known. Unless the quail had never been meant for Aldric at all.

He dismissed the thought the way he dismissed most thoughts that arrived after midnight, folding it away with the practiced motions of a man who had spent a lifetime putting dangerous things in small compartments. He banked the fire, checked the bar on his door twice, and lay down in his narrow bed.

Sleep came slowly. When it came, it brought the valley.

He dreamed of the Glass Steppe as he always dreamed of it: the pale grass going silver in the wind, the boundary stones standing in their ancient ring like patient teeth, the light so clean it seemed rinsed. In the dream he was walking the market road with his mother's hand around his, and she was pointing out the stalls — the honey-seller, the man who dyed wool in colors that seemed to hum — and she was saying, as she always said in the dream, in the voice that was equal parts love and warning:

"Remember, little Q. We stay inside the stones. Whatever calls you from outside them, however sweetly it sings — we stay inside."

In the dream he always promised. In the dream he was six years old and believed promises were walls.

He woke before dawn with the taste of bitter almond still ghosting his tongue, and lay listening to the castle breathe around him — the settling of timbers, the far-off cough of a guard, the ordinary machinery of a sleeping court. Somewhere in that machinery was a person who had brewed one perfect grain of death and threaded it through honey. Somewhere was a person who now believed the king had eaten it and lived by miracle, or luck, or divine favor.

Let them believe it, Quentin thought. Miracles made poor investigations.

He rose, washed his face in the basin, and dressed in the grey-and-white of his office. In the glass, his reflection looked back at him: a lean man of thirty-four with his mother's dark eyes and his father's ordinary brown hair, unremarkable in every feature except the steadiness of the gaze. He had built that steadiness deliberately, swallow by swallow, year by year. It was the only inheritance he had chosen for himself, and he wore it like armor, and like armor it was heavy, and like armor it was beginning — though he would not admit this for some time yet — to fit a little too closely, in the way of things made for a smaller man than the one who must wear them.

Down in the kitchens, the morning's bread was coming out golden, and the head cook was shouting about eggs, and Bess the scullion waved at him with a ladle. Quentin took up his station beside the breakfast trolleys, lifted the first cup of the king's morning posset, and tasted.

Clean. Honey, cream, nutmeg. Nothing beneath.

He nodded the tray through, and stood in the steam and bustle of the great kitchen, and permitted himself, for exactly four seconds, the luxury of believing the quail had been an accident of a different kind — a spice mislabeled, a kernel ground in error, the world merely clumsy rather than cruel.

Then the chamberlain's steward came hurrying down the stair with a parchment in his fist and a look on his face that Quentin had learned to read the way he read sauces: something bitter underneath the sweetness.

"Taster Underhill," the steward said, slightly breathless. "His Majesty commands your presence. There are heralds on the road from the capital — riding for the Steppe." He swallowed. "Riding for the valley, Underhill. With a proclamation."

Quentin set down the tasting spoon with great care, aligning it parallel to the bowl's rim, because his hands wanted to do something else entirely.

"What proclamation?" he asked.

The steward glanced at the cooks, at the scullions, at the steam and noise, and lowered his voice as though the words themselves needed permission.

"The Gift-Hunt," he said. "Every bearer of magic in the realm is commanded to register with the crown." A pause, and then, softer, almost apologetic: "Or burn."
