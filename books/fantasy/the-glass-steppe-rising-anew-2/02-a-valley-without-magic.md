## 2. A Valley Without Magic

Market day in the Glass Steppe began with the sound of bells that meant nothing.

They hung from the eaves of the tanner's shop, from the well-post, from the crooked neck of the old watchtower, and when the morning wind came down off the eastern ridge they all rang at once — tin, copper, cracked bronze — a chaos of small music. In the lowlands beyond the valley, Quentin had been told, such bells were wards. They turned away ill-wishing, marked boundaries against things that crawled through the dark between one place and another. Here they were decoration. Here they caught the light and made noise, and that was all, and that was enough.

Quentin walked the market row with his hands in his pockets and let the valley be ordinary around him.

It was his one day without duty. The palace kitchens did not need him until evening, when the king's supper would pass under his tongue spoonful by spoonful, and so he had given himself the morning like a man spending coin he'd been saving. The Steppe rewarded this. It was a bowl of pale stone and paler grass, walled on three sides by ridges of white quartz that caught the sun and threw it back doubled, so that even in autumn the valley seemed lit from below, as if the earth itself remembered some older warmth. The river ran glass-clear over beds of the same white stone, and the town of Merrow's Ford had grown up along it in the colors of dust: ochre, ash, the faded blue of shutters left too long in the weather.

He stopped first at Brann's stall for apples, because he always did, because Brann always overcharged him and Quentin always paid, and this ritual was as much a part of him as his own heartbeat.

"You look tired," Brann said, weighing the fruit with thumbs that had never once been accurate. "Palace feeding you badly?"

"Palace feeds me everything," Quentin said. "That's rather the point of me."

Brann laughed. Everyone in Merrow's Ford knew what Quentin Underhill was. There was no shame in it here; there was no shame in anything here, or very little, because the valley flattened all distinctions into the same pale dust. The king's taster bought apples like everyone else. The king's taster haggled badly and tipped too much and walked home with his collar up when it rained. Whatever he was inside the walls of the capital — instrument, servant, the last swallow before a royal throat — out here he was just the Underhill boy, odd and quiet, whose mother had died young and left him a house with too many books.

He was paying when he noticed the woman at the end of the row.

She sat behind a table draped in green cloth, and on the cloth she had arranged her wares: bundles of dried yarrow, knotted cords, a dish of river-stones each painted with a single white eye. A charm-seller. Every market had one. This one was new to him — young, maybe thirty, with ink stains on her fingers and the defiant set of the mouth that people wore when they'd come from outside and hadn't yet learned how the valley would receive them.

"Protection charms," she called out, not loudly. She had already learned something. "Blessed cord. Wards against ill-luck."

An old man ahead of Quentin picked up one of the knotted cords, turned it over, and set it down with the gentleness of long practice. "Pretty work," he said. "My granddaughter would like the colors."

"It's *warded*," the woman said. "Truly. I sang the binding myself, at the ford, with salt—"

"Salt won't hold here," the old man said, still gentle. "Nothing holds here. That's rather the point of the place." He smiled at her, patted the table once as if comforting an animal, and moved on.

The woman's eyes followed him, then found Quentin, who had not moved. Something in his face must have told her he would listen, because she leaned forward and lowered her voice as though the stones themselves might inform on her.

"They say the magic doesn't cross the boundary stones," she said. "Is it true? Truly true? Because I've come a long way, and I've—"

"I've lived here all my life," Quentin said. "I've never seen a spell do anything but fail."

He watched her take that in. Watched her hands flatten on the green cloth, watched her look at her own wares — the eye-painted stones, the sung-over cords — as if they had suddenly become the toys of a child she no longer recognized. She had traded everything, probably. A life, a name, maybe a lover left behind at whatever border she'd crossed. People arrived in the Glass Steppe the way water arrives at the lowest ground: running, and with no plan past arrival.

His mother had been one of them.

* * *

She had come up the western pass in the last month of her pregnancy, in the cold season, when the quartz ridges held snow and the road was more intention than fact. Quentin knew this the way he knew his own hands — not from memory, since she had died before he could keep any, but from the letter he had never finished reading, folded and refolded so many times its creases had gone soft as cloth. He kept it in the drawer of his mother's writing desk, and once or twice a year he took it out, read the first lines, and put it away again. The first lines were easy. *If you are reading this, you survived, which means I did the only thing I was ever truly good at.* The rest of it he could not yet bear, and so the letter waited for him, patient as the valley.

What he knew otherwise had come in pieces, from neighbors who had loved her and from the parish record that listed her, oddly, as *Maren Underhill, seamstress*, which was like listing a storm as *weather*.

She had been gifted. Not hedge-gifted, not the small warm talent of finding lost keys and easing fevers. The fragments of story that survived her suggested something vast and precise, a mind that could reach into the world's workings and adjust them, and the world outside the valley had noticed. There had been men who came asking questions, in the years before the Gift-Hunt had a name — collectors, scholars, one polite horror in a gray coat who had stood in her doorway in the capital and told her that gifts belonged to the crown, that she would be registered, measured, used. She had run instead. Pregnant, alone, carrying less than she carried in her body, she had climbed into the one place in the known world where her gift would simply stop working, the way a lamp stops working underwater.

She had traded it for him. That was the arithmetic Quentin had done since childhood, the sum beneath every other sum: her whole self, drowned, so that he could be born somewhere no witch-finder would ever look. The valley asked nothing of her gift but its silence, and she had paid it gladly, and then she had paid again, four years later, with a fever that a gift might have burned away in an hour. She had died in a house full of failed remedies, in a valley that protected her son by refusing to protect anyone, and Quentin had grown up understanding that safety was a thing with edges, and that everything precious lived exactly on them.

He had been four. He remembered almost nothing — a smell of thread and lavender, a voice humming, hands. Once, walking the market as a boy, he had sworn he saw her in the crowd, and run, and of course it had not been her, and he had cried in the alley behind Brann's father's stall, and old Brann senior had given him an apple and said nothing, which was the correct medicine.

Now he crossed the square toward the charm-seller's table, because he wanted to see her face when she understood where she had landed. It was a cruelty, maybe. Or a kindness. With newcomers it was hard to tell the difference, and the valley had never taught him to distinguish them.

* * *

"You'll want to unpack slowly," Quentin said.

The woman looked up at him with the wariness of someone who had been chased before. Up close he could see the ink stains went up under her sleeves — she wrote her bindings, then, rather than speaking them, which was a discipline he'd read about and never seen survive a single night past the boundary stones.

"The cord works," she said. "I've sold forty in Dunsany. Forty."

"In Dunsany," Quentin agreed. "Dunsany is two days' ride east, and the boundary stones are one day west of here. What you're selling is real, and it dies at the stones like a fish out of water. You can sell them as pretty things. Pretty things do well here. We have little enough color."

"That's—" She stopped. Started again. "Why would anyone live in a hole where nothing works?"

And there it was, the question every newcomer asked by the second week, usually while crying. Quentin picked up one of the painted stones and turned it in his fingers. The white eye stared up at him, blank and earnest, promising protection it could not give.

"Because outside, things work," he said. "Things work at you. A gift finds you whether you want it or not. A witch-finder finds you. A debt finds you. Here—" he set the stone down among its brothers, where they lay like a clutch of blind eggs, "—here nothing finds anybody. The valley keeps the world out. That's not a flaw in the design. That's the design."

"My sister didn't want keeping out. My sister *burned down our barn* the first time she sneezed with a fever, and the finders took her, and I'm not going to spend my life being quiet about it—"

"No," Quentin said quietly. "You're going to spend your life being safe about it. It's different. It takes getting used to."

He bought a cord after all — the blue one, because it was pretty, and because he suspected she needed the sale more than the truth needed repeating — and left her arranging her stones with slightly steadier hands. Around them the market went on in its flat, unmagical way: a woman arguing over the price of eels, children daring each other to touch the cold iron of the well-post, the hedge-witch Marta arguing cheerfully with a farmer about whether toadstone or plain patience was better for warts. Marta had been calling herself a witch for sixty years, and her cures worked exactly as often as any grandmother's, and she was beloved, and harmless, and entirely aware of both facts. In the lowlands she'd have been in a finder's wagon by now. Here she was a fixture, like the tower, like the bells.

Quentin walked on with the blue cord in his pocket and the old grief walking beside him, familiar as a limp.

This was the bargain he had inherited. His mother had bought his childhood with her self, and the valley had honored the purchase, and he had repaid it by leaving every day — riding east to the capital, serving at a king's table, swallowing death by the spoonful — and returning each time to the one place where nothing could touch him. He had told himself for years that the arrangement was balance. The Steppe kept him human; the capital kept him useful; and between the two he had built a life as durable as anything built from avoidance could be.

But lately — and he could not have said when it started, only that it was now constant, a hum below hearing — lately the valley had begun to feel less like a refuge and more like a held breath.

* * *

He felt it first at the well.

Not the hum itself — that came later, that came with the heralds — but the way the crowd had arranged itself, suddenly, into the shape of news. People don't scatter when nothing is happening. They had gathered near the market cross in that loose, leaning way that meant someone official had arrived, and the sound coming off them was wrong for a festival and wrong for a fire: too quiet, too attentive.

Quentin shouldered through with the ease of a man everyone knows, and reached the front of the crowd in time to see the herald climb the cross's mounting block. The man wore the king's colors, and he carried a scroll with wax seals heavy enough to swing, and he had the rehearsed boredom of someone delivering the same words for the ninth town running.

"By order of Aldric, King, Sovereign of the Realm and All Its Gifts," the herald read, and the phrase *All Its Gifts* landed in the crowd like a stone in still water, "let it be known throughout the land: all persons bearing the old blood, the wild art, or any manner of gift are commanded to present themselves for registration within sixty days. Those who register shall be numbered, examined, and bound to lawful use. Those who do not register, being discovered, shall be accounted enemies of the crown."

A pause. The herald licked his thumb and turned the page, and the paper crackled in the silence like something stepping on dry leaves.

"Harboring the unregistered is treason. Concealing the gifted is treason. Knowledge unreported is complicity." He rolled the scroll closed. "Registration opens at the parish house at week's end. Long live the king."

Nobody said it. Nobody said *long live the king*. The crowd stood in the particular stillness of people doing arithmetic — who among us, who knows whom, who will speak first — and Quentin stood among them feeling the valley change around him, the way a room changes when a window breaks in winter. Same air. Different air.

Old Marta the hedge-witch laughed, high and uncertain, and said, "Well, they'll be registering half the grandmothers in the Ford, for wart-cures," and got a scattering of laughter back, thin stuff, quickly spent. But Quentin saw the farmer beside her not laughing. Saw him looking at Marta with a new expression, an expression with a door in it, and behind the door something calculating.

The charm-seller from the east was gone from her table. Her green cloth remained, weighted with stones, each white eye staring upward, and beside them a single knotted cord laid straight as a plumb line — abandoned mid-sale, or left deliberately, or simply forgotten by a woman who had crossed a kingdom to escape exactly this and found it waiting for her anyway, patient as weather, at the bottom of the safest valley in the world.

Quentin walked home the long way, along the river, past the boundary stones visible far off on the western ridge — pale teeth in the pale grass, holding back everything his mother had died to keep from him. He touched the blue cord in his pocket. It was just string. It had been just string since the moment it crossed the ridge, and would be just string forever, and he had bought it anyway, because some part of him had wanted, desperately, to hold a piece of the world that worked.

That night, in his mother's house, he took the letter from the drawer. Read the first line. *If you are reading this, you survived.*

He put it back. Not yet. Not yet.

Three days later, the cook's boy came running to the palace kitchens with word that something was wrong with the porridge, and Quentin's protected, ordinary, carefully walled life came to find him at last — wearing an apron, and smiling.
