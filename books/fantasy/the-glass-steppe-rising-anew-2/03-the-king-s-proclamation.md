## 3. The King's Proclamation

The heralds came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays in the Steppe were for bread — Quentin had eaten the market's sour rye every Tuesday of his life, standing in the same spot by the cooper's stall, and he had assumed, in the way one assumes the sun will rise and the well will hold water, that the days would keep their shapes.

They did not.

There were six of them, riding through the western gate on horses too fine for the dust of Glass Steppe roads, their livery a deep hunting green shot through with silver thread. Quentin saw them first from the steps of the assay house, where he'd gone to collect his quarter's stipend — thirty crowns and a token vial of antidote he was contractually forbidden to need. The riders moved with the particular confidence of men who had never been asked to hurry anywhere, and behind them they dragged a cart, and on the cart stood a drum, and beside the drum a man unrolled a scroll so long it pooled at his feet like something shed.

"Make way," someone said, though no one was in anyone's way. The valley made way the way water makes way: silently, entirely, without being asked twice.

Quentin should have gone home. He knew this even as his feet carried him toward the square, weaving between the vegetable carts and the pen of geese that belonged to old Marta Hollis, who swore each bird had a name and lied about which. But there is a gravity to official things, a pull like the pull of an open cellar door, and Quentin Underhill — who had spent thirty years swallowing other people's malice without flinching — discovered he could not swallow curiosity.

The square filled faster than any festival ever managed. Bakers still flour-white, smiths still smoke-dark, children hoisted onto shoulders to see. The herald in green cleared his throat, and the drum sounded once, and the whole valley held its breath the way a held plate holds its contents: perfectly, precariously.

"By order of Aldric, King of the Reaches, Protector of the Low Marches, Warden of the Covenant-Broken Lands—" the titles went on for some time, each one a small stone laid across a grave "—let it be known throughout his realm."

A pause. The herald wet his lips. Quentin noticed, with the detached precision that was his curse and his trade, that the man's hands trembled. Not from cold. From knowing what he read.

"All persons bearing the gift of craft — called by some sorcery, by others witchery, by the old law *the gifted* — shall present themselves before a registry officer within forty days of this proclamation. Registration shall be freely given and honestly recorded." A breath. "Those who do not register, being found after the term expires, shall be adjudged enemies of the crown and the common peace, and shall suffer death."

No one screamed. That was the terrible part, Quentin thought later. No one screamed, because screaming would have meant admitting they'd understood, and no one wanted to be the first to understand out loud.

The herald continued into details — the registry offices, the oath forms, the penalties for harboring — but Quentin had stopped hearing words and started hearing the valley instead: the geese, the wind off the glass plains, the creak of Marta Hollis's cart, and beneath all of it a silence with edges, the silence of three hundred people deciding, all at once and separately, what survival required of them now.

He looked around at faces he had known his whole life. Tam Broadfield, whose daughter Lissie could coax a stubborn cow to milk with a hummed note and nothing more — Tam's jaw was set like a man doing arithmetic. Widow Crane, who sold charms that never worked and everyone bought anyway for luck, was edging backward through the crowd with her basket clutched to her chest, already becoming evidence. And near the fountain, the Perrin brothers, who ran the ferry and had hated the Kettlewrights over a land dispute older than Quentin himself, stood close together, whispering, and the shape of their whispering was not fear.

It was opportunity.

Forty days, said the parchment. Quentin heard it differently: forty days for the valley to eat itself.

He walked home the long way, past the boundary stones, gray and patient at the edge of the plowed fields, where the world outside began and the Steppe's strange quiet ended. His mother had brought him here when he was small enough to ride her hip. *This is where the world stops,* she'd told him, tapping the stone. *And we are what it stopped for.* He had thought she meant the magic — the wildness, the fire-craft, everything the stories said lived beyond the stones like wolves beyond a fence. He had thought sanctuary was a wall.

Standing there now, watching a herald's cart roll back toward the capital trailing its scroll like a tail, Quentin understood that walls do not only keep things out. They keep things in. And what the wall had kept in, all these years, was not safety.

It was them. All of them, every neighbor and ferryman and widow, marinating together in the belief that distance was innocence. And the king's ink had just dissolved that belief like honey dissolving bitter almond — sweet on the tongue, poison underneath, and by the time you tasted the truth of it, you had already swallowed.

That night, Quentin did not sleep. He sat at his kitchen table with a cup of chamomile he tested three times out of pure habit and watched the moon move across the floorboards, and he listened to his neighbors' houses, and he wondered which lights going dark were people sleeping and which were people writing letters.

By morning, the first denunciation had been nailed to the meetinghouse door.

Two hundred miles east, in a tower office that smelled of iron filings and cold tea, Witch-Finder Serane Voss read reports by candlelight and disliked most of them.

She disliked the report from the Marches, which claimed a hedge-witch in Farrow's Bend had been burned correctly but that the fire had taken four hours, suggesting damp wood or divine intervention, neither of which she intended to record officially. She disliked the report from the coast, which used the word *manifestation* eleven times and described, upon her third reading, nothing more alarming than bioluminescent fish. She disliked the wine, which was the crown's idea of hospitality and tasted of vinegar and ambition.

But the report from the Glass Steppe she read twice, then a third time, sitting very still.

The Steppe was supposed to be clean. It was the one clean thing left — a valley where the gift simply failed, where the covenant's last great working had drained the air of possibility the way a wound drains fever. Her predecessors had filed the same sentence about it for sixty years: *no manifestations, no bearers, no irregularities.* Serane had always found the repetition suspicious. Clean places are made, not born, and made things require maintenance, and maintenance leaves fingerprints.

Now here was a physician's note, forwarded up the chain because a junior clerk had flagged it for the phrase *anomalous humoral balance.* A routine bloodwork filing — the Gift-Hunt's new bureaucracy demanded baseline samples from all crown servants, and the taster of the royal household, naturally, was sampled quarterly.

The numbers were wrong. Not poisoned wrong — Serane had drunk enough poisons herself, in training, to know what poisoning looked like on paper, a chaos of markers like a room turned over by thieves. This was different. This was orderly. Compound after compound that should have killed a man, logged in his blood across years, present but neutralized, bound to something in his constitution the physicians couldn't name. Arsenic. Belladonna derivatives. Three alkaloids the apothecaries' college hadn't catalogued. A body that had spent decades learning death intimately and making peace with it, dose by dose.

Any taster built such tolerance; that was the trade. But not like this. Tolerance wore off. This was architecture. This was a man whose blood had been *renovated*, and renovation implied a builder, and builders of blood were exactly what the Hunt existed to find.

Serane set down the page and pressed her fingertips together.

Quentin Underhill. She pulled his file. Thirty-one years in crown service. Food-taster to three monarchs, appointed at age nine — nine, which was legal only because the appointment predated half the labor statutes, and which meant someone had wanted a child in that position specifically. Mother deceased, records thin. Father unrecorded. Born in the Glass Steppe. Resident of the Glass Steppe. The valley where magic went to die.

And yet.

She rose and went to the window. Below, the city of Carrowmere spread out in its smoky evening grandeur, and somewhere in it, tonight, frightened people were deciding whether to register, and other people were deciding whether to report them, and the machinery she had spent eleven years building was beginning, at last, to turn. She had wanted this — God knew she had bled for this, had buried a sister and a town for this, had learned to spell *control* in ash. Order was not cruelty. Order was the only mercy that lasted. Unregistered fire burned houses; registered fire warmed them. That was the whole of her philosophy, and she had never once needed more than that sentence.

So why did the taster's file make her uneasy?

Because it was too neat, she decided. Because a man cannot live in the one place magic fails and carry magic's fingerprints in his veins unless the place itself is lying. Sanctuaries that work too well are hiding something. She had learned that lesson in a town called Emberhollow, at fourteen, from a sister who had seemed perfectly safe right up until the night she wasn't.

Serane returned to her desk and drew a fresh sheet of parchment. She wrote with the economy of a woman who believed adjectives were a form of theft:

*Subject: Underhill, Q., food-taster, Household of His Majesty. Recommend immediate interview under standard protocols. Anomalous constitution consistent with covenant-lineage markers (ref: Archives, Pre-Dissolution Records, shelf 9). Do not alert subject. Travel to Glass Steppe authorized.*

She signed it, sealed it with the witch-finders' sigil — a hand holding scales, the scales tipped — and set it in the dispatch tray, where it lay white and innocent among a dozen others.

Then she sat for a long moment with her cold tea, looking at nothing.

*A covenant-lineage marker.* She had seen the phrase once before, years ago, in an archive so restricted she had needed the Grand Finder's personal seal to enter. Old documents, pre-Dissolution, describing the ancient arrangement by which magic had supposedly been bound to law — a pact renewed by mortal vessels, generation after generation, until the line broke and the wildness spilled everywhere and the burning started and did not stop for a hundred years. Scholars disputed whether the vessels had ever truly existed. Serane had always assumed the whole covenant was a fable the mages told themselves to feel lawful.

But if the bloodwork was right, the fable had a living descendant. And he served wine to the king.

She smiled, without warmth, at the darkening window. Somewhere west, past the boundary stones, in the pretty sterile valley, a man was drinking chamomile and testing it three times out of habit. He had no idea yet that the parchment had come for him too — that dread travels faster than horses when the road is paved with paperwork.

Serane Voss blew out her candle and went to pack.
