## 4. Poisoned Porridge

The kitchens woke before the castle did.

Quentin had learned that in his first year of service, when he was still a boy with a taster's sash too big for his shoulders and a stomach that hadn't yet learned its trade. The kitchens were the first heart of any palace to beat — the banked coals raked alive at the third bell, the great copper kettles filled in the dark, the scullery boys shuffling through the cold like penitents on their way to a mercy they didn't believe in. By the time the king's chamberlain rang for breakfast, an army of hands had already touched every spoon, every sieve, every grain of salt that would cross the royal threshold.

That was why Quentin loved the kitchens. Not the food — though he'd eaten more of it than anyone alive could claim — but the arithmetic of it. A hundred witnesses to every pinch. A thousand small chances for a poisoner to be seen. In the great hall he sat among nobles who smiled like drawn blades; here, among flour and firelight, danger wore an honest face or none at all.

He came down at the fourth bell, as he always did, before the morning's first tasting. Marta the cook had his place ready: a low stool near the hearth, out of the way of the traffic, close enough to the bread oven that the warmth soaked into his bones. She kept a chipped cup there for him and filled it with whatever the servants drank, because she said a man who tasted fear all day ought to drink something kind.

"Porridge this morning," she said, stirring the great pot with a paddle longer than her arm. "Barley and honey. His Majesty asked for it special."

"The king asked for porridge?" Quentin raised an eyebrow. King Aldric's tastes ran to capons and spiced wine and things arranged on the plate to look like other things.

"He asked," Marta said, with the particular flatness she reserved for royal whims, "for plain food. To show the realm he shares its hardships. There's a proclamation coming, they say." She said it lightly, but her eyes flicked toward the door, toward the heralds' tower, and Quentin felt the word *proclamation* settle into him like a stone dropped into a well.

He'd heard the criers yesterday, at the market. *All bearers of the gift shall present themselves for registration.* He'd watched old Fenwick the cooper point a shaking finger at the widow Anselm, who had cured his daughter's fever with words and a knotted cord for twenty years without harming so much as a housefly. He'd walked home fast, hands in his pockets, telling himself the valley would stay the valley. Magic couldn't follow anyone into the Glass Steppe. That was the whole point of it. That was the whole mercy of it.

But the proclamation wasn't about magic following anyone in. It was about dragging people out.

"Taste," Marta said, and held out the spoon.

* * *

He tasted everything twice. That was his rule, one of the three rules his mother had drilled into him before she died, before he even understood what the rules were *for*. Never leave the valley. Never refuse a plate. Never let them see you flinch.

The first taste was ritual. He let the porridge sit on his tongue, warm and bland, honey blooming slow at the edges. Barley. Milk. Salt. Honey. He catalogued each flavor the way a clerk catalogues coin, and found nothing amiss, and said nothing, because the first taste never says anything. The first taste is only the door being opened.

The second taste was the true work. He took a fuller mouthful, held it, breathed through his nose the way Odile — no, not Odile, he didn't know anyone named Odile, he'd dreamed that name once and couldn't say why — the way his mother had taught him, drawing air across the back of the throat where bitterness lived longest.

There.

Faint. Fainter than faint. Beneath the honey, beneath the milk-fat, something green and mineral, like the smell of a crushed stem, like rain on slate. It wasn't almond. It wasn't any of the forty-one poisons he carried names for, the ones the crown's apothecaries had fed him in measured doses since he was nine years old until his blood learned each one and shrugged.

This was new.

Quentin did not flinch. Rule three. He swallowed, set down the spoon, and smiled at Marta the way he smiled at everyone, the smile that said *all is well, the king may eat.*

But his mind was already moving through the kitchen like a cat through tall grass. Who had access to the pot? Marta, obviously. The two undercooks, Pell and young Sef. The scullery girl who'd fetched the milk. The pantry steward who'd weighed the barley. The honey came sealed from the royal apiary — he'd watched them break the wax himself a hundred mornings. The milk came up from the dairy in churns that were sealed and signed.

And yet. Something green and mineral, hidden under honey, dosed not into the whole pot — he was almost sure of that now, tasting the memory of it — but into a single portion. Into *his* portion. Because Marta always gave him the first bowl off the fire, the taster's bowl, the bowl that existed so that anything meant for the king would meet Quentin's tongue first.

Someone had poisoned the taster.

* * *

He should have spoken. He knew that even as he sat there, holding the chipped cup of kindness-tea in both hands, watching steam curl up past his face. He should have said, *Marta, don't serve the pot,* and turned the morning into shouting and seals and men with white gloves carrying the porridge away in a locked box. That was the procedure. That was what the sash on his shoulder meant.

Instead he heard himself say, "Let me try it again after it sits. Honey changes as it cools. You know how the king complains."

Marta laughed. "Fussiest man in seven kingdoms, and we love him for it." She ladled a fresh bowl and set it aside to cool, and went back to her bread, and Quentin sat very still and did the arithmetic no taster was ever supposed to do.

If he reported the poison, the kitchens would be sealed, the staff questioned, and whoever had done this — if they were still among these warm, flour-dusted people — would know their dose had failed. And if it failed because Quentin's blood was not ordinary blood, then reporting it would hand the witch-finders exactly the thread they needed. *The taster survived a poison. What kind of man survives poison?* Serane Voss had looked at him across the courtyard last week with eyes like instruments, and he had thought nothing of it then, and he thought a great deal of it now.

Or he could say nothing. Let the pot be served. Trust the dose was his alone — trust the aim of a stranger with his life.

His mother's voice, worn soft by years of remembering: *Never let them see you flinch, Quentin. Flinching tells a hunter where the heart is.*

He drank his tea. The porridge cooled. The bell rang for the king's breakfast, and the world went on being ordinary, and Quentin Underhill committed the quietest crime of his life: he let a poisoned pot leave the kitchen, betting everything on the theory that he had been the target and the target alone.

* * *

The king ate his plain porridge before his assembled court, making a show of it, remarking loudly on the virtue of simple fare. Quentin stood at his shoulder with the tasting spoon and performed his office, and every mouthful he lifted to his own lips was a prayer he didn't believe in. Nothing. Nothing again. Whatever was in the pot had been thinned by the whole batch, or it had been meant for one bowl only, or —

Or it had been meant for him, and the king was eating it anyway, and Quentin's palms went damp inside his gloves.

Aldric finished, pronounced the meal good, and dismissed the court to the business of the realm. Quentin walked out of the hall with his spine straight and his thoughts screaming, and made it as far as the colonnade before the bells began.

Not the breakfast bells. Not the hour bells. The alarm bells — three strokes, pause, three strokes — the pattern that meant death in the palace, and everyone within the walls stopped where they stood, the way people do, turning toward the sound as if sound could tell them whose.

They came from the kitchens.

* * *

By the time he pushed through the crowd at the service door, the physician's men were already there, and the witch-finders' men too, gray-cloaked and wrong in the steam and warmth of the place like crows in a dovecote. Someone had thrown open the shutters and the morning light fell hard across the flagstones, across the great cold hearth, across Marta.

She lay by the bread ovens where she must have fallen, one arm flung out, the paddle still near her hand. Her face was gray in a way that had nothing to do with death's usual pallor; there was a greenish cast around her mouth, a stain like crushed stems, like rain on slate. The same signature. The same poison.

She had tasted her own cooking. Of course she had. Forty years a cook — you taste as you go, you adjust, it's not pride, it's craft. She'd dipped the spoon, the same spoon, the one she'd held out to him that morning with such easy trust, and her body had done what bodies do when they are given a thing they cannot forgive.

Quentin stood in the doorway and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, three things at once.

First: the poison had been in the whole pot after all. Thinned through thirty servings, it had been nothing — a whisper, a trace, harmless to the king, harmless to Pell and Sef and the scullery girl. But Marta had tasted it *neat*, from the stirring paddle, concentrated at the bottom of the pot where the heavy sediment of it had settled. She had been killed not by malice aimed at her but by the slop of someone else's arithmetic.

Second: he had eaten the same whisper, the same trace, and it had done nothing to him. Less than nothing. He had catalogued it like a spice. Which meant the compound was one his blood already knew — or one his blood was built to burn. Either answer was impossible, and both were true.

Third: someone had cooked a poison into the king's own breakfast that could only have been meant to test one man in the palace. Not to kill him. To *see*. A dose calibrated to pass through an ordinary body unnoticed and to announce itself, loud as a bell, in a body that carried what his carried. They had used the king's table as a lamp, and Quentin as the moth.

"Everyone out," said a voice, and the crowd parted, and Serane Voss came through it.

She was smaller than her reputation, which was itself a kind of armor; men expected a giant and got a woman with ink-stained fingers and a face like a closed ledger. She crossed the kitchen without hurry, crouched beside Marta, and did not touch the body. She looked at the green stain at the dead woman's mouth for a long moment. Then she looked up — not at her own men, not at the weeping scullery girl, not at the trembling undercooks.

At Quentin.

"You're the taster," she said. Though of course she knew. Everyone in the palace knew the taster; that was rather the point of a taster.

"I am, my lady."

"You sampled this morning's pot."

"Every dish is sampled, my lady. That is my office."

"And you're standing there," she said, rising, brushing nothing from her gray skirts, "quite alive."

"The dose was thin, spread through the pot. Cook Tarnish tasted it from the paddle, where it had settled. Concentrated." He heard his own voice deliver the explanation, clean and reasonable, and marveled distantly at how easily the lie of omission dressed itself in truth. Every word he said was accurate. Together they were a wall.

Voss considered him. Around them the kitchen dripped and steamed and grieved, and her men moved through it marking things with chalk, and Quentin stood in the middle of the room where he had felt safest for nineteen years and understood that the room was gone. The chipped cup by the hearth. The stool that fit him. The warmth that soaked into his bones. All of it was evidence now, or would be, the moment she decided to look.

"A curious thing," Voss said mildly. "The compound in this pot — our apothecaries will confirm it by noon, but I know it already — is called *verdis root*, steeped and reduced. It's rare. It's expensive. And it has one interesting property." She took a step closer. This close, he could see that her eyes were not cold at all; they were burning, quietly, the way peat burns underground, without flame, without end. "It does nothing to ordinary blood. Passes through like water. But in blood that carries the old taint — covenant-blood, gifted-blood — it *sings*. Marks the drinker. Leaves a residue for days."

The kitchen was very quiet. Even the grieving had gone silent, the servants straining to hear without seeming to.

"So either," Voss continued, "someone spent a fortune to poison a pot in a way that could harm no one in this palace — which would be a strange extravagance. Or someone wished to learn whether a certain man at the king's shoulder would light up like a festival lantern when tested." She tilted her head. "You look pale, Master Underhill. Sit down, perhaps. On your stool. By the hearth. Where you always sit."

"My lady," Quentin said, and was proud, later, of how steady it came out, "I have been poisoned professionally for nineteen years. My blood is a pharmacy. If your root sings in gifted blood, it may simply have found my immunity harmonious. Ask any apothecary in the city what twenty years of measured doses does to a man."

"Oh, I intend to ask many questions," Serane Voss said. "That's the whole of my profession. But not here." She glanced once more at Marta, at the paddle, at the green-stained mouth of a woman who had been kind to him every morning for nineteen years, and something moved behind the peat-fire of her eyes — not softness, exactly. Recognition, maybe. One hunter noting another's kill. "Tomorrow, Master Underhill. Tea, in my office. You'll come willingly, I hope. The alternative paperwork is so tedious for us both."

She left. Her men left, chalk-marks drying on the flagstones like a language he refused to read. The servants began to wail again, and the physicians did their offices, and somewhere above it all the bells resumed their ordinary hours as if the morning had been a bad dream the palace agreed to wake from together.

Quentin stayed until they made him go. Then he walked back through the colonnades with his spine straight and rule three held tight between his teeth — *never let them see you flinch* — and only when he reached his own narrow room, with the door barred and the shutter closed, did he put his head in his hands and shake.

Because the arithmetic would not stop. Verdis root sang in covenant-blood. He had drunk it and felt nothing, and *nothing* was the loudest answer there was. Someone had paid a fortune to make him sing, and he had stood silent, and tomorrow he would sit across from Serane Voss over a cup of tea she would certainly poison in front of him, smiling, watching, waiting for the note only his blood could sound.

And underneath the fear, quieter and worse, a single question kept surfacing like a drowned thing:

Who, outside these walls, had known what he was — before even he had let himself know it?

On his desk, beneath the bar of afternoon light, sat a letter he had never opened. His mother's handwriting. His mother's seal, unbroken these fifteen years. He had told himself he kept it unread out of grief.

Sitting in the dark of his shuttered room, listening to his own heart count faster than the bells, Quentin finally admitted the truth: he had kept it sealed because he had always suspected what it would say.

He reached for it — and stopped, hand trembling, as boots rang in the corridor outside, and a voice he already dreaded said pleasantly through the door:

"Master Underhill? Forgive me. Tomorrow's tea — I find I'd rather it were today."
