## 5. Serane's Questions

The summons came at dawn, folded into the hands of a boy who would not meet Quentin's eyes.

He read it twice by candlelight, though once was enough. *Quentin Underhill, taster to the crown, is requested to attend the Witch-Finder Serane Voss at the ninth hour in the guesthouse of the Blue Kettle Inn. Matters of the kitchens require his counsel.*

*Matters of the kitchens.* Four days since the porridge. Four days since Marta had died with her lips gray and her hands still curled around the ladle she'd held for thirty years, and no one had said a word to him about it — not the steward, not the guard captain, not the priest who'd buried her in the paupers' row behind Saint Ferren's. The silence had been worse than accusation. Silence meant they were still deciding what he was.

He dressed carefully. Not his good coat — that would look like ambition — but not his work clothes either, which might read as insolence. Somewhere in between: the brown wool jacket with the mended elbow, the one that said *I am a man of modest means who has nothing to hide*, which was true, and *I am an ordinary man*, which had never been true for a single day of his life.

The Blue Kettle sat on the market square's east side, wedged between a cooper's yard and a chandler whose tallow smell had soaked into the valley's very stones. Quentin had taken tea there perhaps a hundred times. He knew the innkeeper's cough, the cat that slept in the window, the particular rattle the kettle made when it boiled. This morning the cat was gone from the sill. The window was shuttered against the light.

Two witch-finders stood at the door. Not the king's men in their scarlet — these wore ash-gray, unmarked but for a small iron pin shaped like an open eye. They did not announce themselves or ask his name. One simply opened the door, and the other watched him pass with the flat attention of a man counting coins.

Inside, the common room had been cleared. Every table pushed to the walls except one, set in the center of the floorboards as if for a performance. On it: a teapot, two cups, a plate of honeycakes, and a woman seated with her back to the shuttered window so that the light fell on Quentin's face and left hers in shadow.

She was smaller than he'd expected. He had built Serane Voss in his mind out of rumor and dread — tall, iron-haired, a voice like a gate closing. The woman at the table was slight, dark-eyed, perhaps forty, with the kind of face you forgot in a crowd and then spent years trying to place. Only her hands were memorable: long-fingered, utterly still, resting on the table's edge like instruments set down between uses.

"Master Underhill." Her voice was quiet enough that he had to lean toward her to catch it, which he understood at once was deliberate. "Thank you for coming. Sit, please."

"I received your letter." He sat. The chair scraped loud in the empty room.

"You received my letter," she agreed. "And you came alone, without escort, without protest, without sending word ahead to anyone." She tilted her head. "That interests me."

"Should I have sent word ahead?"

"To whom? That is rather my question." She reached for the pot. "You'll forgive the theater. I find tea loosens people more than questions do. Questions make people arrange themselves. Tea makes them forget they've arranged anything at all."

She poured. The tea was black, bitter-smelling, laced with something floral underneath — he catalogued the scent automatically, the way he catalogued everything that entered his mouth's vicinity, an habit so deep it had become indistinguishable from breathing. Valerian. And beneath the valerian, faint but unmistakable to a tongue trained over twenty years:

*Hemlock. Water hemlock, the root steeped long. Enough to stop a strong man's heart inside a quarter hour.*

His blood went cold and then, strangely, calm. This was familiar territory after all. A poison in a cup. The only difference was that this time the cup was pointed at him openly, and the woman pouring it was watching his face instead of the king's.

She filled both cups. She drank first from hers — a small sip, deliberate, showing him it was the same pot — and then slid his across the wood.

"Please," she said. "It's imported. The valley grows nothing worth drinking."

* * *

Later — much later, in the years when he told this story to students of his own — Quentin would try to explain what those seconds were like. There was no way to make it understood. Every person alive carried a private map of terror, and no map matched another's.

In the moment itself, there was only arithmetic. If he refused the cup, he was a man with something to hide, and men with something to hide were declared gifted on less evidence than a refusal. If he drank and lived, he confirmed whatever her bloodwork had already whispered. If he drank and died — well. That outcome, at least, required nothing further of him.

There was a fourth option, the one his mother would have chosen: the cup palmed, the tea poured into the flowerpot, the spill blamed on his sleeve. But the room was bare. No plants. No shadows deep enough. And her eyes hadn't left his hands since he'd sat down.

So he picked up the cup.

"To matters of the kitchens," he said, and drank.

The taste bloomed across his tongue in layers, the way poisons always did for him — sweet first, then the valerian's dusty warmth, then the hemlock's green bitterness rising at the back of his throat like a weed through pavement. His body met it the way a harbor meets a wave: absorbing, dispersing, breaking it into harmlessness with a chemistry he'd spent a lifetime building dose by dose, sip by swallowed sip, starting at age seven with a single drop of foxglove in warm milk.

He set the cup down. He did not flinch. He had never let them see him flinch.

Serane Voss watched him for a long moment. Then she lifted her own cup and drank properly, deeply, and he saw — because he was looking now, really looking — the fine tremor in her fingers as she set it down. She had drunk the same tea. She was not immune to anything.

"You're wondering if I poisoned myself too," she said. "I did. It's the same pot. It's also irrelevant, because we both know that whatever is in that pot will kill me by noon and leave you hungry for lunch." She folded her hands again. "Water hemlock, Master Underhill. Root-steeped, concentrated. A lethal dose for a grown man. I'm told it tastes like wild carrot gone wrong. Did it?"

"It tasted like tea," Quentin said.

"Mm." Almost a smile. "Everything does, for you. That's what the records say. Twenty-three years in the crown's service, three hundred and eleven dishes sampled, seventeen verified poisoning attempts survived — including, I note, arsenic twice, belladonna once, and a truly ambitious attempt with distilled aconite that should have dropped a horse." She recited it without looking at any paper. She had memorized his life. "A remarkable constitution. Remarkable men interest me. So do remarkable bloodwork."

"The cook died," Quentin said. "Marta Ellery. Thirty years in the palace kitchens. She raised half the scullery boys like a mother. Is that what this tea is about?"

Something moved behind Voss's eyes — not softness exactly, but recognition, the look of someone hearing a question she had already asked herself. "The compound in the porridge was called *greyvein*. It doesn't exist outside three texts, all of them magical, all of them banned. It passes through ordinary blood like water through a sieve. In gifted blood, it binds. It accumulates. It kills slowly, over days, from the inside." She paused. "In your blood, according to the sample my apothecary took while you slept last week—"

"You drugged me."

"We needed a clean sample. You'd have refused otherwise, and I couldn't risk what a refusal would tell me." She said it without apology, the way one states the weather. "Your blood didn't just metabolize the greyvein trace we introduced. It *welcomed* it. Broke it apart like bread. Master Underhill, ordinary immunity doesn't do that. Built tolerance doesn't do that. What your blood does is older than tolerance. It's covenant-work. It's the signature of the old binding, written in the vessel line, and it has been sitting in the Steppe for three generations like a coin lost under a floorboard."

The fire in the hearth popped. Outside, faintly, the market was waking — cart wheels, a fishmonger's cry, the ordinary music of a valley that kept the world out. Quentin thought of his mother's hands, flour-dusted, folding pastry, hiding whatever she was inside the shape of a baker's wife until the day she died.

"My mother was a hedge-wife," he said carefully. "Charms that don't charm. Everyone knows the Steppe folk are eccentric."

"Everyone knows," Voss agreed. "Eccentricity is the valley's chief export." She leaned forward slightly, and for the first time the light caught her fully, and he saw that her eyes were red-rimmed, as if she had not slept — or as if she had slept badly for years and had simply stopped pretending otherwise. "Here is what happens next, and I would rather you hear it from me than from a herald. The Gift-Hunt reaches the Steppe within the month. Names will be required. Blood will be tested. Your neighbors — the ones you've bought eggs from for twenty years — will be asked what they know about you, and I promise you, Master Underhill, they know *something*. Everyone always knows something. The widow who swears her dead husband visits. The child who lights candles by wanting to. The taster who cannot be poisoned."

"And if they say it?"

"Then you are declared gifted, and the law takes its course." She said it gently, which was somehow worse than saying it cruelly. "The law's course is a pyre, Master Underhill. I have attended many. I attend them all. I can tell you that the young ones go quietest, and that this is not mercy, only shock."

The tea sat in his stomach, harmless, and he hated it — hated the safety of it, hated that the proof of his survival was the proof of his condemnation. "You're offering me a choice."

"I'm offering you a bargain." She drew a folded paper from her coat and laid it on the table between them, but did not push it toward him. "Fugitive mages are crossing the borderlands. The Hunt catches the slow ones — children, crones, the ones whose gifts flicker visibly. The dangerous ones slip through, because the dangerous ones can feel a witch-finder coming the way you feel weather in a bad knee. We cannot approach them. But they will approach *you*." She tapped the paper once, lightly. "Covenant-blood. To the old believers, you're not a man, you're a relic. They need a vessel to reforge their broken pact, and there is exactly one candidate left in the world, and he is sitting in my chair drinking my hemlock. They will come for you, Master Underhill. All I ask is that you be findable."

"Bait," Quentin said.

"A lure," she corrected, and then, with what sounded like honesty: "Bait. You would travel under escort to the border villages where they gather. You would be seen. You would be approached. And when they take you — and they will take you — you would send word. A signal. Anything. And my riders would come, and the realm would be rid of the last free practitioners of the art, cleanly, under law, without another pyre." She paused. "Do this, and your name never enters the registry. Your bloodwork is sealed. As far as the crown knows, Quentin Underhill is an ordinary man with an extraordinary liver, and the Steppe remains what it has always been: a place where nothing magical ever happened."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then tomorrow morning the heralds read the Gift-Hunt rolls in the market square, and your name is on them, and everything follows from that." She spread her hands — those still, instrument-like hands — open on the table. "I want to be clear about what I am not doing. I am not threatening you. I am describing the road. Both roads end somewhere; I am merely telling you where."

Quentin looked at the paper. At the tea. At the woman who had poisoned him to his face and confessed to sampling his blood while he slept, and who spoke of pyres with the weary fluency of a midwife speaking of births.

"Why do you do it?" he asked. "The Hunt. The pyres. Why does a woman who drinks her own hemlock spend her life burning people?"

For a moment — a real moment, a crack in the plaster — something raw surfaced in her face and was mastered. "Because I have seen what happens when the gift goes unbound," she said quietly. "I have stood in the ruins it leaves. Control is not cruelty, Master Underhill. Control is the only mercy that scales." She rose, smoothing her coat, and the crack sealed as if it had never been. "You have until the heralds arrive. Consider the roads."

At the door she paused, her back to him.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the tea was a formality. I knew before you drank it. The greyvein told me. Your blood sang to that poison like a struck bell." She glanced back, and her eyes were tired and certain and, in some buried chamber of them, sorry. "You didn't seal your fate today, Master Underhill. You sealed it the day you were born to the wrong mother in the right valley. The only question left is which road you walk it down."

The door closed behind her. The two gray watchers fell in beside her footsteps, and the market sounds rushed in to fill the room — carts, gulls, a child laughing somewhere near the well, the whole indifferent machinery of a morning that did not know it had just ended.

Quentin sat alone at the center table with two drained cups and the folded paper between them. He did not touch it. He turned his own cup over instead, the way mourners do, and stared into the dregs — the dark leaf-silt settling into shapes that meant nothing, that had never meant anything, that had only ever been tea.

Then he noticed the ink showing through the paper's fold. Thin lines, precise, drawn by a careful hand: a map. Border villages. Roads. And marked at their crossing, in fresh ink not yet dry, a single circle around a name he had never heard before but would carry for the rest of his life —

*Osselin Ford.*

Where, three days hence, the fugitives would come for him.
