## 6. Beyond the Edge

They came for him at dawn, when the valley fog still lay over the Glass Steppe like a sheet drawn up to the chin.

Quentin had expected chains. He had expected Serane Voss's polite, terrible smile and a wagon with barred windows. Instead there were four witch-finders in grey wool, a plain cart, and Voss herself standing at his door with two cups of tea steaming in her hands — one in each, identical down to the chipped glaze.

"Choose," she said.

He chose the left. She watched him drink all of it, her pale eyes never leaving his throat, and when he set the empty cup down she nodded once, as if he'd confirmed something she'd already written in her ledger.

"No chains," she said. "The king's bait shouldn't look like a prisoner. Mages spook."

"I'm not bait," Quentin said. "I'm a taster. There's a difference. Bait gets thrown back."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth — not quite amusement, but its cousin. "Get your coat. We cross the boundary stones by noon."

He took almost nothing. A spare shirt, his mother's letter — unread these twenty years, carried from drawer to drawer like a stone too heavy to throw away — and the small iron spoon he'd been given at nine years old, the first tool of his trade. He stood in the doorway of his rooms for a long moment before leaving. The rooms were nothing. One bed, one table, one window looking out on the taster's courtyard where he'd learned to smile while swallowing ruin. But they were *his* nothing, and every wall of them was built inside the valley, where magic could not follow.

"You'll come back," Voss said. It was not a question, and it was not a promise. It was arithmetic. "Bait always comes back. That's how you know it worked."

* * *

The road east climbed through orchards gone wild at their edges, past fields where the barley grew in obedient rows because that was what barley did here, without help, without charms, without anything but rain and stubbornness. Quentin rode in the cart's bed between two silent witch-finders and watched his homeland shrink behind him with the particular ache of a man watching his own funeral procession from an upstairs window.

He knew the Steppe the way a blind man knows a room — by touch, by habit, by the map of it pressed into his feet. He knew which cobbles in the market square rocked under weight. He knew that Marta Two-Rivers watered her onions with her tears some years and no one said anything about it. He knew the smell of the mill at dusk, warm grain and cold stone. None of it was beautiful. All of it was safe, and safety, he was only now beginning to understand, has its own smell: dust and iron and nothing else.

"The boundary stones," the younger witch-finder said, when the orchards ended. His name was Pell. He had the nervous competence of a man who had signed on for a clerkship and received a war. "You'll want to brace yourself. Some folk are sick."

"Some folk?"

"People who've got... ancestry." Pell said the word the way you'd hand back something broken. "Old blood. It doesn't like the stones."

*Ancestry.* Quentin looked at his own hands, resting quiet on his knees. Hands that had held every poison the crown could brew and asked for seconds. Hands his mother had held in the night when he was small, humming songs in no language he'd ever learned, songs she'd stopped singing the year he turned six, the year she'd grown afraid of her own mouth.

The cart crested a long rise, and there they were: the boundary stones.

There were seven of them, arranged in a shallow arc across the width of the pass, each taller than a man, each worn smooth as river glass though no river had run here in anyone's memory. They were not carved. They were *grown*, or perhaps they had simply decided, sometime in the deep past, to stop being anything else and become this. No lichen touched them. No bird perched. Snow did not lie on them even in the hard winters, Quentin realized, though snow lay everywhere else, thick and undisturbed around their bases like a congregation keeping its distance.

Beyond them, the land changed. Not dramatically — the same hills rolled on, the same grey sky leaned down — but the light was different. Warmer. More *interested*. The grass beyond the stones bent in a wind that did not reach the valley side, and Quentin could see it plainly: a line drawn through the world, wind on one side and stillness on the other, bright color bleeding across a border of pale dust.

His whole life, he had lived on the wrong side of that line without knowing it was a line. He had thought the world was simply the way the valley was: muted, quiet, safe as a locked room. Now he saw the lock. And seeing it, he understood that everything he had ever called home was a cell someone had built around him, lovingly, at terrible cost, before he was born.

"Down," Voss said. "Walk from here. The stones don't like wheels."

* * *

They crossed on foot.

Voss went first, unhurried, her boots crunching the frost-brittle grass. The witch-finders flanked Quentin — not touching him, but close, the way men stand near a horse they don't fully trust. Pell had gone grey around the mouth.

Quentin stopped three steps from the nearest stone.

Up close it was worse. The stone's surface wasn't smooth; it was full — dense with faint movement, like smoke frozen mid-turn, or like the skin of a sleeping animal. When he leaned toward it, something leaned back. He felt it against his face before he heard it against his ears: a pressure, a *regard*, patient and vast and old beyond any word he owned.

"It knows me," he said. His voice came out smaller than he intended.

"Everything old knows everything," Voss said. She didn't turn around. "That's why we put up walls. Walk."

He walked.

Crossing the line was like stepping through the surface of a bell.

There was no sound — or rather, the sound arrived a heartbeat late, after the feeling, so that the world rang *through* him before it reached his ears. A hum started in his teeth. He felt it in his molars first, then in the fine bones behind his eyes, then down the ladder of his spine, vertebra by vertebra, as though someone were tuning him. His hands flew to the cart rail — he'd turned back toward it without deciding to — and the wood hummed too. Everything hummed. The grass, the frost, the distant hills, the air itself: the whole world outside the valley was a single sustained note, and he had spent thirty years living in the silence between it, calling the silence *peace*.

"Colors," he whispered. "Why are the—"

The green of the grass hit him like a blow. Not green the way he knew green — the Steppe's honest, dusty olive — but green the way fire is hot: total, insistent, *alive*. Beyond the stones the world was saturated, gilded, every hedgerow burning with a richness that seemed to be looking at him. A crow lifted from a fencepost and its wingbeats left brief gold trails in the air, and no one else on the road appeared to see them, and Quentin understood with a lurch that the trails were not in the air at all. They were in him. Something in him had woken up and opened its eyes, and now it was showing him the world as it truly was, and it would not close its eyes again.

His knees went. Pell caught his arm — and snatched the arm back as if burned, staring at his own palm.

"He's *hot*," Pell said. "Witch-Finder, he's—"

"I can see that." Voss had turned. She looked at Quentin kneeling in the frost with gold still fading off his shoulders, and her expression was the worst thing he'd seen that morning: not triumph. Pity. Pity, filed away. "Breathe," she told him. "It passes. Mostly."

"What *is* it?" He heard his own voice as if from far away, thin and ringing. "What's happening to me?"

"You're hearing the world," Voss said. "People like you hear it louder than most. That's all magic is, Underhill. Volume. The Gifted just can't turn it down." She crouched to his level, and for a moment, absurdly, she looked almost kind. "Your valley turns it down for you. Has your whole life, I'd guess. That's what the covenant-blood does inside a null-land — sleeps. Out here—" she gestured at him, at the gold, at his trembling hands, "—it wakes up hungry."

*Covenant-blood.* She said it like a diagnosis. Like a sentence.

"My mother," Quentin said. The words came out of him sideways, unbidden. "She brought me over the stones. Before I was born. She came *in*."

Something moved behind Voss's pale eyes — recalculation, fast and cold. "Did she," she said.

And Quentin, kneeling in wet grass with the world ringing in his teeth, understood that he had just made a mistake. He had handed the Witch-Finder a nameless woman, a crossing, a bloodline — handed it to her the way a man hands a knife to a stranger handle-first, meaning no harm, and watches her take it anyway.

* * *

They made camp that night in the ruins of a tollhouse, three miles past the stones, because Voss wanted him seasoned slowly. *A fish kept in river water fights harder on the hook,* she said to Pell, not quietly enough, and Quentin lay on a pallet in the corner pretending to sleep and hating her with a clean, clarifying hatred.

He could not sleep. Of course he could not sleep.

The world was too loud. Not with sound — with *presence*. Lying still, eyes shut, he could feel the tollhouse around him: the old grief soaked into its burnt beams, the patience of the stones in its walls, the small stubborn life of moss working at the north sill. Twenty years of dead fires still murmured their last conversations. The hill behind the ruin dreamed in long slow thoughts of roots and rain. And threaded through all of it, faint but unmistakable, came something else — something that made him sit up in the dark with his heart slamming.

Music. Or the memory of music. Far to the south and east, past the black shoulder of the hills, something was being sung, and the song had edges of power on it like a blade has an edge of light, and it was frightened. Whoever sang was hiding while they sang. Hiding and singing anyway, because the song mattered more than the fear.

*Fugitives,* he thought. *Mages. Running from her.* He glanced at the corner where Voss slept sitting upright, sword across her knees, sleeping the way cats do — wholly, instantly, ready. Somewhere out in that loud, bright, terrible darkness were people whose only crime was volume, and he had been carried out of his silent valley to be the cheese in their trap.

He lay back down.

His mother's letter pressed against his ribs where he'd stowed it inside his coat. He did not take it out. Not yet. Reading it here — on the wrong side of the stones, with his blood awake and singing — would be like reading it in front of a crowd. Whatever she'd written, whatever apology or warning or blessing it held, it belonged to the quiet country where she'd hidden them both. He would read it when he understood why she'd traded everything — her gift, her name, her whole self — for a mute valley and a son who tasted poison for a living.

But lying in the dark, he let himself remember her properly for the first time in years. Not the frightened woman at the end, jumping at kettle-sounds, burning her own letters. The young mother. The one who had carried him over those seven stones with the world ringing in her teeth, knowing exactly what it would cost her, knowing the gift would drain out of her like warmth out of an open door, and had done it anyway. Had walked into silence forever so that her child could grow up deaf to the world's loudness and safe from everyone who hunted it.

*Everything out here is what she died to keep from me.*

The thought should have been bitter. It was — but underneath the bitterness ran something else, something that scared him more: the colors were beautiful. The hum was beautiful. This loud, lawless, luminous world, the one she had bled to bar from his door, fit against some part of him like a key turning, and the door it opened had been locked his entire life, and he had never once suspected there was a room behind it.

He was still lying there, wide-eyed in the dark, when he felt it change.

The hum shifted key. Southward. Toward the hills, toward the frightened song — a tightening, like the world drawing breath. And along with it came another sensation, new and cold: a thread of awareness, delicate as spider silk, settling against his mind. Not Voss. Not the witch-finders' blunt grey attention. This was careful. Curious. *Kind.*

Someone out there in the loud dark had noticed him noticing. Someone had felt a sleeping thing wake inside the taster's blood and was, at this moment, deciding what to do about it.

The thread lingered a moment longer — he had the mad impression of someone pressing a hand flat against a window from the other side — and withdrew.

In the corner, without opening her eyes, Voss said: "Go to sleep, Underhill."

He closed his eyes. Outside, miles away in the hills, the frightened song had stopped.

Or been found.
