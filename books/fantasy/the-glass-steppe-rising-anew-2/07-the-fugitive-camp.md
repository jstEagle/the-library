## 7. The Fugitive Camp

The village of Marrow's Edge had three streets, one well, and the particular exhausted look of a place that had learned to keep its shutters closed.

Quentin stood in its square at dusk with a witch-finder's rope around his wrists — loose enough to slip, tight enough to photograph well, Serane Voss had said, though she'd said *look convincing* rather than *photograph*, because this was not that kind of realm. The rope was theater. The rope was the point. Somewhere in the hills above the village, fugitive mages were watching, and what they were meant to see was a prisoner of the crown: a man the Gift-Hunt had caught and dragged to the border for judgment. A man worth rescuing.

Or so Voss had explained it, in her flat, patient voice, over the campfire two nights ago, while he ate the stew she handed him without being asked whether he wanted it.

"You are the most valuable prisoner in the realm," she'd said. "Act like it."

He hadn't slept since. Not because of the rope.

* * *

Marrow's Edge smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool and, beneath it, something Quentin had no name for — a sweetness like cut grass and hot copper that made his back teeth ache faintly, the way sugar ached a hollow tooth. He'd felt it since they crossed out of the valley. He felt it now, stronger, pooling in the square as the light died, and he understood with a lurch that it was not the village at all. It was them. It was magic, sitting in the dark on the hillsides like weather, waiting.

"Stand there," Voss had instructed him that morning, tapping the flagstones by the well with the toe of her boot. "Look frightened. Look angry. Whichever comes more naturally." Her gray eyes had flicked over his face with clinical interest. "You're better at frightened than you think. You've been practicing your whole life."

He'd wanted to say something cutting. He'd said nothing, because that was the first rule of his life — *never let them see you flinch* — and because she was right, and they both knew it.

Now the square emptied. The villagers had been herded indoors an hour ago; Voss's finders crouched behind doorways and haycarts with their iron nets and their lead-lined satchels, and Quentin stood alone by the well with his bound hands and the last orange light going out of the sky, feeling like a lure on a line.

A moth bumped against his knuckles. He watched it go.

*They'll come,* Voss had said. *They always come for bait. It's their one weakness — they can't stand to leave anyone in a cage.* She'd said it with something almost like respect, which from Serane Voss was more frightening than hatred. *And when they come, we take them, and you'll have bought your pardon with their chains.*

He turned the thought over now, in the blue dusk, and found it did not sit in him the way food sat in him. Food he could judge in a heartbeat — bitter almond under honey, hemlock under wine. This had no clean taste. It tasted like the valley curdling, like neighbors' names written on official parchment, like the cook Marta's face the day they pulled her body out of the kitchen he had survived. He had not chosen that poison. But he was choosing this.

Unless he wasn't. Unless he could stand here and tell himself he had no choice, that the rope was real, that his hands were truly tied —

The sweetness in the air thickened.

It happened between one breath and the next. Every lantern in the square went out at once — not blown out; there was no wind — and the darkness that followed was not empty but *full*, humming, and Quentin's teeth ached so hard he clenched his jaw against it. Colors bloomed behind his eyes that had no business existing: a green like the memory of green, a violet that hummed.

Then hands — many hands, quick and sure — and a voice at his ear, low and female: "Don't fight us. We're taking you off the hook."

The rope fell away. Someone threw a cloak over his head, and the world became wool and motion and the taste of that sweet copper flooding his mouth until he nearly gagged — and then, absurdly, a cup pressed to his lips, someone murmuring *drink, drink, it will soften the crossing*, and Quentin, who had spent thirty years drinking everything the world put in front of him, drank.

* * *

They ran him through the hills for hours. Or they carried him — he lost track. The cloak came off once, briefly, when they paused in a streambed to check their backtrail, and he saw them properly for the first time: eight or nine figures in road-gray clothes, young and old, a boy no older than fourteen whose eyes reflected light like a cat's, a broad woman with frost-white streaks in her hair that moved when she moved, as if a wind followed only her. None of them looked like the monsters from the proclamations. They looked tired. They looked like people who had been running long enough that running had become their posture.

The woman with the moving hair caught him looking and gave him a hard, unreadable stare. "Keep the cloak on," she said. "No offense to you. But your face is known, and mine is worth more than yours."

They went up. The path steepened into switchbacks, then into a scramble over scree, and Quentin's legs burned and his lungs filled with the sweet copper taste until he thought he would be sick, and still the hum in his teeth grew, climbing toward some frequency just below pain. When at last they passed through a curtain of hanging ivy into a hollow ringed by standing stones older than any kingdom, the sound resolved all at once into silence — a silence so complete it rang.

He staggered. Someone steadied his elbow.

"First time outside the null?" a voice asked — dry, amused, older. "Everyone staggers. The world's been talking about you your whole life and you've never heard a word of it. Then suddenly you can hear."

Quentin turned.

She stood by a fire that burned without smoke, and he knew her at once, though he'd never seen her — because everyone knew her, because her face was on every proclamation nailed to every post from the capital to the coast, rendered in crude woodcut: *ODILE THE SCARRED, HEALER, ARCH-TRAITOR, REWARD TEN THOUSAND CROWNS DEAD OR ALIVE.* The woodcut had flattered nothing. The scar ran from her left temple down across her cheek and throat and disappeared into her collar, a seam of pale tissue like a lightning strike healed wrong. Her eyes were brown and level and very, very awake.

"You're the taster," she said. Not a question.

"I'm nobody," Quentin said. His voice came out rougher than he intended.

Odile smiled, and it transformed her face the way a struck flint transforms a room. "Nobody. Yes. That's exactly what I'd expect a covenant-vessel to say."

The word went through him like a fishhook.

Around the fire, the others had gone quiet. The boy with the cat eyes stared. The woman with the moving hair muttered something that might have been a prayer or a curse. And Odile crossed the hollow toward him with the unhurried certainty of a physician approaching a bedside, took his bound wrist — when had they tied it again? They hadn't; he'd done it himself, some animal instinct — and turned his hand palm-up with a healer's impersonal gentleness.

"May I?" she asked, and waited, actually waited, for his nod, before pressing two fingers to the inside of his wrist where the veins ran close to the skin.

Whatever she felt there made her expression change. The amusement drained out of it. What replaced it was older and heavier — grief, maybe, or awe, or the specific exhaustion of a woman who has been carrying a message for twenty years and has just seen the recipient walk through the door.

"Where is your mother buried?" Odile asked quietly.

The question hit him sideways. "What?"

"Your mother. The one who ran. Where did they bury her?"

"How do you—" His heart slammed. "I don't know what you think—"

"Child." She said it without condescension, the way a doctor says *this will hurt*. "I have been reading blood for forty years. Yours is singing. Do you know what I feel when I touch your pulse? I feel an oath. Old, half-spoken, sleeping — but an oath, wound through you like wire through bread dough. There are perhaps four people alive whose blood carries the covenant-line, and three of them are children who don't know it." She released his wrist and stepped back, and looked at him the way one looks at a letter one has waited a lifetime to receive. "You are the unfinished sentence, Quentin Underhill. And I think you've known something was unfinished your whole life, even if you didn't have words for it."

The fire cracked. Somewhere above the hollow, wind moved through pines, and the sound seemed to arrive late, as if the world were thinking before it spoke.

"My mother fled here pregnant," Quentin said. Each word cost him. "That's all I know. She traded her gift for my safety. That's what my father told me before he died. She never talked about before."

"No," Odile said softly. "They rarely do. Running costs more than the runners ever say out loud." She gestured, and the others drifted back to their tasks — dousing torches, setting watches, a whole small machinery of survival that had clearly been rehearsed until it was reflex. "Come. Sit. Eat. You've been bait all day; the least we can do is feed you."

"And after?"

"After," Odile said, settling onto a stone across the fire from him, "we talk about what the crown wants with you, and what you want with yourself. Those may not be the same conversation anymore."

* * *

The stew was venison and wild garlic and something bitter-green he couldn't identify, and Quentin ate it the way he ate everything — slowly, attentively, cataloguing. No almond. No hemlock. No arsenic's ghost-sweetness. Just food, cooked by people who had salted it generously because salt was cheap out here and fear was expensive everywhere.

He realized, halfway through the bowl, that he could not remember the last time he had eaten a meal he hadn't checked.

The boy with the cat eyes sat down beside him without asking. Up close he looked even younger, all elbows and shadow, with a healing burn along his jaw.

"You're really the king's taster?" the boy whispered.

"I was."

"What's it like? Eating poison on purpose?"

Quentin considered the honest answer and, for once, gave it. "Like being a wall that things break against. You get very good at standing still."

The boy nodded solemnly, as if this were profound. "I lit a candle once," he said. "Just a candle. With my mind. In my village, at winter festival, because the wick kept drowning and my little sister was crying." He touched the burn on his jaw, lightly, the way you touch a scar you've stopped being surprised by. "Someone told the finders. My mother packed me a bag before dawn and put me on the milk cart and I never saw her again." He shrugged, terribly casual. "So. Walls that things break against. That sounds better than running."

Quentin had no answer for that. Across the fire, Odile was wrapping a finder's iron manacle — a trophy, or a warning — in cloth, and the woman with the moving hair was teaching two others a hand-sign language, quick silent conversations flickering between fingers. A camp full of people who had each, individually, been put on a cart or out a window or over a wall by someone who loved them.

*I know how that works,* he thought. *My mother wrote the book.*

Sleep took him eventually, against his will, in a bedroll that smelled of pine and other people's journeys. He dreamed of the royal kitchen — Marta laughing, flour on her forearms — and then the kitchen tilted and became the hollow, and the oven became the standing stones, and Odile stood where the head cook stood, saying *you are the unfinished sentence*, and the sentence itself circled overhead like a bird he couldn't make out against the sun.

He woke once, deep in the night, to voices at the fire's edge.

"—the Voss woman won't stop," the frost-haired woman was saying. "She doesn't do stops. She does pauses. We have a day, maybe two, before she works out where the trail bends."

"Then we move at first light," Odile answered. A pause. "And we take him with us."

"He's the crown's creature. He walked into that square wearing their rope."

"He wore it loosely," Odile said. "I noticed. Did you?"

Silence. Then the frost-haired woman, reluctantly: "He checked the stew before he ate it. I saw. Like a beggar counting coins."

"Exactly like that," Odile said. "Go sleep, Maren. I'll take the middle watch."

Quentin lay still in the dark, heart going, and understood two things at once. First: they had watched him closer than Voss's finders ever had. Second: he had checked the stew out of habit, and the habit had just testified on his behalf to people he had been delivered here to destroy.

He should have felt like a spy discovered. Instead, lying in the pine-smelling dark with magic humming gently at the edges of the stones like a neighbor's music through a wall, he felt — for the first time since heralds had unrolled the Gift-Hunt proclamation in the Steppe market — something dangerously close to safe.

He hated how much he wanted to keep it.

* * *

Dawn came gray and cold. Odile woke him with a hand on his shoulder and a tin cup in her other hand.

"Drink," she said. "Willow bark and something of my own. For the ache in your teeth — it gets worse before it settles."

Quentin took the cup. Turned it. Smelled it, out of thirty years of habit — bitter herb, honey, and underneath, unmistakable to a tongue trained like his, a thread of something else. Something that would drop a horse. Something that, in any other mouth, would stop a heart between the second swallow and the tenth.

He looked up. Odile was watching him with those level brown eyes, and there was no apology in them at all.

"It's a test," she said simply. "Not of loyalty. Of blood. If you are what I believe, it will pass through you like rain through sand — and I will know, beyond doubt, what sleeps in your veins. If you are not..." She spread her scarred hands. "Then the crown loses its bait, and I lose a guest, and we both learn something true. I'm sorry. I've buried too many friends to gamble on hope alone."

Around the hollow, the camp had gone quiet. Everyone was watching. The boy with the cat eyes had his fists clenched. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

Voss's tea, Quentin thought. The same trap, cup by cup, in a kinder kitchen. She had offered him the same bargain with colder eyes: drink, and seal your fate; refuse, and seal it differently.

But Voss had held power over him. This woman held a cup, and nothing else, and was asking him to trust her with the only thing he had ever owned outright — his own survival.

His whole life, he had drunk to stay alive. Every sip a calculation, every meal a battle survived. Never once — not at the king's table, not in Voss's parlor, not in thirty years of swallowing the world's malice — had he drunk to *belong*.

Quentin raised the cup to Odile, met her eyes, and drank it down to the dregs.

For a moment, nothing. Then the hum in his teeth rose past pain into something else entirely — a chord, vast and low, like the valley itself clearing its throat after thirty years of silence — and every standing stone in the hollow began, very softly, to glow.

Odile set down her own untouched cup. Her voice, when it came, was barely a whisper, and it was not addressed to him.

"So," she breathed. "The covenant remembers. Then we have no time at all — because if I can feel that from here, so can she."

Far below, at the edge of hearing, down in the gray morning where the village of Marrow's End smoldered with witch-fire, a horn sounded. Once. Twice. The signal for *found*.
