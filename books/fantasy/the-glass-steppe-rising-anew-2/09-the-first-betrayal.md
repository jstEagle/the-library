## 9. The First Betrayal

Quentin learned to read the camp the way he had once learned to read a table: by what was *not* there.

The mages were careless in ways that would have gotten any court servant flogged. Odile's people left their wards half-finished because the wards themselves were arguments — three of them could not agree whether a boundary should be woven from intention or from salt, and so they did both, badly, and the seams showed. Quentin saw them. He said nothing. That silence had become its own kind of work.

He told himself it was caution. A taster's habit: never reveal what you have noticed until you know who is watching you notice. But the truth sat in his chest like an undigested meal. He was cataloguing their weaknesses the way Serane Voss had once catalogued his bloodwork — patiently, professionally, with a notebook somewhere behind his eyes that he refused to open and could not close.

Nine days past the border village. Nine days since hands he hadn't seen pulled him off the road and into the trees, since the scarred healer had pressed two fingers to his wrist and gone very still, as though listening to something beneath his pulse. *Covenant-blood,* she'd said, and the word had landed on him like a sentence already written.

Now he carried water for them. Split wood. Learned names. Marta, who could coax a dying fire back to life with a hummed note and did so apologetically, as if the flame might resent the imposition. The twins, Bren and Sorrel, whose gift was shared between them like a single lung — one could not warm soup without the other feeling the steam. Old Hask, who grew things. Who had grown, in the corner of the camp where no one looked too closely, a row of beans out of season and out of soil, and who watched Quentin eat them with an expression that was almost tenderness.

"You chew like a man expecting to be executed," Hask observed on the eighth evening.

"I chew like a man who's been poisoned professionally for twenty years."

"Ah." The old man nodded as if this explained everything about the capital, which perhaps it did.

Quentin slept in a lean-to near the horses. He woke before dawn each morning, as he always had, and lay still in the grey light doing the arithmetic he could not stop doing: how far to the valley's edge. How far to the capital. How long Voss had known where they were.

Because she knew. He was nearly certain of it. He had left signs without meaning to — broken ferns, a footprint pressed deeper than the others, the particular way he moved through undergrowth that a man raised in a walled garden moves, all elbows and apology. And Voss tracked the way poison worked: slowly, invisibly, arriving only when it was too late to spit it out.

On the ninth morning, he found the proof.

* * *

It was a pigeon. Stupid, ordinary thing, strutting along the stream bank where the horses drank, wearing a leg-band of green-dyed leather that no farmer's bird wore. Court stock. Message stock.

Quentin stood very still and watched it drink, and felt the trap close around him from both directions at once.

If he caught the bird, he was a spy among people who had fed him beans and taught him which roots sweetened tea. If he let it go, whatever it carried would reach Voss, and Voss would reach them, and the twins with their one shared lung would burn or hang or be marched to a pyre like the child he'd seen on the purge road — the small body, the candle-flame guttering out of a thought that had never learned to be afraid.

He caught the pigeon. It barely struggled. He envied it that.

The message tube held a strip of paper in a cipher he recognized — not because he could read it, but because he had watched clerks carry it through palace corridors, the particular tightness of paper folded twice and sealed with plain wax. Unofficial. Deniable. Voss's hand even at this distance.

He couldn't read it. But he didn't need to. He knew what a raiding order looked like from the shape of the silence around it. Somewhere in these hills, witch-finders were already moving, and the camp's crooked wards — intention *and* salt, argued into uselessness — would hold them for exactly as long as it took to walk around.

He should burn the paper. Warn Odile. Stand up at their fire and say what he was: the crown's creature, the crown's bait, the reason every one of them had been findable at all.

Instead he sat on the stream bank with a dead messenger pigeon in his hands and discovered that he was, at bottom, a coward with excellent manners.

* * *

Odile found him there an hour later. She had a way of arriving without sound that Quentin suspected was not magic at all but the habit of someone who had spent years being hunted — a body trained to apologize for existing.

"You've been sitting here since before light," she said. "Hask says you didn't come for breakfast."

"Hask worries."

"Hask grows beans out of spite. He doesn't worry." She lowered herself onto the rock beside him, joints cracking, and looked at the stream. "You've got bird feathers on your sleeve."

Quentin looked down. Green-dyed leather showed at his cuff where the band had snagged. He had hidden the carcass under a stone like a child hiding a broken cup, and it had marked him anyway. Everything marked him. Twenty years of swallowing other people's poisons and he had never learned to swallow his own guilt quietly.

"Say it," Odile said.

"If I say it, you'll kill me."

"Probably not. I'm a healer. Killing's bad for business." Her voice was dry, but her eyes weren't. "But say it anyway. Things said out loud can be survived. Things swallowed—" she tapped his sternum, gently, right over the place where twenty years of antidotes and doses lived banked in his blood like coins in a miser's vault, "—things swallowed are what's killing you already. I've told you that."

She had. His immunity was a debt, she'd said, and debts came due. Every dose his body had conquered and stored was a soldier garrisoned in his veins, and one day they would mutiny.

"The bird was court stock," Quentin said. "Message tube. Ciphered. I couldn't read it, but I know the fold. It's how the palace sends things it wants to deny later." He made himself look at her. "Voss knows where we are. Or she will within a day. Maybe less."

Odile didn't move. Around them the camp went on waking — Marta humming a fire back to life, the smell of porridge, Sorrel laughing at something her brother had done with the water bucket. Ordinary sounds. Sounds that ended.

"How long have you known?" Odile asked.

"That she'd follow? Since the village. That she'd found us?" He exhaled. "Since last night. I heard horses on the ridge trail. Two, maybe three riders, moving slow. I told myself it was foresters."

"And now?"

"Now I'm telling you the truth, which is more than I've managed since you dragged me out of that ditch."

"We didn't drag you. We rescued you. There's a difference, and the difference is that you keep choosing to stay." She studied him with those healer's eyes that took pulses through skin. "Do you know why I keep you? Not the blood. Plenty of people have covenant traces — thin, diluted, useless. I keep you because when the witch-finder poured you that poisoned tea, you drank it knowing what it was, and your face never changed. That's either courage or a very deep sickness, and I've decided it's both."

"It's mostly sickness."

"Mm." She rose, joints protesting. "Wake the camp. All of it. Then come find me. You're going to show me where you heard those horses."

* * *

They broke camp in ninety minutes, which Odile said was a record and Hask said was a miracle performed by terror. The wards came down last — Marta unpicking them with the same apologetic hum, the air shivering where the boundaries had been, and Quentin felt it go like a blanket pulled off him in winter. Without the wards, the world was louder. Brighter. Magic leaked everywhere out here beyond the Steppe, and his blood drank it greedily and gave nothing back, and some mornings his teeth ached with it like a coming storm.

They moved north through the hills toward the old covenant-stone road, and Quentin rode at the rear and did the arithmetic again, and hated himself for it, and kept doing it.

Here was the problem, laid out as plainly as a dish he'd been ordered to taste: Voss would not be riding alone. Witch-finders traveled with supply trains — wagons of chains and cold-iron manacles, of the ash-salts they used to smother workings, of lamp-oil and hardtack and the ledgers in which they recorded names. Names first. Always names first. The burning was administrative afterward.

A supply train was slow. A supply train was flammable. A supply train, Quentin thought, watching the ridgeline where he'd heard the horses, was the one part of the machine he could actually reach.

And here was the other problem, the one that sat in his stomach like a swallowed coal: if he warned them fully — if he rode ahead and found the train and burned it and came back with smoke on his hands — then there was no version of this where he ever went home. No returning to the king's table, no pleading loyalty, no clever tasting of wines. Arson against the crown's own witch-finders was not a crime you survived by being useful. It was a door closing behind him with a sound he would hear for the rest of his life, however long Voss allowed that to be.

His mother had run from this exact choice. Fled pregnant into a valley where magic couldn't follow, traded her gift for his safety, died with an unopened letter in her trunk. Running was the family trade. He could feel its pull now, gentle as gravity: slip away tonight, alone, before anything happened, and let the mages take their chances. Let history stay tidy. Let his hands stay clean.

His hands had never been clean. They had simply been *employed.*

"Quentin." Odile reined in beside him. Ahead, the column had stopped at a rise; below, half-hidden in a fold of the hills, lay a road. And on the road, parked in the lazy confidence of men who believed themselves the hunters: six wagons. Canvas tops. Draft horses grazing picketed. A dozen men around a cookfire, and among them, unmistakable even at distance, the grey-and-scarlet of witch-finder livery.

"There," Odile said quietly. "Your horses on the ridge trail. Their outriders, I'd guess, marking our line of march." She turned to him. "You know their ways. You served them. Tell me what I'm looking at."

"Water barrels under the second wagon," Quentin said, before he could stop himself, the knowledge pouring out with the terrible fluency of long service. "Lamp oil in the fifth — they carry it for burning seized texts. Ash-salts in the third, which means don't let that one catch, the smoke will drop a grown man in ten breaths. The ledgers ride with the commander's wagon, first or last, wherever he sleeps." He stopped. Swallowed. "I shouldn't know all that."

"No," Odile agreed. "But you do. So." She looked at him, and behind her the whole ragged camp had gone quiet, every face turned his way — Marta with her fire-humming throat, the twins pressed shoulder to shoulder, old Hask holding his bean-stalks like a bouquet. "What does the crown's taster advise?"

Every eye on him. The moment balanced like a cup on a tray, and Quentin understood that whatever he said next would be tasted by everyone, and would poison or nourish accordingly, and there was no spitting it out.

"Burn the wagons," he said. "Not the men. Take the ledgers if we can, scatter the rest. No ash-salt smoke, no deaths — I won't hand Voss martyrs, and I won't make killers of you. Fire, confusion, and gone before they regroup. They'll lose a week resupplying. A week is enough to reach the stone road." He heard his own voice steadying, and marveled at it. "That's my advice. As a servant of the crown." A breath. "Given against the crown."

Nobody cheered. He'd half expected cheering. Instead Hask nodded slowly, the way farmers nod at weather, and Marta reached over and squeezed his wrist once, briefly, like sealing a letter.

Odile smiled with the scarred side of her mouth. "Then let's commit some arson."

* * *

They went down in the blue hour before dawn, when the sentries' fires burned lowest and the draft horses dozed standing. Quentin led, because he knew the layout of a witch-finder camp the way he knew the layout of a banquet — where the authority slept, where the tools were stored, which shadows were patrolled and which were merely dark.

Marta came with him, and Bren, and Odile herself, moving through the grass like a debt coming due.

The plan was simple enough to survive contact with fear: Marta would wake the lamp-oil wagon's cargo with a whispered note — no flint, no spark, nothing a sentry could trace to human hands — while Quentin cut the picket lines to stampede the horses and Bren, with Sorrel's help from the ridge, would pull heat out of the cookfire coals and push it into the canvas of the commander's wagon, slow and deep, so the thing would ignite from inside like a fever.

It went wrong immediately, the way plans do, and then went right in a way no plan deserved.

The horses bolted at Quentin's knife-work, thundering through the camp, and shouting erupted, and a sentry came around the water wagon with a crossbow and found himself looking at the crown's own food-taster crouched in the grass with a knife in his hand. Recognition crossed the man's face — Quentin had sampled at his cousin's wedding feast, once; the realm was small that way — and in that half-second of disbelief, Marta hummed.

The lamp-oil wagon went up with a sound like the world clearing its throat. Heat rolled over Quentin's back, and the night turned orange, and the sentry's crossbow clattered forgotten as he turned to scream for buckets. From the ridge, guided by his brother's touch across a mile of darkness, Bren breathed out, and the commander's wagon bloomed into flame from within, canvas peeling back like petals, and Quentin saw — tumbling, scattering, catching fire and rising — the pages. Ledgers burning. Names burning. Years of careful, deniable record-keeping ascending as sparks.

He stood in the middle of it with his heart hammering and something else hammering harder, something that felt disgracefully like joy.

*Fire answers me,* some animal part of him thought, before he corrected it: *no. Fire answers Marta. You just finally picked a side, and picking a side feels like this.* Like the first sip of wine after a day of tasting only fear. Like exhaling a breath held for twenty years.

They ran. Behind them the witch-finders formed a bucket line around the ash-salt wagon — good discipline, Voss's discipline, even leaderless — and no one pursued, because no one leaves a burning supply train, and by the time the sun rose Quentin was lying in wet grass on the ridge with his lungs full of smoke and laughter he didn't trust, watching the glow fade below.

Odile dropped down beside him. Her face was smudged black except where tears had cut clean tracks; she might have been laughing too, or mourning, or both.

"Well," she said. "The taster has opinions."

"He always did." Quentin stared at his hands. In the growing light they looked like anyone's hands. "I keep waiting to feel guilty."

"Do you?"

He considered it honestly, the way he considered every dish. "I feel guilty that I don't yet. Ask me tomorrow."

"Tomorrow," Odile said, "you'll feel something worse." She nodded east, where the sky was paling, and Quentin followed her gaze and felt the laughter die in his chest.

Smoke on the horizon. Not theirs — theirs was behind them, fading. This was ahead, north, along the covenant road: a broad, greasy column of it, too wide for a house-fire, too steady for an accident. Village-sized. And beneath the wind, faint but unmistakable, carried on the morning air the sound that had followed them down the whole purge road.

Bells. Ringing fast. The way bells ring for executions.

Odile was already on her feet. "Get the others," she said, and the scarred side of her face had gone grim as iron. "Whatever's burning up that road — we're not going around it."
