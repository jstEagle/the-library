## 10. Immunity's Price

The healer's tent smelled of crushed yarrow and woodsmoke and something older underneath — a mineral tang, like rain on hot stone. Quentin sat on a low stool with his sleeves rolled to the elbow while Odile knelt before him, her scarred hands hovering an inch above his forearm without touching it.

"Close your eyes," she said.

"I'd rather not."

"I know you'd rather not." Her voice was dry as seed husks. "Do it anyway."

He closed them. The dark behind his lids filled at once with color — not the black of ordinary blindness but a slow churn of green and gold, threads of light moving through the tent walls as though the canvas were water. He had been living inside this brightness for three days now, ever since they carried him past the boundary stones, and he still hadn't decided whether it was beautiful or unbearable. Both, probably. Most true things were both.

Her fingers touched his wrist. Cool. Steady. He felt something move beneath his skin in answer — a faint stirring, like fish rising toward bread thrown on a pond.

"Hm," said Odile.

"That doesn't sound like a good hm."

"It isn't a bad hm." She released his wrist and took up his hand instead, turning it palm-up, studying the lines as though reading a ledger. "It's an honest one. Hold still."

She pressed two fingers into the soft flesh below his thumb, harder than seemed necessary, and held them there. Quentin counted his own heartbeats. At seven, the pain arrived — deep and wrong, a bruise blooming where no blow had landed, spreading up through his arm into his shoulder, his chest, settling behind his sternum like a stone dropped into a well.

He flinched. He hated that he flinched. Twenty years of never letting them see you flinch, and here he was, wincing before a woman who smelled of goats and poultices.

"Open your eyes now."

He did. The tent was dimmer than he remembered; the firelight had gone thin and gray at its edges. Odile was watching him with an expression he recognized from mirrors — the careful blankness of someone deciding how much truth a person can carry.

"How long," she said, "have you been drinking poison?"

"Since I was nine years old. Formally. My mother started me earlier, on tinctures, when I was small enough that she could pretend it was medicine." He flexed his aching hand. "Why?"

"And how many kinds?"

"I stopped counting at forty. There are more. Some I only tasted once and never learned the names of."

Odile rose, joints cracking, and crossed to the table where her instruments lay arranged with surgical neatness: glass vials, copper needles, a mortar stained green, a bowl of water so clear it looked cut rather than poured. She lifted the bowl and set it in his lap.

"Dip your fingers. All five. Left hand."

The water was cold. For a moment nothing happened, and he almost laughed with relief — and then the surface shivered, and color began to bleed from his fingertips in slow ribbons. Not blood. Something else. Amber first, then a sickly yellow-green, then a thread of blue so dark it was nearly black, each hue unwinding from his skin and curling through the bowl like smoke underwater, layer upon layer, until the water looked like a storm seen from very high above.

"What is that?" His voice came out smaller than he intended.

"You," Odile said. "Everything you've swallowed and survived. Every debt you've banked." She crouched beside the bowl, chin on her knee, and studied the layers the way a moneylender studies a ledger gone wrong. "Amber is arsenic, or near kin to it. The green is hemlock family — several cousins, by the look. That black thread at the bottom is old. Very old. What did they feed you as a child?"

"I don't know. Mother called it 'the bitter one.' She made me take it every full moon until I turned twelve, and then she cried for a day and never gave it again."

"She was trying to build you a wall," Odile said quietly. "Layer by layer. A wall against the world." She looked up at him, and the firelight caught the scar that ran from her temple down across her jaw, pulling the corner of her mouth into what might have been a smile on another face. "Walls have a cost, Quentin Underhill. Did no one ever tell you that?"

* * *

They talked until the candle burned to a stub. Or rather, she talked and he listened, because everything she said required listening twice.

"The body is a ledger," she told him, pacing the narrow tent with the restless energy of a woman who had spent too many years sitting with the dying. "Every poison you survive is written into you. Your blood learns it, holds it, files it away. That's what immunity is — not strength. Memory. Your body remembers forty poisons the way a soldier remembers forty battles, and it carries them all, always, the way a soldier carries his dead."

"And the cost?"

"The cost is interest." She stopped pacing. "A body can hold a little poison the way a purse can hold a little coin. But yours isn't a little. You've spent twenty years making deposits. And the ledger—" she tapped his breastbone, gently, right over the place where the ache lived, "—is coming due."

"That's nonsense. I feel fine."

"You felt fine this morning. How do you feel now?"

He opened his mouth to say *fine* again and found, honestly searching himself, that he couldn't. The stone behind his sternum was still there. So was a new thing: a faint tremor in his left hand that he had blamed on cold, and a headache that had lived behind his eyes since noon, and a tiredness deeper than sleep could reach, the kind that felt less like fatigue and more like erosion.

"I feel," he said slowly, "like a man walking on ice that has been creaking for years."

"Now you're learning to listen to yourself. Good. That's the first lesson of healing, and the hardest." Odile sat down across from him, knees nearly touching his. "Here is the arithmetic, taster, and I won't soften it, because softening arithmetic kills people. Your body holds perhaps ten years of what you've already taken. Ten years, maybe twelve if you're stubborn, and you strike me as stubborn. After that, the deposits come due all at once. Your liver fails. Your blood forgets how to be blood. You will die of everything you ever survived, all in the same week, and it will hurt."

Quentin stared at her. Outside the tent, someone was laughing around a fire — one of the mages, one of the fugitives he had been sent to betray, laughing at some joke in a language he didn't speak. The sound seemed to come from another world.

"I always thought," he said at last, "that I was winning. Every meal. Every cup. I thought I was beating them."

"You were borrowing."

"Borrowing what?"

"Time. From yourself." She took his hand again, the wounded one, and turned it over. "Your mother built you a wall so the world couldn't touch you. But a wall keeps things in as well as out. Everything they ever tried to kill you with is still inside you, taster. Sleeping. Waiting. And walls, given time, fall."

* * *

He walked out of the tent afterward because he couldn't bear to sit still. Night had come down over the fugitive camp, and the valley beyond the ridge glowed faintly with the last violet light, and everywhere — everywhere — magic hummed. He felt it in his teeth. In the fillings of his old wounds. It pressed against his skin like warm water, and he understood now why his mother had fled into the Steppe pregnant and silent: living outside the valley was like standing in a river. You forgot, eventually, that you were wet. But you never stopped being moved.

Odile found him at the camp's edge, where the ground fell away toward the dark tree line. She handed him a cup of willow-bark tea without ceremony, and he drank it, and only realized halfway through what he had done — drunk, unexamined, from a stranger's hand — and laughed, a short startled sound.

"Old habits," he said.

"New ones," she corrected. "You're among friends. Try to let it be strange for a while."

They stood in silence. Then he asked the question he had been carrying since the tent, the one that had been growing heavier with every step.

"The Rite of Return. The covenant. You said my blood could reforge it. You said I'm the unfinished sentence." He turned to face her. "Can a body like mine do it? A body full of — of debts?"

Odile did not answer immediately. She looked out at the dark instead, and when she spoke, her voice had changed — lower, rougher, the voice of a woman who had buried people.

"The Rite requires surrender. Not resistance — surrender. The vessel drinks the covenant-draught and must welcome it, open every door in the body and let it walk through, let it burn out everything false and leave only the oath. It is the hardest thing a mortal can do, and mortals do it, once a generation, because they must." She finally looked at him. "But your doors are barricaded, Quentin. Twenty years of poison, stacked behind them like furniture against a raid. When the draught comes, it will find a fortress, not a man. And it will break itself against those walls, or break you trying to get in. No half-poisoned body has ever survived the Rite. I don't believe one ever will."

The wind moved through the trees below, and somewhere far off a nightbird called, and Quentin stood very still with the tea cooling in his hands.

"So that's it," he said. "I'm poisoned either way. Slowly by the crown, quickly by the covenant. You're telling me I'm already dead — the only question is which death I choose."

"I'm telling you," Odile said, "that there may be a third road. A way to pay the debt before the Rite — to draw the poison out, dose by dose, month by month, a year of purging, maybe two. Painful. Dangerous. It has been done perhaps four times in the history of the gift, and two of those four died doing it." She reached out and gripped his shoulder, her scarred hand surprisingly strong. "And we don't have a year. Voss is regrouping. The king's writ travels faster than any of us. If the Hunt reaches the covenant-stone before we do—"

"Then I drink the draught with a full ledger and die in front of the realm."

"Or we find another way. I didn't spend thirty years learning the body's arithmetic to accept its sums." Her mouth twisted into that almost-smile again. "There is a place east of here. Hot springs, old ones, older than the covenant — the water there remembers how to pull poison from bone. My teacher used it. I used it, after—" she touched her scar, briefly, and did not finish the sentence. "It's eight days' travel. Through country where the Hunt has already been."

Quentin looked down at his cup, at the tea he had drunk without fear for the first time in twenty years, and felt the strange grief of it — that safety, real safety, had lasted three days before the arithmetic came due.

"Eight days," he said. "Through burned country. With witch-finders on our trail and a body full of borrowed time."

"Yes."

He drained the cup and set it on the flat stone beside him, deliberately, the way a man sets down a weight he intends to pick up again.

"Then we'd better leave at dawn," Quentin said. "Because I've just realized something, Odile, and I think you should hear it too. Serane Voss read my bloodwork months ago. She knows what I am better than I do. Which means she knows about the debt." He met the healer's eyes. "She isn't hunting us. She's waiting. She knows exactly how long I have — and she means to collect me when I'm too weak to run."
