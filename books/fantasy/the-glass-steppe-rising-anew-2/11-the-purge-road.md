## 11. The Purge Road

They traveled by night for the first three days, and by the fourth day they stopped pretending it mattered.

The Hunt did not need daylight to find what it wanted. It needed only smoke, and there was smoke enough to steer by — thin gray threads rising from the eastern hills at dusk, a black column standing over the river valley like a cypress planted by some patient gardener of ruin. Odile read the horizon the way Quentin had once read plates: not with fear but with a taster's discrimination, cataloguing what each plume was made of. Thatch, she'd say, and move on. Grain stores. A chapel roof. Once, only once, she went quiet for a long moment before naming it, and that silence told them all it had been people.

Quentin rode a borrowed mule named Sorrel who had opinions about everything except the road ahead, which suited him. Behind him came the others — Odile on her scarred gray mare, the twins Pellam and Wren sharing one horse because neither trusted the other to ride alone, old Master Corvin wrapped in three cloaks against a cold he insisted was imaginary, and the boy Tam, fourteen, who had been a shepherd's son until the Gift-Hunt made him an orphan and a fugitive in the same afternoon. Tam had a gift for finding water. It seemed a small thing, a kind thing even, until you watched him flinch every time someone struck flint.

They were nine days out from the burned camp, six from the wagons Quentin had set alight, and the covenant-stone lay somewhere ahead of them beneath the Steppe's heart — a journey Odile measured not in miles but in how much of the realm they would have to cross while it was actively trying to kill them.

"The purge roads," Corvin called them, and spat, which for a man of his age and dignity was practically liturgy.

* * *

The first village was called Harrow's End, though no sign said so anymore; the sign had been used as kindling along with everything else the fire could reach. They walked their horses through it at midmorning because Odile refused to hide — "We look away once and we deserve everything that follows" — and Quentin understood, walking those streets, why she made the rule.

Fire is honest in a way men are not. It showed exactly where it had been fed. The granary first, then the smithy, then the row of cottages along the stream where the windows had blown outward and hung like torn eyelids. Someone had chalked the sigil of the witch-finders on the wellhead — a circle bisected by a blade — and beneath it, in a different hand, shakier, someone else had written *they were only herbs*. Only herbs. A hedge-witch's stock, probably. Feverfew and comfrey and something for a colicky baby. Quentin stood looking at those words a long time, until Wren touched his elbow.

"There'll be worse," she said. Not unkindly. Wren never managed unkindness; she only managed truth, which was crueler and more useful.

"I know," Quentin said. "I helped build this."

"You built walls," Wren said. "Other people chose to burn things."

He didn't answer, because both things were true and only one of them was his to carry.

In the doorway of the last cottage on the row, a woman sat shelling beans into a basket, moving with the terrible steadiness of someone who has decided that if she stops working she will stop being anything at all. She looked up at the riders, saw the absence of white-and-scarlet livery, and went back to her beans.

"They took Marta's boy," she said, to no one, or to everyone. "Nine years old. He lit a candle. That's all. He was frightened of the dark and he lit a candle without striking it, and his own grandmother — his own *grandmother*, mind — went to the heralds, because she thought they'd only take him for questioning." She shelled another bean. It fell into the basket with a sound like a dropped coin. "Questioning."

Odile dismounted. She gave the woman what was in her saddlebag — willow bark, a jar of honey, the last of their good salt — and asked nothing, which was its own kind of payment. But as they turned to leave, the woman caught Quentin's stirrup, and her eyes when they found his were dry and bottomless.

"You've got the look," she said. "The valley look. You're from inside."

"Yes," Quentin said.

"Then you tell them." Her grip tightened. "You tell whoever needs telling that we were neighbors to magic our whole lives and none of us ever drowned in it. You tell them the wall kept out more than spells. It kept out sense."

She let go. They rode on. Quentin did not look back, because he knew that if he did, he would see her resume shelling beans, and he would carry the image of those steady hands for the rest of his life, and the rest of his life suddenly felt like a thing that might be short.

* * *

The second village still had people in it, which was somehow worse.

Mirefield huddled under a sky the color of dirty wool, half its roofs scorched, the other half conspicuously untouched — spared, Quentin realized, the way a farmer spares seed corn. The survivors had been permitted to remain so that the road could watch them. So that travelers like themselves would see what compliance bought: not safety, only permission to keep breathing near the ashes of your neighbors.

A notice board stood at the crossroads, papered over so many times the layers curled like bark. The topmost proclamation bore the king's seal and Serane Voss's signature beneath it, and Quentin felt the name go through him like a fishhook. He had known her handwriting for less than a season and already his body recognized it before his mind did.

*By order of His Majesty Aldric, Second of His Name: all bearers of the unsanctioned gift shall present themselves for registration within thirty days. Those harboring bearers share their sentence. There is no sanctuary. There is no valley deep enough.*

"No valley deep enough," Corvin repeated sourly. "Poetic. I wonder if he wrote it himself or if some clerk earned a bonus."

"It's Voss's phrasing," Quentin said quietly. "I've read her reports. She writes like someone building a fence — every clause a post, driven straight down."

"And you admire her," Pellam said. It wasn't quite an accusation. With Pellam it never quite was; he simply observed the world aloud and let the world feel accused.

"I understand her," Quentin said. "That's worse, isn't it?"

Pellam considered this with the seriousness he brought to everything, even jokes, especially jokes. "Yes," he concluded. "It is."

They took a room at Mirefield's one surviving inn, because Odile judged that hiding looked guiltier than passing through, and because Tam needed sleep and hot food more than he needed caution. The innkeeper was a broad, silent man with burn scars laddering one forearm, and he served them stew and dark bread and asked no questions, and when Odile pressed a coin into his palm he closed her fingers back over it.

"First hot meal anyone's cooked me since the camp," Odile said afterward, staring into the hearth. "You'd think I'd taste it more."

"Do you?" Quentin asked.

"No," she said. "I taste the ones who aren't here eating it." She looked at him across the firelight, and her scarred face did the thing it did instead of softening — the muscles around her mouth shifted a fraction, the way ice shifts before it decides whether to hold or break. "You do too. Don't think I haven't noticed. You eat like a man taking inventory."

"That's what I am," Quentin said. "It's the only trade I know."

"Then inventory this." She leaned forward. "Tomorrow we pass through Ashcombe ford, and past Ashcombe the purge road runs open country for two days — no cover, no villages worth the name, just the stone markers and whatever the witch-finders leave hanging on them. If we're going to be seen, better to be seen doing something than seen running. I want your eyes tomorrow. Taster's eyes. You can read a plate for poison; read these towns for me. Tell me what's been done to them and how, because how tells us who, and who tells us where Voss is, and where Voss is tells us how much road we have left."

Quentin nodded. And because it was Odile, and because lying to her had begun to feel like lying to a mirror, he added: "There's something else. In Harrow's End. The chalk on the well — *they were only herbs* — that hand was shaking. Whoever wrote it stayed after the soldiers left. Stayed and wrote that. Which means some of these towns have people still in them who aren't afraid, or aren't only afraid. If we're going to be seen anyway—"

"—we might be seen by friends," Odile finished. Something moved behind her eyes, quick and dangerous and almost hopeful. "Careful, taster. Hope's the strongest poison there is. No immunity in the world covers it."

"I know," Quentin said. "I'm counting the dose."

* * *

They reached Ashcombe at noon, and that was where they found the candle.

Later — in the years after, when children asked him how the new covenant began, when the story had worn smooth as river glass — Quentin would say it began at the Rite, before the assembled realm, before the king. But that was the polished version. The true beginning was a village square in Ashcombe, on a gray day, with mud freezing in ruts, and a girl of perhaps eight years old standing on a cart bed with her small hands tied in front of her.

The heralds had come with a portable scaffold, which told Quentin everything about how routine this had become. Two witch-finders in white-and-scarlet stood at ease, spears grounded, faces professionally blank. A crowd of villagers ringed the square at the distance of shame — close enough to watch, far enough to claim they hadn't. And in the center of the square, on a barrel, burned a single candle.

An officer was reading the charges. His voice carried the flat music of a man reciting a shopping list. "*...did, upon the night of the ninth, produce flame by unlawful means, to wit: without flint, steel, or match, in violation of the Edict of Registration, being an unregistered bearer of the unsanctioned gift—*"

"She lit a candle," someone said. It took Quentin a moment to realize the voice was his own.

The officer paused, annoyed, a reader interrupted mid-paragraph. "She confessed," he said. "Willingly. Which is noted in her favor." As if mercy were a line item, a discount applied at sentencing.

Quentin looked at the girl. She was small for eight, or maybe she was seven; fear had compressed her into something the size of her own terror. Her hair was braided with bits of colored wool, the way farm children braided their hair, the way children everywhere marked themselves as belonging to something. Her hands, tied with rope too rough for wrists that young, were chapped red from cold. She wasn't crying. She was looking at the candle on the barrel with an expression Quentin recognized from the inside of his own skull: the look of someone watching the evidence of the only beautiful thing they had ever done become the instrument of their destruction.

She had been frightened of the dark. And she had made light. And the light was going to kill her.

"*...sentence to be carried out forthwith,*" the officer read, "*by the mercy of the crown, by swift and painless means, in view of the community, that all may witness the wages of the unsanctioned gift.*"

Swift and painless. Quentin's taster's mind, that cold professional chamber that had kept him alive for twenty years, supplied the answer before he wanted it: they would use the draught. Bitter almond under something sweet. He knew the recipe. He had tasted its cousins a hundred times at the king's table, and survived them all, and there was a moment — a long, sickening, clarifying moment — when the thought arrived whole and monstrous: *I could drink it for her. I could stand up and say I am the bearer, I am the unregistered gift, give the cup to me, I will swallow her death and walk away from it, and she will live.*

And then the second thought arrived, quieter and colder, wearing Odile's voice: *And then you'd be in irons by dusk, and she'd die tomorrow instead, and so would the eight of us, and the covenant-stone would wait forever for a vessel who died playing hero on a purge road.* 

Tam made a sound beside him, small and strangled, and Odile's hand closed on the boy's shoulder like a shackle and a blessing at once. "Don't," she breathed. "Whatever it costs you, don't. Not yet. Not here. *Not uselessly.*"

On the cart, the officer nodded to a soldier, who brought the cup.

The girl drank it the way children drink medicine — obediently, quickly, wanting it to be over, trusting the adults who told her it was for her own good. For a heartbeat nothing happened, and Quentin's heart rose stupidly on the hope that even now, even here, the world might flinch from what it was doing.

Then she folded, small and complete, like a candle going out.

The crowd made no sound. That was the worst of it — not screaming, not weeping, but the collective held breath of forty people deciding, all at once, to have seen nothing. A mother pulled her own child's face into her coat. An old man studied the sky as if weather were suddenly fascinating. The silence was so total Quentin could hear the candle on the barrel guttering in the wind, still burning, still burning, the one thing in the square that refused to pretend.

Something broke in Quentin Underhill, and something else hardened in the space it left, the way a wound fills not with flesh but with resolve.

He had spent his whole life swallowing. Bitter almond under honey, hemlock under wine, the slow accumulating poisons of his own compromise — *never refuse a plate, never let them see you flinch.* He had called it survival. He had called it pride. Standing in the frozen mud of Ashcombe with a dead child's warmth still rising off the cart, he finally tasted what he had actually been swallowing all these years, and the name of it was complicity, and it was the one poison he had never built any immunity to, and it had nearly finished him.

No more, he thought. Not another plate. Not another cup. Whatever the covenant cost, whatever the Rite took from him — his immunity, his armor, the only self he had ever trusted — he would pay it. He would stand before the realm and drink willingly, and if the stone rejected him, then at least he would die having refused, for once in his life, to swallow what he was served.

"Taster," Odile murmured, watching his face with those healer's eyes that missed nothing. "What are you thinking?"

Quentin tore his gaze from the cart, from the small tied hands, from the candle still burning its stubborn inch of light.

"I'm thinking," he said, and his voice came out level, which surprised him, level and cold and certain, "that I know exactly which cup Serane Voss drinks from. And I'm done letting other people drink mine."

Behind them, unnoticed by any of them, a rider in travel-stained gray had reined in at the edge of the square — and sat there a long moment, watching the taster watch the scaffold, before turning quietly back down the road toward the capital.
