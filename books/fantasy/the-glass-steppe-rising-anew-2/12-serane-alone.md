## 12. Serane Alone

The rain came down on the Purge Road like a judgment nobody had bothered to write out.

Serane Voss stood beneath the eave of a burned tithe-barn and let it fall just short of her boots, watching the water find the channels between the cobbles and carry ash downstream in long gray ribbons. Three days out from the border village where the taster had been taken. Two days since the supply wagons went up in flames at Miller's Ford — green fire, the survivors said, fire that burned wet rope and wouldn't take to flesh, which was nonsense, which was impossible, which was exactly the sort of thing that happened when Quentin Underhill decided where his loyalties lay.

She had known it would come to this. She had poured the tea herself.

"Finder." Corporal Hesk came around the corner of the barn with his hood plastered flat and his face wearing the particular misery of a man who had signed on to hunt witches and discovered that most of the work was weather. "The tracker says they've turned north. Toward the Steppe."

"Toward the stone," Serane said.

"You believe that? That there's anything at the heart of the valley but dirt and turnips?"

"I believe the taster believes it." She turned her collar up. "And I believe Quentin Underhill has never once done something without a reason he could recite like scripture. Go wake the others. We ride within the hour."

Hesk hesitated. He was young enough that hesitation still looked like courage rather than insubordination. "The men are saying things, Finder. About the wagons. About how the fire *chose*. They're saying if a taster can burn crown supplies with a thought, then maybe the Hunt is—"

"The Hunt is what keeps the fire *in* the wagons," Serane said, and heard her own voice come out flat as slate, and knew she had said too much, or not enough, or precisely the wrong amount. "Wake the others."

He went. The rain kept falling. Serane stayed under the eave a while longer, because the barn behind her had been burned two years ago by her own writ — a hedge-healer who'd cured a child's fever and been denounced by the child's grandmother for it — and standing inside would have meant looking at the scorch marks, and she had already looked at enough scorch marks for one life.

*Justice is control.* Her father had said something else once, when she was small. Something about justice being a balance, scales and all that. But her father had died in Ashfall too, along with four hundred other people who had believed balance was enough, and so Serane had amended the definition the way you amend anything that fails: ruthlessly, and alone.

* * *

Her sister's name was Maren, and she had been nine years old when the gift came into her like water into a cracked jug.

Serane had been twelve. She remembered the year by its smallnesses — the way their mother had started sleeping with the shutters latched against nothing; the way the dogs of Hollowmere went silent whenever Maren walked past; the way her sister's shadow, some evenings, did not quite match the position of her body, lagging half a heartbeat behind like a swimmer in shallow water.

It began with lights. Candle flames leaning toward Maren when she laughed. Then the well water rising an inch in its shaft on her birthday, all the village women crossing themselves and saying nothing, because in those days the covenant still held in name and a registered gift was a taxed gift, not a hanged one. But the registry office for their province had closed the winter before — budget cuts, a clerk's stamp, a door locked — and so Maren's gift went unwritten, unbound, growing in the dark the way roots grow under a road, invisible until the surface splits.

Their mother took her to three different priests. The first prayed over her and pronounced her clean. The second charged them a month's wages and pronounced her clean. The third looked at Maren for a long time and then looked at Serane's mother and said, quietly, the way you say things you don't want the child to hear: *It isn't a sickness. It's a tide. And there is no law left in this province to bind a tide.*

No law. That was the phrase that lived in Serane afterward, that grew its own root system through everything she became. Not *no cure*, not *no hope* — no **law**. The machinery that should have caught her sister, measured her, taught her, bounded her, had been allowed to rust, and everyone had shrugged, because the tide was far off and the sea was someone else's problem.

Maren was eleven when the tide came in.

They never established what triggered it. A quarrel, perhaps — Maren had wanted to go to the spring festival and their mother had said no, the way mothers say no, carelessly, certain the world will hold. What the inquest established instead was the sequence: the windows blowing outward in a ring around the Voss house; the well erupting like a geyser; the church bell ringing though no one touched it, ringing until it cracked; and then the fire, which did not behave like fire, which burned downward into the earth and sideways against the wind and which, when it finally guttered out three days later, had taken every building in Hollowmere except the granary, and taken with them two hundred and six souls, among them their mother, among them Maren herself, found at the center of it all with her hands open and her face, the inquest physician wrote, *at peace*.

At peace. Serane had read that word until it stopped meaning anything. Her sister had died at peace, and two hundred people had died screaming, and the difference between those two experiences had been nothing — no skill, no choice, no law. Just pressure finding the weakest wall.

The crown's inquiry lasted a year. Its findings were sealed. Its practical result was the Witch-Finder Corps: funded, staffed, given teeth and writs and a mandate that Serane memorized on the day she swore in, aged nineteen, with her hands shaking and her eyes dry. *To seek, to measure, to bind.* Every gifted person found, registered, trained, held to account — because the alternative was Hollowmere, was Ashfall, was a hundred names on a hundred stones in villages that no longer appeared on maps.

She had spent nineteen years believing it. She still believed it, mostly, the way you believe in a load-bearing wall: not because you love it, but because you have seen what the house looks like when it comes down.

But lately — and she hated this, hated it the way you hate a splinter working toward the bone — lately the arithmetic had begun to trouble her.

* * *

The trouble had a name, and the name was Quentin Underhill, and the trouble had started with a cup of tea.

She had poisoned it openly, in front of him, because that was the test and they both knew it. Wolfsbane and something rarer, something the Guild's apothecaries brewed for exactly this purpose — a compound harmless to ordinary blood, lethal to the gifted, detectable in neither case until it was far too late. She had watched him drink. She had watched him not die. And she had expected triumph, the click of a lock, the satisfaction of a puzzle solved.

What she had felt instead, watching his throat move as he swallowed, was something closer to grief. Because she had seen his hands. Steady, scarred, a working man's hands, a servant's hands — and she had seen, in the way he set the cup down, that he had known exactly what she was doing to him, and had drunk anyway, because refusing was death and drinking was only suspicion, and that calculation — *I will swallow what hurts me because the alternative is worse* — was a calculation Serane recognized from the inside. It was the calculation of someone who had learned early that the world was a table set with poison and the only choices were which cup and how much.

She had offered him the bargain because the bargain was correct procedure. Bait was bait. The Guild had used registered sensitives to draw fugitives for decades; using an unregistered one was merely untidy, not unprecedented. And yet when she'd watched him walk out of her office with the leash around his neck and his spine straight, she had felt less like an officer deploying an asset than like a woman handing a lit lamp to someone standing in a dry field.

Then Miller's Ford. Then the wagons. Green fire that spared flesh. Arson as a signature, mercy as a method — and Serane, reading the reports by lamplight, had understood two things simultaneously. First: Quentin Underhill had chosen sides. Second: he had chosen sides *carefully*, in a way that killed no one, in a way that announced itself, in a way that said *I am here, I am visible, come and find me* — and she could not decide whether that made him the most dangerous fugitive she had ever hunted or the most honest.

Both, probably. Both was usually the answer with people worth hunting.

* * *

They caught up with the fugitives at dusk on the fourth day, where the Purge Road climbed through a pass of black pine toward the valley's rim, and the rain had turned to sleet that ticked against the trees like slow applause.

Serane held the column back at the treeline and looked down into the clearing with her spyglass, and there they were: eight fugitives around a low fire, travel-worn, armed lightly, arranged — she noted this with professional displeasure — in a defensive pattern that suggested one of them had military training. The scarred healer, Odile, sat apart, grinding something in a mortar. Two children slept under a shared cloak. And at the edge of the firelight, apart from all of them, sat Quentin Underhill, turning a cup in his hands and staring north.

Even at this distance, even through glass and sleet, she could see it: he wasn't one of them. He sat among the fugitives the way a hostage sits among captors, or the way a man sits at a table where he has been fed and sheltered and cannot quite bring himself to call it home. She knew that posture. She had worn it for years in the Corps' barracks, surrounded by colleagues, belonging nowhere, performing belonging everywhere.

*You're doing it again,* she told herself. *Reading your own story onto strangers.*

But it wasn't a stranger, was it. That was the whole problem. She had spent hours across a table from Quentin Underhill. She had catalogued his tells the way she catalogued everything: the pause before answers, the habit of touching his left cuff when lying, the way his eyes went to exits. And underneath the tradecraft she had assembled something less orderly — a conviction, unwelcome and persistent, that the man she had blackmailed into service was the only person in either realm who understood what she understood: that poison and power were the same problem, that both were about what a body could survive, and that survival was not the same thing as living.

She gave the order to move at last light, when the camp was stirring and the sleet had thickened, and they came down through the pines quiet as weather.

* * *

It went wrong immediately, in the way things go wrong when the person you're trying to catch wants, on some level, to be found.

There was no ambush. No fight worth the name. The fugitives scattered to the treeline with the drilled efficiency she'd predicted, the healer barking orders, the children swept up and carried — and then Quentin Underhill simply walked out of the smoke of the doused fire into the middle of the road, empty-handed, and stood there, and waited for her.

"Hold," Serane said, and her crossbowmen held, because they were good and because she had told them at dawn, in words that would cost her at her next review: *the taster is mine.*

Up close he looked worse than she remembered. Thinner. There was a new scar at his hairline and a tremor in his left hand that he kept closing into a fist to hide, and his eyes had the glassy brightness of a man running a fever he refuses to name. Poisoned blood, she thought. Banked doses coming due. She had read the old Guild medical folios; she knew what a lifetime of immunity-building actually built, and it wasn't safety. It was debt.

"Witch-Finder." He inclined his head, courteous as a footman. "You're a long way from tea."

"You burned my wagons."

"Your wagons were carrying chains and pyre-oil." His voice didn't rise. "I checked."

"And the men guarding them?"

"Asleep, before I ever struck the flint. You'll find them cold, hungry, and humiliated, but breathing." He paused, and something moved behind his eyes — not defiance; she knew defiance; this was wearier and more dangerous. "I'm telling you this because I want you to know it wasn't carelessness. I chose. Every part of it, I chose."

"So did I," Serane said, "when I put a cup of poison in front of you and watched you drink it," and she saw the words land, saw his jaw tighten, and pressed on because pressing on was the only mode she had ever trusted. "Here is what happens now. My writ covers every soul in that treeline. Eight fugitives, two of them children, all of them bound for registration or the pyre under the Gift-Hunt edict. You can't stop me. You can't outrun me. Your friends in the trees know it, which is why none of them is loosing arrows at my crossbows right now — the healer is smarter than you look, and she knows what a volley does to people carrying children."

"They're not my friends," Quentin said.

"No," Serane agreed. "That's rather the tragedy, isn't it. You sit at their fire like a man at a funeral for a family that adopted him." She stepped closer. Behind her, Hesk shifted nervously; ahead of her, at the treeline, she could see the healer Odile watching with an expression of terrible patience, the way you watch weather you have already decided to walk through. "So here is your final offer, taster, and I will make it once, and I will make it honestly, because whatever else I am, I do not lie across a drawn blade."

She drew it. Slowly, formally, the way the old dueling codes required — steel singing free of the scabbard, point settling toward the mud between them, a line drawn on the ground that both of them could read. Her crossbowmen tensed. Quentin didn't move at all.

"Clemency," she said. "Full pardon, yours, witnessed and sealed. Walk away from them. Walk back down this road with me, register as what you are — a vessel, a sensitive, whatever the clerks decide to call it — and serve the crown openly, under law, with protections, instead of bleeding yourself dry in the dark for people who will spend your blood and forget your name. That is the offer. Refuse it, and I take them, all of them, and I let you run, because I am not in the business of killing messengers, and because—" she stopped. Steadied. Said the true thing, which she had promised herself she wouldn't say, and said it anyway, because two wounded people across a blade owe each other at least that much truth. "Because I have read your file, Quentin Underhill. I know about your mother. I know about the valley. I know you have spent your whole life swallowing what would kill anyone else and calling it survival. And I am asking you — the only time I will ever ask anyone anything — whether you mean to keep calling it that."

The sleet ticked in the pines. Somewhere upslope, a branch shed its load of ice with a soft crash. Quentin looked at the blade, and then at the treeline where the healer waited, and then — last, longest — at Serane herself, and she watched him arrive at the understanding she had been carrying since the tea: that this was not a trap, or not only a trap; that somewhere beneath the writ and the steel there was a girl from Hollowmere who had buried two hundred neighbors and a sister and had spent nineteen years building walls against a tide, and who was now, for one suspended moment, asking permission to be told the walls were wrong.

"You think control is mercy," Quentin said quietly. "I can see it. You look at them and you see your sister."

Serane's breath went out of her like a wound opened. Her point dipped a fraction — a fraction — and steadied.

"Don't," she said.

"She died unbounded. Unregistered, untaught, alone, because the law that should have held her had been left to rot. And so you've decided the answer is more law, harder law, law with teeth." His voice stayed gentle, which was somehow worse than cruelty. "I grew up in a valley with no magic at all, Serane. Walls so high nothing gets in. Do you know what I learned there? That a sanctuary that protects you by keeping the world out isn't sanctuary. It's just a smaller prison with better lighting. I served the crown for twenty years. I swallowed its poisons. I called it loyalty because the alternative was admitting I was afraid. And last week I burned your wagons, and it was the first honest thing I have ever done."

"Then you're a fool," Serane said, and her voice cracked on the second word, and she hated him for hearing it. "You're a fool, and they'll use you, and the covenant you're chasing is a story old women tell, and when it kills you I will write the report and I will write it accurately, and it will say *subject refused lawful clemency*, and no one will ever know that I—"

She stopped.

The blade hung between them. The sleet fell. Behind Quentin, at the treeline, Odile rose slowly to her feet, and the two children peered out from under their cloak, and eight fugitives watched a witch-finder and a taster stand in the ruins of each other's certainties.

"No one will ever know that you what?" Quentin asked softly.

Serane looked at him for a long moment — long enough for the sleet to whiten her shoulders, long enough for Hesk to shift his weight twice, long enough for her to understand that whatever she said next would follow her the rest of her life, whichever life that turned out to be.

"That I hoped you'd refuse," she said.

And she turned the blade aside, point dropping to the mud, and raised her voice to the column and the treeline alike, and gave the order that would damn or save them all:

"Take them. All of them — alive. Bind the healer first." Her eyes found Quentin's over the steel, over the years, over the smoking ruin of everything they had each survived by not feeling it. "As for the taster — let him go. He's had his chance."

Quentin didn't run. That was the worst of it. He stood there while the crossbowmen fanned out toward the trees, stood there while the healer was forced to her knees, stood there with the sleet melting on his shoulders and his fists closed at his sides, and he looked at Serane with an expression she would carry up the mountain and into every report she ever wrote afterward — not hatred, not forgiveness, but recognition, terrible and mutual, the look of one survivor acknowledging another across the wreckage of the only mercy either of them knew how to offer.

"You'll regret the ones you take," he said. "Not the ones you let go. The ones you take."

"Move," Serane said, to no one, to everyone, to herself.

And Quentin Underhill turned north, toward the stone, toward the poison waiting in the heart of the valley, and walked out of her custody and into the storm — leaving Serane Voss alone on the Purge Road with eight prisoners, one drawn blade, and the sudden, vertiginous certainty that she had just made the second-worst decision of her life, and that the worst was still waiting for her at the top of the mountain.
