## 13. Refusal

The road into Marrowfen smelled of wet ash and turnip greens, and Quentin knew before they reached the square that Serane Voss had chosen it deliberately.

Marrowfen was a market town of perhaps four hundred souls, strung along a brown river like beads on a greased cord. Its houses leaned together for comfort. Its people had that particular stillness of places that had already heard the hoofbeats and were waiting to learn whose names were on the lists. And in its square, beneath a scaffold hastily built from barn timber, the witch-finders had arranged their theater with the precision Quentin had come to expect from Voss — everything placed where it would wound most.

The mages knelt in a row. Six of them. Odile at the center, her scarred hands bound in front of her with silvered cord that hissed faintly whenever she flexed her fingers. Beside her, Tam the hedge-healer who had once pulled Quentin's splinters with nothing but spit and patience; the Ferro twins, barely older than children; old Master Corvel, whose gift had been nothing more sinister than knowing when rain would come; and a girl named Wren whom Quentin had only known three days and who laughed like water over stones.

They had been given clean clothes. That was the cruelty of Voss — she dressed her prisoners well, fed them, let them sleep. She wanted the townspeople to see healthy faces on the scaffold, faces like their own, because terror spread fastest when it wore a familiar skin.

Quentin stood between two witch-finders, his wrists bound in ordinary rope. They had not searched him well. He still had the taster's knife in his boot — small, curved, made for opening oysters and, in darker hours, for opening throats — but he had looked at the six kneeling figures, then at the forty crossbows lining the square's edge, and done arithmetic.

He had survived every poison ever put in his cup. He could not survive forty quarrels.

"Quentin Underhill." Voss stood on the scaffold beside him, her voice pitched to carry. She wore her gray coat buttoned to the throat despite the warmth, and her hair was pinned back so severely it seemed less style than discipline. "You have been held against your will by these fugitives. The crown extends you clemency. Step forward, renounce them, and walk free."

*Step forward.* As if he had been dragged here in chains rather than walking beside Odile by choice since the burning of the wagons. As if the whole realm hadn't heard by now what he'd done — the taster who turned traitor, who torched the supply train on the Purge Road and vanished into the hills with the king's enemies.

He stepped forward. Voss's mouth tightened almost imperceptibly; she had expected him to refuse, and she had prepared for that too, and some part of him understood that this whole ceremony existed because she had prepared for it. Clemency offered publicly had to be refused publicly. Refusal made him a villain in the town's eyes, and villains were easier to hunt than victims.

"Say it," she said quietly, for his ears alone. "Say *I renounce them*, and I'll let you walk out of this square alive. You have my word."

"Your word," Quentin said.

"I keep my word. It's one of the few things left that I do."

He looked at Odile. She was watching him with those flat gray eyes that had seen too much death to be impressed by more of it, and she gave him the smallest shake of her head. *Don't give them anything,* the gesture said. *Not even your grief.*

But grief was all he had left that they couldn't take.

"No," Quentin said.

It came out quieter than he meant. He cleared his throat and said it again, louder, letting it roll across the square to the shuttered windows and the drawn curtains behind which Marrowfen watched. "No. I won't renounce them. I *chose* them. Nobody held me. Nobody enchanted me — you'd know if they had, wouldn't you, Witch-Finder? You've been measuring my blood since the day we met."

A murmur went through the crowd like wind through wheat.

Voss raised one hand, and the murmur died. "Then you are declared gifted," she said formally, "and subject to the Hunt." But her eyes flicked — just once, just briefly — toward the crossbows, and Quentin saw the thing she was actually doing. She wasn't going to kill him. Not here, not publicly, not with half the realm watching to see whether the famous taster died on a scaffold or walked away.

She was going to let him go. And make sure everyone watched him run.

"The prisoner Underhill is remanded to escort for transport to the capital for trial," Voss announced. "The others—" she turned to the kneeling row, and her voice did something terrible: it softened. "—will face the ordeal at dawn."

*Transport.* Not execution. Not even proper imprisonment. A word with a hole in it, big enough to climb out of.

* * *

The hole revealed itself an hour later, at the town's western gate.

They marched him in a cart with two guards, both of whom spent the entire ride looking anywhere except at him, which was how he knew. At the gate the cart stopped. One guard dismounted, checked the axle he had no reason to check, and muttered to his companion about horses needing water. Then, without a word to Quentin, they both walked twenty paces down the road and stood there with their backs turned, examining the treeline as though it owed them money.

The cart sat unguarded. The gate stood open. Beyond it, the hills rolled green and gold toward the covenant-stone country, and somewhere out there, presumably, freedom.

*Run*, the arrangement said. *We are looking the other way. Run, and be the coward the ballads will describe.*

Quentin sat in the cart for a long time. Long enough that the guard shifted his weight. Long enough that the afternoon light began to go amber on the hillside.

He understood it now, completely, the way you understand a poison's structure once you've tasted enough of them. Voss could not kill him without making a martyr, and could not hold him without making a trial, and a trial was the one thing she feared — because at a trial, questions would be asked about bloodwork and covenants and why a food-taster's veins carried the signature of the old law. So she had built him a door and painted it gold and called it mercy.

And the truly brilliant part, the part he almost admired, was that it *was* mercy — technically, legally, in every way that mattered to a magistrate. Witch-Finder Serane Voss had released her prisoner. If he fled, that was his crime, not hers. If he ran far enough, he might even live. She had taken six mages off the board and lost nothing, and she had sent the seventh scurrying into the hills with his shame rattling behind him like a tin can tied to a dog's tail.

*Running was always my family's answer.*

His mother had run. Fled into the valley pregnant, traded her gift for his safety, buried her name under a new one and never — not once in all his childhood — spoke of what she'd left outside the boundary stones. Running had bought him thirty years of honeyed quail and careful sips and a life measured in survived meals. Running had worked.

And running had left Odile kneeling in silvered cord with dawn coming for her.

He thought of Wren laughing. Of Tam's hands, steady as a carpenter's, wrapping a fevered child in cool linen. Of the Ferro twins arguing about whether a candle counted as magic if you lit it by accident. Of Master Corvel, who had wept quietly the night before last and apologized for it, saying he was too old for this, saying he should have registered years ago, saying *I'm sorry, I'm sorry* to no one in particular.

Of Odile, telling him by firelight what the covenant was: *Magic bound itself to law through a mortal vessel's willing oath. Your bloodline is the unfinished sentence.*

Unfinished. He had spent his whole life thinking himself finished — a completed thing, a survivor, a man whose body had answered every question poison asked it. But he wasn't finished. He was a sentence with no period, and Voss had just handed him a full stop in the shape of an open gate.

The shame arrived first, hot and total, flooding him the way arsenic used to flood his mouth before his tongue learned to read past it. Shame for standing in that square and saying *no* while doing nothing else. Shame for every year he'd swallowed in silence and called it wisdom. Shame for his mother, who had deserved better than a son who inherited her flight and mistook it for inheritance.

And then, underneath the shame, the way sweetness hides under bitter almond: decision. Clean, cold, unmistakable.

He would not run. He would go back.

Not tonight — tonight he would play the part Voss had written for him, because a performance required an audience and hers was watching. He climbed down from the cart, picked up his skirts of invisibility — the taster's oldest trick, being too unremarkable to look at twice — and walked through the open gate like a man with every right to be there. Behind him, one of the guards exhaled audibly.

* * *

He doubled back after dark, which took longer than courage would have liked and less than sense demanded.

Marrowfen's jail was the cellar of the tithe-barn, one door, two windows barred, four witch-finders on rotation. Quentin circled it twice from the shadow of a hayrick, memorizing the watch changes, and found what he had hoped he'd find and dreaded finding: Voss herself, standing at the barred window past midnight, speaking softly to someone inside.

He crept close enough to hear.

"—dawn, and then it's out of my hands," Voss was saying. Her voice had none of the courtroom steel in it. It was low and tired and almost gentle, and that gentleness frightened him more than the crossbows had. "There's a register. Sign it, swear the binding oath, wear the collar-mark, and you live. It isn't the end of the world, Odile. It's paperwork."

"And my patients who can't afford paperwork?" Odile's voice, dry as winter grass.

"Some laws are imperfect."

"You burned a child on the Purge Road."

"I burned no one."

"Your *Hunt* burned her. Your lists. Your registers. You built the pyre and act surprised when it lights."

A long silence. When Voss spoke again, each word was laid down like stones across a stream. "I have seen what a gift does when it has no law around it. I carried the survivors out on my own back. Do not lecture me on fires."

Quentin pressed himself into the dark and felt the shape of her grief through the wall — vast, old, armored in procedure — and hated how much he recognized it. Two wounded people. He'd thought that once, blade between them. He'd been wrong about one thing only: recognition wasn't peace. Sometimes it was just two animals scenting the same wound.

She moved away from the window. Quentin waited until her boots faded, then waited longer, then did the thing he had decided to do while lying awake in the hills rehearsing failure after failure.

He went to find the guard rotation's weakest joint, and he found it: the tithe-barn's eastern wall, where decades of rain had rotted a sill wide enough for a thin man with a taster's patience. It cost him skin. It cost him nearly an hour. It cost him the small knife, snapped in the wood, and he mourned it the way other men mourned swords.

But it got him into the hayloft above the cellar, and from there, through the gap where the loft floor didn't meet the wall, he could drop into the dark below.

Six faces turned toward him. In the gloom, Odile's eyes caught what little light there was and held it.

"You're supposed to be gone," she whispered.

"I know." He knelt, drew the shard of broken knife-blade he'd salvaged from the sill, and set to work on the silvered cord at her wrists. It bit his fingers; he ignored it. His hands had handled worse. His hands had been *made* for worse. "I'm slow. Blame my mother — she never taught me anything except how to leave."

"Quentin." Odile's voice had an edge he'd rarely heard from her, sharper than any cord. "If they catch you here, they kill you and no one calls it mercy. Whatever you think you owe us—"

"It isn't about owing." The cord parted. Blood welled along his palm; he wiped it on his trousers and moved to Tam's bindings. "I spent thirty years surviving. Every meal, every sip, every day — survive, survive, survive. I told myself it was strength. It was just fear wearing armor." He glanced up at her, and his voice steadied into something he didn't fully recognize as his own. "I'm done surviving. I'm going to the stone, and I'm going to say the oath, and I'm going to drag your stubborn hide there to teach me how — because the Rite needs a vessel and I am apparently the vessel, and I'll be damned if I learn it from memory while you're ash."

Silence. Then Tam snorted softly. The Ferro twins grinned at each other in the dark like conspirators born to it. Old Corvel whispered something that might have been a prayer or a curse, and Wren — quiet Wren, who had laughed like water over stones — reached over and squeezed his bleeding hand, transferring more courage than any charm could have.

Odile studied him for a long moment. Then she sighed, the sigh of a woman adding a weight to a load she'd already decided to carry.

"The east window bar is loose," she said. "Tam loosened it the first night, before you even arrived. We were waiting for a fool brave enough to get us out." Her gray eyes glinted. "Congratulations, taster. You're the bravest fool in Marrowfen."

* * *

They went out through the fields in the black hour before dawn, single file, breath steaming, moving the way hunted things move — fast and low and utterly silent. Quentin led them along the riverbank where the reeds swallowed sound, past sleeping cattle, up onto the ridgeline where the covenant-country began its long roll westward.

At the top of the ridge he stopped and looked back.

Marrowfen lay below, embers of lamplight dying one by one. And at the tithe-barn, torches were flaring — the alarm, late by half an hour, raised by a guard checking a rotting sill and finding it freshly broken. Tiny figures swarmed. A bell began to ring, ragged and urgent.

And apart from the swarm, motionless at the barn door, one gray-coated figure stood watching the ridge. Even at this distance, Quentin knew the posture: hands clasped behind her back, head tilted, unreadable.

Serane Voss was looking directly at him. Across half a mile of night air, he felt her gaze land on him like a hand on his shoulder.

*Go*, the posture said. *Run. I have your measure now, taster. I know exactly what you'll do next.*

And she was right, he realized — that was the horror of it. She had built a cage out of his own decency, and he had broken out of it, and now she would follow, and the following would be merciless, because he had proven the one thing she could not forgive: that mercy, in her hands, was just another trap, and he had declined to be caught in it.

"Quentin." Odile touched his arm. "Riders will be saddled within the hour. We need distance."

He tore his eyes from the distant figure. Ahead, the land rose toward the high passes, and beyond the passes, somewhere at the heart of everything, the covenant-stone waited in the valley of his birth — the stone his mother had fled, the stone that had been waiting thirty years for an Underhill to come home and finish the sentence.

"They'll expect us to scatter," he said. "So we don't. We go straight for the stone, openly, loudly. Let her chase us. Every soldier she pulls off the villages to hunt us is a village that keeps its children." He started walking, and the others fell in around him, and his voice hardened into something that tasted, for the first time in his life, like truth instead of poison. "She gave me mercy. I intend to spend every coin of it making her regret the gift."

Behind them the bell rang on. Ahead, the hills waited, and dawn came up bloody behind the riders already cresting the eastern pass.
