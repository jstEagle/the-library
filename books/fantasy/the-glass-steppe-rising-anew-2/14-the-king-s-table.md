## 14. The King's Table

The capital smelled of smoke and river, of tar and roasting meat and the particular sweetness of a city that believed itself safe. Quentin had forgotten how loud it was — the Glass Steppe had taught him silence so thoroughly that the noise of Corvenhall struck him now like weather. Cart wheels on cobbles. A fishmonger's cry. Somewhere a bell counting out the hour with the smug authority of a place that had never once been burned.

He walked through it in his old coat, the taster's coat, dove-grey with the crown's silver thistle stitched at the shoulder. He had taken it from a line behind a dyer's shop two towns back, and if the fit was slightly wrong at the shoulders, no one who mattered would look closely enough to see. That was the whole trick of it, he thought. No one looked at the taster. They looked *past* him, the way one looks past a chair or a candle. He had spent eleven years being furniture, and furniture, he was learning, could be moved anywhere.

*You will go back,* Odile had said, gripping his wrist hard enough to leave marks he still carried, faint yellow-green ghosts above the pulse. *You'll walk into that hall and they'll think you've come crawling home, and you will let them think it. Pride is a door they never guard.*

*And when they open the door?*

*Then you show them what's standing behind you.* She had smiled then, the scar pulling her mouth sideways into something between tenderness and threat. *Us. All of us. Every name on every pyre. You're not going alone, Quentin Underhill. You never were.*

He touched the inside of his sleeve where the small vial rode against his skin — covenant-draught, distilled from Odile's own careful arts, harmless as rainwater to anyone else and unmistakable as a shout to anyone whose blood remembered the old law. It would not kill. It would not even sicken, unless the drinker's blood held what his held. But it would *speak*. That was all he needed it to do.

The palace gates rose ahead, white stone gone honey-gold in the late light, and Quentin felt the strangest sensation: not fear, exactly. Recognition. This was the room where his life had been spent, coin by coin, sip by sip. He knew its smells better than his mother's grave. He knew which floorboard near the dais creaked, which servant watered the wine, which of the king's cousins bit his nails during long petitions. He had been the most invisible man in this kingdom, and invisibility, it turned out, was a kind of key.

"Name and business," said the gate sergeant, a broad man with a boiled-red face.

Quentin lifted his chin and let his voice come out flat and tired, the voice of a man who had walked a long way to do an unpleasant duty. "Quentin Underhill. King's taster. I ran when the witch-finders came for me — cowardice, nothing more." He let his shoulders slump just so, let shame sit on him like a wet cloak. "I've come to surrender myself to His Majesty's justice."

The sergeant's eyes went wide, then narrow, then wide again, cycling through suspicion and greed and the particular joy of a small man handed a large moment. "Underhill. The taster that run off. Gods' teeth, half the city's got a ballad about you." He spat thoughtfully to the side. "They'll want you alive, then."

"They'll want me kneeling," Quentin said. "That I can manage."

* * *

They kept him in a holding cell beneath the east wing for two days, which suited him perfectly, because two days was exactly how long word needed to travel, and travel it did. He heard it in the guards' talk through the door. *The taster's come back. Walked in the front gate like a lamb. Voss is furious — says it's a trick. The king says a penitent's a gift, whatever color it's wrapped in.* And once, lower, almost reverent: *They say the fugitives are tearing the Purge Road apart looking for him. Say he burned Voss's wagons himself. Say he's got the covenant-blood.*

Say. Say. The city fed on say the way a fire feeds on dry grass, and Quentin lay on his cot in the dark and let it eat. Every rumor was a brick in the wall he was building between himself and the pyre. A man who fled out of cowardice was pathetic. A man who fled and burned supply wagons and vanished into the hills was *valuable* — too valuable to burn before he'd been milked dry. Aldric wanted the mages. Aldric wanted the valley's secrets, the fugitives' routes, the names Odile had trusted him with. The king would keep his returned taster breathing right up until the moment the breathing stopped being useful.

Which meant Quentin had perhaps three days. Maybe four. After that, the milking would end and the slaughter begin, and Serane Voss — who did not believe in lambs — would get her way at last.

On the third morning, they came for him.

Two chamberlains and four halberdiers, and a tailor's apprentice carrying a clean shirt, because the king would not receive even a traitor in filthy linen. They scrubbed him, dressed him, bound his wrists with silk cord that would have been insulting if it hadn't been so practical, and marched him up through the palace he knew blindfolded — past the kitchens where Marta the undercook had once slipped him honeycakes, past the gallery where the portraits of dead kings watched him with their varnished patience — and up to the great doors of the audience hall.

The herald's voice rolled ahead of him like thunder down a valley. "QUENTIN UNDERHILL, KING'S TASTER, RETURNED TO THE CROWN'S JUSTICE!"

The doors opened.

* * *

The throne room of Corvenhall had not changed, and seeing it unchanged made Quentin feel as though he had stepped backward into his own grave. Same banners hanging from the vaulted dark. Same shafts of colored light falling from the clerestory windows onto a floor polished by centuries of knees. Same crowd — courtiers in their winter silks, ministers with their ink-stained fingers, witch-finders in their grey-and-ash livery lined along the walls like fence posts. And at the far end, elevated on seven steps, King Aldric IV, crowned even indoors, his beard trimmed to a point, his hands folded on the armrests of a throne carved with the words THE LAW IS THE KING'S HAND.

Aldric had grown older in the months Quentin had been gone. Or perhaps Quentin had simply grown younger — hungrier, quicker, more awake than he had ever been inside these walls.

"Your Majesty." Quentin knelt, silk cords and all, and bowed his head with the precise degree of humility he had rehearsed on the road: enough to satisfy protocol, not so much as to look broken. "I have come home."

"So I am told." Aldric's voice filled the hall without effort; it always had, that trained bellows-deep calm. "You fled your post, Underhill. You consorted with fugitive mages. Witch-Finder Voss reports you destroyed crown property and aided the escape of no fewer than nine condemned criminals." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on a table. "Why should I not have your head now, and skip the tedium of questions?"

Behind the king's left shoulder, half in shadow, stood Serane Voss.

She had not changed either, except around the eyes, where something had hardened into something else — grief annealed to iron. She watched Quentin with the stillness of a hawk that has already done its arithmetic and is merely waiting for the answer to confirm it. Her hand rested on her sword hilt, not gripping. Just resting. As though the sword were a friend she consulted often.

Quentin raised his head. "Because my head is worth more attached, Majesty. And because I have something to return to you that you cannot take by force."

"Bold words from a bound man."

"I learned boldness from watching you, sire." He let that hang in the incense-thick air while murmurs stirred through the court like wind over wheat. "I served this table eleven years. I drank poison for you more times than I can count — bitter almond under the quail, henbane in the spiced wine, three separate attempts on the Feast of Saint Verene alone. My body is a ledger of everything this court's enemies ever sent against you, and I swallowed all of it, and I lived, and I never asked why I lived." He paused. "Until someone poisoned the kitchen porridge last autumn. Remember? Cook Ellin died at her own hearth. I did not."

Voss shifted. Just slightly. The sound of her boot on marble was very small and very loud.

"The compound meant for me," Quentin said, "kills any ordinary blood. Mine metabolized it. Do you know why, Majesty? Because my mother was a vessel of the old covenant. Because her blood — my blood — carries the oath that bound magic to law, back when magic *had* a law instead of a pyre. Your Gift-Hunt is burning people whose only crime is inheriting a promise this crown has forgotten it made."

"Lies," said the Lord Chancellor smoothly, from his cushioned place below the dais. "Fairy stories for frightened hedge-witches. There is no covenant. There is no blood-oath. There is only sedition wearing a saint's name."

"There is a way to know," Quentin said quietly. "There has always been a way. I am the king's taster. Testing what goes into the royal body is the whole of my office." He turned his bound hands palm-up, deliberate, ceremonial. "Give me a cup, Majesty. Give me the king's own wine. Let me prepare it, as I have prepared a thousand cups before this court, and then let me drink it in your presence — and watch what happens. If I am a liar, I die a liar's death, cheaply and quickly, and you may go back to your hunt with a clear conscience. If I am not—"

"If you are not," Aldric said, "then you expect me to believe a conjurer's trick proves some ancient contract?"

"I expect you," Quentin said, "to uphold your own law. Trial by ordeal is written in the Codex Aldric the First — your namesake, Majesty, your foundation. *If a man's claim cannot be proven by witness, let it be proven by ordeal, for the realm holds that truth survives what falsehood cannot.* You have invoked it against mages a dozen times this year. Every mage you burned was offered the same bargain: prove innocence by ordeal, or die condemned. None could pass it, because the ordeals were built to fail them." He met the king's eyes across forty feet of marble and colored light, and did not blink. "Build one that doesn't. Let me drink. Let the realm watch. That is all I ask — and it is, incidentally, the law."

The silence that followed had weight and texture. Quentin heard his own heartbeat in it, steady, unhurried — eleven years of practice, keeping his face smooth while death sat in his mouth.

It was Voss who broke it.

"Majesty." Her voice was level, but Quentin heard the wire under it. "This is theatre. He is bait in a trap of his own making. Whatever he drinks, he has prepared his body to survive. Kill him, interrogate the fugitives through proper means, and be done with it."

"Proper means," Aldric repeated musingly, "have yielded us remarkably little, Witch-Finder. Your prisoners do not talk. Your hunts find empty camps." He descended one step, then another, the crown catching the window-light. "No. The taster has found the one argument I cannot dismiss, which is that refusing him costs me more than granting him. If I kill him unheard, every whispering fool in this hall goes home believing the covenant is real and that I feared the proof. If I let him drink and he dies — good. One less traitor, and the spectacle instructs the mob. If he lives..." The king smiled, thin and cold as frost on glass. "Then we shall learn precisely what he is. And knowledge, Master Underhill, has always been the crown's favorite vintage."

He gestured. "Bring the king's wine."

* * *

They gave him the silver ewer and the great gilded cup on a lacquered tray, and the Lord Chamberlain himself poured, dark red wine catching the light like something alive. Two witch-finders flanked Quentin at the foot of the dais steps, close enough that he could smell the ash-residue on their grey cloaks — the burnt-hair smell that never quite washed out, he'd noticed it years ago and never known its source until Odile told him: they wore it deliberately, a warning, a uniform of terror.

His hands were still bound. That was part of the theatre; Aldric wanted no accusation that the taster had doctored anything unseen. So Quentin did the one thing left to him, the thing he had practiced since the night he left Odile's fire.

He tipped the vial from his sleeve into the wine with his bound hands, a small motion disguised as the fumbling of a nervous man adjusting his grip on the tray — and the draught went into the king's cup, clear as water, tasteless as forgiveness, dissolving into the dark red like a secret into a confession.

Then he lifted the cup in both bound hands and raised it toward the throne.

"To the health of King Aldric," he said, and his voice did not shake, and somewhere in the galleries he heard a woman draw breath sharply, and somewhere along the wall Voss's hand finally closed around her sword hilt. "To the law. And to the truth, which survives what falsehood cannot."

He drank.

Deeply. Deliberately. Three swallows, the way the ritual required, letting the wine run cold and bright down his throat — and beneath it, arriving a half-second later, the draught woke.

Oh, it woke.

It was not pain, exactly. It was *presence*. Heat bloomed outward from his sternum like a lamp being lit inside a stained-glass figure, and Quentin felt it pour down both arms, felt it rise up his neck and settle behind his eyes, and the whole hall sharpened as though someone had wiped dust from the world — he could see the individual threads in the banners thirty feet away, hear the Chancellor's swallow, taste the wine's history, the vineyard, the rain, the barrel oak, all of it laid open like a book. Magic, touching him from the inside. His mother's inheritance, answering the roll call.

And the cup — the cup began to shine.

Not brightly. Not dramatically. But unmistakably: a soft gold-white luminescence rising through the gilded metal, pulsing gently, twice, three times, in a rhythm like a slow heartbeat, like a knock at a door long locked. Gasps rippled through the court. Someone dropped a fan. In the gallery above, a young maid crossed herself with both hands.

Quentin lowered the cup and stood very straight, glowing faintly at the edges like a man backlit by his own ancestry, and let the silence break over him in waves.

"The covenant," he said, and his voice rang strangely in the hush, doubled somehow, as though the stone itself were repeating it a half-beat behind. "Is real. It sleeps in my blood, Majesty, and it wakes at need, and it has just testified — before your court, under your roof, by the ordeal of the cup that your own Codex prescribes. I am the vessel. The last unfinished sentence of an oath your throne swore and abandoned." He set the cup down on the tray with a click that sounded, in that silence, like a lock turning. "And I am here to tell you what the covenant requires: renewal, lawful and witnessed, before the assembled realm. Refuse, and every drop of magic in this kingdom stays wild and hunted and angry, and your Gift-Hunt burns until there is nothing left of this realm to hunt. Accept—" he spread his bound hands, "—and magic comes home under law. Registered. Bounded. Answerable to courts instead of pyres. Exactly as it was in your ancestors' time, before the last vessel broke the chain."

King Aldric had not moved. But Quentin, reading the king's face now with senses no taster had ever possessed, saw the calculation running behind the royal eyes like quicksilver — saw the exact moment the arithmetic finished and the trap closed from the other side.

"You realize," Aldric said softly, "what you have done. You have drunk poison before my court and survived it by sorcery, in violation of the very laws of the hunt you invoke. By the strict letter, Underhill, I could condemn you this instant."

"You could," Quentin agreed. "But you'd be condemning the proof along with the man, and your own Codex with it, and every noble in this hall knows the difference between law and appetite. You built your reign on the claim that the crown *is* the law, Majesty. Here is your chance to prove it isn't just a carving on a chair."

For a long moment the king was silent. Then he laughed — short, genuine, entirely without warmth, the laugh of a chess player watching his opponent sacrifice the queen.

"Oh, you are your mother's son," Aldric said. "Very well. You want the covenant's terms honored to the letter? Then honor mine. Tomorrow at dawn, before the assembled realm, you will submit to trial by ordeal — the true ordeal, the Rite as the old records describe it. The covenant-draught, full strength, brewed from the recipe sealed in my grandfather's archive. Drink it and live, and the covenant is renewed, and I will call a halt to the hunt and bind magic under law with my own hand. Drink it and die—" the smile again, frost-thin, "—and every gifted soul in my dungeons dies with you, and the hunt continues, and no one in this hall will ever say King Aldric broke faith."

From the shadow by the wall, Voss spoke one word, quiet and final, like a stone dropped down a well:

"Dawn."

Quentin bowed, glowing faintly, bound hands pressed to his heart, the taste of the ordeal already sitting on his tongue like a promise.

"As Your Majesty commands," he said. "I have never refused a cup yet."
