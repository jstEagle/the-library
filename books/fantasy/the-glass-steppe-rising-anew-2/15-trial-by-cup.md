## 15. Trial by Cup

They gave Quentin a cell with a window.

He understood the cruelty of it at once. The window faced east, toward the Glass Steppe, and in the first gray light of morning he could see the boundary stones catching the sun like a row of filed teeth. Somewhere beyond them lay the covenant-stone, and somewhere beyond that, in the dungeons beneath the Hall of Records, Odile was chained in the dark for the crime of existing. The king wanted him to look at what he'd lost every day until the trial. It was working. He looked.

Three days they kept him there. Three days of bread and water brought by guards who would not meet his eyes — he tasted each mouthful anyway, out of habit, out of something deeper than habit, cataloguing the absence of poison the way other men counted their wounds. On the second night he heard the whispers through the wall. *The taster. They say he put magic in the king's cup. They say his blood is old blood.* By the third morning the whispers had become a sermon, and the sermon had become a mob gathering outside the palace gates, and Quentin lay on his cot listening to the roar of a city deciding whether he was a saint or a serpent, and thought: *Mother, you traded everything to keep me out of a story like this one.*

On the fourth day they came for him dressed in white.

* * *

The Hall of Justice had been built by a king who believed law was a form of architecture — that if you made the ceilings high enough and the stone cold enough, truth would have nowhere to hide. Aldric's grandfather had raised the pillars. Aldric had filled them. Every seat was taken, every gallery packed shoulder to shoulder with lords in their winter furs and guildmasters in their dyed wool and common folk who had queued since before dawn for the privilege of watching a man be weighed against his own confession.

They walked Quentin up the central aisle between two ranks of witch-finders in their bone-pale cloaks, and Serane Voss walked opposite him, matching him step for step, as though they were two halves of a single procession. She would not look at him either. That was how he knew she was afraid. Serane Voss did not look away from things; she looked *at* them, the way a blade looks at flesh. If she couldn't bear his face this morning, it was because she had already seen what was coming and found no mercy in it.

The throne dais stood where an altar might have stood in older halls, and King Aldric sat above it wearing the full weight of the crown, which was iron and old and heavier than any honest man should ask of his neck. His face was composed. His hands were not. They gripped the armrests of the throne like a drowning man grips driftwood, and Quentin, who had spent twenty years reading the small truths of the royal table, read this one easily:

*The king did not want this trial. The king was forced into it.*

Good, Quentin thought. Let him sit in the trap. Let him feel the teeth of the law he'd sharpened.

The Herald of Precedents rose from her desk — a thin woman with ink-stained fingers and a voice like a bell struck in a stairwell — and unrolled a scroll long enough to reach the floor.

"Let it be entered into record," she cried, "that on the feast of Saint Verrin, before the assembled court, one Quentin Underhill, food-taster to the crown, did openly declare before witnesses that his blood carries the old covenant, and did prove the same by means of sorcery worked upon the royal cup."

"I proved nothing," Quentin said, and the hall went silent around his voice, hungry for it. "I poisoned the wine. With hemlock and moonwort, mixed as my mother taught me, in a measure that sickens but does not kill. There was no sorcery in it." He let the silence stretch. "What I proved was that when I drank, the sickness left me in under a minute. No mortal body sheds hemlock so fast. What I proved was that the king's own physicians examined the cup and found the poison gone — not diluted, not disguised. *Gone*. Swallowed by something older than chemistry. I proved that the covenant-blood is real, because I carry it."

Aldric leaned forward. "You confess to poisoning your king."

"I confess to poisoning a wine," Quentin said. "The man who drank it was never in danger, and every soul in this hall knows why. Your Majesty, you are cornered by your own law, and we both know it. The Edict of the Hunt says all bearers of the gift must register or die. Very well. Register me. But the Statute of Vessels — the law your grandfather wrote, the law that ended the Mage Wars, the law you swore at your coronation to uphold *above all others* — says that a claimant of covenant-blood may demand trial by ordeal, and that the realm is bound to hear it. You cannot hunt the gifted with one hand while holding the covenant closed with the other. Choose which law you serve."

"You dare lecture me on law—"

"I dare," Quentin said quietly, "because I learned it at your table. Twenty years, Your Majesty. I watched you rule. You always said the crown's strength was that it bound itself — that a king who could break any law protected nothing. I am asking you to be that king. One last time."

The hall held its breath. Somewhere in the galleries a child coughed, and the sound cracked across the silence like ice.

Aldric sat back. For a long moment he only looked at Quentin, and Quentin saw the war behind the king's eyes — pride wrestling with terror, terror wrestling with the memory of a boy-prince swearing oaths over a stone. When the king finally spoke, his voice was flat and careful, the voice of a man stepping onto ice and testing each inch.

"The ordeal is granted."

The roar that came off the galleries was less like cheering than like weather.

* * *

They read the terms aloud, and Quentin listened to his death being scheduled.

The Rite of Return required three preparations. The draught itself must be brewed from the covenant-stone's leachings — water that had passed through the heart of the old stone in the Steppe, carrying whatever slept there. The vessel must drink it fasting, at dawn, before the assembled realm or its lawful representatives. And the vessel must survive.

"If the covenant accepts him," the Herald recited, her bell-voice steady even as the words grew monstrous, "the bond between magic and law is renewed, and all gifts within the realm fall under its governance, as in the days of the first kings. If the covenant rejects him—" she paused, and Quentin watched her knuckles whiten on the scroll, "—then the claim of covenant-blood is proven false, the prisoner dies as a poisoner, and the sentence of the Gift-Hunt stands in full force against all registered and unregistered bearers alike."

*All mages die,* the hall heard. *And Quentin Underhill with them.*

Odile was dragged up from the dungeons to confirm the rite's form — they needed a mage's testimony that the draught was genuine, that the king wasn't substituting simple poison for the true ordeal. They brought her in chains, and she was thinner than Quentin remembered, her healer's hands raw at the wrists, but when her eyes found him across the hall she smiled. Actually smiled. As if he were a patient who'd sat up in bed and asked for breakfast.

"Is the draught true?" the Herald demanded. "Will you attest to its preparation?"

"It's true," Odile said. Her voice carried without effort; healers' voices were trained for screaming wards. "Water from the stone's root, drawn at the dark of the moon, sealed in glass. I supervised the drawing myself, under your witch-finders' very attentive supervision." A ripple of uneasy laughter moved through the benches. "I will add only this, and then you can take me back to my cell: the rite does not care whether the vessel is brave. It cares whether he is *honest*. The stone will know the difference. So will he."

Serane Voss spoke from her place beside the throne. "The Witch-Finder's Office objects to the ordeal entire. This is theater. The man has confessed to poisoning—"

"The man has invoked statute," Aldric said, and something in his tone silenced her more completely than anger would have. "Your objection is noted, Witch-Finder, and denied. Unless you mean to argue that the crown is free to ignore the Statute of Vessels?" He let that hang. "No? Then we proceed."

Voss sat down slowly. And now she did look at Quentin — one long, level look across the width of the hall, her gray eyes unreadable and yet, to a man who had spent his life reading faces at tables where a wrong swallow meant death, perfectly readable. She was telling him something. He turned it over all through the rest of the proceedings, all through the signing of the writ and the scheduling and the reading of the banns, and only understood it much later, alone in his cell with the eastern window going gold:

She had looked at him the way she once must have looked at her sister. *I am sorry, and I will not stop you, and I do not know which of those is the betrayal.*

* * *

That evening they allowed him a visitor, which surprised him until the door opened and it was Lord Chancellor Merric, the king's oldest servant, carrying a covered dish.

Quentin laughed before he could stop himself. "You're bringing me supper? At this point in my life?"

Merric set the dish down — plain bread, plain cheese, a cup of water — and uncovered it with ceremony. "Old habit. In this building, tonight, I trust no kitchen but mine." He lowered himself onto the cell's single stool with the groan of a man whose joints predated two kings. "I've served four monarchs, Underhill. I poured Aldric's first wine. I held the crown steady on his head when the weight nearly took him sideways at his coronation. So believe me when I tell you what happened today has never happened in this hall before, and I include the time Duke Ferran challenged the king to single combat over a fishing dispute."

"What did you come to say?"

"That you should lose." Merric said it mildly, the way he might comment on the weather. "Understand me. I hold no love for the pyres. What's been done on the purge roads shames us, and I've told the king so to his face, which is more than most of his council dares. But if you succeed — if the covenant wakes and magic comes back under law — do you know what you actually give the realm? Not justice. *Arguments*. Ten thousand gifted people with grievances and power, and ten thousand frightened people with torches, and a body of law young enough that every lawyer in the capital will spend thirty years finding its loopholes. The wars your grandmother's generation ended — they'll start again, slower, in courtrooms instead of fields. Whereas if the stone rejects you, we bury one poisoner and go on being merely wicked instead of possibly damned."

Quentin ate a piece of bread, tasting it carefully, and considered the old man across from him. "Is that what you believe? Or is it what lets you sleep?"

"Both, usually. That's the trick of serving kings." Merric stood, joints protesting. "One more thing, since you've earned honesty. The king asked me today — asked *me*, privately — whether the ordeal could be arranged to fail. Whether the draught might be weakened, the stone-water cut with well water. I told him the mages would detect it, and worse, that *you* would detect it, and that a rigged ordeal would hang the crown twice: once for the lie and once for the law. He accepted that." The chancellor paused at the door. "But understand what it means, boy. Tomorrow, when you lift that cup, no one in that hall will be helping you. Not the king, not the witch-finders, not God Himself if He's still taking an interest in this valley's affairs. It will be you, the stone, and whatever your mother bought for you with her life. Drink well."

The door shut. Quentin sat a long while with the untouched water, and then, deliberately, drank it down. Plain water. The one thing left in the world that had ever been safe for him. Tomorrow it wouldn't be.

* * *

Dawn came up the color of hammered tin.

They processed him through streets lined ten deep with watchers — silent, mostly, which was worse than shouting — to the great courtyard before the Hall, where a scaffold had been raised and, beside it, on a bier of black oak, the covenant-stone's daughter: a shard of the original rock, brought from the Steppe under guard, dark as wet slate and humming faintly, so faintly, that only Quentin seemed able to hear it. It sounded like his mother humming in a kitchen. It sounded like the moment before a storm decides.

The draught waited in a chalice of plain glass, because the mages had insisted nothing crafted with intent touch the water — no gold, no blessing, no ornament. Just the stone's leaching in a cup, gray-green, still, smelling of rain on hot rocks and something underneath it, something mineral and ancient and *waiting*.

Aldric stood on the dais with the law-book open on a lectern before him, reading the words of the ordeal himself, because the statute required it and because, Quentin suspected, the king wanted the humiliation distributed evenly. Around the courtyard: the whole court, the whole guard, the witch-finders in their white ranks, and in a cage of iron mesh at the platform's edge, Odile, gripping the bars, her scarred face fierce and fixed on him.

"When you feel it start," she had whispered to him the night before, when they'd allowed the condemned counsel of a fellow prisoner, "it will offer you every meal you ever survived. Every dish. Every swallowed death. It will show you them so you'll fight — because fighting is what kept you alive, and the body doesn't know any other way. You have to let them burn past you. All of them. You have to be, for one minute, the most vulnerable man alive." Her fingers had found his wrist, found the pulse there, held it gently, the way she must hold the dying. "You've spent your whole life being the man who can't be hurt. Tomorrow, be the man who can."

Now the herald called the silence. Now the king read the terms: acceptance or death, renewal or ruin, the lives of every gifted soul in the realm balanced against one taster's swallow. Now the crowd breathed like a single animal.

Quentin stepped forward. The chalice was cold in his hands. He looked at it, and past it at the shard of stone humming its kitchen-song, and past that at the eastern sky where, far away, the boundary stones of the Glass Steppe caught the first true light.

*Never leave the valley,* his mother's rules began. *Never refuse a plate. Never let them see you flinch.*

He had broken the first rule to save strangers. He would break the third now, before thousands, if breaking it was the price of the second.

He thought of Odile smiling through chains. Of a child on the purge road, lighting a candle with a thought, and what followed. Of Serane Voss's one long look, mercy and refusal tangled together. Of plain water, drunk safely, one last ordinary time.

Quentin Underhill raised the cup, and the courtyard went so quiet he could hear the stone humming, and he drank.

For one heartbeat, nothing. The crowd's held breath. The tin-colored sky.

Then the draught found him — and the world caught fire from the inside.
