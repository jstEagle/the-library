## 19. Magic Under Law

The first gift to flare lawfully in the Glass Steppe belonged to a girl of nine who could make bread rise by humming at it.

Quentin watched it happen from the steps of the old assize hall, which the masons had spent three weeks scrubbing clean of the Hunt's insignia and re-christening the Hall of Registers. The morning was pale and ordinary, the kind of morning his mother would have called *a good day for nothing*, and the whole valley had turned out anyway — farmers with their hands still smelling of loam, market-wives in their second-best shawls, children perched on fences because there were not enough chairs for a ceremony no one quite believed would actually occur.

The registrar was a round, sweating man named Pellworth who had spent thirty years weighing grain tariffs and now found himself weighing souls instead. He read from a parchment so long it pooled on the table like spilled milk.

"By covenant restored," he recited, "and by the King's Seal affixed thereto, every bearer of the gifted blood shall present themselves for registration within ninety days. No bearer shall be burned, banished, or bound save by judgment of a lawful court. No bearer's gift shall be used against the person or property of another. And no officer of the crown shall raise hand against a registered bearer except under warrant of law."

He said it twice more, in case anyone thought they'd dreamed it.

The bread-girl went up first, dragged forward by her grandmother, who kept making apologetic little curtsies as though her granddaughter's talent were an embarrassing bodily function discussed too loudly in company. Pellworth asked the child to demonstrate. She hummed. A loaf someone had brought for exactly this purpose swelled gently in its basket, crust splitting into a golden smile, and the crowd made a sound Quentin had never heard in the Steppe before — not fear, not the careful silence that hedge-folk had learned to wear like a coat, but something between a gasp and a laugh.

The girl looked terrified. Then she looked delighted. Then she looked terrified again, which Quentin thought was probably the correct order for miracles.

He stood at the edge of the platform in his plain gray coat, the taster's chain still around his neck because he could not yet bring himself to take it off, and felt something loosen in his chest that had been clenched since before he knew it was clenched. Ninety days. Courts instead of pyres. Warrants instead of witch-finders' torches.

It did not feel miraculous. It felt like paperwork. And that, he realized, was precisely what made him want to sit down in the dust and weep.

* * *

The registers filled slowly at first, then all at once.

Word traveled the way word always traveled — faster than riders, faster than sense. In the river towns, women who had hidden their hands in their sleeves for forty years walked into assize halls and laid them palm-up on the tables. A shepherd in the eastern hills turned out to be able to call fog, and registered himself mostly, he admitted to the clerk, so he could stop being blamed for it. An entire family of storm-sensers came down out of the moors together, blinking in the daylight like creatures unearthing from winter.

There were failures, of course. There were towns where the heralds read the proclamation and the crowd stayed silent and no one came forward, because ninety days is not long enough to unlearn a lifetime of flinching. There were at least two lynchings in the borderlands that Quentin heard about — mobs who moved before the law could reach them, who had tasted the Hunt's permission and found it sweet. Aldric hanged six men for the second one, publicly, and made the magistrates watch, and after that the lynchings stopped, or at least learned to be quieter.

Justice arriving institutional rather than miraculous, Odile called it, when she wrote to him from the capital. It is slower. It is uglier. It requires clerks. But a miracle saves one village on one night, and a court can save everyone, forever, if we keep feeding it.

Her letters were full of such sentences — practical, unsentimental, occasionally furious about quorum rules. Odile had been drafted into the new Office of Gifted Affairs despite having expressed, in her words, "a professional preference for setting broken bones over setting policy." She complained constantly. She was also, Quentin suspected, the best thing that had ever happened to the Office of Gifted Affairs.

To you she entrusts the provinces, another letter read, because you are the only person alive whom both the crown and the covens trust, and because you cannot be poisoned anymore, which in this work is nearly a qualification.

He smiled at that, alone in his rooms above the taster's quarters, and then sat with the ache of it for a while. He could not be poisoned anymore. Three months past the Rite and his body was still learning how frightening the world had become. A fever had put him abed for nine days in early autumn; a bad oyster had nearly finished him. Odile sent tonics. The young mages he trained sent flowers and, in one memorably disastrous instance, a charm meant to ward off illness that instead made his hair stand permanently on end for a week.

But the gifts themselves — the registered, bounded, lawful gifts — those worked now, even here, even in the valley. That was the strangest part. For two hundred years the Glass Steppe had been silent ground, dead to magic, and Quentin had assumed the silence was stone-deep and permanent. But the covenant had never been a wall, only a door, and doors swing both ways. Magic came back across the boundary stones like water finding its old channel: cautiously, then greedily. The hedge-witches who had been merely eccentric their whole lives discovered that the charms they'd mouthed without meaning had been waiting inside them like seeds in dry earth. Old Marta at the farrier's stall levitated a horseshoe by accident and had to sit down on a feed sack until her legs returned.

The valley was becoming a place where magic lived, instead of a place where magic was buried. Quentin walked its lanes some evenings just to feel it — that low hum at the edges of things, the neighbor's music his mother had died to keep from him, now playing openly through open windows.

* * *

In the fourth month, the crown dissolved the Order of Witch-Finders.

There was no battle. That was the thing no ballad would ever capture, Quentin thought later: the great cruelties rarely ended with a sword-stroke. They ended with a writ of dissolution, signed in a room that smelled of ink, witnessed by bored clerks. Serane Voss received the order on a Tuesday, read it once, folded it along its original creases, and set it on her desk beneath the paperweight she'd owned for nineteen years — a lump of glass slag, melted from the ruins of the town her sister had destroyed.

Then she began the work of ending herself.

It took eleven weeks. Eleven weeks of inventorying confiscated grimoires and deciding which went to the new archives and which were simply books and should simply be returned. Eleven weeks of processing warrants that would now expire unexecuted, of releasing prisoners whose crimes had been redefined overnight from heresy into nothing, of standing in front of her officers — four hundred and six of them, some loyal to her, some loyal to the pay, most loyal to nothing they could name — and telling them that the work they had given their lives to was over.

Some of them wept. Some of them spat. One man, Corven, who had served beside her since the Ashen Reaches, asked her quietly whether she believed any of it had been worth doing, and she told him the truth, which was: "I believe my sister needed someone to stop her. I believe we became very good at stopping people. I do not believe those are the same skill." Corven nodded as though she'd handed him something heavy, and left the order the same day, and Serane never learned what he did afterward.

She kept the last cell block for herself to empty personally. Fourteen prisoners remained when the final writ came down — mages taken in the last months of the Hunt, some of them people she had arrested during the pursuit of Underhill and his fugitives, back when the world had been simpler and worse.

One of them was a boy of perhaps sixteen named Tam, who had been caught lighting a candle with a thought in a village on the purge road. She remembered him. She remembered the way he'd cried without noise, the way experienced prisoners did, saving their breath.

On the day of his release, Tam stood at the cell door and did not leave. He looked at her the way people had looked at her for years — the look she had told herself was respect, and had known, in the honest hours, was fear — but there was something else in it now.

"You're the one who let Underhill go," Tam said.

"I'm the one who failed to catch him," Voss said. "The distinction seemed important to the tribunal."

"Is it important to you?"

Serane considered the question with the same thoroughness she gave everything, because carelessness was the one sin she had never permitted herself.

"When I took this post," she said, "I believed control was justice. That a gift unbounded was a fire in a thatched town, and the finder's job was to be the rain. I watched rain fail once. I have spent twenty years trying to drown the whole sky." She unlocked the cell and pushed the door wide. "Underhill offered the realm something else. Not control. Covenant. Boundaries chosen instead of walls imposed. I fought it because fighting was all I had left of her." She stepped aside. "Go home, Tam. Register your candle. Light it lawfully. And if you ever find yourself wanting to burn something down—" she paused, "—come find me first. I understand arson better than most."

The boy stared at her. Then he walked out of the cell, and out of the Order's gray halls, and into the lawful bright morning, and Serane Voss stood alone in the corridor listening to his footsteps fade, and felt the strange vertigo of a structure collapsing inward that had spent decades pretending it was a fortress.

* * *

They dissolved the office formally in the capital, with ceremony, because the new age loved its ceremonies almost as much as the old age had loved its pyres.

Quentin attended. He told himself it was duty — the vessel of the covenant ought to witness the instruments of the Hunt being laid to rest — but honesty compelled him to admit, in the privacy of his own skull, that part of him wanted to see it. Wanted to watch the black-and-silver banners come down. Wanted to hear some clerk say aloud, in an official voice, that the hunting was over.

The hall was the same one where he had drunk the king's wine and refused to die, where trial by cup had turned a courtroom into an altar. Now it hosted a different rite. Aldric spoke briefly — the king had grown measurably more brief since the Rite, as though the experience had cured him of the belief that history required volume — and then the Witch-Finders' registry was carried forward, a ledger of names thick as a tombstone, and sealed, and consigned to the archives with instructions that it be opened only by court historians.

And then Serane Voss walked to the center of the floor, in her finder's coat for the last time, and laid her badge of office on the table, and the sound it made — a small iron click, hardly anything — went through Quentin like a blade going through water.

Afterward, in the colonnade outside, he found her standing alone, watching the light move on the flagstones.

"Witch-Finder Voss," he said.

"Not anymore." She didn't turn. "Just Voss. Or Serane, if the covenant permits."

"The covenant permits anything," Quentin said. "That was rather the point of renewing it. Everything bounded, nothing abolished. Even former hunters."

"Hm." She studied her own hands — broad, scarred at the knuckles, hands that had cuffed and chained and occasionally done worse. "I've been offered positions. The Office wants me advising on containment law. They think I know more about dangerous gifts than anyone alive."

"Do you?"

"Yes." A pause. "That's not the same as being right about them."

"No," Quentin agreed. "It isn't."

She finally turned to face him, and up close he could see what the ceremony had cost her — the exhaustion sitting behind her eyes like a guest who intended to stay through winter. Twenty years of purpose, ended by a click of iron on wood. He knew something about surviving the loss of a defining self. He had surrendered his immunity on an altar stone and woken up fragile as anyone, and the fragility had turned out to be not an ending but a door.

"My sister," Serane said abruptly, "would have been twenty-nine this year."

Quentin said nothing. He had learned that from Odile — that silence, offered correctly, was a kind of shelter.

"She burned our town because no one taught her boundaries. Because everyone was afraid, and fear makes cowards of teachers, and so she grew up thinking her gift was either a curse or a weapon, depending on who was talking." Serane's jaw tightened. "The new courts will teach children like her. Registers. Mentors. Law. If they hold—if the clerks keep feeding it—then somewhere a girl like Lira will grow up lighting her candles lawfully and never once believing she deserves the pyre." She exhaled slowly. "I watched the first registrations come through the archives this month. Nine thousand names. Nine thousand people who are not going to burn. Do you know what I did when I counted them?"

"What?"

"I sat on the floor of my office and I cried like my sister's name was in there." Her voice did not waver; that was somehow worse. "It wasn't. It will never be. But the shape of the grief changed. That's all I have to report, Underhill. The grief changed shape."

Quentin reached out and, carefully — asking with the gesture, the way Odile had taught him, because consent was also a covenant of small things — rested his hand on her shoulder.

"You could come to the Steppe," he said. "In spring. There's a festival. We drink water out of ordinary cups and dare the world to poison it." A ghost of a smile. "You might hate it. But you'd be among people rebuilding, and I've found that helps. Eventually. Mostly."

Serane looked at his hand on her shoulder the way she might have looked at an unfamiliar spell — wary, cataloguing, and beneath the wariness, hungry.

"I'll consider it," she said.

From Serane Voss, Quentin understood, that was practically a vow.

* * *

He walked home through the capital at dusk, and the city was singing.

Not all of it — cities don't change all at once; they change the way ice melts, in patches, in surprising places. But in the Lamplighters' Row a registered flame-tender was relighting the street lamps with a flick of her wrist, each lamp blooming as she passed, children running behind her like gulls after a plow. In a tavern window, a storm-senser had chalked tomorrow's weather on a slate for the fishermen: *rain by noon, clear by dusk, safe crossing*. In the temple square, an old woman was teaching three teenagers to still their gifts — a levitating pebble wobbling obediently between her palms — and the lesson was public, unhurried, unafraid, and a city watchman stood nearby not to arrest anyone but simply because it was pleasant to watch.

Lawful magic. Bounded, registered, answerable. It was not the wild song the fugitives had dreamed of around their fires, and it was not the silence Quentin had grown up breathing. It was something harder and better: magic with neighbors, magic with manners, magic that could be summoned into a courtroom and made to swear an oath.

Odile would say it needed defending. Every day, forever, by clerks and quorums and stubborn people willing to be bored on behalf of the future. Miracles end; institutions must be fed. Quentin had stopped arguing with her about it.

At his door, a messenger was waiting — one of the Office's riders, mud-spattered, breathless, holding out a letter sealed not with the crown's wax but with Odile's personal mark, the knotwork sigil of her healing line, pressed deep enough to crack the wax.

Quentin broke it open under the lamp.

*Quentin — come at once. The northern registers have found something in the old records, something about the first covenant, the original oath, before vessels. There is a clause none of us ever saw, and it concerns the heirs of vessels, and Quentin — the girl who made the bread rise, the one from your valley, the nine-year-old — her blood answers to it. Come quickly. Bring nothing. Tell no one.*

He stood in the doorway a long moment, the letter trembling slightly in his hand, while behind him the lamplighter's blooms lit the street one window at a time, and somewhere a child laughed, and the hum of lawful magic rose soft over the roofs like a neighbor's music through an open wall.

He went inside and packed a bag.
