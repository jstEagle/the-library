## 22. The Glass Steppe Rising Anew

The valley smelled of wet earth and woodsmoke the morning of the spring festival, and Quentin Underhill stood at his mother's grave with a cup of water in his hand.

It was nothing. That was the whole point of it. Rainwater, drawn from the cistern behind the market hall, carried up the hill in a clay cup a child had made at the festival stalls — lopsided, thumb-marked, fired crooked in a kiln that had not existed two years ago because there had been no one with fire-gifts permitted to run one. The water was not blessed. It was not warded. It was not tested by anyone's tongue before anyone drank it, and that fact still made something old and cold turn over in Quentin's chest every time he thought about it, which was constantly, which was the point.

He could die from a poisoned cup now. Anyone could kill him with what had once taken an army of alchemists to even bruise him. A few drops of bitter almond in his morning tea. Hemlock in his soup. The ordinary cruelties of any kitchen in the realm, the ones he had spent thirty years swallowing so that kings could eat without fear — those would end him now, swiftly, ingloriously, like any man.

He set the cup down on the flat stone that marked his mother's resting place and knelt in the grass.

"I'm going to drink it," he told her. "I want you to know that first, before anything else, because I know you'd worry."

The wind moved through the Steppe grass the way it always had, in long silver waves, unbothered by magic or its absence. At the valley's far edge, where the boundary stones stood in their patient row, the air shimmered faintly — not with warding anymore, but with the hum of lawful craft, registered and bounded, the covenant's new warmth lying over the old silence like a blanket laid across a sleeping child. Magic did not enter the valley uninvited. But it was *there*, at the borders, polite as a neighbor's music through a wall, and sometimes on quiet evenings Quentin could feel it against his skin like the memory of sunlight.

His mother had traded everything for this silence. He understood that now in a way he hadn't when he'd read her letter, kneeling on this same ground with the ink blurring and his hands shaking. She had not fled here to keep magic from him. She had fled here to buy him time — time to grow into whatever he was going to be, unmarked, unclaimed, until the world was ready for him or he was ready for the world. She had died believing she'd failed, because the world had come for him anyway, dragging him out past the stones in chains of courtesy and threat.

She had been wrong about the failure. He wished he could tell her so in more than words.

"The covenant holds," he said. "You'd laugh at how much paperwork that involves. There are courts now. Actual courts, with clerks, for disputes between a weather-worker and a farmer whose orchard got hailed on. Odile sits on the council of vessels and complains about the clerks constantly. She says law is just magic's way of growing old." He smiled at the stone. "She says it fondly. Mostly."

A cart rattled along the road below, and he heard children shouting — the sound of the festival assembling itself, stalls going up, garlands of early wildflowers being strung along the market hall's eaves. In the old days the spring festival had been a small thing: bread and beer and a fiddler, the whole valley grateful simply to have survived another year inside its walls of silence. This year the roads were full. People came from beyond the edge now — mages with their registration papers folded in their coats, hedge-witches who had spent lifetimes pretending to be merely eccentric and could finally stop pretending, even a few of the curious unafflicted, come to see the place where the covenant had been reborn, to walk the Glass Steppe and feel nothing at all, which was, Quentin liked to say, the valley's particular gift to everyone.

He picked up the cup. The water trembled slightly, and he was honest enough with himself to admit his hand was doing the trembling.

*Survival was never the point.* Odile had said that to him once, months ago, sitting beside his sickbed while his body learned, agonizingly, to be an ordinary body again — to fever, to ache, to be vulnerable to every small poison the world brewed without malice. He had raged at her then, weakly, from the depths of the worst illness of his life. *Easy for you to say. You've never drunk for a living.*

And she had taken his hand, scarred and steady, and said: *No. I've healed for one. Same trade, different direction. We both spent our lives standing between other people and death. The difference is you thought the standing was the living.*

He stood up. Brushed the grass from his knees. Looked east, where the capital lay beyond the hills, where King Aldric sat a throne that no longer required a taster to die quietly in his place — though Aldric had asked, awkwardly, formally, whether Quentin might continue anyway, as witness rather than shield, and Quentin had surprised them both by saying yes. Some habits deserved to become institutions. Someone should sit at the king's table and remember, with every mouthful, that power is fed by ordinary hands.

But that was for next week. Today was the valley's.

Quentin raised the cup toward the boundary stones, toward the shimmer, toward all the lawful wonders waiting politely outside — and then turned, and raised it instead toward the valley itself, the long green floor of it, the market and the graves and the kitchens where he had once found a dead cook and understood that his safest room was a lie. Toward home, which had protected him by keeping the world out, and which was learning, slowly, painfully, joyfully, to open its gates instead.

"To my mother," he said aloud, "who kept me safe when safety was all there was. And to everyone who taught me that keeping safe was never the same as being alive."

He drank.

The water was cold and clean and tasted of rain and clay and nothing else — no almond, no hemlock, no debt coming due in his blood. It was the most dangerous thing he had swallowed in years, and it went down like a blessing, and he laughed, actually laughed, standing alone on the hill with an empty crooked cup, because it tasted like nothing, and nothing had never tasted so good.

* * *

By midmorning the festival had swallowed the valley whole.

Quentin walked the market road and let the crowd carry him. Children ran shrieking between the stalls with paper windmills that spun — some of them spun by wind, and some by small registered gifts, and it had become a game among the young ones to guess which, shrieking *craft!* or *breeze!* at each spinning pinwheel, and the parents pretended to disapprove of the noise and did not mean a word of it. A woman Quentin didn't recognize was telling fortunes at a corner table, honestly, with cards, no magic involved, and when a boy asked her why she bothered she said, "Because the future's more fun when it isn't certain," and Quentin stopped walking for a moment, struck, and decided he would steal that line for the opening of his next book.

Odile found him at the cider stall, as he knew she would. She looked well — better than well; the scars on her face caught the spring light and she wore them the way she wore everything, without apology. Her registration medallion, the new style, plain bronze with the covenant's interlocking rings, hung at her throat next to an older charm she refused to take off, a cracked luck-piece from the fugitive days.

"You're late," she said. "The vessel of the realm, tardy to his own valley's festival. There will be talk."

"There's always talk. Half of it used to be about whether I was cursed. Now half of it is about whether I'm *blessed*. I preferred the curses. Less pressure."

"Drink your cider," Odile said, and handed him a mug, and watched him do it — not testing him, exactly, but witnessing, the way she always did now, the way the mages who had once needed him for his blood now guarded him for his fragility. It had taken him a long time to stop flinching under that watchfulness. Longer to understand it wasn't pity. It was arithmetic, the kind Odile understood best: he had spent his whole life standing between others and death, and now they stood there too, and the ledger balanced, and no one kept score out loud.

"Any word from Voss?" he asked, because he asked every time, and Odile answered every time with the same shrug.

"She writes to the council. Dry reports. Excellent penmanship." A pause. "She sent a letter last week. To you, I think. It's at my tent."

Quentin looked at her. "You've been sitting on it since last week?"

"I wanted you to have it on a good day." Odile's mouth twitched. "Also I wanted to read it first. I'm old and nosy and I've earned it."

"What does it say?"

"That's yours to find out." She pressed the folded paper into his hand — he'd felt its weight in his coat pocket all morning without knowing what it was — and steered him firmly toward the dancing green before he could open it. "After. Some letters need music around them. Trust me. I'm a healer."

* * *

The dancing began after noon, the way it always had, with the fiddlers tuning and the old people claiming benches and the young people pretending they weren't going to dance and then dancing. Quentin danced twice — once with a widow who'd known his mother and told him three stories, two of which were probably true, and once with a girl of perhaps nine who had recently manifested a gift for making flowers open early and who gripped his hands very seriously and informed him that she had read about him in a book.

"You're in a book?" he said.

"My tutor has one. About the Rite. You're on the cover holding a cup and looking very serious."

"I don't look serious."

"You look extremely serious. My tutor says that's accurate."

He laughed until his ribs hurt, and the girl looked pleased with herself, and somewhere in the middle of the laughing Quentin realized that this — this exact moment, breathless and undefended and surrounded by people who knew precisely how killable he was and loved him anyway — was the thing his immunity had never once given him. Thirty years of surviving everything, and he had never survived a single afternoon this well.

As the sun leaned westward, he slipped away from the green and climbed back up the hill, to the grave, to the letter.

He sat with his back against his mother's headstone and unfolded Serane Voss's careful hand.

*I am not good at letters,* it began, *as you are probably aware from the reports Odile reads over your shoulder.*

*The witch-finders are dissolved. You know this. What you may not know is what I do now, which is little and strange: I testify. Before the covenant courts, about what we did during the Hunt — the pyres, the interrogations, the names. There are many names. I say them all, out loud, in rooms full of clerks. It is the least elegant justice imaginable and it takes forever and I think it may be the only kind that works.*

*My sister's town has a memorial stone now. Small. Local. The court ordered it and the townspeople paid for it themselves, which surprised me more than the order did. I visited in the autumn. I stood there for a long time expecting to feel nothing, because feeling nothing is what I am best at, and instead I felt — lighter. Not forgiven. Lighter. There is a difference and it took me fifty years to learn it and I am telling you because you are the only person I know who might understand that survival and peace are also different things.*

*You chose vulnerability in front of ten thousand people. I cannot yet choose it in front of one. But I am practicing. Consider this letter practice.*

*— S.V.*

Quentin read it twice. Then he folded it along its careful creases and put it in his coat, over his heart, and sat for a while watching the light go gold across the Steppe grass, and did not try to name what he felt, because some things did not need naming to be real.

"Practice," he said finally, to the stone, to his mother, to the whole listening valley. "That's all any of it is. The covenant, the cups, the courts. Practice." He smiled. "You'd have liked her, I think. Eventually. After about forty years."

* * *

Evening came down soft, and the festival lit its lanterns.

They strung them along the market hall and floated them on the mill pond, and this year — the second year of the covenant, the first year anyone dared — the lanterns rose. Not all of them. Just a dozen, lifted gently off the water by volunteers from the registry, mages who had come for the festival and asked permission first, formally, in writing, and been welcomed with bread and beer and slightly nervous laughter. The lights drifted up over the valley like slow golden birds, and the crowd below went quiet, and Quentin stood among his neighbors with an empty cup still in his hand and watched magic rise over the Glass Steppe like dawn arriving decades late.

Nobody panicked. That was the miracle nobody wrote songs about. Two years ago, a floating lantern would have meant screaming, denunciation, a herald's parchment, a pyre. Tonight it meant people pointing, and children cheering, and old Marta the baker saying loudly, "Well, about time," and going back to her beer.

Odile appeared at his shoulder with two more cups and handed him one. Water again. She didn't ask if he wanted it tested. Nobody asked anymore, and every single time it happened, Quentin felt the old flinch rise and fall, rise and fall, a tide going out further each season.

"To the valley," Odile said.

"To the valley," Quentin agreed. "Which kept me safe by keeping the world out, and is learning to keep me safe by letting the world in. Which is either a contradiction or a covenant. I haven't decided."

"It's both. Everything worth anything is both." She touched her cup to his. "Drink, taster. It's only water."

"It's only water," he echoed, and found that the words had become, without his noticing, the proudest sentence he owned.

They drank. Around them the festival roared and sang, the lanterns climbed, the fiddlers sawed, the children guessed at windmills, the fortune-teller dealt her honest uncertain cards. And Quentin Underhill — former taster, former bait, former vessel, current witness, friend, neighbor, man — stood in the open air of the only home he'd ever known, undefended, unarmored, killable as any sparrow, and felt no fear at all.

Somewhere beyond the hills, a letter waited in his pocket about a woman learning to practice. Somewhere ahead, a book waited to be written about a valley that learned to open its gates. Somewhere below, a girl who made flowers bloom early was teaching her friends the difference between breeze and craft, laughing, certain the world would hold her.

He raised his empty cup once more — to his mother, to Odile, to Serane, to the cook whose name he still said every morning, to everyone who had ever swallowed something bitter so someone else could eat in peace — and then he set it down, because it was empty, and because he was done drinking to survival.

The night was warm. The music played. And Quentin stepped down off the hill and into the crowd, alive, to live.
