## 1. The Valley Where Magic Cannot Follow

The first thing Rosalind Whitlock did every morning, before he opened his eyes, before he even allowed himself to be fully awake, was count.

Twelve years of counting. Twelve years of the same small litany, murmured into the dark behind his eyelids like a man checking the locks on a door that had never once been kicked in—but might be, any night now, and the checking was the only thing between him and ruin.

*No thunder. No pressure behind my teeth. My hands are only hands.*

He flexed his fingers under the wool blanket. Ordinary hands. Calloused, scarred from the forge, a burn mark on the left thumb shaped like a teardrop. A farrier's hands. He had spent twelve years making them true, or as true as they would ever be.

The cottage was cold in the way Greyhollow cottages were always cold—no hearth-charm to bank the coals overnight, no warming-stone tucked beneath the mattress, no whispered word to the fire to make it generous. Here, if you wanted heat, you built it with your own two hands and fed it wood you'd chopped yourself, and when the wind came down off the rim of the valley at night it came all the way in, because there was no warding-sigil on the doorpost to argue with it.

That was the whole point of Greyhollow. That was why the king's men had drawn their maps the way they had, and why the exile-writ had named this place specifically, in ink that looked almost apologetic: *the Valley Where Magic Cannot Follow.*

Rosalind got up, dressed in the dark by feel, and went outside to do what he did every morning of his life now, the thing that kept him alive and nameless and half a man: the ritual.

* * *

It wasn't much to look at. Anyone watching—and someone was always watching, in a valley of ninety-three souls where gossip moved faster than weather—would see nothing but a farrier taking his morning walk up the sheep path behind his forge, boots crunching frost, breath steaming. He stopped at the same flat stone he always stopped at, the one half-buried in the hillside above the village, and he crouched, and he pressed both palms flat against the rock.

And he listened.

Not with his ears. With whatever was still buried in him, coiled twelve years deep, sleeping the sleep he had taught it. He listened for the thing inside himself the way a man with a bad tooth probes it with his tongue—carefully, wincingly, needing to know.

*Still there?*

*Still there.*

It answered him the way it always answered: not with words, but with a sensation like the first hot day of spring felt through a closed window. A pressure. A restlessness. A vast green smell of rain that existed nowhere in Greyhollow, because Greyhollow got its water as gray drizzle and honest snowmelt and nothing else, ever, no matter what the sky beyond the rim was doing.

Greyhollow sat in a bowl of dead stone, ringed by hills the color of old iron, and something about the valley—the old-timers said it was the ore veins, the cold iron running through everything like gristle—drank magic the way sand drank water. Spells died here. Charms curdled. The gods, such as anyone remembered them, did not answer. A prayer spoken in Greyhollow was a letter posted to an address that had burned down.

For most people, this was simply a fact of geography, like the bad road or the short growing season. They complained about it the way farmers complained about anything, without meaning it.

For Rosalind, it was the wall between him and the rope.

He stayed crouched at the stone longer than usual today. Something had been different lately—he couldn't have said what, exactly, only that the sleeping thing inside him had begun to turn over in its sleep, the way a dog turns when it dreams. Twice this week he'd woken with the taste of lightning at the back of his throat, metallic and bright, gone as soon as he noticed it. Once, three nights ago, he'd caught himself humming a storm-chant under his breath while shoeing Marta Ellery's mule—a chant he hadn't thought of in twelve years, in a language he'd sworn he'd forgotten—and he'd bitten his tongue bloody to stop it.

Nothing was wrong. He told himself that. Nothing was happening. The valley was doing its work, drinking him dry every day, keeping him safe and small and human. The ritual was just ritual. Habit. An old soldier polishing a sword that would never be drawn again.

But he stayed crouched a little longer anyway, palms on the cold stone, listening to the silence inside himself and trying to convince it to stay silent.

* * *

Down in the village, the forge was already breathing when he arrived, because Tam Osgood had come early again. Tam was fourteen, all elbows and appetite, the son of the widow Osgood, and Rosalind had taken him on two winters ago mostly because the boy wouldn't stop showing up. He'd stand at the forge door in the snow until Rosalind gave in, which was how most things in Greyhollow got decided.

"You're late," Tam said, which was a lie, since Rosalind was early too, just less early than Tam.

"You're a liar," Rosalind said, and hung up his coat, and that was the whole conversation, and it was a good one. It was the kind of conversation he knew how to have. Talk about shoes and nails and the temper of iron. Talk about anything except the past.

The morning's work waited on the bench: four plow-shoes for the Hedley farm, a repair job on the miller's cart axle, and the mule again—Marta Ellery's beast had cracked a hind shoe on the scree path and Marta, who was seventy and sharp as a chisel, had informed Rosalind that if her mule went lame she'd be following shortly after, so he'd best treat the animal as family.

"Hot shoe or cold?" Tam asked, already working the bellows, because after two years the boy knew the rhythm of the day better than Rosalind did some mornings.

"Cold. She won't stand for the smoke today." Rosalind took up the rasp. "She remembers the last time."

"She remembers you dropped one on her foot."

"I remember that," Rosalind said. "She remembers it better."

Tam laughed, and the sound filled the forge the way sound fills a small stone room, bouncing and warm, and Rosalind worked, and for a while—for maybe an hour, maybe two—the morning was just a morning. Iron rang under the hammer. Frost melted off the south window. Somewhere outside, the village woke in its ordinary way: doors, dogs, the creak of the well-chain, old Bram Hedley coughing his way through his chores the way he'd coughed his way through them for a decade.

This was the life. This was the shape of it, the whole shape, top to bottom. A forge. A boy who needed teaching. Neighbors who called him *Whitlock* only because he'd had to give them something to call him, though he'd given them the surname like a false bottom on a merchant's cart—there was no Whitlock family anywhere in the census rolls of the Marches, he'd made sure of that before he ever walked down into this valley. Let them think he was from nowhere. Nowhere was safe. Nowhere had no records, no kin, no one who could look at a storm-cloud over the Steppe and say, *that's his work, I know his hand.*

Twelve years ago there had been another name, and another life, and a storm that had come out of a clear summer sky and killed a prince.

He didn't think about it. That was the discipline, the real ritual, deeper than the stone and the listening. He didn't think about it the way a man doesn't think about the open sea when he lives on a boat: constantly, structurally, with his whole body braced against the thinking.

* * *

At midday, Bram Hedley came to the forge, and Rosalind saw right away that something was wrong.

Bram was a big man gone soft at the edges, sixty-odd, with a laugh you could hear across two fields. Today there was no laugh. He stood in the doorway holding his cap in both hands, turning it, turning it, and his face had the gray, careful blankness of a man carrying news he hasn't decided how to set down.

"Rosalind." Bram never used the name lightly; nobody in Greyhollow did. Names here were thin currency, spent carefully. "It's Mother Cass. She's asking for you."

Mother Cass was not anybody's mother and had never been anybody's cass—she was Cassia Vole, eighty-one years old, keeper of the valley's one shared herb-garden and its entire accumulated medical knowledge, which fit in a wooden box and a head that was starting to misplace things. She had pulled Rosalind's tooth once, badly, and apologized for a week.

"Asking for me," Rosalind repeated. He put down the hammer. His chest did something strange—not fear exactly, but the neighbor of fear, the cousin who shows up uninvited. "Why me?"

Bram turned the cap. "She's dying, son. The lung-rot. You've seen it coming same as the rest of us." He swallowed. "She's been at it since dawn. Praying."

The word landed in the forge like a dropped horseshoe.

"Praying," Rosalind said.

"To the old ones," Bram said. "You know how she gets. Her mother's people were Steppe-folk, before the crown burned the temples. She says—" He stopped, embarrassed, the way people in Greyhollow were always faintly embarrassed by anything that smelled of magic, the way a man is embarrassed by a limp. "She says somebody ought to pray with her, and everyone else is too frightened, or too busy, or they think it's nonsense. But she asked for you specific."

"Specific," Rosalind said. His mouth was dry. "What did she say? Exactly?"

Bram looked at him then, really looked, and Rosalind understood with a cold drop in his stomach that Bram had carried more than a message up the hill. Bram had carried a question, and had been deciding all the way up whether to ask it.

"She said," Bram said slowly, "*send me the farrier. Send me the one who listens to stones.*"

The forge was very quiet. Tam had stopped working; Rosalind could feel the boy staring at his back. Outside, the grey drizzle ticked against the roof, patient as ever, magicless as ever, and somewhere beyond the valley's rim, beyond the iron hills, the world went on having weather with opinions.

"She's an old woman losing her mind," Rosalind said. "Old women say things."

"Aye," Bram agreed, too quickly, too easily, and turned his cap again. "Aye, that'll be it." But he didn't leave. He stood there a moment longer, and what was in his face wasn't suspicion—it was something sadder and more ordinary than that. Pity, maybe. Or the particular loneliness of a man standing at a deathbed door, wanting company and not caring much anymore what the company was hiding.

"She's asking for tonight," Bram said. "After dark. She says the praying's better after dark." He put his cap on, finally, as if that settled something. "You'll come?"

Rosalind heard himself say yes. He heard it from a long way off, the way you hear your own voice in a dream—agreeing to something before the part of you that calculates consequences can get a word in.

Bram nodded and went out into the drizzle, and the forge door swung shut behind him, and Tam said, from the anvil, "You listen to stones?"

"I lean on them," Rosalind said. "I'm a tired man. Tired men lean on things."

Tam looked at him for a long moment with those fourteen-year-old eyes that missed nothing and understood perhaps a third of it, and then, mercifully, went back to the bellows.

* * *

He finished the mule. He banked the fire. He ate bread and hard cheese standing up at the counter without tasting either, and all the while the litany ran underneath his thoughts like water under ice:

*No thunder. No pressure. Nothing has happened. Nothing is going to happen. An old woman is dying and old women reach for old gods the way hands reach for bedrails. It means nothing. It means nothing.*

Except.

Except that for twelve years, the sleeping thing inside him had slept like the dead, and this week it had begun to stir, and now, the very week it stirred, a dying woman was asking for him by a title no one should know he bore. Coincidence happened. Coincidence happened all the time. But he had learned, in another life, in a tower full of weather-mages arguing over omens, that coincidence was usually just causation wearing a mask.

And underneath the dread—this was the shameful part, the part he would never say aloud, the part he pressed down every morning along with everything else—underneath the dread was something that had no business being there.

Hope.

Twelve years since anything had answered anybody. Twelve years of the valley's holy silence, which had saved his life and starved him in the same breath. And now a woman was praying into that silence, and something in Rosalind's blood had lifted its head like a deer scenting rain, and he hated himself for the tiny, treacherous, half-alive feeling of it. Because hope was the first thing the storm had ever taken from him. Hope was what had made him believe he could call the rain gently, that he could be a servant of the sky instead of its weapon, that a boy from nowhere with too much lightning in him could matter without destroying anything.

Then the sky had opened over the royal progress, and the prince had drowned in his own saddle, and Rosalind had learned what his hope cost other people.

He banked the forge low. He told Tam to go home, and Tam went, looking back twice at the door. And when the dark came down over Greyhollow—real dark, total dark, the valley's darkness unrelieved by a single charm-light or witch-lamp, because there were none, because there could be none—Rosalind took a lantern and walked down through the village toward Mother Cass's cottage at the end of the lane.

Behind him, his shadow stretched long and thin in the lantern light, and for just a moment—one heartbeat, no more, long enough that he could tell himself later he'd imagined it—the shadow flickered. Not like a flame-flicker. Like a cloud-shadow crossing a field.

Like the shadow of something with wings.

Rosalind stopped in the middle of the empty lane. The drizzle fell. The village breathed around him, door by door, ember by ember. And from the last cottage, from behind a door left unlatched for him, he heard an old woman's voice, cracked and clear and utterly unafraid, rising into the dark:

"—and I know You're listening. I know it in my bones, which are the only honest parts of me left. So come, then. Come and see an old woman out. *Answer.*"

And in the silence that followed—in the dead, magicless, godless silence of the Valley Where Magic Cannot Follow—

the candle flames behind the cottage window all bent sideways at once, toward the door, as if bowing.

Rosalind stood in the lane with his lantern shaking in his hand, and the thing inside his blood opened one eye.
