## 2. A Prayer in the Smokehouse

The trouble with a valley that eats magic is that it also eats time.

Twelve years in Greyhollow and I could mark the seasons only by the work. Foaling season meant sleepless nights in the barns with lanterns hung low so the mares wouldn't thrash. Summer meant the forge running hot past dark, shoes stacked like black loaves on the cooling rack. Autumn meant the drovers coming down from the high pasture with their herds and their complaints—*this one throws a shoe every third mile, Whitlock, what are you even paying you for*—and winter meant quiet, and cold, and the particular loneliness of a man who has made himself into a tool because tools don't get asked questions.

I was good at being a tool. I want that understood. Twelve years of being nobody's storm-witch, just the farrier with the careful hands and the flat voice who never drank past the second cup and never stayed for the dancing at harvest. The valley had learned to trust me the way you trust a fence post. It doesn't ask where the post came from. It only asks that it hold.

Old Tam Ashdown held too, right up until the morning he didn't.

I'd known Tam since my first week in Greyhollow, back when I still flinched at my own shadow and checked the sky for riders out of the capital three times an hour. He'd been the one who put a hammer in my hand and said nothing about the way my knuckles went white around it, nothing about the burn scars up both forearms that no smith's accident explains. He'd fed me stew and told me which elders were worth flattering and which were worth avoiding, and when I finally asked him why he was kind to a stranger, he'd shrugged and said, *You looked like a man carrying something heavy. Figured you didn't need me asking what.*

That was Tam all over. He never asked what anyone carried. He just helped them set it down.

So when his granddaughter Bryn came pounding on my door before dawn, breath steaming, eyes red-rimmed, I was already pulling my coat on before she got the words out.

"It's Grandda," she said. "He's asking for—" and then she stopped, and I watched her decide not to say whatever she'd been about to say, and that frightened me more than anything else she could have told me.

" Asking for what, Bryn?"

"Nothing." She wiped her nose on her sleeve. "Come on."

* * *

The Ashdown place sat at the crook of the river where the valley floor pinched narrow, a long low house of grey stone with a smokehouse tacked onto its flank like a loyal dog. The smokehouse was Tam's pride. Forty years of hams and hanging sausages and apple-wood smoke, and he'd built it himself, he liked to say, back when his hands still did what he told them.

They carried him there because it was the warmest room he could be moved to without climbing stairs, and because he'd asked for it—or so they thought. Later I would understand that he hadn't asked for the smokehouse at all. He'd asked for something else, and grief had bent the words into something easier to hear.

Inside, the room was dim and close and smelled of salt and cured meat and woodsmoke, and underneath all of it, the smell I knew too well from the forge and from the fever tents during the coughing winter: the smell of a body finishing its work. Tam lay on a pallet against the far wall, propped on rolled blankets, and he was smaller than I remembered him being, though I'd seen him not two weeks past. Dying does that. It takes a man and folds him down into the space he'll leave behind.

His family crowded the walls. His wife had gone years ago; his sons stood together by the door like fence posts themselves, big men made awkward by helplessness; Bryn hovered near the pallet with a basin of water she kept dipping cloth into and wringing out and dipping again, as if the repetition itself might hold him here.

And Tam was talking.

Not to any of them. That was the first thing wrong. A dying man talks to his family, gives away his tools, argues about who gets the good plow horse. Tam was looking past all of them, past the rafters, past the roof, at some point in the air above the pallet, and his voice—a ruined whisper, but steady, steady as a man reciting something he'd memorized long ago and never needed until now—was saying:

"*Grandmother of the deep roots, hear me. Grandfather of the standing stones, hear me. You who walked before the crowned ones, you who kept the springs sweet and the winters short—*"

My blood went cold. Not metaphorically. I felt it happen, felt the warmth drain out of my hands and feet as if someone had opened a sluice-gate inside me.

Nobody prayed to the old gods anymore. That wasn't law—that was worse than law, that was simply *done*, the way you don't whistle indoors or speak ill of the dead at table. The old gods had gone silent centuries ago, and silence, in Greyhollow especially, was sacred. We lived in the one place in the world where the new gods' magic couldn't reach, where the court's witch-finders had no power to follow, and we owed that safety to the old gods' sleeping. Every child in the valley learned it before they learned letters: *the gods sleep, and while they sleep, no fire falls from the sky.* Praying to them wasn't blasphemy exactly. It was more like shaking a sleeping bear to ask it for honey.

But Tam Ashdown was dying, and dying men stop caring about bears.

"*—I have no gold to offer,*" he whispered. "*No blood, no name-day feast. I have only this: I remember you. Forty years I've smoked meat in this house and every year I burned the first strip for you, and never once did I forget, and now I'm going under the roots to wherever your people keep the dead, and I am asking—Grandmother, Grandfather—I am asking you to remember me back.*"

Bryn touched his shoulder. "Grandda, hush, save your strength—"

"I've plenty of strength for this," he said, and there was a flicker of the old stubbornness in it that almost made me smile. "Hush yourself, girl. Let an old man talk to his landlords."

A few of the family laughed, wet-eyed and grateful for it. I didn't. I was watching the candles.

There were four of them, tallow stubs in clay holders, one at each corner of the pallet—the valley custom, four lights so the dying man can find his way whichever direction the dark comes from. They had been burning steadily. And now, as Tam's prayer settled into the smoky air like ash into wool, all four flames leaned.

Not guttered. *Leaned.* All four, at once, toward the center of the room. Toward Tam. As if drawn by a draft—but the door was shut, the vents were closed, the smokehouse was sealed the way Tam had built it, airtight against the wind. There was no draft. There was nothing in that room moving but breath and smoke.

And frost.

It bloomed on the wall behind the pallet. I saw it start as a single point, a starburst no bigger than a coin, and spread outward in feathered arms, white ferns of ice crawling across grey stone in patterns I recognized with a sickness that started in my stomach and climbed my throat. Because those weren't random patterns. Frost doesn't make spirals. Frost doesn't make *rings*. But these were rings, concentric, precise, spreading out from behind Tam's head like ripples from a stone dropped in still water—and at the center of each ring, faint as breath on glass, a shape took form and dissolved and took form again. Something branching. Something rooted. Something that might have been a tree, or a woman with her hair full of branches, or both.

"*Remember me back,*" Tam whispered.

The candles went out. All four. No smoke trail, no sputter. Just darkness, total, and in the darkness the frost-rings glowed very faintly, the way snow glows under a moonless sky—not light, exactly, but the memory of light.

And inside my chest, behind the wall I had built brick by brick over twelve years of careful, gray, anonymous days, something turned over in its sleep.

I need to describe what that felt like, and I'm going to do it badly, because there are no words in the valley's plain speech for it and the words I used to know belong to a life I buried. It felt like a second heartbeat waking up below the first. It felt like the moment before lightning strikes, stretched out and folded small and tucked behind my sternum, and it was *rising*, pressing up against the inside of my ribs like water rising in a well shaft, and every hair on my arms lifted toward the frost on the wall the way iron filings lift toward a lodestone.

The storm-blood. My inheritance, my crime, my death sentence. Dormant for twelve years in the one valley in the world where it couldn't wake.

Waking anyway.

Because the old gods weren't asleep anymore. Or they weren't entirely. Or one of them had opened one eye at the sound of a dying smoker of meats who had never once forgotten to burn the first strip, and that eye had fallen through the world, through the wards and the quiet and the twelve years of my careful gray life, and found *me*—found the last storm-caller in the world standing in a smokehouse full of grieving family, and pressed against the inside of my skin like a hand testing a window latch.

I did the only thing I knew how to do. I did the dawn ritual, right there, standing among Tam's family in the dark, faster than I'd ever done it, silent, desperate. *I am Rosalind Whitlock, no—I am nobody, I am the farrier, I am a fence post, I hold, I hold, I hold.* I pictured the valley's boundaries, the ridgelines, the river gates, the great slow silence of the place, and I imagined my power as floodwater and the valley as a dam, and I pushed it down, down, down, the way I had every morning for twelve years, except this time it pushed back.

This time it *pushed back*.

For one heartbeat—one single contraction of my traitor heart—I felt the whole shape of it. The storm in me wasn't a thing I had. It was a thing I *was*, a weather system wearing a man's body, and it had been patient for twelve years the way winter is patient, and it knew, somehow, in the way weather knows a change in pressure, that the rules had changed. That somewhere beyond the ridgeline, the sky had begun remembering its own name.

Then Tam sighed.

It was a long sigh, unhurried, the sigh of a man setting down a heavy load after a long carry, and in the darkness I heard Bryn's breath catch, and heard one of the sons say, "Da?" in a voice cracked straight down the middle.

The candles relit.

All four, at once, no hand near them, flames standing tall and calm as if they'd never been out. And the frost on the wall was fading, the rings dissolving, the branching shape thinning to a ghost and then to damp stone and then to nothing at all—except, I noticed, and I would keep noticing for the rest of my life, except in one spot. At the exact center, where the first starburst had bloomed, a single small mark remained, no bigger than a thumbnail: a spiral, tight and perfect, like a fiddlehead fern, like a whorl in a fingerprint.

Like a signature.

Tam was breathing again. Slow, shallow, but even, and his face—which had been grey as the smokehouse stones—had come back to something like its old color, and when Bryn grabbed his hand he squeezed hers, weakly, and said:

"Told you. Plenty of strength."

The room erupted. Sons weeping, Bryn laughing and sobbing at once, someone fumbling flint to relight lamps that no longer needed relighting, someone else already saying they'd send for the herb-wife, clearly a miracle, obviously a miracle, the old man had been hours from the door and now look at him—

Nobody looked at the wall. Nobody looked at the candles. Nobody but me.

And nobody, thank every silent god, looked at me—because I had backed myself into the corner by the door, and I was holding my hands behind my back, and I could feel why. I could feel the sparks crawling under the skin of my palms, tiny static prickles, the ghost of a storm trying to ground itself through my fingertips. If I'd kept my hands visible, someone would have seen the blue-white shimmer dancing between my fingers like summer heat made visible. Like the first tremor before the thunder.

I got out of there. I don't remember deciding to; I remember the cold air hitting my face and the yard gravel under my boots and realizing I'd walked out mid-celebration without a word, and that people would talk about that later, and that I couldn't bring myself to care.

* * *

I walked the river path until the Ashdown place was out of sight, then farther, until Greyhollow village itself was a scatter of orange windows behind me, and then I stopped at the bend where the water goes quiet over the ford and I let myself feel it.

Dread first. Dread like a stone in the gut, cold and specific. Because I knew what it meant when the old gods stirred. I had learned it young, in another life, in a palace school where they taught the children of the storm-calling families the old histories alongside etiquette: *when the gods wake, the vessels wake with them.* We weren't practitioners. Practitioners borrowed power and gave it back. We were *vessels*—born hollowed and shaped for one purpose, and when our god stirred, we filled whether we wanted to or not. The storm hadn't asked my permission when I was nineteen. It wouldn't ask now.

And if it woke fully—if I woke fully—then the valley's protection failed. That was the other thing every child learned. The magicless quiet of Greyhollow wasn't a wall that kept magic out. It was a silence that kept the *sleepers* asleep, and I was the one sleeper who lived inside it. Twelve years, the elders had sheltered me—knowingly, I'd always suspected, though we never spoke of it—because a dormant vessel in the valley hurt nothing and the valley's mercy cost nothing. But a waking one? A waking storm-caller was a beacon. Witch-finders could track a vessel across a hundred miles of ordinary country. In the magicless valley they'd be blind.

Unless the blindness was ending.

The dread said: *run. Tonight. Take the bay mare, cross the southern pass before the thaw closes it, get ahead of this.* Twelve years of survival instinct, all of it screaming the same word.

But underneath the dread, quieter, in a voice I barely recognized because I hadn't heard it since I was young enough to believe the world might be kind—underneath it all was the other thing.

Hope.

Forbidden, rotten, wonderful hope. Because Tam had prayed, and something had answered. Not the new gods, with their gilded temples and their licensed miracles and their witch-finders. The *old* ones. The ones who kept the springs sweet. The ones my grandmother had whispered about, the ones whose names had been scrubbed from every church record in the kingdom, the ones who had supposedly gone under the roots forever. Something had heard a dying man with no gold and no blood-offering and had said, in frost and candlelight: *we remember you back.*

And if they remembered Tam—a farmer, a smoker of hams, a man whose entire devotion was one strip of meat a year—what else might they remember? What debts might they call in? What bargains might they honor?

I thought of Wren.

I hadn't let myself think of her in years. Thinking of Wren was like pressing on a wound to check if it still bled—it always did, so why check? But I stood at the ford in the pre-dawn dark with frost forming on the reeds around my boots—frost, in a sheltered riverside hollow, in late autumn, frost blooming in a neat ring exactly where I stood—and I thought of her anyway. Fourteen years old, furious, magnificent, standing in the ruins of the courtroom where they'd sentenced me, telling the Undercourt's black-robed steward in a voice that shook the chandeliers: *take mine instead. Take my soul as surety. He keeps his life, I keep my—whatever you want. Take it.*

She'd sold herself to the court beneath the mountain to pay my blood-price. Twelve years she'd been theirs. Twelve years I'd told myself she was beyond saving, that the Undercourt didn't trade, didn't forgive, didn't return, that hope was just pain wearing a prettier coat.

But the old gods were waking. And the old gods, my grandmother used to say, before the fires took her—*the old gods hate nothing in this world so much as they hate the courts that hoard souls.*

The frost ring at my feet pulsed. Once. Softly. Like a heartbeat under the ground.

I crouched and put my palm flat against it, and the static under my skin sang, and I swear to you—though I had no proof then and only story now—I swear the cold rose up to meet my hand like a dog lifting its head to a familiar footstep.

"All right," I said aloud, to the frost, to the river, to whatever had opened one eye beneath the world. "I see you. I hear you. But I'm telling you now, whoever you are—" and my voice cracked on it, twelve years of flat farrier's silence splitting down the middle, "—if you're waking up, then wake up all the way. Because I have a debt of my own. And I am done paying it in exile."

The frost melted. All at once, everywhere along the riverbank, as if the whole valley had exhaled.

Behind me, upstream, in the grey half-light before sunrise, a crow called—once, twice—and the sound of it followed me all the way home, and it did not sound like a bird.

It sounded like something that knew exactly what I'd just promised, and had come to collect.
