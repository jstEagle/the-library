## 3. The Crow That Knew My Name

The frost from Tam's smokehouse had melted by noon, but the story hadn't. It moved through Greyhollow the way stories always did—slowly at first, then all at once, like water finding a crack in a wall. By the time I'd finished shoeing the miller's bay mare, three separate people had found reasons to mention it to me.

"Old Tam's gone strange in the head," said the miller, holding his mare's lead while I worked the last nail. "Talking about voices in the smoke. Gods answering prayers. As if the gods ever listened to anyone out here."

"As if there were any gods to listen," his wife corrected from the doorway, though she crossed herself with the new-god sign anyway, the two-fingered one they'd brought up from the capital when the roads were built. Old habit. New god. Neither of them looked comfortable doing it.

I kept my eyes on the hoof and my voice level. "Smoke does strange things to a man's mind. All that dark, all that quiet."

"That's what I told him." The miller nodded, satisfied. "That's exactly what I told him."

But his wife was still looking at me, and her look had a question in it that I didn't want asked. She'd been at the well this morning when I came for water. She might have seen the way my hands shook around the bucket. She might have noticed that I'd taken no candle stubs from the community box, where everyone left them for the sick, because taking a candle felt too much like admitting something.

I finished the last nail, set the mare's foot down gentle as an apology, and straightened up slow so my back wouldn't announce itself.

"He'll be wanting his fee," I said. "Tell him I'll come by tomorrow with the bill."

"You're a good man, Whitlock," the miller said.

And there it was. My name. Not *my* name—the name I wore, the shell of it, the farrier's name I'd carried into this valley twelve years ago along with a forged letter of introduction and two silver pieces sewn into my coat lining. Whitlock. A good gray name, a stone-of-a-name, the kind of name you find on half the gravestones in any border province. I had chosen it from a list of the dead in a parish registry three valleys east, and I had worn it so long now that sometimes I answered to it before I remembered I was supposed to hesitate.

*Hesitate*, I reminded myself, even now. *Always hesitate. A guilty man remembers his name; an honest one just answers.*

"Whitlock," I agreed, and took my coins, and walked home through the long gold light of an afternoon that felt, for the first time in twelve years, like the held breath before a storm.

* *

My house sat at the valley's edge where the fields gave up and the scree began—a farrier needs to be near horses but away from people, I'd said, and nobody had argued, because nobody in Greyhollow wanted to live next to anybody if they could help it. Exile suits people who are tired of each other. The whole valley was built of folk who'd been somebody somewhere else: failed farmers, disgraced clerks, widows with convenient stories, a one-time smuggler or two. Nobody asked questions here. Asking questions meant inviting them back.

I shut my door, dropped the bar, and stood in the middle of my single room with my heart going like a smith's hammer.

*Something answered.*

Tam's words, from last night, still hanging in the smoke. I hadn't imagined the cold—I'd *felt* it, felt it the way you feel a hand on the back of your neck. And I hadn't imagined what happened after, either. The thing I'd told myself was exhaustion, the thing I'd blamed on bad air and old grief: that moment when the smoke seemed to lean toward the dying man, and something under my breastbone had leaned with it, and for one terrible half-second I had felt the shape of my own power the way a man feels an amputated limb. There. Aching. *Wanting.*

Twelve years of dawn rituals, and one prayer from a dying fool had cracked all of it open in a night.

I lit no lamp. I went instead to the loose flagstone behind the hearth and lifted it, and took out the little iron box I'd made myself in my first winter here, when the silence of the valley had seemed like a blessing instead of a sentence. Inside: a whetstone, a handful of coins I didn't need, a dried sprig of rowan that had long since lost its scent—and a child's wooden bird, carved clumsy and sweet, its wings spread as if caught mid-launch.

Wren's bird. She'd carved it the summer before everything, sitting on our mother's workbench with a knife too big for her hands, tongue between her teeth. "It's a storm-bird," she'd said, seven years old and certain. "It flies inside thunder. That's why you never see it. But it sees *you*."

I turned it over in my fingers until the edges bit, and I let myself remember her—not the bargain, not the ledgers, not the sound the Undercourt's agent's quill had made scratching her soul into collateral. Just her. Freckles like scattered sparks. The gap in her smile she refused to let anyone fix because she said it whistled when she laughed and laughter deserved music.

Then I put the bird away, and the box, and the flagstone, and I made myself do the ritual.

It wasn't much to look at. Cold water from the rain barrel, drunk down in nine swallows. Nine breaths, counted slow, each one released through the teeth. And then the words—not a spell, nothing so grand, just a rhythm I'd built out of horseshoe cadence and prayer-book fragments and sheer desperate repetition, a pattern with no meaning in it, which was the entire point. Meaning called things. Rhythm only kept them out.

*One-two-three, four-five-six, seven-eight-nine.* Breathe. *Again.*

Halfway through the third round, something knocked on my window.

Not tapped. Knocked. Three deliberate strikes of a beak against glass, spaced like a polite visitor.

There was no glass in my window. There never had been. Just oiled cloth stretched across the frame, which made the sound worse—soft, muffled, patient. Like knuckles.

I stopped counting breaths.

For a long moment the room was absolutely still, and I was absolutely still in it, and outside the evening light lay flat and gold on the scree, and nothing moved except the cloth, bulging slightly inward, as if something small and heavy had settled onto the sill.

Greyhollow had crows, same as anywhere. They stole grain and mobbed hawks and made their rude opinions known at dawn. In twelve years, not one of them had landed on my windowsill, because I'd spent twelve years being uninteresting, and uninteresting is armor.

I crossed the room. I pulled the cloth aside.

The crow stood on the sill with its head cocked, black as a hole cut out of the sky, and it looked at me with one eye, and the eye was wrong.

Crow eyes are black. Everyone knows that. This eye was hazel. Warm brown flecked with green, ringed in white the way human eyes get ringed with age or crying, set in the crow's feathered face with an accuracy that made my skin crawl—the whites showing, the pupil contracting against the low light, blinking sideways and upward with lids that shouldn't have existed. It was a person's eye. It was looking at me not the way a bird looks at a man but the way a *person* looks at someone they've been searching for.

"Shoo," I said. My voice came out thin.

The crow did not shoo. It opened its beak, and what came out was not a caw. It was a voice—small and rusted, like a hinge in a door long unopened, like wind through a keyhole—but it was a *voice*, and it said:

"Rosie."

The world went very narrow and very bright, the way it does in the half-second before lightning strikes.

Nobody called me Rosie. Nobody alive. It had been a child's name, a family name, a name coined by a gap-toothed girl on a workbench because her big brother was afraid of storms and she'd decided that naming the fear would tame it—"Rosalind Storm-Caller, Rosie the Thunder-Tamer"—and it had lived in exactly two mouths in all the world. Mine, and hers.

And hers had been sold. Written into a ledger beneath a mountain, priced against my exile, sealed in a vault where souls go to wait out contracts older than kingdoms. Wren. My sister. Twelve years dead-alive, and I had trained myself so thoroughly not to think of her that thinking of her now felt like tearing scar tissue off a wound that had never once healed underneath.

The crow watched me come apart with a dead girl's eye, and waited.

"Who are you?" I whispered. "What are you?"

It hopped once, closer. Up close I could see the feathers weren't quite feathers—there was a darkness to them that drank the light, a depth, as though each one were a window into someplace vast and cold and full of wings. When it moved, the shadows in the room moved a half-beat late, like an echo.

"Wren says," the crow rasped, in that rusted-hinge voice, and every word cost it visible effort, its throat working like a bellows, "*the thirteenth moon turns soon. She says you always counted moons badly, so count carefully. Thirteen turns, and the door shuts forever.*"

My legs decided for me. I sat down hard on the stool by the window, the one I mended harness in, and the crow hopped down onto the floorboards and stood there, absurd and impossible, tilting that hazel eye up at me.

"She says," it continued, "—and these are her words, farrier, I'm only the post—*you'll want to say it isn't real. You'll want to make it a dream, a trick, grief playing games. Don't. I waited eleven years to find a crack in the walls wide enough to send anything through, and I nearly broke the courier getting him to you, and if you waste this on doubting, I will haunt you, brother, I will haunt you specifically and with enthusiasm.*"

A sound came out of me that I didn't recognize. Half laugh, half something else entirely. Because that was her. That was exactly, precisely her—the fury dressed as jokes, the jokes carrying the fury, the little sister who had once threatened to haunt me over a stolen honey cake and had made good on it for a week of whispering through floorboards.

"She's in there," I said. "She's awake in there?"

"The court sleeps its souls, mostly. Sleeps them shallow." The crow's feathers rippled, unsettled. "Yours fought the sleeping. Yours has been *listening*. Twelve years of listening, farrier. She knows things in there that the court would burn vaults to keep quiet." It paused, and the hazel eye blinked, slow and human and unbearably weary. "And she knows what happens at the thirteenth turn. So do you, if you think. Debts come due. Ledgers close. What's written as forfeit becomes written as *property*."

The word landed in me like a nail driven to the quick.

Property. Her soul, stamped and shelved, owned by something that had never loved her, never heard her whistle through the gap in her teeth, never sat up with her through a fever telling storm-stories until she slept. Property. Forever. Because I had called a storm in grief and rage, and the price of a king's son's life had been paid in the only coin we had between us.

"I can't," I said aloud, to the crow, to the room, to the empty valley outside. "The valley doesn't let magic cross its bounds. That's the whole—that's why they put me here. If I leave, I'm *me* again. Everything I buried comes back, and I don't know if I can bury it twice."

"No," the crow agreed mildly. "You probably can't."

"It would take weeks to cross the Steppe. The court's agents watch every road. I have a forge. I have—horses. Clients." I heard my own voice climbing, listing, the litany of the small safe life I'd built out of exile's wreckage, and hated every word of it. "I'm a farrier. I'm nobody. That's how I've survived."

The crow was quiet a moment. When it spoke again, the rusted voice had gone gentler, which was somehow worse.

"She said you'd say that too. She said, tell him: *you were never nobody, you were just hiding where nobodies go. And I know the difference, because I've spent twelve years being actually nobody, and it's terrible, and I'd like my brother back.*"

I put my face in my hands.

Outside, the light was failing. The scree slopes went blue, then violet, then dark, and the first stars came out over the ridge where the valley's boundary stones stood in their ancient silent ring—the stones that had swallowed my power the day I crossed them, that had held me in this magicless quiet like a jar holds a firefly, safe and dim and slowly suffocating. Somewhere down-valley, Tam was dying, and had prayed, and something had answered. Something was waking. The candles in a smokehouse had guttered, and frost had bloomed in midsummer, and the world's locked doors were coming unlatched one by one, all across the land, and I had felt it in my chest like a second heartbeat.

And a crow with a stolen eye stood on my floorboards, delivering mail from the dead.

"Why you?" I asked finally. "Why a crow? Why not a dream, a vision, anything?"

"Because dreams are the court's roads," the crow said. "They patrol them. Visions belong to the new gods, and the new gods are bought." It ruffled, settled. "Crows are nobody's. Crows belong to the space between things—between fields, between lives, between the living and whatever waits after. There's an old power that keeps the crows. Older than courts. Older than crowns. He heard a soul screaming in a vault, and he thought: *that one's interesting.*"

"And what does he want? Nothing's free. Especially not gods."

The hazel eye glittered.

"Straight to business. Wren said you'd do that as well." It hopped to the window sill, gathering itself, and the shadows in the room leaned after it reluctantly. "He wants what all the old ones want, farrier. To matter again. To be *needed*. The world forgot them, and forgetting starves a god thinner than any chain." The crow paused at the edge of the sill, half-turned, that human eye finding mine one last time. "So. Count your moons. Sharpen what needs sharpening. And when the crows come again—come louder—don't bar the door."

"What crows? What door? Wait—"

But the crow simply stepped backward off the sill into empty air, and the air accepted it the way water accepts a stone, without comment, without ripple, and the twilight closed over the place where it had been as though nothing had ever perched there at all.

I sat in the dark for a long time. Long enough for the stars to wheel. Long enough for the cold to reach my bones and find, to my horror, that it didn't feel foreign anymore—it felt like company, like the first touch of a power I'd spent twelve years drowning, rising in me now with the patience of deep water.

Finally I got up, lit the lamp, and took out the wooden bird again.

Storm-bird. Flies inside thunder. Sees you, even when you can't see it.

"Thirteen turns," I said to the little carving, to my sister, to the listening dark. "All right. I'm counting."

And far off, beyond the boundary stones, past the sleeping valley, out on the black shoulder of the world where the Glass Steppe began—thunder rolled. Once. Softly. Almost politely.

Like a knock.
