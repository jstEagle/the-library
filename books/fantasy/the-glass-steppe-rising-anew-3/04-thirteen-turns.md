## 4. Thirteen Turns

The crow came back at dusk.

It landed on the fence post outside the forge like it had always lived there, head cocked, one eye catching the last light—and that eye was wrong again, amber where it should have been black, human where it should have been a bird's. Rosalind set down his hammer. His hands were steady. Twelve years of practice had taught his hands steadiness even when everything under his skin was screaming.

"You're not going to tell me anything else, are you," he said.

The crow said nothing. Crows don't. But it didn't leave either, and when he went inside to eat his bread and cold beans, it hopped along the fence line after him, and when he shut the door it settled on the sill like a gargoyle with feathers, watching him through the gap in the shutter.

*Wrenlet.*

Nobody alive called him that. Nobody alive *knew* that name. He'd been Rosalind Whitlock for twenty-two years and then he'd been nothing at all—no name on the valley rolls, just *the farrier*, *the smith*, *you there*. Greyhollow kept no records of exiles. That was the point of the place. You came in as whatever you said you were, and the valley's dead air pressed down on your magic until even your own past felt like a story someone else had told you.

But Wren had called him Wrenlet because she was three years older and had named him herself before their mother could stop her, and she'd never once used his real name after that, not even when the magistrates were dragging him away in chains, not even then. She'd shouted it across the courtyard. *Wrenlet! Wrenlet, look at me!* And he had looked, and what he'd seen in her face had kept him alive through twelve years of quiet.

He sat with his untouched supper and listened to the frost tick against the window, and he understood, finally, with a cold certainty that settled into his bones like the winter itself: something had answered her prayer in the smokehouse because something was answering prayers again everywhere. The old gods were waking. And if they were waking—if Corvass or whoever wore that crow's eye had reached across twelve years and a valley's worth of silence to find him—then they knew about Wren too.

They knew where she was.

He hadn't let himself think about where she was in longer than he could count. Thinking about it was like pressing a bruise; the pain was proof of life but it bought you nothing. She had sold her soul to pay his blood-price. That was the arithmetic of it, clean and terrible. A king's heir dead by storm. A storm called by a boy who hadn't known his own strength. The crown demanded a life for a life, and Rosalind's life was forfeit—but the Undercourt, which had come sniffing around the edges of the trial like vultures around a battlefield, had offered an alternative ledger. Not his death. Something better, the Undercourt's advocate had purred. Something *lasting*.

Wren had signed before the ink on his sentence was dry.

He'd screamed at her. He remembered that with awful clarity—the chains, the stone floor, his voice cracking apart. *Don't you dare. Don't you DARE.* And she had looked at him the way she used to look at him when he was small and feverish, that fierce unbearable tenderness, and she'd said, "You're the one with the gift, Wrenlet. I'm just the one who can carry things." As if her soul were a sack of grain. As if it weighed nothing.

Then the guards took him to a wagon, and the wagon took him east, and the mountains closed behind him, and the magic drained out of the world.

That was the last time he'd seen her face.

* * *

He slept badly, which was to say he dreamed, and the dreams were the old ones, the ones he'd spent a decade drowning.

The night of the storm. He was seventeen and furious, and the fury had a shape he could feel in his teeth. Prince Aldric Vane—young, golden, cruel in the way only beloved men can afford to be—had come through their river town on progress with his retinue, and he'd decided, on a whim, that the farrier's daughter had a pretty mouth and should use it. Wren had told him no. Aldric had laughed, and his friends had laughed, and one of them had put a hand on her arm, and Rosalind—who had spent his whole life swallowing the static that lived in his blood, who had been taught by their mother to breathe slow and think of gray things, of ash and slate and river mud—had stopped breathing slow.

He remembered the sky going green. He remembered the taste of copper. He remembered that he hadn't even meant to do it—that the storm had simply risen out of him like water out of a broken well, that his rage had found the clouds overhead and *recognized* them, and the clouds had answered like dogs hearing their master's whistle. The lightning had walked across the festival square on legs of white fire. It had taken the prince off his horse and left him smoking on the cobbles, and it had gone on and gone on, because Rosalind couldn't stop it, because no one had ever taught him how, because the kingdom that executed storm-touched children instead of teaching them had gotten exactly what it cultivated.

Three others died that night. He counted them every day for years. A baker. A child. A guardsman who'd only been reaching for his daughter's hand.

And then the trials, and then the Undercourt's advocate sliding out of the shadows of the courtroom like oil off water, offering the crown a bargain sweeter than blood: the storm-caller exiled to Greyhollow, where his power would be smothered forever, and the debt sealed with collateral. *A soul freely given,* the advocate had said, bowing toward Wren. *The court finds such currency... durable.*

Freely given. He'd learned later what those words meant in the Undercourt's grammar. Nothing in that place was free. Everything was owed.

He woke before dawn with his heart slamming and the crow still on the sill, watching him with its borrowed eye, and he thought: *you've been sent. Someone sent you. Which means someone is keeping accounts, and I want to know whose ledger my sister is written in.*

* * *

He didn't go to the forge that morning. For the first time in twelve years, he broke his own ritual—not the dawn working, he did that, he knelt in the frost and breathed the gray breath and pressed the storm down flat—but after it, instead of iron and horseshoes, he walked up through the village to Old Marta's door, because Marta had been dying slowly for a season and dying people hear things, and Marta had prayed aloud in the smokehouse two nights ago and the world had *answered*.

She was propped up on pillows by her window, thin as kindling, eyes bright as a girl's. The frost-ferns from that night still clung to the inside of her glass in places that never thawed, delicate white spirals that no fire would touch. She watched him come in with an expression he couldn't read.

"Heard you fixed the Hendricks' plow horse," she said. "Heard you did it for nothing."

"Their boy's got a bad lung. They need the horse more than I need coin."

"Mm." She studied him. "You know what I prayed for, that night in the smokehouse?"

"I heard you."

"Then you know I didn't pray for myself." Her hands worked at the blanket's edge. "I prayed for this valley. Twelve years we've been nothing here, Rosalind—" and he flinched, because she'd said his name, his true name, plain as bread, and she saw him flinch and smiled like a knife. "Oh, don't. You think I don't know? I've known since the week you came. A man doesn't flinch like that at thunder unless he's got weather in him. But the valley keeps its secrets, and so have I, all these years. Because you shoe our horses and mend our plows and you've never once asked for thanks, and that's worth more than any magistrate's wanted notice."

"Old woman," he said carefully, "if you know what I am, why are you telling me now?"

"Because the gods are awake, boy, and they don't care what we keep secret." She turned her face to the window, to the frost spirals. "I prayed for rain for the spring planting. And something answered. Something *old*. And it said—I don't know how it said, there were no words, but I knew—it said the doors are opening. All the doors. And it said the court beneath the mountain is afraid."

Rosalind went very still. "What court?"

"The one your sister paid." Marta's eyes came back to him, and they weren't dim at all. "You think Greyhollow doesn't remember you? We remember everyone. We're a valley of forgotten people; remembering is all we have." She coughed, wet and long, and waved off his help. "There was a peddler came through last autumn, before the waking. Half-mad, talked to himself. But he'd come down off the Steppe, and he said the mountain had opened its gates early this year. Said the Undercourt was calling in debts. Said there was a turn coming—a thirteenth turn of the moon—and when it completed, every soul held in their vaults would be *sealed*. Pressed into the ledger permanent. No ransom. No recall. Forever."

The room tilted. Rosalind caught the bedframe.

"How long," he heard himself say. "How many turns since autumn?"

Marta counted on her fingers, lips moving, and he watched each finger rise like a nail being driven into a coffin. "Twelve," she said. "Come the next full moon, it makes thirteen."

* * *

He walked home without knowing how his feet did it. The crow lifted off the fence and paced him overhead, a black stitch moving across gray cloth, and the whole way he could hear Wren's voice from the courtyard, *Wrenlet, look at me*, and underneath it, newer, colder, the advocate's oiled voice: *a soul freely given, the court finds such currency durable.*

Thirteen turns. One left.

For twelve years he had survived by not thinking of her. By folding her into the same box as the storm, as the prince, as the three dead strangers—as everything he'd done and everything it cost—and sitting on the lid. That was the ritual under the ritual. The gray breath wasn't just for the storm-blood. It was for the grief. Breathe slow. Think of gray things. Do not open the box.

He opened the box now, standing in his own doorway with the frost ticking against the glass, and he let it hurt the way it should have hurt for twelve years, all at once, full flood. Wren at nine, teaching him to skip stones. Wren at fourteen, standing between him and their father's belt. Wren at nineteen, signing her name in a courtroom while he screamed. Wren in the dark somewhere beneath a mountain, twelve years in a vault, waiting for a brother who had never once tried to come for her.

Never once tried. Because trying meant admitting she'd needed saving, and admitting that meant admitting it was his fault, and—

No. He was done with that arithmetic. It had never been honest. Guilt wasn't penance; guilt was just another way of hiding. He'd hidden in Greyhollow for twelve years and called it exile, and she had paid the price of his hiding with the only thing she had.

The anger came up through the grief like heat through snow, and it was cleaner than anything he'd felt in a decade.

"One moon," he said aloud. His breath smoked. "One moon, and I'm coming."

The crow dropped out of the sky and landed on the gatepost in front of him, close enough to touch. Up close, the amber eye had depth to it, layers, like light through honey. It regarded him for a long moment. Then it dipped its head once—precise, deliberate, unmistakable—and hopped twice along the fence rail toward the east, toward the pass, toward the Steppe, and looked back over its shoulder.

An invitation. Or a summons. With gods, he suspected, the difference didn't matter.

"I'll need to cross the boundary," he said. "The moment I do, the valley's dead air lets go of me, and everything comes back. Everything." His hands flexed at his sides. He could already feel it, anticipatory, like the pressure before a summer storm—the static crawling up his forearms, the clouds over the eastern peaks gathering themselves the way dogs gather at a whistle. Twelve years of suppression, and on the other side of that ridge, all of it at once. "I haven't called weather since I was seventeen. The last time I called weather, four people died."

The crow said nothing. Crows don't. But it held his gaze with that human eye, and he had the sudden, unshakable sense of being *assessed*—of something vast and patient weighing him the way a moneylender weighs coin, checking for cracks, checking for weight.

Then it cawed, once, harsh and ordinary, and flew east, and did not look back again.

Rosalind stood in his yard until the cold drove him inside. Then he did what he'd done only once before in his life: he began to prepare for a journey he might not survive. He banked the forge fire low. He sharpened the knife his mother had carried. He wrapped his father's hammer—he'd never used it for horseshoes, never trusted himself to hold it—in oilcloth and bound it to a pack frame. He ate everything in the larder, because a body walking into the Steppe in winter needed fuel, and he laid out his good coat, the one with the mended elbow, and he slept four hours dreamless and woke before dawn.

At first light he knelt in the frost and performed the gray breath one final time—and this time, halfway through it, he stopped. Deliberately. Let the breath go ragged. Let the static rise.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. Greyhollow's silence pressed it flat. But he could feel it down there, coiled and waiting, all the weather he'd ever swallowed, and for the first time in twelve years he didn't press it back down. He just left it there, like a blade left loose in its sheath, and stood up, and shouldered the pack.

The elders would call it treason. Leaving the valley was permitted—Greyhollow's cruelty was subtler than walls—but leaving with intent, leaving *toward* power, a storm-witch walking out of the one place that could hold him? They'd send word ahead. There were warrants older than his exile, and names on them.

Let there be names. He was done being nameless by choice.

He paused at the gate with his hand on the latch and looked back once at the forge, at the little house, at the fence rail where a crow had sat. Twelve years. The longest half-life anyone had ever lived on purpose.

"One moon," he said again, quieter, and it wasn't a deadline anymore. It was a promise.

Then he opened the gate and started east, toward the ridge, toward the boundary, toward the cold water of his own returning power—and toward the figure he could just make out on the road beyond the last farmhouse, riding a gray horse through the frost mist. A rider in travel-stained white, armored at shoulder and forearm, with a sword slung across her back and a broken crescent moon stamped into the breastplate.

A paladin. Here, in Greyhollow, where no god's servant had any business being.

The crow wheeled overhead, laughing its harsh laugh, and Rosalind Whitlock walked out to meet his fate with his father's hammer on his back and one moon left to spend.
