## 5. The Paladin of the Broken Order

Sera Vane had been riding for eleven days, and the horse hated her.

Not in the way horses usually hated things—the ordinary grievances of bit and spur and bad weather. This was a deeper, more principled resentment. Moon-blessed mounts could feel an oath-breaker the way dogs felt thunder. Hers had been snubbing her hand at every water crossing since the pass.

"You and everyone else," Sera muttered, and swung down at the lip of the valley road.

Greyhollow opened below her like a held breath. She had seen it on maps—a green notch in the eastern mountains, marked with the cartographer's shorthand for *dead land*: no ley-lines drawn, no shrine-symbols, not even the little crosses where holy men had died. The maps were right. As she rode down through the terraced barley fields, she felt the silence settle over her like wet wool. Her moon-blessing, which lived in her blood as a low silver hum, went quiet. Not gone—she could still feel it, coiled somewhere beneath her sternum—but muted, as if someone had wrapped her heart in felt.

Twelve years ago, this valley had been chosen as a prison precisely because of that silence. Magic could not follow you here. Neither, apparently, could grace.

She passed a shrine at the first crossroads. It was a small stone thing, older than the kingdom, its carved goddess worn faceless by weather. Someone had left an offering anyway: a clay cup of milk going to skin in the cold. Sera reined in and looked at it longer than she should have.

*Old gods,* the reports said. *The old gods are stirring.* Three shrines in the western provinces had lit their own votive flames. A well in Marrowdale had answered a child's prayer with clean water where there had been brack. And here, at the edge of nowhere, a farmer's wife swore that frost had bloomed indoors on a warm night, in patterns like feathers, while a dying man prayed aloud.

The Order of the Silver Veil would have dispatched a full conclave once. Six paladins, white-cloaked, singing the Rite of Witness. But the Order of the Silver Veil was a ruin with good posture, and what remained of it sent one disgraced knight on one tired horse, because no one else would come.

"Fine," Sera told the valley. "I'm nobody too."

* * *

Greyhollow smelled like every poor farming town: woodsmoke, wet earth, animal sweat, bread. What it didn't smell like was magic, which was exactly how she knew something was wrong.

She put up at the inn—the only inn, called the Hearthstone, run by a widow named Maren who talked enough for three people and asked nothing for the room beyond coin and company. Sera paid for both. She let Maren talk. In twenty minutes she learned the harvest schedule, three feuds, the price of horseshoes, and the fact that Greyhollow kept exactly one farrier.

"Whitlock," Maren said, refilling her cup unasked. "Came here—oh, must be twelve years back now. Quiet man. Best hands in three valleys, I'll say that. You'd never catch a horse of his shying at a nail." She leaned in, conspiratorial. "Odd one, though. Keeps to himself. Lives above his forge like a monk. And he does a strange thing at dawn—I've seen it from my window, before the mist burns off. Stands out there with his bare feet in the dirt and his arms out, just so, like a man holding a door shut. Every morning. Rain or snow."

"Holding a door shut," Sera repeated.

"That's what I thought! My late husband said it was stretching. But my husband also said the barley failed because of gophers, so."

Sera smiled the small smile that made people tell her more, and Maren told her more: the farrier took no apprentices, attended no festivals, drank exactly two cups of ale at market days and left. He'd once pulled a drowning boy from the mill-race in winter, diving into water that should have killed him, and come out steaming like a kettle—and then been sick for three days after, gray-faced, as if the act of surviving his own strength had poisoned him.

"And his name," Sera said carefully. "Whitlock. That's all?"

Maren shrugged. "Just Whitlock. Though old Tam swears he heard the tax-man call him Rosalind once, and got a look for it that'd freeze a forge. Rosalind! Can you imagine? Big scarred fellow like that." She laughed. "Anyway. If your horse needs shoeing, he's your man. Everyone's horse needs shoeing eventually. It's practically scripture here."

It was, Sera reflected later, almost too easy. Twelve years hiding in a valley that erased magic, and he'd built a life with a door wide enough to drive rumor through. Unless he wasn't hiding anymore. Unless whatever woke the gods had woken him too.

She slept badly, in the way of people whose vows had been broken and replaced with work.

* * *

The forge sat at the town's edge, where the cobbles gave up and became mud. Smoke rose from it straight and thin in the windless morning. Sera left her horse tied at the rail—the animal shot her a look of pure vindication—and walked in under the great oak lintel.

He was working when she entered. That was her first impression: *working*, in the full sense, the whole body given to it. He was a big man gone lean with years, forearms like cable, dark hair shot with early gray and bound back carelessly. An old burn scar climbed the left side of his neck. He stood over an anvil with a glowing bar of iron and did not look up.

"I'll be with you directly," he said. His voice was low and even, a voice used to being careful.

"Take your time." She leaned against the doorpost. "I'm told you're the best hands in three valleys."

"People say things."

"They do." She watched him draw the bar out, set it, strike. Each blow rang through the little stone building and came back off the walls, and Sera noticed—because noticing was the only skill she had left—that the sound didn't echo quite right. It rang twice. Once for the iron, and once for something underneath the iron, faint and far away, like a bell rung in another room. "They say you pulled a boy out of the mill-race last winter."

His hammer paused. Half a heartbeat. Then it fell again. "Water's cold here. Makes a man quick."

"Mm. They say you came out steaming."

"Body heat. Big men steam." He turned the bar, struck again. Still not looking at her. "You've ridden a long way to repeat gossip. Your accent's court-north. Your cloak's cut for a uniform but the badges are gone—you can see the ghost of them where the sun hasn't faded the wool. Paladin, or was. So either you're hunting me, or you've nowhere else to be. Which is it?"

So much for the quiet farrier. Sera pushed off the doorpost and walked fully into the forge, into the heat and the orange light, and stopped where he could see her if he chose.

"My name is Sera Vane," she said. "I was a Knight-Vigilant of the Silver Veil. I held the Third Oath and broke the First, and I was stripped and cast out for it, and now I do errands for what's left of the Order. This is the errand." She let the silence stretch. "The old gods are waking. Prayers are being answered all along the mountain provinces. The Order wants to know why. And there's a story about this valley—twelve years old—about a storm-caller who killed a king's heir and was exiled to the one place magic couldn't reach. I'm told the storm-witch is dead. I'm told it often. I'm rarely told true."

The hammer came down and stayed down. The bar of iron cooled between them, its glow dying from orange to black, and in the sudden stillness Sera heard it again: that second ringing, that bell-in-another-room. Except now it wasn't in the iron. It was in him. A hum beneath his skin, faint as a struck tuning fork, and getting fainter by the second as he mastered himself—as he *held the door shut*.

When he finally looked up, his eyes were the color of a sky deciding.

"You're wrong about one thing," Rosalind Whitlock said quietly. "This valley doesn't erase magic. It starves it. There's a difference. Starving things learn patience." He set the hammer down with deliberate care. "Now. You've said what you are. Say what you want, plainly, because I've spent twelve years learning to hear the lie under a sentence, and I'm very good at it."

"The truth?" Sera crossed her arms. "I don't know yet. Yesterday I wanted to find the storm-witch and drag her to the capital in chains, because that was my duty and duty is the last thing I own. Today I've watched frost bloom where prayers go up, and I've heard iron ring wrong in your forge, and I've realized the Order sent me here with a question they already fear the answer to." She tilted her head. "So I'll ask the farrier instead of the witch. Have you noticed anything strange lately? Crows watching you. Frost in odd places. Dreams of bells?"

Something moved behind his eyes—there and gone, a shutter closing over a window with light behind it.

"No," he said.

"There it is," Sera said softly. "That pause. You know, I interrogated war criminals for six years. Innocent men answer fast, because they have nothing to weigh. Guilty men weigh. You just weighed."

"And you," he returned, "ask questions like a woman reading a confession she's already written. So we're both liars, Vane. That makes us nearly honest." He picked up the tongs and thrust the cooled iron back into the coals, and the flare of light threw his face into relief—tired, hard, and underneath it, so briefly she might have imagined it, afraid. "Your horse needs shoes. Front pair, worn to the quick. Leave her till tomorrow and I'll do it. Then get out of my valley."

"It's not your valley."

"It is," he said, turning back to his work, "the only thing anyone's ever let me keep. Go."

* * *

She didn't go.

She spent the day doing what she did instead of sleeping: walking Greyhollow's edges, counting things. Counting was prayer, the Order taught, when words failed. She counted seven shrines, all ancient, all recently cleaned of moss by unseen hands. She counted the offering cups—four of them fresh. She counted crows: a dozen roosting on the meeting-house roof, silent, all facing the same direction.

All facing the forge.

At dusk she climbed the hill behind the Hearthstone and watched the farrier perform his dawn-ritual at dusk instead, out of rhythm, agitated—bare feet in the cold dirt, arms slightly raised, head bowed, holding his invisible door. Even from the hill she could see the effort of it, the tremor in his shoulders, the way the ground around his feet stayed impossibly dry in the drizzle, in a perfect circle, like something underneath him refusing to be rained on.

And above him, on the forge roof, sat a crow.

It wasn't doing anything. That was what made Sera's skin crawl. It simply sat, in the rain, unmoving, its head cocked, and even at this distance she could see that one of its eyes caught the last light wrong—not crow-black but a pale, human gray, glinting like a coin at the bottom of a well.

The old gods were waking. She had believed the reports the way she believed dispatches—professionally, without heat. But this was different. This was a god's attention resting on one man in one muddy valley like a thumb pressed to a bruise, patient as only immortal things are patient.

And the man was spending everything he had to hold the door shut against it.

Sera sat down in the wet grass and thought about oaths. Her First Oath had been simple: *protect the innocent, even from the righteous*. She had broken it to protect the innocent from the righteous, and the Order had never forgiven the arithmetic. Since then she'd kept a smaller oath, private and mean: *see clearly, act truly, owe no one*. Seeing clearly was all she had left, and what she saw now was this: a hunted man starving his own soul in a hole in the world, a god waiting outside his door with something in its beak, and a thirteenth-moon deadline ticking somewhere in the dark that she couldn't see yet but could feel, the way horses felt thunder.

Her duty said: report the storm-witch found alive. Seal the valley. Bring the conclave, such as it was.

Her oath said: *even from the righteous.*

Below, the farrier finished his ritual and went inside, and the light in his forge burned late, and the crow never moved, and the circle of dry earth slowly filled with rain.

Sera Vane lay back in the grass and looked at the clouds breaking apart over the eastern peaks—and there, through the gap, the moon rose fat and pale and one night shy of full, and she counted, out of habit, out of prayer, the turns since the last dark of the moon.

She stopped counting at twelve.

"Oh," she said, to no one, to the valley, to whatever was listening. "Oh, that's not good."

Down in the town, in the forge that rang twice, a man was banking his fire for the night—and every crow in Greyhollow lifted off the meeting-house roof at once, silent as a held breath, and wheeled east, toward the mountains, toward the Steppe, like smoke running from a fire.
