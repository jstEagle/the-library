## 6. An Arrest That Isn't

Sera Vane had made forty-one arrests in her years with the Order of the Silvered Moon, and every one of them had begun the same way: hand on the hilt, oath on the lips, the words of the Rite of Seizure spoken clean and certain so that the person being taken knew exactly what was happening to them and why. *By the moon's pale witness, you are bound.* Forty-one times she had said it. Forty-one times she had meant it.

She stood now in the doorway of a farrier's forge in the magicless valley of Greyhollow, dawn light slanting through the smoke-holes, her hand nowhere near her sword, and found she could not make her mouth form the words.

The farrier stood on the other side of his anvil, and between them the air still shimmered faintly with what he'd done—or what had been done through him—less than an hour ago. She'd felt it from the inn: a pulse like a struck bell, a taste of ozone on a morning that should have tasted of nothing at all. Greyhollow was a dead valley. That was its whole purpose. The crown had hollowed it out generations ago, scraped it clean of the divine the way you'd scrape mold from bread, and dumped the god-touched here to rot in quiet. Nothing answered prayers in Greyhollow. Nothing woke.

Except something had woken this morning, and it had woken wearing a farrier's face.

"You felt it too," Rosalind Whitlock said. Not a question. His voice was low and even, the voice of a man who had spent twelve years practicing how not to be noticed, but his hands—she watched them carefully, the way she'd been trained to watch hands—his hands were trembling against the anvil's edge. "I can see it in your face. You came through my door like a woman walking into a burning house."

"I came through your door," Sera said, "because I'm a paladin of the Silvered Moon, and I have sworn vows about exactly this."

"About farriers?"

"About the god-touched." She stepped inside properly, letting the door swing shut behind her. The forge was small, immaculate, obsessively ordered. Tongs hung by size. Nails sorted into clay pots. A man who controlled everything he could control because there was one thing he couldn't. "And about what happens when the god-touched stop being quiet."

He didn't flinch. She gave him credit for that. Most people, hearing what she was—the silver crescent at her throat, the moon-blessing that still clung to her like a scent most days—most people flinched, or groveled, or ran. This man just looked tired, the way a soldier looks when the war he thought was over sends him a letter.

"Then do what you came to do," he said.

*By the moon's pale witness, you are bound.*

The words sat right there, ready. She'd said them in her sleep. She'd said them over children, once—no. She put that away. That was a different night, a different failure, and she was not going to think about the village of Ashenfen while standing in this forge deciding whether to destroy the second innocent life the Order's doctrine had ever put in front of her.

Because that was the thing, wasn't it. That was the crack running straight down the middle of her certainty. She had spent three weeks tracking rumors out of Greyhollow—a storm-calling, they whispered, a storm-calling after all these years—and she had ridden through the pass expecting a monster. That was what the Order taught the god-touched became, given time and fear: monsters. It was why the Rite existed. Containment before corruption.

What she'd found instead was a village where everyone loved their farrier. Where the innkeeper's wife pressed a heel of bread on Sera and said, *you'll want to meet Rosalind, he'll know what to make of you, stranger, he knows everything about this valley.* Where children waved from fences. Where a dying old woman in a smokehouse had prayed aloud to gods that hadn't answered anyone in centuries—and something had answered, and the something had been standing right here, shoeing horses and keeping his head down, for twelve years.

Twelve years. He'd been here since the Purging of the Steppe courts. Since the king's heir died in a storm no weather-reader could explain. Since—

Sera went very still.

Since the exile of the storm-witch who killed Prince Aldric. The file had crossed her desk during training, one of the cautionary tales they made novices memorize. *Rosalind Whitlock. Storm-blood. Sentence: exile to Greyhollow, blood-price paid by kin.* Paid by kin. The records never said what the payment had been. The Undercourt handled those transactions, and the Undercourt kept its ledgers closed.

She looked at the farrier—at the way he held himself, weight back, always ready to absorb a blow—and understood that he knew she'd figured it out. Had probably known since she walked in. And he was standing there anyway, offering her his wrists, because twelve years of hiding had taught him that fighting only made it worse.

"You're him," she said quietly. "Whitlock. The storm-witch of the Steppe."

Something moved behind his eyes—not fear. Grief, maybe. Or relief, terrible and exhausted, the relief of a man who has carried a name so long his shoulders have reshaped themselves around it.

"I'm the farrier," he said. "That's the only name this valley knows."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I've got left."

* * *

They ended up sitting, which surprised them both. There were two stools by the cold hearth, and after a long silence in which Sera did not draw her sword and Rosalind did not run, they sat down across from each other like two travelers sharing shelter from rain.

"The Rite of Seizure," Sera said. "I'm supposed to say it. Bind you, send word to the Chapterhouse at Saltmere, and they'd send riders within the week. You understand what that means."

"It means I hang." He said it the way he might say *it means I need a new hammer*. "Or worse. They don't hang god-touched anymore, do they. Not since the reforms. They take us to the Chapterhouses and they—" He stopped. Started again. "My sister used to tell me stories about what happens in the Chapterhouses. She collected horrors the way other girls collected ribbons."

"Your sister." Sera kept her voice flat. Careful. "Wren Whitlock. Sold to the Undercourt as your blood-price."

The tremor in his hands stopped. Went perfectly, terribly still. "You've read my file."

"I've read every file the Order keeps on the Steppe purgings. It's part of why they sent me—why I *came*, nobody sent me, I'm disgraced, nobody sends disgraced paladins anywhere." She heard herself saying too much and reined it in. "The point is, I know what you are. I know what you did. And I know what the law says I must do about it."

"And yet here we sit." His gaze flicked to the sword at her hip. "Stools instead of shackles. Why?"

It was a fair question, and she deserved to answer it honestly, and honesty was the one weapon she'd never learned to hold correctly. Her whole disgrace rested on that flaw.

"Because this morning," she said, "something answered a prayer in a valley where nothing can answer prayers. Because the candles guttered and frost bloomed on a midsummer hearthstone and a dying woman saw the face of a god she'd been taught was dead. Because I've spent six months chasing reports from a dozen provinces—wells that taste of memory, crows that speak, a bridge in the east that demands names as toll—and every report lands closer to Greyhollow, like ripples closing on a stone. Something is waking, Whitlock. The old gods. All of them. And the Order's answer is to arrest whoever they touch and pray the problem goes away." She laughed, short and bitter. "*Pray.* That's their answer. Pray to the new gods, the court gods, the tidy ones with contracts and tithes, and hope the old ones stay asleep."

"And yours?" he asked. "What's your answer?"

"My answer is that I stood in your doorway for the length of ten heartbeats trying to say four words, and I couldn't do it." She leaned forward. "So here's what happens instead. Here's the offer I have no authority to make. You help me find out why the gods are waking. You let me look at whatever it was that stirred in you this morning—you don't hide it, you don't run, you give me the truth as we go. And in exchange, I don't send word to Saltmere. I don't write my report. As far as the Order is concerned, I found nothing in Greyhollow but good horseshoes and bad ale."

He studied her for a long moment. Outside, the valley went about its morning—a cart rattling past, someone calling a dog, the ordinary blessed noise of people who had no idea their quiet world had developed a hairline fracture.

"You'd break your oath," he said at last. "For me. For a man you've known less than an hour. A convicted kingslayer."

"A convicted *nothing*." The words came out harder than she intended. "I've read the file, Whitlock. A storm rises off the Steppe, kills the prince, kills eleven others, and the court needs someone to blame, and wouldn't you know it, the only storm-blood in three hundred miles is standing right there with smoke coming off his skin. Did you call that storm? Truthfully. Not for me—for you. Did you call it?"

His jaw worked. When he finally spoke, his voice had dropped to almost nothing, worn smooth by twelve years of disuse.

"I called a rain-shower. For the barley. A *rain-shower*. I was nineteen and showing off for Wren and I pulled more than I could hold, and something else took it and ran with it, something in the sky that was hungry, and by the time I wrestled it down the prince was dead and the king's magisters were pointing at me and there was no version of the story where I walked away clean." His breath shuddered out. "I've told myself a thousand times I should have died that day. Instead they took my sister and gave me this valley. So no, paladin. I didn't kill Aldric. But I've paid for it every morning since, with a ritual I do at dawn to keep my blood asleep, and if you're asking whether I'm dangerous—"

"I'm not asking that."

"—then yes. Yes, I am. Whatever woke this morning, it woke *in me*, and I don't know what it wants, and that frightens me more than your sword does."

There it was. The truth under the calm, and it was worse and better than anything in the file. Sera had interrogated smugglers and heretic-priests and once, horribly, a man who wore his dead brother's face, and none of them had frightened her the way this quiet honesty did—because honesty couldn't be managed, couldn't be turned, could only be believed or refused.

She chose to believe it. And in choosing, she felt something shift in her chest, a sound like ice cracking on a spring river: the first fracture in twelve years of flawless, joyless duty.

"Then we're agreed on one thing," she said. "Neither of us knows what's happening. Which means neither of us can afford to be stupid about the other."

"Agreed." Almost—almost—a smile. "Though I'd point out you're the one who broke her oath first."

"I haven't broken anything yet. Oaths are broken by action. What I've done is merely... deferred one." Even as she said it, she knew it was sophistry, the kind of lawyer's arithmetic the Undercourt itself would admire. The Rite had been owed. She had withheld it. Somewhere in Saltmere, her former sisters were going about their morning without knowing that Sera Vane had just chosen a farrier over the moon.

No. Not over the moon. That was the distinction she'd carve into herself if it was the last thing she did. The Order served the moon-goddess Selune—or claimed to. But Selune's own oldest hymns spoke of the *pale witness*, the light that sees truly in darkness, that loves the hidden things enough to find them. Somewhere along the way the Order had traded witnessing for policing, seeing for seizing, and called the trade reform.

If the old gods were waking—if Selune herself stirred beneath the Steppe with all her forgotten kin—then maybe the Order had been serving a portrait of the goddess so long it had forgotten the woman.

Maybe Sera had too.

"There's one more thing," Rosalind said. He rose, went to the shelf above the hearth, and took down a small tin box. From it he drew a single black feather, iridescent at the edges, and laid it on the anvil between them. "This was on my windowsill this morning. Before you arrived. Before the answering, even—I think it came *with* the answering. Crows don't come to Greyhollow, paladin. Nothing wild comes here. The valley doesn't allow it."

Sera picked up the feather and immediately wished she hadn't. Cold ran up her arm, a clean cold, like moonlight given weight. And threaded through the cold, faint as a voice through a wall, she heard—not words, never words—but a sense of attention. Of being *regarded*.

"This is corvid-sign," she said. "Old rite. The crow-messengers belonged to Corvass, the god of small prayers. He was the first of the old pantheon the courts suppressed, because he was the one mortals actually talked to. Baker's prayers, farmer's prayers, the little desperate things." She set the feather down with exaggerated care, as though it might bite. "If Corvass is reaching into Greyhollow, into *you* specifically—"

"Then the rumors are true," Rosalind finished. "The gods aren't stirring randomly. They're looking for something. Someone." He met her eyes. "And I have a horrible suspicion I know who."

Outside, a crow landed on the fence rail. Just one. It did not caw. It turned its head and regarded the forge with one eye that caught the dawn light and held it a moment too long, a moment too knowingly, before lifting away northward—toward the Steppe, toward the mountain, toward whatever waited beneath it.

Sera watched it go and felt her oath, whole and unbroken until this morning, settle around her like a garment with a seam already split.

"Well then," she said, standing, buckling on the rest of her resolve like armor that no longer quite fit. "We ride north. And Whitlock—you'd better teach me that dawn ritual of yours. If your storms wake on the road, someone has to be there to anchor them, and it seems the moon has decided that someone is me."

He looked at her then—really looked, the first honest look either of them had given—and nodded once.

"One condition," he said. "When we find out why the gods are waking... if it turns out my sister's soul is tangled in it somewhere, we follow that thread. No matter where it leads. Even if it leads into the Undercourt itself."

Sera thought of the ledger she'd glimpsed once, as a novice, in the restricted archive: *Ledger of Forfeit. Blood-prices, soul-bonds, debts freely owed.* Thought of the name that must be written in it, in some clerk's patient hand, twelve years now gathering dust in the dark.

"Even there," she said. "Especially there."

Neither of them knew yet what that promise would cost. Neither of them knew that the crow, wheeling north over the pass, was already carrying word of their bargain to a god who kept accounts of his own.

But the fracture had opened. And through it, faint and far away, something ancient had begun—very softly—to laugh.
