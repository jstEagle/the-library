## 7. What the Gods Want

The crow came at dusk, as crows do, but this one came through the window.

Not the door. Not down the chimney like a soot-hearted omen out of some grandmother's tale. Through the window of Sera Vane's rented room above the farrier's forge, where the glass had been patched with waxed linen, and it folded its wings on the sill with the composure of a magistrate arriving to hear a case.

Rosalind was there because Sera had insisted he be there. "If the gods are talking," she'd said, "they'll talk where both of us can hear, and I'm not chasing you across a valley at midnight again."

So they sat in the lamplight—Sera with her broken sword across her knees, Rosalind with his hands still black-rimmed from the day's shoeing—and watched the crow watch them.

It had one human eye.

He'd seen it before, three days ago, when it had spoken Wren's name for him in his childhood nickname, *Rosie*, in a voice like his sister's voice worn thin by distance. He had not slept properly since. He'd told himself it was a trick, a spirit, a cruel jest of the waking world. But here it was again, and this time it did not speak first. It waited, head tilted, that single round eye catching the lamplight and holding it like a coin.

"You're real," Rosalind said finally. His voice came out rougher than he intended. "You're actually real."

The crow's feathers rippled. When it spoke, the sound did not come from its beak so much as from the air around it, from the rafters, from somewhere behind Rosalind's sternum—a voice layered, many-voiced, the way a choir is one voice made of throats.

*I am as real as you let me be,* it said. *That is what prayer is. That is what I have been learning these twelve years, in the dark, with the rest of us.*

Sera had gone very still. Her hand rested on the flat of her broken blade—not gripping it, just touching, the way a woman touches a scar. "Corvass," she said quietly. "God of small prayers. The moon-cult kept your name in the third litany. We were taught you were dead."

*Taught,* the crow repeated, and something dry moved through the layered voice, almost amusement. *Yes. The cults taught many things. Your order taught that the gods sleep because they choose to. A comfortable doctrine. It lets a paladin feel that heaven's silence is a courtesy rather than a wound.* The human eye fixed on her. *We did not sleep, daughter of the Broken Order. We starved.*

"Starved," Rosalind said.

*On what do you imagine gods feed?* The crow hopped once along the sill, restless, its shadow huge and wrong against the wall—many-winged, many-eyed, glimpsing for a heartbeat at the shape beneath the bird. *Not worship. Worship is seasoning. We feed on exchange. A prayer given, an answer returned. A name spoken into the dark and something in the dark speaking back. The old traffic between mortal and divine, the small commerce of hope. Every answered prayer was a meal. Every covenant, a harvest.*

"And then?" Sera asked. Her voice was flat, controlled, but Rosalind heard the crack running under it. She had built her whole life on an order whose god had gone silent. She had been disgraced by that silence as much as by her own choices.

*And then the Undercourt learned to hoard.* The word came out of the air like a stone dropped down a well. *You know the court beneath the mountain? Every village knows it as a story. The place where debts are collected. Where souls go as surety. Mortals have always pawned pieces of themselves there—a year of luck, a memory, a love. Small pawns. Small interest. And the court always held them, always, because a held soul is a soul that cannot pray. Cannot be answered. Cannot feed a god.*

Rosalind felt cold move up his spine. "They've been eating your food."

*Worse.* The crow's feathers lifted, all of them, so that for a moment it looked twice its size and half again as angry. *They have been cornering the market. For nine hundred years the Undercourt has collected—not only the little pawns mortals offered freely, but forfeited souls, abandoned souls, souls sold by others. Debts inherited. Blood-prices. Collateral taken for another's crime. They gathered and gathered until the flow of souls between the living world and the divine thinned to a trickle, and we gods grew thin with it. Some of us died outright. The rest went quiet, conserving what we were, waiting.*

"Waiting for what?" Rosalind asked.

*For someone to break the dam.*

The lamplight guttered. Frost feathered up the inside of the windowpane, though the night outside was merely cool, and Rosalind felt it—the stirring under his skin, the pressure of weather looking for a sky. He pressed his palms flat against his thighs and breathed the way he'd breathed every dawn for twelve years, the ritual of small motions, of held storms.

The crow watched him do it with its human eye, and did not pretend not to understand.

*You felt it already,* Corvass said. *When your neighbor prayed in the smokehouse and something answered. You felt the gods wake in you.*

"It wasn't me," Rosalind said. "I didn't pray."

*No. She prayed. And for the first time in centuries, there was enough of us left awake to answer—and the answer needed a door. Prayers need a debtor, farrier. An answer must come from somewhere, through someone. The gods wake, and they reach for the nearest conduit, the deepest buried vein of power in the region of the asking.* The eye did not blink. *You have spent twelve years burying yourself alive. Do you understand what that makes you? A wellspring under a floorboard. The moment we stirred, you began to fill.*

"I don't want to be a wellspring." His voice cracked on it. "I want to shoe horses and be left alone. I have paid for what I did. Twelve years, I have paid—"

*Paid to whom?*

The question landed in the room like a dropped hammer.

Rosalind opened his mouth and found nothing in it. Because he knew. He had always known, in the place where he kept the things he could not look at directly. The storm that killed Prince Aldric had not been called by him alone—it had been torn out of him, half-commanded, half-run-wild, and the crown had needed a witch and gotten one. And the price of exile had not been paid to the crown. The crown took his freedom. The rest—

"The blood-price," he said slowly. "My exile was the public sentence. But there was a private debt. The court took—"

*Your sister,* Corvass said, without mercy, because gods who deal in small prayers cannot afford to flinch. *Wren Whitlock. Sold to the Undercourt thirteen years ago—no. Twelve years, eleven months, and some days. Her soul has been held in the Ledger of Forfeit ever since, as collateral against the debt your storm created. She is not dead. She is not suffering, exactly. She is* held. *A coin in a vault. And while she is held, she prays to no one, answers no one, feeds nothing—and the court grows fatter, and the gods grow thinner, and the world goes quiet.*

Sera rose. Rosalind hadn't seen her decide to; she was simply standing, the way soldiers stand when the ground shifts. "How many," she said. "How many souls do they hold?"

*Tens of thousands. Perhaps more. The ledgers themselves have forgotten. Whole villages' worth of forfeits. Generations of the desperate. The Undercourt does not eat souls, paladin—that would be waste. It lends them out. Trades them. Uses them as weight to back its bargains. Every favor the court has ever done a mortal man was paid for with someone else's held soul. They are the bank beneath the world, and the world has been borrowing from its own people for nine hundred years.*

"And now the gods are waking," Sera said, "because—"

*Because the vaults are overfull and the prayers have nowhere to go, and hunger makes even gods reckless. Because a dying woman in a smokehouse prayed aloud to names she barely remembered, and we reached for her, and we found instead a buried storm with a stolen sister and a grievance twelve years old.* The crow turned its head, and both eyes—for a moment it had two, and both were human, and both were ancient—fixed on Rosalind. *We woke because the balance broke. And you, Rosalind Whitlock, are where it broke.*

The frost on the window had spread. It bloomed in patterns Rosalind almost recognized—feather-patterns, wing-patterns, the geometry of a storm seen from above. His hands ached. Under his skin, something vast turned over in its sleep and murmured.

"What do you want from me?" he asked.

*Want is a strong word for a starving thing,* Corvass said. *But yes. We want. We want the vaults opened. We want the hoarded souls returned to the living world, to the bodies that wait for them or the winds that will carry them home. We want the Undercourt's ledger burned back to blank pages. And we want it done before the thirteenth turn of the moon, when the court seals its books for the great reckoning—after which nothing held in those vaults comes out again. Ever. The seals are old magic, older than us. Even waking, we cannot crack them. Only a debt freely owed can open the gates. Only someone the court recognizes as a debtor can walk in and walk out.*

"And I'm a debtor," Rosalind said. "My sister is my collateral. My name is in their book."

*Your name is the key to their book.*

Silence. The lamp steadied. Outside, somewhere in Greyhollow, a dog barked at nothing, and the ordinary sounds of the valley—the valley where magic could not follow—went on around the impossible thing sitting on the windowsill.

Sera spoke first. "This is a quest," she said. "You're asking him to cross the Steppe, enter the Undercourt, and steal from the most powerful creditor in existence. With what army?"

*With a disgraced paladin whose moon-blessing still works, whatever her order says. With a conduit the gods themselves will shelter. With a crow who knows which doors forget to lock.* The layered voice softened, or seemed to. *And with twelve years of a brother's guilt, which is a fuel no court has ever learned to extinguish.*

"That's not an answer," Rosalind said. "That's a flattery."

*It is both,* Corvass said. *I am a god of small prayers, farrier. I do not command. I ask. Here is my asking: come to the Steppe. Let the storms you buried teach you what they are instead of what you fear. Find the gates beneath the mountain. Offer the court a debt freely owed, and take your sister out of their ledger before the thirteenth moon turns. In doing so, you crack the first seal of the great reckoning, and everything after follows from that crack like water from a split dam.*

"And if I say no?"

The crow was quiet a long moment. When it answered, the many voices had narrowed to something almost gentle.

*Then we starve slowly, the world stays silent, your sister spends eternity as currency, and you spend it shoeing horses in a valley that cannot hear you scream. Which is, I grant, a life. Many men have had worse.* The human eye held him. *But you did not ask why the candles guttered when she prayed. You did not ask why the frost blooms when you are afraid. You have been asking, every dawn for twelve years, whether the storm in you is a curse. I am telling you: it is a door. And doors are not curses. Doors are only doors. What matters is what you carry through them.*

Rosalind looked at his hands. Farrier's hands, burn-scarred, nail-split, steady as stone for twelve years. Under the skin, faint as heat-lightning behind cloud, something flickered.

"Wren used to say," he heard himself begin, and stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "She used to say I never could leave a locked thing alone. Gates, boxes, other people's secrets. She said I'd die picking some lock I shouldn't." His throat worked. "She said it laughing."

*Then you know what she would tell you to do,* said the crow-god.

Sera sat back down. Slowly, like a woman lowering herself into cold water. "The thirteenth turn," she said. "How long?"

*Nineteen days.*

"Nineteen days to cross the Glass Steppe, survive whatever haunts it, find gates that may not exist, and bargain with a court that has had nine hundred years to perfect the art of the bad bargain." She laughed—one short, sharp note with no humor in it. "My order sent paladins on crusades with better odds and worse reasons."

*Yes,* said Corvass. *Will you come?*

She looked at Rosalind. He looked back. Between them lay twelve years of rumor-hunting and storm-fear, a broken sword and a buried name, and something new being built in the space between—flimsy as a footbridge, load-bearing as one too.

"She'll be in there," Rosalind said. Not a question. "Her soul. In the ledger. I could be standing in the same room as her and not know it."

*You will know,* Corvass said. *Souls that are loved leave marks on the people who love them. Yours has had twelve years to learn her absence. You will know.*

Rosalind stood. His knees ached from the day's work, and his hands wanted a hammer, and none of that mattered at all. Nineteen days. Nineteen days, and a sister held as coin, and a storm under his skin that had finally, finally been given a reason to rise.

"All right," he said. "Crow. Corvass. Whatever you are. I'll cross your Steppe. I'll knock on the Undercourt's gate. But I'm not doing it to feed you." He pointed a scarred finger at the bird, and had the strange pleasure of seeing a god lean back. "I'm doing it for Wren. The rest of it—your dams, your reckonings, your starving heaven—that's yours. My business starts and ends with one soul in one ledger. Anything beyond that is a bargain we can discuss after she's free."

The crow's feathers settled. The layered voice, when it came, carried something that in a man Rosalind would have called respect.

*A god of small prayers asks for small things,* Corvass said. *One soul. One brother. One door opened. Everything large in this world is built from such small things, farrier. Go and sleep. Both of you. Tomorrow you will need to lie to your elders, pack light, and decide which lies about yourselves you are ready to stop telling each other.*

It spread its wings—and paused, on the sill, half-turned back toward them, one human eye catching the last of the lamplight.

*Oh. One more small thing.* The voice had gone careful, the way a guide's voice goes careful at the edge of a drop. *The court knows its collateral is stirring. The moment you cross the valley's boundary, your power returns to you fully—and the moment it returns, the Undercourt will feel it, the way a lender feels a debtor come into money. There are agents on the roads already. Collectors. Things that smell of ink and iron. You will not see the first one coming.*

The frost on the window cracked, all at once, a single sharp report like a snapping branch.

When they turned back, the sill was empty, and the night outside was full of wings going south, toward the Steppe, toward the mountain, toward the gates that had held Rosalind's sister for twelve long years.

Sera blew out the lamp.

"Nineteen days," she said in the dark.

Below them, in the forge, Rosalind's old anvil sat cold in the shadows—and on its steel face, though neither of them saw it, frost was blooming in the shape of a handprint, small, like a child's, reaching up.
