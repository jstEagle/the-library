## 8. Leaving Greyhollow

The elders came to the forge at dusk, which was how Rosalind knew they had already decided.

They never came at dusk. Dusk was for their own hearths, for the small rituals of a valley that had spent twelve years teaching itself not to believe in anything larger than weather and wheat. If Maren Odell and the others were standing in his doorway with their hats in their hands and their faces arranged into something like sorrow, then the decision had been made hours ago, probably over mutton and cider, probably with the same careful gentleness they used when putting down a lame horse.

"Rosalind." Maren used his false name, the one he'd worn so long it fit like an old boot. "We know about the smokehouse."

He kept his hands still on the anvil. Behind them, the banked coals breathed red. "Know what about it?"

"Don't." Old Tam's voice cracked like green wood. He was the eldest of them, ninety if he was a day, and he had been a boy when the valley sealed itself against magic. "Don't insult us with it. The frost on the windows. The candles burning blue. Hetta Corrigan dying with her eyes full of light and her prayer *answered*. We know what walked through our valley last week, boy, and we know what you are."

*What I am.* Rosalind set down the tongs. Twelve years of practice let him keep his face flat, his breathing even, but underneath the calm his heart was doing something frantic and birdlike, beating against the bars of his ribs.

"And what am I?" he asked quietly.

"A farrier," Maren said. "That's what we're asking you to be. That's all we can ask you to be. Whatever woke up in you—whatever answered that poor woman's prayer—you put it back down. You bury it deeper than before. And in the morning, this is all forgotten."

"In the morning," Rosalind repeated.

"The valley is safe because nothing follows us in here," Tam said. "No gods. No courts. No storms that think. That's the covenant our grandfathers bled for. You've lived under its roof your whole exile. Honor it."

Rosalind looked past them, through the open door, at the grey evening settling over Greyhollow—the crooked lanes, the smoke rising straight from forty chimneys, the mountains holding the valley like two cupped hands. Safe. Quiet. A room with no windows, and he had been grateful for the room for twelve years because the alternative had been a cell, or worse.

But Hetta had died smiling. He couldn't stop thinking about that. She had died smiling, and something had been there to make her smile, and the valley wanted him to pretend that light had never happened.

"I need until dawn," he said.

Maren's shoulders dropped half an inch. Relief or disappointment; he couldn't tell which. "Until dawn," she agreed. "Not past it."

They left. Rosalind stood alone in the forge with the coals ticking as they cooled, and he understood, with the clarity that comes only after a decision has already been made somewhere below thought, that he would not be here at dawn.

He was leaving tonight.

* * *

He packed the way he'd once planned to pack, in the first year, before the planning stopped—before the days sanded him smooth and the idea of anywhere else became abstract as a story told to children. Bread wrapped in oilcloth. His good hammer, because a hammer was a tool before it was anything else and he refused to travel unarmed by his own trade. The coil of silver wire he used for decorative work, which he now understood he'd been saving for something without knowing what. And at the bottom of the chest, wrapped in three layers of linen, the thing he had never once looked at in twelve years: a child's wooden bird, painted blue, carved by a girl with quick hands and a laugh like water over stones.

Wren's bird. Wren's laugh. Wren's soul sitting in a vault beneath a mountain while her brother learned to shoe horses and call it living.

He held the little bird in his palm. It weighed almost nothing. It had always weighed almost nothing, and somehow it was the heaviest thing he owned.

"You knew," he said to it. "Didn't you? Even at eight years old, you knew what you were doing when you sold yourself to pay my blood-price. You knew I'd come for you eventually. You just overestimated how fast I'd move."

The crow was on the windowsill.

It had been appearing for days now—on the fence post, on the forge roof, once absurdly perched on his anvil while he worked, watching him with that wrong eye, the human-colored one, hazel and patient. It didn't speak. It didn't need to. Its presence was a question asked every day, and every day he had answered by looking away.

Now he looked at it directly.

"I'm going," he told it. "If that's what you're waiting to hear. I'm going, and I don't know the way, and I'm fairly sure everything between here and the Undercourt wants me dead or worse. So if your god has directions, now would be the time."

The crow tilted its head. Then it lifted off the sill, circled the yard once, and flew toward the eastern ridge—the direction of the Steppe, the direction of everything. It paused at the edge of sight, a black comma against the bruised sky, and waited.

Waiting for him. Gods, it was actually waiting for him.

Rosalind closed his hand around the wooden bird, took one last look at the forge where he had buried himself alive for twelve years, and walked out of Greyhollow.

* * *

The boundary wasn't marked. That was the first strange thing—that after all the weight the valley's silence carried, after twelve years of living inside it like a bell inside a wool blanket, the edge of it was just a place where the grass changed color slightly, where the pines gave way to scrub oak, where a stream ran a little faster as if relieved to be leaving too.

The crow led him along a shepherd's track through the pass, and the elders' words followed him like his own shadow. *Traitor*, they would call him by morning, when they found the cold forge and the empty chest. He tried to feel the sting of it and found, instead, a strange lightness, as though he'd been carrying stones in his mouth for twelve years and had finally spat them out. They could brand him traitor. Traitor to what? To a covenant of silence? To a life that had never been his?

But the terror was there too, walking beside the exhilaration like an old married couple who'd long since stopped speaking to each other. Because he remembered what he was. He remembered the night of the storm, the sky tearing open at his grief, the king's heir thrown from his horse like a doll—and he remembered, more vividly than any memory he owned, the feeling of it. The power moving through him like a second heartbeat. How easy it had been. How *good* it had felt.

That was the part nobody warned you about, being made of storm. Not the destruction. The joy of it.

The track climbed. The crow wheeled overhead. And then, near the top of the pass, where the last pine gave way to bare stone and the whole grey bowl of Greyhollow lay spread out below him one final time—

—he crossed the line.

Magic hit him like cold water.

There was no other word for it. One stride he was dry, and the next he was drowning, gasping, every nerve in his body suddenly awake and screaming that it had been asleep for twelve years, that the world was *full*, that the air itself was a fabric woven through with threads of power and meaning and attention, and he had been living in a hole in the weave. He went to his knees on the stony track. His breath came in white plumes though the evening was mild. Under his skin, along his arms, across his chest where the old scar sat—it crackled. Actually crackled, faint blue light crawling in the lines of the storm-mark the court's branding had left on him, waking like a limb remembering it had fingers.

"Oh," he said aloud, and his voice came out doubled, harmonized with something vast and low, a rumble of distant thunder folded into a man's voice. "Oh, that's—"

The crow landed in front of him and regarded him with its hazel eye, and he had the distinct impression it was amused.

"This is what it's like," Rosalind whispered. "All the time. Outside. This is what everyone gets to feel, and I got *nothing*, for twelve years, and I thought—" He laughed, and the laugh sparked. "I thought the numbness was peace."

He knelt there on the boundary stone with magic flooding back into him like cold water into a man pulled from a desert, and he understood that the valley hadn't been protecting him. It had been *suppressing* him. A scabbard. All those years of quiet, all those dawns of ritual and restraint, and the valley itself had been doing half the work, pressing him flat, keeping the storm-blood dormant simply by being the kind of place where power couldn't breathe. He had thanked the valley for his safety. He should have thanked it for his anesthesia.

And now he was out, and the storm under his skin was stretching muscles it hadn't used in twelve years, and it was *hungry*.

A cloud formed above the pass. There had been no cloud. He watched it assemble out of clear evening air, swirling, darkening, and felt—with sickening intimacy—that it was responding to him. To his pulse. His pulse had weather attached to it now.

"No," he said to it, through his teeth, gripping the track stones hard enough to hurt his fingers. "No. Down. Sit *down*."

The cloud hesitated, a dog hearing its name, and slowly unraveled.

Rosalind stayed on his knees, shaking, sweat freezing on his spine. Below him Greyhollow glowed with its forty hearth-fires, small and warm and utterly unaware of how close it had just come to weather with opinions. Above him the first stars were coming out, sharp and clean, and the crow stood patiently at his elbow like a nurse waiting for a fever to break.

"Twelve years," he said to it. "Twelve years of discipline, and it takes one step outside the line to learn the discipline was never mine. It was the valley's. Take me ten miles further and what happens? Take me onto the Steppe?" He looked at the crow. "Can I even do this? Can I cross a hundred leagues of open country carrying a lightning strike under my ribs, waiting for a reason?"

The crow said nothing, obviously. But it hopped closer, and touched its beak, very gently, to the back of his scarred hand—and where it touched, the crackling light went quiet. Not gone. Quieted. Held, the way a mother holds a crying child, not by force but by presence.

Corvass, he realized. The god was touching him. The god of crows and small prayers was steadying his storm the way you'd steady a spooked horse, and it was working, and that meant it was *possible*—that the storm could be ridden, not just endured.

"Right," Rosalind said. He stood. His legs held. The world hummed around him, alive and attentive, and he made himself look at it instead of flinching. "Right. One step at a time. Storms are weather, and weather moves, and I have a sister to steal back from a court of soul-brokers before the thirteenth moon turns." He glanced east, where beyond the darkening hills the Glass Steppe waited, flat and pale and mirror-bright even under starlight, a horizon that reflected horizons. "How far to the edge of the Steppe?"

The crow lifted off and flew east. Then it came back. Then it flew east again.

"That far," Rosalind said grimly. "Good. Walking will give me time to practice not destroying things."

* * *

He camped that night in the lee of a boulder, three miles out, close enough that he could still see the valley's fires if he craned his neck. He didn't crane his neck. He built a small fire, ate his bread, and held the wooden bird up to the flames' light.

"They'll call me traitor by morning," he told it. "Maren will say it gently, because Maren says everything gently, and old Tam will say it like a curse, and they'll both be wrong. I'm not betraying the valley. I'm betraying the lie. There's a difference." He turned the bird over. Blue paint, faded nearly white now. "You'd have liked Sera Vane, I think. The paladin. You always did like people who were bad at lying."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, wind moved through the scrub oak, and Rosalind felt it move *through him too*, felt the night air acknowledge him, brush against the storm-mark like a hand checking a fever. The world outside Greyhollow knew he was in it. Everything knew. Every gust, every stream, every crow on every branch—they all seemed to lean slightly in his direction, the way a room leans toward a lit candle.

It should have been terrifying. Some of it was. But underneath the terror, steady as bedrock, was the other thing—the thing he'd been chasing for twelve years without knowing its name.

Belonging. Not to the valley, which had sheltered him the way a wall shelters a prisoner. Not to a name, which he'd surrendered long ago. Belonging to the *world itself*—to the enormous, awake, attentive everything that had finally noticed him again and said, without words: *there you are. We wondered where you'd gone.*

"I'm coming, Wren," he said to the fire, to the bird, to the night. "Thirteen moons. I'll be there before the last one turns. I swear it by whatever's left of me to swear with."

The fire flared briefly blue. He chose to take that as agreement.

He slept badly, dreams full of falling rain that fell upward, and woke before dawn with frost on his blanket shaped, unmistakably, like feathers. The crow was already awake, already facing east.

Rosalind broke camp quickly. Behind him, far below, a thin thread of smoke rose from Greyhollow—earlier than any hearth should be lit—and he knew, without knowing how he knew, that the elders had found the empty forge, and that Maren Odell was standing in his doorway right now, saying the word *traitor* to the morning air, gently, the way she said everything.

"Let them," he said softly. And then, because the storm under his skin was listening, and because he was done pretending otherwise, he let himself smile—a real one, wide and wild, the first in twelve years. "Let them call me traitor. Traitors run *from* something."

He shouldered his pack and turned east, toward the Steppe, toward the mountain, toward his sister.

"I'm running *toward*."

The crow screamed once—approval or mockery, impossible to say—and the two of them went down out of the hills together, a man and a god's messenger, into the wide bright country where magic ran like blood in the world's veins. By midmorning the land had opened into rolling grassland, and by noon Rosalind could see it ahead: a line of pale light on the horizon, flat as a blade, stretching wider the closer they came.

The Glass Steppe. Mirror-flat. Haunted, if the stories were true, by reflections that did not wait politely to be cast.

Rosalind stopped at the last rise and looked at it, and the storm under his skin looked with him, and for a moment neither of them breathed.

Then, far out on the glass, in a place where nothing stood to cast it—a figure rose up out of the plain and began walking toward him.

It was wearing his face.
