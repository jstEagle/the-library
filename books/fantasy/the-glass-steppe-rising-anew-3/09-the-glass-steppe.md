## 9. The Glass Steppe

The valley's boundary was a line you could step over.

That was the cruelest part of it. Twelve years of walls in his head—elders' warnings, the brand on the gatepost, the way children were taught to cross the street when he passed—and all of it had been guarding a line no wider than a bootprint. Sera crossed first. Then Corvass, riding her shoulder like a black epaulet. Then Rosalind stood with one foot on Greyhollow's packed clay and one foot on the world, and stepped.

Magic came back like cold water poured down his collar.

He felt it everywhere at once—in the grass, which suddenly *meant* something; in the wind, which had opinions; in his own blood, which woke all at once like a man shaken from a deep sleep who comes up swinging. The storm that had slept under his skin for twelve years rolled over and stretched, and every joint of him crackled with it. His hair lifted off his neck. The air went sharp and metallic, tasting of rain that hadn't fallen yet.

"Steady." Sera's hand closed on his arm, moon-blessing cool against the heat building in his palm. "Breathe. It's not a wave, it's a tide. Let it come and go."

"It's not coming and going," he managed. "It's *talking*."

"What's it saying?"

He almost laughed. What did a storm say after twelve years of silence? *Where have you been. What did they do to us. I remember the king's son. I remember the sky going white.* He said none of it. "It says we should keep moving."

Behind them, Greyhollow's elders had come as far as the boundary stones would let them, a row of bent figures in grey wool, watching him leave. Old Marta raised her hand—not in farewell. In the old warding sign, the one farmers used against blight. He'd seen it thrown at crows, at wolves, at tax collectors. Never at himself.

Corvass croaked softly from Sera's shoulder. "*They fear what they cannot hold,*" the crow said. "*Walk on, storm-child. The Steppe is long, and your reflection is already waiting.*"

Rosalind turned. "My what?"

The crow only looked at him with its one human eye, and said nothing more.

* * *

The Glass Steppe began three days out from the valley, and it began without ceremony. One morning the grass simply stopped, and the ground went flat and pale and smooth as a frozen lake, stretching to a horizon so clean it looked ruled with a knife. Not grey, exactly—the surface held color the way still water does, taking the sky's blue and the sun's white and giving them back slightly wrong, slightly delayed.

Sera knelt and pressed her palm flat against it. "It's warm," she said, frowning. "Stone this exposed should be cold at dawn."

"Everything about this place is wrong," Rosalind said. "Look at our feet."

She looked. Their reflections looked back—but a half-beat late. When Sera shifted her weight, her mirror-self followed a breath behind, like an echo arriving after the shout. When Rosalind waved, the figure below him waved a moment afterward, politely, as if remembering to.

"That's normal," Sera said, but her voice didn't believe itself. "Reflections lag. Light has to travel."

"Light doesn't travel *upward*, Vane. And light doesn't get tired of copying you." He pointed. For the past hour, his reflection had been drifting—a finger's width here, a handspan there—out of true position, the way a boat drifts from its mooring. Now it stood a full pace to his left, though he stood nowhere near anything to reflect off of. There was no water. No ice. Just the Steppe, holding their images the way a tongue holds a word it hasn't decided to say.

Sera's hand went to her sword. "Corvass?"

The crow hopped down onto the glass. It tilted its head, regarding the dark shapes beneath the surface. "*They are not reflections,*" it said at last. "*Not anymore. The Steppe remembers everyone who crosses it. Some of those memories stand up and walk.*"

"Whose memories?" Rosalind asked, though he already knew. He could feel it in the storm under his skin, which had gone quiet again—not calm, but *attentive*, the way a dog goes quiet when it smells something on the wind.

"*Yours,*" said Corvass. "*Mostly.*"

* * *

They walked all day across a world with no landmarks. That was the second cruelty of the Steppe: it gave you nothing to measure yourself against. No hill, no tree, no stone. Just the horizon, always the same distance away, never closer. Sera navigated by the sun and by a compass-needle of moon-silver she carried on a chain, and by midday even that was trembling, spinning slowly, pointing at nothing in particular—or at everything.

Rosalind counted his steps to keep his mind occupied. At four thousand, he realized he could hear footsteps that weren't his.

He stopped. Sera walked three paces ahead, her boots ringing faintly on the glass. But there was another sound underneath hers—lighter, quicker, a stride he knew because it had been his own for thirty years before it became anyone else's. A stride with a farrier's roll in it, weight kept low, ready for a kick or a bolt of lightning.

"Sera," he said quietly. "Don't turn around."

She turned around anyway. Paladins.

Twenty paces behind them, walking where Rosalind walked, stopping when he stopped, stood a second Rosalind Whitlock.

It wore his body the way a coat fits someone slightly larger. Everything was right—the broad hands, the burn-scarred forearm, the set of the shoulders—and everything was wrong. The face was too composed. The eyes were too steady. And it was clean. Twelve years of forge-soot and horse sweat had been washed out of it, leaving a version of him that belonged to some other life, some life where he'd never swung a hammer, where he'd stayed at court, where he'd learned to use what was in his blood instead of burying it.

Its mouth moved. No sound came out. But Rosalind read the shape of the words easily enough, because he'd shaped them himself, ten thousand times, in the silence of the forge:

*You shouldn't have come back.*

"Gods' teeth," Sera breathed. Her sword cleared its sheath with a sound like a struck bell, and moonlight crawled up the blade. "Is it dangerous?"

"It's me," Rosalind said. "So yes."

The reflection smiled. It was a good smile. It was the smile he used on frightened horses and frightened people, and seeing it on another face made his stomach drop, because now he finally understood how much of his own warmth was performance.

"*Do not fight it here,*" Corvass said sharply, rising off Sera's shoulder. "*On the glass, a wound given is a wound remembered. Strike it, and the Steppe will write the scar into you.*"

"Then what do we do?"

"*Walk. It can only follow. For now.*"

They walked. It followed. All afternoon, twenty paces back, matching them stride for stride, and the worst of it wasn't the fear. The worst was that Rosalind kept *looking*. Over his shoulder, again and again, the way you probe a sore tooth. Because the thing wore his grief well. It walked like a man who had lost a sister. It walked like a man carrying twelve years of guilt, and it carried them lightly, easily, the way he wished he could.

Toward dusk it tried speaking again. This time sound came through—thin, warped, like a voice heard through a wall.

"You buried it," the reflection said. Its voice was his voice, but smoother, unscarred by smoke and shouting. "Twelve years, Rosalind. You nailed a lid over the best part of yourself and called it penance. I never did. I kept it alive. Down there, in the dark, I kept *us* alive."

"Keep walking," Sera murmured.

"It knows things," Rosalind said. "Things only I know."

"Then it'll know this too." She stopped, turned, and faced the thing directly, sword low but not sheathed. Moon-pale light ran along her jaw. "I'm Sera Vane. I broke my order to be here. If you're really him—if you're really the part of him that stayed honest—then tell him to stop looking at you and start looking at the road."

The reflection regarded her with mild interest, the way a clerk regards weather. Then its gaze slid back to Rosalind, and it said, gently, almost kindly:

"She'll fail you. They always do. Ask her about the village sometime. Ask her what she *wouldn't* burn."

Sera went very still.

And Rosalind saw it—the flicker, the half-second where her composure cracked and something older and rawer showed through, guilt with teeth. The reflection saw it too. It smiled again, that good false smile, and folded its hands, patient as a debt collector.

"We camp," Rosalind said roughly. "Now. Before I say something stupid."

* * *

They made camp in the middle of the featureless plain because there was nowhere else—no rise, no hollow, just the glass, warm underfoot like a sleeping animal. Sera chalked a circle of moon-signs around the bedrolls, wards against scrying and worse. Rosalind built no fire; the Steppe offered nothing to burn, and he distrusted what his power might do to a flame out here, where magic ran thick as floodwater.

He took first watch. Of course he did. Sleep felt like an invitation.

The reflection sat twenty paces out, beyond the chalk, mirroring him exactly now—sitting when he sat, head tilted when his head tilted. Watching him watch it. Under the two moons it looked less like a copy and more like a portrait, something painted by someone who loved him and understood him not at all.

"You're not me," Rosalind said at last, quietly, so as not to wake Sera.

"No," it agreed. Its voice carried perfectly across the flat, though he'd barely whispered. "I'm what you'd have been. That's different. I'm the version that didn't run to a valley and pick up a hammer. I stayed at court. I learned the old craft properly. And when Wren was taken, I didn't spend twelve years sharpening horseshoes about it. I *negotiated*."

His chest tightened. "Negotiated with what?"

"With the court beneath the mountain. With the Understeward. With the things that hold her soul in a ledger like a line of credit." The reflection leaned forward, and its eyes caught the moons and gave them back wrong. "I made terms, Rosalind. Reasonable ones. I serve them, yes—it's not so bad, service. They value me. And in exchange, I've seen her. Spoken with her. She's *waiting*, brother. She waits, and she wonders why the great storm-witch of Greyhollow never came."

Every word landed like a hammer on a nail. He let it hit. He'd learned that much from the forge: don't flinch from the strike, or the metal fights you.

"You're lying," he said. "Or you're telling the truth in a way designed to rot me. Either way—" he nodded at the chalk circle, at Sera's sleeping form, at the crow roosting on her pack with one eye open— "I'm not crossing alone anymore. Whatever you are, you're selling solitude. And I've had twelve years of that product. It's garbage."

Something moved behind the reflection's face. For an instant the composure slipped, and what showed underneath was hunger—vast, cold, and old, far older than any version of him. Then it smoothed over, and the false smile returned.

"The thirteenth moon turns soon," it said. "When it does, the gates seal, and every soul in the Ledger belongs to the court forever. Including hers. You can't steal from a vault, brother. You can only be *let in*." It rose, unhurried, and began to walk backward into the dark, growing fainter, like ink dropped in water. "Think about the door. Think about who holds the key. And ask the paladin about the village."

Then it was gone, and the Steppe lay empty and silver to every horizon, and Rosalind discovered he was shaking.

* * *

Sera found him at dawn, sitting where he'd sat all night, staring at the glass.

"It spoke to you," she said. Not a question.

"It told me to ask you about a village." He kept his voice level. Kept his eyes on the horizon, where the sun was coming up and giving their shadows back a half-beat late. "It knew your name. It knew things. And I watched it land, Vane. I watched it hit."

For a long moment she said nothing. When he glanced over, she was looking east, jaw tight, hand resting on the pommel of her sword the way a person rests a hand on a wound.

"There was a village," she said finally. "In the Ashmark. God-touched children. My order ordered it burned, and I—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I'll tell you. All of it. But not because a thing wearing your face told you to ask. Because you deserve to hear it from me, when I choose to give it. That's the difference between us and it. We get to choose what we surrender."

Rosalind looked at her—at the disgraced paladin who had broken her oath for a stranger, who had crossed a cursed plain beside a fugitive storm-witch because someone had to—and felt the reflection's words lose a little of their poison. Not all of it. Words like that don't die easy. But some.

"All right," he said. "When you choose."

He stood, joints cracking, and looked out across the endless mirror-flat toward the mountain that wasn't yet visible but that he could feel, deep in the storm under his skin, pulling at him like a tide. Somewhere out there, past the haunted glass, past the corpse-road, past the gates, Wren was waiting in a ledger. And somewhere behind them—or below them, or inside the Steppe itself—his reflection walked, patient as interest accruing.

They broke camp and started walking.

By midmorning, Rosalind noticed that his shadow had begun pointing the wrong way—away from the sun, straight ahead of them, like a finger.

Like a guide.

Or like something leading them exactly where it wanted them to go.
