## 10. The Reflection Bargain

The reflection came for Rosalind on the third night of the Steppe, and I let it.

Not because I wanted to. Because I'd been watching it circle us since dusk, skimming along the glass horizon like a blade drawn thin, and every time I reached for my sword it stopped moving—went still as painted glass—and waited for me to look away before it started again. You can't fight a thing that only exists when you're not watching. I know that now. I didn't know it then, and so I did the harder thing: I sat by our fire, and I watched, and I said nothing.

Rosalind was mending a shoe. Of course he was. Twelve years in Greyhollow had ground the habit into him deeper than any oath—I'd seen paladins pray with less devotion than this man brought to a hoof and a hammer. He worked by lantern-light on a flat shelf of glass that had risen from the Steppe like a table set out for us, and his reflection worked with him, hammer meeting hammer, spark meeting spark.

Except the reflection's sparks were falling upward.

I saw it first. I want that recorded somewhere, even if nowhere is left to record it. Sera Vane, disgraced knight of the Order of the Waning Moon, saw the wrongness before the storm-witch did, because she was watching instead of sleeping, which is what disgrace is good for. Disgrace keeps you awake. Grace would have let me rest.

"Rosalind," I said quietly. "Don't look up."

His hammer paused mid-swing. "That's the worst thing you could have told me."

"I know."

He looked up anyway. Men always do. I'd have done the same at twenty-five, back when I still believed warnings were for other people.

The reflection looked back at him. And here is the thing I will never be able to make anyone believe who hasn't stood on the Glass Steppe under a thirteenth-moon sky: it wasn't him. It wore his face—the tired face, the grey-shot beard, the burn-scarred forearms of a farrier—but it wore them the way a courtier wears borrowed clothes. There was no weariness in it. No twelve years of careful anonymity. Its eyes were bright and cold and *amused*, and they moved independently of its head, tracking me, tracking the crow asleep on Rosalind's pack, tracking everything at once.

"Hello, brother," the reflection said.

Rosalind went white. "I don't have—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I'm not your brother."

"You are, though." The reflection set down its hammer—a mirror of Rosalind's motion, but late, deliberate, a mockery of mimicry. "That's rather the point. Rosalind Whitlock. Storm-caller. Kingslayer, if we're being generous with the word *king*—he was only an heir. Fratricide-adjacent, at best." It smiled with Rosalind's mouth, and the smile fit badly. "You've been hiding behind *farrier* so long you've forgotten how the old words taste. Say one. Go on. Say *storm*. Nothing will happen. Nothing ever happens here. That's what the valley taught you, isn't it? That you're nothing?"

"Stop," Rosalind said.

"It has a voice," I said. "Reflections on the Steppe aren't supposed to have voices. Corvass said—"

"Corvass," the reflection said, tasting the name, "is a carrion bird wearing a god like a stolen coat. Do you take counsel from crows now, Serah Vane? From *crows*?" It turned those sliding eyes on me fully, and I felt the weight of being *known*, which is the Undercourt's oldest weapon. They never threaten you first. They introduce themselves. "You of all people should understand what I'm offering him. You gave up everything for an order that burned you. He can have his back for free."

I put my hand on my sword anyway. Not because it would help. Because my hand needed something to do besides shake.

"What exactly," I said, "are you offering?"

* * *

Here is what I knew about the reflections by then, from three days on the glass and one long, unpleasant conversation with Corvass perched on my shoulder like a judgment:

The Steppe remembers everyone who crosses it. That's its nature—it's not land so much as it is memory, cooled and flattened into mirror, the corpse-glass of some cataclysm the old gods don't talk about. Every traveler drags their reflection behind them like a sled. Most reflections are just echoes. Dumb light.

But the Undercourt had gotten there first. The court beneath the mountain had been walking the Steppe for years, seeding it, teaching certain reflections to *wait*. A reflection that waits long enough stops being an echo and becomes an ambassador. And ambassadors negotiate.

So while Rosalind stared at the thing wearing his face, I did the arithmetic I'd been trained for: it hadn't attacked. It had waited until we camped. It had waited until he was alone with his hands busy and his guard down. Everything about it said *invitation*, and everything about invitations from the Undercourt said *contract*.

"Your sister," the reflection said to Rosalind, gentle as a knife going in. "Wren. Let's speak plainly, since the paladin insists on plainness. She is a ledger-entry. A line of ink in a vault beneath the mountain, and she has been a line of ink for twelve years, and she will be a line of ink forever unless someone does something spectacularly foolish. You are planning to do something spectacularly foolish. I admire it. Truly. But consider the alternative."

It spread Rosalind's hands—his hands, scarred and capable—and the glass beneath them rippled like water.

"The court will release her. Freely. Tonight, if you like. Her soul returned to her body—which still lives, did you know that? They keep the bodies breathing. It's tidier for the ledgers. She wakes in Greyhollow tomorrow with no memory of the price, and you walk away from all of this. No Steppe. No Hunt. No thirteenth moon ticking down over your head. You go home. You go back to the forge. You go back to being nobody, which you've gotten so terribly good at." The reflection leaned closer to the surface of the world, close enough that I could see it had no breath to fog anything. "All it costs is this: you stop. You turn around. You let her go—not to freedom, but to the ledger—and you live the life you were already living, except now you'll know, every dawn, exactly what you chose."

"That's not an offer," Rosalind said hoarsely. "That's just my life with the guilt turned up."

"Yes," the reflection agreed pleasantly. "That's what most lives are. We're very efficient at the Undercourt. We rarely need to invent new torments. We simply itemize the ones people carry already." It tilted its head. "You're thinking about it. Don't be ashamed. Everyone thinks about it. The wise ones think about it fastest and then refuse quickest."

And here is the part I have replayed ten thousand times since, the part where I almost failed, not because the enemy was strong but because I was proud:

I said nothing.

Because some ugly, honest corner of me wanted to hear his answer. Because if he took the bargain—if he abandoned Wren, abandoned the quest, abandoned *me* out here on the glass—then I'd be free of it. Free of the storm-witch, free of the broken oath I was compounding daily, free to crawl back toward whatever shreds of the Order might still have me. His cowardice would be my absolution. All I had to do was stay quiet and let him hang himself.

One breath. Two. The fire cracked. Somewhere out on the dark glass, another reflection—mine, probably—was watching me watch him, and learning.

"No," Rosalind said.

The word came out small. Then again, louder, with something underneath it that made the lantern-flame lean: "*No.*"

"I spent twelve years paying for one night," he said, and his hands had begun to tremble, and I swear on the Waning Moon the trembling wasn't fear. "Twelve years being nobody, doing nobody's work, and I told myself it was penance, but it wasn't. Penance would have been going after her in year one. Year two. Any year. I called it penance because penance is comfortable. Penance lets you sit still." He stood, and the glass under his boots hummed. "You're offering me my life back? My life was the prison. I'm not buying the cell twice."

The reflection smiled its ill-fitting smile. "Then we proceed to terms two through nine."

"There are more terms?"

"There are always more terms," I muttered.

* * *

Terms two through nine, delivered rapid-fire while the fire burned lower, were exactly what you'd expect from a bureaucracy staffed by the damned: the court would release Wren's soul but retain her *name*; or release her name but retain her memories; or release both but hold a lien against her firstborn; or grant full freedom contingent on annual renewal, payable in—

"In what?" Rosalind demanded. "Payable in what?"

"Gratitude," said the reflection, "properly witnessed and notarized," and I laughed before I could stop myself, one sharp bark that surprised us all, including, I think, the reflection.

"Oh, that's the tell," I said, standing, because the laughter had cleared something in my head the way a slap clears a swimmer's. "That's how I know this whole menu is rot. Real bargains cost something real. This is a shell game—they shuffle the same soul between nine cups and charge you admission each time. Rosalind. Look at its feet."

He looked. So did I, and there it was: the reflection cast no shadow. Not in the lantern-light, not in the firelight, not in the moonlight that was just beginning to silver the eastern edge of the Steppe. Everything casts a shadow on the glass. The glass *is* shadow, in a way—memory pressed flat. But the ambassador stood in a hole in the world, and through the hole I could see, faintly, far below, the glint of obsidian halls and the slow drift of paper-white things that might have been souls or might have been moths.

"It's not negotiating," I said. "It's *stalling*. It's keeping you talking until the moon sets, because—" and then I understood, all at once, coldly, completely, "—because the bargain doesn't need your yes. It needs your *time*. Every hour you spend haggling is an hour off the thirteen turns. They don't want your soul, Rosalind. They want the clock."

The reflection's smile finally, finally fell off Rosalind's face, and what was underneath was older and hungrier and much less human.

"Clever paladin," it said. "They broke you for less than that."

"They tried," I said, and drew my sword.

* * *

I won't pretend the fight was glorious. Fighting a reflection is fighting smoke with a grudge. My blade passed through it twice and on the third pass met something solid—a spine of black glass where its heart should have been—and the thing shrieked in a chorus of voices, ours mostly, mine and Rosalind's and others I didn't recognize, and shattered across the Steppe like a dropped window.

The shards didn't lie still. They crawled, weakly, back toward the east, toward the mountain, gathering themselves as they went. An ambassador destroyed returns to file a report. Somewhere beneath the mountain, a clerk was already dipping a pen.

Rosalind stood shaking in the ruins of the fire, and I did the thing I'd sworn I wouldn't do on this journey, the thing that gets knights killed and disgraced and worse: I put my hand on his shoulder.

"You hesitated," I said. Not cruelly. Just true.

"I know."

"For maybe four seconds. Maybe five."

"I know." He wouldn't look at me. "Four seconds where I was back at the forge. Where none of it had happened. Where she was—" His voice cracked clean through. "She used to call me Roz. Nobody else ever did. Our mother died when we were small and it was just the two of us inventing a family out of nicknames, and for four seconds I got to have it back, and gods forgive me, Sera, I *wanted it*."

"Of course you did." I sat down across from him, sword across my knees, because my legs had decided the argument for me. "That's what they sell. Not your old life. The *feeling* of it, four seconds at a time, forever. It's the cruelest product in creation and half the souls in that court bought it willingly."

"How do you know?"

And there it was. The question I'd been dodging since Greyhollow, since the smokehouse, since the moment I recognized the gods' fingerprints on him and chose to break my oath by silence instead of by report. He asked it gently, which was worse. Accusation I could parry. Gentleness went straight through the mail.

"Because I took a version of that bargain," I said.

The crow opened one eye on the pack. Even Corvass was listening. Traitor.

"My order—the Order of the Waning Moon—we kept the old vows. Not the crown's version, the real ones. Shield the god-touched. Shelter the small prayers. Never raise steel against a soul the gods have marked." I turned my sword over in the firelight, watching the moon-pale metal drink the glow. "Eleven years ago they sent me to a village in the Thornmarks. Plague quarantine, they said. Except it wasn't plague. It was god-touched children—thirty of them, marked by waking gods the way Rosalind is marked, the way the whole valley of Greyhollow was built to bury. The crown wanted them burned before the waking spread. My commander ordered it. I refused."

"And?"

"And they did it anyway. I stood in the square and I refused, loudly, at sword-point, and they burned the village around my refusal, and afterward the Order offered me a choice. Be stripped and exiled—or sign a reconciliation. Public recantation. Acknowledgment that my disobedience had been a failure of discipline, that the burning had been regrettable but necessary, that I accepted correction." I heard my own voice go flat and far away, the voice I use for the worst memories, the inventory voice. "I signed it, Rosalind. I stood in the great hall and I recanted, and they let me keep my rank and my oath-metal and my name, and I told myself it was strategy—that a paladin inside the Order could protect the next village, could change it from within."

"Could you?"

"There was no next village. There was never going to be a next village. I'd traded thirty children for a chair in a hall where no one ever again asked my opinion about anything that mattered." I finally looked at him. "That's what the reflection sells. Not your old life. The *chair*. The warm lie that staying inside the walls means you're still fighting. I carried that lie for eleven years, and it wasn't until I found you—a fugitive farrier breaking every law I ever swore to, for the purest reason I've ever seen—that I understood I'd been dead since the signing. Walking around. Notarized."

The fire had burned to coals. Out on the glass, the shards of the ambassador had finished crawling east and gone, and the silence they left was enormous.

"So when you hesitated," I said, "four seconds, five, wanting your sister and your forge and your name back—I'm the last person alive entitled to judge you. I sold thirty children for less. At least your four seconds were only in your head."

Rosalind was quiet a long time. When he spoke, his voice was rough.

"Why are you telling me this? You could have let me think you were granite. Granite's useful on a quest."

"Because you'll hesitate again," I said. "On the mountain, at the gates, when they offer you something shaped exactly like what you lost. And next time I need you to know that the person telling you *don't* has stood where you're standing and paid the toll and regrets it with her whole rotten heart. That's not granite. That's just a map of the pit, drawn by someone who fell in."

He looked at me across the coals for a long moment. Then, slowly, he held out his hand—not to shake, but palm-up, the way farriers offer sugar to strange horses.

"Sera Vane," he said. "Who broke her vow to keep it."

I took his hand. His grip was a smith's grip, warm and certain, and I thought: *this is how trust actually starts. Not with oaths. With two people showing each other the exact shape of their worst night.*

"Reluctantly," I said. "Trust me reluctantly. It lasts longer."

Something that was almost a laugh went through him. Above us the thirteenth moon climbed, one turn shorter than before, and far to the east the mountain sat under its lid of cloud, patient as a ledger.

We slept in shifts after that. I took first watch, and as Rosalind finally drifted down into exhausted sleep, I noticed the crow had left the pack and settled near his head, and that its single human eye was fixed on me with an expression I could only describe as *approval, pending further review*.

"Don't look at me like that," I whispered. "I'm still going to betray him. Or save him. I haven't decided which one they'll call it."

The crow blinked, slow as a judge.

And then it ruffled its feathers, hopped twice to the east, and pointed—with its whole rigid body, the way birds do—at the horizon, where the darkness above the mountain had begun, very faintly, to flicker. Not lightning. Something colder. Regular as a pulse.

Like a gate, opening early.
