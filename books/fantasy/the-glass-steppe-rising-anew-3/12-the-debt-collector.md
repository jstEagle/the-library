## 12. The Debt Collector

The trouble with Saltmere, Sera decided on her third morning there, was that it smelled like everything she'd failed to be.

Salt and lantern oil and fish guts, the brine-stink of a border town that lived off the last traffic before the Steppe. She'd grown up in cities that smelled of incense and candlewax, where the moon-cult's silver bells rang the hours and every child knew the Litany of Turning. Here, nobody rang anything. The lanterns in their salt-crusted cages burned with a stubborn yellow light that had nothing holy in it at all.

She sat in the common room of the Drowned Lantern—of course it was called that—and watched Rosalind argue with a mule dealer over shoeing prices. He was good at it. That was the thing nobody warned you about watching him: he was good at so many ordinary things. Haggling. Reading weather. Finding the exact spot on a mule's pastern where the nail wouldn't seat right. Twelve years of exile had honed him into a tool of perfect mundane usefulness, and beneath all of it the storm sat coiled like a sleeping dog, and he pretended not to feel it, and mostly succeeded.

He caught her looking and raised an eyebrow. *Later*, the eyebrow said. They'd developed an entire vocabulary of eyebrows since leaving Greyhollow, because neither of them trusted their voices not to say too much.

She looked away, out the window, toward the harbor where fishing boats knocked against each other like old men telling the same stories.

That was when she saw the man in grey.

He stood at the end of the pier, and everything about him was almost-right. His coat was almost fashionable. His face was almost forgettable. He stood almost casually. But the gulls wouldn't land near him—they circled wide, screaming—and the tide around his pilings moved wrong, sliding *away* from the shore as though the sea itself had been asked for a favor it didn't want to grant.

Sera's hand found her knife before her mind finished the thought. Undercourt. She'd seen agents of the court exactly once, years ago, during the fall of her order—a grey man walking untouched through burning halls, stepping over bodies with the polite indifference of someone crossing a flooded street. She had been nineteen. She had thrown a moon-blessed hammer at him, and he had caught it, examined it, and set it down gently, the way you'd move something a child had left in your path.

Then he'd bowed to her. She still didn't know why.

She crossed the room and touched Rosalind's shoulder. He tensed under her palm—the farrier reflexes again, the flinch of a man who'd learned that hands from behind meant hoods and ropes—and turned.

"Grey man," she murmured. "End of the pier. Don't look."

He looked. Of course he looked.

"Is that—" he started.

"Yes."

"And they know we're here because?"

"Because the gods are waking and the court keeps ledgers, and we've been asking loud questions in every town between here and Greyhollow." She kept her voice flat. "Or because I'm a disgraced paladin traveling with the most wanted storm-caller in the kingdom and I've been naive enough to sleep in actual inns."

Rosalind exhaled slowly. "Options."

"We can run. We can fight. Or—" she hated herself even as she said it, "—we can hear what he has to say. Courts don't send collectors for executions. Collectors are for debts."

"I don't owe them anything."

"You do," Sera said quietly. "You just don't know the amount."

He stared at her, and she watched him decide—not to trust her, exactly, but to trust that she wouldn't steer him into open water without a reason. It was more than she deserved. It was, she was beginning to suspect, more than anyone deserved, which was a thought she kept burying under work and miles.

They went down to the pier together. The grey man waited with the patience of furniture.

* * *

"Lady Vane." He pronounced it correctly, which was worse than getting it wrong. A stranger would have said *Vayn*. He knew the cult's pronunciation, the one used only by people who'd read her trial records. "And Master Whitlock. Or should I say Master Ferro? Your forge name travels well. Good shoes. Very even tempering."

"What do you want?" Rosalind said.

"To talk. Nothing more." The agent gestured to a bollard as though offering a chair in a parlor. Neither of them sat. He didn't seem to expect them to. "I am called Master Quillon. I collect for the court beneath the mountain. And I have come, Lady Vane, specifically for you."

"For me." Her mouth had gone dry.

"The Order of the Turning Moon was dissolved eleven years ago for heresy and dereliction," Quillon said. He recited it the way a clerk reads grain prices. "Its paladins were stripped of blessing and title. Its grandmaster burned. Its records sealed. All of its members, save one, were either executed or died within the decade of grief, drink, or misadventure." He smiled—it was a small, tidy smile, the smile of a man balancing a column. "Save one."

"I know how many of us are left," Sera said. "I've counted."

"Have you? At night?" He tilted his head. "The court admires survivors, Lady Vane. The court is, above all things, an institution that respects a well-kept account. And your account is remarkable. You were offered clemency at your trial. Renounce the moon, serve the crown's courts instead, and walk free. You refused. Why?"

"Because it was the right refusal."

"Yes," Quillon said, with something almost like warmth. "That is what makes this offer interesting."

Rosalind shifted beside her. "Get to it."

Quillon ignored him with the ease of long practice. "Here is what the court knows. A storm-witch walks toward the Steppe. With him travels a fallen paladin whose blessing, though stripped, still answers her call—which is itself a theological impossibility the court would very much like to study. The court also knows that the witch seeks a soul held in the Ledger of Forfeit, and that such attempts historically end one way: the soul stays, and the seeker becomes another entry." He folded his hands. "The court is prepared to be merciful. To *you*, Lady Vane. Full restoration. Title, blessing, standing. The order refounded under crown charter, with you as its first knight-commander. Everything they took from you, returned. Everything you lost, unlost."

The words went through her like a blade finding the gap in armor she'd worn so long she'd forgotten it had gaps. Knight-commander. Bells ringing the hours. Children learning the Litany from *her* voice. The village she hadn't burned, vindicated. The grandmaster's death meaning something after all.

"And in exchange," she heard herself say, because if she didn't say it, the silence would say it for her, worse.

"In exchange," Quillon agreed, "you deliver the storm-witch to the court's gate at the thirteenth turn. Alive. Willing, ideally—he must come freely, or the ledger won't accept him—but the court is flexible about how freely is achieved. People can be persuaded to want many things, given time and the correct pressures."

The harbor sounds filled the pause. Water. Rope. The screaming gulls, still refusing to land.

"He saved my life on the corpse-road," Sera said. "When the wraiths came through my shield like it was smoke, he pulled the whole storm down around us like a blanket and held it there until dawn. Not a weapon. A roof. For a woman he'd known six weeks."

"Yes," said Quillon. "We are aware of the corpse-road. The court keeps excellent records."

"Then you know what my answer is."

"I know what your answer will be," Quillon said. "I've read your trial transcript. I merely have to make the offer; the making of it is my commission. Refusals count the same as acceptances, in the accounting." He produced, from nowhere, a slip of paper—grey paper, soft-edged, wrong somehow, like a page from a book that had drowned—and held it out. "For the record."

She took it. It was warm. It had her name on it in writing that moved slightly, like something breathing under cloth, and beneath her name, a single line: *Offered and refused, of her own free will.*

The moment her fingers closed on it, it crumbled into ash that smelled like candle smoke and cold iron, and Quillon nodded once, satisfied, a debt entered in a book somewhere far below the world.

"A pleasure, Lady Vane. Master Ferro." He bowed—to both of them, equally, which she noticed, and hated noticing. Then he walked down the pier, and the tide slid back toward the shore behind him, and the gulls finally landed, shrieking, on the spot where he'd stood.

* * *

"Well," Rosalind said, back in the Drowned Lantern, turning his cup without drinking. "That was cheerful."

"They'll keep coming," Sera said. "Collectors are cheap. The offer gets worse each time—or better, depending how much they think you're worth."

"They offered you everything."

"They offered me a leash with a commander's knot." She made herself smile. "I declined. We move tomorrow, before dawn. There's a salt-caravan heading onto the Steppe in two days and I want to be attached to it when it goes."

He studied her across the table, and she felt the look like pressure against a bruise. He was not a stupid man. Twelve years of hiding had made him a connoisseur of concealment; he could smell it on others the way she could smell rain coming.

But he only said, "You'd have taken it? If it had been just you—if the order could've come back and no one else paid?"

She let the question sit. Lied badly on purpose, or told the truth badly—she honestly wasn't sure which came out.

"No," she said. "Ask me tomorrow, though. My answer improves with distance."

That got a real laugh out of him, short and rusty, the first one she'd heard since Saltmere, and she banked it like a coal against the cold she could already feel arriving.

Because here was the thing she did not tell him, walking up the stairs later, lighting the candle in her narrow room: the offer hadn't ended. Quillon had leaned close in the moment before the bow—close enough that only she could hear—and said, *The offer stands until the thirteenth turn, Lady Vane. The court finds that loyalty, like everything else, appreciates under pressure.*

Which meant that every mile closer to the mountain, her betrayal became worth more. Which meant the court wasn't testing her refusal.

It was investing in it.

Sera blew out the candle and lay in the dark listening to the harbor knock against itself, and did her accounting the way she'd been taught—every sin named, numbered, entered. She had refused. That was true, and it was hers, and no ledger could take it.

But she had not told him. And the space between *refused* and *told him* was exactly wide enough for a soul to fall through.

Outside, far across the dark harbor, a single crow sat on the Drowned Lantern's sign, head cocked, one eye catching the lantern-light like a coin at the bottom of a well.

It watched her window until dawn.
