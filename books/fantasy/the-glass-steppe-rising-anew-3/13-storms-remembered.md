## 13. Storms Remembered

The storm-wells announced themselves before we saw them.

It began as a hum in Rosalind's teeth, a vibration that climbed up through his boots and settled somewhere behind his eyes like a second pulse. He'd felt nothing like it since Greyhollow—no. That was a lie, and he was done lying to himself. He'd never felt anything like it. In the valley the power had been a banked coal he smothered every dawn with ritual and routine. This was different. This was a furnace door swinging wide.

"Stop," he said.

Sera Vane reined in her horse and looked back at him, one eyebrow raised in that way she had—half challenge, half assessment, as if she were perpetually deciding whether to arrest him or follow him. Twelve days on the road and she still hadn't decided. He could see it in her shoulders when she thought he wasn't looking.

"The wells," he said. His voice came out wrong. Thin. Stretched. "They're close."

"How close?"

He didn't answer because he didn't know. The Steppe stretched ahead of them, glass-flat and silver under a sky bruising purple at the edges, and somewhere out there beneath that mirror surface the old storms of the world lay sleeping in their pockets of dark stone. He could feel them now the way a man who has been deaf for twelve years feels sound come flooding back—not hearing so much as drowning.

* * *

The first well broke open at dusk.

They'd made camp in the lee of a shelf of rock—the only broken ground they'd seen since dawn—and Rosalind was kneeling over the fire pit, striking flint, when the hum in his skull became a roar. The flint sparked. And the spark did not die.

It hung there in the air, a mote of orange light, and then it *grew*, unwinding into a thread of lightning no thicker than a hair but singing with potential, and Rosalind watched it with the detached horror of a man watching his own hand do something he hadn't commanded.

"Rosalind." Sera's voice, sharp. "Rosalind, let it go."

"I'm not holding anything."

"You are. You're holding everything."

She was right. He could feel it—the whole sky leaning down toward him, the pressure building in clouds that shouldn't exist in a rainless season, static crawling along his arms and lifting the hairs there like a field of tiny black needles. The spark-thread thickened. Somewhere far to the east, thunder answered him, rolling across the Steppe like something vast turning over in its sleep.

He tried to do what he'd always done. Breathe. Count. Name the things he could see: flint, iron, wool blanket, the scarred knuckles of his own hands. The litany that had kept him small and safe and nameless for twelve years.

It didn't work. The storm wasn't outside him anymore. It was in the well beneath them, and the well had found what it had been looking for, and it wanted *out*.

"Rosalind!" Sera dropped to her knees beside him and grabbed his wrist.

Lightning jumped from his skin to hers—a white-blue arc that snapped and crackled—and instead of crying out she *leaned into it*. Her free hand came up, palm outward, fingers spread, and moonlight poured off it. Not reflected moonlight; there was no moon yet, just the last bruised smear of dusk. This was older. Silver light that moved like water and smelled like cold stone and myrrh, and where it touched the lightning the two forces braided together, held each other, quieted.

"Breathe," she said. Her face was very close. Her grey eyes had gone pale, almost luminous, and he could see the strain in them—the blessing costing her something, drawing on reserves she'd sworn never to touch again. "In through the nose. Out slow. I've got the excess. Just breathe."

He breathed.

Slowly—so slowly—the pressure eased. The thread of lightning dimmed, thinned, and sank back into the flint like a snake returning to grass. The clouds unraveled. The hum faded from a roar to a murmur to a whisper to nothing, and Rosalind found himself flat on his back on the cold glass of the Steppe, chest heaving, Sera Vane's hand still wrapped around his wrist like a shackle.

"Well," he managed. "That's new."

"Don't." She released him and sat back on her heels, wiping sweat from her brow with the back of her hand. In the dying firelight she looked ten years older than she had that morning. "Don't joke. Not about this."

"Why not? It helps."

"It doesn't help." She stood abruptly and walked to the edge of the firelight, arms crossed, staring out at the darkening plain. He watched the set of her shoulders and understood: she was frightened. Not of him—or not only of him. Of what she'd just done. What it meant.

"Sera."

"Don't." 

"Sera. How much did that cost you?"

A long silence. When she turned, her face had composed itself into the paladin's mask he knew so well by now—impassive, professional, closed. But her hands wouldn't stop trembling, and she shoved them into her cloak before he could see more.

"Eat your supper, farrier," she said. "We ride at dawn."

* * *

But sleep wouldn't come for either of them, and an hour past full dark they found themselves sitting on opposite sides of the rebuilt fire, neither pretending anymore that rest was possible.

The Steppe at night was its own country. The glass surface caught the starlight and threw it back doubled, so that they seemed to sit in an island of flame adrift in a sea of falling stars. Far off, one of the wells glowed faintly beneath the plain—a blue-white shimmer like moonlight drowned in deep water.

"That's what woke me," Rosalind said quietly. "Back in Greyhollow. The gods stirring under the Steppe—it traveled, somehow, even into the valley where magic can't follow. Like a bell rung in another room." He turned his hands over, studying the calluses, the old burn scars from the forge. "I spent twelve years thinking I'd buried it. All that time I was just waiting for someone to knock."

"And now everything's knocking." Sera fed a stick to the fire. "The wells. Corvass. The Undercourt." A pause. "Me."

"You?"

Her jaw tightened. She stared into the flames, and for a long moment he thought she wouldn't answer. Then, without looking at him:

"What I did today. The anchoring. That's not supposed to be possible anymore. The moon-blessing isn't a tool you keep in a drawer, Rosalind. It's a covenant. It flows through vow, through order, through the chain of consecration going all the way back to the Moon-Mother herself. And I—" Her voice caught, steadied. "I burned my chain twelve years ago. Same as you buried your storms. When the Order fell, I renounced it. Every oath. Every grace. I told the Mother to take her light and give it to someone worthier."

"But she didn't."

"No. She didn't." Something raw moved through her expression, quickly mastered. "Today, when I reached for it—expecting nothing, expecting ash—it came. Full strength. Like it had been waiting." She laughed, short and bitter. "Do you understand what that means? It means I lied to myself for twelve years. It means the vows never broke, because *I* never stopped meaning them. I just stopped believing I deserved them."

Rosalind said nothing. There was nothing to say that wouldn't be either pity or platitude, and she'd gut him with her sword before she accepted either.

Instead he said, "Tell me why. The Order. Why it fell. You've danced around it since Saltmere, and I've let you, because I figured you'd earned the right to your own timing." He looked at her across the fire, steady. "But you anchored me tonight with grace you think you don't deserve. So I think you should tell me while you're still angry enough to say it true."

Another long silence. Out on the plain, the drowned moonlight of the well pulsed once, twice, like a slow heartbeat.

"There was a village," Sera said finally. "Godswatch, it was called, out on the western marches. Small place. Farmers, a mill, a shrine to the old gods that nobody had tended in a generation. And then the gods started waking—this same waking, twelve years ago, the first stirrings—and the children there started... changing. God-touched. Marked. One boy had crow feathers growing along his spine. A girl's shadow moved wrong, independent. Little Mara, seven years old, whose tears fell upward." Her mouth twisted. "My order's mandate was purification. Containment of divine contamination. The Grandmaster sent three hundred swords to Godswatch to burn it clean before the crown noticed and used it as pretext against us."

"And you refused."

"I rode ahead of them." Now she looked at him, and her eyes were bright and hard and terrible. "One woman against three hundred. I stood on the shrine steps and I declared the village under moon-sanctuary, which is the oldest law we have—older than the Order, older than the crown. And then I waited to see which of my brothers and sisters would strike me down."

"Did they?"

"No." Her laugh was hollow. "Worse. They hesitated. Long enough for the villagers to flee, long enough for the children to scatter into the hills. And in that hesitation the whole edifice cracked. Because if sanctuary could be declared by one paladin and honored by three hundred, then the Grandmaster's authority was nothing but breath. The order tore itself apart within the year—sanctuary faction against purification faction. By the time the smoke cleared, the Order of the Silver Vigil existed only in disgrace and fragments, and I was its most famous traitor." She spread her hands. "So there it is. The crime of Sera Vane: she loved the wrong thing, at the wrong time, in front of everyone."

Rosalind turned this over. The fire popped. Somewhere in the darkness beyond the light, a crow called once—a single dry note that might have been Corvass listening, might have been nothing.

"They call you disgraced," he said at last. "The stories I heard in taverns, before I learned to stop listening—they talk about the paladin who broke her order."

"I did break it."

"No." He shook his head slowly. "You *held* it. For one day, in one place, it actually stood for what it claimed to stand for. Everything after was just the structure catching up to the truth you carried." He shrugged, self-conscious suddenly. "I know how that works. I killed a king's heir. Everyone agrees on that. Nobody asks what the heir was doing when the storm took him."

Sera went very still. "What was he doing?"

"Burning a granary." The words came easier than he expected, like a splinter working loose. "In Wrenhold. My home, before Greyhollow. There was a famine that winter and the crown's tithe collectors decided the stores belonged to the king regardless of who starved. The heir presided personally. I was seventeen, and the storm was already in me, untrained, terrified—and when I saw the torch go to the grain, I didn't call anything. I didn't decide anything. I just *broke open*." His hands curled. "Forty dead in the lightning. The heir among them. And my sister—my little sister Wren, nine years old, who hid me in the smokehouse while the soldiers searched—she sold the only thing she had to buy my exile instead of my execution. Her soul. To the Undercourt. A child's bargain, signed in her own blood, and I have spent twelve years being alive on the interest of that loan."

The fire had burned low. Sera rose, fetched another armload of scrub-wood from their dwindling store, and built the fire up carefully, deliberately, the way she did everything—as if even tending flames were a discipline worthy of devotion.

"My grandmaster taught us," she said, settling back down, "that grace is not a reward. It's a current. It finds the channel that's open and it flows, whether or not the channel believes itself worthy. Tonight, when I anchored you, I finally understood—I was never cut off. I was just standing upstream of my own life, refusing to let it move." She met his eyes. "You buried yourself in a valley. I exiled myself inside my own oath. We've both been living in Greyhollow, Rosalind. One of us just had better scenery."

Despite everything—the ache in his bones, the memory of lightning crawling his skin, the thirteen-turning clock ticking down over Wren's soul—he laughed. A real laugh, startled out of him, rough from disuse.

"Better scenery," he repeated. "That's generous. My valley smelled like horse hooves and hot iron."

"And mine smelled like incense and hypocrisy." She smiled then, small and tired and real, and for a moment the firelight made her look young. "Get some sleep, storm-caller. Tomorrow we cross the first of the wells proper, and I need you sane."

"And you? Sleeping?"

"I'll take first watch."

"You took first watch last night."

"I did." She drew her cloak around her and settled against her saddle, sword across her knees, silver light still ghosting faintly at her fingertips where the blessing lingered like warmth in cupped hands. "And I'll take it again. Some vigils, farrier, you keep because you can't sleep. And some you keep because the thing you're watching over finally matters."

Rosalind lay back on the hard glass of the Steppe and pulled his blanket to his chin. Above him the stars doubled themselves in the mirror-plain, and he fell asleep looking down into that other sky, feeling—for the first time in twelve years—not hunted by the storm in his blood, but accompanied.

He did not see Sera turn, in the deepest hour of the night, toward the eastern dark—where, on the horizon, a light had appeared that was not a storm-well. It was thin and red and deliberate, a lantern-signal, blinking in a pattern she knew from a lifetime ago. A pattern that meant, in the old cant of the Silver Vigil:

*We have found you. Come alone, or we come for what you carry.*

Her hand drifted to her sword hilt. The blessing flickered at her fingertips, and did not go out.
