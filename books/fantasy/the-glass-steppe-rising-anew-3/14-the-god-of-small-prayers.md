## 14. The God of Small Prayers

The crow arrived at dawn, as it always did now.

Rosalind had stopped being surprised by that. Twelve years in Greyhollow taught him the value of routine—feed the forge before the light came up, bank the coals, keep your hands busy and your name quiet—and the crow had simply folded itself into the pattern like a coin slipped into a pocket. It landed on the anvil's horn while he was still shaking the frost off his gloves, tilted its head with one human eye gleaming amber, and said:

"You dreamt about her again."

"I don't want to talk about it."

"Nobody ever wants to talk about it. That's why gods exist." The crow—Corvass, when he bothered to introduce himself properly; *the bird* most other times, in Rosalind's head—hopped down to the quenching trough and drank from the rim where the ice hadn't quite taken. "You called for me in your sleep. Did you know that? Not my name. You don't know my name. You just said *please* in a very particular tone, and prayers travel, boy. Prayers are the only currency I deal in."

Rosalind set down the rasp he'd been cleaning and looked at the god. It was easier than looking at the empty road south, which was what he'd been doing instead of sleeping. "I didn't pray."

"Everyone prays. Most of them just call it something else." Corvass shook water off his beak. "A wish. A curse. A bargain struck with yourself at three in the morning when you can't stop doing arithmetic on a thing that happened twelve years ago. That's all prayer is. Attention pointed past the edge of the world." He fluffed his feathers, and for a moment the black of them went deep and wrong, feathers like a hole cut in the air, and then he was just a crow again, scruffy and ordinary. "And lately, someone's been answering. Which brings us to why I'm here, sitting on cold iron at an hour no self-respecting god should be conscious."

Sera was coming up the lane. Rosalind could see her through the open forge door—a tall shape wrapped in a gray traveling cloak, moving with that paladin's stride she couldn't unlearn, each step placed like she expected the ground to owe her an accounting. She'd slept badly too. He could tell by the way she carried her left shoulder. She carried guilt there, he'd noticed. Right next to whatever was left of her faith.

"Good," Corvass said, following his gaze. "She'll want to hear this. And I'd rather say it once. Repeating myself is for lesser deities."

* * *

They gathered in the smokehouse because it was the only building in Greyhollow with a lock Rosalind trusted and walls thick enough to hold a secret. Sera lit the lantern. Corvass perched on the beam above the hanging hams, which struck Rosalind as either deeply irreverent or exactly right for a god whose congregation was mostly farmers asking after their pigs.

"Tell them," Sera said. She'd taken the news of the waking gods harder than Rosalind had, in some ways. To him it was dread. To her it was a professional catastrophe—an entire cosmology failing to stay dead on her watch. "All of it. You've been circling the edges for two days. Circle time's over."

Corvass preened. "Direct. I remember when paladins were polite."

"I remember when gods answered prayers. Times change."

That landed somewhere between them, in the space where neither would look directly. Rosalind leaned against the wall, arms crossed, and waited. Waiting was a farrier's skill. Horses could smell impatience, and so, apparently, could gods.

"Very well," Corvass said. "Here is what you think you know: the old gods died. Or faded, or went to sleep—the theologians argued about the word for four hundred years and produced nothing but careers. The courts rose in their absence. The Undercourt beneath the mountain learned to collect souls, and the Crown learned to sell them, and everyone agreed the arrangement was efficient, and efficiency is what people build when they've stopped believing anything larger exists."

"And?" Rosalind asked.

"And that story is wrong. We didn't die. We *starved*." The word came out flat and enormous. The lantern flame bent toward the crow, as if leaning in. "A god is not a creature, Rosalind. I know I wear feathers and crack jokes and drink from your trough like any common thief-bird, but the feathers are a courtesy. What I actually am is a *relationship*. I am every small prayer ever spoken over a dead lamb, every whispered *please let it rain*, every mother's breath against a fevered forehead. Take away the prayers and there's nothing left of me but habit." His head turned, that amber eye fixing on Rosalind with terrible gentleness. "The courts took our congregations. Not with armies—with ledgers. Why pray to a god who might not answer, when you can buy a guaranteed outcome? Certainty is addictive. Souls flowed downhill into the Undercourt's vaults, and we starved on the mountainside, and the world called it death because the alternative was admitting the world had been robbed."

Sera had gone very still. "The moon-cult taught that the gods withdrew. Chose silence as discipline."

"The moon-cult taught what the moon-cult was paid to teach. Your order kept a trickle of devotion flowing—enough to keep a few of us breathing—but the Grand Synod signed concords with the Undercourt two centuries ago, and every paladin who found out what those concords cost was quietly broken and disgraced." Corvass's beak clicked. "Ask Vane how her order treats the ones who ask questions."

"Don't," Sera said. But her hand had gone to her left shoulder again.

Rosalind felt the floor of the conversation tilt under him, the way it had been tilting for weeks now, every certainty he'd built his exile on revealing itself as scaffolding around a much larger structure. He made himself ask the question anyway. "So the gods wake because—what? The Undercourt got greedy?"

"The Undercourt got *careless*." Corvass dropped from the beam and landed on the cutting table between them, close enough that Rosalind could see the human eye wasn't just shaped like a human eye—it had a whole person in it, or the memory of one. "Souls are currency, yes, but currency needs circulation. The court began hoarding. Vaulting. Sealing souls behind contracts so baroque that even the signatories can't read them anymore. And here is the part you need to understand, farrier, the part that will make you angry, and should: a hoarded soul doesn't stop praying. It can't. Prayer isn't a choice. It's what a soul *is*—attention aimed at something beyond itself. Thousands of sealed souls, stacked in the dark, all of them wishing. All of them whispering *please* into vault-stone that never answers." He paused. "Until it started to answer. Because prayer has to go somewhere, and enough of it, pressed hard enough against the world, finds cracks."

"So we woke up because the Undercourt's prisoners prayed us back to life," Sera said slowly.

"In essence. The vaults became temples without knowing it. The hoard became a congregation. And the old gods opened their eyes in the dark and heard—" Corvass's voice did something strange then, something that didn't fit in a crow's throat, "—heard *ten thousand voices saying please*, and we remembered what we were for."

The lantern guttered. Frost feathered up the inside of the smokehouse wall, blooming in patterns like ferns, like feathers, like the frost that had come the night Old Marta prayed over her dying husband and something had answered. Rosalind stared at it and felt the storm under his skin turn over in its sleep.

"That's the cosmic architecture," he said. "Fine. Beautiful. Horrifying. Now tell me why it matters that *I'm* standing here."

Corvass didn't answer immediately. He walked the length of the table, claws clicking, and stopped in front of Rosalind, and the amber eye held him the way a hook holds a fish.

"Because a conduit is not the same as a congregation," the god said. "The prayers of the sealed souls woke us—all of us, everywhere, faintly. But prayers need a *debtor*, Rosalind. That's the oldest law, older than the courts, older than names. Nothing given freely binds. Every gift must be owed, or it drifts. When a god answers a prayer, a debt opens—and the debt must land somewhere. On the one who prayed, usually. But the sealed souls can't pay. They have nothing left to pay with. So their prayers have been landing..." He spread one wing, encompassing the smokehouse, the valley, the twelve years of Rosalind's careful gray life. "*Loose.*"

"No," Rosalind said. He heard how it sounded—flat, final, a man bracing a door with his body. "No. I know what you're going to say."

"Do you? Then say it with me." Corvass's voice softened into something almost kind, which was worse. "Every unanswered prayer in this valley for twelve years has been pooling in the magicless dark, looking for a vessel. And the valley's one magicless man—the exile, the storm-blooded, the one whose power sleeps so deep it's practically a vault of its own—you weren't empty, Rosalind. You were *available*. A locked room in a house with no other doors. Every *please* spoken under Greyhollow's silent sky has been sliding under your skin for twelve years, and you thought it was just the ache. Just the weight of a half-lived life." The eye blinked. "It was the weight. It was ten thousand weights. You are not haunted, farrier. You are *funded*."

The frost on the walls had reached the ceiling. Sera's breath showed white in the sudden cold, and she'd drawn her knife without seeming to notice she'd done it—not at the crow, just at the world, at the shape of what was being said.

"The storm I felt the night Marta prayed," Rosalind said. Each word came out measured, the way he spoke to horses about to bolt. "That was me. That was mine?"

"That was the first payment arriving. Your power woke because the debts came due, and you are the account they draw on." Corvass hopped closer. "Understand the design of it. The Undercourt took everything from you—your name, your sister, your sky. They made you a hollow place. And hollow places fill. Their own cruelty built the vessel their prisoners' prayers needed. There is a symmetry there that even I find elegant, and I find nearly everything tedious."

"Then take it back." Rosalind pushed off the wall. The cold was in his teeth now, and underneath the cold, deeper, something vast turning over, restless, *listening*. "Take it back. Whatever's pooled in me—I didn't ask for it. I never wanted to be anyone's answer. I wanted to shoe horses and stay invisible and count the moons until I died of old age in a valley that forgot me. Find another vessel."

"Can't."

"*Can't,*" Rosalind repeated, and laughed, and the laugh came out with a crackle in it that made the lantern jump. "Convenient."

"It's not convenient, it's *law*, and I hate law more than anyone alive, but there it is." Corvass ruffled up, and for a moment he looked less like a god and more like a tired bird in a cold room. "Debts attach. Yours attached the day you crossed the valley line with your power buried and your grief intact. Unmaking it would mean unmaking twelve years of accumulated prayer—which means unmaking every desperate *please* this valley has spoken since you arrived, including the ones that worked. Including the fever that broke. Including the rain that came the summer the wells went dry. You've been answering prayers for a decade, Rosalind Whitlock. You've just been doing it asleep."

The silence after that had texture. Sera sheathed her knife, slowly. Outside, a rooster announced a dawn that already felt like it belonged to a different world.

"Wren," Rosalind said finally.

"What about her?"

"Her soul's been in their vaults for twelve years. Praying." His voice stayed level. It cost him. "Sealed. Whispering *please* into the dark. Was she—did some of that—"

"Yes." Corvass didn't dress it up. "Some of what's pooled in you is hers. Her prayers found the nearest hollow place, and the nearest hollow place was her brother." He cocked his head. "That's why the crow found you before the others. That's why you heard your childhood name in a bird's beak. She's been reaching for you through the debt since the day they locked her away. You aren't carrying strangers' wishes, farrier. You're carrying a congregation—and its choir lead knows every song you know."

Rosalind sat down. There was a stool by the cutting table and his legs simply decided the matter for him. Twelve years of arithmetic—the same sum he'd run every night for a decade, *her price against my crime, my exile against her soul*—and it had never once occurred to him that the ledger ran both directions. That while he was counting what he'd lost, she might have been finding ways to send it back.

"She's been talking to me," he said. "For twelve years. And I thought it was grief."

"Grief is a prayer," Corvass said. "I told you. Everyone prays."

Sera crouched in front of him, and her face had the expression he was learning to read—the paladin's discipline wrestling something older and warmer underneath. "This changes the math," she said. "If the court's own prisoners powered the gods' return, then the court isn't just hoarding souls. It's sitting on the powder that's going to blow its vaults open. They'll know. They must already know. Which means when we get to the mountain—"

"They won't be guarding a treasury," Rosalind finished. "They'll be guarding against a *revival*. And they'll want the conduit." He looked down at his hands. Storm-scarred, burn-marked, steady on the anvil, trembling now. "Me. They sold me cheap twelve years ago. They'll pay anything to buy me back."

"Not buy," Corvass said. "The Undercourt doesn't buy. It collects. And it has an original contract with your name on it, signed in blood-price the night your sister knelt in the dark and traded herself for your life. They don't need to hunt you, farrier. They just need you to walk through their gate. The contract does the rest."

Frost covered the walls entirely now, silver fern-patterns climbing into the rafters, and in the middle of the cold Rosalind felt the pooled weight of ten thousand answered prayers shift inside him like a tide finding its channel. Somewhere under his breastbone, threaded through the storm, something small and fierce and familiar pressed against the inside of his skin—once, gently, the way Wren used to knock on his door twice before letting herself in.

"Corvass," he said. "When the thirteenth moon turns. When they seal the gates and the vaults go permanent. What happens to the prayers? To the congregation in the dark?"

The crow was quiet long enough that the lantern flame steadied.

"Nothing good," the god said at last. "Which is why, farrier, you need to decide what you're going to do with ten thousand voices and one pair of hands—before the moon turns. And before the court decides for you."

Through the frost-laced window, low over the eastern ridge, the moon hung pale and thin in the daylight sky—waning, waning, running out of itself like a candle in a draft.

Two turns left.

Rosalind stood up, and the storm under his skin stood with him, and for the first time in twelve years it didn't feel like a prisoner waking. It felt like an army.

"Then we'd better move," he said.
