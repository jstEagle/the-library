## 15. The Moon's Twelfth Waning

Sera Vane had kept vigil by moonlight for twenty years, and she knew its faces the way a shepherd knows his flock. The waxing moon was a promise. The full moon was an oath. The waning was a debt coming due.

Tonight the moon hung over the Glass Steppe like a coin worn thin by spending, one sliver of silver left of the twelve turns since the gods woke. Thirteen nights from now—or fourteen, if the court counted generously, and the court never counted generously—the thirteenth turn would seal the Undercourt's gates for another age.

And Wren Whitlock's soul would be locked inside with it. Forever.

Sera stood the last watch before dawn, because she no longer trusted herself to sleep. Sleep brought dreams, and dreams lately brought the Debt Collector's voice, smooth as poured tallow: *The offer stands, paladin. His name for your honor restored.* She had refused him once on the road to Saltmere. Refusal, she was learning, was not a single act but a discipline, renewed nightly, like prayer.

She checked the camp without waking anyone. Rosalind slept curled against his pack like a man who'd learned not to take up space, storm-light flickering faintly beneath the skin of his forearms even now, even dreaming. The crow—Corvass, when he deigned to speak—roosted on the tent pole with one human eye open, watching her watch them.

"You count the nights too," she said quietly.

The crow clicked its beak. "I count everything. It's what I'm for." Corvass's voice came out of the bird's throat like wind through a keyhole. "Twelve turns gone. One remains. Do you know what happens at the thirteenth turn, paladin? Not the sealing. Everyone fears the sealing. I mean what happens *before*."

"Tell me."

"The court opens its books." The crow ruffled its feathers, and for a moment Sera saw something older looking out of that black body—a god wearing a bird the way a man wears a coat too large for him. "For one night, every debt in the Ledger of Forfeit is called for review. Every soul, every bargain, every clause read aloud in the Deep Hall. It is the one night the Undercourt shows its work."

"And why would they do that?"

"Because they are proud," Corvass said. "Because their power is the power of the properly recorded thing. And because—" the human eye fixed on her, unblinking, "—they believe no one who hears the terms can do anything about them anyway."

Sera looked at Rosalind sleeping, at the storm under his skin, and felt the old paladin arithmetic begin: risk weighed against souls, odds against duty. Except her order was ash and her oath was broken and all she had left was the arithmetic itself, honed sharp by twenty years of counting what could not be saved.

"One night," she said. "One reading. And then the gates seal at dawn?"

"At dawn," Corvass agreed. "Which means whatever we steal, we steal in the dark, in front of the whole court, with every ledger-clerk and hunter in the mountain watching the books being balanced." He paused. "I want you to understand the shape of this. You are not planning a theft anymore. You are planning a performance."

* * *

Rosalind woke badly.

Sera heard it from across the cold camp—the hitch in his breathing, the sudden crackle of static, frost blooming on the bedroll where his hands gripped. She was beside him before she'd decided to move, one hand hovering over his shoulder, not touching. Touching a waking storm was a lesson you learned once.

"Whitlock." She kept her voice level. "You're here. You're on the Steppe. Breathe out longer than you breathe in—that's how my order taught it. Out longer than in."

His eyes opened, gray as slate before rain. For three heartbeats he didn't know her. Then he did, and the shame arrived, and the shame made the static worse, so she talked over it, fast and low.

"What did you dream?"

"Wren." He sat up slowly. Frost slid off the bedroll and became ordinary dew. "She was standing in a hall of black shelves taller than cathedrals, and every shelf was full of little lights, and she was reading the spines. She looked up at me and said—" his jaw worked, "—she said, 'Rosie, they're afraid of the counting.'"

Sera went very still.

Corvass dropped from the tent pole and landed between them, head cocked. "Say that again," the god said. "Exactly."

"'They're afraid of the counting.'" Rosalind rubbed his face. "Then the shelves started humming, all of them, like struck bells, and she laughed. I haven't heard her laugh since she was nine years old. Then I woke up."

The crow was silent long enough that Sera glanced down at it—and found the bird trembling. A god, trembling.

"Dream-contact," Corvass said at last. "Through me, though she doesn't know she's using me. Twelve years in the vaults and she's learned to walk the ledgers in her sleep." The human eye blinked. "Paladin. Farrier. I am going to tell you something I have told no mortal in nine hundred years, and you will understand why the telling costs me."

"We're listening," Sera said.

"The Undercourt does not fear storms. It does not fear paladins, or thieves, or armies. Armies die and get filed. What it fears is *error*—a misread clause, an unpaid debt discovered, a soul held against terms that were never validly struck. Because the court's entire dominion rests on the sanctity of its own records. If the records are wrong, the walls are wrong. If the walls are wrong—" the crow spread both wings, and for one instant Sera saw them as they truly were: vast, star-shot, ragged as torn night, "—everything filed inside walks out."

* * *

They broke camp in the blue hour and walked, because the Steppe allowed no other speed. The glass ground rang under their boots like a struck bowl. Behind them, far off, the mountain of the Undercourt had become visible two days past—a black molar rising from the mirror-plain, wearing clouds like a crown it hadn't earned.

Sera walked beside Rosalind and laid it out for him the way she'd once laid out battle-plans for knights better than she'd ever been.

"Thirteen nights. On the final night, the court reads its ledgers aloud—the Night of Review. Every forfeited soul gets named, valued, and confirmed. Including Wren's. At dawn, the gates seal and the books close until the next age."

"So we get in before the reading," Rosalind said. "Find her shelf. Take her light."

"No." Sera shook her head. "Corvass says the vaults only open to the reading. And we couldn't carry her out anyway—you don't just pick up a soul, Whitlock, it has to be *released*, formally, by someone with authority over the debt. Which means we don't break into the court." She met his eyes. "We attend it. We stand in the Deep Hall during the Night of Review, and when Wren's entry is read, we challenge it. Publicly. Before the whole court."

"Challenge it how? We don't have standing. We don't have—"

"You have standing," Corvass said from the air above them. "You are the counterparty to the original debt. Your exile-price purchased her soul; therefore your blood is on the contract; therefore you may contest its terms. That is not my law, farrier. That is *their* law. They wrote it to be unbreakable. Unbreakable laws cut both ways."

Rosalind stopped walking. The glass rang and went silent around him, and Sera felt the pressure drop the way it did before weather—his weather—arrived.

"They'll hear the challenge and laugh," he said. "Then they'll hunt us."

"Probably," Sera said.

"And if we lose the challenge, we're standing inside the walls when the gates seal."

"Yes."

He looked east toward the mountain for a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice was quiet and terribly steady. "Twelve years ago I traded everything to keep her soul out of their hands and failed at even that. I'm not walking away from the one night they open the books." He started forward again. "So teach me the law, paladin. All of it. Whatever you remember from your order's days fighting them. I want clauses. I want precedents. I want to know their rules better than the clerks who serve them."

Sera felt something in her chest that she refused to call hope, because hope was a luxury for people whose orders hadn't burned. But it sat there anyway, warm and unwelcome, like a coal in cupped hands.

"I'll teach you," she said. "But understand what you're asking. My order spent three centuries studying Undercourt law, and do you know what we learned? That the court cannot be beaten by force, cannot be beaten by trickery, cannot be beaten by righteousness. It can only be beaten by *precision*. By finding the one clause, the one error, the one word miswritten four hundred years ago, and holding it up to the light in front of everyone." She grimaced. "It is the slowest, most tedious form of war ever devised, and it is the only one that works."

"Good," Rosalind said. "I've had twelve years of shoeing horses. I can be precise."

* * *

They made the salt-flats by dusk, where the Steppe's glass gave way to crusty white pans that crunched like old snow, and there Sera built the fire small and began the teaching. She drew the court's structure in salt with a stick: the Gates of Assessment, the Hall of Weights, the Deep Hall where the Ledger of Forfeit lived, the Steward's tiers above.

"Three things will kill us," she said, tapping the salt-map. "First: entry. The court admits debtors, claimants, and those carrying a debt freely owed—we knew that already, it's why we came this way. Second: the challenge itself. When Wren's entry is read, you must state your objection in formal terms, in the Old Formula, or the clerks won't even record it. Unrecorded objections don't exist. Third—"

"Third," Corvass said, dropping onto a salt hummock, "is the Hunter-Chief. Vessik. He attends every Review. He has unmade forty-one challengers in nine centuries, and he will smell the storm on you from across the hall."

Rosalind fed a stick to the fire. "Then I suppose I learn to smell him first."

Sera watched him across the flames—young still, under the gray and the weariness, with his sister's same stubborn set to the mouth—and thought about the Debt Collector's offer, sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone. *His name for your honor restored.* She had refused. She would refuse again. She was almost sure she would refuse again.

And beneath that stone, another thought, colder: thirteen nights. In thirteen nights either Wren Whitlock walked free or she didn't, and either Rosalind Whitlock survived his own storm or he didn't, and either Sera Vane finally paid for the village she'd saved by burning her own life down, or she didn't.

The moon rose, one turn from dead, thin as a paring.

"Paladin," Rosalind said suddenly, looking up from the salt-map. "This Night of Review. The court reads every entry. Every soul. Aloud. In front of everyone."

"That's the tradition, yes."

"Then Wren will hear it too." His eyes were bright and wet and fierce. "She'll know we came. She'll know I'm standing right there in the hall. Whatever happens after—" his voice caught, steadied, "—whatever happens, she'll know I came for her."

Sera looked at him and thought: *that is either the thing that saves us or the thing that breaks us, and I no longer have the wisdom to tell which.*

"Get some sleep, Whitlock," she said instead. "Tomorrow I teach you the Old Formula. It has nine hundred words and every one of them can kill you if you say it wrong."

He lay back. Within minutes his breathing slowed—but the storm-light under his skin did not fade tonight, and Sera sat watch alone with the fire, counting.

Above the salt-flats, far to the east, the mountain's crown of clouds lit from within—once, twice—like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

And out of the dark between the stars, a second light detached itself and began moving toward their camp. Small. Steady. Wrong.

Not a crow. Not a bird at all.

A lantern, swinging on no hand, coming across the salt-flats at a walker's pace, and Sera knew—before it reached the firelight, before the first whisper of tallow-scent reached her—exactly whose voice would be inside it.

The Debt Collector had come to make his offer a third time.

And this time, she saw with sinking certainty as the lantern stopped at the edge of the light, he had not come alone.
