## 16. The Hollow King's Road

The road began where the grass gave up.

One stride we were walking on Steppe turf, dry and silvered and whispering underfoot; the next, the ground beneath us was pale as knucklebone and smooth as a riverbed after the water's gone. It stretched away north in a long, unbroken seam, wide enough for six wagons abreast, and it did not bend. Roads bend around hills. This one went through them—or rather, the hills had been made to move aside, or had never been permitted to rise at all.

"A god built this," Sera said.

"A god died building it," Corvass answered from my shoulder, and his talons shifted in a way I had learned to read as discomfort. "The Hollow King. He was old when your moon-cult was young. He walked the Steppe carrying the dead to wherever dead things go, and the road is the path he wore into the world."

"And now he's gone," I said.

"Now he's *hollow*," the crow said. "There is a difference, and you will feel it before the day is out. Walk in the center. Do not touch the verges. And whatever calls your name—"

"Let me guess," I said. "Don't answer."

"Don't answer *kindly*. That's the trap. They won't ask for help like beggars. They'll ask like children. Like your sister asking you to look at something she found. Answering kindly is how they get their fingers into the seam of you."

I looked down the road, at all that bone-pale distance, and thought: *twelve years I kept myself closed like a fist, and now every mile teaches me a new way to be opened.*

"All right," I said. "Show me how to walk it."

We walked.

* * *

By midday I understood why they called it hollow.

The road wasn't silent. Silence would have been a mercy. Instead there was a sound underneath the sound—the wind moving over us but not through us, the way wind moves over an empty jar—and if I let my attention slip toward it, the emptiness leaned back. Not hostile. Hungry the way an open doorway is hungry, the way a question is hungry. The road had been built to carry something toward rest, and it had been carrying nothing for so long that it wanted *cargo.*

"They're here already," Sera murmured. Her hand rested on the pommel of her sword without gripping it. She'd stopped wearing the sunburst sigil days ago, but she still moved like a woman whose body remembered being armor. "I can feel them the way you feel someone standing behind you in a dark room."

"How many?"

"How many souls has the Undercourt hoarded? How many have slipped its ledgers and gotten lost on their way to wherever?" She shrugged, and the shrug was worse than any number. "Enough."

Corvass ruffled his feathers. "The court doesn't lose them," he said. "It *drops* them. A soul in transit is a soul that might change its mind about being sold. Easier to let a few fall by the roadside than risk them arriving with objections." He tilted his head at the empty road ahead. "These are the ones who fell. Some centuries ago. Some last winter."

I thought of Wren. Twelve years in the vaults of the Undercourt, and I had spent those years telling myself she was asleep in there, dreaming, untouched. But a soul is not a coin in a strongbox. A soul is a fire in a room, and rooms have doors, and doors have hinges that rust, and—

"Stop that," Sera said quietly.

"Stop what?"

"You've gone somewhere. Your jaw does a thing." She glanced at me sidelong. "Whatever you're imagining, imagining it twice as bad won't make finding her feel twice as good later."

"That's not how fear works," I said.

"No," she agreed. "Fear works however it likes. That's rather the problem with it." And she lengthened her stride, which was how Sera Vane ended conversations she didn't want to have.

* * *

They came at dusk, and they did not come like monsters.

The light was failing—that strange Steppe dusk where the sky goes the color of hammered tin and the mirror-flat land doubles everything, so the horizon seems to close over your head like a book. And out of that doubled light, shapes gathered on the road ahead of us. At first I took them for heat-shimmer, then for mist, and then one of them resolved enough to break my heart.

A child. A girl of perhaps seven, standing barefoot on the pale road, her outline soft as breath on glass. She wasn't translucent, exactly. She was *unfinished*, the way a sketch is unfinished—all the important lines present, all the detail still promised. And she looked at us with eyes that had nothing behind them but want, and she said:

"Mister? Mister, I lost my mother."

Every hair on my arms stood up. Because it wasn't the voice of a wraith. It wasn't a moan or a shriek. It was a small girl's voice, pitched exactly right, cracked exactly where a tired child's voice cracks, and I felt my mouth begin to shape an answer before my mind caught up.

*I lost my mother.*

*So did I,* some traitorous part of me wanted to say. *In a way. I lost her to a bargain I couldn't see and a debt I'm still paying.*

"Rosalind." Corvass's beak pressed hard against my ear. "*Kindness.* There it is. Feel it pulling?"

I felt it. Gods, I felt it. It felt exactly like love.

"I see her," I managed, through teeth I hadn't realized I'd clenched. "What do I do?"

"What you must never do is comfort her. Comfort is a door. What you can do—" the crow paused, and I felt him weighing something, deciding how much truth to spend on me, "—is what the Hollow King did. He carried them. He didn't speak to them. He didn't promise. He simply made himself a shelter while they passed through the world one last time, and then he set them down wherever dead things go."

"And where do dead things go?"

"The gods used to know. The gods are remembering." His eye—his ordinary crow eye, the human one hidden today—glittered. "You carry a storm, farrier. You've been carrying it like a knife since the valley wall. Time you learned it isn't one."

More of them were coming. Out of the verges, out of the doubling light, dozens now—a lean old man with his hands cupped like he was carrying water he'd spilled decades ago; a soldier missing half of himself, the missing half still trying to march; a woman in a nightgown who walked with the particular careful gait of someone following a thread only she could see. None of them attacked. That was the horror of it. They simply converged, drawn the way cold air is drawn to an open window, and their wanting pressed against my skin like the moment before lightning.

The girl reached me first.

"Mister? I lost my mother."

And I did the thing that twelve years of exile had trained me for, though I'd never once known I was training: I held still. I held still the way you hold still when a wild thing lands near you, when a frightened horse reaches for your hand, when anything wounded decides whether you're danger or shelter.

The storm woke in my chest—not the killing thing, not the white roar that had torn a prince out of the world and a name out of my mouth. Something else. Something I'd been carrying folded up small for twelve years because no one ever told me a storm could be gentle.

I opened my hands. I didn't reach for her. I just—made room. The way the valley had once made room for me, magicless and quiet and safe from everything I was. I pulled that quality up out of memory and wrapped it around the road, around the fifty feet of pale ground where the wraiths gathered, and I let the storm fill it like rain filling a cistern—but slow. Warm, if a storm can be warm. A low rolling pressure, a hush, the feeling of the air just before snow, which every child knows means *stay inside, you're safe, the world is being taken care of.*

The girl stopped.

Her unfinished face turned up. Around me the others stilled too, the soldier, the old man, the woman in the nightgown, dozens of dropped souls standing in the amber pressure of a storm that had decided, for once, to be a roof instead of a fist.

"Oh," the girl said. And her voice changed—it lost the hook in it, the practiced ache, and became simply a tired child's voice at the end of a very long day. "It's warm."

"It's warm," I said, because it seemed suddenly cruel not to, and because Corvass had been wrong about one thing: kindness wasn't the trap. Kindness without a *promise* was just weather. "Stay as long as you need."

She stayed maybe a minute. Maybe ten. Time went strange under the hush. Then she sighed—a sound like frost leaving a window—and came apart, gently, into motes of light that rose instead of fell, drifting upward through my storm like sparks through a chimney, and were gone.

The old man followed. He wept, or did the thing that wraiths do instead of weeping, and then rose. The soldier straightened to full height for the first time in what might have been centuries, saluted something none of us could see, and rose. One by one, the dropped souls of the Hollow King's Road stepped into the shelter I was making and let it carry them the last little distance of their long delay, and rose, and rose, and rose.

I stood in the middle of it with my arms open and my heart breaking in the good way, the way a dam breaks when the flood is finally allowed to go where it always needed to go, and I understood—with a clarity that felt like the first honest thing anyone had told me in years—what Corvass had been trying to say.

My power had never been a weapon. Weapons are simple. My power was *weather*, and weather is what happens when the sky takes care of the world whether the world deserves it or not. I had spent twelve years calling it a curse because it had done one terrible thing. But a hammer that builds a house is not defined by the one time it fell on your thumb.

The last wraith—the woman in the nightgown, still following her invisible thread—paused beside me on her way up. For a moment her unfinished face was very close to mine, and I felt her look at the place where my storm-mark scarred my forearm, and I felt something almost like recognition pass between us, two people who had been dropped by the same court.

Then she rose, and the road was empty, and the dusk had deepened into true dark, and I was on my knees on pale stone with Sera's hand on my shoulder and tears freezing in my beard.

"Well," she said, and her voice was rougher than usual. "That was the single most dangerous thing I have ever watched anyone do."

"It worked."

"Yes. And next time it might not, and you'll know that, and you'll do it anyway. That's what makes it dangerous." She crouched beside me. In the dark her face was all shadow and starlight-reflection off the glassy road. "You didn't fight them. Every instinct I have, every drill, every oath—I'd have drawn steel. You made them a *home* for five minutes."

"Somebody should have," I said. My voice came out cracked. "Someone should have made Wren a home for five minutes, twelve years ago, instead of letting the court file her under *forfeit*."

Sera was quiet a long moment. When she spoke again, something had shifted in her voice—something guarded had been set down, briefly, like a pack taken off before a fire.

"When we get her back," she said, "she'll want to know what you did while she was gone. Most men would lie. Make themselves the hero of the waiting." She squeezed my shoulder once, hard, and let go. "You'll tell her you learned to shelter strangers on the road to save her. That's a better story than most heroes ever earn."

* * *

We made camp in the lee of one of the road's milestones—great slabs of pale stone, taller than a man, carved with script worn nearly smooth. Corvass perched atop ours and refused, for once, to talk. I got the sense he was doing something gods do, something like counting.

I fed the small fire with dead scrub pulled from the verge—Sera had warned against wood from the road itself, and I didn't ask why—and sat watching the flames with the storm curled quiet under my breastbone, purring, if storms can purr, like a big cat that has finally been allowed to do the thing it was made for.

"She's still afraid of you," I said to Sera, nodding at the sword across her knees. She'd been cleaning it for an hour without needing to.

"She should be. So should you." She didn't look up. "Everything I was trained for says tonight was an abomination. Dead souls walking. A man commanding storms. On any other night of my life, I'd have put you down and reported the road to what's left of my order."

"But?"

"But." She set the sword down and looked into the fire, and for a moment the firelight showed me something young in her face, something that predated the disgrace and the broken order and the hard miles. "When I was a novitiate, they taught us that mercy was a discipline. That you earned the right to show it by first proving you could withhold it. Oath, then judgment, then grace—in that order, always." Her thumb traced the pommel, around and around. "Tonight a farrier skipped straight to grace and it *worked*, and I don't know what to do with that. It's like watching someone walk on water. Either everything I was taught is wrong, or you're something the teachings never imagined."

"Could be both," I offered.

"Could be both," she admitted, and almost smiled.

Corvass dropped from the milestone, landing in the firelight with a whump of feathers. Whatever counting he'd been doing, he'd finished it, and he did not look pleased.

"Trouble?" Sera asked.

"The road ends tomorrow at the Godgate—the pass where the Hollow King used to carry his cargo up and out of the world." He paced a tight circle, wings half-lifted. "The gate still stands. The gate still *works*. Which means it still demands payment of everyone who passes."

"We paid tolls before," I said. "Salt at Saltmere. Names at the bridge."

"Not this kind." The crow stopped pacing. In the firelight his one visible eye was very bright and very old. "The Godgate doesn't take what you carry. It takes what you're *hiding*. Everyone who passes must drop one true thing they've never told another living soul, and the gate keeps it forever, and the road carries it up to wherever dead things go—which, these days, is nowhere, which means it *piles up*." His feathers flattened. "There are centuries of secrets stacked in that pass, farrier. Some of them belong to people who came back for them. Some belong to things that were never people at all."

Sera had gone very still. Stillness was her weapon, I was learning; she drew it the way I drew storms.

"And if someone refuses?" she asked.

"Then the gate shows everyone else what they're hiding," Corvass said, "and lets them through anyway. Naked, in the only way that matters." He looked at her a moment too long. "Paladin. You've been carrying something since Greyhollow. I've watched you decide four times not to say it."

The fire popped. Sera stared into it, and her hand had gone, quite without her noticing, to the collar of her shirt—to the faded line of a scar I'd glimpsed once and never asked about, running from her throat down beneath the fabric like the track of a tear that had never dried.

"Tomorrow," she said at last, to the fire, to the road, to no one. "Tomorrow I'll deal with tomorrow."

But she didn't sleep that night, and neither did I, and the milestone above our camp stood in the starlight like a headstone for every secret the road had ever swallowed—including, come morning, one more.
