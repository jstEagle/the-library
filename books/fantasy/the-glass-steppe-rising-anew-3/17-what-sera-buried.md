## 17. What Sera Buried

The fire came back to Sera Vane the way it always did: not as memory but as weather.

She woke on the Steppe's edge with frost in her lashes and the taste of smoke that wasn't there. Rosalind slept across the dead coals of their camp, one arm flung over his eyes, his storm-marked forearm pale in the moonlight. The crow was a shapeless hump on a stone, head tucked under its wing, though she had long since stopped believing it ever truly slept.

*Ashfall,* she thought. *It always starts with ashfall.*

She sat up and let it come, because she had learned—slowly, at cost—that burying a thing only taught it to dig.

* * *

Nine years ago, the order of the Silver Crescent had still been whole.

Sera remembered it whole because she had been whole inside it: Sister-Commandant Sera Vane, thirty years old, keeper of the western vigil, bearer of a moon-blessing so clean and bright that novices asked to touch her scabbard for luck. She had believed then that the blessing was a reward. She would learn, later, that it was more like a debt—the moon lends its light to those who will carry it into places light does not want to go.

The village was called Emberlow, which even then struck her as an ugly joke of a name, though no one laughed. It sat in a fold of hills three days' ride from the Vigil-house, a scatter of turf-roofed houses around a shrine so old the stone had gone soft as bread. The report that reached them called it a plague. The report was written by a man who had never been there.

What it actually was, was children.

They came out to meet the column of paladins—eleven of them, Sera counted, ranging from a girl of perhaps four to a boy nearly grown—and they were god-touched in the old way, the way the histories described and the Crown had outlawed two centuries past. The smallest girl's shadow moved a half-second behind her, like a dancer who had learned the steps from someone else. A pair of brothers walked with frost blooming and dying under their bare feet in summer. The eldest boy, when he grew frightened, made the lanterns gutter—not with wind, just with wanting them dark.

Their parents stood behind them in the lane with the particular stillness of people who have already decided how they will die.

"They don't sleep," the reeve said quietly, to Sera alone, because he had somehow understood that she was the one who mattered. "The little ones. They dream out loud. Whole village hears it through the walls." He rubbed his face. "We sent to the Crown first. They sent you."

"We are not the Crown," Sera said.

"No," the reeve agreed. "But you're what came."

Her commander, Ser Aldous Crane, dismounted at the head of the column with his white cloak immaculate and his face already arranged into the expression she would come to hate more than any enemy's: pity shaped like duty. He carried the writ in a lead case against his chest, because silver-cloaks did not speak such orders aloud where common folk could hear.

"Sister-Commandant," he said. "Walk with me."

They walked to the shrine, and there, in the soft old stone, he opened the lead case and showed her the wax and the seal and the single line of script beneath the royal arms: *Purge.*

"You knew before we rode," she said.

"I knew the order existed. I didn't know the—" he glanced back down the lane, where the smallest girl was showing her lagging shadow to a paladin's warhorse, and the horse, sensibly, was unbothered. "I didn't know the scale."

"Eleven children."

"Eleven vessels." Crane closed the case. "You've read the same doctrine I have, Vane. God-touched children grow into god-touched adults, and god-touched adults draw the old gods like wounds draw flies. Every heresy of the last two hundred years started in a village like this. We burn the field so the fire doesn't reach the forest."

"The field is *people.*"

"The field is *kindling*," he said, without heat, without cruelty, and that was the worst part. She had spent years afterward wishing he'd been cruel. Cruelty could be fought. What Crane had was certainty, worn smooth as river stone, and it fit his hand perfectly after twenty years of carrying it. "The writ is signed. The rite is prepared. We do it at dawn, quickly, with the proper prayers, and we grieve properly after. That is the mercy available to us. Take it."

She looked at the shrine stone. Soft as bread. Older than the Crown, older than the Silver Crescent, older than every law that had made this morning possible. She thought: *this stone has been prayed at by ten thousand hands, and none of them needed our permission.*

"I'll take the other mercy," she said.

Crane looked at her for a long moment. "Don't," he said, and for one heartbeat his certainty cracked and she saw underneath it something almost like pleading. "Sera. Don't make me write your name in the report."

"Then don't light the pyre."

He lit the pyre.

* * *

Here is what Sera buried, and here is what she dug up now, lying awake under alien stars while a storm-blooded farrier breathed across the ashes:

She did not stop it.

That was the sentence she had served nine years in exile for, and she had never once said it plainly, not to herself, not to the tribunal, not to the moon whose blessing still clung to her like a smell that wouldn't wash out. In the official account—in the account she gave, voice steady, kneeling on cold marble before the Grand Council of the Crescent—she had refused the order, been stripped of rank, cast out, and the tragedy had proceeded without her. A clean story. A story with her hands washed.

The truth was that refusal, spoken at dusk, changed nothing by dawn. She had spent the night doing everything short of the one thing that mattered. She argued doctrine with Crane until midnight. She sent a rider toward the capital with a plea for clemency—a plea she knew would arrive weeks too late, but sending it let her feel like she was acting. She went house to house in Emberlow telling parents to flee, knowing they wouldn't, knowing their grandparents lay in the churchyard and their whole lives were stacked in these rooms, and they looked at her with terrible gentleness, the way you look at a child explaining why the rain won't fall.

And when dawn came, and the pyres were built in the square, and the eleven children were brought out—weeping, the little ones; silent, the eldest boy, who looked straight at Sera with lantern-guttering eyes—she stood in the second rank, because Crane had ordered her confined to quarters and she had disobeyed that easily enough, and she raised her hand, and her moon-blessing rose with it, silver-white, furious—

—and she hesitated.

One breath. That was all. One breath in which the oldest question in the world held her throat: *what if I'm wrong?* What if Crane was right and the doctrine was right and eleven small bodies really were kindling? What if she unmade the order she loved, the only home she had ever had, on the strength of her own single uncertain heart?

In that breath, Ser Roderick Hale—her own sworn sword, a man she had knighted herself—stepped forward and spoke the words of severance, and the blessing left her hand like a bird released, and the fires were lit by other hands, and by the time her will came back to her it was ashfall, grey snow drifting over the turf roofs, and the eldest boy was dead with his eyes open and pointed at her.

Not at Crane. Not at Hale. At her. Because she was the one who had almost. Because *almost* is a thing children can smell.

She killed no one that day. She also saved no one. And the tribunal, when it convened, found this more damning than either: a Sister-Commandant who defied a lawful purge and then failed to see it through was not a heretic or a hero, merely a broken instrument, and broken instruments are discarded. They took her cloak. They took her name from the rolls. They left her the blessing—which should have told her something, even then, even in the marble cold of that judgment hall. The moon does not take back its light from those who hesitate in the dark. It only waits to see what they do next.

* * *

Nine years of next.

She had drunk in border towns and sobered up in worse ones. She had worked as a caravan guard, a bounty tracker, once—for six humiliating months—as a town watchwoman in a place so small the job consisted mostly of retrieving goats. She had told herself the story of Emberlow the clean version so many times it had worn smooth as Crane's certainty, and she had hated him righteously the whole way, because hating him was easier than the arithmetic.

Then the gods had woken. Then the prayers had started landing again like arrows finding targets. Then a rumor had reached her in a Saltmere tavern—*storm-witch in Greyhollow, valley that eats magic, mark of the old blood*—and she had ridden there hunting a fugitive, because hunting was the one prayer she still knew how to say.

And found instead a tired farrier with careful hands and a dead man's name, performing a dawn ritual with the desperate precision of a man holding shut a door against a flood. Found instead the fingerprints of the waking gods all over him, pressed there like a child's hands in dough. Found instead, sitting at his fire, listening to him talk about a sister sold to pay his debts, the arithmetic she had outrun for nine years catching her at last:

*Eleven children burned because good people hesitated one breath. One soul held hostage twelve years because a brother ran. The world is full of Emberlows, and I have been standing in the second rank my whole life.*

"Sera."

She blinked. Rosalind was awake, propped on one elbow, watching her with the particular attention of a man who had spent twelve years reading faces because his life depended on misreading none of them.

"You're doing the thing," he said.

"What thing?"

"Where you look at me like I'm a ledger entry you can't balance." He sat up. The crow opened one eye—its human eye, brown and unsettling and ancient—and fixed on her with what she had come to recognize as interest. "Every night since the Steppe started. You go somewhere and come back with frost on you."

"It's cold."

"It's not cold." He nodded at the sky. "Your moon's up. Frost doesn't stand a chance near that thing when it's on you."

She almost laughed. Almost. "Go back to sleep, Whitlock."

"You first." But he lay back down, and after a while his breathing lengthened, and the crow tucked its head away again, and Sera sat alone with the buried thing finally above ground, turning it over, examining its roots.

Here was what she understood now, nine years late, watching the thirteenth moon swell fat and white over the mirror-flat dark of the Glass Steppe: her disgrace had never been the disobedience. Disobedience, done wholly, might have been righteousness—might have been eleven children alive and an order shattered but honest. Her disgrace was the hesitation. The half-vow. The hand raised and stayed. She had spent nine years wearing her shame like a stolen cloak when in truth it fit her badly; the shame belonged to the breath she lost, not the choice she made. Her honor hadn't died at Emberlow. It had been wounded there, and wounds, the moon-cult taught in its first lesson, are how the light gets in.

The court beneath the mountain wanted her to believe otherwise. The Debt Collector had known exactly which coin to offer—restoration, rank, her name read aloud again in the marble hall—and she had refused it, and she still hadn't told Rosalind, and the secret sat in her chest like a swallowed coal. She would tell him. Soon. When the moment was right. When it couldn't hurt them.

*That's what hesitation sounds like,* said a voice in her head that sounded like the eldest boy of Emberlow, grown now, in whatever world souls go when ledgers burn. *Almost is a thing I can smell.*

"All right," she said aloud, to the moon, to the crow, to no one. "All right. No more second rank."

The crow's head came out from under its wing. The human eye caught the moonlight and held it, and when Corvass spoke, his voice was dry leaves and old doors.

"Spoken like a woman about to do something expensive."

"Aren't they all?" Sera said. "The worthwhile things?"

The crow made a sound that might have been laughter, in some older tongue. "Tomorrow you cross onto the glass proper. There's a bridge ahead—the corpse-road crosses the river of names, and nothing crosses free." Its eye glittered. "Bring your true self, paladin. The bridge will know if you don't."

Sera looked east, where the Steppe waited, flat and silver and patient as a held breath, and felt—for the first time in nine years—not exiled.

Deployed.
