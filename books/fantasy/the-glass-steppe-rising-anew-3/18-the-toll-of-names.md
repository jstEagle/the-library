## 18. The Toll of Names

The bridge appeared at dawn, the way bridges do in stories told to frighten children—suddenly, and where no bridge had any right to be.

We had walked all night along the corpse-road, following the dead god's spine eastward through a country of gray grass and standing stones. Sera had gone quiet somewhere past midnight, her moon-blessing dimming with the stars, and by the time the sky went the color of old pewter I could hear the river before I saw it: a wide black ribbon of water moving without sound, as if it had been warned not to wake anything.

And there was the bridge. White bone-pale stone, arched high, its railings carved with faces I chose not to look at too closely. It spanned a gorge so deep that mist rose out of it like breath from a sleeping mouth.

"Bridge-wardens," Sera said, when I asked what waited on the far side. She said it the way you'd say *tax collectors* or *plague*. "Old contracts, older than the courts. They don't serve the Undercourt. They serve the principle of payment itself."

"What's the toll?"

She looked at me, and there was something almost gentle in her face, which frightened me more than any weapon.

"A name," she said. "It's always a name."

---

He stood at the center of the span—a figure robed in gray so pale it seemed cut from fog, his face hidden beneath a hood that held shadow the way a cup holds water. He did not move as we approached. He did not need to. The bridge itself announced him; every footstep we took rang against the stone like a struck bell, and each ring carried our weight forward to him whether he wished it or not.

Corvass rode my shoulder, feathers slicked flat against the wind coming up from the gorge. "Careful," the crow murmured, low enough that only I heard. "Wardens weigh what you give them. Give true weight or none at all."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the bridge refuses. And the river below—" Corvass clicked his beak. "The river has opinions about those who try to swim it."

Sera stopped ten paces from the warden and bowed her head—not deeply, but formally, the bow of one professional acknowledging another. "Passage for three," she said. "Two living, one divine."

"One divine," the warden repeated. His voice came from everywhere and nowhere, like wind through a keyhole. "The crow may pass freely. Gods owe nothing and are owed nothing." A pause. "The two who remain must pay."

"We know the toll," Sera said.

"The toll is a name." The hood tilted toward me, and though I couldn't see eyes beneath it, I felt seen—felt *weighed*, the way I imagine a butcher weighs meat, assessing not just what you are but what you're worth. "Not a title. Not an alias. A name. The word your mother spoke over your cradle. The sound that, when spoken truly, makes the world remember you exist."

My stomach dropped.

Twelve years. Twelve years since anyone had spoken my name aloud, since I'd let it die quietly in the valley where magic couldn't follow. In Greyhollow I was *the farrier*, *old man*, sometimes *you there*. Even in my own head I'd worn the name down smooth, like a stone handled too long, until touching it felt less like identity than habit.

And here was a stranger asking me to hand it over like a coin.

"What happens to a name once it's given?" I asked.

The warden's hood tilted again, considering. "Names given in toll become part of the bridge. They hold it up." A gesture at the arches, the carven faces along the rails—and I understood, suddenly and horribly, that the faces weren't decoration. They were names. Hundreds of them. Thousands. People who had crossed and paid, their identities pressed into the stonework like flowers into wax, holding up the road behind them while they walked ahead diminished.

"You keep walking," the warden said. "But lighter. You will forget things. Small things first—the smell of your childhood kitchen, the tune your father hummed. Then larger ones, if the name was heavy. Who loved you. Why you left. What you're crossing this bridge to do."

Beside me, Sera had gone very still.

"There are other ways across," she said carefully.

"There are other ways down," the warden agreed. "None other across."

---

I turned away from him, ostensibly to look at the river, actually to look at Corvass.

"Is he telling the truth?"

"Crow's honor? Yes." The crow shifted on my shoulder. "Bridges built on lies fall. Bridges built on names stand forever. That's why wardens demand them—it's not cruelty, it's engineering."

"Wonderful. And if I pay with a false name?"

Corvass went quiet for a moment. When he answered, his voice had lost its usual dry humor. "Then the bridge will know. Bridges always know. And it will reject the payment—which means rejecting *you*, Rosalind-whose-name-I-will-not-say-on-this-stone. You'll be turned back to the near shore, and the turning-back will cost something too. Nothing's free. Especially refusals."

Behind us, the corpse-road stretched back westward, gray and endless, the way we'd come. Ahead, the mist rising from the gorge curled around the bridge's far end and swallowed whatever lay beyond.

I thought about Wren. About twelve years of careful forgetting, careful anonymity, a life lived at half-volume because full volume would have gotten people killed. I thought about how I'd practiced saying nothing for so long that silence had become my native tongue.

And I thought: *what's one more lie?*

---

"I'll pay," I told the warden, and stepped forward.

Sera's hand caught my sleeve. "Rosalind—"

"It's fine."

"It isn't. Names aren't—"

"*It's fine.*" I said it quietly, but with the edge of storm in it, and she let go.

The warden extended a gray hand from his gray robe, palm up, waiting.

I gave him the false name.

"Tomas Merrick," I said. It was the first thing that surfaced—a farmer's name, common as dirt, the kind of name that belonged to a hundred men in any village you cared to name. I'd used it before, in markets, at inns. It had never failed me.

For a moment, nothing happened. The wind moved. The mist breathed. Somewhere far below, the silent river went on being silent.

Then the bridge *shuddered*.

It wasn't dramatic. No crack of thunder, no splitting of stone. Just a deep, structural groan, like a ship taking on water, like a foundation settling wrong. The carved faces along the railing—all those surrendered names pressed into the architecture—all of them turned at once, slowly, to look at me.

"No," said the warden, in a voice that was suddenly much closer, much larger, filling the space inside my skull. "That is not yours."

"I don't know what you mean—"

"*That is not yours.*" The words landed like stones dropped into still water. "You have spoken a borrowed thing. The bridge cannot accept what you do not own. And the bridge—" he paused, and I heard, beneath the formality, something that might have been pity— "the bridge does not permit debtors to leave unpaid."

The shudder came again, harder. Mist boiled up from the gorge. Behind us, the near end of the bridge began, very gently, to *recede*—the stone flowing backward like water finding its level, the gap between us and solid ground widening with every heartbeat.

"He's telling the truth," Sera said tightly. Her sword was out now, though what she planned to do with steel against architecture I couldn't guess. "Rosalind, give it the real one. Now."

"I haven't said it in twelve years."

"Then say it badly. Say it like a stranger. But *say it*."

The gap widened. Ten feet of empty air now between me and the shore, growing. Corvass dug his claws into my shoulder, hard enough to hurt, hard enough to anchor.

"Storm-child," the crow murmured, "some doors only open to the truth. This one is older than most."

I closed my eyes.

And there it was, exactly where I'd buried it: under the farrier's calluses, under the exile's silence, under twelve years of *Tomas Merrick* and *hey, you* and *no name, thanks, just passing through*. Buried so deep I'd nearly convinced myself it wasn't mine anymore.

*Rosalind Whitlock.*

The son of a horse-farmer who'd taught me to read hoof-health like scripture. Brother of Wren, who sang off-key and laughed too loud and sold herself to a court of shadows so that her brother might live. The boy who called a storm out of a clear summer sky and killed a king's heir with it. The man who'd run to a magicless valley and spent twelve years pretending the running had worked.

I opened my eyes.

"My name," I said, and my voice cracked on the second word like ice on a pond, "is Rosalind Whitlock."

The bridge stopped receding.

For one long moment everything hung suspended—me on the shrinking span, Sera with her blade drawn, the warden's hood tilted upward, the thousand carven faces watching with the patience of the dead.

Then the bridge *accepted* it.

I felt it happen. Felt the name leave me like a tooth pulled—not pain exactly, but the sudden absence of a thing that had always been there, a hollow spot my tongue kept returning to. The word *Rosalind Whitlock* sank into the stone beneath my feet, and the stone drank it down, and somewhere in the architecture of the bridge a new pressure settled into place, holding up the road the way all the other names were holding it up.

The gap to the shore sealed itself. The mist settled. The warden's hood inclined—gracious, now, almost warm.

"The toll is paid," he said. "Cross."

---

I made it maybe twenty steps before my legs gave out.

Sera caught me under the arms and lowered me onto a bench-like jut of stone near the bridge's crest, and I sat there shaking, hands flat against my knees, breathing like a man who'd surfaced from deep water.

"It doesn't feel like I expected," I managed. "Lighter. Everyone says lighter. It just feels—*absent*. Like a room where furniture used to be."

"That's because it's not gone." Sera crouched in front of me, searching my face with an intensity I hadn't earned yet. "Given isn't taken. There's a difference. The bridge holds it, but you can visit." Her mouth twisted. "If the Undercourt falls, maybe the bridge falls too. Maybe you get it back."

"And if it doesn't fall?"

She didn't answer. She didn't have to.

Corvass hopped down from my shoulder onto the stone beside me, and did something I'd never seen him do: he bowed. A small, formal, bird-shaped bow, head dipping low.

"Well said," the crow murmured. "Well paid. Do you know how many souls cross this bridge and lie? How many spend their last moments here clutching a false self like a drowning man clutching driftwood?" His human eye caught the strengthening light. "You spoke it badly. You spoke it scared. You spoke it anyway. That's the whole art, storm-child. That's the entire craft of being a person."

"Very inspiring," I rasped. "Now tell me something useful. What did it cost me? Really. What did I lose back there?"

Corvass considered.

"Ask yourself something," he said. "What did your mother smell like?"

I reached for the memory and found—I found the shape of a memory, the outline, the knowledge that there had been a smell, flour and lye soap and horse sweat. But the smell itself was gone. A window painted over. I knew the frame existed. I couldn't see through it.

"Oh," I said.

"It gets worse before it gets better," Corvass said, not unkindly. "Small things first. But here's the secret nobody tells you about paying with names: what the bridge takes, the bridge keeps *alive*. Your name isn't dead in that stone, Whitlock. It's working. Holding up a road. Doing honest labor." The crow's eye glittered. "And roads, unlike courts, can be unmade."

I looked back down the length of the bridge—at the thousand faces in the railings, at the mist curling off the gorge, at the far shore waiting in the strengthening light. Somewhere beyond it, the Undercourt. Somewhere beyond that, Wren. Thirteen turns of the moon, and fewer remaining than I dared count.

I stood up. My legs held. My name didn't, but my legs did, and that morning that was enough.

"Come on," I said. "We've got a court to rob."

Sera sheathed her sword, and for the first time since the valley, I saw her smile—small, tired, real.

"That," she said, falling into step beside me, "is the first thing you've said all journey that sounded like the man whose name you just gave away."

We crossed.

The far side of the bridge opened onto country I didn't recognize—black soil, red grass, a horizon bruised purple with approaching weather that I could feel answering something in my chest, a low harmonic hum like a tuning fork struck against my ribs. My storms, waking. Recognizing open sky.

But it was Corvass who went rigid on my shoulder, feathers lifting, human eye fixed on something ahead.

"Storm-child," the crow said quietly. "How attached are you to arriving at the Undercourt unmolested?"

Ahead, perhaps a mile down the road, a figure waited for us. Alone. Unarmed. Dressed in traveling leathers the color of dried blood.

Smiling.
