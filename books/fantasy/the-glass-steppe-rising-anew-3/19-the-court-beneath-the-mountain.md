## 19. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The mountain had no name on any map we could find, which Sera said was the first sign you'd found the right one.

"Courts that want visitors put themselves on maps," she said, squinting up at a wall of black stone that rose out of the Steppe like a knife left standing in a table. "Courts that don't, don't."

We'd been walking toward it for two days without ever seeming to get closer. That was the second sign. The Glass Steppe stretched flat and silver in every direction, and the mountain sat on the horizon like a fixed star—always ahead, never nearer—until the moon waned to a sliver and then, all at once, it wasn't far anymore. It was *there*, filling half the sky, close enough that I could see the seams in its face: veins of obsidian running through basalt like frozen lightning.

Corvass rode my shoulder the whole way, heavier than any crow had a right to be. He hadn't spoken since dawn. Gods, I was learning, went quiet before thresholds.

The gates were at the mountain's foot, and they were not doors so much as a wound. A seam of absolute black split the rock from ground to a height of forty feet, edges polished smooth as ice, and standing before it I felt the same pressure I felt in Greyhollow's magicless valley—except inverted. The valley pressed magic *out*. This place pulled it *in*. My storm-blood stirred under my skin like fish rising to feed, and for the first time since leaving home I understood that what waited beyond that seam wanted exactly what I carried.

"There's no handle," Sera said. She'd dismounted an hour back; horses wouldn't come within a mile of the place, and we'd turned them loose with our thanks and half our rations. "No knocker, no bell. How do you knock on a hole in the world?"

"You don't," Corvass said. His voice came out like gravel poured slow. "You *owe*."

I looked down at him. One eye was crow-black. The other—the human one, the one that unsettled everyone who saw it—regarded me with something I hadn't seen in him before. Pity, maybe. Or the memory of pity, worn smooth by centuries of use.

"The debt must be freely offered," he said. "That is the law older than the court itself. Older than the gods who are awake now, though they'll not thank me for saying so. You cannot break in. You cannot trick your way through—not truly, not past the first hall. The gate opens only when someone stands before it and gives something away. Willingly. Completely. With no take-backs written into the giving."

"And if I refuse?" Sera asked.

"Then you stand here until the thirteenth moon turns, and somewhere below us a girl's soul is filed away forever." Corvass preened once, a sharp, mechanical motion. "The court is patient. It has never needed to be anything else."

Sera looked at me. I looked at the seam.

Twelve years I'd practiced wanting nothing. That was the whole discipline of exile, really—you burned your wants down to coals small enough to hide: warm bread, dry socks, one more year. And now I stood in front of a door that fed on wanting, that would smell every coal still banked in me, and I understood that this was the true test. Not the storms. Not the Hunt, when it came. Whether I could stand at the mouth of the thing that held my sister and offer myself honestly, without flinching, without bargaining in my heart while my mouth made promises.

"I'm ready," I said, and was surprised to find it mostly true.

* * *

There was a ritual to it, Corvass explained, and the ritual was almost insultingly simple. Stand before the gate. Speak aloud what you offered. Mean it.

"The court will test the meaning," he added. "It always does. It will show you the thing you most want to keep, dressed up as the thing you're offering. If you reach for it—even in your mind—the gate stays shut, and the court notes your name in a ledger you do not want to be noted in." He hopped from my shoulder to a shelf of black glass, settling like a judge taking his bench. "I cannot help you in there. Once the offering begins, I am, by ancient compact, a bystander. Even gods must be bystanders here. That is rather the point of the place."

"What compact?" Sera asked. "With whom?"

"With everything," Corvass said. "The Undercourt is where all compacts go to be enforced. It does not sign them. It *is* them." He tucked his head under one wing, then pulled it back out, unable to quite commit to looking away. "Rosalind Whitlock. Before you speak—know that whatever you offer, the court will collect to the last coin. It does not round down. It does not accept partial payment. Whatever words you choose, choose them as if your life depends on their precision. Because it does."

I thought about Wren. Twelve years old when they took her, all elbows and fury, screaming at the Undercourt's agents that she'd sell her soul but they'd better write down that she hated us for letting her. They'd written it down. Of course they had. Somewhere below my feet, in a ledger with her name in it, there was probably a line item recording the exact temperature of her hatred, indexed and cross-referenced and accruing interest.

I stepped up to the seam.

Up close, the black surface wasn't empty. It was full—of reflections, faint and slow, faces moving beneath the polish like fish under ice. None of them mine. All of them watching.

"My name is—" I stopped. Started again. "My name doesn't matter here. What matters is what I owe."

The seam did not react. But the pressure against my skin deepened, and I felt it listening the way you feel a hand pressed flat against your chest.

"I owe a blood-price," I said. "Paid twelve years ago by my sister, Wren Whitlock, who sold her soul to settle a debt that was mine. The storm that killed Prince Aldric was called by me, carelessly, in grief and rage. She paid for my crime. I have come to pay it back."

The word *back* hung in the air. Somewhere in the black, a reflection smiled.

"I offer my service," I said. "Freely. To the court beneath the mountain, for such term as it requires, in payment of the debt of Wren Whitlock. I offer it knowing the court collects to the last coin. I offer it with no conditions, no reservations, and no take-backs written into the giving."

I meant it. That was the terrible part—I meant every word, and meaning it felt like opening a vein. The storm under my skin rose toward the surface, eager, and I let it rise, because hiding anything from this place was the one mistake I couldn't afford.

And then the gate showed me the thing I most wanted to keep.

* * *

It didn't come as a vision. It came as a morning.

Greyhollow. The forge, cold because it was rest-day, and Wren sitting on the anvil with her boots swinging, eating stolen plums and talking with her mouth full about nothing—about the miller's dog, about whether clouds had bones, about a boy in the village who'd looked at her funny and how she'd looked back funnier until he tripped over a bucket. Ordinary. Sunlit. So real I could smell the plum juice on her fingers.

*Rosalind,* she said, in the voice I'd spent twelve years failing to forget. *You don't have to do this. Walk away. Live your little life. I'm fine where I am.*

"You're not fine," I whispered. "You're filed."

*I'm fine,* the reflection insisted, and it was perfect, it was her down to the chipped tooth, and that perfection was the tell. The real Wren had never once in her life claimed to be fine. The real Wren met catastrophe the way she met everything—loudly, with her chin up and both fists ready.

"You're not her," I said, louder. "She'd never tell me to walk away. She'd tell me to hurry up."

The morning flickered. For half a heartbeat the sunlit forge showed its other face—a vault of black stone, a drawer, a label—and then it was gone, and the seam stood plain and patient before me, and my offering still hung in the air between us, unclaimed or accepted, I couldn't tell which.

Beside me, Sera had gone rigid. Her lips were moving. Whatever the gate showed her, she was fighting it silently, jaw clenched, one hand white-knuckled around the broken sigil of her order. I wanted to ask. There was no time to ask.

From the shelf of glass, Corvass made a sound I'd never heard from him—a low, resonant note, almost like a bell struck underwater. A warning. Or a blessing. With him it was hard to say.

The seam opened.

Not swung—*unzipped*, the black peeling apart along its length with a sound like a held breath released after forty years. Beyond it: a corridor of obsidian, lit by no visible source, receding farther than the mountain had any right to be deep. Cold air flowed out, smelling of ink and iron and old paper. The smell of ledgers. The smell of my sister's prison.

A figure stood in the entrance. Tall, robed in grey so dark it read as a hole in the world, faceless—or rather, faced with smooth obsidian, featureless except where light moved across it. When it spoke, its voice was neither male nor female, neither loud nor soft, simply *final*, like the click of a lock.

**"The service of Rosalind Whitlock, freely offered, is entered into the record. The debt of Wren Whitlock is marked pending settlement. Enter."**

"That's it?" Sera said behind me. "No contract? No signing?"

**"The contract was spoken,"** the figure said. **"It is binding. All words spoken at this gate are binding. That is what the gate is *for*."**

I looked at Corvass. He had turned his head fully away now, honoring some compact I didn't understand, and only his human eye stayed fixed on me—one bright, mortal-looking eye in a face of feathers and old divinity.

*Don't die in there,* the eye said. *Or do. But be interesting about it.*

"Watch her," I told Sera, meaning the soul in my keeping, the storm-mark where Wren's essence slept hidden against the day I could free her properly. Actually—no. I couldn't bring that in. Could I? Every instinct screamed that carrying contraband through the front door of the universe's greatest bureaucracy was madness.

But the alternative was leaving her outside, unguarded, with a gate that had just demonstrated it could wear her face.

"She comes with me," I said. "Part of the service. I keep what's mine until the debt is settled."

The obsidian face tilted. Light crawled across it, and I had the distinct impression of a vast attention turning over my words, weighing them, finding them—barely, grudgingly—acceptable.

**"Entered,"** it said. **"The debtor keeps his collateral. Note: collateral held within the court remains subject to court law. Any attempt at extraction prior to settlement voids the service-offering and invokes the original terms."**

Meaning: if they caught me trying to sneak Wren out early, my pledge was worthless and the original contract—the one that had taken her soul in the first place—snapped back into force with twelve years of accrued interest.

"No extraction," I agreed. "Settlement first."

**"Enter, then."**

Sera caught my arm. Her grip was stronger than it looked, calloused from sword-hilt and prayer-rope both. "Once we're in," she said quietly, "there's no way back out that isn't through them. You understand that. Gates like this open twice: once for the willing, once for the dead."

"I've been dead for twelve years, Sera. Walking around in it." I managed something that was almost a smile. "Might as well make it official."

She didn't laugh. She looked at me a moment longer than she needed to, and there was something in her face I couldn't read—guilt, maybe, or the shape guilt leaves behind when it's been buried shallow—and then she let go of my arm and squared her shoulders like a paladin entering a cathedral, which was, in a way, exactly what she was doing.

We crossed the threshold together.

* * *

The cold hit first, then the silence. Inside the mountain, sound behaved wrong—our footsteps landed and were *collected*, each one filed away into the dark instead of echoing. The corridor walls were seamless obsidian, and in their polish I caught glimpses of movement that weren't quite reflections: figures walking parallel to us, a step behind, a step ahead. Other debtors, maybe. Other offerings, still being honored, still being collected, in halls we'd never see.

The corridor opened into a vestibule the size of a village square, and the vestibule opened onto the court itself, and I stopped walking, because the court beneath the mountain was not a throne room.

It was a *counting house*.

Shelves. Endless shelves, rising into darkness beyond any lantern's reach, and on them—ledgers. Tens of thousands of ledgers, bound in leather and vellum and things less comfortable to think about, each spine labeled in script that rearranged itself when I tried to read it. Between the shelves moved the clerks: grey-robed, obsidian-faced, carrying quills cut from feathers I suspected I recognized. Somewhere far above, a bell tolled with no clapper, marking an hour that had no name.

And threaded through it all, faint as smoke, I felt them—souls. Hundreds. Thousands. Not screaming, not wailing, nothing so dramatic. Just *present*, the way a sleeping house is present around you, breathing. Hoarded. Filed. Earning interest.

Wren was down there somewhere. In one of those books. On one of those shelves.

"Rosalind." Sera's voice came thin in the dead air. She was staring up at the shelves with an expression I'd only seen on her once before—at the ruins of her order's chapterhouse. Recognition. Grief. "This is where prayers used to go unanswered. This is why. They weren't ignored. They were *invoiced*."

An obsidian-faced clerk detached from the shelves and approached us with a gait like a pendulum. It stopped precisely three paces away and inclined its smooth head.

**"Debtor Rosalind Whitlock. Service pledged at the outer gate, pending settlement. Collateral retained on person. You will follow. Your associate—"** the blank face turned to Sera **"—is not entered in any ledger. Associates without entries may proceed as observers. Observers may not speak in negotiation. Observers may not touch the ledgers. Observers who violate these rules become entrants."**

"I understand," Sera said, and her hand found the broken sigil at her throat and held it like a talisman.

The clerk turned. We followed, down an aisle between shelves that hummed faintly, and I kept my hand pressed flat over my chest, over the storm-mark where my sister slept, and I walked deeper into the counting house of the damned with my freedom signed away and no way back—

—and that was when, three shelves away, a ledger fell open on its own, and a voice I knew better than my own heartbeat whispered up out of its pages:

*"Rosie. Don't sign anything else."*
