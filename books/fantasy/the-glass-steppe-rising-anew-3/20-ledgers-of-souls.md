## 20. Ledgers of Souls

The gates closed behind us with the sound of a ledger being shut.

That was the first thing I understood about the Undercourt: it was not a dungeon, not a cathedral, not any of the hells the moon-cult's liturgies had promised. It was an *office*. Vast beyond reason, yes—corridors of black glass running off into distances that hurt to look at—but every corridor lined with desks. Every desk manned. And every clerk, when they looked up at us with their too-patient faces, wore the expression I'd spent six years wearing behind the Chapterhouse's front counter: *you are wasting my time and I am very good at making you feel it.*

"Queue's to the left," said the nearest one.

It had a body like a man poured thin and left to set wrong, robes of grey felt, fingers stained silver from handling coin that wasn't coin. Its eyes were the worst part. Not cruel. Not hungry. Just *tired*, the way Rosalind was tired, the way every soul in this place had been tired for so long that tiredness had become architecture.

"We're not here to queue," I said.

"Everyone says that." It turned a page. "Everyone queues."

Behind me, I heard Rosalind breathe. We'd crossed the threshold on his pledge—an oath of service, freely offered, spoken into the dark before the obsidian—and I'd watched it take hold of him like frost taking hold of a window. He hadn't spoken much since. His hands kept flexing, opening and closing around something invisible. Around the shape of what he'd just given away.

I put my hand on his shoulder. "We have an appointment."

"No one has appointments." The clerk looked up again, and this time its gaze snagged on something at my hip—the broken sigil of my order, half-melted where the Chapterhouse fire had taken it. Something moved behind the tiredness. Recognition. Or its cousin, fear. "Paladin," it said, softer.

"I resigned."

"There is no resignation." It rose from its desk, joints moving like a ledger's spine. "There is only transfer. Come. You'll want the Hall of Forfeit, if it's souls you're after. They always want the Hall of Forfeit." It paused, and the tiredness in its face cracked just enough to show me something underneath—something almost like pity. "I'll walk you myself. Otherwise you'll be stopped eleven times, and each stop costs time, and time—" it gestured at the endless corridors, the endless desks, "—is the only thing we don't sell."

* * *

The Hall of Forfeit smelled like a chapel and a counting-house had drowned together. Incense and ink. Candles burned down to pools of wax that never needed replacing, because down here nothing was ever used up, only transferred. Shelves rose into darkness overhead—not shelves of books, though there were books, thousands of them, but shelves of *jars*. Small ones. Clay ones. Ones that glowed faintly, or didn't glow at all, and somehow that was worse.

Souls. The hoard. Corvass had told us through the crows: the old gods wake because the Undercourt has been fattening itself on mortal souls for centuries, holding them as currency against debts no living person knew they owed. Standing under those shelves, I finally understood the scale of it. Not a vault. A *nation*. A country of the taken, filed alphabetically by creditor.

Rosalind had stopped walking.

He stood in the center of the hall with his head tipped back, and I saw him doing the arithmetic I was doing—counting jars, multiplying shelves, arriving at numbers with too many digits—and I watched the storm come up under his skin. Literally. Static crawled along his forearms; the candle flames leaned toward him like flowers toward sun.

"Don't," I said quietly.

"They're all *here*." His voice came out scraped. "Sera, they're all just—*here*. In jars. Like preserves."

"Yes."

"And we walked past a thousand clerks who could tell us where each one belongs, and none of them care, because caring isn't in the ledger—"

"*Rosalind.*" I took his wrist. The static bit my palm and I held on anyway. "If you burn this place down now, Wren stays lost in the ashes. We do this in order. We find her. Then we decide how angry to be."

Something in him banked. Slowly, grudgingly, the way a real storm banks—unconvinced, merely waiting. He nodded once.

The clerk had watched all of this without expression. Now it produced a second book from somewhere inside its robes—a slim one, bound in grey leather gone soft with handling—and licked a silver-stained thumb.

"Name?"

"Whitlock," Rosalind said. "Wren Whitlock. Sold into the court twelve years ago. Blood-price against her brother's exile."

The clerk's pen scratched. Stopped. Scratched again, slower. I watched its face do the same thing the gate-clerk's had done—that flicker beneath the tiredness, that cousin-of-fear—and I understood, with the cold certainty of someone who has read enough omens, that our name meant something down here. That *storm-witch of Greyhollow* had been entered into some ledger of its own, long ago, and entries in ledgers do not forget.

"Forfeit Ledger, sub-column: blood-price, collateral class." The clerk began to walk. "You're in luck. Collateral-class holdings are stored near the reading desks. The court likes them accessible." It glanced back over its shoulder, and for a moment the tiredness fell away entirely and I saw what was underneath: an old, old grief, worn smooth as river stone. "Accessible means *remindable*. The court finds it profitable to let creditors look at what they own. Hope keeps debtors compliant."

"And if the debtor can't pay?" Rosalind asked.

"Then the collateral matures." The clerk turned a corner. "At the thirteenth turn of the moon, all outstanding blood-prices mature. Standard terms."

*Standard terms.* Four words, delivered like weather. Somewhere above us, under the sky that had turned against us on the mountain, the twelfth moon was waning toward its last thin crescent. I did the count I'd been doing since Saltmere and arrived, again, at days. A handful of days. Maybe fewer.

* * *

They found her shelf in the eastern stacks, and I will carry the sound of it to whatever grave I'm headed for: the sound Rosalind made. Not a shout. Something smaller and more terrible than a shout—a single syllable bitten in half, the way a man bites down when he's been struck.

Her jar sat at eye level, third row, between a farmer's soul that glowed wheat-gold and a child's that gave no light at all. It was plain clay, unremarkable, stoppered with wax. But inside—

Inside, something moved.

Not thrashing. Nothing so dramatic. Just a slow turning, the way a sleeper turns toward a familiar voice, and as Rosalind stepped closer the movement quickened, circled, pressed itself flat against the glass on his side of the jar. The wax seal fogged with warmth.

"She knows me," he whispered. "Sera. She *knows* me."

"Of course she does." My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "She's been waiting twelve years. Waiting is the one thing souls down here get very good at."

He raised both hands to the jar and stopped himself a finger's width away. I watched him fight for discipline the way I'd once watched novices fight for theirs—jaw set, breath counted, power shoved down and held. If he touched that seal with storm-blood still crackling under his skin, gods only knew what the contract would read as. Tampering, maybe. Default, possibly. Down here, everything was a clause.

"Wren," he said instead, low. "It's Roz. I'm here. I'm getting you out."

The thing in the jar pressed harder against the glass. And then—it was so fast I nearly missed it—the warmth of it flared once, twice. Three times. Pause. Three times. Pause.

Two. Three.

I'd been a paladin of the moon-cult for eleven years before the fall. Old codes, older signals. Two-three-two: *not safe. wait.*

"She's telling you to hold," I said. "She's telling you it's not safe."

Rosalind's hand hovered, trembling. "How do you know—"

"Because I trained a hundred years of sisters to tap messages on stone walls, and because whoever sold her here taught her the same codes I learned." I looked at the jar—at the small warm presence straining against the clay—and felt something in my chest turn over. Twelve years in a jar, and her first act upon seeing her brother wasn't begging. It was a warning. "Your sister is smarter than both of us. She's saying wait. So we wait, and we listen, and we find out what she knows."

* * *

What she knew took an hour of tapping to unfold, and by the end of it my hands were shaking and not from cold.

The clerk—its name, extracted reluctantly, was Nine-Debts-Withheld—let us use a reading desk in exchange for Rosalink's pledge-mark being displayed openly on his wrist, where any passing official could verify it. We sat under a lamp that burned without oil while Rosalind pressed his palm flat to the desk and I translated, letter by letter, the taps that came back through the floor. Souls in jars could feel the stone. Souls in jars could speak through it, faintly, if they'd had twelve years to learn the grammar.

Wren's grammar was fluent.

*The court fears the waking,* she tapped. *Badly. The hoard is not wealth. It is a wall.*

"A wall," I repeated aloud, and watched Nine-Debts-Withheld go very still across the desk.

*Against what?*

*Against the gods. Every soul they hold is a prayer unanswered. A god cannot rise where its faithful are silent. The court has been silencing the faithful for centuries and calling it bookkeeping.* A pause in the tapping, longer than punctuation. *My brother's storm woke the valley prayers. That is why the price on him. Not exile. Silencing.*

I looked at Rosalind. He had gone white to the lips.

"They didn't sell Wren to punish me," he said slowly. "They sold her to keep me *quiet*."

"To keep the whole valley quiet," I said. "Greyhollow is magicless because the court made it a vault. Your storms were the loudest prayer in a hundred miles, and they buried you both under the silence." I thought of the elders branding him traitor at the boundary, and felt my stomach turn. Had they known? Did anyone know what they were guarding? "And now the gods are waking anyway, which means the wall is cracking, which means—"

"—the thirteenth turn," Rosalind finished. "Collateral matures. They call in the blood-price. What does that mean for her? What actually happens to a soul when the debt matures?"

Nine-Debts-Withheld answered without looking up from its book. "Matured collateral is *spent*, paladin. Spent against the court's need. And the court's present need—" it finally raised its tired eyes, and I saw clearly now that the tiredness had always been shame wearing a uniform, "—is war. Against the waking ones. They intend to spend the hoard as fuel. Thousands of souls, burned in one great offering, to snuff the gods before the gods grow strong enough to collect what the gods believe they are owed."

The lamp flickered. On the desk, under Rosalind's palm, the tapping started up again—fast, urgent, hammering.

*I know how to move my soul. I've had twelve years to learn the locks. Get me to the vault proper. Get me out of the jar. Before maturity. There is a way.*

"How?" Rosalind demanded of the air. "*Wren, how?*"

The taps came slow and deliberate, each one placed like a foot on ice:

*Find the Understeward. Ask to see the original blood-price contract. Read every line. Trust nothing signed in haste—including by me.*

Then, after a moment:

*And Roz. Whatever they offer you. However good it sounds. Remember what I cost, and refuse anything cheap.*

The tapping stopped. In the jar three rows up and one row over, the small warm presence settled, patient as a held breath, and Rosalind Whitlock stared at his empty palm as if his sister's voice might still be printed on it.

Above us, far above, through leagues of mountain and glass and lies, I felt—did not see, *felt*—the twelfth moon slide another degree toward its dying.

Days. We had days. And somewhere in this office of the damned, an Understeward was already drafting an offer, and every instinct I had left told me the offer would be addressed to the man sitting next to me, and that it would be generous, and that generosity, in this place, was the sharpest blade they owned.
