## 22. Wren, Unbodied

The vault had no walls.

Wren had learned this in the first year, when she still thought in terms of rooms and doors and the bodies that moved through them. The Undercourt kept its souls in a ledger that was also a place, and the place was a dark so complete it made her laugh—she who had grown up on a mountain where the fog came down like wool and you learned to find your way by feel. This was just more of that. More of the same. She could do this.

She had done it for twelve years.

Time down here was a rumor. There were no moons in the ledger-dark, only the *turns*—a shiver that ran through everything at intervals, like a great animal rolling over in its sleep. Wren counted them because counting was a thing souls could do, and because her brother had taught her to count stars through smoke holes, and because if she stopped she would become what they wanted: not a soul but an entry. A line of figures in a book. Collateral against a debt some clerk had once tallied with dry fingers and no face.

*One soul, female, aged nine years at forfeiture. Held against the exile-price of Rosalind Whitlock.*

She knew herself by heart now. It was the only prayer they'd left her.

But here was the thing the clerks didn't know, the thing she'd discovered in the seventh year and guarded ever since like a coal cupped in both hands: the ledger was made of prayers. Every soul in it had been someone's answer to something—a bargain struck, a price paid, a desperate hand signing away what it loved most. And prayers, even dead ones, even *filed* ones, still carried their original shape. Still whispered. If you lay very still in the dark and pressed your attention against the binding of the world, you could hear the other souls breathing. You could hear the court's clerks muttering over their columns. And once—only once—you could hear something else entirely.

A crow had landed inside her darkness, twelve turns ago, and looked at her with one human eye.

"Little wren," it said, in a voice like a door opening in a house long empty. "You've been reading."

---

They came for her sometimes. Not often—the clerks were lazy about collateral, and collateral was all she was—but enough that she'd learned the ritual of being *examined*. A cold weight settled around her, the sensation of fingers that weren't fingers turning her over, checking her edges, confirming she hadn't frayed or fled. Souls couldn't flee. That was the first law of the vault, repeated so often it wore grooves into the dark: *the ledger holds; the ledger holds; nothing signed may be unsaid.*

Wren had spent eleven of her twelve years believing it.

Then the gods woke.

She felt it before anyone told her—a tremor in the ledger's binding, like frost cracking on a pond. The old powers stirring under the Glass Steppe, shaking off their long silence, and every soul in the vault felt it as warmth. Real warmth. First heat she'd known since the smokehouse night when her brother's storm tore the sky open and a king's heir fell out of it, broken.

After that, the clerks stopped being lazy.

She heard them through the binding now, their dry voices quickening, columns being recounted, entries cross-referenced with a urgency that tasted like fear. Fear was new. Fear was interesting. Fear meant the court was afraid of something, and anything a thing feared was a lever, and Wren Whitlock had been pulling levers since she was small enough to stand on a stool and work her father's bellows.

So she listened. She listened the way she'd once listened for weather.

And what she heard, in the long cold turns while the clerks scurried, was this: the gods waking beneath the Steppe were owed things. Old things. Prayers from centuries of faithful mouths that the court had intercepted and converted—hoarded, banked, folded into its ledgers as currency. Every soul in the vault was a stolen payment. Every prayer the old gods had ever answered, the Undercourt had found a way to invoice.

And the gods, newly awake, were beginning to ask for receipts.

"They cannot war," one clerk hissed to another, in the turn when the tremors grew strong enough that even the vault's dark seemed to lean toward the surface. "Not openly. Not yet. But if they call in the debts—all of them at once—the ledgers fail. Everything we hold becomes *theirs* again."

"Then we make war quietly," said the other. "We have a hunter. We have contracts older than their sleeping. When the thirteenth moon turns, the seal closes, and what is written stays written. Forever."

"The storm-child," the first said, and Wren went very still, though stillness meant little when you had no body to still. "He is coming. The crow-god has marked him. If he reaches the gates before the turn—if he tries to redeem the collateral—we invoke the original contract. The debt plus interest. His soul against hers." A pause, dry as paper. "Let him sign. Let him feed the ledgers. A soul that size would fund a century of war against the sky itself."

Wren laughed.

It made no sound—it never did—but she felt it move through whatever she was made of now, bright and bitter and *hers*. They thought her brother was the weapon. They thought he'd walk through those obsidian gates with his big grieving heart and his terrible power and they'd simply *hand him a pen*, and he'd sign himself into their war-chest because that was what Rosalind did: he paid. He'd sold his name to exile, his sister to blood-price, twelve years of his life to a forge and a hammer, and he'd pay again, gladly, because paying was how he loved.

She knew him. She was nine years old and she knew him, and twelve years in the dark had not dulled that knowing one whit.

And so, when the crow came back—on the turn they called the twelfth waning, settling into her darkness with its one human eye gleaming like a coin dropped down a well—she was ready.

---

"Little wren," Corvass said. "You've been busy."

"I've been listening," Wren said. "You're late."

The god's feathers rustled, which she suspected was amusement. "Gods keep their own calendar. Yours is about to run out. Thirteenth turn. Do you understand what happens then?"

"The seal closes. I stay forever. My brother signs himself into your war whether he means to or not." She let the words sit. "Yes. I understand the shape of it."

"You are remarkably calm for a soul in a jar."

"I'm remarkably calm for a soul who's spent twelve years doing sums." She pressed close to the edge of herself, such as it was. "Crow-god. Corvass. Whatever you are. Listen to me, because you have maybe half a turn before a clerk comes to check my binding, and there are things you need to carry to the surface."

"Speak."

"First: the contract. The original one, the one they'll wave at my brother—it has a redemption clause. All the old ones do; the clerks are too proud of their own law to write without one. Redemption requires the debtor to present the collateral *freely offered by its holder.*" She paused. "I am the holder. No one has ever asked me."

Silence in the dark. Then, softly, with something that might have been wonder: "Twelve years, and the court never asked the soul herself."

"Why would they? Souls can't refuse. Souls can't *do* anything." She let her laughter flicker through the words. "That's what they think. But a freely offered release isn't a thing the ledger can refuse either. Their own law cuts both ways. It always has. They wrote it to protect themselves from the gods, and they never imagined a soul might read it."

"And second?"

"Second." She gathered herself, all twelve years of listening compressed into what came next. "When the time comes—and it will come fast, at the gates, in front of the clerks—they will offer him the trade. His soul for mine. Debt plus interest. And he will say yes, because he's Rosalind, and saying yes is how he loves. So someone has to stop him. Someone has to show him what's buried in the fine print: that his signature doesn't buy my freedom, it funds their war on the waking gods. That his soul becomes a siege-engine pointed at everything he's spent twelve years learning to protect."

"You want me to warn him."

"I want you to get me *to* him." The truth of it rose up in her, huge and hot. "Dream-contact. Soul-to-soul. You're a god; the vault's binding can't stop what you carry, only what it holds. Take me into his sleep, one time, before he reaches the gates. Let me talk to my brother. Let me read him the clause myself, because he won't believe a god and he won't believe a paladin, but he'll believe me. He believed me when I was nine and telling him the ice on the trough would hold. He should have listened then, too."

The crow's human eye blinked, slow, considering.

"You know what you're asking. To reach into the dream of a storm-caller on the eve of his reckoning. If his power wakes mid-dream, it will tear the contact apart—and possibly tear you."

"Everything tears eventually," Wren said. "Better torn reaching than whole rotting. Twelve years in the dark, crow-god. I've done my time being careful. Careful got me filed." Her voice—her voiceless voice, the one only gods and brothers could hear—took on an edge like wind off the Steppe. "Now I'm done being collateral. I'm going to be the guide."

Another rustle. Then the darkness shifted, and she felt it: a thread, thin and bright, spinning out from the vault's binding, up through stone and soil and miles of mirror-flat glass, toward a campfire she could not see, where a man with storm-scars slept badly beside a paladin with a broken oath.

"Hold on," Corvass said. "This will feel like falling."

"Good," said Wren. "I've forgotten what that feels like. I want to remember."

And she fell—upward, outward, through the ledger-dark and the cold stone and the roots of the mountain, through the frozen skin of the Steppe and its haunted reflections, into a dream that smelled of woodsmoke and ozone, where a man knelt at a forge with his hands full of lightning, hammering a horseshoe that rang like thunder, and did not look up until she spoke his childhood nickname, the one only two people alive had ever used—

—and when he turned, and saw her, and dropped the hammer, and said her name like it was the last word of a prayer he'd been reciting for twelve years, Wren understood suddenly, completely, why the court feared the waking gods.

Because the gods were waking. But so was everyone they'd ever loved.

She opened her mouth to speak the clause that would save him—and felt, far below, deep in the vault, a clerk's dry finger come to rest on her binding, and begin, slowly, to check the ledger line that bore her name.

* * *

Down in the dark, the finger paused.

Something was wrong with the entry. Something was *missing*—a thread pulled loose from the binding, thin as spider silk, running up and out and away, and the clerk who found it did not cry out, because clerks of the Undercourt did not cry out. It simply set down its pen, closed its ledger with a sound like a tomb sealing, and went to tell the Hunt that the collateral had begun, impossibly, to move.

Above, in a dream full of rain and light, Wren took her brother's face in her insubstantial hands and began, quickly, to teach him how to read a contract the way a soul reads it—from the bottom up, where the teeth are hidden.

"We don't have much time," she said. "So listen carefully, Rosalind. Because I'm only going to get to say this once."
