## 23. A Bargain Poorly Read

The quill was heavier than it looked.

Rosalind held it above the parchment and felt its weight the way a man feels a rope about his neck—light at first, then all at once not light at all. The Understeward's office had no windows. The walls were obsidian polished to a shine so deep that his reflection stood in every direction he looked, a dozen thin Rosalinds holding a dozen thin quills, all of them waiting for him to be brave or stupid enough to finish.

"Take your time," said the Understeward.

That was the worst part. In twelve years of hiding, Rosalind had learned to read every kind of danger—the elder's narrowed eyes, the border patrol's bored hands drifting toward iron, the storm itself gathering in a sky that had no business darkening in a magicless valley. Danger always hurried you. It pressed at your back and made you choose before you were ready.

The Understeward did none of that. He sat behind a desk of black glass with his long fingers folded, and he smiled the way a moneylender smiles when the borrower has already counted out the coins. His face was almost human. *Almost* was doing terrible work in that sentence. The jaw was a fraction too long, the eyes a shade too patient, and when he blinked—and he blinked rarely—it was sideways, like something remembering how eyes worked and getting it wrong on purpose.

"The terms, once more," the Understeward said, "for clarity's sake. We are scrupulous here. The court prizes nothing so highly as clarity."

"I know," Rosalind said. His voice came out flat and farrier-calm, the voice he'd used on frightened horses for twelve years. "You've said it four times."

"And I will say it a fifth if it pleases you." The Understeward unfolded one hand and gestured, and the parchment rearranged its lines like ants forming words. "Your soul, freely given, in exchange for the soul entered under Ledger of Forfeit, entry nine-and-forty-thousand-one-hundred-twelve: Wren Whitlock. Debts reconciled. Interest forgiven. The original blood-price of your exile considered paid in full, with the crown's claim extinguished and no further recourse available to the Undercourt or its agents. You walk out of here with her. She walks out of here free. No hunter follows. No recall is issued."

"No clause," Sera said from beside him.

Sera Vane stood at his shoulder with her arms crossed and her moon-blessing banked down to embers—he could feel it, a cold silver hum against the heat that lived in his chest now. She hadn't stopped watching the Understeward since they'd sat down. She hadn't blinked much either.

"No hidden clause," she corrected herself, because the Understeward's smile had widened by a degree, and that degree had been answer enough. "No clause at all beyond what's written."

"Paladin," the Understeward said gently, "you wound me. Every word is on the page. That is the entire point of a page." He turned those too-patient eyes back to Rosalind. "The court has no interest in trickery. Trickery breeds grievance, and grievance breeds litigation, and litigation—" he shuddered delicately, an insect shudder— "is *vulgar*."

Rosalind looked down at the contract.

The script was beautiful. That should have warned him. Nothing honest was written that beautifully. The letters curled like frost on a smokehouse window, and the ink was the red-black of old blood, and every line resolved into sense the moment he read it: *the undersigned offers freely*... *in perpetuity*... *surrendering all claim, name, and nature*...

His soul. For Wren's.

Twelve years ago he had signed something similar in spirit—a bargain made by a fourteen-year-old girl with a crow perched on her shoulder and tears freezing on her face, trading her own soul to pay the blood-price her brother's storm had incurred. He'd learned about it secondhand, from a magistrate's sneer, years later, when there was nothing left to do but hate himself efficiently and get up before dawn to shoe horses in a valley where magic couldn't follow.

Now the ledger was open on the desk beside the contract, and he could see the entry. Entry nine-and-forty-thousand-one-hundred-twelve. *Whitlock, Wren.* Her soul was represented as a single line of light beneath the glass, pale and small and steady as a candle flame in a draftless room.

It pulsed when he leaned close.

"You can see her," the Understeward observed. "Most petitioners cannot. It's a mercy we extend to family."

"She's in there," Rosalind said.

"She is *held* here," the Understeward corrected, with a bureaucrat's love of precision. "Held is not kept. There is a difference, and the difference is everything. A kept soul suffers. A held soul simply... waits. Your sister has waited twelve years without a single hour of anguish. You have our word on it." The smile again. "The court honors its word. Ask anyone who has ever dealt with us."

*Ask them,* something whispered in the back of Rosalind's mind, in a voice that sounded like wind through a corpse-road of dead branches. *Ask them where they are.*

He picked up the quill.

"Ros." Sera's voice, low.

"I'm reading it."

"You've read it three times."

"Then I'll read it a fourth." He kept his eyes on the page. "Corvass said the court fears the waking gods. Corvass said the gods are waking because the court hoards souls. And you want me to believe—" he looked up— "that the court will hand over a soul it needs, freely, out of the goodness of its ledgers?"

Something flickered across the Understeward's face. It was gone too fast to read, but it had been there, and Sera saw it too; he heard her breath change.

"A fair question," the Understeward said smoothly, "deserving of a fair answer." He laced his fingers again. "Yes. The court has uses for souls. Yes. The times are... uncertain. But your sister's soul is a small denomination, farrier. A peasant girl's soul, sold cheap by a child in a panic. In the great economy of the court, she is a copper bit." He spread his hands. "We are pleased to be rid of her at any price, and the price offered is *magnificent*. Your soul is storm-wrought, god-touched, saturated with the essence of three waking deities. Do you understand? We are not cheating you. We are *overpaying*."

The logic was clean. That was the trouble with it. Everything the court did was clean—clean halls, clean script, clean smiles—and Rosalind had spent twelve years learning that the cleanest things in any valley were the things that had eaten whatever used to live inside them.

But Wren's line of light pulsed beneath the glass, steady, waiting, and twelve years was twelve years, and the thirteenth moon was already waning. He could count its nights on his fingers now. If he walked out of this office without signing, the gates would seal, the ledger would close, and his sister would become a permanent line item in a bureaucracy of the damned.

He put quill to parchment.

The ink touched the page and *moved*.

Not flowed—moved, deliberately, curling away from the nib like a worm avoiding a hook, and then settling, and then the whole contract seemed to exhale, the letters relaxing into their final shapes. Ready to receive him.

And in the space between one heartbeat and the next, Rosalind fell asleep sitting up.

---

He knew it was a dream because the forge was burning.

Not the little forge of Greyhollow, the one he'd tended for twelve years with its modest coal fire and its smell of iron and horse sweat. This was the forge of his childhood, the one their father had built before the war took him, with the wide stone hearth and the bellows handle worn smooth by two generations of hands. It burned blue-white, storm-colored, impossible, and the heat of it didn't burn at all—it *remembered*, the way heat remembers itself in a fever dream.

"Wren?" he said.

She was standing at the anvil with her back to him, and she was sixteen again, the age she'd been when he last truly saw her—before the court took her, before twelve years of vault-dark. Small and sharp-faced, hair escaping its braid, wearing the patched smock she'd refused to replace even when their mother begged. Her hands were busy with something on the anvil's face. Writing. Dragging her finger through the metal as if it were frost on a window.

"You're reading it wrong," she said, without turning around. "You always read too fast. You read what it wants you to read."

"Wren—"

"*Listen to me.*" She spun, and her face was sixteen and also not; her eyes had a depth no sixteen-year-old's should, the look of someone who has spent twelve years in the dark learning to see in it. "We don't have long. Dream-contact costs Corvass more than he lets on, and the court's dream-wards are thick as wool tonight—they know you're here, Ros, they can feel you deciding. So shut up and listen."

He shut up and listened. Some things, apparently, survived death, exile, and soul-vaulting intact.

"Read the fourth paragraph," she said. "Not the way it's written. Read what's *underneath* it."

"There's nothing underneath it."

"There's always something underneath it. That's how they write. That's how they've *always* written." She grabbed his wrist—her grip was cold, vault-cold, and it went through his dream-skin like winter through a torn coat. "Ros. When I signed my bargain, I was fourteen and terrified and I read every word, I swear to you I did, I read it until my eyes bled. And I still missed it. Because the words say one thing and the grammar says another, and the grammar is where they keep the teeth."

"The grammar," Rosalind repeated flatly.

"The grammar." Her eyes blazed. "Paragraph four. Read it aloud. Aloud matters—the contract listens to voices, it's listening right now, so whisper it, but *whisper it exactly*, every word, even the ones that sound like filler. Especially those."

He closed his eyes. The contract floated up behind his lids, beautiful and curling, and he found paragraph four and read it aloud in a whisper:

*"In consideration of the soul herein surrendered, the court shall receive the same freely and in perpetuity, such that its essence may be applied toward the maintenance and furtherance of the court's lawful interests, wherever those interests may lie, without limitation as to purpose, duration, or means."*

Silence in the dream-forge. The blue fire crackled.

"That sounds like boilerplate," he said.

"It does," Wren agreed. "I thought so too, at fourteen. Now listen to what it actually says." She ticked the words off on frost-cold fingers. "*Without limitation as to purpose.* Not 'this purpose' or 'lawful purposes'—*any* purpose. *Duration*—forever, no term, no sunset. *Means*—" her voice dropped— "means *how*. Ros, I've spent twelve years in their vaults. I've shelved their ledgers. I've filed their contracts. Do you know why the court is selling your soul's freedom so cheap? Why the great economy suddenly finds a god-touched soul worth a copper-bit trade?"

"...Why?"

"Because it isn't buying your soul." Her grip tightened. "It's buying *fuel*. Paragraph four makes your essence applicable to the court's interests without limitation—which includes the war it's planning against the waking gods. They don't want you in a vault, Ros. Vaults are storage. They want you as *kindling*. Your storms, your god-touch, everything Corvass and the others woke in you—they'll pour it into their hunters, their wards, their engines. They'll burn you against the gods you're trying to serve. And the beautiful part, the *legal* part, is that you'll have agreed. Freely. In writing. With witnesses." She smiled, and it was the most heartbroken thing he'd ever seen. "They'll make you the weapon that kills your own gods. And Wren Whitlock goes free as payment rendered."

The forge-fire roared.

"They showed you her," Wren said quickly, quieter. "The little light under the glass. That pulse—that wasn't me, Ros. Souls in the Ledger of Forfeit don't pulse. They're archived. Stabilized. That light was bait dressed as mercy, and you leaned toward it like a moth, and I felt you lean from three floors down through two inches of obsidian, which is why Corvass is spending himself ragged getting me into your head right now." She let go of his wrist. "Don't sign. Whatever you feel in that room—and you'll feel it, they'll make sure you feel it, guilt and hope and twelve years of missing me—all of it is *furniture*. They furnished that office the way mother furnished the front room when the tax collector came. To make you sit down and stay polite."

"How do you know all this?" he whispered. "How do you know their grammar, their furniture, their—"

"Because I've been reading their contracts for twelve years," Wren said. "From the inside. Somebody had to." Something moved across her face—pride, fierce and unbanked, the exact expression she'd worn at eight years old when she'd shod her first pony and dared him to say a girl couldn't. "You spent twelve years becoming a farrier. I spent twelve years becoming an expert on the Undercourt. You're not the only one who got good at surviving, big brother."

The dream was thinning. He could feel it—the edges of the forge going grey, the fire guttering toward ordinary orange, the dream-wards closing in like a fist.

"Wren—"

"Listen. Last thing." She stepped close, and for one moment she was neither sixteen nor soul-old but simply his sister, the girl who used to fall asleep against his shoulder during thunderstorms because his storms never scared her. "Don't sign. Don't refuse either—not loudly, not yet. Refusal closes doors, and you need doors. Sign nothing. Tell them you require a night to pray on it—they respect piety, it's in their rules somewhere, they have to grant a night's grace to a petitioner who invokes prayer. It's the one loophole their own liturgy leaves open." Her mouth twitched. "They wrote rules for dealing with gods, and now the gods are awake, and they *have* to follow them. Isn't that wonderful?"

The forge was nearly gone. Grey everywhere. Her face the last color in the world.

"Find paragraph seven before morning," she said, fast now, words tumbling. "Paragraph seven is the recall clause. Read its grammar. And Ros—when they give you your night of grace, don't spend it praying. Spend it finding me. Corvass knows the way back. Third floor. Shelf of the Forfeit. I'll leave the light on."

"What light? Wren—"

"Souls don't need lamps," she said, grinning as the world dissolved, "we *are* lamps, you idiot, I've been trying to tell you that since the smokehouse—"

---

He woke with the quill still in his hand and a bead of red-black ink trembling on its nib, one heartbeat from the page.

The Understeward watched him with those sideways-blinking eyes. Beside him, Sera had gone rigid; he could feel the moon-blessing flaring silver-hot against his arm, and he understood that from the outside he had simply paused—two seconds, maybe three—but that Sera had seen something cross his face that made her ready to burn the office down.

"Well?" the Understeward said pleasantly. "Do you find the terms agreeable?"

Rosalind set the quill down. Carefully. The click of it against the glass desk was the loudest sound in the world.

"I find," he said, "that a bargain touching the gods deserves a night of prayer."

For the first time, something moved behind the Understeward's patience—something quick and hungry and *disappointed*, there and mastered, but there.

"Naturally," said the Understeward. "Piety will be honored. One night." And as Rosalind rose, as Sera's hand found his elbow and steered him toward the door, the Understeward's voice followed them, silk over stone:

"Pray hard, farrier. The gods wake slowly. And paragraph seven is such a *short* clause."

Behind them, in the dark of the office, the contract curled its letters closed like a fist.

And three floors down, on a shelf in the Ledger of Forfeit, a small pale light began, very deliberately, to burn brighter.
