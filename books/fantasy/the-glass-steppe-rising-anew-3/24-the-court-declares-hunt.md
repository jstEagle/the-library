## 24. The Court Declares Hunt

I heard the ledger close before I understood what it meant.

It was not a loud sound. That was the horror of it—a single page turned, a binding pressed shut somewhere deep in the Undercourt's stacks, and the whole obsidian world around us went still the way a forest goes still when something with teeth enters it. Three hundred years of paladin training narrowed to a point: *listen*.

"Rosalind," I said. "Walk. Don't run."

He was already looking at me across the corridor of black glass, Wren's soul-marked ledger clutched against his chest like a stolen child. The false writ in his other hand—the one we'd forged from Saltmere's moon-sigils and Corvass's borrowed authority—had stopped glowing. Dead ink. The court had read it, weighed it, found it hollow.

Behind him, the corridor's far end was no longer where it had been.

The halls of the Undercourt did not move the way living things moved. They moved the way arithmetic moves—inevitable, silent, correct. One moment the passage behind Rosalind ran forty feet to a junction of stairs; the next it ran sixty feet, then ninety, the walls sliding apart like a hand opening to keep us. Not blocking us. *Measuring* us. I understood then that the court had known for some time exactly how fast we could walk, and was simply deciding when to stop letting us.

"The clause," Rosalind breathed. "Wren said—Sera, she said if they found out before the thirteenth turn—"

"They've found out." I drew my sword. It was a stupid gesture; there was nothing yet to fight. But my hand wanted steel between me and this place, even if the steel was only honest about being useless. "The gates seal at the turn. We have until then to be anywhere except here."

* * *

We ran.

The corridors of the Undercourt were built for processions, not flight—vast ribbed tunnels of glassy stone lit by veins of cold blue light that pulsed like slow thought. Our footsteps came back at us wrong, delayed half a beat, as though the floor were considering whether to admit we'd touched it. Ahead of us the passage forked into seven branches, and every branch looked identical, and I knew without knowing how I knew that six of them were lies.

Corvass landed on my shoulder hard enough to bruise, his one human eye bright with something between amusement and fury.

"Leftmost," he croaked. "And don't thank me. The walls can hear gratitude. Gratitude has weight here."

"What does that mean?"

"It means everything you feel is currency, moon-child, and you are spending it loudly." He ruffled his feathers, and for just a moment I saw through the crow-shape to something vast and tired underneath—an old god doing bookkeeping in enemy territory. "The court knows what you took. It hasn't declared the Hunt yet because declaring costs it something too. A debt must be *named* before it can be collected."

"Then let's be gone before it names us," Rosalind said.

But the leftmost branch ended in a wall.

Not appeared-ended-in—*became* a wall, the stone flowing smooth over what had been open air an eyeblink before, so seamlessly that a stranger would swear no passage had ever existed there. Rosalind skidded, caught himself on his palm, and the glass under his hand frosted white. His storm-blood answering fear. In Greyhollow that frost would have been impossible. Here it was a beacon.

"Right," I said, turning. "New plan. We stop choosing corridors."

"That's not a plan, Sera, that's surrender with extra steps—"

"No." I put my back to the new wall and made myself look up, at the ceiling ribs arching away into dark, at the blue light-veins pulsing their slow pulse. "The halls shift for *us*. They're reading our intent—where we want to go—and closing those paths. So we stop wanting anything." I looked at Corvass. "Can you walk us blind? Carry us somewhere the court isn't watching?"

The crow-god was quiet a moment too long.

"There is one place," he said finally. "A stair that predates the court. The Undercourt was built *on top of* something, the way a fortress is built on a grave. The builders never finished the bottom floors because the bottom floors were never theirs. If I take you down—" his human eye fixed on Rosalind, "—the walls won't know your wants, because those halls were never taught to read them."

"And the cost?" Rosalind asked. He'd learned, these weeks on the Steppe, that Corvass never gave directions without a bill attached.

"You'll hear the grave," Corvass said simply. "That's all. You'll hear it, and it will hear you, and neither of us can say what comes of that. Choose quickly. The blue light is counting your heartbeats."

I hadn't noticed it, but he was right—the veins in the walls had changed rhythm since we'd stopped running. Slower. Deliberate. Like a clerk tallying.

"Down," I said.

* * *

Corvass flew ahead of us through a gap in the floor I would have sworn had not existed, a seam of older stone beneath the obsidian—rougher, warmer, veined with something that wasn't blue but a deep ember-red, like the memory of fire. The stair beyond wound down into dark that swallowed our lantern-light whole.

Rosalind's breathing echoed strangely. Everything echoed strangely. Sound down here didn't bounce; it *lingered*, hanging in the air after its cause was done, so that I kept hearing my own footsteps from moments ago overlapping my footsteps now—a crowd of one woman walking.

Then I began to hear the others.

"Sera." Rosalind's voice, very low. "Tell me you hear it too."

Voices. Hundreds. Thousands. Far below and all around, murmuring—not words, not quite, more like the shape words leave behind when the words are taken away. Souls in vaults. The hoarded dead-and-not-dead the Undercourt traded in, stacked in ledgers like coin. And threaded through them, thin and unmistakable once I'd found its pitch:

"*Ros?*"

Wren. Not the dream-contact version, not Corvass's relayed whisper—her actual voice, carried up through whatever the red-veined stone was, faint as breath on a window.

Rosalind stopped dead on the stair. I saw his hand go to his chest, to the place where—though we hadn't known it until Wren's dream-warning—her soul already hid, slipped from its ledger into the storm-scars on his skin during the dream. She was *with* him. But her voice came from below anyway, from the vaults, from the echo of her that the court's books still claimed to own.

"Don't answer it," Corvass said sharply. "Whatever you do. An echo answered becomes a claim. That's how they catch souls that wander."

"It's not an echo." Rosalind's frost was spreading off his boots in feathering white lace, climbing the old stone, and where it spread the murmuring *stopped*, hushed, like wind flattening grass. "It's her. She's trying to tell us something. Sera—listen—"

I listened. Paladin discipline, the old order's first lesson: *in noise or silence, attend.* Beneath Wren's voice-thread, beneath the murmur-mass, there was another sound. Rhythmic. Mechanical. Coming *up* the stair after us.

Boots. Many. Perfectly synchronized.

"They're not declaring the hunt," I said, and my mouth had gone dry. "They've already declared it. That's what the boots are. The declaration *is* the boots."

* * *

What came down the stair after us were not soldiers. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever survives of my account—that I have faced knights, revenants, and the burning judgment of my own former order, and none of it prepared me for the clerks.

They wore grey robes and carried ledgers. Their faces were smooth as the corridor-walls, features suggested rather than committed to, and they did not run—they *advanced*, at a pace precisely calibrated to ours, maintaining thirty steps of distance no matter how we hurried. When Rosalind stumbled on a worn tread, the entire column behind us stumbled in unison, a perfect mirror of his error, and I understood with cold clarity that they weren't pursuing.

They were *matching*. Keeping pace. Because we were not prey fleeing hunters.

We were debtors being escorted to collections.

"Corvass," I said. "The bottom floors. How much farther?"

"Farther than you have." The god had gone flat-voiced, which for Corvass was the equivalent of screaming. "The stair ends at the grave-door. The grave-door opens only outward—it lets things *leave* the court, never enter. We came in through the gates above. There is no second entrance."

"Then we're in a dead end with a column of accountants behind us."

"We are in a dead end," Corvass agreed, "with the only door in the Undercourt the court itself cannot open. Which means, moon-child, that everything now depends on what is standing in front of the door instead of behind it."

I didn't understand until we rounded the final spiral and the stair opened into a vaulted chamber of raw red-veined rock, and I saw what stood before the grave-door.

Souls.

Dozens of them—no, hundreds, pressed against and around the ancient door like moths at a sealed lamp, shapes of pale light without bodies, drawn from the vaults by some call only they could hear. They turned toward us as we entered, and the chamber filled with the murmur-mass at full strength, and at its center, brightest, a small fierce figure shaped like a girl of nineteen who had been dead of paperwork for twelve years.

Wren's echo. The part of her the ledgers still held. And she was holding the door *shut*.

*Ros.* Her voice rang off the red stone, and every soul in the chamber turned toward Rosalind like flowers finding sun. *You brought it. You actually—you absolute idiot, you brought yourself. Listen fast. The door opens for the free, not the owned. Every soul touching it is still owned—that's why it won't move for them, why it won't move for ME. It needs someone unclaimed.*

The boots were louder now. The grey column filled the stairway behind us, spilling into the chamber's edges, opening their ledgers in unison with a sound like a thousand wings unfolding.

"I'm unclaimed," Rosalind said.

*No,* Wren's echo said, and there was anguish in it. *You're marked. Storm-blood, exile-price, contract-pending. The court owns a piece of you, Ros, it always did, that was the original bargain—my soul for your exile. You can't open it while any part of you belongs to them.*

The lead clerk stepped forward. Its blank face tilted, considering, and when it spoke, its voice was the corridor-voice, the arithmetic voice, patient as interest accruing.

**"ROSALIND WHITLOCK. THE COURT FINDS YOUR WRIT FORGED. THE COURT FINDS YOUR SISTER'S LEDGER EMPTY. THE COURT DECLARES HUNT. ALL DEBTS ARE NOW DUE AT ONCE."**

Every ledger snapped shut. The blue light died. In the sudden ember-dark, the clerks' faces grew features after all—drawn on, ink-fresh, smiling.

Rosalind's storms broke loose under his skin. Lightning crawled his arms; frost and heat warred in the air around him; the red-veined stone drank the glow and threw it back tenfold. He was magnificent and terrified and mine to protect, and I planted myself between him and the column with a sword that suddenly mattered again, because the clerks had produced pens, and the pens had blades.

"Wren!" I shouted over the rising wind. "If he can't open it—what can?"

Her echo flared brighter, and I felt rather than heard her laugh, wild and furious, her brother's laugh.

*Nobody,* she said. *Yet. But the door doesn't check hands, Sera Vane. It checks debts. And there's one person in this room whose every debt was paid the day she burned her oath—*

She meant me. I knew it the way you know lightning before thunder.

The grave-door, ancient and patient, waited for the only soul in the Undercourt who owed it nothing—and the clerks' inked smiles all turned toward me at once.
