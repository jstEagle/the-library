## 25. The Moon Turns Thirteenth

The halls of the Undercourt were eating themselves.

I felt it in my teeth first—a grinding hum in the obsidian underfoot, like the whole mountain was a jaw deciding whether to bite down. Behind us, the great doors of the Ledger Hall had slammed with a sound I'd carry to my grave, and the corridors that had been straight when we entered now bent and braided like roots. The court was rearranging itself around us the way water rearranges around a drowning man.

"Left," Sera said.

"That's a wall."

"It was a wall." She was already moving, moon-blessing flaring pale along her blade, and where her light touched the stone it remembered being a corridor again. "The court can't hold a shape where the old light falls. Keep close."

Keep close. As if I could do anything else. My sister's soul was somewhere in this shifting dark—had been, an hour ago, before Wren's dream-warning cracked the Understeward's bargain open like a rotten egg—and every second we ran was a second the Hunt gained.

Above us—though *above* meant nothing here—the moon was turning its thirteenth turn. I could feel it the way you feel weather coming in a bad knee. Somewhere past all this stone, the sky was rolling over, and when it finished, the gates would seal for another age, and Wren would be ledger-paper forever.

Corvass flew ahead of us, or rather his crows did—a dozen black shapes pouring through gaps too small for any living bird, their voices overlapping in one ragged chorus. *"Faster, storm-child. The warden of doors has been called. The warden of doors is never late."*

"The warden of doors," Sera panted, "is the reason we came through the ash-ducts instead of the gate."

"The warden of doors," the crows said together, "is the reason there are no more ash-ducts."

As if to prove it, the wall beside us exhaled. Not wind—*breath*, warm and patient, smelling of ink and cold iron. A seam opened in the obsidian, and something looked out. It had no face I could fix on. It had a door-knocker where a mouth should be, and when it spoke, the knocker swung.

**"Thief of ledgers. The moon turns. Stand and be inventoried."**

Sera didn't stand. She threw herself sideways, blade sweeping a crescent of moonlight across the thing's threshold, and the seam shrieked shut like a burned hand snatching back from a stove. "Run," she said, very calmly, which was how I knew she was terrified.

We ran.

* * *

Here is what I knew, and what I carried:

Wren's soul was not in the vault anymore. Had not been, perhaps, since the dream. Three nights ago—or three years ago; time moved strangely inside Corvass's crow-dreams—my sister had come to me wearing a body of rain and memory, and instead of weeping, which I would have forgiven her, she had *worked*. Twelve years in the court's vaults, she told me later, and the souls there talk, and the oldest of them remember when the court was young and made mistakes. She had learned where the ledgers kept their seams. And when the dream ended and I woke with frost on my eyelashes, I had thought: *she told me everything except how to use it.*

She hadn't. She just hadn't used words.

Because that same morning, in the grey light before dawn, my storm-mark had changed.

You have to understand what the mark was. A spiral scar on my left forearm, put there the night of the storm that killed the king's heir—not by magic but by lightning, honest lightning, the last honest thing about me. For twelve years it had been dead skin, quiet as the valley of Greyhollow itself. In the weeks since the gods woke, it had begun to ache before weather, itch before power, glow faintly when Corvass spoke.

That morning it had been *warm*. And when I pushed back my sleeve, the spiral had a second line coiled inside it, fine as spider-silk, silver-bright, turning slowly against the grain of my own scar.

A thread. A ledger-line. A soul, folded small enough to hide in a scar.

*I slipped my page out of their book,* Wren had said in the dream, grinning the way she used to grin when she'd stolen honey off the comb and gotten away clean. *Souls are entries, Rosalind. Entries can be recopied. Find me a body after the gates seal, and the court can keep its deadline—I won't be behind it.*

So we weren't running to steal her anymore. We were running because stealing her had already happened, and the court hadn't checked its books yet.

The court was checking its books now.

* * *

The corridor opened without warning into a gallery so vast my storm-sense stuttered trying to find its walls. Pillars like petrified trees. Between them, shelves—no, *cases*, glass and iron, stretching up into a darkness that swallowed the ceiling. The Vault of Forfeit. We had come out the wrong side of it, or it had come out around us.

And in the cases: souls.

I don't know what I expected. Light, maybe. What I saw was people—the way you see people in the corner of your eye, the way a house feels occupied. Each case held a shape of pressure, a person-shaped absence that hurt to look at directly. Some pressed against the glass. Some had stopped pressing long ago.

"Don't look at them," Sera said quietly. "Not yet. When the court falls, they go home. Every one. But if you stop—if you let them see you stop—we die here, and they wait another age."

"I know."

"You're crying anyway."

"I know that too."

A case near us rattled. The pressure-shape inside had turned—it couldn't see, souls in cases don't see, but it *knew*, the way dogs know thunder—and it was throwing itself against the glass with everything it had left. And underneath the rattle, thin as a hair pulled taut, I heard it make a sound.

It sang. Four notes, over and over. A lullaby. Our mother's lullaby, the one Wren used to hum while she mended nets, the one I hadn't heard in twelve years because the only other person who knew it was sold to pay my blood-price.

"Wren—" I started toward the case, and Sera caught my arm hard enough to bruise.

"That's not her."

"It's her song—"

"It's a *bait*. Look at the glass." Her voice shook, and I understood she hated saying it. Frost crept across the case from the outside in, delicate as fern-work, spelling out in letters of ice: **CLAIMED. INVENTORY PENDING.**

"They know," Sera said. "They've known since the dream. That's not your sister—that's a trap wearing her lullaby, and the moment you touch that glass the Hunt stops chasing and starts *collecting*."

My hands were fists. My storm-mark burned like a coal against my wrist, and the silver thread inside it turned faster, agitated, and I realized I could feel her—actually feel her, a small bright pressure against the inside of my fear, steady as a heartbeat. *Not there,* the pressure said, in the wordless way of storms. *Not me. Keep moving.*

"She's telling me to run," I said.

"Then for once in your life," Sera said, "listen to your sister."

* * *

The warden of doors found us at the Gallery's far arch.

I say *found* the way you'd say a tide finds a sandcastle. One moment the arch was empty; the next, the space in it was full. The warden was tall the way a verdict is tall—it filled the doorway top to bottom and made the doorway seem like the smaller thing. Its body was layered paper, contracts upon contracts, and its head was a lock, and its voice, when it spoke, was every signature ever given under duress.

**"The moon turns,"** it said. **"Turn with it. Surrender the stolen entry and be assessed leniently. Resist and be assessed."**

"How leniently?" Sera asked, and I could have kissed her for asking, because every second it talked was a second closer to the turn.

**"Your order was dissolved for mercy. Leniency is a debt you cannot service."**

"Yeah," Sera said. "I've heard that before." And she lit.

I have seen her moon-blessing burn before—on the Steppe, in the corpse-road dark—but never like this. She wasn't fighting the warden. She was standing in the archway being *moonlight*, all of it, the whole cold honest face of it, and the warden's paper body curled away from her like smoke from a window. Doors, I understood dimly, hate open sky. The moon is the one thing no contract can clause away.

"Rosalind." Her voice came strained, a bowstring note. "The turn. How close?"

I reached into the mark, into the storm sleeping under my skin, and asked the sky through six hundred feet of mountain. The answer came back tasting of iron and held breath.

"Now," I said. "It's happening *now*."

Somewhere above us, impossibly far, the thirteenth moon finished turning.

I felt the gates seal. You'd think a sound that vast would be a boom, a crash, the mountain slamming shut. It wasn't. It was a *click*. Small, precise, final—the sound of a book closing, of a lock finding its tongue, of a door deciding that whatever was outside it no longer existed. The pressure of it rolled down through the stone, and every soul-case in the Gallery shivered, and the warden of doors straightened with terrible satisfaction.

**"Sealed,"** it said. **"The entry remains ours until the next age. The debt stands. The thief stands within our walls. Assessment begins—"**

"No," I said.

The warden paused. Even Sera glanced at me.

I held up my arm and pushed back my sleeve, and I let the mark show. Let the spiral show. Let the fine silver thread coiled inside it show, turning slow and bright against my scar, singing four notes over and over in a voice only I could hear.

**"Recall,"** the warden said, and its lock-head began to turn, key-teeth rotating, reaching for the ledger-line the way a bailiff reaches for a collar. **"The entry is located. The entry will be returned to inventory. The entry—"**

The entry laughed.

I felt it before I heard it—a ripple up my arm, through my chest, out of my mouth, because the thread in my scar was laughing with my throat and it sounded exactly like my sister at eleven years old getting away with something magnificent. And along the silver line, letter by letter, script bloomed and rewrote itself. The warden read it. I watched it read it. I watched a creature made entirely of contracts experience, for the first time in its existence, a *contradiction*.

**"This entry,"** it said slowly, **"bears no ledger-number."**

"That's right," I said.

**"This entry bears no court-seal."**

"That's also right."

**"This entry bears"—** the lock-head shuddered— **"a counter-signature. In storm-script. Which is not a script. Which is weather. Weather cannot sign."**

"Tell that to the sky," I said, "the next time it answers a prayer."

Because that was Wren's trick, the one she'd learned from the oldest souls in the vaults, the ones who remembered the court's childhood mistakes: the ledgers only hold what they can *name*. An entry needs a number, a seal, a line of court-script binding it to the book. Wren had copied herself out of her page and re-inked herself in storm-script—in *weather*, in the language of the waking gods, a language the court had spent an age teaching itself not to read. She wasn't a stolen entry anymore. She was an unfiled document. She was, as far as the Undercourt's books were concerned, *weather that happened to be shaped like my sister*, riding in the one place in creation the court's writ had never reached: the body of a man the valley had scrubbed of magic, carrying a storm the court had no category for.

The deadline hadn't been broken by force. There had been no force at all. My sister had simply, quietly, twelve years ahead of everyone, learned the court's grammar better than the court—and then written herself out of its sentence.

**"Assessment,"** the warden said, and its voice had gone strange, papery, uncertain, **"cannot proceed. The entry is... illegible."**

"Then I guess," Sera said, dropping her blade-point to the floor, breathing hard, moonlight guttering around her like a candle in wind, "you'll have to let us go. Since you can't assess us. Rules are rules. I hear this place is very fond of rules."

For a long moment the warden stood in the archway, lock-head ticking softly, reading and rereading the impossible line in my scar. Around us the Gallery held its breath—every soul-case, every pillar, the dark above the shelves. Then, from everywhere and nowhere, a new sound: bells? No. Pages. Thousands of pages turning at once, deep in the mountain, as the court's ledger-crats tore through their books looking for a precedent that did not exist.

The warden stepped aside.

It did not open a door. It simply ceased to be in the archway, the way a shadow ceases when the light moves, and the passage beyond lay open, sloping upward, breathing cold air that smelled—for the first time in hours—of snow and sky and the world above.

**"Go,"** it said. **"But the court declares what it declares. And the court has declared hunt."**

"We know," Sera said. "We heard."

* * *

We climbed. The passage narrowed, then widened, then gave up on architecture altogether and became a crack in the world, and we climbed it with our fingers bleeding and the silver thread in my arm humming our mother's lullaby the whole way, off-key, triumphant.

Near the top, where the black stone gave way to frozen earth and the air went sharp enough to taste, Corvass landed on my shoulder—all of him now, one crow, one eye human and one eye a coin of stormlight.

"You knew," I said. "In the dream. You knew what she was planning."

"I am a god of small prayers," Corvass said. "I know what sisters tell each other in the dark. Besides—" he tilted his head, and his coin-eye reflected the thin line of dawn above us— "I owed her. She prayed to me once, twelve years ago. Did you know that? From inside the vault. One small prayer. *Let my brother live long enough to come for me.*"

The words went through me like the old lightning.

"Gods wake," Corvass said, hopping toward the crack of light, "because prayers need a debtor. And prayers get answered, storm-child, because someone always pays. Come. Your sister is weather, the court is hunting, and the moon has turned. I want to see the sky before someone makes it illegal."

We came out of the mountain into a snowstorm, and the snowstorm stopped.

Every flake hung still, mid-fall, catching the dawn—thousands of small white lanterns suspended in the air around us. And in the stillness, the snow began to move against the wind, gathering, swirling, drawing inward and upward into a shape. A shape with my mother's laugh and Wren's grin, made of falling weather, looking at me with eyes like the gap between clouds.

"Well," said my sister's soul, wearing the storm like a borrowed coat. "You're late. But then, you always were."

Behind us, far below, the mountain began to ring like a struck bell.

The Hunt was coming.
