## 26. Above the Mountain

The last thing the Undercourt gave us was a door, and the door was a lie.

It looked like an exit—obsidian frame, cold light spilling through it, the honest gray of open sky. Sera went through first with her sword drawn, and I followed with Wren's soul burning in my scars like swallowed lightning, and for three heartbeats I believed we had won.

Then the door closed behind us and there was no mountain at all. There was no ground. There was only snow, falling upward.

I don't know how else to say it. We stood on nothing—a shelf of black stone no wider than a coffin, jutting from the mountain's crown into a storm that did not fall but *rose*, flakes streaming past us toward some heaven that wanted them back. The wind came from every direction and none. My ears popped. The cold went through my coat, through my skin, through the place where twelve years of Greyhollow quiet had taught me to keep everything still.

"Rosalind." Sera's voice, flat and controlled, the voice she used when control was all she had left. "Don't look down."

I looked down.

Far below—so far it might have been memory rather than distance—the Steppe spread out like a sheet of hammered silver, catching the last of the dying moonlight. And on that silver, small as flecks of ash, things were moving. Climbing. The mountain's face was crawling with them.

"Hunters," I said.

"More than hunters." She turned a slow circle, blade up, moon-blessing guttering along its edge like a candle in a draft. "Look at the sky."

I looked up instead, because I am contrary even at the end of the world, and that was when I understood what she meant. The clouds above us were not clouds. They were turning—slowly, deliberately, the way a man turns to face a sound behind him. The whole sky had rotated its attention onto this one shelf of stone, onto me, onto the soul I was carrying in my body like a smuggled coal.

*The sky itself turns against them.* Corvass had warned us, back in the corpse-road dark: *the court bought the weather long ago, little storm. Every cloud between here and the sea owes it a debt.* I hadn't understood. I understood now. The storm rising around us wasn't weather. It was collection.

"Wren," I said quietly. "Are you there?"

She answered the way she always answered now—not in words, not exactly, but in warmth. A pulse along my old burn scars, the ones the storm had written on me the night I killed the king's heir. A flicker, like someone tapping a code on the inside of my ribs. *Here. Here. Here.*

Twelve years in a vault, and my sister had learned to be patient in ways I never managed. But patience was running out for both of us. Somewhere below, the thirteenth moon was waning toward its turn, and though her soul had slipped its ledger into my storm-mark during the dream—Wren's cunning, Wren's theft, done while the Understeward thought he held the pen—the court would not simply let a debt walk out wearing my skin.

"They'll invoke the contract," Sera said. She'd gone very still, watching the climbing shapes. "If they can get you to speak your name freely—if they can trick one word of assent out of you—they can recall her. Or you. Both, probably. The original debt plus interest." Her eyes cut to me. "So we don't talk. We climb."

"Climb what? There's nothing above us but the part of the sky that hates us."

"There's always something above us." She pointed with her sword, up and east, where the rising snow thinned against a smear of darker air. "That's a storm-well. I can feel it pulling. Your storms will feel it too—it's a throat, Rosalind. Weather goes down it. And if weather goes down—" her jaw tightened, "—maybe souls can go sideways."

I didn't ask how she knew. She was a paladin of a broken order; knowing things she shouldn't was half of what remained of her faith.

* * *

We climbed.

There was no path. There was barely a mountain—just handholds of frozen glass where the Steppe's reflections had crawled up the rock over centuries and died there, each one a frozen silhouette of someone who had once doubted themselves on the flats below. I tried not to look at their faces. Some of them wore mine.

Sera went first because she was lighter and because she refused, with the whole rigid architecture of her soul, to do anything else. I went second with Wren pulsing warnings against my ribs whenever the stone went bad—*left, left, don't trust the blue ice*—and I marveled, even with my fingers cracking and the hunters' wings whispering somewhere below us, at how quickly you could relearn a sister. Twelve years of vault-silence, and already she knew my climbing better than I did.

The cold was the worst of it. Not the sharp kind, which only hurts, but the deep kind, the kind that makes arguments. Halfway up the second pitch my hands stopped being my hands, and a voice that was not quite the wind and not quite the court began speaking through the snow itself.

*Rosalind Whitlock.*

My name. Spoken aloud, in the open, by the sky. It hit me like a hook behind the sternum.

*You are carrying stolen property. Return it, and the court forgives the storm that killed a prince. Return it, and you may go back to your valley. Back to your forge. Back to being no one, safely, forever.*

"Don't answer," Sera said through her teeth. She didn't look back. "It wants your voice. Any word counts. Even *no*. Especially *no*—a refusal is consent to negotiate, and negotiation is jurisdiction."

So I climbed in silence, and the sky spoke my name again and again, softer each time, gentler, until it sounded like my mother calling me in for supper, until it sounded like Wren at nine years old standing in the doorway of the forge saying *Ros. Ros. Rosalind.* It knew things about my childhood it should not have known. It offered me things I had wanted every night for twelve years: the valley unexiled, the elders' faces when they saw me come home clean, a life where the storm stayed asleep and nobody died.

And underneath it all, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, Wren pulsed: *here, here, here.* Not arguing with the voice. Just reminding me which name mattered.

The hunters broke the treeline of the sky just past the third pitch.

They came up out of the rising snow like ink dropped in water—six of them, then ten, then too many to count, shapes of black glass and borrowed wind, each one wearing a fragment of the court's seal burned into where a face should be. They didn't attack. That was the horror of it. They formed a ring around our shelf of stone and waited, patient as clerks, and the sky's voice said:

*Last offer, farrier. The girl's soul for your silence. You need only nod.*

A nod. Not even a word. That was how hungry they were—that was how much the ledger slipping their grasp had frightened them. I almost laughed. Twelve years I had lived by never nodding, never signing, never giving the world one inch of my true shape, and now the entire machinery of the Undercourt was begging for the smallest motion of my head.

Sera stepped off the wall.

Just—stepped off, backward, into empty air, and for one obscene moment I thought she had chosen to die rather than watch me break. Then her moon-blessing caught her, silver light sheeting off her armor, and she fell *upward*, riding the rising snow like a swimmer riding a wave, and she hit the nearest hunter with her sword leading and her oath screaming behind it.

The ring shattered. Hunters scattered like startled crows—which, I thought wildly, Corvass would have found insulting—and Sera landed back on the stone beside me, breathing hard, her blessing dimmed by a full shade.

"That bought us a minute," she said. "Climb."

* * *

The storm-well was real.

We felt it before we saw it: a pull in the teeth, a hush in the roar, the sensation of walking into a room where everyone has just stopped talking. Then the rock ended and there it was—a column of descending calm, a shaft maybe thirty feet across where the snow fell *downward*, straight and silent, vanishing into blue-black depth. Around its rim the winds of the hostile sky bent away like water around a stone.

Weather went down it. Which meant, if Sera was right, if Corvass was right, if any of the mad cartography of the waking gods was right—

"It's a throat," I said. "Whose?"

"The moon's," Sera said. "Or the sky's. Or something older than both." She was staring into it with an expression I recognized from mirrors: the look of someone about to bet everything on a story they can't prove. "Corvass said the gods wake because prayers need a debtor. Well—debts need a road home. This is the road. Everything that falls here falls *toward*."

"Toward what?"

"Does it matter?" For the first time since Saltmere, she smiled, and it was a terrible, wonderful thing. "Rosalind. Your sister is made of soul-stuff and cunning. Your storms are made of sky. If anything in this world knows how to ride a road that leads toward—"

Below us, the hunters reformed. All of them. The ring closed, and this time they weren't waiting. The sky's voice came down like a verdict:

*Then we take the property by force, and the farrier's soul as damages.*

The first hunter leapt.

I did the only thing I had ever been good at. I called the storm.

Not as a weapon—I had learned that much on the Hollow King's Road, learned it through mercy, learned it holding shelter over strangers while wraiths screamed against the wind. I opened myself the way you open a gate for refugees. Every scar on my body lit white, and the storm that had slept in Greyhollow for twelve years, the storm that had killed a prince and exiled a boy and waited and waited and waited, came up through me like a tide answering the moon.

But I didn't throw it at the hunters. I threw it *around* us. A shell of thunder, a sphere of howling air with Sera and me and Wren at the center, and then I stepped off the mountain into the well.

Falling. Downward, finally, honestly downward, wrapped in my own weather, the hunters' claws scoring lightning off the outside of my storm-cocoon while inside there was only rain and Sera's grip on my arm and Wren's pulse singing *here, here, here* faster now, brighter, like laughter, like my sister at nine years old running ahead of me down a hill she wasn't supposed to climb.

The blue-black depth rushed up—or we rushed down—and at the last instant, before whatever waited at the bottom of the moon's throat could close over us, Wren flared once, hard, deliberate, a single pulse shaped like a word I could almost read:

*Almost. Hold on. Almost—*

* * *

We hit water.

Cold, real, ordinary water—a river, shallow and fast, under a sky that was merely a sky, gray and indifferent and so beautiful I nearly wept into it. I surfaced gasping, dragged Sera up by the collar, and we lay on a gravel bar while the current pulled at our boots and the rain—gentle, normal, falling-the-right-way rain—came down on us both.

Above us, far above, a seam of wrong-colored light marked where the well had been. The hunters did not follow. Whatever rules governed the moon's throat, the court's contracts apparently did not cross it.

"We're out," Sera said. She said it twice, testing it. "We're actually out."

I lay back on the gravel and laughed until it hurt, and then I reached for Wren, reaching inward, reaching for the pulse—

And found silence.

Not absence. Not loss—I would have known loss, would have felt it tear something out of me. This was different. This was a held breath. A door closed gently from the other side. And beneath my skin, in the oldest of my storm-scars, something had changed weight, the way a house changes when someone moves quietly through a room and settles into a chair.

"She's here," I said slowly. "Deeper. She's—hiding. She slipped the ledger into my marks during the dream, and now she's slipped deeper, into—" I pressed my palm flat against my chest, feeling the faint, stubborn warmth banked there like coals raked over. "Into the storm itself. She's hiding in my weather."

Sera sat up, dripping, and looked at me with an expression caught between relief and dread. "Then the court can't take her without taking you."

"No," I agreed. "It can't."

"And it will try." She nodded upstream, where the gray light was beginning to fail, and where—on the far bank, at the edge of the trees—crows had begun to gather. Dozens of them. Silent. Watching us with eyes that were, every single one, human.

One of them hopped forward, cocked its head, and spoke with Corvass's dry, ancient patience:

"You led them a fine chase, little storm. But hear me now, and hear me well." The crow's feathers lifted in the rain. "The thirteenth moon turns in six days. And the hunter they've sent after you is not one of those glass clerks you left behind on the mountain."

Something in my chest went cold that the river couldn't account for.

"What is it, then?"

The crow looked at me for a long moment, and for the first time since I'd met him, the god of small prayers looked afraid.

"It's the one who wrote your contract," he said. "And it already knows where your sister sleeps."
