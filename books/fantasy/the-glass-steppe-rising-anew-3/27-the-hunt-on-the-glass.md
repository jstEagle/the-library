## 27. The Hunt on the Glass

I felt them before I saw them—the way you feel a storm front rolling in over open water, a pressure behind the eyes, a wrongness in the light.

We were three days out from the mountain. Three days of running across glass that gave back our faces like a lie told well. Rosalind had Wren's soul banked somewhere under his skin—I could see it if I looked at him sideways, a faint silver shimmer beneath the storm-marks that crawled up his forearms now and never fully faded—and the court wanted it back. Wanted *him* back. The Hunt had been called at moonrise two nights ago, and the sky had been the color of a bruise ever since.

"Stop," I said.

Rosalind stopped. He'd learned to trust my instincts on the Steppe, mostly because my instincts had kept us alive through the salt-flats and the wraith-road and the bridge where he'd had to say his own name aloud for the first time in twelve years. He stopped the way soldiers stop—weight dropping, hands loose, already reading the ground.

"What is it?"

"The reflections." I turned a slow circle. The Steppe stretched to every horizon, mirror-flat, reflecting a sky that didn't match the real one anymore. Above us: bruise-purple, cloudless. Below us: black thunderheads stacked like siege engines. "Look down."

He looked. So did Wren—or the body of rain and light that walked beside us wearing her shape, her soul given flesh by Rosalind's surrender of everything he was. She was still learning how to be embodied. Her feet left no prints. When she spoke, frost formed on her lips.

The reflection showed four riders.

There were only three of us.

"They've been following us since the wraith-road," Wren said. Her voice came out doubled, hers and something older underneath. Twelve years in the court's vaults had taught her things. "They travel underneath. Through the glass. They surface when they're ready."

"How long?" Rosalind asked.

Wren tilted her head, listening to something we couldn't hear. "Minutes."

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about disgrace: it doesn't lift. You carry it like a stone sewn into your coat, and after enough years you forget which pocket it's in, but you never forget the weight.

I had been Sera Vane of the Order of the Silver Veil once. Sworn to the moon-cult, oath-bound to hunt god-touched abominations for a crown that feared what it couldn't control. Then I'd stood in a burning village square with a torch in my hand and forty god-touched children huddled behind me, and I'd put the torch out in a rain barrel and said *no*, and everything I was had ended right there.

Now here I was, standing on the corpse of a dead god's hunting ground, about to do the same thing again. Say no. Draw a line. Hold it alone.

Some vows you break your way into honor. Others you break your way out of it. I'd stopped trying to keep score.

"We run," Rosalind said. It wasn't a question, exactly, but it wasn't a plan either.

"No." I was already unbuckling my sword belt, checking the straps. "You can't outrun them. Not carrying her. Not with your power still—" I gestured at his arms, where the storm-marks flickered like faulty lamplight. His control had gotten better on the Hollow King's Road, but better wasn't good, not against what was coming up through the glass.

"Then we fight together."

"You fight, you burn out. I've watched it happen. Your storms need anchoring or they eat you from the inside, and I'm the anchor." I pulled my gauntlets tight. "That's the whole arrangement, Rosalind. That's been the arrangement since Saltmere. You just haven't had to say it out loud yet."

He stared at me. Behind him, Wren's borrowed face went very still.

"Sera—"

"Take her east. There's a seam in the glass half a league out—I felt it this morning, old god-work, pre-Undercourt. If Corvass is telling the truth about the covenant roads, it'll take you off the Steppe entirely. Out of their jurisdiction." I drew my sword. Moon-steel, dull as pewter now; my blessing had been bleeding out of it for days, spent in small mercies, and there wasn't much left. There was going to be even less soon. "Get her through the seam. Don't look back."

"I'm not leaving you."

"You are, actually. That's the job." I smiled, and I meant it, which surprised me. "I spent fifteen years taking orders from men who thought duty meant obedience. This is the first order I've ever given that I actually believe in. Take it."

* * *

The Hunt surfaced four minutes later, exactly as Wren had said, and they were worse than the reflections promised.

Four riders on horses made of smoke and ledger-paper, their bodies stitched from contracts and forfeitures, faces blank as unsigned documents. The chief hunter rode first—the one from the court, the one whose voice had read the original contract aloud in a hall of obsidian, the one who had invoked Wren's name like a knife. Where its face should have been there was a seal, wax-red, unbroken.

It looked at me. A seal has no eyes, but I have been looked at by worse.

"Paladin," it said. Its voice was the sound of a stamp coming down on parchment. "The debt stands. Stand aside."

"I'm standing," I said. "This is me standing."

"The farrier owes a soul. The contract is valid. Interference constitutes breach."

"Funny thing about contracts." I rolled my shoulders, and let what was left of my blessing rise. It came up through me like cold silver water, thin and fading, the last of the moon's light the cult had ever poured into me. "I broke mine years ago. Turns out it's contagious."

The chief hunter raised one hand—a hand of vellum and dried ink—and the other three riders fanned out across the glass, cutting off the east. Cutting off Rosalind's route.

Good. That was the point. Every second they spent on me was a second he wasn't spending running.

I heard his footsteps behind me, fast, and Wren's voice, sharp: "*Go, you idiot, go—*" and then the sound of them running, two sets of footfalls where one should have been silent, and I planted my feet and stopped listening because listening would make me want to turn around.

The blessing flared.

* * *

Fighting on the Glass Steppe is fighting yourself. Everything reflects. Every move you make comes back at you doubled, tripled, a hall of mirrors with edges. The first rider came at me and I met it blade-to-blade and my own reflection met it too, from below, rising up through the glass like a drowned woman finally getting her chance.

I killed my reflection first. It's a strange thing, driving steel through your own face. It doesn't hurt. It just feels *final*, like signing something.

The rider came apart into loose pages. The second was already on me.

Moon-blessing isn't a weapon. That's the first thing they teach you at the Veil, and the last thing anyone remembers. It's a *light*. It shows things as they are. Under my failing silver glow, the riders' true shapes showed through the contract-flesh: not demons, not gods. Clerks. Bureaucrats with swords. Souls that had been sold so long ago they'd forgotten they'd ever been anything else, pressed into service as enforcers of terms they'd never read.

I almost pitied them. Almost.

The second rider's blade took me across the shoulder and the pain was bright and clean and honest, the most honest thing I'd felt in years. I answered with the flat of my moon-steel and a word in the old liturgy—the one that means *the night sees you*—and the blessing went out of me in a rush, pouring down my arm, into the blade, into the rider, and it came apart like fog in sunlight.

Two left. Plus the chief.

My shoulder was a ruin. My blessing was a candle stub. Somewhere east, half a league away, a man who used to be a farrier was running with his sister's soul under his skin, and every step he gained was bought with blood I was spending like it was going out of fashion.

Because it was.

* * *

Here's the thing I hadn't told Rosalind. Here's the thing I'd buried so deep even I'd stopped visiting it.

The Debt Collector hadn't just offered to forgive my disgrace. It had offered to give me back my *oath*. Restored rank. Restored blessing, full and burning, more than I'd ever held. All I had to do was deliver the storm-witch to the court, and I'd be Sera Vane of the Silver Veil again, whole and honored, the village of children rewritten into a footnote, the torch relit in my memory as a test I'd passed.

I'd said no. And saying no had cost me—not the refusal itself, but what the refusal revealed. That the blessing I carried, the moon-light I'd been spending all these years, was a loan against a vow I no longer held. Every miracle I worked drew down a balance that would never be replenished. I was a lamp burning oil that stopped being poured the day I said *no* in that village square.

I had maybe a tenth of it left when the third rider reached me.

I spent it anyway.

* * *

The third rider was fast. Faster than the others—it had been a knight once, I think, before the ledgers got it. It fought like someone who remembered loving the work. We went across the glass in a ringing clash of steel, and my reflection rose to fight its reflection, and for thirty seconds the Steppe was nothing but four women killing each other in perfect symmetry.

I won by cheating. That's the other thing they teach you at the Veil, or rather the thing they *don't* teach you, the thing you learn when you're disgraced and desperate and holding a dying blade: honor is a luxury of the undefeated. I dropped my sword, caught its wrist, and drove my gauntleted fist through the contract-seal at its throat.

Pages everywhere. Ink in the wind. The glass drank it down.

One left.

The chief hunter hadn't moved. It sat its smoke-horse ten paces away, watching me with its unbroken seal, and when it spoke, its voice was almost gentle.

"You have nothing left, paladin."

It was right. I could barely stand. The blessing was gone—not dimmed, *gone*, the first time in twenty years I'd stood in full darkness without the moon's borrowed light in my veins. My shoulder bled freely onto the glass. My knees had opinions.

But I had a sword arm that still worked, and I had twenty years of knowing exactly what I was willing to die for, and I had the memory of forty children's faces looking up at me in a burning square while I chose, finally, correctly, *no*.

"I have enough," I said.

It dismounted. That was the worst part—that it took me seriously enough to dismount, to come at me on foot, seal-face tilting as it drew a blade of black iron that drank the daylight. It moved like the end of an argument.

I moved like a woman with nothing left to lose and one thing left to protect.

* * *

We broke together. Steel, blessing-less and human, against black iron and contractual certainty. It cut me twice more—ribs, thigh—and I gave it everything I had left, which was technique and fury and the particular stubbornness of people who have already lost everything except the choice of how.

And then, when it raised the black blade for the final stroke—

—the sky answered.

Not my sky. *His.* Thunder without clouds, lightning without storm, a white-hot crack across the bruise-colored heavens like the world splitting along a seam. The chief hunter froze. Even the glass beneath us shivered.

And I laughed. I couldn't help it. Blood in my teeth, blessing gone, legs giving out—and I laughed, because I knew that thunder. I'd anchored it for weeks. I'd felt it sleeping under my palms during the bad nights on the road, wild and frightened and learning, slowly, to be gentle.

He'd felt it. All the way from the seam, half a league east—he'd felt me falling, or Wren had told him, or the storm in his blood simply knew the way it knows weather coming. It didn't matter. What mattered was the sound of him turning around, which I heard in the thunder, plain as speech:

*I'm not leaving you either.*

The bolt came down between us and struck the glass, and the Steppe rang like a temple bell, and the chief hunter's seal cracked down the middle.

It looked at the crack. Then at me. Something that might have been understanding passed across its blank face—the look of a clerk realizing the ledger itself has been audited.

"The debt," it said, "has been contested."

And it sank backward into the glass like ink into water, and was gone.

* * *

I woke to rain.

Real rain, warm and impossible, falling on a Steppe that had never known weather. Rosalind knelt over me with his hands glowing faintly silver—my own spent blessing, reflected back, returned. Wren crouched at his shoulder, and where her tears fell, small white flowers opened in the glass.

"You absolute idiot," I rasped.

"Takes one to know one." His voice cracked on it. "Sera, your blessing—it's gone. I can feel the shape of where it was. It's empty."

"I know what empty feels like. I've been empty for fifteen years." I tried to sit up and discovered my ribs had filed a formal complaint. "The difference is, today I emptied it *on purpose*. On something that mattered."

Wren took my hand. Her grip was cool and startlingly solid. "The moon will give it back," she said. "Or something better. I read the ledgers, paladin. Nothing given freely is ever truly lost. It's only *moved*."

I wanted to believe her. I was too tired to argue, which amounted to the same thing.

Rosalind lifted me, careful as a man carrying a wounded bird, and we started east toward the seam, toward whatever came next. Behind us, the thunderheads in the reflection were breaking apart. Ahead, the real sky was doing something it hadn't done in twelve years anywhere on the Steppe.

It was clearing.

And far to the west, at the edge of hearing, I heard the court beneath the mountain begin to scream.
